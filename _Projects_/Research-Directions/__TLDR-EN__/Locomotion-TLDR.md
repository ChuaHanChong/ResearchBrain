# TL;DR: Promising Research Directions: Locomotion, Bipedal & Quadruped

> [!info] What this is
> A quick TL;DR of [[Locomotion|Promising Research Directions: Locomotion, Bipedal & Quadruped]]. For each direction: **the bet**, the reasoning, the sharpest open questions, and the risks. Full detail (related-work tables, all hypotheses, benchmarks) stays in the source. Plain-language version: [[__ELI5-EN__/Locomotion-ELI5|ELI5]].

> [!abstract] Overview
> A legged robot must act on things it cannot sense. Terrain friction, ground height ahead of the swing foot, payload, contact, and model error exist in sim but not on hardware. So a deployable policy must recover or bound that hidden state, from proprioception, exteroception, or a learned model. 10 directions in 2 clusters. The non-consensus bet: the lever is the *mechanism that extracts more from each step*. Feasible references over more demos. Looking ahead over reacting. Off-policy reuse and world-model imagination over PPO throwing away each rollout. Not more data, scale, or domain randomization.

## Cluster map
| Cluster | Directions | Shared bottleneck |
|---|---|---|
| A: Bipedal Locomotion & Dynamic Skills | A1–A8 | High-DoF whole-body balance with only partial observation. Flat RL fails here, and the reference/constraint structure is the lever |
| B: Quadruped Locomotion & Real-World Adaptation | B1–B3 | Recovering or bounding the hidden physical state (μ, h, payload, model error) so the policy is deployable |

## A: Bipedal Locomotion & Dynamic Skills
*The humanoid's legs: whole-body balance and walking under partial observation, plus the agile skills (terrain traversal, parkour, dance, fall-recovery) that make a humanoid more than a slow walker. Flat RL fails; the reference/constraint structure is the lever.*

### A1: Perceptive Terrain Traversal & Vertical Mobility
> [!abstract] The bet
> Mix online reference generation (G1 WBC-Gen+Track-style) with learned active gaze (TAGA-style, sensor pointing under limited field of view, not the map-attention that reads a full passive scan more selectively). The mix beats gaze alone and generation alone, biggest where the height-scan is sparse. On stepping-stone / wide-gap tasks (≥70 cm spacing), it holds ≥0.95 SR at ≤40% full-scan *training* compute, a number that must be re-baselined on onboard wall-clock inference latency and elevation-map-noise robustness, the cost the robot actually feels at deploy; on dense rough terrain it ties full-scan within 2 pp. There is also an *interior-optimal reference horizon*: SR is non-monotone in horizon under ~0.5 m perturbation (PHP-class).

**Why**: A humanoid clearing a 75 cm box picks its swing path before the foot lands, and the deciding ground shape is *sparse*, a few load-bearing footholds matter. Perceptive-beats-blind is over (PRIOR-Loco 100% traversal, G1 WBC-Gen+Track's 0.962-vs-0.230 box-climb, Deep WB Parkour 100% on a 1.2 m OOD range). Open: *what to look at, over what horizon*. Consensus pays for a full fixed-horizon scan, but TAGA matches it at 65.2% lower cost.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* a full scan goes stale and wastes effort, yet only a few footholds matter. *Challenged:* not that gaze beats a full scan — that's TAGA's own already-settled result. The live assumption is that the two proven levers, online reference generation and learned where-to-look, are *substitutes*: every cited system uses one or the other, never both. *Wager:* the two levers are complements, super-additive on sparse-foothold terrain, not merely additive.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) A 2×2 test (fixed full scan vs learned camera-gaze) × (no reference vs online-generated reference) on sparse stepping-stones, with the gaze-by-generation interaction term as the pre-registered primary result: ≥0.95 SR at ≤40% full-scan training compute *and* at matched inference latency? 2) Sweep the 0.5 s horizon against ~0.5 m perturbation: interior-optimal horizon (non-monotone SR)? 3) Does a jointly-trained fused policy avoid the gaze-generation deadlock (gaze looking away from where the generator then swings the foot into) that a naive frozen-modules composition risks?

> [!warning] Risks
> - Depth dropout mid-vault can be fatal. → Keep a DWL-class proprioceptive safety mode underneath (A3); report fall rate under forced dropout.
> - Generated references can be infeasible. → Filter via G1 WBC-Gen+Track RL fine-tuning; report the tracker's reject/clamp rate.
> - Parkour-class skills (1.25 m walls, 3.41 m/s vaults) damage hardware. → Limit claims to tested platforms; report contact-force and motor-temperature (A4).
> - Three networks (generator, gaze predictor, tracker) compete for one 50 Hz control budget, and the ≤40% figure is a training cost, not deploy latency. → Gate every headline SR on a composed onboard deployment-latency budget, and treat the gaze-generation deadlock (gaze looking away from where the generator then swings into) as a second go/no-go alongside the main test.

### A2: Dynamic Agile Skills via Physically-Feasible Motion Imitation
> [!abstract] The bet
> On extreme agile skills (flips, martial-arts), the bet is a single lever: an *adaptive tracking-tolerance schedule* (Adaptive Motion Tracking/KungfuBot-style) beats a matched fixed-tolerance project-everything pipeline (SPIDER/Opt2Skill-style) — cutting tracking error to KungfuBot's 53.25 mm (vs >233 mm OmniH2O/ExBody2) and lifting downstream RL to ReActor's 97.45% (G1) at zero penetration. Clip-level *rejection* is demoted to a secondary diagnostic, not the bet: OmniXtreme already reaches 91.08% overall real-world SR across 24 high-dynamic motions (96.36% on flips) with no clip-level reject gate at all, and even KungfuBot's own lab swapped rejection for relaxed tracking in its follow-up, KungfuBot2 (92.68% SR).

**Why**: A human backflip breaks the robot's torque, contact, and balance limits, so raw mocap is a target it cannot hit. First step: projection onto the dynamically-feasible manifold, $\xi \mapsto \Pi_{\mathcal F}(\xi)$, now standard at scale (SPIDER projects 2.4 M frames across 9 embodiments at 100% task SR). Open: what to do with the leftovers that stay untrackable — an adaptive tolerance schedule, not rejection, is where this direction bets.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* tracking makes sense only if the robot can do it; KungfuBot's *filtering* gives the 233→53 mm cut. *Challenged:* not that feasibility beats scale, SPIDER and friends prove projection scales just fine. The narrower claim is that a *fixed* tolerance on top of that projection is enough on its own. *Wager:* an adaptive tolerance schedule beats a matched fixed-tolerance pipeline on the extreme tail — that's the whole bet. Clip-level rejection is a separate, secondary diagnostic (see the questions below), not part of it.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) Does an adaptive (early-loose/late-tight) tolerance learn skills a fixed tolerance cannot — the direction's primary test? 2) [Secondary diagnostic] Five-arm test (track-raw / OmniXtreme-style actuation-aware project-everything / LIMMT-style upstream data-curation filter / Extreme-RGMT-style continual consolidation / rejection+adaptive-tolerance), split by rejection ratio on a shared cross-controller protocol: is rejection's gain concentrated on the tail (>30% episode-collapse) and near-zero below it, or does it add nothing once tolerance and actuation-awareness are matched? 3) Does a reference-free policy (GaitSpan/WARL-style, no mocap clip to filter or reject at all) match either arm on the extreme-skill tail, sidestepping the whole projection/tolerance question?

> [!warning] Risks
> - Physics-filtering is only as good as the URDF/dynamics model. → Check the manifold against hardware; report the sim-vs-real tracking-error gap.
> - Filtering throws away expressive motions. → Pair with EFGCL-style force-guidance that *expands* feasibility; report the recovered-skill fraction.
> - The +15.22 pp downstream-RL gain may not transfer. → Test the feasibility→trainability curve across skill classes, not one average.
> - The rejection diagnostic may already be moot. → OmniXtreme hits 91.08% SR with no rejection gate at all; treat it as exploratory, the headline rests on the tolerance-schedule result alone.

### A3: Autonomous Fall Recovery as Non-Periodic Whole-Body Control
> [!abstract] The bet
> Add a phase-clock / periodic foot-contact prior to a prior-free recovery policy, and the getting-up SR from any starting pose *drops* ≥10 pp versus prior-free HUMANUP / HoST. Also, HUMANUP's two-stage discover/refine and HoST's single-stage multi-critic reach *parity* (within 5 pp) on a matched posture/terrain set. So the lever is curriculum-shaped exploration, not one specific breakdown, and gait structure actively hurts.

**Why**: Getting up has no gait cycle, starts from any pose, and gives one binary end reward, so locomotion biases work *against* it: phase-clocked, foot-contact priors push the policy away from the contact-rich ground moves recovery needs (HoST 100% standing, VIGOR beats HoST/FIRM up to 5×). HUMANUP's discover/refine over 20,000 postures and HoST's multi-critic reach parity by different routes.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* the sparse-reward setting needs *some* scaffold (HUMANUP single-stage fails to converge; HoST scores 0% without multi-critic), but a *gait-like* one is wrong. *Challenged:* the field's clock-based-RL default has a specific holder — Blind Bipedal Stair Climbing carries a cyclic phase-clock over unmodified from flat-ground RL into blind stair traversal and reports it "error-correcting" (80%/100% real stair ascent/descent). The same lab's later work directly tested that default on standing push-recovery and found a clock-*free* controller generally outperformed the clock-based one (perfect rejection at 258 N), but never tested the harder, fully non-periodic ground-up regime. *Wager:* the periodic prior fights the non-periodic ground-up moves and lowers SR there too.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) Add a phase-clock / foot-contact prior to a prior-free policy: SR drop ≥10 pp vs prior-free HUMANUP? 2) Do HUMANUP's two-stage and HoST's multi-critic reach parity (within 5 pp) at matched data, while an unstructured single-stage fails? 3) Can a combined locomotion+recovery stack finish a multi-obstacle course *including* falls with no human help?

> [!warning] Risks
> - Recovery motions stress hardware (flailing limbs, impacts). → Use HUMANUP-style regularization; report contact-force and temperature, treat smoothness as first-class (A4).
> - Discovery may find unsafe trajectories. → Refine into a deployable policy; report the discovery→deployment safety-margin gap.
> - The 20,000 postures may miss hard real falls. → Report the coverage curve and failure modes by initial-configuration class.

### A4: Embodiment-Grounded Locomotion Constraints (Force, Acoustic, Thermal)
> [!abstract] The bet
> A real gait pays three costs at once, motor heat, footstep noise, ground-reaction force, but nobody has measured how they trade against each other under one policy. A single threshold-conditioned cost-head (QuietPaw-style CNCP) fed heat + acoustic + predicted GRF (QuietWalk's R²≈0.99) traces a genuine three-way cost front nobody has: pushing noise down ≥3 dBA provably raises peak motor temperature (the thermal-vs-acoustic curve, the moat), and pushing peak GRF down by a comparable margin measurably moves at least one of heat or noise, both read off *one* swept policy that holds overheating below Thermal-Aware Residual's <10% and noise within +1 dBA of QuietWalk's quiet mean at ≤5% task-SR loss. That training this sweep once is cheaper than retraining a fixed-weight specialist per operating point is a real efficiency the mechanism inherits from QuietPaw/PCHC, already demonstrated on real hardware, not the finding this direction is betting on.

**Why**: Sim rewards task success and ignores physical cost: motors overheat, gaits are loud, forces spike, deployment-fatal. A gait is bounded by hard embodiment limits (motor-temperature ceilings, force limits, noise budgets) that live *off* the reward surface and *compete*. Single-cost fixes exist (Thermal-Aware Residual drops overheating 70%→<10%, QuietWalk cuts noise 7.17 dBA; IMF couples energy + impact, 35% peak-power cut), and Olaf even co-regulates heat **and** noise in one policy, but with *fixed* weights, no force axis, and no swept trade-off. QuietPaw and, on a humanoid, PCHC already run a single conditional cost-head, and AMOR runs the same weight-conditioned mechanism on character-motion trade-offs, so the head-sweeps-a-Pareto mechanism itself is not new either. Every one of these results still covers at most a *pair* of costs; none has fed one policy all three and swept the joint surface.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* a policy optimizing only task success pushes embodiment limits until hardware fails, Thermal-Aware Residual's 70% overheating is the evidence. *Challenged:* Olaf's own design choice, one policy with *fixed*, separately-tuned reward weights per cost axis, thermal and noise reported in separate figures, no sweep, is the contested position. Olaf proves fixed weights are good enough for two costs on real hardware, neck-pitch actuator held at 77.3°C over a 1-hr run vs ~100°C in 40 s baseline, noise down 13.5 dB. This direction bets that recipe caps what's reachable: a real heat-noise coupling exists and stays unexploited without a joint sweep, and adding force (which Olaf omits) only sharpens the case. Whether a conditional head can sweep a Pareto front at all stays settled, not reopened, QuietPaw and PCHC already do that on real hardware. *Wager:* one head fed heat + acoustic + force traces a genuine three-way front nobody has measured, quieter is measurably hotter, and lower-force measurably moves at least one of the other two axes. Training that sweep once instead of retraining N fixed-weight specialists is a bonus the mechanism inherits, not the point.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) Sweeping the noise and force thresholds under a fixed heat budget (and vice versa) on one conditional head: is the resulting three-way front real, first past a measurement-repeatability check confirming the signal clears the cross-surface/cross-ambient noise floor of the dBA/temperature/force metrics? 2) Does the head hold overheating <10% and noise within +1 dBA at ≤5% SR loss once force is added in, without breaking either bound, and does a swept fixed-weight composite (~8 weight vectors) reach the same three-way front at comparable total training cost? 3) When a dialed-in preference sits outside the achievable front (max-quiet + max-cool + max-force-limited at once, unreachable together), does the head degrade gracefully to the nearest feasible point, or breach a true hard safety limit? 4) A GRF/thermal predictor fit on one platform and deployed with zero refitting on a structurally different platform: does its error blow past a usable threshold, unlike Towards-bridging-the-gap's physics-grounded energy model, which travels across ANYMAL/TYTAN/MINIMAL on only ~20 s of per-platform recalibration (32% Cost-of-Transport cut on ANYMAL D, 0.97 on TYTAN)?

> [!warning] Risks
> - A quiet or cool gait may be slower. → Use Thermal-Aware Residual's residual structure; report the cost-vs-task Pareto front, not one number.
> - GRF/thermal models are platform-specific, R²≈0.99 may not transfer to a structurally different robot. → QuietWalk's own held-out-footwear ablation only probes footwear, not platform; question 4 above is the direct test, treat predictors as per-platform-calibrated until re-validated, and compare against Towards-bridging-the-gap's physics-grounded precedent.
> - Acoustic and thermal metrics are measurement-fragile, dBA depends on surface/room, peak temperature on ambient/duty-cycle/sensor placement. → the three-way front could sit below its own measurement noise floor; run the repeatability pre-test first (QuietWalk reports noise per surface, across 4, as the precedent).
> - A dialed-in preference can be jointly infeasible, a user could request max-quiet + max-cool + max-force-limited at a point that may not exist on the front at all. → confirm the head degrades to the nearest feasible point rather than a true hard-limit violation; don't deploy without an infeasibility detector if it doesn't.
> - The thermal/latency axis gates the whole A1+A2+A3 spine, not just a wing of this direction. Parkour (A1) and recovery (A3) already stress motors, and stacking cost estimators competes for the same onboard compute and thermal budget. → Carry the thermal budget as one composed deployment-latency-plus-heat gate on every A1/A2/A3 headline number; keep the acoustic/GRF/force Pareto as the separate bet.

### A5: Sample-Efficient Off-Policy & Flow Locomotion Learning
> [!abstract] The bet
> A flow-matching policy head can represent the multimodal action distribution (which foot, which transition) that a unimodal Gaussian head collapses to its mean. That off-policy already beats PPO on wall-clock is settled background, not the bet: the bet is whether a flow policy (FPO++/PolicyFlow-style) beats a Gaussian off-policy learner (SAC Legged Locomotion-style) given *matched mode/phase conditioning* on multimodal-contact skills, and ties on smooth walking. A parallel Gaussian-plus-structure literature (per-gait AMP, MoE) already deploys multi-gait humanoids, so a clean null (conditioning-matched Gaussian ties flow) is a publishable, commit-worthy outcome, not a failure.

**Why**: Off-policy beating PPO on wall-clock is settled (Parallel Q-Learning 2023, SAC Legged Locomotion *closes the gap entirely*, FastTD3 solves HumanoidBench <3 hrs) — that's background here, not the open question. The open edge is the flow axis: PolicyFlow prevents mode collapse where Gaussian baselines fail, but the premise is threatened, CoRe-MoE already handles multi-terrain gait selection with a plain Gaussian mixture-of-experts, so structured conditioning may already make each mode locally unimodal. A separate, lower-stakes opportunity sits beside it: Humanoid Loco 15min's 15-minute loop makes a reward/curriculum search affordable that PPO cannot, but that builds on the settled off-policy result above and isn't this direction's bet.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* a policy's action distribution must represent every mode the control law needs; locomotion's contact is multimodal and a Gaussian head collapses it to its mean, no amount of off-policy reuse fixes that. *Challenged:* the entire off-policy locomotion literature assumes a Gaussian head suffices, without questioning whether it under-fits; PolicyFlow/FPO++ bet the opposite, but structured conditioning (CoRe-MoE) may already dissolve the multimodality. *Wager:* flow beats a conditioning-matched Gaussian on multimodal contact — a qualitative bet, no numeric threshold exists in the corpus yet; a clean null is a real, publishable outcome.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) Flow vs a *conditioning-matched* Gaussian at matched compute, split by contact multimodality: does flow win on agile-contact, or tie (a clean null)? 2) On smooth, unimodal walking, does flow *tie* the Gaussian, or does the richer distribution carry a persistent tax? 3) Does flow's sim-to-real SR-retention ratio match a matched Gaussian's, or does the extra sampling cost more crossing the reality gap? 4) Does the off-policy win *narrow or flip* on sparse-reward sub-tasks (A3)? (Background, not this direction's bet: at fixed wall-clock, do N fast 15-min reward/curriculum rounds beat one PPO run?)

> [!warning] Risks
> - Flow's real cost is onboard *inference* latency, not the training-time figure. → A flow/CNF policy needs multi-step integration every control step; gate the bet on a composed onboard inference-latency budget against the 50–200 Hz control period, not the <50%-extra training number.
> - The dense-reward edge may not hold for fall-recovery (A3). → Limit off-policy claims to dense-reward locomotion; report where off-policy trails PPO on sparse tasks.
> - Fast-trained policies may overfit sim. → Both Humanoid Loco 15min and FastTD3 deploy on real robots; report the sim-to-real SR gap.
> - Vision policies (A1) cost more on consumer GPUs. → Report wall-clock for state-based and vision-based separately; the 15-min number is state-based.

### A6: Regime-Contingent MPC-Learning Coupling for Legged Control
> [!abstract] The bet
> Run the head-to-head nobody has, at *matched onboard latency*, not matched training compute: hold platform and task fixed, and swap only the coupling type, RL-as-contact-scheduler, RL-residual, learned-differentiable-surrogate, in-loop generative sampler, MPC-as-teacher, plus the pure-RL and pure-MPC endpoints, across a contact-richness × observability × horizon regime grid. The field's most-cited coupling number, Residual-MPC's own reported +78% velocity-range margin, shrinks to within 2 pp of the cheapest coupling on a well-modelled, contact-sparse cell once latency is normalized, *and* re-emerges at ≥10 pp specifically on contact-rich/actively-disturbed cells, a regime-flip most readers of that paper wouldn't predict. More broadly, no single coupling wins by more than 5 pp everywhere.

**Why**: Every hybrid-control paper validates its own coupling against the same two baselines, pure RL and pure MPC, at its own training-compute budget, and reports a win, but nobody re-scores the couplings against each other at matched onboard latency. Residual-MPC's headline +78% comes from running a 100 Hz MPC *inside* the RL loop against a lighter baseline that never spends the same latency budget — nobody has checked how much of that number survives a fair re-scoring. The one paper that runs both pure endpoints on a shared harness already shows they split the axes: RL wins on energy and one disturbance direction, MPC wins the other and lateral stability, neither dominates.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* MPC enforces a dynamics model and hard constraints by construction, so it's reliable where the model is right and the horizon is short; a learned policy compiles experience into a reactive map, so it's fast and robust where the model is wrong or the state is unobserved. Which one matters is regime-indexed (contact-richness, observability, horizon), not universal. *Challenged:* that a coupling's reported margin over the endpoints is architectural, not a compute-budget artifact — every coupling paper matches compute to its own chosen baseline, never to a sibling coupling's latency. *Wager:* the field's most-cited margin partially dissolves under matched-latency scoring, and survives only in a narrower, specific regime than its headline framing claims.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) Does Residual-MPC's +78% margin shrink to within 2 pp of the cheapest coupling on a well-modelled, contact-sparse cell at matched latency, and re-emerge at ≥10 pp on contact-rich/disturbed cells — and is that re-emergence a *tail-quality* effect specifically, not a tail-latency one, since Residual-MPC's fixed-iteration ADMM solver (a hard 25 iterations every step) makes wall-clock solve time near-constant by design, so the tail should show up instead as the QP's p99/mean *optimality-gap* ratio, >2× on those cells vs <1.2× for the cheap couplings, since a fixed compute budget converges worse the further a sudden disturbance pushes the true optimum from the prior warm-start? 2) Across the broader regime grid, does the per-cell winner change coupling to coupling, with no coupling's margin exceeding 5 pp everywhere? 3) Under payload/friction shift and partial observability, do the online-adaptive-surrogate and MPC-teacher couplings win the cell while offline-fixed couplings and pure-MPC degrade fastest?

> [!warning] Risks
> - Re-implementing four couplings faithfully is a large engineering surface, and a weak arm fakes a regime-contingent result for implementation reasons, not architectural ones. → Reproduce each coupling against its own paper's reported number on its own platform first, as a validity gate before the cross-coupling grid.
> - The endpoints and couplings don't share a platform today (MIT Humanoid, Centauro, Go1/Go2 are all different). → Port every arm onto one shared platform and metric contract; treat any result that needs a different platform as out of scope.
> - Matched compute is ambiguous between training and inference, a coupling can win on training-sample efficiency yet lose on onboard latency once an MPC solve is in the loop — and a fixed-compute-budget controller doesn't miss its deadline in a hard state, it just returns a worse-converged solution there, silently degrading quality during exactly the disturbance-recovery moment being tested. → Fix both a training wall-clock budget and an onboard inference-latency budget; log the full p50/p95/p99 optimality-gap distribution per cell, not just the mean, and report success under each separately.

### A7: Persistent Actuator-Health State Across Episode Resets (Cross-Episode Wear-Leveling)
> [!abstract] The bet
> Every RL/MPC training loop treats episode reset as a physical reset of the robot's body: peak torque, heat, and contact-force spikes are wiped clean at each reset. Real hardware doesn't reset, fatigue and wear pile up across the robot's whole service life. First, check whether today's within-episode cost proxies (peak temperature, peak ground-reaction force) already track that cumulative damage. If they correlate well (Pearson ρ≥0.5), this collapses into A4 with a longer reward horizon, not a new direction. If they don't, a policy that carries a persistent damage state across resets and actively redistributes load away from the most-worn joint should extend simulated cycles-to-failure by ≥15% over a matched policy trained on today's within-episode costs alone, at ≤3% task-success cost.

**Why**: QuietWalk and Thermal-Aware Residual already measure real embodiment cost, noise, ground force, motor heat, and QuietWalk's own problem statement even names "accelerated hardware wear" as the motivation. But every one of these numbers is charged within a single walk or session: a 650 m run stays under 50°C, a session runs 27 minutes before overheating, a rollout is 7.17 dBA quieter. None of it survives the reset into tomorrow's episode. Fault-Tolerant-MoE routes around a joint only after it has already failed, reactive, not proactive. Meanwhile a much older field, health-aware and prognostics-and-health-management control, already solves exactly this for other machines (a factory-arm planner that reweights motion away from a weakening joint using its estimated remaining life), but nobody has connected that idea to a legged robot that resets every episode.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* actuators don't know an episode ended, wear keeps accumulating on the robot's own clock. This doc's own math already sets aside a slot for "actuator state" inside the hidden context every adaptation module already infers from proprioception, nothing stops that slot from also holding a damage number. *Challenged:* that regulating cost within one episode is enough, because nothing forces the robot to remember what happened last episode. One nearby paper trains against a scripted, fixed degradation instead of one the robot's own choices caused; another carries a decaying fatigue number through training as an exploration nudge, useful, but not fatigue physics and never checked for whether it survives a real reset. *Wager:* a physically grounded, persistent damage state, not a training heuristic, unlocks proactive wear-leveling that neither comes close to.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) Do today's peak-temperature and peak-force numbers already predict cumulative damage well (the make-or-break check, run first) — and against *which* damage counter? A cyclic wear counter (rainflow/Miner's-rule, the right physics for bearings and mechanical fatigue) is not the same instrument as a sustained-heat counter (Arrhenius kinetics, the right physics for winding insulation — the "10°C rise halves insulation life" rule). Can a quieter, cooler gait by today's metrics still rack up more total wear because it takes more, smaller steps to get there on the cyclic counter — and can it *also* rack up more sustained-exposure insulation damage than a spikier baseline, with the two counters disagreeing on which gait is actually safer? 2) If not, does a policy that carries a damage state across resets and actively spreads load away from the worn joint actually last longer before failure, at only a small task-success cost? 3) Does redistributing load *before* a joint fails beat only reacting once a fault is already detected? 4) Does adding a slow-moving damage number to the existing hidden-context inference break the fast-moving terrain/payload inference already living there?

> [!warning] Risks
> - The whole direction can collapse into "A4, but longer" if the correlation check comes back positive. → Run that check first and cheaply, before any expensive wear-leveling training.
> - Every simulator needs new plumbing to carry a damage state through a reset, this is not a one-line reward change. → Start with a lightweight surrogate accumulator, not a full physics model, and validate its own accuracy before trusting downstream numbers.
> - No real hardware has been run to actual failure to check the surrogate against, so the whole exercise could be tuned to the wrong physics. → Ground the surrogate in established fatigue-accounting math and report its own validation error.
> - Charging existing agile-skill directions against a service-life cost they've never paid could make their headline numbers look worse and invite pushback. → Report it as an added axis alongside today's success rate, not a replacement for it.

### A8: Thermally-Derated Feasibility Manifolds for Motion Imitation
> [!abstract] The bet
> A2's projection onto the feasible manifold is computed once, offline, from the robot's torque limit, a single number pulled from the URDF at room temperature. But a motor's sustained-torque budget is set by how much heat it can shed before crossing a safety ceiling, and that headroom shrinks as the winding heats up. Re-project KungfuBot's own extreme-skill corpus against a temperature-derated torque bound, built from Towards-bridging-the-gap's PACE dissipation model and scaled to Thermal-Aware Residual's measured 30/50/58°C envelope (a 60°C ceiling), and at least 10% of the clips KungfuBot's room-temperature filter currently accepts should get newly rejected once actuator temperature nears 58°C, the point where sustained-duty headroom to the ceiling is nearly exhausted. A policy retrained against that shrunk manifold should hold KungfuBot's own 53.25 mm tracking error through a sustained duty cycle at that temperature, where the static-bound-trained policy degrades or torque-saturates.

**Why**: Every method that filters or retargets a mocap clip onto what the robot can do (KungfuBot, SPIDER, Opt2Skill, Implicit Kinodynamic Retargeting, ReActor) reads a torque limit once from the URDF and treats it as fixed for the lifetime of training. But electrical dissipation scales with resistance, and resistance rises as a winding heats (PACE's own energy model formalizes this), so the same torque draws more heat the hotter the motor already is. Two nearby papers get close and stop: PACE re-identifies inertia/damping/friction from ~20 s of real data but calibrates once, offline; Thermal-Aware Residual regulates real-time temperature at the control layer but, confirmed by direct read, never touches the torque limit or the feasible manifold itself, it is the operating-envelope data anchor, not a solution.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* a motor's sustained torque is bounded by how much heat it can still shed before a safety ceiling, and that headroom shrinks as its own temperature rises, an electrical fact independent of training. Thermal-Aware Residual's own 70%→<10% overheating swing is the real-hardware evidence the headroom moves by a large margin. *Challenged:* every cited imitation method treats the torque limit as a hardware constant fixed before training, inherited from the URDF's own static convention, never argued for. *Wager:* re-scoring against a temperature-derated bound rejects a real, double-digit-percent slice of clips a static bound wrongly accepts near the thermal ceiling, and a policy trained against that shrunk target survives a sustained hot duty cycle where a statically-trained one doesn't.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) Does temperature-derating the torque bound (PACE's dissipation relation, scaled to Thermal-Aware Residual's measured envelope) newly reject ≥10% of KungfuBot's room-temperature-accepted clips once actuator temperature nears 58°C, the decisive test? 2) Does a policy retrained against that shrunk manifold hold KungfuBot's own tracking-error benchmark through a sustained hot duty cycle where the static-bound policy degrades or saturates? 3) Do runtime compensation (Thermal-Aware Residual's residual reward) or offline recalibration (PACE) already cover this without touching the feasible manifold, or does the policy still attempt clips a temperature-derated bound would have pre-emptively refused? 4) Does the derating envelope, measured on a quadruped, transfer unmodified to the humanoid platform the bet is scored on? 5) Does temperature-conditioning which clips are attempted compose with KungfuBot's own adaptive tolerance schedule for how tightly a clip is tracked, or do the two axes fight each other?

> [!warning] Risks
> - The operating-envelope anchor is measured on a different platform (quadruped) than the bet is scored on (humanoid). → Test the cross-platform transfer before trusting the headline numbers; re-measure on the target platform if it doesn't hold.
> - Sustained duty near the thermal ceiling may be hard to produce outside simulation, short demos and cool restarts are the field's default. → Run the test in a validated thermal simulation first, escalate to real hardware once directional.
> - Counting rejected clips against the whole corpus double-counts the ones already rejected at room temperature. → Score the new rejection rate only against the room-temperature-accepted subset.
> - Plumbing a temperature input into the offline projection pipeline is new infrastructure, not a small reward change. → Stage the cheap offline re-scoring test first, before committing to full retraining.

## B: Quadruped Locomotion & Real-World Adaptation
*Recovering or bounding the hidden physical state, friction, ground height, payload, model error, between a sim-trained quadruped policy and a deployable one. Covers proprioceptive robustness, world-model dreaming for few-shot adaptation, and perceptive mapless mobility-to-goal.*

### B1: Proprioceptive-Only Robustness under Disturbance & Payload
> [!abstract] The bet
> Make the durable contribution the **diagnostic**: a stratified payload(0→80% body weight) × discontinuous-terrain protocol that maps the payload-vs-terrain trade-off *curve*, not the verdict on which backbone wins. Under a controlled backbone swap (TCN / contrastive-IMC / Transformer / Transformer-XL) at *identical* privileged supervision, the curve shows *where* each backbone trades RMA's 12 kg (80% body weight) payload against HIM/TERT's discontinuous-terrain ceiling (≥60% stairs where TCN scores 0%), with the 80%-payload stratum as the unowned cell. (The which-backbone-wins verdict is fragile, TAR already holds both axes in one zero-shot demo.)

**Why**: A deployable quadruped runs on proprioception alone; sim's privileged context (friction μ, payload m, ground compliance) is unavailable on hardware, and the two escapes (a camera, or on-robot fine-tuning) both add cost. Proprioceptive inference is mature across *many backbones* (RMA's TCN at 12 kg, DreamWaQ's Beta-VAE, HIM's contrastive embedding, LoadAdapt's explicit estimator). The mechanism is settled; what each backbone *trades* is not. TAR already holds 12 kg payload, 150 N pushes, and discontinuous steps zero-fine-tune on a real Go2 in one model, but its own backbone swap covers only {contrastive-recurrent, MLP, TCN}, no Transformer or Transformer-XL, so it says nothing about whether attention wins the conjunction.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* a supervised module turns proprioceptive history into a context estimate, RMA's 10 Hz module holds 12 kg with zero fine-tuning. But payload and stairs live on different timescales, payload near-static, terrain a fast step-to-step transient, and a single fixed-size recurrent backbone must compress both into one state — which is why TERT's Transformer already beats RMA's TCN on stairs alone (60% vs 0%) at identical supervision. *Challenged:* not that the payload-and-terrain conjunction is unreachable, TAR already holds both in one model. The narrower claim is that TAR's own finding, its contrastive-recurrent backbone "consistently outperformed" MLP/TCN, generalizes to the *joint* stratum; it shouldn't, since TAR never tested attention and only analyzed factors *in isolation*, never jointly. *Wager:* at the joint 80%-payload/stairs stratum specifically, an attention/long-context backbone beats the contrastive-recurrent backbone by ≥5 pp joint SR, extending TERT's terrain-only win rather than contradicting TAR's isolated-factor result.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) Hold supervision fixed, swap TCN ↔ contrastive-IMC ↔ Transformer ↔ Transformer-XL; sweep payload (0→80% body weight) × terrain (flat→stairs): a payload-vs-terrain Pareto? 2) Does proprioception match exteroception on *dynamics* context and only lose on *anticipatory geometry* (gaps, steps)? 3) Does LocoFormer's in-context adaptation match RMA's supervised inference *without* a privileged target?

> [!warning] Risks
> - Proprioception cannot see geometry ahead, a step or gap is invisible until contact. → Limit B1 to dynamics-context inference; geometry ahead is A1's job.
> - Adaptation supervision needs privileged sim. → Standard for the RMA family. Cross-ref [[Sim2Real|Sim2Real]] for the privileged-to-proprioceptive distillation.
> - The TCN-vs-Transformer gap may be task-specific, TERT's stair win may not transfer. → Run the backbone ablation per-terrain, not as one average.

### B2: World-Model Dreaming for Few-Shot Real-World Adaptation
> [!abstract] The bet
> *Check the bound before trusting it.* An epistemic-uncertainty estimate that looks calibrated in sim can decorrelate from true held-out error once sim-to-real drift sets in, so before committing to dreaming-as-core, confirm the estimate stays calibrated under drift. If that check passes: give it a small budget, ≤5 trajectories / 15–30 min of real data. A *bounded latent dreamer* (DreamTIP + RWM-U's epistemic penalty, 0.91 ANYmal D) reaches DreamTIP's 100%-vs-10% on a 52 cm climb, and *beats reconstruct-then-retrain* (LoopSR's 100% Stair-Up via digital-twin) on held-out terrain: dreaming generalizes across the latent, while a reconstructed sim overfits the rebuilt geometry. If the check fails, dreaming-as-core is premature and reconstruct-then-retrain is the safer default.

**Why**: Deep RL needs millions of interactions; the field's two answers (heavy domain randomization, on-robot RL) both waste them. A learned dynamics model is a *multiplier*: each real transition updates the model, which imagines thousands, so 5 trajectories become worth thousands (DreamTIP: ~5 → 100% on a 52 cm climb where a non-dreaming baseline gets 10%). Anti-DR few-shot adaptation is consensus through *two* mechanisms, reconstruct-then-retrain (LoopSR) and latent-dreaming (DreamTIP, DayDreamer), but nobody has run them head-to-head, and neither has an epistemic bound whose validity has actually been checked.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* sample efficiency depends on model accuracy per interaction, not raw count. *Challenged:* whether *rebuilding a digital-twin sim and retraining* is the right way to spend the trajectories; it needs a faithful rebuild and gives no uncertainty estimate. *Wager:* once instrumented as calibrated, latent dreaming gets more from each trajectory than reconstruct-then-retrain, and the epistemic bound (lacked by LoopSR and Simulator Adaptation, claimed by RWM-U) is what should make the tiny budget safe, conditional on that calibration check, not assumed from it.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) Does the epistemic-uncertainty estimate stay calibrated under sim-to-real drift, tracking measured held-out model error as drift grows, rather than decorrelating from it? (A verified 2026 paper, "Biased Dreams," documents exactly this decorrelation — an "attractor" effect pulling RSSM latent rollouts toward well-supported regions even as true error grows — but only on off-legged DMC control tasks under OOD-initial-state shift, not sim-to-real drift directly, and only qualitatively, no fix proposed. Real evidence that motivates the check without settling it for legged sim-to-real.) This gates everything else. 2) Three-arm test at fixed real-data budget (DR-blind / reconstruct-then-retrain / latent-dreaming); adapt on A, test on held-out B: does the bounded dreamer win? 3) Does RWM-U's epistemic penalty β have an interior optimum matched to long-horizon prediction error? 4) Does DreamTIP's 5-trajectory adaptation overfit the adapted terrain, and does the penalty fix it?

> [!warning] Risks
> - The epistemic estimate may not be calibrated under drift, a bound that looks fine in sim can end up regulating noise once real-world drift sets in. → Run the calibration-under-drift check first and gate the dreaming-vs-reconstruction commit on it; default to reconstruct-then-retrain if it fails.
> - Long imagined rollouts drift. → Use RWM-U's epistemic penalty; report prediction error vs horizon, cap rollout length where uncertainty spikes.
> - 5-trajectory adaptation may overfit. → Report held-out-terrain SR, not the adapted terrain.
> - Dreaming needs a good pretraining simulator. → Push diverse large-scale pretraining (SimDist). Cross-ref [[WAM|WAM]] for substrate quality, [[Sim2Real|Sim2Real]] for the sim side.

### B3: Perceptive Mapless Locomotion-to-Goal & Traversability
> [!abstract] The bet
> The mapless-memory advantage *scales with how map-friendly the course is*. The SRU-style mapless-minus-mapped SR gap is near-zero on feature-rich static courses, where Wheeled-Legged NavLoco's map→plan→track works best. The gap *grows* to ≥20 pp as features get sparse, dynamics rise, and drift builds up. SwarmDiffusion-style joint traversability+trajectory and COTRATE cross-platform perception keep this advantage across embodiments.

**Why**: The classical map-build → plan → track pipeline is brittle on rough terrain (drift, moving obstacles, no GPS) and cuts the link between perception and gait, but that brittleness is *claimed*, not measured against a graded map-friendliness axis. SRU's learned recurrent spatial state lifts mapless SR 23.5% over LSTM/GRU, transfers zero-shot 100+ m; HiPAN hits 94.7% SR / 83.6 SPL in dead-ends.

**First-principles**: *Principle:* a learned recurrent spatial state ("where have I been, where is the goal relative to me"), tied to the gait, beats a lossy metric map under drift and dynamics. *Challenged:* that goal-reaching splits cleanly into map-build → plan → track; Wheeled-Legged NavLoco holds this strongest (kilometer-scale urban nav, 0 collisions), but SRU's 23.5% gain and HiPAN's dead-end SR bet memory wins where the map breaks first. *Wager:* the advantage is a *function of map-friendliness*, not constant.

**Sharpest questions**: 1) Grade courses by map-friendliness (feature density, dynamics, drift): SRU-minus-NavLoco gap near-zero on feature-rich and ≥20 pp as terrain degrades? 2) Does a SwarmDiffusion-style head that *jointly* infers traversability + trajectory beat a perception-only substrate (COTRATE) for cross-embodiment goal-reaching (Spot→Husky)? 3) Does SPL drop below SR more on loop-prone courses, and does path conditioning narrow the gap?

> [!warning] Risks
> - Mapless policies can loop or get stuck. → Report SPL next to SR, not SR alone (HiPAN's SPL catches this).
> - High-level goal *reasoning* is VLN territory. → Scope this to low-level mapless control + traversability; cross-reference language-instruction goal-reasoning to [[Embodied-AI|Embodied-AI]].
> - Traversability self-supervision needs experience, COTRATE learns from rollouts. → Report where cross-platform transfer (Spot→Husky) holds zero-shot vs needs continual learning.
