# Biased Dreams: Limitations to Epistemic Uncertainty Quantification in Latent Dynamics Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Model-based RL uses ensemble disagreement as an epistemic-uncertainty signal to guard against exploiting an inaccurate dynamics model, a technique validated on low-dimensional proprioceptive dynamics. This paper tests whether that guard still works once the dynamics model is a latent world model — Dreamer's Recurrent State Space Model (RSSM) — and finds it does not: ensemble disagreement stays locally informative but decouples from the true, compounding rollout error, because RSSM imagination exhibits an attractor bias that pulls predictions toward well-supported latent regions regardless of accuracy, which can make the model overestimate returns.

## Problem

- Ensemble-based epistemic uncertainty is well-established for proprioceptive (low-dimensional state) dynamics models, where it mitigates model exploitation, guides exploration, and promotes caution in offline/model-based RL.
- Whether this same signal is trustworthy for latent dynamics models — dominant for image-based model-based RL via architectures like Dreamer's RSSM — is largely untested; the field implicitly assumes the proprioceptive result transfers unchanged.
- If ensemble disagreement decouples from the true compounding error accumulated over a long imagined rollout, a policy or planner that trusts the uncertainty signal can be silently misled into exploiting the inaccurate part of the latent model, with no warning from the uncertainty estimate itself.

## Method

- An ==external ensemble of one-step latent transition predictors== (M=5) is trained alongside a Dreamer-style **RSSM** (stochastic state size 30, deterministic state size 200) and a **Categorical RSSM** variant (32 categoricals × 32 classes), with epistemic uncertainty measured as the ==Geometric Jensen-Shannon divergence== over the ensemble's predicted next-latent-state distributions.
- Four DMC Suite tasks — **Cartpole Swingup**, **Cheetah Run**, **Hopper Hop**, **Walker Run** — are trained for 5 runs of 1M environment steps each, plus **MuJoCo HalfCheetah** run as a proprioceptive-dynamics baseline for comparison.
- Initial states are partitioned into ==in-distribution (ID) vs out-of-distribution (OOD)== sets by ensemble uncertainty, then N=1000 rollouts of horizon T=50 are unrolled in closed-loop imagination, comparing the ensemble's *predicted* epistemic uncertainty against the *measured* compounding model error (decoded back to proprioceptive state) over the rollout.

## Results

- Proprioceptive ensembles show the expected behavior: unstable, appropriately-elevated uncertainty on OOD rollouts. RSSM latent rollouts instead show an ==attractor behavior== — predictions converge toward dominant, "well-supported" transition flows regardless of whether they track the true environment dynamics.
- OOD ensemble-disagreement uncertainty starts elevated but **rapidly converges to in-distribution (ID) levels** as the rollout proceeds, even as the true proprioceptive-decoded rollout error keeps growing — local uncertainty stays informative step-to-step but decouples entirely from the trajectory-level compounding error it is meant to track.
- The attractor bias causes closed-loop imagined rollouts to **systematically overestimate returns** on dense-reward tasks, because the attractor regions the rollout drifts toward coincide with high-reward behavior regions of the latent space.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Epistemic uncertainty quantification does not automatically transfer from proprioceptive to latent dynamics models — RSSMs have a structural failure mode (attractor bias) that proprioceptive ensembles do not exhibit.
> - A world model's uncertainty estimate can look calibrated locally (single-step ensemble disagreement) while being wrong globally (multi-step compounding rollout error) — local uncertainty is not a license to trust a long imagined rollout.
> - The paper proposes no fix within the current uncertainty-estimation framework; it argues the failure needs to be addressed at the level of the latent dynamics model itself (latent-space restructuring, alternative parametrizations), leaving a formal mechanistic account of the attractor effect as open work.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL), specifically addressing challenges in dynamics modeling when dealing with high-dimensional observations like images. MBRL aims to improve sample efficiency in reinforcement learning by learning a model of the environment dynamics, which can then be used to generate simulated experience for policy learning. The field broadly distinguishes between "physical" dynamics models, which operate on low-dimensional proprioceptive inputs, and "latent" dynamics models, which process high-dimensional observations by first learning a compact latent representation.

A prominent family of latent dynamics models is the Recurrent State Space Model (RSSM), foundational to the Dreamer algorithms (Dreamer, DreamerV2, DreamerV3) and their extensions. These models have become state-of-the-art in vision-based MBRL due to their ability to learn predictive dynamics from pixel data.

A critical component often employed in MBRL, particularly with physical dynamics models, is epistemic uncertainty quantification. Epistemic uncertainty arises from limited data or imperfect learning and is typically used to inform exploration strategies, mitigate model exploitation by avoiding overconfident predictions in unknown regions, or promote caution. While its application and interpretation are considered well-understood for physical dynamics models—often through ensemble-based methods that measure disagreement among multiple predictors—this work investigates its transferability and reliability in latent dynamics models. Many existing latent dynamics approaches, such as those used for exploitation mitigation or curiosity-driven exploration, implicitly assume that epistemic uncertainty in latent space reflects true model reliability in a similar manner to physical models. This paper critically examines this assumption, suggesting a gap in the systematic analysis of epistemic uncertainty in latent space models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to provide a systematic empirical analysis of epistemic uncertainty quantification in latent dynamics models, specifically those based on the Recurrent State Space Model (RSSM) architecture. The authors aim to identify potential limitations and biases that may arise when transferring established uncertainty quantification techniques from physical dynamics models directly to latent space models.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several observations and assumptions within the current MBRL landscape:
- **Prevalence of Latent Dynamics Models:** Latent dynamics models, particularly the RSSM and its variants, are widely adopted in vision-based MBRL due to their ability to handle high-dimensional observations effectively.
- **Direct Transfer of Epistemic Uncertainty Methods:** Epistemic uncertainty quantification is a standard practice in MBRL, typically employed using ensemble methods. However, its application to latent dynamics models often involves a direct transfer of principles from physical dynamics models without thorough scrutiny of its reliability in the context of learned latent spaces.
- **Importance of Reliable Uncertainty:** Reliable uncertainty estimates are crucial for various MBRL components, including robust exploration (guiding agents to informative states), preventing model exploitation (avoiding policies that perform well only in simulated but inaccurate model predictions), and ensuring safety in unknown environments. If these estimates are unreliable in latent space, the efficacy of downstream MBRL components could be compromised.
- **Lack of Systematic Analysis:** To date, a systematic analysis of epistemic uncertainty quantification in latent dynamics models has been limited. The authors aim to fill this gap by empirically examining core assumptions.

Specifically, the paper sets out to address three key questions:
(i) How model-based rollouts, originating from both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) states, evolve under latent and physical transition dynamics. This aims to understand the qualitative behavior of latent trajectories compared to physical ones.
(ii) Whether ensemble-based one-step epistemic uncertainty in latent space correlates with deviations between latent rollouts and true environment behavior (physical discrepancy). This directly assesses the reliability of latent uncertainty estimates as indicators of predictive error.
(iii) How any potential mismatch between latent uncertainty and true predictive error affects policy learning, particularly through the lens of reward predictions during latent rollouts. This investigates the practical consequences of unreliable uncertainty on agent learning.

Through this detailed analysis, the authors seek to highlight fundamental challenges and limitations in relying on epistemic uncertainty as a direct measure of predictive reliability in RSSM-based latent dynamics models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs an empirical approach to evaluate the behavior of latent dynamics models and their associated epistemic uncertainty. The core methodology involves comparing rollouts generated by latent dynamics models with those from a physical dynamics model, under both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) starting conditions.

**Model Architectures:**
- **Latent Dynamics Models:** The primary models investigated are the **Recurrent State Space Model (RSSM)** (Hafner et al., 2019b) and its categorical variant, **Cat-RSSM** (Hafner et al., 2020; 2023). These models learn a compact latent representation of environment dynamics, consisting of a representation model, observation model, transition model, and reward model. RSSM uses a stochastic state `z_t` and a deterministic recurrent state `h_t`, while Cat-RSSM replaces the Gaussian stochastic state with categorical variables.
- **Physical Dynamics Model (for comparison):** A Probabilistic Ensemble (PE) model, commonly used in physical dynamics MBRL (e.g., Infoprop from Frauenknecht et al., 2025), is used for comparative analysis. This model operates on physical states and quantifies uncertainty through ensemble disagreement.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Environments:** Experiments are conducted on four tasks from the DeepMind Control Suite (DMC Suite): Cartpole Swingup, Cheetah Run, Hopper Hop, and Walker Run.
- **Training:** Agents are trained for 1 million environment steps using 5 random seeds for each task and architecture. The RSSM implementation follows Becker & Neumann (2022), and Cat-RSSM follows Becker et al. (2024). All models are trained with the Dreamer framework, alternating between RSSM training, actor-critic optimization, and environment interaction.
- **Rollout Types:**
 - **Prior rollouts:** Generated solely by the transition model (`p(z_t | z_{t-1}, a_{t-1})`), relying only on learned dynamics without observation updates.
 - **Posterior rollouts:** Use latent states from the representation model (`q(z_t | ·)`) updated at each step, representing the most informed latent trajectories.
 - **Posterior-informed rollouts:** Perform one-step transition model predictions initialized from posterior latent states, decoupling transition evaluation from observation noise.
- **In-Distribution (ID) and Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Start States:**
 - **ID states:** Chosen as the physical state closest to its k-nearest neighbors (k=100) in the replay buffer from the best-performing agent.
 - **OOD states:** Manually designed per environment to be physically valid but unlikely to occur under the training distribution (e.g., Cheetah tripping over its front leg, Hopper skydiving). Cartpole Swingup did not use OOD states due to the nature of its full state representation.
 - For the comparative physical dynamics model, ID and OOD trajectories are chosen based on lowest and highest predictive uncertainty, respectively, over random rollouts.

**Key Evaluation Metrics and Techniques:**
1. **Attractor Evaluation (Section 5.1):**
 - **Embedded Dynamics:** To visualize latent trajectories, 1000 random prior and posterior rollouts (50 steps with 3 warm-up steps) are collected. Deterministic features (recurrent state `h_t` and mean/mode stochastic state `z_m_t`) are normalized and projected into 2D using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). One-step displacements are binned to create vector-field-like visualizations, with exemplary ID and OOD posterior trajectories overlaid. This allows for qualitative assessment of how latent states evolve.
2. **Physical Discrepancy (Section 5.2):**
 - **Physical State Decoder:** The latent dynamics model is augmented with a physical state decoder `p(s_t | z_t)`, trained jointly with the ELBO. This decoder predicts environment states (excluding x-position) from latent states, allowing for evaluation of the physical realism of latent trajectories. The decoder's impact on RL performance was assessed and found to be negligible.
 - **Uncertainty Estimation:** An ensemble of M=5 one-step latent transition predictors `g_i(z_t | z_{t-1}, a_{t-1})` is trained on a next-state reconstruction objective. Epistemic uncertainty is computed using the Geometric Jensen-Shannon divergence between ensemble members, applied to prior rollouts.
 - **Computation of Physical Discrepancy:** This is quantified as the mean `ℓ_1` distance between predicted (via the physical state decoder) and ground-truth body positions. Ground truth is obtained by simulating the predicted action sequence in the actual environment. This metric directly measures how much the latent model's interpretation of the world deviates from reality.
3. **Reward Evaluation (Section 5.3):**
 - **Reward Discrepancy:** The step-wise signed difference `r_{pred,t} - r_{sim,t}` is calculated. `r_{pred,t}` is the reward predicted by the latent model from its latent trajectory, and `r_{sim,t}` is the reward obtained by executing the same action sequence in the actual environment. This evaluates the accuracy and bias of reward predictions in latent rollouts.

The approach systematically dissects the behavior of latent dynamics models, contrasting their latent space properties with their physical predictive capabilities and the reliability of their uncertainty estimates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several empirical findings regarding the limitations of epistemic uncertainty quantification in latent dynamics models, particularly those based on RSSM architectures.

#### 4.1. Attractor Behavior in Latent Transitions

The analysis of embedded dynamics revealed a distinct "attractor behavior" in latent dynamics models.
- **Comparison with Physical Models:** When starting from Out-of-Distribution (OOD) states, the physical dynamics model (PE) exhibited unstable predictions and accumulated errors, as expected. This behavior is typical for models encountering novel or poorly represented states, leading to divergence from true dynamics.
- **Latent Space Attractors:** In contrast, for both RSSM and Cat-RSSM, trajectories initialized from OOD states did not destabilize but rather converged rapidly to "dominant transition flows" within the latent space. These flows represent well-represented regions of the latent space, effectively pulling the OOD trajectories back into familiar patterns. This phenomenon was consistently observed across all DMC Suite tasks.
- **Deviation from True Dynamics:** This convergence suggests that the latent transitions are biased towards regions frequently encountered during training, potentially causing the latent model to "recover" from out-of-distribution physical states by mapping them to known latent states, even if this mapping deviates significantly from the true environment dynamics.

#### 4.2. Unreliability of Epistemic Uncertainty

The attractor behavior was found to undermine the reliability of epistemic uncertainty estimates in latent space.
- **Physical Discrepancy vs. Latent Uncertainty:**
 - **Prior Rollouts:** Both in-distribution (ID) and OOD prior rollouts showed a steady increase in physical discrepancy (measured as mean `ℓ_1` distance between predicted and ground-truth body positions) over time, reflecting model error accumulation.
 - **Posterior-Informed Rollouts:** While posterior-informed rollouts remained accurate in ID settings, their physical discrepancy also rose under OOD conditions, similar to prior rollouts.
 - **Decoupling:** Despite the persistent and growing physical errors in OOD trajectories, the ensemble-based epistemic uncertainty (measured via Geometric Jensen-Shannon divergence), which initially started high for OOD conditions, rapidly dropped to levels comparable to ID rollouts.
- **Masking Model Errors:** This decoupling indicates that the latent dynamics model, due to its attractor behavior, quickly re-establishes confidence in its predictions when an OOD state is drawn back into a well-represented latent region. Consequently, the epistemic uncertainty estimates in latent space fail to accurately reflect the true physical discrepancy or model error. The model "appears confident" in latent space even when its predictions diverge from the actual environment dynamics. This suggests that ensemble-based uncertainty, typically assumed to correlate with predictive error, becomes less reliable in latent dynamics models under OOD conditions.

#### 4.3. Systematic Reward Overestimation

The attractor behavior also introduced a systematic bias in reward predictions.
- **Reward Discrepancy:** Posterior rollouts, which are constantly updated with observations, accurately predicted rewards. However, prior rollouts, relying solely on the learned transition model, consistently overestimated rewards across all tasks and architectures (RSSM and Cat-RSSM).
- **Attractors and High-Reward Regions:** This overestimation suggests that the latent attractors, towards which OOD trajectories converge, frequently coincide with high-reward states. The model, when operating in imagination, biases its predictions towards these "familiar" and "rewarding" latent regions, regardless of whether these correspond to the actual physical outcomes.
- **Distorted Policy Learning:** Such a systematic overestimation bias in predicted rewards can negatively impact policy learning. Agents might become overly confident in behaviors that, in reality, do not yield the high rewards predicted by the model, leading to suboptimal policies and potentially unsafe actions.

In summary, the study empirically demonstrated that latent dynamics models exhibit an attractor behavior that skews latent transitions toward well-represented regions. This behavior leads to unreliable epistemic uncertainty estimates that fail to detect true physical discrepancies and systematically overestimates predicted rewards, challenging the direct transfer of uncertainty quantification paradigms from physical to latent dynamics models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work carries significant implications for the field of Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL), particularly for methods relying on latent dynamics models and epistemic uncertainty quantification.

**Fundamental Limitation Identified:** The most significant contribution is the empirical identification of a structural limitation in epistemic uncertainty estimation within RSSM-based latent dynamics models. The discovery of an "attractor behavior" in latent space, where out-of-distribution (OOD) trajectories are systematically pulled towards well-represented, low-uncertainty, and often high-reward regions, challenges the fundamental assumption that latent uncertainty reliably reflects true model error. This phenomenon is distinct from error accumulation observed in physical dynamics models and reveals a characteristic inductive bias of the DVAE-based architecture of RSSMs.

**Re-evaluation of Current Practices:** The findings necessitate a critical re-evaluation of current practices in MBRL that directly transfer epistemic uncertainty quantification techniques from physical to latent dynamics models. Many existing approaches for exploration, exploitation mitigation, and safety in latent MBRL rely on the premise that low latent uncertainty implies high predictive reliability. This paper empirically demonstrates that this assumption can be violated, as latent models may appear confident (low uncertainty) while simultaneously diverging significantly from true environment dynamics.

**Impact on Policy Learning and Safety:** The observed systematic overestimation of rewards due to this attractor behavior has direct consequences for policy learning. If agents are trained on overly optimistic reward predictions, they may learn suboptimal policies that fail to achieve desired outcomes in the real environment. This also poses a risk in safety-critical applications where models might falsely indicate safety (low uncertainty, high reward) in situations that are physically hazardous or unpredictable.

**Motivation for Architectural Advancements:** The study suggests that addressing this issue may require structural modifications to latent dynamics architectures, rather than merely improving existing uncertainty estimators. This could involve approaches focused on latent space restructuring (e.g., Li et as., 2024; Becker et al., 2024) or more principled variational inference methods (e.g., Becker & Neumann, 2022) to ensure that the latent space better reflects the underlying physical structure of the environment and that uncertainty estimates are more faithfully aligned with real-world predictive error.

**Future Research Directions:** The work explicitly motivates future research into a formal analysis of the causes and mechanisms underlying this attractor behavior. Understanding the mathematical or algorithmic roots of this bias could lead to more robust model designs. Additionally, the need for new methods to reliably quantify model uncertainty in latent spaces, perhaps by directly linking latent uncertainty to physical discrepancies, is highlighted.

In conclusion, "Biased Dreams" serves as a cautionary tale, emphasizing that the unique properties of learned latent spaces in vision-based MBRL demand a specialized understanding of uncertainty. It significantly advances the understanding of limitations in current latent dynamics models and calls for novel architectural and methodological developments to build more reliable and trustworthy MBRL systems.
