# Turning Sand to Gold: Recycling Data to Bridge On-Policy and Off-Policy Learning via Causal Bound

> [!summary] Summary
> By applying a novel causal upper-bound loss optimization (SUFT) within Deep Reinforcement Learning, researchers bridge the gap between on-policy and off-policy learning through the efficient reuse of discarded value network outputs. This leads to substantial gains in sample efficiency and agent performance across diverse DRL benchmarks, with negligible computational cost.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents face high computational and resource demands due to extensive training steps and large experience replay buffers.
- On-policy DRL algorithms are sample-inefficient, discarding collected data after each update for stable convergence.
- Off-policy DRL algorithms improve sample efficiency by reusing data but often introduce learning instability from policy divergence, requiring large buffers and extensive training to stabilize.

## Method

- A novel theoretical ==causal upper bound== on the factual (on-policy) loss is derived from the Neyman-Rubin potential outcomes framework, using observed counterfactual loss and an estimated treatment effect.
- This causal bound is integrated into DRL algorithms by mapping factual/counterfactual losses to on-policy/off-policy losses and the estimated treatment effect to a SUFT Off-Policy Evaluation (OPE) term.
- Previously discarded value network outputs (Q-values or V-values) are stored in the experience replay buffer and reused to compute the SUFT OPE term, enhancing loss optimization with minimal computational and storage overhead.

## Results

- The SUFT method introduces negligible computational (approx. **0.8%** of training time) and memory overhead (approx. **0.003%** additional space per experience).
- Agents augmented with SUFT achieved substantial performance gains, with SUFT DQN showing a **383.19%** mean reward ratio improvement on Atari 2600 games and SUFT SAC reaching **224.53%** on MuJoCo, consistently outperforming baselines.
- SUFT enables high performance with significantly smaller replay buffers, with SUFT DQN using a **4K** buffer outperforming a **100K** buffer baseline DQN by **437.05%**, demonstrating a drastic reduction in memory requirements.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The factual (on-policy) loss in DRL can be effectively upper-bounded by the observed counterfactual (off-policy) loss combined with an estimated treatment effect, offering a principled theoretical bridge between on-policy and off-policy learning.
> - Storing and reusing previously discarded value network outputs (Q or V values) from the experience replay buffer provides a computationally negligible method to quantify and leverage the estimated causal effect between behavior and target policies.
> - The proposed causal bound optimization (SUFT) significantly improves DRL performance by explicitly accounting for policy divergence, outperforming simpler solutions like larger replay buffers or general inertia regularization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents have demonstrated efficacy across various complex decision-making tasks, including gaming, robotics, healthcare, and autonomous driving. Despite these achievements, DRL typically necessitates extensive training steps and substantial experience replay buffers, resulting in high computational and resource requirements. For instance, large-scale DRL applications like AlphaGo Zero and OpenAI's Rubik's Cube robotic hand required millions of self-play games or thousands of years of simulated experience.

DRL agents are broadly categorized into on-policy and off-policy types. On-policy agents, such as A3C and PPO, operate by collecting experiences with a behavior policy and then optimizing a target policy. To maintain consistency between the behavior and target policies, all previously collected data is typically discarded after each optimization iteration. This approach contributes to stable convergence but at the cost of sample inefficiency, as new data collection is required for every learning step. In contrast, off-policy agents, including DQN and SAC, are designed to learn from previously collected experiences, allowing for distinct behavior and target policies. While this methodology enhances sample efficiency by reusing stored data in a replay buffer, it can introduce learning instability due to the potential mismatch between the policies. Mitigating this instability often requires a large number of training steps and extensive replay buffers, thereby reintroducing significant computational and resource demands.

Existing research efforts to address these DRL challenges include enhancing experience replay buffer sampling strategies to improve convergence rates. However, these methods often maintain high computational resource requirements and do not fully resolve the fundamental divergence between on-policy and off-policy learning. Other approaches propose additional computable terms to bridge this divergence, but these frequently involve complex techniques and demand extensive data collection.

This work integrates into the emerging field of Causal Reinforcement Learning (CRL), which aims to improve agent performance by incorporating causal inference methodologies. Within CRL, treatment is often defined as a dynamic sequence of actions (a policy), aligning with the DRL framework's focus on long-term policy effects. CRL research spans areas such as generalizability, agent safety, sample efficiency, and mitigation of spurious correlations. This paper contributes primarily to the aspects of sample efficiency and addressing spurious correlations by leveraging causal principles.

Specifically, the research positions itself within the context of potential outcomes causal bounds, rooted in the Neyman-Rubin framework. Previous works in this area have primarily focused on establishing generalization-error bounds to estimate individual treatment effects from observational data, typically by bounding the counterfactual loss. These methods often consider single interventions as treatments and are usually applied in general machine learning settings, frequently comparing a single target treatment against a single control treatment.

The present work differentiates itself by adapting the potential outcomes framework to the DRL context, redefining treatment as a policy, and uniquely focusing on establishing a causal upper bound on the *factual* loss (analogous to the on-policy loss) rather than the counterfactual loss. This is achieved by utilizing observed counterfactual loss (off-policy loss) and an estimated treatment effect. A key innovation lies in extending this concept to scenarios with multiple control treatments, which enables leveraging the diverse experiences typically stored in a DRL replay buffer. Furthermore, it introduces a mechanism to reuse previously discarded data (past value network outputs) to compute the estimated treatment effect, aiming to improve sample efficiency with minimal additional cost.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent challenges associated with scaling Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents to complex, real-world problems. Despite DRL's success in various domains, its practical application is frequently hindered by substantial computational and resource demands. This is largely attributed to two interconnected issues: sample inefficiency, particularly in on-policy methods, and learning instability in off-policy methods.

On-policy DRL algorithms achieve stable convergence by ensuring that the behavior policy (used for data collection) and the target policy (being optimized) are consistent. However, this stability comes at a significant cost: after each policy update, all previously collected experiences are discarded, necessitating fresh data collection for subsequent optimization steps. This "data discarding" mechanism leads to high sample inefficiency, requiring vast numbers of environmental interactions. Conversely, off-policy DRL algorithms aim to improve sample efficiency by learning from historical data stored in an experience replay buffer, allowing the behavior and target policies to differ. While this permits data reuse, the divergence between the policies can introduce instability, often requiring very large replay buffers and extensive training to stabilize learning. Consequently, both on-policy and off-policy paradigms, albeit for different reasons, contribute to the high computational and resource demands observed in DRL.

Existing approaches to mitigate these challenges, such as advanced replay buffer sampling strategies or the introduction of additional computable terms in the loss function, have offered partial solutions. However, these often fail to fully bridge the divergence between on-policy and off-policy learning or introduce their own computational complexities. There was a recognized gap in developing a method that could systematically account for the differences between behavior and target policies, thereby improving both sample efficiency and learning stability, without incurring substantial additional overhead.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of this research were formulated:

1. **Develop a Novel Causal Theoretical Result**: To establish a theoretical foundation rooted in causal inference that addresses the on-policy/off-policy divergence. Specifically, the objective was to derive a causal upper bound on the factual loss (equivalent to the on-policy loss in DRL). This bound was intended to be computable using the observed counterfactual loss (the standard off-policy loss) and an additional term representing the estimated treatment effect. This approach represents a reversal from conventional methodologies that typically bound the counterfactual loss.
2. **Integrate the Causal Bound into the DRL Framework**: To seamlessly adapt the derived causal bound into existing DRL algorithms. This involved defining causal inference concepts (treatment, outcome loss, factual/counterfactual losses, estimated treatment effect) within the DRL context (policies, accumulated rewards, temporal difference losses, OPE terms). The goal was for this integration to explicitly quantify and account for the estimated causal effect difference between the target policy and the behavior policy used to generate experiences.
3. **Enhance Sample Efficiency and Reduce Computational Demands**: To achieve these improvements by innovatively reusing existing data. The specific objective was to leverage previously discarded data, specifically past value network outputs (Q-values or V-values) that are computed during action selection, by storing them in the experience replay buffer. This stored information would then be utilized to compute the estimated treatment effect term, thereby enhancing the loss optimization process with causal insights at a negligible computational and storage cost.
4. **Demonstrate Broad Applicability and Performance Gains**: To empirically validate the effectiveness of the proposed method (named SUFT) across a diverse range of DRL agents and challenging environments. This involved evaluating its impact on popular off-policy agents (DQN, SAC) and on-policy agents (PPO) in both discrete (Atari 2600) and continuous (MuJoCo) control domains, assessing metrics such as reward ratio improvements, training convergence rates, and the ability to reduce experience replay buffer size.

By pursuing these objectives, the research aimed to provide a principled, computationally efficient, and broadly applicable method to improve the performance and accessibility of DRL algorithms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research involves a multi-stage approach encompassing theoretical development within causal inference, adaptation to the Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) framework, practical implementation strategies, and extensive empirical validation.

**Theoretical Foundation: SUFT Causal Bound**
The core of the methodology is a novel theoretical result derived from the Neyman-Rubin potential outcomes framework, a cornerstone of causal inference.
1. **Causal Framework Concepts**: The framework defines an observation space (X), an outcome space (Y), and a treatment space (T). In this work, a "treatment" is conceptualized as an agent's policy, where a 'target treatment' corresponds to the policy being optimized, and 'control treatments' correspond to various behavior policies that generated past experiences. The framework posits potential outcomes (Y_0, Y_1..., Y_N+1) depending on the assigned treatment (policy). The neural network hypothesis, denoted ϕ(x; θ_t), represents the model (e.g., Q-network) parameterized by weights θ_t for treatment t.
2. **Key Assumptions**: The framework assumes a joint distribution P(X, T, Y_0, Y_1..., Y_N+1) and relies on the standard strong ignorability condition, which asserts that potential outcomes are independent of treatment assignment given observed covariates (X), and that all treatments have a non-zero probability of assignment. This condition ensures that the estimated treatment effect is identifiable. Additionally, the methodology relies on a variation of the inverse triangle inequality, L(x, y) - L(x', y') ≤ L(x, x') + L(y, y'), which is proven to hold for L1 loss. While DRL typically uses L2 loss, empirical analysis was conducted to provide intuition for L2 performance.
3. **Loss Definitions in Causal Context**:
 - **Expected Outcome Loss (ℓ_ϕ(x, t))**: The expected loss between the true outcome and the neural network's prediction for a given observation x under treatment t.
 - **Expected Factual Outcome Loss (ϵ_F_ϕ)**: This refers to the loss when the treatment being optimized matches the treatment applied to generate the observations, analogous to an on-policy scenario.
 - **Expected Counterfactual Outcome Loss (ϵ_CF_ϕ)**: This refers to the loss when the treatment being optimized differs from the one used to generate the observations, analogous to an off-policy scenario.
 - **Estimated Treatment Effect Loss (ψ_ϕ)**: This term quantifies the discrepancy between the predictions of different treatment-specific models, e.g., L(ϕ(x; θ_behavior), ϕ(x; θ_target)).
4. **Novelty in Causal Bounding**: Unlike most prior works that bound the counterfactual loss, this research establishes a causal upper bound on the *factual* loss. This critical shift is motivated by the observation that in DRL, off-policy methods inherently work with counterfactual data, while factual (on-policy) data is typically unobserved. The theoretical result (Theorem 4.10 and B.19) states that the expected factual outcome loss (ϵ_F_ϕ) is bounded by the expected counterfactual outcome loss (ϵ_CF_ϕ), the estimated treatment effect loss (ψ_ϕ), and a constant term (δ) independent of the model parameters. This bound was extended from a binary treatment case to a multiple control treatment scenario, which is necessary to model the diverse experiences in a replay buffer.

**Adaptation to DRL Framework**
The causal bound is then seamlessly integrated into the DRL framework:
1. **Mapping Terms**:
 - The "factual loss" is directly mapped to the "on-policy loss" (ϵ_On-Policy_Q/V).
 - The "counterfactual loss" is mapped to the "off-policy loss" (ϵ_Off-Policy_Q/V).
 - The "estimated treatment effect loss" is mapped to the "SUFT Off-Policy Evaluation (OPE) term" (ψ_SUFT_Q/V). This term quantifies the value discrepancy between the behavior policy and the target policy using off-policy data.
2. **Optimization Objective**: The resulting DRL formulation becomes: ϵ_On-Policy_Q/V ≤ ϵ_Off-Policy_Q/V + ψ_SUFT_Q/V + δ. Since δ is independent of the network parameters (Q or V), it can be disregarded during gradient-based optimization. The final objective function optimized is the SUFT causal bound: ϵ_Off-Policy_Q/V + λ_TF * ψ_SUFT_Q/V, where λ_TF is a tunable coefficient.

**Recycling Value Network Outputs**
A key practical innovation is addressing the challenge of computing ψ_SUFT_Q/V, which requires access to the Q-values (or V-values) of *past behavior policies*. Storing the full network weights for every past behavior policy is impractical.
1. **Solution**: The methodology proposes storing the *output* of the value network (Q(s, a; θ_behavior) or V(s; θ_behavior)) in the experience replay buffer at the moment an action is selected. These values are normally computed for exploitation purposes and then discarded. By storing them, this previously "discarded" information becomes available for computing the SUFT OPE term during optimization.
2. **Experience Tuple Modification**: For Q-network agents like DQN, the experience tuple expands from (s, a, r, s') to (s, a, r, s', Q(s, a; θ_behavior)). For V-network agents like Actor-Critic architectures (e.g., PPO, SAC), it becomes (s, a, r, s', V(s; θ_behavior)).

**Universal Implementation and Experimental Setup**
The SUFT OPE term can be integrated into any DRL agent with a value network (Q-value or V-value) that influences its policy. For value-based methods (e.g., DQN), it's added to the standard loss. For Actor-Critic methods (e.g., SAC, PPO), it's incorporated into the critic's loss. PPO, despite being an on-policy algorithm, benefits due to the policy divergence that arises during its multiple epochs of optimization over the same rollout buffer.

**Empirical Validation**:
1. **Environments**: Experiments were conducted across 57 Atari 2600 games (discrete action space) and five MuJoCo continuous control environments.
2. **Agents**: Double DQN, Vanilla DQN, and standard DQN for Atari; PPO for both Atari and MuJoCo; and SAC for MuJoCo.
3. **Training Protocol**: Each agent-environment configuration was trained for 10 independent runs, with training limited to 400K steps (Atari) and 1M steps (MuJoCo). A relatively small experience replay buffer size (4K) was used to emphasize sample efficiency. Median smoothed training reward was used for performance analysis.
4. **Evaluation Metrics**: Performance was assessed using reward ratio improvements, percentage improvement, and statistical significance (t-tests).
5. **Ablation Studies**: Comparative experiments were performed, including comparing SUFT DQN against larger buffer baselines and against an inertia regularizer to ascertain that improvements stemmed from the causal bound optimization.
6. **Empirical L2 Validation**: An empirical analysis investigated the validity of the L1 loss assumption for L2 loss in practice, finding that the inequality holds in a significant majority of cases. The magnitude of the constant δ term was also empirically evaluated to confirm its insignificance during optimization.

This comprehensive methodology, ranging from foundational causal theory to practical implementation and rigorous empirical testing, aims to establish SUFT as a viable approach for enhancing DRL performance and efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of the SUFT method demonstrated consistent improvements across various Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents and domains, coupled with minimal computational overhead.

**Computational Efficiency:**
The computational and space overhead introduced by SUFT was observed to be negligible. For a Vanilla DQN agent, the additional computation for the SUFT OPE term constituted approximately 0.8% of the total training time. In terms of memory, storing a single 64-bit scalar (the old Q-value) per experience tuple in a 4K replay buffer resulted in an additional 0.003% space overhead. This indicates that SUFT enhances efficiency without introducing substantial resource demands.

**Performance Improvements Across Agents and Domains:**

- **Atari 2600 (L2 Loss):**
 - **DQN:** Agents incorporating SUFT achieved a mean reward ratio of 383.19% compared to the baseline DQN. SUFT outperformed the baseline in 52 out of 57 games, with statistically significant gains (p-value < 0.05) in 49 games. For valid games, SUFT surpassed the baseline in 28 out of 29 instances, with 48.3% showing over 100% improvement and 93.1% showing over 10% improvement.
 - **Double DQN:** SUFT-enhanced Double DQN achieved a mean reward ratio of 226.91%. It outperformed the baseline in 48 out of 57 games, with 37 showing statistically significant improvements. In valid games, SUFT surpassed the baseline in 35 out of 40 cases, with 20% exceeding 100% improvement and 72.5% exceeding 10% improvement.
 - **Vanilla DQN:** This agent also benefited, achieving a mean reward ratio of 228.12% relative to its baseline, outperforming it in 49 games with 33 statistically significant gains.
 - **PPO:** For PPO, SUFT yielded a mean reward ratio of 197.34%. It outperformed the baseline in 50 out of 57 games, with 40 statistically significant gains. For valid games, SUFT surpassed the baseline in 39 out of 42 cases, with 38.1% achieving over 100% improvement and 83.3% achieving over 10% improvement.
 - **Human-Level Performance**: In several Atari games, SUFT-enhanced agents not only surpassed their respective baselines but also achieved rewards exceeding those of an average human.

- **MuJoCo (L2 Loss):**
 - **SAC:** SUFT improved SAC performance, achieving a mean reward ratio of 224.53% and outperforming the baseline in 4 out of 5 environments, with 3 showing statistically significant gains.
 - **PPO:** For PPO in MuJoCo, SUFT resulted in a mean reward ratio of 111.21%, outperforming the baseline in 4 out of 5 environments (with one showing identical scores) and achieving statistically significant gains in 2 environments.

**Buffer Size Reduction:**
A notable finding was SUFT's capability to maintain high performance with significantly smaller replay buffers. SUFT DQN, utilizing a 4K buffer, achieved a mean reward ratio gain of 437.05% when compared to an identical baseline DQN agent employing a 100K buffer (25 times larger). This suggests the method's potential to facilitate high-performance DRL with substantially reduced memory requirements.

**Ablation Study:**
An ablation study on the DQN agent confirmed the distinct contribution of the SUFT OPE term. SUFT DQN achieved the highest Human Normalized Mean of 63.45%, outperforming a baseline DQN (25.04%), a DQN with a 100K buffer (29.29%), a Vanilla DQN (41.26%), and a Double DQN (42.31%). These results indicate that the performance gains from SUFT are not merely attributable to increased buffer size or more complex algorithm variants but stem from its specific mechanism.

**Causal Bound Optimization vs. Inertia Regularizer:**
To isolate the effect of the causal mechanism, a Double DQN agent with an inertia regularizer (a common technique to stabilize learning) was compared to SUFT Double DQN. The inertia regularizer *degraded* the Double DQN's performance to an 83.21% mean reward ratio, whereas SUFT led to a 226.91% improvement. This finding suggests that SUFT's benefits arise from a principled causal bound optimization that addresses the divergence between policies, rather than a general regularization effect.

**Empirical Validity of L2 Loss for Theorem 4.1:**
While the core theoretical assumption (Theorem 4.1) for the causal bound is proven for L1 loss, DRL agents commonly use L2 loss. Empirical analysis showed that the core inequality from Theorem 4.1 held for approximately 82% of synthetic random samples. Furthermore, a relevant inequality used in the proof (considering off-policy terms) held in approximately 79% of cases across the Atari 57 benchmark with a DQN agent. This empirical prevalence provides intuition for the observed strong performance when using L2 loss in experiments.

**δ Term Magnitude:**
Empirical analysis of the δ term, which is constant and ignored during optimization, indicated that its magnitude remained consistently an order of magnitude lower than the standard DQN loss throughout training. This supports the decision to omit the δ term, as it does not appear to significantly loosen the causal bound in practice.

In summary, the main findings establish that SUFT is a computationally inexpensive method that significantly enhances sample efficiency and convergence rates, leading to substantial reward improvements across a diverse range of DRL agents and environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents SUFT, a method that introduces a causal upper-bound loss optimization to Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), aiming to bridge the gap between on-policy and off-policy learning. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood through its theoretical contributions, methodological innovations, and empirical performance.

**Theoretical Significance:**
The work introduces a novel theoretical result by establishing a causal upper bound on the factual loss within the Neyman-Rubin potential outcomes framework. This is a departure from traditional causal inference applications that primarily focus on bounding the counterfactual loss. By adapting this framework to DRL, the paper formalizes how the on-policy loss can be bounded by the standard off-policy loss combined with an estimated treatment effect term. This theoretical link provides a new causal perspective on the fundamental divergence between on-policy and off-policy methods, offering a principled way to address it. The extension of the framework to multiple control treatments allows for more realistic modeling of experiences within a replay buffer.

**Methodological Innovation:**
A key methodological innovation is the approach to compute the estimated treatment effect. This typically requires knowing the value network outputs of past behavior policies, which is often impractical. The paper's solution involves "recycling" these past value network outputs by storing them in the experience replay buffer at the time of action selection. This approach transforms data that would usually be discarded into a valuable component for improving loss optimization with causal insights. This data reuse mechanism contributes to sample efficiency at a negligible computational and storage cost. The method's universal applicability to various DRL architectures that employ value networks (Q or V) further enhances its utility.

**Improved Sample Efficiency and Reduced Computational Demands:**
The empirical results consistently demonstrate that SUFT significantly enhances sample efficiency and reduces computational demands. Agents augmented with SUFT achieved substantial reward ratio improvements (up to 383% in DQN on Atari) and faster convergence rates compared to baselines. Crucially, the method enabled agents to achieve high performance with significantly smaller experience replay buffers (e.g., a 96% reduction in buffer size while outperforming agents with 25 times larger buffers by up to 437.05%). This indicates that SUFT can alleviate the need for vast experience buffers, which are a major source of DRL's high resource requirements. The measured computational and space overheads were marginal (less than 1%), suggesting a practical and efficient solution.

**Broad Applicability and Robustness:**
The method's effectiveness was demonstrated across a diverse range of DRL algorithms, including off-policy (DQN, SAC) and on-policy (PPO) agents, and across different control domains (discrete Atari games and continuous MuJoCo environments). This broad empirical validation suggests that the causal insights provided by the SUFT OPE term are generally beneficial for DRL agents struggling with policy divergence. The ablation studies confirmed that the performance gains are attributable to the causal bound optimization itself, rather than acting as a mere regularization effect or being surpassed by simpler alternatives like larger buffers.

**Potential Future Impact:**
The ability of SUFT to improve DRL performance while significantly reducing resource requirements holds substantial potential for broadening the applicability of DRL, particularly in scenarios with real-world constraints. For example, in physical robotics, where data collection is expensive and memory or computational resources might be limited, SUFT's reduced-resource approach could make DRL more practical and accessible. By enhancing sample efficiency, the method could accelerate research and development in DRL, allowing for more rapid iteration and exploration of complex problems. Furthermore, the integration of causal inference principles into DRL provides a framework for developing more robust and reliable agents by explicitly accounting for the cause-and-effect relationships tied to different policies. This could lead to DRL systems that are better at understanding the consequences of their actions and learning from diverse data sources.
