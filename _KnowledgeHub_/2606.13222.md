# Proprioceptive-visual correspondence enables self-other distinction in humanoid robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Eastern Institute of Technology, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and Peking University developed a self-supervised framework enabling humanoid robots to acquire self-other distinction and build a kinematics-free 3D self-model from proprioceptive-visual data in multi-agent scenes. This approach achieved over 99% accuracy in self-distinction and allowed the robot to successfully perform physical tasks such as target reaching and collision-aware motion planning.

## Problem

- Robots need to distinguish their own bodies from others for social intelligence (e.g., imitation, coordination) in multi-agent environments.
- Current self-modeling approaches often assume single-agent settings, creating a circular dependency where self-distinction is needed to build a self-model, but a self-model is needed for distinction.
- Robots lack the ability to learn self-other distinction and self-modeling from raw sensorimotor experience without predefined identity labels, kinematic models, or geometric descriptions.

## Method

- A two-stage, self-supervised framework leverages ==proprioceptive-visual correspondence== in multi-agent scenarios, operating solely on proprioceptive states and visual observations.
- A self-other distinction module identifies the robot's body among multiple agents by aligning proprioceptive state features with visual instance features via a two-level, attention-guided contrastive optimization.
- A kinematics-free implicit 3D self-modeling module then uses the robustly identified self-masks as pseudo-ground truth supervision to learn a differentiable representation of the robot's body's 3D occupancy and visibility.

## Results

- Achieved **99.69%** self-other distinction accuracy in real-world human-robot scenes and **99.50%** in simulated scenes with morphologically identical humanoid distractors, significantly outperforming VLM baselines.
- The learned 3D self-model produced coherent and accurate 3D occupancy predictions, with fidelity closely matching an oracle model trained with actual ground truth masks.
- Enabled the robot to perform downstream physical interaction tasks, including **88.0%** success in target reaching (mean distance **51.3 mm**), **71.4%** success in collision-aware motion planning, and human-to-robot motion retargeting with a mean per-keypoint error of **36.1 mm**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Proprioceptive-visual correspondence provides a sufficient self-supervised signal for robots to achieve robust self-other distinction and self-modeling in multi-agent settings, even with visually identical distractors.
> - Initial self-other distinction can break the circular dependency with self-modeling, providing the necessary pseudo-ground truth labels for learning a detailed 3D self-model.
> - Current advanced vision-language models do not effectively solve this cross-modal correspondence problem, highlighting that this task requires grounding in sensorimotor experience rather than purely semantic understanding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Distinguishing oneself from other agents is a fundamental aspect of social intelligence in biological systems. This capability is considered a prerequisite for complex social behaviors such as imitation, coordination, and collision avoidance in shared environments. Cognitive science research suggests that proprioceptive-visual correspondence plays a crucial role in human infants' ability to detect the contingency between their internal body signals and external visual feedback of their own movements, enabling self-other differentiation.

The increasing deployment of humanoid robots in social environments alongside humans and other robots necessitates that these artificial agents also acquire the ability to differentiate themselves from others. Prior research in robotics has explored self-modeling, where robots learn a predictive representation of their own body from proprioceptive-visual experience. However, much of this work has been conducted in single-agent settings, where the robot is assumed to be the sole active body, thereby bypassing the problem of identifying *which* body in a multi-agent scene belongs to the self. This creates a logical coupling: distinguishing self requires a notion of one's own body, yet learning about one's body presupposes knowing which body to attend to.

This paper addresses this challenge by extending the concept of self-modeling to multi-agent scenarios and resolving the aforementioned circularity. Specifically, it investigates whether a robot can learn self-other distinction and self-modeling from raw proprioceptive signals and visual observations, without any predefined identity labels, kinematic models, or geometric descriptions of its own morphology. The approach draws inspiration from developmental psychology, where perceptual self-other distinction in human children precedes the maturation of detailed body representations. By demonstrating a self-supervised method for self-other distinction that subsequently bootstraps self-modeling, this work aims to provide a foundational capability for robots operating in complex social settings, moving beyond the limitations of pre-programmed knowledge or reliance on explicit external cues for self-identification. The paper also contrasts its approach with large vision-language models (VLMs), indicating that current VLMs do not effectively solve this cross-modal correspondence problem.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a humanoid robot to achieve self-other distinction and learn a predictive three-dimensional (3D) self-model in multi-agent environments. This capability is to be acquired solely from proprioceptive states and visual observations, without relying on any prior knowledge such as identity labels, kinematic models, or geometric specifications of the robot's body.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and observations:

- **Necessity for Social Intelligence:** As humanoid robots become more integrated into human workspaces and social settings, the ability to distinguish their own body from others becomes a prerequisite for effective and safe interaction. Without this, robots cannot reliably perform tasks like imitating a demonstrator, coordinating with a partner, or avoiding collisions with bystanders. This addresses the fundamental question: "which body is mine?"
- **Addressing the Self-Other/Self-Modeling Coupling:** Existing research has shown that robots can learn self-models in single-agent environments. However, in multi-agent scenes, the robot faces a dilemma: it needs a self-model to distinguish itself from others, but it needs to distinguish itself to learn a self-model. This paper aims to break this circular dependency by establishing self-other distinction first, which then provides the necessary supervision for self-modeling.
- **Mimicking Biological Development:** The research is motivated by findings in developmental psychology, which indicate that human infants acquire perceptual self-other differentiation before developing detailed body representations. The proposed framework follows this progression, allowing robots to first visually identify themselves and then construct a predictive self-model based on that distinction.
- **Learning from Raw Sensorimotor Experience:** A central motivation is to demonstrate whether proprioceptive-visual correspondence alone, without any engineered priors (e.g., CAD models, URDF files), is sufficient for grounding both self-other distinction and self-modeling in robots. This aligns with neurocognitive accounts of body ownership in humans, where the sense of self arises from moment-to-moment congruence between internal (proprioceptive) and external (visual) signals.
- **Enabling Downstream Robotic Tasks:** The ultimate motivation is to equip robots with a functional bodily self-representation that can support practical interaction tasks in the physical world. This includes applications such as target reaching, collision-aware motion planning, and human-to-robot motion retargeting, which require precise spatial reasoning about the robot's own body.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to outline a pathway for robots to autonomously acquire fundamental aspects of bodily self-representation from experience, thereby enhancing their capacity to act and coordinate effectively in shared physical environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework enables a humanoid robot to achieve self-other distinction and learn a kinematics-free 3D self-model through a two-stage, self-supervised process, operating solely on proprioceptive states and visual observations. Experiments were conducted using a 29-degree-of-freedom (DoF) Unitree G1 humanoid robot observed by a fixed external RGB camera. Both simulated and real-world multi-agent scenes were utilized for evaluation.

**Data Collection and Input Preparation:**
Real-world data consisted of 25,667 frames of the Unitree G1 robot interacting with a human distractor. Simulated data comprised 9,000 frames of two morphologically identical humanoid robots generated in Isaac Gym. Each RGB frame was paired with the robot's proprioceptive state, which includes joint configuration (29-DoF), root orientation (quaternion), and root translation. Candidate body masks were extracted from RGB images using a class-agnostic segmentation model (Segment Anything Model 3).

**Self-Other Distinction Module:**
This module addresses the problem of identifying the robot's own body among multiple visible agents.
- **Proprioceptive State for Distinction:** For self-other distinction, the robot's global root translation was excluded to focus on body configuration, using a 30-dimensional state (`θ_t = [q_t, ψ_t]`) comprising 29 joint angles and global yaw. This state was encoded into an L2-normalized feature (`f_state`) via a proprioceptive state encoder.
- **Visual Instance Encoding:** Two candidate masks (one for the ego robot, one for a distractor) were cropped, position-normalized, and scale-normalized before being processed independently by a shared custom CNN image encoder. This yielded L2-normalized instance features (`f_image,k`).
- **Two-Level Self-Supervised Optimization:**
 - **Inner Level (Latent Self Selection):** Feature similarities (`s_k = f_state^T * f_image,k`) between the proprioceptive state and each visual candidate were computed. These similarities were converted into attention weights (`α_k`) using a softmax function with a temperature parameter (`τ_a`). A frame-level visual representation, termed the "bag feature" (`f_bag`), was formed as a weighted sum of the candidate visual features, guided by the attention weights. This mechanism implicitly selects the visual instance most consistent with the robot's proprioceptive state.
 - **Outer Level (Frame-wise Contrastive Alignment):** An InfoNCE objective was applied to train the encoders. For an anchor proprioceptive state, the `f_bag` from the same time step served as the positive visual representation. Bag features from other frames in the training batch were treated as negative samples. This temporal contrast rewarded models that brought the selected self-bag closer to the current proprioceptive state than to bags from different time steps, allowing instance-level self-labels to emerge from frame-level proprioceptive-visual correspondence.

**Kinematics-Free Implicit Self-Modeling Module:**
Once self-other distinction identified the robot's own body, the selected self-masks served as pseudo-ground truth supervision for learning a 3D self-model.
- **Pose Normalization:** To mitigate global drift in real-world data, the full 36-dimensional robot state (`S_t = [q_t, r_t, p_t]`) was transformed into a local coordinate system relative to the first frame of each sequence (`~S_t`).
- **Pose-Conditioned Coordinates:** Sampled 3D points along camera rays were transformed into a robot-centered canonical space using the relative root pose, disentangling global motion from articulated body shape.
- **Visibility-based Implicit Self-Model:** The core of the self-model was an implicit function `F_ϕ` that mapped a canonical 3D query point, viewing direction, and the normalized robot state to volume density (`σ_k`) and view-dependent visibility (`v_k`).
- **Part-Aware Pose Encoder:** The 29 joint angles were partitioned into torso and bilateral limb groups. Each group was processed by a part-specific MLP, and features were fused into a global posture representation, providing a structured inductive bias without an explicit kinematic model.
- **Bounded Volumetric Mask Rendering:** The model predicted a 2D foreground mask by adapting NeRF-style alpha compositing. A key modification was the use of a bounded terminal interval for ray sampling, ensuring that occupancy was explained within the calibrated robot body volume and avoiding spurious opacity from background elements. The main reconstruction loss was an MSE objective between the predicted mask and the pseudo-GT self-mask.

**Downstream Spatial Reasoning with the Learned Self-Model:**
The learned self-model provides a differentiable mapping from joint configurations to 3D body occupancy, enabling a neural inverse-kinematics approach for various tasks.
- **Target Reaching:** A dedicated left-hand self-model was trained. The robot optimized its seven left-arm joint angles to minimize the distance between the density-weighted center of its learned hand occupancy and a given target point.
- **Collision-Aware Motion Planning:** The full-body and left-hand occupancy models were integrated into a target-biased Rapidly-exploring Random Tree (RRT) planner. The full-body model checked for collisions with obstacles (e.g., a board with an aperture), while the hand model identified the target region.
- **Human-to-Robot Motion Retargeting:** The self-model was extended with multiple part-specific output heads. 3D human keypoints, extracted from monocular video using a human pose estimator, were transformed into robot-compatible spatial targets by preserving human segment directions and rescaling lengths to the robot's dimensions. The robot's 29-DoF configuration was then optimized to minimize the mean squared error between the density-weighted centers of its predicted body parts and the retargeted targets.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented a series of evaluations addressing the three core questions: self-other distinction, self-modeling, and application to physical interaction tasks.

**Self-Other Distinction from Proprioception:**
The system demonstrated robust performance in distinguishing the robot's own body from others.
- In real-world human-robot scenes (25,667 frames), the model achieved 99.69% accuracy.
- In simulated two-humanoid scenes with morphologically identical distractors (9,000 frames), accuracy was 99.50%.
- These results significantly surpassed zero-shot vision-language model (VLM) baselines (e.g., LLaVA v1.6, InstructBLIP, GPT-5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview), which performed at or slightly above chance (e.g., 43.05% for GPT-5.5 in human-robot scenes). This suggests that current VLMs do not effectively bind specific joint configurations to corresponding visible bodies.
- A "direct fusion" variant, which averaged per-mask similarity scores rather than using attention, also showed marked degradation (75.09% in human-robot scenes), indicating the effectiveness of the attention-guided fusion mechanism.
- Analysis of feature similarities demonstrated that after training, the proprioceptive feature remained consistently closer to the self-mask than to the distractor's mask, even when distractors were visually identical. Synchronous proprioceptive-visual similarities were sharply higher than asynchronous ones, and t-SNE projections showed a clear alignment of same-frame proprioceptive and robot mask embeddings post-training, indicating a specific binding of the current proprioceptive state to the robot's configuration.

**Self-other Distinction Enables Self-Modeling:**
The pseudo-ground truth masks generated by the self-other distinction module were effectively used to train a kinematics-free 3D self-model.
- The learned self-model produced coherent 3D occupancy predictions in both simulation and the real world. The generated point clouds accurately tracked the robot's body across various poses and orientations while consistently excluding human distractors.
- The fidelity of the self-model, when trained with pseudo-GT masks (derived from >99.5% accurate self-other distinction), closely matched an oracle model trained with actual ground truth masks.
- An ablation study systematically degrading the accuracy of pseudo-GT masks revealed a direct correlation with self-model fidelity. As accuracy decreased, the predicted body shape deteriorated, leading to "ghost occupancy" around distractors and eventual collapse of the self-other boundary. Quantitative metrics (IoU, MSE, MAE, Chamfer Distance) confirmed this progressive degradation. For instance, IoU dropped from 0.902 with 100% correct masks to 0.107 with 50% correct masks.

**From Self-Model to Physical Interaction:**
The differentiable nature of the learned self-model enabled the robot to perform various physical interaction tasks.
- **Target Reaching:** The robot successfully reached a tabletop night-light in 88.0% of trials (44 out of 50), with a mean best distance of 51.3 mm, satisfying the 100 mm activation threshold. This demonstrated the self-model's ability to guide precise control.
- **Collision-Aware Motion Planning:** In a more complex scenario involving reaching a target through a circular aperture in a board, the robot achieved a 71.4% success rate (10 out of 14 constrained settings). The mean final distance to the target was 89.3 mm, and the average path length was 155.4 waypoints, indicating the self-model's utility for spatial reasoning and collision avoidance.
- **Human-to-Robot Motion Retargeting:** The system successfully retargeted whole-body human demonstrations to the robot. The optimized robot motion sequences showed a mean per-keypoint error of 36.1 mm across 50 poses, which is approximately 2.7% of the robot's body height. This was achieved without any paired human-robot training data, demonstrating the flexibility of the learned geometric representation.

These results collectively indicate that proprioceptive-visual correspondence can serve as a sufficient basis for self-other distinction and self-modeling in humanoid robots, enabling them to acquire knowledge of their own bodies and perform physical tasks in shared environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several contributions to the fields of robotics and artificial intelligence, particularly in the domain of social robotics and embodied cognition.

**Fundamental Advancement in Robot Self-Perception:** The work addresses a foundational challenge in artificial intelligence: enabling robots to differentiate themselves from others in multi-agent scenes without pre-programmed identity. This capability is presented as a prerequisite for higher-level social cognition in robots, such as imitation, coordination, and theory of mind, which often assume a predetermined self-other boundary.

**Resolution of the Self-Other/Self-Modeling Circularity:** The paper demonstrates a mechanism to break the logical circularity between self-other distinction and self-modeling. By showing that proprioceptive-visual correspondence alone allows for initial self-other distinction, which then provides the necessary self-supervised signal for building a detailed 3D self-model, the research offers a plausible developmental pathway for robots, mirroring observations in human infants. This suggests that bodily self-representation can emerge from experience rather than requiring engineering or a priori specification.

**Self-Supervised Learning from Sensorimotor Data:** The framework's ability to learn robust self-other distinction and a functional 3D self-model purely from self-supervised signals (temporal co-occurrence of proprioceptive and visual data) is significant. This eliminates the need for expensive and often complex manual labeling, explicit kinematic models, or geometric CAD data, thereby reducing the engineering overhead for deploying robots in diverse environments.

**Beyond Semantic Recognition:** The comparative analysis with advanced Vision-Language Models (VLMs) highlights that self-other distinction in this context is not a purely semantic object recognition problem. The failure of VLMs to bind internal joint states to visible bodies underscores that this task requires learning cross-modal correspondences from sensorimotor experience, aligning with neurocognitive accounts of body ownership.

**Enabling Advanced Physical Interaction and Control:** The learned differentiable self-model is shown to be functionally valuable for guiding complex physical interactions. Its successful application to target reaching, collision-aware motion planning, and human-to-robot motion retargeting demonstrates that the robot can use this internal representation for precise control and spatial reasoning within its environment, without relying on traditional kinematic controllers.

**Potential for Broader Applicability and Future Research:**
- The underlying principle of proprioceptive-visual correspondence is morphology-agnostic, suggesting that the framework could be extended to other types of articulated robots beyond humanoids.
- Future work could integrate the upstream segmentation process into an end-to-end learning framework, reducing dependence on external components.
- Scaling to denser social scenes with a larger number of distractors represents a natural progression.
- A notable limitation is that the current self-model is geometric rather than physical. Extending it to incorporate physical reasoning (e.g., gravity, contact dynamics) would enable more advanced behaviors like unassisted standing balance during motion retargeting, a complex problem that typically relies on manually provided dynamic models.
- The establishment of this fundamental self-other distinction provides a necessary foundation upon which higher-level social competences in robots can be built and progressively acquired from interaction, potentially leading to more adaptable and socially intelligent robotic systems.

In conclusion, this research outlines a compelling route for robots to autonomously acquire a sense of their own bodies through experience, facilitating their integration and effective operation in shared physical and social environments.
