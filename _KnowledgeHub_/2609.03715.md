# MINERVA: How Small Can a Manipulation Policy Be and Still Solve LIBERO?

> [!summary] Summary
> MINERVA is a family of deliberately minimal visuomotor manipulation policies that replaces language encoders with a 40-entry task-ID embedding, uses a from-scratch CNN with spatial-softmax keypoints, and an MLP-mixer action-chunk head. Scaled down until the benchmark breaks, a 0.54M-parameter model reaches 95.1% average success on standard LIBERO, 2.4 points below a model 7,700x larger, while replanning in 5-9 ms on a laptop CPU.

## Problem

- Billion-parameter VLA models dominate LIBERO, but nobody has measured how much capacity the benchmark actually demands, leaving deployment cost unjustified.
- Efficient-VLA work only trims existing backbones, retaining language pathways and pretrained components, and none descends below roughly 0.2B parameters.
- Single-run ablations on LIBERO report roughly one-point deltas without calibrating training-seed variance, so many published design conclusions may be noise.

## Method

- **MINERVA** architecture: shared from-scratch ==depthwise-separable CNN== with ==FiLM== task modulation and ==spatial-softmax keypoints== over two views, plus a 40-entry task-ID embedding replacing any language encoder.
- Action head is a 4-layer ==DiT== with self-attention over 16 action tokens swapped for a ==token-mixing MLP== and shared ==AdaLN-Zero==, trained by ==conditional flow matching== or direct ==L1 regression==.
- Smaller members are trained by ==velocity distillation== from a larger teacher; inference uses ==ACT-style temporal ensembling== with per-step replanning and mode-seeking initial-noise temperature 0.85.

## Results

- The **0.54M** model scores **95.05%** (**95.75%** with the L1 head) over **2,000** rollouts; success saturates near **1M** (**96.75%**) and collapses to **88.6%** at **0.24M** and **68.3%** at **0.09M**.
- Across three seeds only chunk length (**-3.16** at chunk **8**, **-2.20** at chunk **32**) and vision starvation (**-1.41**) exceed the **+/-1**-point seed band; flow matching versus L1 regression is **+0.34**.
- Permuting task IDs collapses success from **96.75%** to **6.5%**; LIBERO-Plus perturbations drop models to **46-56%**, with lighting robustness at most **6.5%** at every scale.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Benchmark scores near saturation can be met by task-indexed memorization at half a million parameters, so gains above roughly 97% likely measure something other than capability.
> - For tiny visuomotor policies, parameters pay in the perception encoder, not the action head, and one-pass regression matches generative flow matching at up to 3.8x lower latency.
> - Always calibrate a benchmark's training-seed band before trusting ablation deltas; sub-one-point single-run improvements are indistinguishable from seed noise.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen rapid advancements with the advent of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which leverage large pre-trained vision-language backbones and typically contain billions of parameters. Models such as OpenVLA (7B), π0 (3-4B), and π0.5 (3-4B) have demonstrated high success rates on benchmarks like LIBERO. This trend, however, is associated with substantial computational costs for training and inference, requiring multi-GPU setups and large VRAM.

Prior work has focused on making VLAs more efficient while largely retaining their vision-language backbones and capabilities. Examples include TinyVLA (sub-1B VLM), MiniVLA (1B VLM), DeeR-VLA (dynamic early-exit inference), FAST (action token stream compression), SmolVLA (450M), and TurboVLA (0.2B, removing language model from control pathway). These efforts generally aim to reduce the inference cost while preserving open-vocabulary or generalization capabilities. Even the most efficient of these models, however, maintain parameter counts in the hundreds of millions.

This paper distinguishes itself by addressing a complementary question: *how much capacity does a fixed task set actually demand?* It posits that for a deployed robot with a fixed set of tasks, the critical metric is the minimal sufficient capacity required to solve that set, rather than the generalization capabilities afforded by larger models. This perspective is rooted in the concept of deployment-efficient reinforcement learning, which prioritizes constraints binding at deployment.

The work also builds upon observations from studies like LIBERO-PRO, which suggest that standard LIBERO evaluation largely functions as a memorization test, where language instructions primarily disambiguate among known tasks. By designing a policy explicitly for this memorization context, the authors aim to establish an empirical capacity floor. In doing so, MINERVA extends previous work on small visuomotor policies, such as ACT (80M transformer) and Diffusion Policy, by pushing parameter counts to significantly lower magnitudes (e.g., sub-1M) while maintaining multi-task capabilities across 40 tasks. The study thus contributes to understanding the intrinsic complexity of a benchmark under specific conditions, providing a quantitative measure for task-specific capacity requirements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to empirically determine the minimum parameter count a manipulation policy can possess while still effectively solving the fixed task set of the standard LIBERO benchmark. The authors term this the "empirical capacity floor" for the given task setting.

The motivation for this investigation is multifaceted:

1. **Deployment Efficiency:** Current state-of-the-art VLA models for robotic manipulation are computationally intensive, often requiring multi-gigabyte weights and significant VRAM, making them unsuitable for deployment on resource-constrained edge devices or systems without dedicated GPUs. From a practical perspective, once a robot's operational task set is defined and fixed, the ability to execute these tasks using the smallest possible policy directly translates to reduced deployment costs, lower power consumption, and faster inference times. This aligns with the "deployment-efficiency" paradigm, which emphasizes optimizing for constraints relevant during deployment rather than solely during model development.

2. **Challenging Assumptions about Benchmark Demands:** The prevalence of large VLA models in achieving high scores on LIBERO might lead to an assumption that such high capacity is inherently necessary for the benchmark. This research aims to test whether the LIBERO benchmark genuinely demands such large models, especially in light of analyses suggesting its standard evaluation protocol is primarily a test of memorization. If the benchmark primarily assesses memorization, then a much smaller, purpose-built policy might suffice, thereby exposing the true capacity demands of the task set itself, rather than the capacity required for generalist pretraining or open-world generalization.

3. **Guiding Policy Construction and Distillation:** By identifying an empirical capacity floor, the research seeks to provide guidance for future policy development. Knowing this floor can inform whether to construct minimal policies directly or to distill larger, generalist models down to a size suitable for specific deployment contexts. This could lead to more tailored and efficient robotic systems.

4. **Investigating Architectural and Training Choices:** A secondary objective is to explore how architectural decisions, training methodologies, and inference strategies influence performance at these minimal capacities. The relatively small parameter count of the proposed policies makes extensive exploration of the design space feasible. This allows for ablations to identify which components are genuinely critical for performance on the benchmark and which contribute to overhead without significant benefit for a fixed task set.

In essence, the study is motivated by a practical need for efficient robot control and a scientific curiosity about the true capacity requirements of a widely used benchmark when generalization capabilities are not the primary focus.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The MINERVA (MINimal Efficient Robotic Vision-Action policy) project adopts a design principle of including "nothing the benchmark does not demand." This approach is based on the observation that standard LIBERO evaluation, which uses fixed scenes, tasks, and instructions for both training and evaluation, can be largely solved via memorization. Consequently, the language pathway typically found in VLA models is replaced with a 40-entry learned task-ID embedding, directly mapping to the 40 known tasks. The entire parameter budget is then allocated to visual state estimation and closed-loop action generation.

**A. Architecture:**
The MINERVA policy family consists of a perception module and an action head.

- **Perception:**
 - **Input:** Two camera views (agent-view and wrist), initially 256x256 pixels, resized to 144x144, and then random-cropped to 128x128 during training (center-cropped during evaluation).
 - **Encoder:** A shared, from-scratch Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). It comprises four stride-2 stages of depthwise-separable residual blocks, a design choice inspired by MobileNets for efficiency.
 - **Conditioning:** The CNN features are FiLM-modulated by the learned task embedding, integrating task-specific information early in the visual processing stream.
 - **Feature Extraction:** Spatial-softmax keypoint extraction is applied after the CNN stages, projecting visual information into a set of keypoints.
 - **Proprioception:** An 8-dimensional proprioceptive state is encoded by a two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).
 - **Conditioning Vector:** The final conditioning vector `c` is formed by concatenating the per-view features, proprioceptive state features, and the task embedding over `n_obs=2` observation steps, as using two steps showed improved performance for long-horizon tasks.

- **Action Head:**
 - **Generation:** Actions are generated as chunks `a = a_{1:H}` with `H=16` (default chunk length) using conditional flow matching.
 - **Training Objective:** The model is trained to regress a velocity field `v_theta` towards `(a - epsilon)`, where `a_tau = tau * a + (1 - tau) * epsilon` is an interpolant formed from a drawn noise `epsilon` and a noise level `tau`. The loss function is `L_fm = E_{a,epsilon,tau} ||v_theta(a_tau, tau, c) - (a - epsilon)||^2_2`. `tau` is sampled from a Beta distribution tilted towards high noise.
 - **Architecture:** The action head is a 4-layer Diffusion Transformer (DiT)-like architecture. To minimize parameters, self-attention over the 16 action tokens is replaced by a token-mixing MLP, and the AdaLN-Zero modulation MLP is shared across blocks.
 - **Alternative:** A direct L1 regression head was also tested, which produces the action chunk in a single forward pass instead of iterative integration.
 - **Auxiliary Head:** A small (less than 9k parameters) auxiliary head predicts episode progress during training, but is removed during inference.

- **Model Family and Scaling:**
 - The study defines a family of MINERVA models with varying parameter counts: MINERVA-0.5M (0.54M parameters), MINERVA-1M (0.99M parameters), MINERVA-5M (4.89M parameters), and MINERVA-10M (9.66M parameters). These models differ in CNN widths, number of keypoints, and action head width/architecture (e.g., 5M and 10M models reintroduce full-width CNNs and self-attention).
 - **Velocity Distillation:** Smaller models are trained using velocity distillation from a larger teacher model. The teacher's prediction `v_phi` is added as a second regression target to the loss: `L = L_fm + lambda * ||v_theta - v_phi||^2_2` with `lambda=1`. The teacher is excluded from parameter counts and not used during inference.

**B. Inference-time Strategy:**
To maximize success, the policy replans at every control step and employs ACT-style temporal ensembling. This involves averaging overlapping chunk predictions, with executed actions being an exponentially weighted average (decay 0.01 per step of age). For sub-1M models, mode-seeking sampling is applied by scaling the initial noise of the flow integration with `sigma = 0.85 < 1`. This strategy was compared against BID-style candidate selection and RTC-style soft inpainting.

**C. Experimental Setup:**
- **Data:** All models were trained on the `lerobot/libero` dataset, consisting of 1,693 demonstrations and 273,000 frames across 40 tasks from the four standard LIBERO suites.
- **Training:** Training typically ran for 90-120k steps with a batch size of 128, using the AdamW optimizer (learning rate 10^-4, cosine decay). Long-suite episodes were upweighted by 3x for sub-1M models.
- **Evaluation:** Standard LIBERO results are based on 2,000 rollouts (50 episodes per task, 10 tasks per suite, 4 suites) with fixed seeds and hard environment resets. Ablation studies used 25 episodes per task for screening, reconfirmed at 50 episodes.
- **Ablations:** Key architectural and training choices (action-chunk length, vision-action capacity allocation, generative vs. regression objectives, action-token mixing) were ablated. Robustness of findings was assessed by re-training key configurations with three different random seeds.

**D. Probes for Task Understanding and Robustness:**
- **Permutation Probe:** To causally test the role of task conditioning, the task-ID mapping was intentionally rewritten during evaluation of a fixed model, observing the impact on success rates.
- **LIBERO-90 Extension:** The 1M architecture was trained on a larger dataset (LIBERO-90, 90 tasks, 3,959 demonstrations) to assess scalability to more tasks within a closed set.
- **LIBERO-Plus Audit:** Models were evaluated on LIBERO-Plus, a benchmark with 10,030 perturbed variants across 7 factors (e.g., new objects, sensor noise, light, camera changes) to assess robustness beyond standard memorization scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the capacity demands of the LIBERO benchmark and the design of efficient visuomotor policies.

**A. Headline Performance and Capacity Floor:**
- A MINERVA policy with 0.54 million parameters achieved an average success rate of 95.05% across the four standard LIBERO suites. This result is 2.4 percentage points below the reported 97.5% from the 4.1 billion parameter LeRobot π0.5 model, which is approximately 7,700 times larger.
- The 0.99 million parameter MINERVA model achieved 96.75% success, closing the gap to 0.75 percentage points.
- When re-trained with a direct L1 regression head (rather than flow matching), the 0.54M architecture scored 95.75%, surpassing its flow matching counterpart. This regression variant is the configuration shipped.

**B. Parameter Scaling and Performance Saturation:**
- Performance on the LIBERO benchmark saturated near 1 million parameters. Increasing the model size from 1M to 10M parameters only resulted in a marginal average success gain of 0.7 percentage points (from 96.75% to 97.45%).
- Below 0.54M parameters, performance declined sharply, with an average success rate of 88.6% at 0.24M parameters and 68.3% at 0.09M parameters.
- The collapse in performance was primarily driven by failures in the long-horizon suite, which exhibited a more pronounced drop (e.g., 73.0% at 0.24M vs. 95.2% for Spatial suite at the same size). This suggests that the long-horizon tasks are where LIBERO genuinely requires more capacity for chaining subgoals.

**C. Robustness of Architectural Choices (Seed Averaging):**
- **Training Seed Variability:** The average success rate across three training seeds for the 1M baseline model showed a ±1 percentage point variation (ranging from 94.60% to 96.75%), indicating that small single-run deltas can be within the inherent noise of training.
- **Action-Chunk Length:** This was one of the two factors that consistently survived the seed band.
 - A chunk length of 8 steps resulted in a significant average drop of -3.16 points, primarily breaking the Goal suite (86-89% success).
 - A chunk length of 32 steps caused an average drop of -2.20 points, mainly impacting the long-horizon suite (80-82% success). The default chunk length of 16 steps was found to be a balance, avoiding significant degradation in either suite.
- **Vision Allocation:** Reducing vision capacity by shrinking the CNN encoder (from 0.49M to 0.13M parameters while keeping total parameters constant) led to a replicated average performance drop of -1.41 points across three seeds, with substantial damage to the long suite (-4.6 points).
- **Flow Matching vs. L1 Regression:** Across three seeds, flow matching showed no detectable advantage over direct L1 regression (a +0.34 point difference in favor of regression, well within the seed band). Given that L1 regression requires only one forward pass compared to ten Euler steps for flow matching, it offers up to 3.8x faster inference. Consequently, the regression variant is the default choice for the shipped policies.
- **Other Factors:** Single-run ablations indicated that conditioning styles (AdaLN-Zero, concatenation, FiLM), distillation at the 1M scale, and replacing the MLP mixer with self-attention in the action head (which increased parameter count by 26%) had performance differences within the ±1-point seed band, suggesting they were not robustly impactful choices for this benchmark. The MLP mixer was preferred for its parameter efficiency.

**D. Optimal Budget Allocation:**
- For a fixed parameter budget around 1M, allocating 50-80% of parameters to the vision encoder consistently resulted in high performance (96.3-96.8% success). Starving the vision encoder consistently led to performance degradation, demonstrating that visual capacity, rather than action head capacity, was the more critical factor for achieving high success rates.

**E. Inference-time Strategy:**
- Replanning every step combined with ACT-style temporal ensembling significantly outperformed other chunking strategies, including BID-style candidate selection and RTC-style soft inpainting.
- Mode-seeking sampling (initial noise temperature 0.85) consistently improved performance for sub-1M models (adding +0.2 to +2.25 points), especially for weaker models, but offered no benefit for models at 1M parameters and above.

**F. Computational Efficiency:**
- The 0.54M MINERVA model with the L1 regression head achieved a replan time of approximately 5 ms per chunk on eight laptop CPU threads, without a GPU. This is 113 times faster than SmolVLA and 1,400 times faster than π0.5, making real-time control loops (e.g., 100 Hz) feasible on embedded CPUs.

**G. Causal Role of Task Conditioning:**
- A permutation probe demonstrated the causal role of task-ID conditioning. When the task-ID mapping was rewritten for a fixed 1M model, average success collapsed from 96.75% to 6.5%. Forcing all tasks to the suite's task-0 ID resulted in 16.2% success. This confirmed that task identity is primarily conveyed through the conditioning channel, with visual context providing some disambiguation in specific cases (e.g., Object suite).

**H. Scalability to More Tasks (LIBERO-90):**
- The 1M architecture trained on LIBERO-90 (89 tasks) achieved 94.6% average success. This indicated that the recipe can scale to a larger number of tasks within a closed set without requiring a language encoder or pre-trained vision.

**I. Robustness under Perturbations (LIBERO-Plus):**
- When evaluated on LIBERO-Plus, which introduces various perturbations, the MINERVA models' average success rates dropped substantially (46.7% for 0.54M, 46.0% for 0.99M, 55.7% for 4.89M), contrasting with their high standard LIBERO scores.
- Robustness was not capacity-limited below 1M parameters, but the 4.89M model showed a +10 point improvement in robustness over smaller models, primarily on semantic and geometric perturbations (e.g., new objects).
- Photometric robustness (e.g., changes in lighting) remained near zero across all tested scales (≤ 6.5% success), quantifying the memorization aspect of the models and highlighting a significant limitation of the from-scratch CNN encoder.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several significant implications for the understanding, design, and deployment of robotic manipulation policies:

1. **Quantification of Benchmark Demands:** The study provides an empirical measurement of the capacity floor for the standard LIBERO benchmark, establishing that approximately 0.5 million parameters are sufficient to achieve near state-of-the-art performance (95.1%) and around 1 million parameters for higher long-horizon headroom. This quantitatively supports previous qualitative claims that standard LIBERO evaluation is largely a memorization test, rather than a measure of open-world generalization or complex language understanding.

2. **Redefining "State-of-the-Art" Context:** By demonstrating that highly competitive scores on standard LIBERO can be achieved with significantly reduced model sizes and without language encoders or pre-trained vision, the work prompts a re-evaluation of what high scores on this specific benchmark signify. It suggests that performance gains above approximately 97% on standard LIBERO may not reflect advancements in the capabilities that large generalist models are designed for, such as open-vocabulary perception or transfer to unseen tasks. This highlights the importance of perturbation-based benchmarks like LIBERO-PRO for evaluating true generalization.

3. **Enabling Deployment-Efficient Robotics:** MINERVA demonstrates the feasibility of extremely lightweight visuomotor policies capable of high-frequency control (e.g., ~5 ms per action chunk on a laptop CPU, enabling 100 Hz control loops). This directly addresses a critical barrier to real-world robot deployment: computational cost and resource constraints. Such efficient policies can be integrated into edge devices or existing robotic systems without dedicated GPUs, significantly reducing hardware requirements, power consumption, and latency. This aligns with the principles of deployment-efficient reinforcement learning.

4. **Guiding Policy Design and Distillation:** The detailed ablation studies provide practical guidance for designing efficient policies. Key findings, such as the critical importance of vision capacity over action head capacity, the comparable performance and superior efficiency of direct L1 regression over flow matching, and the optimal action-chunk length, offer concrete recommendations for building or distilling policies to meet specific capacity targets. The findings encourage focusing parameter allocation where it demonstrably yields returns.

5. **Opening New Research Avenues:** The concept of an "empirical capacity floor" for specific task sets opens a research program akin to information-theoretic task complexity measures in reinforcement learning. Future work can extend this methodology to measure capacity floors across different environments, sensing modalities, embodiments, and task complexities (e.g., robust manipulation under perturbations), providing a more nuanced understanding of what various robotic tasks truly demand.

6. **Open-Source Contribution:** The release of code, recipes, and checkpoints facilitates further research, replication, and practical application of these efficient policies by the wider robotics and machine learning communities.
