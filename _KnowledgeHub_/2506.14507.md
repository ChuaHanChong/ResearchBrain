# Can Pretrained Vision-Language Embeddings Alone Guide Robot Navigation?

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Iowa State University investigated if frozen, pretrained vision-language model (VLM) embeddings, without additional fine-tuning or specialized modules, can guide robot navigation, achieving a 74% success rate in language-guided tasks but requiring 3.2 times more timesteps than an expert.

## Problem

- Traditional language-guided robot navigation relies on extensive task-specific training data and specialized architectural designs, limiting generalization to novel commands or environments.
- Existing integrations of large vision-language models (VLMs) into robotic systems typically involve fine-tuning on action data, complex architectures, or supplementary perception modules.
- A fundamental question remains whether the inherent semantic representations from frozen VLMs are sufficient for basic navigation, or if specialized architectures and fine-tuning are consistently indispensable.

## Method

- A minimalist framework was developed, utilizing a frozen, pretrained VLM (SigLIP) as the sole feature extractor to generate joint vision-language embeddings.
- A simple feedforward neural network policy was trained via behavioral cloning on expert demonstrations, mapping these VLM embeddings directly to robot wheel velocity commands.
- The approach explicitly avoided VLM fine-tuning, specialized spatial reasoning capabilities, and mapping or exploration modules, operating reactively based on single-frame vision-language embeddings.

## Results

- The learned policy, guided by frozen SigLIP embeddings, achieved a **74%** success rate in navigating to language-specified targets within a simulated environment.
- Successful navigation by the learned policy required an average of **369.4** timesteps, significantly more than the expert policy's average of **113.97** timesteps.
- SigLIP consistently outperformed other tested VLMs, achieving **74.0%** success compared to CLIP's **62.0%** and ViLT's **40.0%**, correlating with its superior inherent spatial sensitivity and higher embedding dimensionality.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Frozen vision-language embeddings provide sufficient semantic grounding for basic navigation, allowing robots to correctly identify and move towards language-specified visual targets without task-specific VLM adaptation.
> - Despite enabling a 74% success rate, these embeddings alone exhibit limitations in complex spatial reasoning and efficiency, leading to navigation paths that are approximately 3.2 times longer than an expert's.
> - The VLM's intrinsic spatial sensitivity directly correlated with navigation performance, and the inclusion of relative spatial cues in language instructions was crucial for effective guidance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The capability for robots to interpret and act upon natural language instructions is a critical aspect for their effective integration into human environments. Traditional approaches to language-guided robot navigation have typically relied on extensive task-specific training data and specialized architectural designs to establish a connection between language and visual observations. These methods have demonstrated limitations in generalizing to novel commands or environments, as their understanding often remains confined to predefined categories.

The advent of large-scale vision-language models (VLMs) such as CLIP and SigLIP, which are trained on vast internet-scale datasets, has introduced new opportunities for robotics. These foundation models offer rich semantic representations that can capture diverse visual and linguistic concepts. Recent research has explored various strategies for integrating VLMs into robotic systems. Some approaches, exemplified by RT-2 and Robotic-CLIP, involve fine-tuning VLMs directly on action data to enable them to generate robot commands from visual and linguistic inputs. Other systems, including CLIP-Nav, VLM-Social-Nav, SpatialVLM, VLMaps, and ZSON, combine VLMs with specialized navigation architectures, such as modules for mapping, spatial awareness, or explicit spatial reasoning. These integrated systems have exhibited advanced capabilities but often entail complex architectures, extensive fine-tuning processes, or the inclusion of supplementary perception modules specifically adapted for navigation tasks.

This paper distinguishes itself within this landscape by investigating a fundamental question: whether the inherent semantic representations derived from *frozen*, pretrained VLMs are, by themselves, sufficient for basic navigation tasks, or if specialized architectures and fine-tuning are consistently indispensable. Unlike previous works that enhance VLMs with additional training or dedicated modules, this research adopts a minimalist framework. It explicitly avoids fine-tuning the VLM on task-specific data, integrating specialized spatial reasoning capabilities, or incorporating mapping/exploration modules that provide spatial memory. Instead, the study treats the VLM strictly as a feature extractor, with a simple behavioral cloning policy learning to operate reactively based solely on single-frame vision-language embeddings. By stripping away these common augmentations, the work aims to establish an empirical baseline for the standalone capabilities of pretrained VLM embeddings in embodied navigation, providing insights into their intrinsic transferability and limitations without additional task-specific engineering.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to empirically answer a fundamental question in embodied artificial intelligence: can pretrained vision-language embeddings, without any additional fine-tuning or specialized architectural components, sufficiently guide a robot for navigation tasks based on natural language instructions?

This overarching question is motivated by several specific considerations:

- **Resource Efficiency:** Contemporary robotic systems often involve complex architectures and extensive fine-tuning procedures that demand substantial computational resources. The researchers aim to determine if a simpler, more resource-efficient approach leveraging existing pretrained embeddings could achieve acceptable performance, thereby reducing computational overhead and engineering effort.
- **Transfer Learning Insights:** Understanding the intrinsic capabilities and limitations of frozen vision-language embeddings provides valuable insights into their direct transferability across different embodied tasks. This knowledge can inform how general-purpose representations can be effectively utilized in novel robotic applications without extensive adaptation.
- **Informed System Design:** For robotics researchers designing new systems, clarity on the inherent strengths and weaknesses of pretrained embeddings is crucial. Knowing when these general-purpose representations are sufficient versus when specialized modules (e.g., for spatial reasoning, mapping, or memory) become necessary can guide more informed architectural decisions and practical trade-offs between system complexity and desired performance.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a minimalist framework designed to decouple the perception component from the policy learning process. This framework operates on the principle of using a frozen VLM as the sole source of environmental and instructional representation. The specific technical distinctions of this approach from prior work include:

- **Absence of VLM Fine-tuning:** Unlike methods that adapt VLMs to robotic action data, this work deliberately avoids fine-tuning the VLM, thereby bypassing the associated computational costs and data requirements.
- **No Specialized Spatial Reasoning:** The framework does not augment the VLM or policy with explicit spatial reasoning capabilities, such as those found in models designed to enhance spatial understanding.
- **Omission of Mapping or Exploration Modules:** The system explicitly foregoes the use of mapping or spatial memory modules, which are common in many navigation systems, requiring the policy to operate reactively solely based on current, single-frame vision-language embeddings.

Through this minimalist design, the research intends to provide a clear empirical baseline, highlighting both the inherent potential and specific limitations of leveraging foundation models as "drop-in" representations for embodied navigation, thereby offering practical insights for robotic system design in resource-constrained or general-purpose scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research consists of a two-phase process: first, the generation of expert demonstrations using a privileged policy, and second, the training of a student policy via behavioral cloning solely on frozen vision-language representations derived from these demonstrations.

**Phase 1: Privileged Expert Policy**

An expert behavioral policy, denoted as $\pi_{\beta}(s, g)$, was developed to reliably navigate to a specified goal. This expert policy operates with privileged information, meaning it has access to the full state ($s$) of the environment and a precise goal specification ($g$). The goal is represented as a one-hot identifier for the target object (one of five colored spheres) combined with its exact 3D position in the environment. This setup allows the expert to function as a perfect GPS-based navigator, as it knows both the target's identity and location.

The expert policy was implemented using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Preliminary evaluations confirmed its robustness, achieving a 100% success rate by consistently reaching the correct sphere and stopping within 0.1m of the target. Crucially, the expert policy does not process natural language instructions; its role is solely to generate optimal trajectories for the student policy to imitate.

A dataset of navigation trajectories was collected using this expert policy. Each trajectory began with the robot initialized at a random location and orientation within a 3m x 3m arena. One of the five colored spheres was randomly designated as the target. For each target, natural language commands were generated, incorporating both the semantic color identification (e.g., "red ball") and a relative spatial positioning cue based on the target's angular position relative to the robot's heading (e.g., "to your left," "straight ahead"). During each episode, the expert controlled the robot until the goal was reached, and the following data were recorded: RGB camera images from the robot's perspective, the generated language instructions, the expert's actions (two-dimensional wheel velocity commands), and reward signals based on distance to the goal. The resulting dataset, $D$, comprised approximately 500 trajectories, totaling tens of thousands of state-action pairs.

**Vision-Language Model Selection**

Prior to training the student policy, the researchers conducted an empirical comparison to select an appropriate vision-language model (VLM) that not only encoded semantic object information but also demonstrated sensitivity to spatial relationships relevant for navigation. Three prominent models were evaluated: ViLT (VQA-finetuned), CLIP (ViT-B/32), and SigLIP (So400M-Patch14-384).

A spatial distance test was designed using joint embeddings of images and language prompts. For various reference images containing a colored sphere and an associated natural language instruction, comparison images were generated. These comparison images depicted the same colored object and instruction, but with the object either in the same 3x3 spatial grid cell (same spatial semantics) or a different cell (different spatial semantics). Background and lighting were controlled to isolate the effect of spatial positioning. Cosine distances between the joint image-text embeddings were computed. A lower cosine distance implied greater semantic and spatial similarity.

Results showed that all three models exhibited sensitivity to spatial positioning. Notably, SigLIP demonstrated the most pronounced distinction between same and different spatial contexts, with a 26.5% difference in mean cosine distance, compared to 13.0% for CLIP and 16.3% for ViLT. This indicated stronger inherent spatial sensitivity in SigLIP's joint embedding space, a factor considered beneficial for navigation tasks. This finding, combined with SigLIP’s efficient architecture, led to its selection as the vision-language backbone.

**Vision-Language Embedding with SigLIP**

The selected SigLIP model was utilized to create a joint embedding that fuses visual and linguistic information. SigLIP comprises an image encoder and a text encoder, projecting inputs into a shared 1152-dimensional latent space. For each timestep in the collected dataset:
1. The RGB camera image ($I_t$) and the corresponding language instruction ($L$) were processed by SigLIP's pretrained encoders.
2. The resulting image and text features were L2-normalized.
3. These normalized features were summed and normalized again to produce a joint embedding vector ($o_t$).

This vector $o_t$ served as a compact representation capturing the robot's visual perception, the language instruction, and the implicit spatial relationship between the robot and the target. Critically, the SigLIP model remained entirely frozen throughout this process and during policy training, with no updates made to its weights.

**Behavioral Cloning Policy Learning**

A behavioral cloning policy, $\pi(a|o)$, was trained using the processed dataset $D' = \{(o_t, a_t)\}$, where $o_t$ is the joint embedding and $a_t$ is the expert's action. The policy was implemented as a feedforward neural network that takes the 1152-dimensional joint embedding $o$ as input and outputs a two-dimensional action $a = (\omega_1, \omega_2)$ representing normalized wheel velocity commands.

The policy was trained to minimize the mean squared error (MSE) between its predicted actions and the expert's actions:
$L(\theta) = E_{(o,a) \sim D'} \parallel \pi_{\theta}(o) - a \parallel^2$
The optimization was performed using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of $1 \times 10^{-3}$, which decayed over time.

A key aspect of this approach is that the student policy, during deployment, receives only the current camera image and language instruction. It must implicitly learn navigation behaviors, such as turning towards a visible target or exploring when the target is out of sight, purely from the demonstrations without access to the explicit state information that the expert used.

**Experimental Setup**

- **Simulation Environment:** All experiments were conducted in NVIDIA Isaac Sim and NVIDIA Isaac Lab, high-fidelity robotics simulation frameworks.
- **Robot Platform:** The agent was modeled after the NVIDIA Jetbot, a differential-drive robot equipped with a forward-facing RGB camera providing 256x256 resolution images. Actions were two-dimensional angular velocity commands applied at 60Hz.
- **Task Definition:** The task involved navigating to one of five randomly chosen colored spheres (red, green, blue, yellow, pink) in a 3m x 3m arena. No obstacles were present. The robot received a language instruction specifying the target color and its relative spatial position (e.g., "red ball which is to your left"). Success was defined as reaching within 0.1m of the correct target within a maximum of 1000 timesteps.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** Both the expert policy and the learned policy were evaluated on 100 test episodes with randomized starting positions. Metrics recorded included success rate, number of timesteps to completion, cumulative reward, and trajectory efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation yielded a clear comparison between the privileged expert policy ($\pi_{\beta}$) and the learned policy ($\pi$) guided solely by frozen vision-language embeddings.

**Overall Performance Gap:**
The expert policy achieved a perfect 100% success rate, consistently reaching the designated targets. In contrast, the learned policy achieved a 74% success rate in navigating to the language-specified targets.

**Efficiency Gap:**
A significant disparity was observed in navigation efficiency. For successful episodes, the learned policy required an average of 369.4 timesteps to reach the goal, which is approximately 3.2 times longer than the expert policy's average of 113.97 timesteps. The best-case performance for the learned policy was 216 timesteps, compared to the expert's minimum of 73 timesteps. The worst-case for the learned policy extended to 828 timesteps, whereas the expert's maximum was 154.

**Reward and Consistency:**
The cumulative rewards further illustrated the performance difference. The learned policy exhibited considerable variability in rewards across episodes, ranging from approximately -8 to -13, indicating less consistent performance across different starting configurations. The expert policy, conversely, maintained stable and high rewards in all test episodes.

**Learning Dynamics (Behavioral Cloning Loss):**
The behavioral cloning (BC) loss for SigLIP showed rapid initial convergence, followed by a more gradual improvement, eventually stabilizing around 0.06. This suggests that while the policy learned to approximate many of the expert's actions, a persistent performance gap remained, likely attributable to scenarios where the expert relied on privileged information not fully captured by the VLM embeddings.

**Baseline Evaluation: Multi-Model Comparison:**
A controlled comparison was conducted across three VLMs (ViLT, CLIP, and SigLIP) using identical behavioral cloning training and evaluation procedures.
- **Performance Hierarchy:** SigLIP demonstrated superior performance, achieving a 74.0% success rate. CLIP followed with 62.0% success, and ViLT showed the lowest performance at 40.0% success.
- **Efficiency in Multi-Model Comparison:** SigLIP also exhibited superior efficiency among the learned policies, requiring an average of 369.4 timesteps for successful episodes, compared to CLIP's 417.6 and ViLT's 472.0 timesteps.
- **Embedding Dimensionality and Performance:** The observed performance ranking aligned with the embedding dimensionality of the models: SigLIP (1152-dimensional) outperformed ViLT (768-dimensional) and CLIP (512-dimensional), suggesting that richer representational capacity correlated with improved navigation performance.
- **BC Loss vs. Navigation Performance:** Counterintuitively, SigLIP, which achieved the highest navigation success, exhibited a higher behavioral cloning training loss compared to ViLT and CLIP. This finding indicates that BC loss, while measuring imitation fidelity, may not be a direct predictor of downstream task performance for embodied applications. It suggests that SigLIP's richer embeddings, despite being harder to compress into direct action predictions by the BC network, contained more navigation-relevant information enabling better overall task performance.
- **Spatial Sensitivity Validation:** The navigation performance hierarchy across the models directly mirrored the spatial sensitivity ranking established in the initial VLM selection study. SigLIP, which demonstrated the most pronounced spatial distinction in its embeddings, also achieved the highest navigation success, confirming the importance of inherent spatial understanding within VLM embeddings.
- **Statistical Significance:** The 95% confidence intervals indicated statistically significant differences between SigLIP (74.0% [65.4% - 82.6%]) and ViLT (40.0% [30.4% - 49.6%]), with minimal overlap between SigLIP and CLIP (62.0% [52.5% - 71.5%]).
- **VQA Fine-tuning Limitations:** ViLT's relatively poor performance (40.0% success), despite being fine-tuned for visual question answering, suggested that task-specific fine-tuning on non-embodied datasets may not effectively transfer to robotic navigation, which requires continuous spatial reasoning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes an empirical baseline to the understanding of pretrained vision-language embeddings in embodied navigation. The findings provide a nuanced perspective on their capabilities and limitations when used as a sole representation source without task-specific modifications.

**Semantic Grounding vs. Spatial Reasoning:** The 74% success rate demonstrates that frozen vision-language embeddings can effectively support basic semantic grounding, allowing the robot to correctly identify and move towards language-specified visual targets. This capability aligns with the core strengths of models like SigLIP and CLIP. However, the significant efficiency gap (3.2 times longer paths) reveals a limitation in handling more complex spatial reasoning, long-horizon planning, and temporal memory. While the VLM selection confirmed some inherent spatial sensitivity in SigLIP, this level of understanding appears insufficient for optimally efficient navigation. This highlights a distinction: VLMs can ground semantics and possess basic spatial awareness, but may require complementary, dedicated spatial reasoning mechanisms or architectural augmentations for tasks demanding efficient path planning and exploration.

**Prompt Engineering Sensitivity:** The study underscored the importance of prompt engineering. Simple color-only instructions resulted in considerably lower performance. The inclusion of relative spatial cues (e.g., "to your left") in the language instructions was crucial for the VLM embeddings to effectively capture both semantic object identity and directional guidance. This implies that the linguistic framing directly influences the VLM's ability to extract relevant features for embodied tasks.

**Performance-Complexity Tradeoffs:** The minimalist approach highlights a practical tradeoff in robotics system design: the balance between simplicity and performance. While more complex, fine-tuned, or architecturally enhanced approaches can achieve higher performance, they demand substantial computational resources and engineering effort. This work demonstrates that a reasonable level of performance can be achieved with minimal complexity by leveraging frozen pretrained embeddings and a simple behavioral cloning policy. This insight is particularly relevant for resource-constrained applications or scenarios where rapid deployment and reduced development costs are priorities. The findings suggest that for basic navigation with clear visual targets, frozen embeddings might offer sufficient capability, whereas tasks requiring highly efficient or complex reasoning may justify additional architectural complexity.

**Implications for Future Research:** The findings suggest several avenues for advancing embodied robotics with foundation models:
- **Hybrid Architectures:** Addressing observed failures (e.g., repeated circling, target confusion) could involve combining the semantic richness of pretrained embeddings with explicit spatial memory components, potentially through world models, to maintain consistent object identification across varying viewpoints and enable more direct navigation.
- **Task-Specific Representation Learning:** The challenges with distinguishing visually similar targets suggest that generic VLM embeddings may not optimally encode all navigation-relevant distinctions. Future work could explore embodiment-aware representation learning techniques, building upon the inherent spatial sensitivity of models like SigLIP, to create features more specifically tailored for navigation tasks.
- **Data-Efficient Adaptation:** To mitigate timeout failures and enhance systematic exploration capabilities, lightweight adaptation techniques could be developed. These would aim to align pretrained representations more closely with navigation-specific requirements without incurring the full computational cost and data demands of comprehensive fine-tuning.

In conclusion, this research provides an important empirical foundation for understanding the intrinsic utility of pretrained vision-language embeddings in embodied navigation. By demonstrating their capacity to achieve a 74% success rate in language-guided tasks, it underscores their potential as lightweight, general-purpose representations. However, the substantial efficiency gap compared to privileged experts highlights the necessity for further inductive biases or specialized architectural components to achieve expert-level performance. This work thus informs practical architectural decisions, guiding researchers on when to leverage foundation models "as-is" versus when to invest in tailored modifications for specific performance requirements in the evolving landscape of embodied robotics.
