# RL's Razor: Why Online Reinforcement Learning Forgets Less

> [!summary] Summary
> Online reinforcement learning (RL) consistently causes less catastrophic forgetting in foundation models compared to supervised fine-tuning (SFT), across both language and robotics tasks. The paper identifies the forward KL divergence between the fine-tuned and base policy, measured on the new task, as a universal predictor for the degree of forgetting and proposes "RL's Razor," which suggests that online RL implicitly seeks KL-minimal solutions.

## Problem

- Foundation models, despite their capabilities, suffer from catastrophic forgetting, losing previously acquired knowledge when continually adapting to new tasks or environments.
- Existing methods for mitigating catastrophic forgetting often rely on heuristic strategies, lacking a fundamental, universal principle to explain its underlying causes.
- A clear understanding of the mechanistic differences between SFT and RL that lead to varying levels of forgetting when fine-tuning foundation models was needed.

## Method

- The research extensively compares the forgetting susceptibility of SFT and on-policy RL across large language models and robotic foundation models through empirical experimentation.
- It systematically evaluates various candidate metrics to identify a universal predictor for catastrophic forgetting, focusing on ==distributional and parameter-level changes==.
- A theoretical framework is developed to explain why on-policy RL inherently converges to solutions that minimize ==KL divergence== from the base model, thereby preserving prior knowledge.

## Results

- RL fine-tuning consistently exhibited significantly less catastrophic forgetting than SFT, achieving performance gains on new tasks with minimal degradation of prior capabilities across diverse LLM and robotics benchmarks.
- Forward KL divergence between the fine-tuned and base policy demonstrated a strong functional relationship with prior-task performance, serving as a robust predictor for forgetting (e.g., R² = **0.96** in a controlled setting).
- A theoretical analysis validates that policy gradient methods, particularly with binary rewards, implicitly converge to the KL-minimal optimal policy, providing a formal basis for the "RL's Razor" principle.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Catastrophic forgetting across different fine-tuning algorithms and tasks is consistently predictable by a single metric: the forward KL divergence between the fine-tuned and base policy on the new task, establishing an "empirical forgetting law."
> - "RL's Razor" postulates that online reinforcement learning inherently prefers solutions that are closest in KL divergence to the original model, explaining its superior knowledge retention compared to SFT.
> - The on-policy nature of the training data, rather than the use of negative gradients, is the critical factor responsible for RL's reduced KL divergence and subsequent lower forgetting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the crucial intersection of **foundation models, continual learning, and catastrophic forgetting**. Foundation models (e.g., Large Language Models, Vision-Language Models, Robotic Foundation Models) have emerged as the backbone of modern AI, exhibiting remarkable capabilities across diverse domains. However, a significant challenge remains: these models, once pre-trained and deployed, struggle to continually adapt to new tasks, environments, or objectives without losing previously acquired knowledge. This problem is known as **catastrophic forgetting**, a long-standing issue in neural networks that causes models to severely degrade performance on old tasks when trained on new ones.

Traditionally, catastrophic forgetting has been addressed through various mitigation strategies, such as constraining weight updates (e.g., Elastic Weight Consolidation), preserving learned features, or regularizing output distribution shifts. While effective heuristics, these methods often treat the *symptoms* rather than identifying the *root cause* of forgetting. This paper explicitly seeks a deeper, unifying principle to explain *when* and *why* forgetting occurs, transcending specific algorithmic implementations.

The work also contributes to the ongoing discussion and comparison between different post-training methods, particularly **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** and **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) or pure Reinforcement Learning (RL)**. While prior research has compared SFT and RL on metrics like new-task performance, generalization, or transferability (e.g., showing RL's superiority in generalization), the relative susceptibility of these methods to catastrophic forgetting has been largely underexplored. The paper explicitly differentiates itself by making forgetting the central focus of this comparison. The finding that KL divergence, a measure often used heuristically for regularization in RLHF (like in PPO), is actually a fundamental predictor of forgetting provides a more principled understanding of existing practices and suggests new directions for algorithm design. The paper also acknowledges and contrasts its findings with concurrent work (Lai et al., 2025) that also observed RL's superior forgetting performance but attributed it to different mechanisms, emphasizing the novel contribution of "RL's Razor" and its theoretical grounding.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the aspiration for **long-lived AI agents** that can continually learn and adapt in deployment. Current foundation models, despite their scale, are largely static and prone to catastrophic forgetting when adapted to new requirements. This limitation severely hinders their potential as truly adaptive and useful long-term assistants.

The authors observe a **striking empirical gap**: when comparing SFT and RL for fine-tuning, even when both methods achieve similar performance on a new task, RL consistently preserves prior knowledge and capabilities significantly better than SFT. This unexpected finding raises a fundamental question: **What underlying mechanism allows RL to improve on new tasks with minimal impact on prior knowledge, unlike SFT?**

This core question drives the paper's key objectives:
1. **Quantify and demonstrate the difference in forgetting between RL and SFT:** To empirically show that RL indeed forgets less than SFT across various domains (language and robotics).
2. **Uncover a universal predictor for catastrophic forgetting:** To identify a simple, measurable metric that reliably predicts the degree of forgetting, independent of the specific training algorithm or hyperparameters. The authors term this the "empirical forgetting law."
3. **Explain the mechanistic difference between RL and SFT regarding forgetting:** To elucidate *why* RL, particularly on-policy methods, is inherently less susceptible to catastrophic forgetting. This mechanism is coined "RL's Razor."
4. **Provide theoretical justification:** To offer a formal understanding of why on-policy RL updates lead to smaller distributional shifts (KL divergence) and thus less forgetting.
5. **Validate the proposed principle:** To provide empirical evidence, including using an "oracle SFT" distribution, that confirms the identified predictor and mechanism.

In essence, the paper aims to shift the understanding of catastrophic forgetting from a phenomenon to be mitigated by ad-hoc methods to one governed by a fundamental, measurable principle, thereby guiding the design of future continually learning systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a multi-faceted methodology combining extensive empirical experimentation across diverse domains with theoretical analysis and controlled ablations.

#### 3.1. Empirical Comparison of SFT vs. RL

- **Tasks and Models:** Experiments were conducted on:
 - **Large Language Models (LLMs):** Qwen 2.5 3B-Instruct fine-tuned on Math reasoning, Science Q&A, and Tool use tasks.
 - **Robotics Foundation Models:** OpenVLA 7B fine-tuned for a Pick and Place task in the SimplerEnv environment.
 - **Controlled Toy Setting:** A 3-layer MLP trained on a ParityMNIST task (predicting even/odd digits) while measuring forgetting on FashionMNIST. This simplified setting allowed for full convergence and systematic ablations.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** For each task, models were fine-tuned using either SFT or on-policy RL (specifically GRPO, without explicit KL regularization). Performance was measured along two axes:
 - **New Task Performance:** Accuracy or success rate on the newly introduced task.
 - **Prior Task Performance:** Average score on a diverse set of unrelated benchmarks (e.g., Hellaswag, MMLU for LLMs; drawer opening/closing for robotics) to quantify catastrophic forgetting.
- **Pareto Frontiers:** To fairly compare methods despite varying hyperparameters, dozens of models were trained under diverse settings. The Pareto frontier (optimal trade-off between new-task gain and prior-task retention) was then identified and plotted.

#### 3.2. Searching for a Forgetting Predictor

- The authors systematically ablated various candidate variables hypothesized to predict forgetting, including:
 - **Weight-level changes:** L1 norm, Fisher-weighted L2, spectral norm of parameter changes.
 - **Representation-level changes:** L1 and L2 distances of hidden activation shifts, and Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)/CKNNA for representational similarity.
 - **Sparsity and rank of updates:** Examining the nature of parameter updates.
 - **Distributional distances:** Forward KL, Reverse KL, Total Variation, and L2 distance between the fine-tuned and base policies on the new task.
- **Focus on Forward KL:** Through this ablation, the **forward KL divergence** between the fine-tuned and base policy, evaluated on the *new task*, emerged as the most consistent and strong predictor.

#### 3.3. Validating the KL Hypothesis and Explaining RL's Bias

- **Oracle SFT:** In the ParityMNIST setting, an "oracle SFT" distribution was analytically constructed. This distribution provably minimizes KL divergence to the base model while achieving 100% accuracy on the new task. Training SFT with this oracle distribution allowed a direct test of whether explicit KL minimization reduces forgetting, even for SFT.
- **Algorithm Class Comparison:** To isolate the specific aspect of RL responsible for its KL-conservatism, four objectives were compared on the Science Q&A task:
 - **GRPO:** On-policy, uses negative examples (advantage function).
 - **1-0 Reinforce:** On-policy, only uses positive examples (binary reward).
 - **SFT:** Offline, only uses positive examples (fixed annotations).
 - **SimPO:** Offline, uses positive and negative examples (contrastive loss).
 This setup helped determine if "on-policy" or "negative examples" (or both) were the critical factor.
- **Theoretical Perspective:** The authors provide a theoretical justification by framing policy gradient methods, especially with binary rewards, as an iterative process involving **Information (I-) and Expectation (M-) projections** in probability space. They prove that this process converges to the **KL-minimal optimal policy** within the representable family, even without explicit KL regularization.

#### 3.4. Additional Analysis

- **Representation Preservation (CKNNA):** Quantified how much the internal representations of models changed during SFT vs. RL fine-tuning using CKNNA on unrelated data.
- **Optimization Dynamics:** Analyzed the per-step relationship between KL change on the new task and the gradient similarity with respect to previous-task loss.
- **Scaling Effects:** Investigated if larger model sizes (7B, 14B Qwen 2.5) mitigate the SFT forgetting trade-off.

This comprehensive approach, spanning empirical demonstrations, systematic ablation, theoretical formalization, and targeted experiments, allows the authors to robustly establish their core claim and principle.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key findings, rigorously supported by empirical evidence and theoretical analysis:

1. **RL Forgets Significantly Less Than SFT:** Across all LLM tasks (Math reasoning, Science Q&A, Tool use) and the robotic Pick and Place task, RL fine-tuning consistently achieves performance gains on new tasks with minimal or no observable catastrophic forgetting of prior capabilities. In contrast, SFT achieves comparable new-task performance only by incurring substantial degradation of prior knowledge, as evidenced by the Pareto frontiers (Figure 2). This empirical gap is robust across domains.

2. **The Empirical Forgetting Law: KL Divergence Predicts Forgetting:** The research identifies a powerful and consistent predictor for catastrophic forgetting: the **forward KL divergence between the fine-tuned policy and the base policy, measured on the new task**. Plotting prior-task performance against this KL divergence reveals a single functional relationship that holds across different training algorithms (SFT and RL) and hyperparameters. In the controlled ParityMNIST setting, this relationship is remarkably strong, with a quadratic fit achieving R² = 0.96 (Figure 3, middle). A similar, though weaker, correlation (R² = 0.71) is observed in LLM experiments (Figure 11).

3. **RL's Razor: On-Policy Bias Towards KL-Minimal Solutions:** The paper proposes "RL’s Razor": *among all ways to solve a new task, RL prefers those closest in KL to the original model*. This bias explains why RL forgets less. Empirical analysis shows that RL achieves high new-task accuracy with significantly smaller shifts in KL divergence from the base model compared to SFT (Figure 3, right).

4. **On-Policy Nature is Key, Not Negative Gradients:** By comparing four algorithm types (GRPO, 1-0 Reinforce, SFT, SimPO), the authors demonstrate that the **on-policy nature** of the training data is the critical factor for reducing KL divergence and thus forgetting. On-policy methods (GRPO and 1-0 Reinforce) maintain smaller KL shifts and better prior-task retention, while offline methods (SFT and SimPO) behave similarly, regardless of whether negative examples are used (Figure 4). This contradicts alternative hypotheses suggesting negative gradients as the cause.

5. **Theoretical Justification for KL-Minimal Convergence:** The paper provides a theoretical framework explaining that policy gradient methods, particularly with binary rewards, can be understood as an iterative optimization process analogous to the Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm. This process implicitly performs a sequence of Information (I-) and Expectation (M-) projections in probability space, converging to the **KL-minimal optimal policy** within the representable policy class (Theorem 5.2). This formalizes the "RL's Razor" principle.

6. **Validation with Oracle SFT:** Training SFT on an "oracle distribution" that is analytically designed to minimize KL divergence to the base model while achieving perfect new-task accuracy in the ParityMNIST setting yields even less forgetting than RL. This critical experiment directly validates that explicit or implicit KL minimization is the fundamental driver of reduced forgetting, independent of the algorithm's label (SFT or RL) (Figure 3, left).

7. **Alternative Hypotheses Ruled Out:** Through comprehensive ablation, other common candidate predictors for forgetting, such as weight-level changes, representation-level changes (though representation *does* drift, it's not the primary *predictor* of forgetting), sparsity, or rank of updates, were found to correlate poorly or inconsistently with catastrophic forgetting (Table 1).

8. **Representation Preservation by RL:** Analysis using CKNNA reveals that RL-trained models retain a much higher representational similarity to the base model compared to SFT-trained models, further supporting the idea that RL integrates new skills without fundamentally altering the model's learned feature space (Figure 7).

9. **Scaling Does Not Eliminate Forgetting for SFT:** Experiments with larger LLMs (7B and 14B Qwen 2.5) show that while larger models start with better baseline performance, the fundamental trade-off of catastrophic forgetting during SFT remains (Figure 8).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of "RL’s Razor" have profound significance for the future of AI, particularly in the realm of foundation models and continual learning:

1. **A New Guiding Principle for Continual Learning:** The most significant impact is the proposal of a new, fundamental principle for designing post-training algorithms: **algorithms should explicitly aim to minimize KL divergence from the base model to achieve continual adaptation without forgetting.** This shifts the focus from merely optimizing for new task performance to a dual objective that considers the preservation of prior knowledge.

2. **Enabling Truly Long-Lived AI Agents:** By providing a clear understanding of why forgetting occurs and how to mitigate it, this work paves the way for building foundation models that can truly "learn for life." Such models could continuously acquire new skills and adapt to evolving needs in real-world deployments (e.g., in robotics, personal assistants, scientific discovery) without constantly degrading their core capabilities.

3. **Informing the Design of Future Post-Training Methods:** The "KL-minimal path" insight offers a concrete design axis for new algorithms. Researchers can now develop methods that combine the efficiency of SFT with the knowledge-preserving properties of RL, perhaps through novel regularization techniques, curriculum learning strategies, or hybrid approaches that explicitly guide learning along KL-conservative trajectories. This could lead to more stable and robust fine-tuning paradigms.

4. **Deeper Understanding of Existing Practices:** The paper provides a principled explanation for why KL regularization, often used as a heuristic in RLHF methods like PPO to stabilize training or prevent reward hacking, is also effective in reducing catastrophic forgetting. This moves KL regularization from an engineering trick to a theoretically grounded strategy for knowledge retention.

5. **Refocusing Research on the Mechanisms of Forgetting:** By ruling out several alternative hypotheses for predicting forgetting, the paper directs future research toward understanding the mechanistic link between large KL shifts on new tasks and the disruption of prior knowledge (e.g., representational interference, implicit capacity limits). This fundamental understanding is crucial for further advancements.

6. **Implications for Off-Policy RL and Data Efficiency:** While the current work focuses on on-policy RL, the identified principle suggests that off-policy RL algorithms would need specific mechanisms (e.g., explicit KL regularization, replay buffer strategies) to maintain KL-conservatism and avoid forgetting. Future research can explore how this principle applies to more data-efficient off-policy methods.

In conclusion, "RL’s Razor" offers a unifying and actionable principle for tackling catastrophic forgetting, moving beyond ad-hoc solutions to a deeper, mechanism-based understanding. Its impact extends from informing algorithmic design to enabling a future where AI systems can continually learn and evolve without sacrificing their accumulated intelligence.
