# One-Shot Learning from Demonstration of Contact-Rich Robotic Manipulation by Identifying Physical Interactions

> [!summary] Summary
> A one-shot learning-from-demonstration method for contact-rich manipulation that explicitly identifies what physical interactions occur, where (interaction frames), and when (segments), then derives contact-based transition conditions. A hybrid pose/wrench controller tracks demonstrated trajectories per segment, switching on measured contact making/breaking, with online trajectory adaptation. Real Franka experiments on cabinet opening, bolt screwing, dislodging, and surface contouring show robustness, generalization, and adaptivity from a single demonstration.

## Problem

- Most few-shot LfD methods model trajectories and high-level subtasks without explicitly representing contact making and breaking, which are decisive during contact-rich manipulation.
- General-purpose learned policies need large physical-force datasets that are scarce, and produce opaque models that are hard to interpret, debug, or guarantee.
- Prior interaction-aware work models what, where, or when separately, never all three together for complex, sequential, contact-rich tasks with quantified robustness and adaptivity.

## Method

- ==Unilateral interaction classes== split each frame axis into positive and negative directions, giving twelve idle/free/impeded/constrained labels per segment instead of six bilateral ones.
- ==Transition conditions== inferred from class changes between segments: constraint making, constraint breaking, or tracking-progress completion, expressed in whichever adjacent ==interaction frame== needs fewest conditions.
- ==Hybrid pose/wrench control== in interaction frames with contact-aware monitoring, relaxed tolerances during expected contact changes, pose/wrench exploration, and online ==trajectory blending== using measured offsets.

## Results

- Hinge-latch opening: stiff pose control gave **50%** success at **8.4 deg** offset; hybrid control raised the opened-hinge segments to **70%**; online adaptation succeeded consistently up to **17.0 deg**.
- Barrel latch failed for pose, hybrid, and contact-aware switching at **5.6 deg** offset; online trajectory adaptation completed it and held to **11.3 deg** before gripper-door collision.
- Hole search found only **2 of 10** offset holes on a 20 mm circle (**20%**) via linear extrapolation, while offline generalization with known hole poses reached **100%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Contact events are grounded haptic checkpoints, more reliable than noisy pose estimates; relaxing tracking tolerances when a contact change is expected buys real robustness.
> - Robustness through contact awareness converts naturally into adaptivity: the pose where contact actually occurs reveals the unknown geometric offset for later segments.
> - Interpretable discrete task models let robustness, generalization, and adaptivity be implemented explicitly, but threshold sensitivity and weak exploration cap performance on multi-dimensional searches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the field of Learning from Demonstration (LfD), also known as imitation learning or behavioral cloning, a sub-area of robot learning. LfD aims to enable robots to acquire manipulation skills directly from human demonstrations, reducing the need for manual programming and enhancing robot versatility across various applications such as healthcare, logistics, and manufacturing.

A significant challenge in robot learning, particularly for complex, contact-rich, and sequential manipulation tasks, is the substantial requirement for physical data (kinematics, forces). This contrasts with the abundance of representational data (visual, language) available online. General-purpose learning methods often necessitate large datasets, which are costly and time-consuming to collect in the real world, and transfer from simulation remains a non-trivial problem. Such methods also tend to result in large, difficult-to-interpret models that lack explicit performance and safety guarantees and require considerable computational resources.

This research distinguishes itself by focusing on robotics-specific LfD methods that aim to reduce data requirements by leveraging domain knowledge. Specifically, it targets "few-shot LfD," meaning learning from a limited number of demonstrations (in this case, a single one). Most few-shot LfD approaches typically model tasks at two levels: trajectory models (e.g., using Probabilistic Movement Primitives, Gaussian Processes, or Dynamic Movement Primitives) for instantaneous signal properties, and high-level models (e.g., Hidden Markov Models) for subtask segmentation. However, these methods often implicitly or incompletely capture physically meaningful states, such as contact sequences.

This work specifically addresses a gap in the literature by proposing an LfD method that *explicitly models physical interactions*—identifying *what* interactions occur, *where* in the environment they occur, and *when* they occur during a task. While prior research has highlighted the benefits of explicitly modeling aspects of physical interactions for LfD (e.g., choosing specific control methods, deriving meaningful task segments, planning for robust reproduction, or placing reference frames in relevant geometric features), this paper notes that these three aspects (what, where, when) have not been simultaneously modeled for complex, sequential, and contact-rich tasks. By integrating these elements, the authors aim to close a gap in interpretable few-shot LfD for robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the increasing demand for versatile robots capable of performing diverse manipulation tasks across various labor settings. Manually programming robots for each new task is inefficient and costly. Learning from Demonstration (LfD) offers a solution by allowing robots to learn directly from human examples. However, existing LfD methods face several limitations:

- **Insufficient Modeling of Physical Interactions:** Most LfD approaches do not explicitly model crucial physical interactions, such as the making and breaking of contact, which are fundamental to many manipulation tasks. These interactions are often recurring and can form a basis for robust, generalizable, and adaptive task reproduction.
- **Data Scarcity for Complex Tasks:** Complex, contact-rich, and sequential manipulation tasks involve long time horizons with high-dimensional physical data (positions, velocities, forces) related by non-linear dynamics and constraints. Collecting sufficient real-world physical data for general-purpose learning methods is challenging, and simulated data transfer to reality is not straightforward.
- **Interpretability and Resource Issues with General-Purpose Methods:** Large, general-purpose learning models trained on extensive datasets are often difficult to interpret, debug, extend, or provide performance and safety guarantees for. They also require substantial computational resources. The authors advocate for more efficient, robotics-specific learning methods that leverage domain knowledge and result in interpretable models.

Against this background, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop an LfD Method Explicitly Modeling Physical Interactions:** To propose and implement an LfD method that directly utilizes information about *what* physical interactions occur, *where* (in which reference frame), and *when* (in which task segment) during a single human demonstration. This extends previous work on contact-based task modeling by deriving transition conditions and facilitating force/trajectory tracking and generalization.
2. **Enable One-Shot Learning of Complex, Contact-Rich Tasks:** To demonstrate that explicit physical interaction modeling allows for the reproduction of complex, sequential, and contact-rich manipulation tasks using only a single demonstration, without requiring prior knowledge specific to the task.
3. **Facilitate Robustness to Unknown Geometric Variations:** To show that the proposed method enables robustness to unforeseen changes in the environment's geometry, primarily through interaction-aware control.
4. **Support Generalization to Known Geometric Variations:** To illustrate that the method can generalize task reproduction to new situations when geometric variations are known a priori, for instance, through external information like a vision system.
5. **Enable Online Adaptation:** To demonstrate that the method facilitates online adaptation of task reproduction using geometric information explored or discovered during the task execution.
6. **Provide Interpretability for Few-Shot LfD:** To present a human-interpretable framework that explicitly discusses how robustness, generalization, and adaptivity can be implemented, thereby aiming to close a gap in the interpretable few-shot LfD literature.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed LfD method extends previous work on contact-based task modeling by integrating contact-aware transition conditions, trajectory generalization, and a hybrid position-force controller with monitoring and switching logic.

**A. Extended Task Modeling**

The method begins by recording a single task demonstration, yielding end-effector poses, twists, and wrenches. From this data, a task model is constructed:

1. **Time Segmentation (When):** The raw data is segmented into *N* discrete time segments. This is achieved by identifying points where the norms of twist-wrench signals cross predefined thresholds. Improvements over previous work include requiring a minimum integral of signal magnitude between threshold crossings and refined filtering of consecutive or momentary spikes to reduce oversegmentation.
2. **Interaction Frame Identification (Where):** For each segment *n*, a task-relevant reference frame, called the interaction frame {*n*χ}, is identified. This frame is selected to minimize and decouple the Cartesian mechanical power components (twists and wrenches) within that segment, effectively identifying a coordinate system where task dynamics are simplified. Four types of interaction frames are considered, based on whether their orientation and origin are constant with respect to the ground or the end-effector body.
3. **Interaction Classification (What):** Within each identified interaction frame, the physical interactions along each of the six degrees of freedom (three rotations, three translations) are classified. The original work classified interactions into Idle (no substantial wrenches or twists), Constrained (substantial wrenches, no twists), Free motion (substantial twists, no wrenches), and Impeded motion (substantial twists and wrenches).
4. **Deriving Unilateral Interaction Classes (Extension):** To model unilateral interactions (e.g., contacting a plane), the classification is extended to 12 interaction classes. The twist-wrench pair is split into positive and negative directions for each frame axis, allowing independent classification for each direction. This enables the detection of interactions such as a contact in one direction while allowing free movement in the opposite.
5. **Inferring Transition Conditions (Extension):** By observing how these 12 interaction classes change between consecutive segments, simple transition conditions are inferred. These conditions include:
 - **Constraint making (∧c):** When an axis transitions from Free or Impeded motion to Constrained.
 - **Constraint breaking (∨c):** When an axis transitions from Constrained to Free or Impeded motion.
 - **Tracking progress completion (>τ):** For all other cases, indicating that a desired state was reached by tracking a reference signal.
 The most compact representation (lowest sum of ∧c and ∨c conditions) is chosen for comparison between segment frames.

**B. Pose Trajectories and Geometric Generalization**

To reproduce tasks, the method tracks demonstrated pose trajectories, which are stored as time-dependent interaction frame poses {*n*P̃χ(τ)}.

1. **Geometric Generalization:** The task model can be adapted to new geometric situations if variations are known (e.g., from vision). This adaptation assumes variations occur only in the environment.
 - **Applying Constant Offsets:** If a group of segments shares a common geometric offset in the environment, this constant pose offset is applied to the ground-respective components of the interaction frame parameters for those segments.
 - **Blending Trajectories:** When two adjacent segments have different geometric offsets (e.g., one segment is not offset, the next is), the trajectory of the intermediate segment is modified. This is done by blending the original demonstrated trajectory (transformed to match the start of the new segment) with a trajectory suited to the end of the new segment, using a sigmoid blending function to preserve local curvatures at segment boundaries.

**C. Task Reproduction**

The task reproduction phase uses a hybrid position-force controller (Section V.A).

1. **Controller Choice:** For each axis of the interaction frame, control is chosen based on its identified interaction class:
 - **Pose Control:** Applied for Free, Idle, and Impeded motion axes (assuming Impeded motion due to friction is intentional movement). This tracks the demonstrated pose trajectory.
 - **Wrench Control:** Applied for Constrained axes. This tracks the demonstrated wrench profile.
2. **Hybrid Position/Force Control:** A control wrench is computed in the interaction frame as a blend of proportional-integral-derivative (PID) pose control and proportional-integral (PI) wrench control with feedforward. A binary selection vector, determined by the interaction classes, selects which control type applies to each axis.
3. **Monitoring and Switching Control:** The controller continuously monitors conditions to determine when to switch to the next segment:
 - **Tracking Completion:** For segments where no contact changes are expected (>τ), switching occurs when the progress variable τ reaches 1 and pose tracking errors (on pose-controlled axes) are within predefined tolerances {p*}.
 - **Contact-Aware Transitions:** If contact making (∧c) or breaking (∨c) is expected, the controller also monitors the online-measured interaction classes (Ŝh). To provide robustness to unknown variations, the pose error tolerances are relaxed by a multiplier (α) when contact changes are expected.
 - **Earlier-Than-Expected:** If the expected contact transition occurs before τ=1, and errors are within relaxed tolerances, the controller switches to the next segment immediately.
 - **Later-Than-Expected/Exploration:** If τ=1 and the expected contact transition has not occurred (and errors are within relaxed tolerances), the controller initiates exploration. This involves linearly extrapolating the last portion of the reference pose trajectory (for pose exploration) or increasing the reference wrench (for wrench exploration, specifically for expected constraint loss) until the contact condition is met or relaxed tolerances are exceeded.
4. **Online Trajectory Adaptation:** If a segment concludes at a pose different from the demonstration due to unknown geometric variations (discovered during exploration or contact changes), the subsequent segment's trajectory is adapted online. If both current and next segments involve a constraint, a constant offset is applied. If the next segment is in free space, trajectories are blended back towards the original demonstration's features.

**Implementation Details:**
Experiments use a Franka Research 3 robot with a SensONE force-torque sensor. The task modeling and monitor/control methods are implemented in Python 3.11.4 and run at 100 Hz. Gravity compensation is applied. Parameters for modeling thresholds, reproduction tolerances, and controller gains are fixed across tasks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The LfD method was evaluated on several complex, sequential, and contact-rich robotic tasks: cabinet opening (hinge latch, barrel latch, door), bolt screwing (picking, hole search, screwing), and surface following (dislodging, contouring). Each task was demonstrated once. The evaluation progressively enabled controller capabilities to assess robustness, generalization, and adaptivity to increasing geometric variations.

**A. Robustness to Unknown Geometric Variations (Steps 1-3)**

1. **Pure Pose Control (End-Effector Frame):** This baseline approach, using only pose control in the end-effector frame, exhibited limited robustness. It failed or had low success rates even for small geometric variations (e.g., hinge latch 8.4 deg offset, bolt picking 5 deg plane offset). Issues included gripper slipping, mechanical interference, and inability to meet strict tracking tolerances at contact points due to stiff control and misalignment.
2. **Hybrid Control (Interaction Frames):** Switching to hybrid position/force control expressed in interaction frames significantly improved performance.
 - **Improved Compliance:** Wrench control provided compliance, preventing gripper slip and reducing unwanted forces (e.g., increased hinge latch success from 50% to 70%).
 - **Better Contact Maintenance:** Essential for tasks requiring sustained contact (e.g., bolt screwing), where the pose controller alone failed immediately. The hybrid controller successfully maintained contact.
 - Despite improvements, this step still struggled with larger variations where expected contact changes occurred away from the demonstrated trajectory endpoint or if misalignments led to blockages.
3. **Contact-Aware Monitoring and Switching:** Adding explicit monitoring of contact transitions (∧c, ∨c) and relaxing pose tolerances (α > 1) when transitions were expected enhanced robustness:
 - **Tolerance for Deviations:** Relaxed tolerances allowed successful segment completion even if the robot's pose deviated from the reference at the moment of contact, accommodating earlier-than-expected contacts (e.g., hinge latch, bolt picking).
 - **Exploration for Later Contacts:** Implemented pose and wrench exploration strategies for later-than-expected contacts. Linear pose extrapolation was effective for most tasks (e.g., dislodging when clamp was further, barrel latch). Wrench exploration (increasing force) was successful for dislodging tasks requiring higher forces (e.g., dislodging with perpendicular clamp offset).
 - This step generally enabled success for moderate variations, but limitations existed where exploration strategies were too simple (e.g., only 2 out of 10 holes found in bolt screwing with 20mm offset) or where physical collisions prevented reaching the target pose for contact (e.g., large cabinet angle offsets).

**B. Adaptivity to Online Discovered Geometric Variations (Step 4)**

1. **Online Trajectory Adaptation:** This step involved adapting subsequent segment trajectories based on the actual pose achieved at the end of the current segment. This was crucial for managing larger variations that led to significant pose offsets.
 - **Improved Task Completion:** By adapting subsequent trajectories, the robot avoided mechanical interference and successfully completed tasks where previous steps failed (e.g., hinge latch 17.0 deg offset, barrel latch 11.3 deg offset, bolt picking 10-15 deg plane offset, surface contouring 10 mm offset). This demonstrated the ability to react to new environmental geometries discovered during execution.
 - **Limitations:** The effectiveness was still tied to the exploration capability. For instance, in hole search, online adaptation could only make use of the holes found by simple linear extrapolation (20% success rate), not all possible hole locations on a circle.

**C. Generalization using A Priori Known Geometric Variations (Step 5)**

1. **Offline Generalization:** When geometric variations were known beforehand (e.g., provided by a vision system), the task model was generalized offline before reproduction.
 - **Highest Success Rates:** This approach yielded the highest success rates, enabling task completion for variations that previously resulted in collisions or unmanageable deviations. For example, cabinet opening with 14.1-30.0 deg offsets could be completed by modifying the approach trajectory to avoid collisions. All 10 hole positions in bolt screwing could be successfully addressed if known beforehand. This confirmed that given accurate prior information, the task model's structure supports robust generalization.

**Overall Consistency:** The method demonstrated high consistency in quantitative evaluations, often resulting in either 0% or 100% success rates, which aids in drawing clear conclusions about the impact of each implemented feature.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by demonstrating the benefits of explicitly modeling physical interactions within a Learning from Demonstration (LfD) framework. The significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Interpretability:** Unlike many "black box" machine learning approaches, this method is human-interpretable. By explicitly identifying *what* physical interactions occur *where* and *when*, the task model provides clear, physically meaningful parameters. This interpretability allows for direct insights into why a task might fail or succeed, facilitating debugging, extension, and the provision of performance and safety guarantees, which are often lacking in opaque models.

2. **Efficient One-Shot Learning:** The work demonstrates that complex, sequential, and contact-rich manipulation tasks can be successfully reproduced from a *single demonstration* without requiring task-specific prior knowledge. This significantly reduces the data requirements compared to data-intensive, general-purpose learning methods that necessitate large datasets or extensive pre-training. This efficiency makes LfD more practical for real-world robotic deployments where collecting numerous demonstrations can be prohibitive.

3. **Robustness to Unknown Geometric Variations:** The study highlights how compliant hybrid control in task-relevant interaction frames, combined with explicit contact-aware monitoring and judiciously relaxed tracking tolerances, intrinsically provides robustness. The ability to detect and react to contact changes earlier or later than expected, even initiating simple exploration strategies when necessary, allows the robot to adapt to unknown but minor geometric discrepancies in the environment.

4. **Adaptivity to Online Discovered Information:** The method introduces online trajectory adaptation, where the robot adjusts subsequent task segments based on newly measured pose or contact information discovered during runtime (e.g., through exploration). This capability is crucial for managing larger, previously unknown geometric variations, preventing mechanical interference, and allowing the robot to complete tasks that would otherwise fail.

5. **Generalization to Known Geometric Variations:** By building task models with meaningful, adjustable parameters (e.g., interaction frames), the approach facilitates generalization when geometric variations are known a priori (e.g., from a vision system). This enables the robot to perform the same task on objects or in environments with different poses or dimensions by intelligently modifying the demonstrated trajectories. Furthermore, the identified interaction frames can inform external systems (like vision) about which geometric features are most relevant for generalization, enhancing scene understanding.

6. **Closing a Research Gap:** The paper successfully addresses a gap in the LfD literature by simultaneously modeling the "what," "where," and "when" aspects of physical interactions for complex, sequential, and contact-rich tasks. This integrated approach, combined with its emphasis on interpretability, represents a step towards more versatile, intelligent, and deployable robotic systems.

While the work presents a significant advancement, it acknowledges limitations such as the use of discrete elements and thresholds (suggesting future continuous methods), the focus on single demonstrations (implying benefits from multiple demonstrations), and assumptions about static, rigid, and gravity-compensated environments (pointing to future work on dynamic and compliant interactions). Nevertheless, the current findings provide constructive insights into the design of robust, adaptive, and generalizable LfD systems for contact-rich robotic manipulation.
