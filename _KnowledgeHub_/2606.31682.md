# HABIT: Human-Aware Behavior and Interaction Training Dataset for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> HABIT, a large-scale dataset of bimanual robot manipulation demonstrations, explicitly incorporates a co-present human to enable robots to learn human-aware behaviors. Training on HABIT leads to substantial improvements in task success rates, such as increasing success in Coworker tasks like Waste Sorting from 40% (Robot-only) to 95%, and facilitates sample-efficient adaptation to new human-robot interaction scenarios.

## Problem

- Existing large-scale robot demonstration datasets primarily feature robots operating in human-absent settings, limiting their ability to function in shared environments.
- Robots trained on human-absent data lack critical 'human-aware behaviors' such as coordination, yielding, and anticipating human motions, which are essential for safe and effective deployment.
- These interactive behaviors are difficult or impossible to learn from data collected without a co-present human, creating a significant gap in current robot policy capabilities.

## Method

- The **HABIT** dataset was created, comprising 10,563 episodes (164.19 hours) of ==bimanual robot manipulation== data where a human is consistently co-present and interactive.
- Tasks are categorized into ==Collaborator, Coworker, and Supervisor roles==, defined by ==task workflows== to capture diverse human-robot interaction modes and ensure consistency.
- A data collection protocol was designed to elicit specific human-aware behaviors like yielding under safety-first priority, temporal adaptation, and robust gesture grounding from visual cues.

## Results

- Training the π0.5 model on HABIT significantly improved task success rates, achieving **95%** in Coworker tasks like Waste Sorting compared to **40%** for the Robot-only baseline.
- HABIT-trained policies exhibited emergent human-aware behaviors, including reduced precondition violations in Collaborator tasks, fewer collisions in Coworker tasks, and improved gesture grounding in Supervisor tasks.
- Mid-training with HABIT enabled sample-efficient adaptation, allowing models to achieve success comparable to **200** direct fine-tuning demonstrations with only **50** fine-tuning demonstrations for new HRI tasks like Waste Sorting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human presence in robot demonstration data represents a critical and learnable dimension of diversity, enabling robots to acquire essential human-aware behaviors.
> - Structured task roles and workflows effectively capture and train robots on distinct human-robot interaction modes, from direct collaboration to independent action in shared spaces.
> - Training on human-present datasets provides a valuable prior, leading to sample-efficient adaptation and faster learning for new human-robot interaction tasks with limited additional data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of data-driven robotics, specifically focusing on training robotic manipulation policies through large-scale demonstration datasets. Recent advancements in this area have leveraged extensive datasets, incorporating diversity across robot embodiments, tasks, and even human demonstration videos. These datasets have facilitated the development of generalized Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World Action Models (WAMs) capable of operating across various scenes, robot platforms, and tasks.

However, the authors identify a significant limitation in existing large-scale robot datasets: they are predominantly collected in human-absent settings. In such environments, the robot functions as the sole active agent. This collection paradigm results in robot policies that, while proficient in isolated tasks, lack the ability to exhibit "human-aware behaviors" essential for deployment in shared human environments like homes or factories. These behaviors include coordinating with co-present humans, following cues, anticipating human motions, and avoiding collisions. The paper argues that these behaviors are absent not due to inherent difficulty in learning, but because the necessary interactive demonstrations cannot be captured without a human in the scene (e.g., a robot cannot learn to hand over an object if no recipient is present).

The HABIT dataset directly addresses this gap. It differentiates itself from existing large-scale robot manipulation datasets by explicitly incorporating human presence and interaction. While prior work in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) has explored how robots and humans share roles and tasks, existing HRI datasets often involve either a human teleoperating the robot as a tool or the robot executing pre-scripted behaviors with a reacting human, rather than the robot actively perceiving and responding to an independent human partner. HABIT therefore contributes to the landscape by providing a large-scale, robot-action-paired manipulation dataset where the robot is trained to actively perceive and respond to a human across diverse interactive tasks. It introduces human presence as a new dimension of dataset diversity, moving beyond scene, task, and embodiment variations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that despite significant progress in data-driven robot learning, existing large-scale demonstration datasets predominantly feature robots operating in isolation. This leads to a critical deficiency: robots trained on such data are ill-equipped for environments shared with humans, where human-robot coordination and awareness are paramount. Robots deployed in homes, factories, or other shared workspaces require abilities such as following human cues, anticipating human movements, and avoiding collisions – behaviors that are inherently difficult or impossible to learn from data collected without a co-present human. For instance, a robot cannot learn to safely hand over an object if it has never been demonstrated receiving or giving an object to a human. The absence of such human-aware behaviors constitutes a significant gap in current robot policy capabilities, hindering their practical and safe deployment in real-world human-inhabited settings.

Against this background, the key objectives of this work are:

- **To introduce HABIT (Human-Aware Behavior and Interaction Training dataset):** The central objective is to create a large-scale robot demonstration dataset specifically designed for human-present environments, thereby addressing the aforementioned data gap.
- **To enable the learning of human-aware behaviors:** The dataset aims to facilitate the training of robot policies that can exhibit specific, socially compatible behaviors, including spatiotemporal synchronization with human actions, yielding to humans in shared workspaces, and grounding human gestures as direct cues for robot actions.
- **To demonstrate improved task success rates:** The research aims to show that training on HABIT data leads to higher task success rates in human-robot interaction tasks compared to training on conventional human-absent datasets.
- **To facilitate rapid adaptation to new human-robot interaction tasks:** An objective is to establish HABIT as a valuable prior for fine-tuning, enabling efficient learning and adaptation of robots to novel human-robot interaction scenarios with limited additional data.
- **To promote the development of safe and socially compatible robot policies:** Ultimately, the work aims to contribute a foundational resource for developing robot policies that are not only capable of manipulation but are also genuinely safe and socially intelligent when interacting with humans.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces HABIT, a large-scale robot demonstration dataset comprising 10,563 episodes and 164.19 hours of bimanual manipulation data across 60 tasks. A distinguishing feature of HABIT is the consistent presence of a co-present human in every episode.

#### 3.1. Task Design

Tasks are categorized into three roles, adapted from prior HRI literature, to capture distinct human-robot interaction modes:
- **Collaborator:** Human and robot work together towards a shared goal, often involving direct physical interaction (e.g., object handover, joint holding). This role emphasizes spatial and temporal coordination.
- **Coworker:** Human and robot perform separate tasks within a shared workspace, without direct physical contact. The robot's primary challenge is collision avoidance and yielding.
- **Supervisor:** The human directs the robot through explicit cues like gestures or demonstrated actions, requiring the robot to infer human intent from visual input.
To ensure consistency across demonstrations, tasks are defined by "task workflows"—directed graphs specifying ordered subtasks for both human and robot agents. This minimizes ambiguity and variability in how tasks are executed.

#### 3.2. Workspace Setup

The physical setup includes two Franka Research 3 arms with Robotiq grippers, teleoperated using Meta Quest 3 controllers. A front table serves as the shared workspace, while a side table is designated for human-only activities.
Data capture employs five synchronized RGB cameras:
- Three robot-side cameras: One on each wrist and a central egocentric camera capturing the human and shared workspace. These three streams are used for training robot policies.
- Two human-side cameras: A head-mounted egocentric camera and a third-person view of the entire workspace. These are included in the released dataset for potential future research.

#### 3.3. Data Collection Protocol

The protocol is designed to elicit specific human-aware behaviors and support generalization:
- **Reactive Interaction:** Operators are instructed to act only in direct response to the partner's observable behavior, prohibiting pre-execution or uncaptured coordination signals. This ensures that all cues for robot actions are present in the recorded visual data.
- **Behavior Elicitation:**
 - **Yielding under safety-first priority:** In potential collision scenarios, the robot operator retracts the arms, establishing yielding as the robot's default recorded response.
 - **Temporal Adaptation:** The human operator intentionally varies movement speed across episodes, compelling the robot policy to learn to synchronize its tempo with the human.
 - **Gesture Grounding:** For Supervisor tasks, the human operator varies the wait time before making a pointing gesture, preventing policies from relying solely on language instructions and requiring them to attend to the visual gesture.
- **Additional Diversification:** Conditions such as clothing color, object manipulation order, and human operators with different body types are varied to enhance generalization.

#### 3.4. Dataset Statistics

HABIT contains an average of 3.67 robot and 4.30 human subtasks per episode. Analysis of task workflows reveals that Collaborator and Supervisor tasks are dominated by cross-agent ordering edges (human-robot dependencies), while Coworker tasks feature more single-agent edges (independent actions in a shared space). The dataset encompasses 182 distinct human subtasks, 157 distinct robot subtasks, forming 308 unique human-robot subtask pairs. Episode lengths vary, with a mean of 59.9 seconds.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Framework

Evaluation goes beyond manipulation success to include workflow compliance and human safety. Each robot subtask is scored on three binary criteria:
- **Manipulation:** Successful physical completion and object state achievement.
- **Workflow compliance:** Adherence to task workflow conditions (ordering, synchronization, cue following).
- **Human safety:** Absence of human-robot collision or contact with human-held objects.
A trial is considered successful only if all robot subtasks meet these three criteria.
Six representative tasks (two per role) were selected for evaluation, chosen to highlight role-specific challenges (e.g., tight coordination for Collaborator, collision avoidance for Coworker, gesture interpretation for Supervisor).

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

Two open-source VLA models, π0.5 and GR00T N1.6, were fine-tuned on HABIT's bimanual Franka configuration. A "Robot-only" baseline, using the same task, environment, and teleoperator but without a co-present human, was compared against HABIT-trained models. Training involved 5,000 steps with a batch size of 128. For evaluation tasks, approximately 200 episodes were used per condition. Each (task, model, condition) cell was evaluated over 20 independent trials by the same human operator.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of policies trained on HABIT yielded several findings regarding task success, emergent human-aware behaviors, and sample-efficient adaptation.

#### 4.1. Improved Task Success Rates

- Training the π0.5 model on HABIT consistently improved success rates over the Robot-only baseline across all comparable tasks. The most substantial gains were observed in Coworker tasks, such as Waste Sorting (Robot-only: 40%; HABIT: 95%) and Box Packaging (Robot-only: 45%; HABIT: 95%). These improvements are attributed to the model's ability to learn reactive yielding behavior in shared workspaces, which is critical for resolving path conflicts with a human.
- In Supervisor tasks, HABIT demonstrated gains in tasks requiring more complex gesture grounding, such as Food Storage (π0.5 Robot-only: 65%; HABIT: 85%), where human pointing provided a visual cue at a critical branching point in a two-stage trajectory. For simpler, single-stage Supervisor tasks like Donut Serving, where language instructions alone were often sufficient, the performance of HABIT and Robot-only models was comparable.
- The GR00T N1.6 model exhibited similar trends, with HABIT providing relative benefits over Robot-only, particularly in Coworker tasks, although its absolute success rates were lower than π0.5. This consistency across models suggests the improvements are primarily dataset-driven.

#### 4.2. Emergent Human-Aware Behaviors and Reduced Role-Specific Failures

- Analysis of failure cases revealed that training on HABIT significantly reduced role-specific failures, which are distinct from generic manipulation errors.
- **Collaborator Tasks:** HABIT-trained policies showed a reduction in "precondition violations" (e.g., the robot initiating an action before the human has completed their preceding subtask). For instance, in Shelf Cleaning, π0.5 's precondition violation rate dropped from 15% (Robot-only) to 0% (HABIT).
- **Coworker Tasks:** HABIT led to a notable decrease in collisions with the human or human-held objects. In Waste Sorting, π0.5 's collision rate fell from 60% (Robot-only) to 0% (HABIT).
- **Supervisor Tasks:** Policies trained on HABIT exhibited fewer "gesture-following failures," indicating improved ability to ground human pointing gestures in the visual scene. For Food Storage, π0.5 's gesture-following failure rate decreased from 50% (Robot-only) to 15% (HABIT). These results indicate that HABIT enables models to internalize social context, leading to the emergence of synchronized, yielding, and gesture-following behaviors.

#### 4.3. Sample-Efficient Adaptation to New Tasks

- Mid-training π0.5 on a general subset of HABIT demonstrations, followed by fine-tuning on limited task-specific data, consistently resulted in higher success rates and improved sample efficiency for new human-robot interaction tasks.
- For Waste Sorting, mid-training with only 50 fine-tuning demonstrations achieved a success rate comparable to direct fine-tuning with 200 demonstrations. With 100 demonstrations, mid-training surpassed direct fine-tuning's performance at all tested data budgets.
- In Shelf Cleaning, direct fine-tuning struggled, reaching only 45% success at 200 demonstrations, whereas mid-training with 100 demonstrations achieved 60%. This suggests that HABIT provides a robust prior for learning new HRI tasks.

#### 4.4. OOD Robustness Analysis (Appendix Findings)

- Under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, such as changes in human clothing or body silhouette, HABIT-trained policies experienced only modest performance degradation. This degradation was primarily due to manipulation failures rather than a breakdown of human-aware behaviors like yielding or synchronization, which largely remained stable.
- An exception was noted in Supervisor tasks, where OOD-silhouette conditions led to an increase in gesture-following failures, indicating that grounding pointing gestures may be more sensitive to variations in human appearance. The intentional diversity incorporated during data collection (e.g., varied clothing and operators) is hypothesized to contribute to this robustness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HABIT dataset and its associated findings present several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotics:

- **Addressing a Critical Data Gap:** HABIT directly tackles the deficiency of human-absent datasets by providing a large-scale, structured collection of robot demonstrations in human-present environments. This fills a crucial gap for training robots that operate in shared spaces with humans.
- **Enabling Human-Aware Robot Behaviors:** The research demonstrates that training on HABIT data enables robots to acquire and exhibit specific, essential human-aware behaviors such as spatiotemporal synchronization, reactive yielding, and robust gesture grounding. These behaviors are fundamental for safe, efficient, and socially acceptable human-robot interaction.
- **Improving Robot Task Performance in HRI:** By showing consistent improvements in task success rates over conventional human-absent training data, HABIT provides evidence that human presence is a critical and learnable dimension of data diversity for robot learning.
- **Accelerating HRI Task Learning:** The finding that mid-training on HABIT facilitates sample-efficient adaptation to new human-robot interaction tasks positions it as a valuable prior for developing generalist robot policies. This can significantly reduce the data requirements and development time for deploying robots in novel interactive scenarios.
- **Foundation for Socially Compatible Robots:** HABIT represents a step toward developing robot foundation models that are not merely capable of manipulation but are also "genuinely safe and socially compatible" in human-inhabited environments. This contributes to the broader goal of integrating robots seamlessly and safely into human society.
- **Advancing Research in Multi-Modal Robot Learning:** The release of five synchronized RGB streams (three robot-side and two human-side) provides a rich resource for future research, including investigating the potential of human egocentric and exocentric views to enhance robot understanding of human intent and interaction context.
- **Ethical Considerations and Transparency:** The detailed ethics statement, including consent procedures, privacy protections (e.g., face blurring), and robust data security measures, sets a standard for transparent and responsible data collection involving human participants in robotics research. This emphasis on ethical practices can encourage similar rigor in the development of future human-centric datasets.

In summary, HABIT contributes a foundational dataset and empirical evidence demonstrating the learnability and impact of human presence in robot training, fostering the development of a new generation of robots that are both capable and socially intelligent.
