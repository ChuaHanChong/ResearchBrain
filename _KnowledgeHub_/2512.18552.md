# Toward Training Superintelligent Software Agents through Self-Play SWE-RL

> [!summary] Summary
> Meta FAIR researchers developed Self-play SWE-RL (SSR), a training paradigm for software agents that autonomously generates learning experiences from real-world codebases through a self-play loop. This approach enabled agents to achieve consistent self-improvement and outperform human-data baselines by +10.4 points on SWE-bench Verified and +7.8 points on SWE-Bench Pro, without relying on human-curated issue descriptions or pre-existing tests.

## Problem

- Current LLM-powered software engineering agents heavily depend on human-curated data (e.g., GitHub issues, human-labeled tests), creating a scalability bottleneck.
- This dependency limits agents to refining human development traces, preventing them from discovering novel problems and solutions independently.
- Existing self-play approaches for LLMs often rely on introspection or 'zero' external data, which may not acquire knowledge beyond fixed environmental rules or the model's existing knowledge.

## Method

- Self-play SWE-RL (SSR) employs an iterative, ==adversarial self-play loop== between a ==bug-injection agent== and a bug-solving agent, both instantiated by a single LLM policy.
- The agents operate in sandboxed real-world code repositories with minimal input assumptions, requiring only source code and installed dependencies, without pre-existing tests or natural language issue descriptions.
- Bugs are formally specified by test patches, and the bug-injection agent generates a ==dynamic curriculum== of increasingly complex bugs, including higher-order bugs from failed solver attempts.

## Results

- SSR consistently demonstrated self-improvement throughout training, outperforming human-data baselines by **+10.4 points** on SWE-bench Verified and **+7.8 points** on SWE-Bench Pro.
- The full self-play framework was superior to isolated 'injection-only' or 'repair-only' training variants, highlighting the necessity of the dynamic adversarial loop.
- The 'removal + history' bug-injection strategy yielded the best performance, generating more realistic and diverse bug patterns compared to simpler direct injection or removal-only approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-play enables software agents to achieve autonomous and open-ended learning, circumventing the limitations of human-curated training data.
> - The continuous, evolving curriculum generated through joint bug injection and repair is essential for sustained learning and improvement in software engineering tasks.
> - Bug generation strategies, particularly combining code removal with history-aware reversion, significantly impact the quality of the generated curriculum and solver performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of software engineering agents, particularly those leveraging large language models (LLMs) and reinforcement learning (RL), has seen rapid advancements, contributing to increased programmer productivity. Methods like SWE-RL, DeepSWE, and Code World Model (CWM) represent efforts to enhance LLMs' agentic capabilities in software engineering tasks by utilizing verifiable rewards derived from open software data. Recent LLM releases also emphasize agentic RL, often trained on extensive software engineering datasets and environments.

However, a recurring limitation in these existing approaches is their heavy reliance on human-curated training data and environments, including issue descriptions and pre-existing test cases. This dependence constrains agents primarily to refining or replaying human-generated software development traces, hindering their ability to autonomously discover novel problems, solutions, or achieve open-ended "superintelligent" capabilities. The reliance on human knowledge also poses scalability challenges and introduces potential unreliability in training signals, as evidenced by the need for human-verified evaluation subsets like SWE-bench Verified.

While efforts such as SWE-smith and BugPilot have explored synthetic bug generation using LLMs, they often presuppose stronger human-data assumptions, such as access to test suites and parsers, or depend on static generation pipelines. These methods also suffer from scalability limitations and struggle to generate optimally informative examples as the agent's capabilities evolve.

The concept of self-play, prominently demonstrated by systems like AlphaGo and AlphaZero in game environments, has shown that AI can achieve superhuman performance by learning primarily from explicit rules and self-interaction, rather than human-curated data. This paradigm has recently been extended to more open domains (e.g., Absolute Zero, R-Zero, LSP). While some "zero" self-play methods rely heavily on LLM introspection, they may struggle to acquire knowledge beyond fixed environment rules or the model's initial knowledge. Corpus-grounded self-play, as seen in SPICE, attempts to address this by incorporating interaction with an external corpus for diverse feedback.

This work, Self-play SWE-RL (SSR), positions itself at the intersection of these fields. It aims to bridge the gap by applying the self-play paradigm to software engineering, grounding the learning process in real-world codebases. Unlike previous methods, SSR minimizes human-data assumptions, requiring only access to sandboxed environments with source code and dependencies. It seeks to enable agents to autonomously generate and solve software engineering problems, thereby moving beyond the constraints of human-curated data and static bug generation pipelines to foster continuous self-improvement and potentially achieve higher levels of intelligence in software development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a foundational step towards training "superintelligent" software agents. This involves enabling agents to learn autonomously from real-world software repositories with minimal reliance on human-curated training data. The specific goal of Self-play SWE-RL (SSR) is to train a single LLM agent, through a self-play mechanism, to inject and subsequently repair increasingly complex software bugs. These bugs are formally defined not by natural language issue descriptions, but by test suite improvements proposed by the agent itself.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several limitations observed in current software engineering agents:

- **Fundamental Barrier to Superintelligence:** Existing LLM-powered software agents, despite their advancements, are largely constrained by their dependence on human-curated training data and environments. This reliance creates a ceiling for their potential, preventing them from achieving open-ended learning and capabilities that surpass human expertise in understanding, modifying, or creating software from scratch.
- **Scalability and Reliability Issues of Human Data:** The process of manually annotating or curating software issues, test cases, and environments for training is not indefinitely scalable. Furthermore, human-curated data can be inconsistent or unreliable, necessitating additional human verification (e.g., SWE-bench Verified), which further exacerbates scalability challenges.
- **Limited Learning Scope:** Agents trained on human-provided data predominantly learn to replicate and refine existing human software development patterns. They are less capable of independently discovering novel classes of problems or innovative solutions, which is a prerequisite for advancing beyond human-level performance.
- **Static Nature of Current Synthetic Bug Generation:** While synthetic bug generation methods exist (e.g., SWE-smith, BugPilot), they typically rely on static pipelines. This limits their adaptability, as they cannot dynamically generate maximally informative or challenging examples that evolve in response to the agent's improving skills. This static nature impedes continuous self-improvement.
- **Inspiration from Self-Play Successes:** The success of self-play in domains like Go and chess, where AI systems achieved superhuman performance purely from game rules and self-interaction, motivates its application to complex, open-ended problems like software engineering. The challenge is to adapt this paradigm to a domain where the "rules" are more amorphous and knowledge needs to be acquired from vast, unstructured real-world data, rather than just introspection or a fixed environment.
- **Minimizing Data Assumptions:** To ensure broad applicability and scalability to diverse software projects, a key motivation is to design a training framework that requires minimal prior knowledge about the codebase. This would involve assuming only access to sandboxed environments containing source code and dependencies, without needing existing tests, test commands, or human issue descriptions.

By addressing these motivations, SSR aims to enable agents to autonomously gather extensive learning experiences from real software repositories, thereby laying a groundwork for systems that could eventually exceed human capabilities in various aspects of software engineering.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Self-play SWE-RL (SSR) implements an iterative self-improvement cycle where a single large language model (LLM) agent generates and solves its own bugs.

#### 3.1. Overview of the SSR Framework

The SSR framework operates within sandboxed, containerized environments, each containing a raw codebase and its dependencies. It assumes minimal prior knowledge, requiring no access to existing tests, test runners, issue descriptions, or language-specific infrastructure. The LLM policy is instantiated in two distinct roles via different prompting mechanisms: a bug-injection agent and a bug-solving agent. Both roles utilize a shared set of tools, including Bash and a text editor, adapted from the Code World Model (CWM).

#### 3.2. Input Assumptions

SSR's design principle emphasizes minimizing prior knowledge about the codebase. The system only requires access to Docker images, each encapsulating a source code repository with its dependencies pre-installed. The bug-injection agent is responsible for dynamically discovering how to execute tests, creating test parsers, and understanding the test suite structure purely through environmental interaction.

#### 3.3. Agentic Bug Injection

The bug-injection agent's task is to introduce a bug and formally specify it through a "bug artifact."
- **Bug Artifact Components:**
 1. `test_script.sh`: A Bash script to run the test suite.
 2. `test_files.txt`: A list of oracle test files that are reset to their original state before evaluation.
 3. `test_parser.py`: A Python script to parse raw test output into a JSON format mapping test IDs to results (passed/failed).
 4. `bug_inject.diff`: A Git diff patch that introduces the bug into the source code.
 5. `test_weaken.diff`: A Git diff patch that modifies or weakens existing tests to conceal the injected bug. This patch, when reversed, serves as the formal specification for the solver.
- **Complex Bug Generation Strategies:** To ensure diversity and challenge, the agent employs two strategies for bug generation:
 1. **Code Removal:** The agent is instructed to remove substantial code files or hunks from the codebase. It then performs compatibility fixes to maintain project runnability, ensuring the resulting bug is semantic rather than a trivial syntax error.
 2. **History-Aware Reversion:** The agent leverages Git logs to identify and selectively revert historical code changes. This strategy aims to introduce bugs that mimic real-world scenarios where previous fixes are unintentionally undone.
 Control parameters (e.g., minimum changed files, passing tests, failing tests) are used to manage the quality and complexity of generated bugs.
- **Consistency Validation:** Each bug artifact undergoes a series of execution-based consistency checks to ensure its meaningfulness. These checks include:
 - Verifying the existence and coverage of specified test files.
 - Validating the functionality of `test_parser.py`.
 - Confirming `test_script.sh` runs successfully and produces passing tests on the original codebase.
 - Ensuring `bug_inject.diff` modifies a minimum number of code files.
 - Confirming a minimum number of tests fail after applying `bug_inject.diff`.
 - Verifying some failed tests pass after applying `test_weaken.diff`.
 - Performing inverse mutation testing to ensure each modification in `bug_inject.diff` contributes to the bug.
- **Higher-Order Bugs:** Failed bug-solving attempts by the solver agent are used to create "higher-order bugs." These new bug artifacts are derived from the solver's erroneous states, mimicking how developers might introduce secondary bugs during their development process.
- **Reward Design (r_inject):** The bug-injection agent receives a reward based on the consistency validation outcome and the solver's success rate (`s`). The reward function is designed to incentivize consistent bugs that are neither trivially solvable (s=1) nor impossibly difficult (s=0).
 - -1.0 if consistency validation fails.
 - -α (penalty, α=0.8) if s=0 or s=1.
 - 1 - (1 + α)s if 0 < s < 1 (optimizing for an ideal difficulty). This reward structure, adapted from previous work, promotes the generation of challenging yet solvable problems.

#### 3.4. Agentic Bug Repair

If a bug artifact passes consistency validation, the solver agent initiates the repair process.
- **Constructing the Buggy Codebase:** The solver's environment is prepared by:
 1. Applying `bug_inject.diff` to the original codebase.
 2. Applying `test_weaken.diff` to hide the bug from existing tests.
 3. (For higher-order bugs) Applying the previous failed prediction patch.
 4. Reinitializing the Git repository (removing `.git` and creating a fresh commit) to prevent information leakage from history.
- **Initial Prompt:** The solver agent receives an initial prompt containing the reversed `test_weaken.diff`. This diff serves as a formal specification of the desired behavior, indicating which tests should now pass. Natural language issue descriptions are explicitly avoided.
- **Bug-Repair Process:** The agent interacts with the buggy codebase using its tools (Bash, editor) and reasoning capabilities to produce a final prediction patch.
- **Evaluation of Predicted Patches:** The correctness of the solver's patch is evaluated by:
 1. Tagging the original codebase as `ssr-original`.
 2. Applying `bug_inject.diff` and `test_weaken.diff` to create the buggy state.
 3. Applying the solver's `pred_patch.diff`.
 4. Restoring the oracle test files (from `test_files.txt`) from the `ssr-original` tag.
 5. Executing `test_script.sh` and parsing the output with `test_parser.py` to determine if all specified tests pass.
- **Reward Design (r_solve):** The bug-solving agent receives a binary reward: +1 if all tests pass after applying its patch, and -1 otherwise. Failed attempts contribute to the generation of higher-order bugs for subsequent training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SSR was conducted using the Code World Model (CWM) pre-RL checkpoint, CWM-sft (32B parameters), as the base model. The same LLM instance was used for both bug generation and repair, with shared policy weights updated during reinforcement learning. Evaluation was performed on two benchmarks: SWE-bench Verified (500 human-verified issues) and SWE-Bench Pro (731 complex public problems). Resolve rates were measured after one attempt per problem, with temperature 1.0 and top-p 0.95.

#### 4.1. Baseline Comparison

SSR was compared against the base model (CWM-sft) and a "human-data" baseline RL. The human-data baseline RL was trained with human-authored issue descriptions and corresponding test suites for reward computation, similar to CWM's agentic SWE-RL training. SSR, in contrast, operated with only raw environment images, requiring self-discovery of problems and validation.
- SSR demonstrated consistent self-improvement throughout its training trajectory on both benchmarks, despite lacking access to task-specific training data.
- SSR consistently surpassed the human-data baseline RL, achieving a +10.4 point improvement on SWE-bench Verified and a +7.8 point improvement on SWE-Bench Pro over the entire training duration.
- These results suggest that self-generated tasks can provide richer and more effective learning signals than human-engineered data, enabling LLMs to improve software engineering abilities purely through interaction with codebases.

#### 4.2. Ablation of Self-Play Components

To understand the contribution of self-play, SSR was compared with two variants: an injection-only variant and a repair-only variant. Resolve rates were averaged over 1231 tasks from both benchmarks.
- **Self-play (SSR)**: Achieved the highest resolve rate (38.0%), indicating that joint training of both bug generation and repair roles is most effective.
- **Repair-only**: Achieved a resolve rate of 33.5%. This variant trained the agent solely on solving bugs derived from valid, solvable, and deduplicated bugs generated in prior self-play runs. Its inferiority to full self-play suggests that the fixed, non-evolving distribution of tasks limits continuous improvement.
- **Injection-only**: Achieved the lowest resolve rate (25.2%). Training the agent solely on bug generation, without feedback from repair attempts, resulted in performance degradation.
- The findings highlight that an online and evolving process of both bug generation and bug solving is crucial for sustained improvement, as it continually expands the training signal and exposes the model to new failure modes.

#### 4.3. Ablation of Bug Injection Methods

The impact of different bug-injection strategies on downstream performance was investigated.
- **Removal + history**: Achieved the best performance (38.0%). This strategy involved randomly sampling between code hunk removal and reverting historical code changes. It was found to incorporate more realistic and diverse bug patterns.
- **Removal-only**: Achieved a resolve rate of 36.0%. This strategy, focusing on removing substantial code hunks, generated stronger bugs that required the solver to reconstruct functionality, thereby deepening its understanding of repository structure.
- **Direct-injection**: Achieved the lowest performance (35.4%). This naive prompting approach often led to trivial bugs characterized by superficial one-line modifications, providing weak learning signals.
- These results indicate that while SSR is effective across various injection strategies, the careful design of bug-injection mechanisms is important for creating a challenging and instructive curriculum.

#### 4.4. Reward Ablation (Solver Feedback in Bug-Injection)

The contribution of solver feedback to the bug-injection reward was examined.
- **With feedback**: Achieved a resolve rate of 38.0%. This variant incorporated the bug's solve rate into the bug-injection reward, incentivizing the generation of bugs with optimal difficulty.
- **No feedback**: Achieved a resolve rate of 35.3%. This variant used a binary reward for bug injection, based solely on whether the generated bug passed consistency checks, ignoring the solver's performance.
- The study found that solver feedback provided only a slight and largely negligible advantage. The hypothesis for this outcome is that the solve-rate feedback might be weak and noisy, making it challenging for the bug-injector to precisely learn factors affecting solve rates from a single scalar value. However, the continuous online joint learning inherent in SSR ensures that the bug-injection agent's evolving policy still produces an adaptive curriculum, even without explicit solver performance feedback in its reward signal.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Self-play SWE-RL (SSR) represents a notable contribution to the field of AI-driven software engineering, particularly in its approach to agent training and development.

#### 5.1. Significance of Findings

- **Advancement Towards Superintelligence**: The work establishes a preliminary framework for training software agents that can learn autonomously, potentially moving towards capabilities that exceed human performance in understanding, modifying, and creating software. This is articulated as a first step toward "superintelligent" software agents.
- **Reduced Reliance on Human-Curated Data**: SSR demonstrates that LLMs can achieve consistent self-improvement in software engineering tasks without depending on human-curated issue descriptions or pre-existing test suites. This addresses a fundamental scalability bottleneck in current agent training paradigms.
- **Autonomous Skill Acquisition**: The system enables agents to autonomously discover and specify problems, formulate solutions, and validate them through self-play, purely by interacting with raw codebases. This contrasts with traditional methods that often confine agents to refining human-generated tasks.
- **Dynamic and Evolving Curriculum**: The self-play mechanism allows for the generation of an evolving curriculum of diverse and challenging bugs. This online generation process naturally adapts to the agent's improving capabilities, which static bug generation pipelines cannot achieve.
- **Insights into Self-Play Design**: The ablation studies provide empirical evidence regarding the importance of specific self-play components, such as the necessity of joint bug generation and repair, and the quality of bug injection strategies for effective learning signals.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Scalability of AI Development for Software Engineering**: By minimizing the need for expensive and labor-intensive human data curation, SSR could significantly reduce the cost and improve the scalability of training advanced software engineering agents. This opens avenues for applying AI to a wider range of software projects where labeled data is scarce or frequently outdated.
- **Enhanced Automated Debugging and Maintenance**: The ability of agents to autonomously generate and resolve complex bugs could lead to more robust and effective automated debugging tools and maintenance systems. This could particularly benefit large-scale, continuously evolving software projects.
- **Foundational Research for Generalist Agents**: The approach contributes to the broader goal of creating generalist AI agents capable of understanding and interacting with complex, open-ended environments. The insights gained from grounding self-play in real-world code repositories could be applicable to other domains requiring deep environmental interaction.
- **Future Directions and Research Pathways**: The paper outlines several promising research directions, including:
 - **Addressing limitations**: Exploring hidden/public test splits to prevent reward hacking, moving beyond unit tests to higher-level goal-based verification, and investigating Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models or separate policies for different roles.
 - **Improving Stability and Scale**: Addressing training instability observed at larger scales to unlock the full potential of self-play.
 - **Advanced Bug Generation**: Implementing distribution control techniques, such as seeding, to guide bug injection towards greater diversity.
 - **Complex Multi-step Tasks**: Developing new scaffolding designs (e.g., multi-context rollouts, automated context compaction) to enable agents to tackle long-horizon, multi-step software development workflows.
 - **Efficient Training Paradigms**: Exploring methods for dense and structured feedback beyond scalar rewards to address the inefficiencies of sparse rewards in long-horizon RL tasks.

#### 5.3. Ethical Considerations and Risks

The paper acknowledges a critical ethical consideration: the capability to intentionally generate bugs and weaken tests, while beneficial for training, also presents risks. This functionality could be misused to create vulnerable or misleading code artifacts. The authors emphasize the necessity of careful sandboxing, responsible deployment practices, and adherence to ethical guidelines to ensure the safe and constructive application of this technology.

Overall, SSR represents a significant step towards enabling AI systems to autonomously learn and master software engineering skills, potentially transforming how software is understood, maintained, and created.
