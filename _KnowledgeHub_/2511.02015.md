# Stein-based Optimization of Sampling Distributions in Model Predictive Path Integral Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Michigan introduce Stein-Optimized Path-Integral Inference (SOPPI), an algorithm that integrates Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) into Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control by applying online, per-time-step SVGD updates within the action space. This approach improves action sampling, achieves lower state mean squared errors and faster settling times in a cart-pole swing-up, reduces pushing errors for a robot arm even with noisy gradients, and enables longer walking durations and stair climbing for a bipedal walker.

## Problem

- Traditional Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control assumes unimodal (Gaussian) action distributions, leading to suboptimal control when optimal action distributions are multi-modal or sparsely distributed.
- MPPI struggles with the curse of dimensionality in high-degree-of-freedom and non-linear systems, necessitating an exponentially larger number of samples to maintain effectiveness.
- Prior integrations of Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) with MPC/MPPI suffer from issues like loss of kernel meaning in high-dimensional trajectory spaces, high computational costs from propagating gradients over an entire horizon, and vulnerability to gradient instability.

## Method

- The Stein-Optimized Path-Integral Inference (SOPPI) algorithm embeds Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) into the ==MPPI== framework, focusing on optimizing the action sampling distribution online and at each time step.
- SOPPI applies SVGD updates to the actions for individual time steps within the prediction horizon, utilizing only the immediate running cost for gradient computation, rather than optimizing over entire trajectories.
- Candidate actions are iteratively refined using SVGD before rolling out the system dynamics for each step, and the final control sequence is derived through standard MPPI weighting based on accumulated trajectory costs.

## Results

- SOPPI significantly improved state mean squared errors and achieved faster settling times for a cart-pole swing-up task, with SOPPI using 500 particles outperforming baseline MPPI with 1,000 particles.
- In a 7-DOF robot arm pushing task, SOPPI demonstrated lower mean steady-state distance errors and maintained performance effectively even when Gaussian noise was injected into gradients, showing robustness where other methods degraded significantly.
- The algorithm enabled significantly longer average walking times for a 6-DOF planar bipedal walker compared to other methods and successfully navigated unmodeled stairs, showcasing its effectiveness in unstable and out-of-distribution environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Applying SVGD updates in the lower-dimensional action space of individual time steps, instead of across the entire trajectory, maintains the meaningfulness of the SVGD kernel and reduces computational burden and gradient instability.
> - Dynamically optimizing action noise distributions with SVGD enables MPPI to effectively capture multi-modal optimal action distributions, leading to more robust and accurate control than unimodal assumptions.
> - The repulsive forces inherent in SVGD improve particle efficiency, allowing SOPPI to achieve comparable or superior performance with fewer samples and enhancing its robustness to noisy or imperfect dynamics gradients.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of optimal control for robotics, specifically focusing on Model Predictive Control (MPC) methods. MPC is a widely used framework that optimizes trajectories based on an explicit model of the system. Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control, a subset of MPC, is an information-theoretic, nonparametric, sampling-based approach to stochastic trajectory optimization. MPPI algorithms generate multiple sample trajectories, evaluate their costs, and then weight them to minimize a cost function, using these weights to update the control sequence.

Traditional MPPI implementations commonly assume that the action distributions from which trajectories are sampled are unimodal and can be represented by Gaussian distributions. This assumption can lead to suboptimal trajectory predictions due to insufficient sample coverage, particularly when the true optimal distribution is multi-modal. Furthermore, for constrained, high-degree-of-freedom (DOF) non-linear systems, MPPI can be susceptible to the curse of dimensionality, necessitating an exponentially larger number of samples to maintain effectiveness.

Concurrently, Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD), or Stein Variational Inference, has gained traction in gradient-based optimization. SVGD employs a kernel function to encourage particles to spread out in the sample space, thereby improving sample efficiency and preventing mode collapse, which is beneficial for capturing multi-modal distributions. Attempts to integrate SVGD with MPC (e.g., Stein MPC) and MPPI (e.g., SVG-MPPI) have been made. However, existing methods face challenges. In strict MPC implementations, the kernel in SVGD can lose meaning when applied across high-dimensional spaces, long horizons, and numerous samples, as it struggles to differentiate particles effectively. Additionally, the recursive updating of the Stein component through cost functions and dynamics over an entire horizon can introduce significant computational overhead. When differentiable simulations are required for SVGD's gradient computations, these methods can also be vulnerable to issues such as exploding or vanishing gradients and increased memory requirements. Some prior integrations with MPPI have consequently simplified systems, used velocity-input models, limited particle subsets, or omitted kernel terms, potentially reducing SVGD's benefits.

This paper addresses these limitations by proposing a novel integration of SVGD into MPPI. It aims to develop a method that leverages the advantages of SVGD for multi-modal sampling and particle efficiency within MPPI, while mitigating the computational and stability issues encountered by previous integration attempts, particularly for complex robotic platforms. The work positions itself as an advancement towards robust, real-time MPPI for higher-dimensional systems, acknowledging the challenges of differentiable simulation and the need for control strategies that can perform with imperfect dynamics gradients.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce Stein-Optimized Path-Integral Inference (SOPPI), an algorithm designed to enhance Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control by incorporating Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) updates. The core motivation is to improve the generation of action samples towards an optimal trajectory, thereby addressing fundamental limitations of traditional MPPI and existing SVGD-MPC/MPPI integration methods.

Specifically, the key objectives and motivations are:

1. **Overcome unimodal sampling limitations of MPPI:** Traditional MPPI assumes action distributions are Gaussian and unimodal. This assumption can lead to suboptimal control, especially in scenarios where the true optimal action distribution is multi-modal (e.g., multiple valid ways to achieve a goal) or sparsely distributed. SOPPI is motivated by the need to dynamically update noise distributions at runtime to better capture these complex, potentially multi-modal, action sampling distributions.

2. **Improve sample efficiency and mitigate the curse of dimensionality:** For high-degree-of-freedom (DOF) and non-linear systems, MPPI requires an increasing number of samples to adequately explore the action space and maintain effectiveness, making it susceptible to the "curse of dimensionality." The authors aim to leverage SVGD's ability to prevent mode collapse and encourage particle spread, thereby increasing particle efficiency and allowing MPPI to function effectively with fewer samples.

3. **Address computational and stability issues of existing SVGD-MPC integrations:** Previous methods that combine SVGD with MPC or MPPI have encountered several challenges:
 - **Loss of kernel meaning in high dimensions:** When SVGD kernels are applied over entire trajectories (high-dimensional spaces) or long horizons, their effectiveness in driving particles apart can diminish, as distance metrics become less meaningful.
 - **High computational cost:** Propagating gradients through an entire control horizon for SVGD updates can significantly increase computation time.
 - **Vulnerability to gradient issues:** When differentiable simulations are used for gradient computations, these methods can be susceptible to exploding or vanishing gradients, and increased memory requirements.

 SOPPI's design is specifically motivated to circumvent these issues by performing SVGD updates *online* and *within the action space of individual time steps*, rather than post-rollout or across the entire trajectory. This approach aims to maintain the meaningfulness of the SVGD kernel, reduce the computational burden of gradient propagation, and mitigate the impact of gradient instability.

4. **Realize MPPI methods suitable for complex robotic platforms:** The ultimate motivation is to develop MPPI methods that are robust and computationally feasible for real-time inference on advanced robotic systems, such as humanoid robots, which often involve high DOF, complex dynamics, and the need to navigate dynamic, uncertain environments. The ability to handle multi-modal action distributions and operate efficiently with a reasonable number of particles is crucial for such applications.

5. **Enhance robustness to noisy or imperfect dynamics gradients:** Given that real-world systems and even complex simulations often involve imperfect or learned dynamics, and that differentiable simulators can introduce noise or instability in gradients, SOPPI is motivated to provide a control solution that performs well despite these challenges.

In summary, the work aims to create a more robust, efficient, and versatile MPPI algorithm by strategically incorporating SVGD to improve action sampling, specifically by addressing the limitations of unimodal assumptions and the practical challenges of integrating gradient-based optimization into trajectory planning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Stein-Optimized Path-Integral Inference (SOPPI), integrates Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) into the Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control framework. The approach focuses on optimizing the action sampling distribution online, at each time step, rather than optimizing the entire trajectory or post-rollout.

**Foundational Algorithms:**

1. **Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI):** The paper builds upon the standard MPPI derivation. For a discrete-time system $x_{t+1} = F(x_t, v_t)$ with state $x_t$ and controls $v_t$, MPPI aims to find an optimal control trajectory $U^* = (u_0..., u_{T-1})$ that minimizes an expected cost-to-go $S(\tau)$, which includes running costs $L(x_t, v_t)$ and a terminal cost $\phi(x_T)$. Traditionally, MPPI generates $K$ sample trajectories by perturbing an initial guess control sequence $u$ with zero-mean Gaussian noise $\epsilon$. The costs $S_k$ for these trajectories are computed, and then weights $w_k$ are derived using an exponential function of the negative cost, normalized by a temperature parameter $\lambda$. The new optimal control $u^*$ is computed as a weighted sum of the nominal control and the perturbations. The first control of $u^*$ is applied, and the process repeats.

2. **Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD):** SVGD is an iterative particle-based optimization method that minimizes KL-Divergence between a set of particles and a target distribution. For a set of particles $\{\theta_i\}_{i=1}^K$, the update rule is $\theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i + \epsilon\phi^*(\theta_i)$, where $\epsilon$ is a step size. The optimal perturbation $\hat{\phi}^*(\theta)$ for a particle $\theta$ is approximated by a sum over all particles. This sum consists of two terms: a scaled gradient log-likelihood of the particle's posterior (driving particles towards optimal states) and a repulsive force derived from the kernel (preventing mode collapse and improving sample coverage). The paper uses a standard Radial-Basis Function (RBF) kernel $k(v_j, v) = \exp(-\frac{||v_j - v||^2}{2\sigma^2})$.

**SOPPI Algorithm (Algorithm 1):**

SOPPI's distinct approach involves applying SVGD updates *online* during the MPPI rollout process, specifically to the actions generated at each individual time step.

1. **Initial Sampling:** At the beginning of each MPPI iteration, $K$ sample trajectories are generated by perturbing an initial control sequence $U_{init}$ with Gaussian noise $\epsilon$. This creates a set of candidate action sequences $v$.

2. **Online SVGD Updates per Time Step:**
 - For each of the $K$ sampled trajectories, and for each time step $t$ within the prediction horizon $N$:
 - The algorithm performs $M$ SVGD updates on the *current action* $v_t^k$ for that specific trajectory and time step.
 - **Cost for SVGD:** Instead of the full trajectory cost, the SVGD update uses the immediate running cost $L(x_{t+1}^k, v_t^k)$ (Equation 1) for the current step. The cost-likelihood function $L_s(x_t, v_t) = \exp(-\alpha L(x_t, v_t))$ is defined, making the gradient of the log-likelihood for SVGD simply the negative scaled gradient of this running cost.
 - **Kernel Application:** The RBF kernel $k(v_j^t, v^t)$ is applied to the actions at time step $t$ across all samples $j$.
 - **Action Update:** The SVGD perturbation $\hat{\phi}^* (v)$ is computed using the single-step cost and the kernel, and applied to update the action $v_t^k$. This means the candidate actions are refined iteratively *before* the dynamics are rolled out for that specific step.
 - After $M$ SVGD updates, the system dynamics $F(x_t^k, v_t^k)$ are rolled out to get $x_{t+1}^k$, and the running cost is accumulated into $S_k$.

3. **Trajectory Cost Accumulation:** After iterating through all time steps $t$ for all trajectories, the terminal cost $\phi(x_N^k)$ is added to each $S_k$.

4. **MPPI Control Update:** The final control sequence $u^*$ for the current environment step is computed using the MPPI weighting scheme (Equations 4 and 5) based on the total accumulated costs $S_k$ for each trajectory. The first action of this $u^*$ is applied to the real system.

5. **Horizon Shifting:** The control sequence $U_{init}$ is then shifted for the next environment step (e.g., $U_{init} \leftarrow [u^*_{1:N}, 0]$).

**Key Methodological Advantages:**

- **Localized Optimization:** By applying SVGD per time-step in the action space, SOPPI reduces the dimensionality of the SVGD problem. This helps prevent the kernel from losing meaning in high-dimensional trajectory spaces, enhancing its ability to capture multi-modal distributions.
- **Reduced Gradient Propagation:** Gradients are computed only for a single time step's cost, mitigating issues of exploding/vanishing gradients and significantly reducing computational load compared to propagating gradients over the entire horizon.
- **Online Adaptation:** The online updates allow the action distribution to adapt dynamically to the environment and system state at each planning step, potentially leading to more responsive and robust control.
- **Sequential Sub-problems:** The approach effectively decomposes the complex trajectory optimization into a series of smaller, more manageable sub-problems, improving sampling efficiency.

**Implementation and Evaluation:**

- **Software:** All simulations were conducted using PyTorch.
- **Dynamics:** Analytical dynamics were used for the cart-pole. For more complex systems (robot arm, bipedal walker), recurrent neural networks (RNNs) were trained to approximate system dynamics from MuJoCo simulations for gradient computations. MuJoCo itself was used for rollouts in the walker task.
- **Comparative Algorithms:** SOPPI's performance was compared against baseline MPPI, Stein MPC, and SVG-MPPI.
- **Experimental Tasks:**
 - **Cart-pole swing-up:** Analyzed mean squared error (MSE) and settling times for position and angle, varying particle counts and horizon.
 - **7-DOF robot arm pushing task:** Evaluated steady-state distance errors. Included experiments with injected Gaussian noise into gradients to test robustness to uncertainty.
 - **6-DOF planar bipedal walker:** Assessed stable "walking time" using a cost structure that referenced a gait learned by Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Tested performance in an unknown disturbance environment (climbing stairs).
- **Metrics:** Performance was quantified using metrics such as Mean Squared Error (MSE), settling times, mean/standard deviation of end distance error, and mean/median walking time. Statistical significance was evaluated using Welch's t-test.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated SOPPI against baseline MPPI, Stein MPC, and SVG-MPPI across three robotic tasks: a cart-pole swing-up, a 7-DOF robot arm pushing a block, and a 6-DOF planar bipedal walker.

**Cart-Pole Swing-Up Task:**
- **Performance Improvement:** SOPPI demonstrated a statistically significant improvement (at the 95% level) in state mean squared errors (MSE) for both cart position (x) and pole angle (θ) compared to MPPI and other methods, even when operating with fewer particles (e.g., SOPPI with 500 particles outperformed MPPI with 1,000 particles).
- **Convergence:** SOPPI achieved faster settling times for the pole angle within wider bounds (5% and 10% of the range). For instance, it converged faster than other algorithms in these wider bounds, as visually supported by faster convergence to steady state.
- **Multi-modal Action Distribution:** Visualizations of action distributions (Figure 2) indicated that SOPPI maintained a multi-modal distribution where appropriate, covering a wider action space and avoiding suboptimal single-mode averages. This property was observed to contribute to its improved performance, particularly in maintaining balance.
- **Trade-offs:** For very tight angle settling times (2%), SOPPI did not perform as well, which the authors attributed to minor "chattering" caused by the bimodal distribution maintaining an unstable equilibrium. In terms of cart position settling time, SVG-MPPI sometimes converged faster, but at the expense of slower angle balance and higher overall x tracking error, which was attributed to SVG-MPPI's propensity to favor heavier, single-directional swings due to its unimodal nature.
- **Particle Efficiency:** The results supported the claim that SOPPI can increase particle efficiency, achieving comparable or better performance with fewer samples than other algorithms.

**Arm Pushing Task:**
- **Pushing Accuracy:** SOPPI achieved lower mean steady-state distance errors in the pushing direction compared to MPPI and other methods, both in standard conditions and with injected Gaussian noise in the gradients.
- **Robustness to Noise:** When noise was added to the gradients, Stein MPC and SVG-MPPI showed significant performance degradation, often resulting in overshoots that were unrecoverable. In contrast, SOPPI's performance changed minimally, indicating better handling of uncertain or noisy gradients.
- **Cautious Behavior:** Both SOPPI and MPPI exhibited more cautious control, preventing overshoots that would render the task unrecoverable for the robot arm. Errors in the lateral direction and desired angle were largely similar across most algorithms, with MPPI showing a slightly larger lateral error.

**2D Walker Task:**
- **Walking Stability and Duration:** SOPPI trials resulted in significantly longer average walking times compared to MPPI, Stein MPC, and SVG-MPPI, with p-values indicating statistical significance (e.g., 0.023 for MPPI, 0.0016 for Stein MPC, and 0.0047 for SVG-MPPI at the 95% level). This suggested improved stability and ability to maintain an upright position.
- **Challenge for Horizon-Based Optimization:** Stein MPC and SVG-MPPI were largely unsuccessful in this task. The chaotic nature of the 6-DOF walker and accumulated errors from the learned dynamics, combined with their horizon-based optimization, prevented them from optimizing successfully. SOPPI's single-step optimization mechanism proved more effective in this unstable environment.
- **Handling Unknown Disturbances:** SOPPI was the only algorithm demonstrated to successfully perform in an out-of-distribution environment—climbing previously unseen stairs—an environment not included in any reference trajectory or trained dynamics. This further highlighted its ability to optimize in unstable and unmodeled scenarios.
- **Variance in Results:** A large variance in walking times was observed across all algorithms, which the authors attributed to the inherently chaotic and unstable nature of the walker system and potentially high sampling requirements for perfect stability.

In summary, the results consistently indicate that SOPPI improves system performance and/or particle efficiency across diverse robotic tasks. Its online, per-time-step SVGD updates enable it to better capture action distributions, handle gradient noise, and operate more robustly in unstable or unmodeled environments compared to established MPPI variants and other Stein-based MPC methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Stein-Optimized Path-Integral Inference (SOPPI) represents an advancement in Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control, offering a method to enhance its performance and applicability in complex robotic systems. The work's significance lies in its strategic integration of Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD) into the MPPI framework, specifically by applying SVGD updates online and at each time step within the action space.

**Key Significance:**

1. **Addressing MPPI's Sampling Limitations:** SOPPI directly addresses the fundamental limitation of traditional MPPI, which often assumes unimodal (Gaussian) action distributions. By dynamically optimizing the noise distributions with SVGD, SOPPI can better capture multi-modal optimal action distributions, leading to more effective and robust control solutions.
2. **Improved Particle Efficiency:** The repulsive forces inherent in SVGD help to prevent mode collapse and encourage a more comprehensive exploration of the action space. This results in improved particle efficiency, allowing SOPPI to achieve comparable or superior performance with fewer samples compared to other MPPI methods, thereby mitigating the "curse of dimensionality" in high-degree-of-freedom systems.
3. **Enhanced Robustness to Gradient Instability and Noise:** By performing SVGD updates in the lower-dimensional action space of individual time steps, SOPPI reduces the length of gradient propagation paths. This design choice effectively mitigates issues such as exploding or vanishing gradients and preserves the meaningfulness of the SVGD kernel in high-dimensional problems. The experimental results, particularly in the pushing task with noisy gradients and the walker task with learned dynamics, demonstrate SOPPI's increased resilience to imperfect or uncertain dynamics models, which is crucial for real-world applications.
4. **Feasibility for Complex, Unstable Systems:** SOPPI's ability to maintain performance in chaotic and unstable environments, as demonstrated by the bipedal walker, and its capacity to handle unknown disturbances, such as climbing unmodeled stairs, highlights its potential for controlling complex robotic platforms that operate in uncertain or dynamic real-world settings. This contrasts with other SVGD-MPC integrations that struggled with such systems.

**Potential Impact:**

1. **Enabling Real-Time Control for Advanced Robotics:** The improved performance and particle efficiency of SOPPI, coupled with its robustness, suggest its potential to enable real-time robust inference for more complex robotic platforms, such as humanoid robots. These systems demand efficient and reliable control strategies that can handle high dimensionality, non-linearity, and dynamic environments.
2. **Reducing Computational Resource Requirements:** By achieving better results with lower particle counts, SOPPI can potentially reduce the computational resources (e.g., GPU processing, memory) required for effective MPPI control. This could make advanced model predictive control more accessible for robots with limited onboard computation.
3. **Bridging Simulation-to-Real Gap:** The demonstrated robustness to noisy and imperfect gradients is significant for real-world robotics. Learned dynamics models or simplified analytical models used in simulation often differ from reality. SOPPI's ability to perform well despite these discrepancies could facilitate a smoother transition from simulated development to physical robot deployment.
4. **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays the groundwork for several future research directions, including:
 - **Integration with Advanced Differentiable Simulators:** Leveraging emerging differentiable simulators (e.g., NVIDIA Warp, Brax) could further streamline gradient computation and potentially enhance SOPPI's performance.
 - **Explicit Multi-modal Search:** Moving beyond implicit multi-modal capture to explicitly identify and optimize modes could further improve sample efficiency.
 - **Deployment on High-Dimensional Real-World Systems:** Implementing SOPPI on platforms like Agility Robotics Digit or Unitree G1 to demonstrate its practical utility in real-world scenarios.
 - **Complex Behavioral Control:** Extending SOPPI to manage multiple gaits or behaviors (e.g., standing, walking, running) and enable dynamic switching between them, which would have substantial implications for versatile robotic autonomy.

In essence, SOPPI offers a more practical and effective approach to leveraging the strengths of both MPPI and SVGD, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable in sampling-based optimal control for complex and uncertain robotic applications.
