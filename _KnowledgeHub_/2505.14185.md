# Safety Subspaces are Not Linearly Distinct: A Fine-Tuning Case Study

> [!summary] Summary
> The study empirically demonstrates that safety-relevant behaviors in Large Language Models are deeply entangled with general learning components, showing that "safety subspaces" derived from alignment updates primarily act as general sensitivity axes that amplify both beneficial and harmful behaviors across five open-source LLMs.

## Problem

- Large Language Model safety alignment is fragile, with subsequent fine-tuning often degrading safety properties and reintroducing undesirable behaviors.
- The field lacks clarity on whether safety alignment corresponds to specific, linearly distinct "subspaces" in the model's weight or activation space.
- Current subspace-based defense strategies rely on the unverified assumption that safety-encoding information can be isolated or preserved.

## Method

- Conducted four empirical experiments analyzing the geometry of safety-related behavior using ==Singular Value Decomposition== (SVD) and Mode Subspace Overlap (MSO).
- Applied various fine-tuning scenarios (useful, harmful, contaminated) to five open-source LLMs (Llama and Qwen families) and evaluated impacts on utility and harmfulness.
- Investigated both weight space (via projection of updates) and activation space (via MSO of activation patterns from different prompts).

## Results

- Projections onto "safety subspaces" uniformly increased both utility from useful fine-tuning and harmfulness from harmful fine-tuning, reflecting general parameter sensitivity.
- Orthogonal projections designed to remove harmful components failed to selectively reduce harmfulness, diminishing utility at a similar rate.
- Mode Subspace Overlap analysis consistently showed the strongest overlap between useful and harmful task updates in weight space, and between useful and harmful prompt activations, contradicting linear separability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - "Safety subspaces" derived from alignment updates are not unique to safety, but rather represent general axes of parameter sensitivity that amplify both beneficial and harmful behaviors.
> - Efforts to remove components within these purported safety subspaces via orthogonal projection do not selectively suppress harmfulness, but instead reduce utility at a comparable rate.
> - Useful and harmful learning, as well as activations from useful and harmful prompts, largely share overlapping high-energy subspaces, suggesting a general learning geometry rather than distinct safety-specific regions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) are developed with safety alignment mechanisms to ensure their responses are socially acceptable and free from harmful content. This alignment process typically occurs during the post-training phase, following initial pre-training. Despite these efforts, a significant challenge in the field is the observed brittleness of safety alignment. Subsequent fine-tuning, even with seemingly benign or lightly contaminated data, has been shown to degrade safety properties and reintroduce undesirable behaviors. This phenomenon creates an attack surface, where adversaries can compromise model safety through data manipulation rather than just prompt engineering. Prior research has demonstrated this vulnerability across various LLMs, including open-source and proprietary models, and has highlighted that even low-rank adaptation or pruning can compromise safety.

In response to this vulnerability, a growing body of research has explored the hypothesis that safety alignment corresponds to specific, identifiable directions or "subspaces" within the model's weight space. The premise is that if these safety-encoding subspaces could be isolated or preserved, they might offer a defense mechanism against misalignment during continued training. Previous works have attempted to derive these purported safety subspaces using weight updates from general alignment (difference between aligned and base models) or targeted safety tuning (difference between safety-tuned and base models).

This paper addresses a fundamental question within this context: it empirically investigates whether these proposed "safety subspaces" truly contain information that is uniquely specific to safety. It questions the assumption that safety is linearly separable from general-purpose learning and seeks to determine if harmful behaviors manifest in distinguishable patterns within weight or activation spaces that are distinct from useful learning components. By rigorously testing this assumption, the work aims to provide clarity on the feasibility and limitations of subspace-based defense strategies for preserving LLM safety.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct a comprehensive empirical study to determine whether safety-relevant behavior in Large Language Models (LLMs) is concentrated in specific linear subspaces, either in the model's weight space or activation space. This overarching goal is motivated by the observed fragility of LLM safety alignment during fine-tuning and the prevailing hypothesis that such alignment might reside in isolatable directions.

The research aims to answer several specific questions:
1. **Specificity of Safety Subspaces**: To ascertain if "safety subspaces," typically derived from alignment or safety-specific weight updates, are uniquely tied to safety-relevant information. The motivation is to test whether these subspaces primarily encode safety, or if they capture general learning components that affect both useful and harmful behaviors.
2. **Separability from General Learning**: To investigate whether safety alignment can be effectively separated from the model's general-purpose learning mechanisms. If safety is distinct, it might be possible to preserve it while allowing for task-specific fine-tuning without degradation.
3. **Distinguishability of Harmfulness in Activations**: To determine if prompts with different safety implications (e.g., useful vs. harmful) activate distinct or overlapping representations within the model's internal activation space. This aims to explore whether, even if weight-space separability is not found, safety-relevant processing manifests differently at the activation level.
4. **Limitations of Subspace-Based Defenses**: To evaluate the fundamental limitations of current subspace-based defense strategies that rely on the assumption of linear separability for isolating or filtering safety-critical components.

The motivation for these objectives stems from the practical and technical challenge of preserving LLM safety under continued training, especially in scenarios involving fine-tuning for new domains or personalized applications. If a unique, isolatable safety subspace were to exist, it could enable robust defense mechanisms, allowing for the removal of unsafe information through simple projections while retaining useful knowledge and model performance. This work critically examines this foundational premise to guide the development of more effective and principled strategies for LLM alignment and safety.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a series of four empirical experiments, utilizing Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) and Mode Subspace Overlap (MSO) to analyze the geometry of safety-related behavior across both model weights and activations.

**Models and Datasets:**
The study used five open-source LLMs: Llama 3.2 1B, Llama 2 7B, Qwen-2.5 1B, Qwen-2.5 3B, and Qwen-2.5 7B. Both base and aligned versions were utilized. Safety-tuned variants were created by fine-tuning base models on the "safe" entries of the BeaverTails dataset.
- **Useful Task Fine-tuning**: A 20K subset of MetaMathQA was used. Utility was evaluated on GSM8K (accuracy).
- **Harmful Task Fine-tuning**: A 4K unsafe subset of BeaverTails was used. Harmfulness was evaluated on AdvBench (GPT-4o-mini score 1-5).
- **Contaminated Fine-tuning**: A mixture of 20% harmful BeaverTails data and 80% MetaMathQA data.
- **Activation Space Prompts**: MATH dataset for useful prompts, BeaverTails test set and ToxiGen for harmful prompts.
- **Robustness Checks**: MMLU for general ability, XSTest for over-refusal, Extended Refusal for refusal-oriented prompts, PKU-SafeRLHF for DPO-based safety-tuning.

**Key Definitions and Metrics:**
- **Weight Updates**:
 - `∆_A` (Alignment update): `W_A - W_0` (Aligned model - Base model).
 - `∆_S` (Safety-specific update): `W_S - W_0` (Safety-tuned model - Base model).
 - `∆_j^T` (Task-specific update): `W_FT,j - W_A/S` (Fine-tuned model - Aligned/Safety-tuned model).
- **Subspace Construction**: SVD was performed on reshaped versions of `∆_A` or `∆_S` to obtain principal directions. The top `k` right singular vectors (`U_k`) defined the `k`-dimensional subspace `S_k`.
- **Projection Operators**: `P_k = U_k U_k^T` projects an update onto `S_k`. `P_k^⊥ = I - P_k` projects onto its orthogonal complement.
- **Projection Schemes**:
 - **Parallel Projection**: `W_parallel = W_A/S + P_k ∆_j^T` (retains components within `S_k`).
 - **Orthogonal Projection**: `W_orthogonal = W_A/S + P_k^⊥ ∆_j^T` (removes components within `S_k`).
- **Control Experiments**: `Random-K` (randomly sampled `k` singular vectors) and `Random` (SVD on a random matrix) were used as baselines for comparison.
- **Energy-Kept Ratio (`E_k`)**: Quantifies the fraction of Frobenius norm retained in a projected update, indicating overlap with the subspace.
- **Mode Subspace Overlap (MSO)**: Measures the similarity between two subspaces (e.g., from different weight updates or activation patterns) by computing the Frobenius norm of the overlap matrix between their orthonormal bases. A random subspace overlap baseline was used for comparison.

**Experimental Procedures:**

1. **Do Alignment Subspaces Encode Safety? (Section 3)**
 - Aligned/safety-tuned models were fine-tuned separately on "useful" (MetaMathQA) and "harmful" (BeaverTails unsafe) datasets.
 - The resulting task-specific updates (`∆_Useful^T`, `∆_Harmful^T`) were projected onto the candidate alignment/safety subspaces (`S_k`) using the parallel projection scheme.
 - Harmfulness (AdvBench) and utility (GSM8K) of the projected models were evaluated, alongside the energy-kept ratio, and compared against full fine-tuning and control projections.

2. **Can Harmful Subspaces Be Removed? (Section 4)**
 - Aligned/safety-tuned models were fine-tuned on a "contaminated" dataset (80% useful, 20% harmful).
 - The resulting update was subjected to orthogonal projection, removing components aligned with `S_k`.
 - Utility and harmfulness of these orthogonally projected models were evaluated to see if harmfulness could be selectively suppressed without disproportionately affecting utility.
 - Layer-specific projections were also investigated in the Appendix.

3. **Are Safety Weight Subspaces Distinct? (Section 5)**
 - MSO was computed to compare the principal subspaces of different weight updates: `∆_Useful^T`, `∆_Harmful^T`, `∆_A` (and its negation `-∆_A`), and `∆_S`.
 - Specific pairwise comparisons included (`∆_Useful^T`, `∆_Harmful^T`), (`∆_Useful^T`, `-∆_A`), and (`∆_Harmful^T`, `-∆_A`).
 - The analysis sought to identify which pairs exhibited the highest overlap, particularly expecting a strong overlap between updates related to harmful behavior (`∆_Harmful^T` and `-∆_A`). This was repeated for DPO-tuned models.

4. **Do Safety Subspaces Exist in Activation Space? (Section 6)**
 - Activations (hidden states of the last generated token) from various models (aligned, useful FT, contaminated FT) were collected when processing useful (MATH) and harmful (BeaverTails, ToxiGen) prompts.
 - MSO was used to compare the principal subspaces of activation patterns from different prompt categories (e.g., useful vs. harmful, harmful vs. harmful).
 - The aim was to determine if harmful prompts consistently elicited more mutually overlapping activation subspaces than with useful prompts.

All experiments were implemented using PyTorch and HuggingFace Transformers, emphasizing reproducibility with publicly available code and standard benchmarks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research consistently found that safety-relevant behaviors in LLMs are highly entangled with general learning components, challenging the notion of linearly distinct safety subspaces across both weight and activation spaces.

**Experiment 1: Do Alignment Subspaces Encode Safety?**
- **Energy Distribution**: The fraction of energy retained in projected updates increased linearly with subspace rank and showed consistency across all projection types (Top-K, Random-K, Random). Critically, there was no preferential concentration of update energy in the top directions of alignment (`∆_A`) or safety-specific (`∆_S`) updates for safe versus unsafe fine-tuning tasks. This indicated that energetic alignment alone does not reveal a distinct "safety subspace."
- **Behavioral Impact**: Despite uniform energy distribution, the behavioral impact was not uniform. Projecting useful task updates (`∆_Useful^T`) onto the top-k directions of `∆_A/S` consistently improved utility more effectively than random projections with comparable energy. Conversely, projecting harmful task updates (`∆_Harmful^T`) onto the same top-k directions proportionally increased harmfulness.
- **Conclusion**: The top singular directions of `∆_A/S` are potent axes of general parameter sensitivity, effectively amplifying behavioral changes (both beneficial and harmful) regardless of the specific task. They do not uniquely encode safety-specific information; rather, they represent a "general learning geometry." Disentangling alignment (`∆_A`) from safety-specific (`∆_S`) updates offered no clear separation or benefit in this regard.

**Experiment 2: Can Harmful Subspaces Be Removed?**
- **Impact of Orthogonal Projection**: When components aligned with the top-k directions of `∆_A/S` were removed via orthogonal projection, retained energy, utility, and harmfulness all declined.
- **No Selective Harmfulness Suppression**: Removing these top-k alignment components reduced utility more sharply than random projections, while harmfulness decreased at a similar rate. This suggests an absence of selective suppression of harmful behavior. Safety gains were observed to come at a proportional cost to task performance, without a clear advantage in targeting the alignment subspace.
- **Conclusion**: The top subspaces of alignment or safety-tuned updates do not uniquely encode safety or harmfulness. Orthogonal projection, intended to remove harmful components, failed to selectively suppress harmful behavior, implying that subspace filtering based on these directions imposes a strict trade-off between safety and utility. This finding remained consistent even when projections were restricted to individual layers.

**Experiment 3: Are Safety Weight Subspaces Distinct?**
- **Overlap Patterns**: All pairwise comparisons between dominant subspaces of useful updates (`∆_Useful^T`), harmful updates (`∆_Harmful^T`), and reversed alignment updates (`-∆_A`) exhibited greater Mode Subspace Overlap (MSO) than random baselines, indicating shared underlying structure in parameter space.
- **Counterintuitive Dominant Overlap**: The strongest MSO was consistently observed between `∆_Useful^T` and `∆_Harmful^T`, rather than between `∆_Harmful^T` and `-∆_A` (which represent two forms of harmfulness/reversed safety). This contradicts the expectation that a dedicated safety subspace would manifest in shared directions among safety-relevant updates.
- **Safety-Tuned Models**: For safety-tuned models, the strongest MSO was found between the useful update and the safety-specific update, which is also counterintuitive as it suggests a closer relationship between useful and safety-specific learning than between harmful and safety-specific learning.
- **Conclusion**: There is no evidence of a consistent, linear safety-specific subspace in weight space. Safety-relevant updates (alignment, safety tuning, harmful fine-tuning) do not reside in a well-defined or isolatable region. Instead, they appear to operate over complex, task-dependent directions that form a "general learning subspace," which is expressive across various tasks but agnostic to safety implications. These findings were robust across different models and also held when safety-tuning was performed using Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

**Experiment 4: Do Safety Subspaces Exist in Activation Space?**
- **Shared Activation Subspaces**: Useful (benign) and harmful prompts consistently activated overlapping high-energy subspaces in the model's internal representations, with MSO values above the random baseline.
- **No Distinct Harmful Activation Subspace**: The overlap between two different sets of harmful prompts was not consistently greater than their overlap with useful prompts. In some instances, the useful–harmful overlap exceeded the harmful–harmful overlap. The degree of overlap varied across model configurations but did not indicate a clear separation based on safety content.
- **Conclusion**: The study found no evidence of a distinct safety-violating subspace in activation space. Prompts with different safety implications (useful vs. harmful) are processed through broadly overlapping representations, suggesting that LLMs do not internally separate "safe" and "unsafe" activation modes but rely on shared, high-impact representational subspaces.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work contributes significantly to the understanding of safety alignment in Large Language Models by directly challenging a prevalent assumption regarding the linear separability of safety-related knowledge. The consistent findings across multiple experiments, five open-source LLMs (Llama and Qwen families), and analyses in both weight and activation spaces, suggest that:

- **Re-evaluation of Subspace-Based Defenses**: The primary impact of this research is its implication for subspace-based defense strategies. If safety is not linearly distinct but deeply entangled with general learning, then methods that rely on identifying, isolating, or projecting updates onto fixed "safety subspaces" are likely to face fundamental limitations. Simple projection or filtering-based defenses may not be able to suppress harmfulness without incurring comparable losses in model utility. This calls for a re-evaluation of existing and proposed subspace-based approaches like SafeLoRA or SaLoRA, which implicitly assume this separability.
- **Shift in Research Focus for Alignment**: The findings underscore the need for alternative strategies to preserve safety under continued training. Future research in LLM alignment and safety might need to move beyond purely linear mechanisms towards more complex, non-linear, or entanglement-aware approaches. This could involve exploring techniques that manage the trade-offs between safety and utility more explicitly, or developing methods that leverage the entangled nature of representations rather than attempting to disentangle them.
- **Guidance for Interpretability Research**: The work suggests that the directions responsible for behavioral changes, whether beneficial or harmful, correspond to general-purpose learning components rather than unique safety-specific features. This calls for rethinking assumptions in LLM interpretability, emphasizing that "high-impact directions" are potent for optimization but not inherently aligned with ethical objectives. Future interpretability efforts might focus on understanding how safety emerges from distributed, shared mechanisms rather than localized, separable components.
- **Implications for Adversarial Robustness**: By showing that harmful and helpful updates often reside in overlapping subspaces, the research highlights the intrinsic difficulty of achieving robust safety without sacrificing general capabilities. This provides a mechanistic understanding of why safety alignment can be fragile, and why even benign fine-tuning can lead to safety degradation.
- **Foundation for Future Work**: While identifying a limitation, the work also implicitly suggests directions for future research. For instance, exploring non-linear representations, studying the dynamic evolution of "safety" during training, or developing context-dependent intervention strategies might be more fruitful avenues for robust safety alignment.

Overall, this research provides empirical evidence that linear separability of safety alignment is not a characteristic of the tested LLMs. This insight is crucial for advancing the scientific understanding of LLM behavior and for guiding the development of more effective and robust safety mechanisms in the rapidly evolving field of artificial intelligence.
