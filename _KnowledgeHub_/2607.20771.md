# Emergent Compositional Skills in Mixture-of-Experts VLAs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Princeton University demonstrated that Vision-Language-Action models equipped with a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) action head can autonomously learn and compose distinct, reusable robot skills from expert demonstrations, achieving performance comparable to dense baselines on the LIBERO benchmark. The approach allows experts to specialize in phase-level behaviors, with some showing 35-45% activation across varied tasks.

## Problem

- Monolithic Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models struggle to extract reusable skills, leading to inefficient adaptation and a lack of interpretability in complex robotic tasks.
- Current modular VLA approaches typically require pre-specified hierarchical structures, manual skill definitions, or external supervision for task decomposition.
- Monolithic VLA policies often act as black boxes, making it difficult to understand or debug their decision-making processes for robotic manipulation.

## Method

- Integrated a ==Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) action head== into existing VLA backbones, replacing Feed-Forward Network sublayers within the transformer decoder.
- Implemented experts as ==LoRA deltas== on top of base FFN weights, leveraging pre-trained knowledge efficiently and reducing parameter count.
- Utilized a ==whole forward pass routing== strategy where a single MLP router selects top-k experts for the entire action generation process, guided by a ==load-balancing loss== term to ensure diverse expert utilization.

## Results

- Qualitatively distinct, phase-level skills emerged, with specific experts consistently mapping to behaviors like object transport (Expert A) or release-and-retract (Expert B).
- The learned experts demonstrated both broad reusability across tasks (e.g., **35-45%** activation across multiple LIBERO tasks) and specialization for unique, task-specific actions.
- The MoE VLA architecture achieved task performance comparable to dense, fine-tuned baselines on the LIBERO benchmark while simultaneously developing specialized and independently functional expert primitives.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Complex robotic manipulation tasks can be autonomously decomposed into distinct, reusable, and interpretable primitive skills by a VLA model with an MoE action head, without explicit supervision.
> - Experts emerge to encode general "phase-level" behaviors, applicable across diverse tasks, demonstrating reusability and abstract skill learning.
> - The router implicitly learns to sequentially compose these specialized primitives to form complete, longer-horizon action trajectories, even making failures more interpretable.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen substantial advancements with the development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models, such as RT-2 (Brohan et al., 2023), Open X-Embodiment (Embodiment Collaboration, 2025), Octo (Octo Model Team et al., 2024), and Open-VLA (Kim et al., 2024), are designed to generalize across a diverse array of robotic tasks by learning directly from large datasets of visual, linguistic, and action data. They typically operate as monolithic policies, meaning a single, unified model is responsible for all aspects of control.

While effective, this monolithic structure presents several challenges. It can be difficult to identify or extract reusable behavioral skills from a single, undifferentiated policy. This characteristic also hinders hierarchical composition of behaviors, where complex tasks could be broken down into simpler, sequential sub-tasks. Furthermore, adapting specific parts of the policy to new scenarios or modifying particular behaviors often necessitates retraining or fine-tuning the entire model, which can be computationally intensive and inefficient.

In contrast, many real-world robotic tasks inherently possess a compositional nature, being naturally decomposable into distinct, reusable primitive skills (e.g., reaching, grasping, placing, retracting). Existing approaches to introduce modularity or hierarchy into VLA systems often rely on pre-specified structures. This includes systems that impose a fixed planner-controller split (Li et al., 2025; Chen et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026) or those that require a manually defined library of skills. These methods necessitate external supervision or human intervention in defining the task decomposition or skill sets.

This research paper positions itself as an exploration into whether such modular and compositional structures can emerge directly from data, without any explicit prior knowledge of task decomposition or a pre-defined skill library. By investigating a simplified Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture for the VLA action head, the authors aim to bridge the gap between monolithic, generalist VLAs and more modular, interpretable robotic policies. The work differentiates itself by proposing a self-supervised approach where the task decomposition and skill specialization are learned implicitly, allowing for the potential benefits of modularity—interpretability, compositionality, and robustness—to arise from the data itself rather than through explicit engineering. This represents a step toward more autonomous and flexible robotic learning systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model, when augmented with a simplified Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) action head, can autonomously learn to decompose complex robotic manipulation tasks into reusable, interpretable primitive skills. This investigation is conducted without any pre-specified notions of task decomposition, hierarchical structure, or manually defined skill libraries.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several limitations observed in current monolithic VLA policies:
1. **Lack of Interpretability:** Monolithic VLA policies, while capable, often operate as black boxes, making it challenging to understand their decision-making process or to identify the specific behaviors they execute at different stages of a task. This lack of interpretability hinders debugging, refinement, and trust in robotic systems.
2. **Limited Compositionality:** Robotic tasks frequently involve sequences of distinct sub-behaviors. Monolithic policies struggle to explicitly represent or leverage this compositional structure, making it difficult to chain learned behaviors for longer-horizon tasks or to transfer specific skills to novel task combinations.
3. **Inefficient Adaptation:** When adjustments are needed for a specific part of a task or for a new variation, monolithic policies typically require retraining or fine-tuning of the entire model. A modular policy, composed of distinct skills, could allow for more targeted and efficient adaptation, where only relevant modules need modification.
4. **Inefficiency in Skill Reuse:** Many manipulation tasks share common low-level primitives like "reaching," "grasping," and "placing." Current monolithic VLAs do not explicitly extract or reuse these skills, potentially leading to redundant learning across tasks. A modular approach could foster the learning of generalizable, reusable skills.

The authors hypothesize that by introducing a Mixture-of-Experts architecture, where a router selects among specialized "experts" conditioned on the current observation and language instruction, an emergent hierarchy can form. In this hypothesized hierarchy, the router would function as an implicit high-level controller, sequencing primitive behaviors, while the individual experts would specialize in distinct, lower-level behavioral modes. This approach seeks to develop modular robot policies that are both interpretable and compositional, with these desirable properties emerging intrinsically from the training data alone, rather than requiring external human-engineered structures or annotations. Such a system could offer advantages in terms of interpretability, robustness, and the ability to perform long-horizon planning and transfer skills to new tasks more effectively.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this work involves integrating a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) action policy into existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) backbones. The aim is for specialized, compositional skills to emerge from this architecture during end-to-end training with expert demonstrations.

**VLA Backbone Integration:**
The proposed MoE architecture is designed to be backbone-agnostic. For experimental validation, it was applied to two pre-trained VLA backbones:
- $\pi_0$ (Black et al., 2026)
- SmolVLA (Shukor et al., 2025)
Both backbones utilize flow matching for action generation, a technique that predicts the velocity field of a stochastic process to generate actions. These VLA models typically consist of a vision-language module that encodes camera observations, language instructions, and proprioceptive state into context tokens, followed by an action expert (a transformer decoder) that processes these tokens and a noised action chunk to produce the desired actions.

**LoRA Mixture-of-Experts Architecture:**
The MoE mechanism is specifically applied to the action expert component of the VLA backbone. Two critical design choices define its structure:
1. **Targeted Application:** The MoE replaces only the Feed-Forward Network (FFN) sublayer within each action layer of the transformer decoder. The self-attention mechanism, which is responsible for contextual understanding across tokens, is shared across all experts. This design allows experts to specialize in action generation while leveraging a common, powerful contextual representation.
2. **LoRA Deltas for Expert Realization:** Each expert is realized as a low-rank (LoRA) delta ($\Delta W = (\alpha/r) BA$) applied on top of the base FFN weights. Here, $r$ is the rank (set to 16), and $B$ and $A$ are low-rank matrices. This approach offers several advantages:
 - **Strong Shared Prior:** By modifying only a small part of the FFN through LoRA, the experts build upon the strong prior knowledge learned by the pre-trained backbone. This facilitates specialization rather than requiring learning from scratch.
 - **Efficient Parameterization:** LoRA significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters per expert, making the MoE more efficient than a full FFN for each expert.
 - **Zero Initialization:** The $B$ matrices for LoRA are zero-initialized. This ensures that at the beginning of training, the policy precisely reproduces the behavior of the pre-trained backbone, providing a stable starting point for expert specialization to emerge.

**Whole Forward Pass Routing:**
Unlike standard MoE transformers that route experts per token per layer, this approach implements a "whole forward pass routing" strategy:
1. **Single Routing Decision:** A small Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) router, denoted $g_\phi$, makes a single routing decision for an entire policy forward pass. This decision is shared across all action-expert layers ($L$).
2. **Contextual Input:** The router's input is a comprehensive context vector $c$. This vector is formed by concatenating the mean-pooled output of the Vision-Language Module (VLM) (which processes camera observations $I$, language instruction $\ell$, and proprioceptive state $s$) with a transformed proprioceptive state $W_s s$.
3. **Top-k Selection:** The router outputs logits over $E$ available experts. From these logits, the top-$k$ experts are selected, and their softmax weights $w_e$ are renormalized.
4. **Coherent Skill Selection:** This single (indices, weights) pair is then uniformly applied across every MoE FFN in the action expert layers. This ensures that a single, coherent expert (representing a "skill") is selected and engaged for the entire action chunk generation process, rather than having independent layer-wise decisions.

**Training Objective:**
The model is trained using a composite objective function $L$:
$$L = L_{FM} + \lambda_{LB} L_{LB}$$
- **Behavior Cloning Loss ($L_{FM}$):** This is the primary loss term, derived from the backbone's flow-matching velocity-prediction loss. It drives the policy to imitate the expert demonstrations by accurately predicting the velocity of the noised action chunk.
- **Load Balancing Term ($L_{LB}$):** An auxiliary loss term, standard in MoE architectures (Fedus et al., 2022), is included to prevent "routing collapse." Without this term, the router might disproportionately select only a few experts, leaving others underutilized. $L_{LB}$ encourages a more balanced distribution of expertise across all available experts, promoting specialization and the full utilization of the MoE capacity. A weight $\lambda_{LB} = 0.01$ was used for this term throughout training.

Through these methodological choices, the authors aim to facilitate the emergent learning of modular, interpretable, and reusable skills within a VLA framework, leveraging existing powerful backbones while introducing a mechanism for specialization and task decomposition.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results address four key questions regarding the emergence, reusability, composition, and independent functionality of learned skills within the Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) VLA architecture. The primary backbone used was $\pi_0$, with additional testing on SmolVLA.

#### 4.1. Qualitative Analysis of Expert Skills (Q1)

The study found that the MoE action head facilitated the learning of qualitatively distinct skills in a self-supervised manner.
- **Distinct Behaviors:** Visual inspection of rollouts on LIBERO-10 tasks revealed that different experts consistently corresponded to specific low-level behaviors. For example, Expert A was observed handling the final transport phase of manipulation, activating when an object was already grasped and being moved to its target across diverse tasks like placing a moka pot, dropping a mug, or putting a yellow mug in a microwave. Expert B specialized in the release-and-retract phase, firing after a drop-off to lift the gripper. Expert C consistently handled the approach-to-grasp phase for objects with thin handles.
- **Phase-Level Skills:** The reuse of the same expert across structurally different tasks and scenes suggests that experts encode general "phase-level" skills rather than task-specific policies. This indicates a degree of abstract skill learning.
- **Emergent Trajectory Stitching:** The router effectively composed these individual experts into full trajectories, switching between them across denoising steps to accomplish a complete manipulation cycle (e.g., approach, transport, release).
- **Capacity Utilization:** The architecture autonomously managed its capacity, with 6 out of 16 experts being largely unused, indicating that the model only specialized as much as demanded by the task distribution. This supports the notion of meaningful specialization rather than mere redundancy.

#### 4.2. Reusable vs. Task-Dependent Skills (Q2)

Analysis of expert selection frequency across LIBERO-10 tasks revealed two types of expert roles:
- **Reusable Experts:** Experts like A and B demonstrated high activation frequencies (35–45%) across a set of five distinct tasks (T0, T1, T4, T6, T7), while being largely inactive on others. Within individual rollouts, the router frequently alternated between these experts. This pattern is characteristic of phase-level skills that are broadly applicable across tasks with similar underlying structures.
- **Task-Specific Experts:** In contrast, Experts C and D exhibited concentrated activity on specific tasks. Expert C accounted for 67% of selections on Task T5, and Expert D for 66% on Task T2, with minimal activity elsewhere. These experts appear to specialize in idiosyncratic behaviors unique to one or a few tasks, potentially allowing reusable experts to maintain their crispness by offloading edge cases.

#### 4.3. Composition of Primitives for Longer Tasks (Q3)

The study demonstrated that the learned primitives are composed to solve longer-horizon tasks:
- **Sequential Composition:** On tasks like T5, Expert C (grasping the book) was followed by Expert A (placing the book). On T2, Expert C (grasping the stove dial) was followed by Expert A (placing the arm onto the handle). This shows the router implicitly learning to sequence primitives to achieve higher-level goals.
- **Interpretable Failures:** In cases of task failure, such as on Task T4, the policy did not exhibit arbitrary or degenerate behavior. Instead, it repeatedly invoked the same pair of primitives (e.g., Experts E and C) to attempt navigation and grasping. This suggests that failures become interpretable as repeated misapplications of known primitives, rather than unpredictable model breakdowns.

#### 4.4. Manual Routing Experiments (Q4)

To verify if experts genuinely differ and generalize, manual routing substitutions were performed:
- **Distinct Behaviors Upon Manual Selection:** Substituting different router assignments for a given action chunk within a trajectory resulted in qualitatively distinct behaviors. This confirms that the experts themselves have specialized capabilities, and the observed mapping between experts and skills is not merely an artifact of the router's consistent selection policy.
- **Out-of-Distribution Generalization:** Experts demonstrated their expected skill behavior even in contexts where the router would not typically select them. For instance, Expert C, a grasping expert, displayed grasping behavior on Task T4 even though the router never naturally used it for that specific task. This indicates that the learned skills possess a degree of generalization beyond their typical activation contexts.
- **Recovery from Failure:** Manual intervention to select a specific expert (e.g., the grasp expert) could enable recovery from a prior grasping failure, leading to a successful grasp. This further underscores the independent utility and robustness of the learned expert primitives.

#### 4.5. Performance on LIBERO

The MoE policy achieved performance comparable to a dense, fine-tuned baseline on the LIBERO benchmark across both $\pi_0$ and SmolVLA backbones. This finding is significant because it indicates that the skill specialization and emergent modularity attained through the MoE architecture did not come at the expense of overall task competence. The MoE maintained task performance while concurrently learning meaningfully specialized action experts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic learning by demonstrating the emergent acquisition of compositional skills within Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The primary significance of this work lies in challenging the necessity of pre-defined hierarchical structures or manually curated skill libraries for developing modular robotic policies.

**Key Contributions and Implications:**

- **Emergent Compositionality:** The most prominent finding is that a simplified Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) action head, when trained end-to-end with expert demonstrations and a load-balancing penalty, can autonomously learn to decompose complex manipulation tasks into a set of modular and reusable skills. This emergence of compositional structure without explicit supervision for task decomposition or skill definition represents a notable advancement over prior approaches that often rely on a fixed planner-controller split or pre-trained skill libraries.
- **Enhanced Interpretability:** The specialization of experts into qualitatively distinct, low-level behaviors (e.g., "grasping," "placing," "retracting") makes the overall policy more interpretable. Researchers and practitioners can observe which specific skill is being invoked at different stages of a task, leading to a better understanding of the policy's decision-making process. This improved interpretability can facilitate debugging and analysis of robotic behaviors. Even failures become more interpretable, as they manifest as repeated misapplications of known primitives rather than arbitrary, degenerate actions.
- **Improved Reusability and Generalization:** The finding that learned experts are reused consistently across diverse tasks suggests that these experts encode generalizable, phase-level skills rather than task-specific policies. This inherent reusability could lead to more efficient learning and better generalization to novel, longer-horizon tasks by allowing the system to compose existing primitives in new sequences. Manual routing experiments further support this, indicating that individual experts possess valuable, generalizable skills even in contexts where the router would not typically select them.
- **Data-Driven Skill Acquisition:** The work provides evidence that hierarchical, skill-based learning may not necessitate extensive additional machinery, such as high-level planners or explicitly defined options. Instead, such desirable properties can emerge directly from large-scale demonstration data when coupled with a suitable architectural bias and learning objectives (like the load-balancing term). This paradigm could simplify the development of generalist robotic agents.
- **Performance Without Compromise:** The MoE policy achieved task performance comparable to dense, fine-tuned baselines. This indicates that the benefits of modularity and interpretability were gained without sacrificing overall task competence, making the approach practical for real-world robotic applications.

**Potential Impact:**

The development of data-driven, emergent compositional skills has several potential impacts:
- **Accelerated Robot Learning:** By learning reusable skills from data, robots could potentially learn new, complex tasks faster by composing existing primitives, reducing the need for extensive new demonstrations for every novel task variation.
- **More Robust and Adaptable Policies:** Modular policies might be more robust to perturbations and easier to adapt to new environments or object properties, as specific skills could be fine-tuned or swapped without affecting the entire policy.
- **Safer Human-Robot Interaction:** Increased interpretability of robot actions can foster greater trust and safety in human-robot collaboration, as the robot's intent and current behavior can be more easily understood.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work opens avenues for future research into understanding the fundamental mechanisms that enable compositionality in VLAs, optimizing expert architectures, and leveraging these learned skills for more advanced forms of compositional generalization, such as zero-shot task execution through novel skill sequencing.

In conclusion, this research marks an important step toward creating robot policies that are not only capable of general task solving but also inherently modular, interpretable, and composed of reusable atomic skills learned directly from observational data.
