# Modeling What Changes: Sparse, Residual World Models for Object-Centric Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A sparse, residual object-centric world model for tabletop pushing: a per-object change gate (discrete, straight-through) selects which objects move, and a shared residual delta head perturbs only gated objects while the rest are copied verbatim. On MuJoCo pushing with 3-8 objects it cuts overall L2 error 2.5-4.6x versus a dense MLP using 8.6-11.1x fewer parameters, though the gain comes from not corrupting static objects rather than better mover dynamics.

## Problem

- Monolithic world models re-predict the entire next state each step, spending capacity on the static majority of a scene that never actually changes.
- Dense regressors inject error into objects that should be copied unchanged, and that error compounds across autoregressive rollouts until the imagined world drifts.
- Object-centric and conditionally-computed models supply relational structure or input-dependent sparsity, but none make sparsity a property of a world model's predicted change.

## Method

- ==Sparse/residual head==: a per-object ==gate network== emits a binary change decision via a ==Gumbel straight-through estimator==, and a ==delta head== emits a pose residual applied only when gated.
- ==Count-agnostic featurization== gives each object its pose, velocity, relative goal and pusher positions, action, and a ==permutation-invariant aggregate== over other objects, with weights shared across objects.
- Composite loss: class-balanced ==BCE== on the gate against the ground-truth changed mask, ==L2== on residuals supervised only on movers, plus a ==sparsity penalty== on mean gate activation.

## Results

- Overall L2 **0.071-0.136** versus dense **0.319-0.347** across N=3,5,8, but essentially tied with a no-op baseline (**0.081** vs **0.081** at N=8); changed-object L2 **0.466** vs no-op **0.470**.
- Change detection reaches F1 **0.80-0.87** at precision **0.92-0.97** where dense is degenerate (recall about 1); unchanged-object L2 falls **0.211** to **0.001** at N=3 through the gate.
- Rollout at horizon 20 is **3.4-6.4x** better than dense yet uniformly worse than no-op; planning success is **0.23 +/- 0.06** versus dense **0.00** and random **0.15**, not significantly above random.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit change gating buys error suppression on inert scene parts, not better dynamics on movers; separate the two error terms before claiming accuracy gains.
> - An oracle-gate diagnostic shows detection is nearly saturated and one-step contact deltas, especially rotation, are the real bottleneck for object-centric predictors.
> - Always report a no-op baseline: beating a dense monolith on sparse-change data can still lose to predicting that nothing moves at all.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge within the field of learned world models, particularly in the context of robot manipulation in physical environments. Traditional or "monolithic" learned world models typically predict the entire next state of a scene at every time step. This approach, exemplified by models like "World Models" and "Dreamer", is common in model-based reinforcement learning and control, where a learned dynamics model is used for imagination, planning, or policy learning (e.g., in PETS, MPPI, CEM).

However, the authors identify two significant drawbacks of this monolithic approach, particularly in physical environments characterized by sparse changes:
1. **Computational and Capacity Overhead:** A substantial portion of the model's parameters and computational resources are dedicated to re-predicting parts of the scene that remain static.
2. **Error Injection and Accumulation:** When a model attempts to reproduce an entire scene, it inevitably introduces small errors into the prediction of static elements. In closed-loop rollouts, these errors can accumulate over time, leading to a drift in the predicted world state, especially for objects that should not have moved.

Furthermore, monolithic models lack explicit interpretability regarding *what* they predict will change, making debugging or targeted control more challenging.

This paper builds upon existing research in several related areas:
- **Object-Centric and Structured World Models:** Previous work, such as Interaction Networks, Graph Networks, and Contrastive Learning of Structured World Models, aims to improve generalization and computational efficiency by structuring models around individual objects and their relationships. While these approaches share computation across entities and generalize across counts, they generally still predict the full state of every object densely.
- **Sparse Coding and Conditional Computation:** The concept of sparsity has been explored in neural networks, from sparse representations of natural signals to conditional computation architectures like sparsely-gated Mixture-of-Experts, which route inputs through subsets of the network. These methods typically apply sparsity at intermediate layers or in input processing rather than directly in the output prediction of a dynamics model's change structure.
- **Pushing Dynamics:** Research in robotic pushing has investigated both analytical mechanics and learned models. Related efforts like SE3-Nets predict per-object rigid-body motions using soft masks from point clouds.

The current work aims to bridge the gap between object-centric modeling and conditional computation by proposing a model that explicitly gates and predicts *only* the changes in the scene, thereby addressing the limitations of monolithic dense prediction, particularly error injection, and enhancing interpretability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether explicitly modeling what changes in a physical environment constitutes a more effective and interpretable inductive bias for learned world models. Specifically, the authors propose and evaluate a sparse, residual world model that explicitly identifies and predicts changes for individual objects, leaving static objects untouched.

The motivation for this specific approach stems from several observations and identified limitations of current world modeling paradigms:
- **Sparsity of Physical Interaction:** In real-world robotic manipulation scenarios, such as pushing an object on a tabletop, typically only a small subset of objects in a scene undergoes significant change. The vast majority of the environment remains static. Standard world models fail to leverage this inherent sparsity.
- **Inefficiency of Monolithic Prediction:** Monolithic world models are inefficient because they constantly expend computational capacity and parameters to re-predict the entire scene, including its static majority. This represents a misallocation of resources, as the model's capacity could be better utilized for predicting the dynamics of the few active elements.
- **Error Accumulation on Static Objects:** A key, and "insidious," problem with dense prediction is the inevitable injection of small regression errors into the poses of static objects. When these models are used in closed-loop, autoregressive rollouts for planning or imagination, these errors accumulate over time, leading to a progressive drift and corruption of the predicted state, particularly for objects that should have remained fixed. This degrades the utility of the world model for long-horizon tasks.
- **Lack of Interpretability:** Monolithic predictors output a single, entangled representation of the next state. They do not explicitly indicate *which* objects are expected to change or why. For applications in robot control and debugging, a model that provides an explicit "change mask" or identifies moving objects would offer a more interpretable and actionable understanding of its predictions.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a model design that is deliberately simple and object-centric. It incorporates a per-object "change gate" that predicts which objects move, and a "residual delta head" that predicts pose increments applied *only* to the objects identified by the gate. All other objects are copied verbatim from the current state. This factorization is designed to mirror the sparsity of physical interactions, share parameters across objects (making it robust to object count variations), and provide an explicit, inspectable change mask.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigates a sparse/residual object-centric world model designed to predict changes in a multi-object environment.

**State and Action Representation:**
- The scene state $s_t$ at time $t$ consists of the pusher's position, the planar pose (x, y, θ) and velocity for each object, and the goal position.
- Actions $a_t$ are defined as end-effector delta-xy commands for a position-controlled pusher.

**Sparse/Residual Model Architecture (Fig. 1):**
The core of the proposed model is its per-object processing structure, which employs shared weights across objects for scalability.
- **Per-object Feature Vector ($\phi_i(s_t, a_t)$):** For each object $i$, a feature vector is constructed. This vector incorporates the object's current pose and velocity, its relative positions to the goal and the pusher, the action taken, and a permutation-invariant aggregate feature derived from the other objects in the scene.
- **Gate Network:** This sub-network takes the feature vector $\phi_i$ as input and outputs a change logit. This logit is then used to produce a discrete gate $g_i \in \{0, 1\}$ (where 1 indicates change, 0 indicates no change). The training of this discrete gate is enabled by a Gumbel straight-through estimator, allowing end-to-end gradient propagation while ensuring a hard (exactly zero update) decision at inference time.
- **Delta Head:** Another sub-network, also taking $\phi_i$ as input, predicts a residual pose increment $\Delta_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ (representing changes in x, y, and θ).
- **Next Pose Prediction:** The predicted next pose for object $i$, $\hat{p}_{t+1}^i$, is computed as $\hat{p}_{t+1}^i = p_t^i + g_i \Delta_i$. This equation explicitly implements the sparse residual update: if the gate $g_i$ is 0, the object's pose $p_t^i$ is copied verbatim to $p_{t+1}^i$; if $g_i$ is 1, the predicted residual $\Delta_i$ is added to the current pose.

**Loss Function (Eq. 2):**
The model is trained using a composite loss function:
- **Gate Loss:** A class-balanced binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss is applied to the gate output $g_i$ against the ground-truth changed mask $m^\star_i$.
- **Residual Regression Loss:** An L2 regression loss is applied to the residual $\Delta_i$. Crucially, this loss is supervised *only* on objects where the ground-truth mask $m^\star_i$ indicates a change, meaning it only penalizes errors in predicting the motion of objects that actually moved. The term is weighted by $\lambda_\Delta$.
- **Sparsity Penalty:** A penalty $\lambda_s \bar{g}$ on the mean gate activation $\bar{g}$ is included to encourage sparsity, i.e., to discourage the gate from always activating. This weight $\lambda_s$ influences the trade-off between gate recall and precision.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Environment and Data Generation:** Experiments are conducted in a procedurally generated MuJoCo tabletop environment. Scenes contain $N$ (ranging from 3 to 8) free 5 cm boxes. A scripted pushing policy generates trajectories. The dataset records $(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})$ tuples, including ground-truth per-object change masks and pose deltas. Data is filtered to a "hard subset" to focus on steps with actual motion. An 80/10/10 train/validation/test split is used, with a configuration-leakage guard.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Dense MLP:** A standard multilayer perceptron (MLP) serves as the primary baseline. It has a hidden width of 256 and 3 layers, and directly regresses all object poses at $t+1$ under an L2 loss.
 - **No-op:** This baseline simply predicts no change for any object ($p_{t+1} = p_t$). This establishes a lower bound on error for static objects.
- **Metrics:** Evaluation relies on:
 - Overall L2 pose error.
 - Changed and unchanged per-object pose L2 error.
 - Change detection metrics: precision, recall, and F1 score for the gate.
 - Model efficiency: parameter count, operation count (FLOPS), and CPU latency.
- **Scaling and Hardware:** Models are evaluated across object counts $N \in \{3, 5, 8\}$. All models are small MLPs (<0.1M parameters) and run on a single laptop.
- **Training Details:** Adam optimizer, learning rate 10⁻³, batch size 128. Sparse models are trained for 15-25 epochs. Gate and delta heads are 2-layer MLPs (width 128).
- **Downstream Planning:** The sparse model is evaluated as a forward simulator within a receding-horizon planner (Cross-Entropy Method - CEM) for a push-to-goal task. Planning involves 256 samples and 3 refit iterations over a horizon of 15.
 - **Planner Feature Refinement:** Initial planning attempts failed due to out-of-distribution states visited by the planner. This was addressed by adding a "contact-aware, velocity-free feature mode" and retraining on diverse mixed-policy data that covers states encountered during planning.
 - **Planning Baselines:** Oracle (true simulator), scripted controller, and random actions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**Prediction Accuracy (Tables I, II, VIII, App. F):**
- The sparse model achieved a 2.5–4.6× lower overall L2 pose error compared to the dense MLP baseline across object counts (N=3, 5, 8), while utilizing 8.6–11.1× fewer parameters.
- However, the primary source of this overall accuracy advantage was identified as error suppression on the static majority of the scene. The sparse model's overall L2 performance was comparable to or only marginally better than the "no-op" baseline (which simply predicts no change). For N=5, sparse L2 was 0.101 ± 0.004 compared to no-op's 0.107; for N=8, it was 0.071 ± 0.016 versus no-op's 0.081. In one configuration (N=8, seed 0), the sparse model's L2 was equal to the no-op baseline (0.081 vs. 0.081).
- On objects that actually moved, the changed-object L2 error for the sparse model was at or barely above the no-op baseline (e.g., N=8: sparse 0.466 vs. no-op 0.470; N=5: sparse 0.426 vs. no-op 0.446). This indicates that the sparse model did not achieve significantly superior accuracy in predicting the precise dynamics of moving objects compared to simply predicting no motion for them.
- The sparse model demonstrated effective change detection, maintaining F1 scores between 0.80 and 0.87 (with precision 0.92–0.97). In contrast, the dense baseline exhibited "degenerate" change detection metrics, partly due to the definition of the metric. The sparse model effectively reduced L2 error on unchanged objects from 0.211 (dense at N=3) to 0.001 (sparse at N=3).
- Regarding efficiency, the sparse model exhibited significant parameter efficiency. While its operation count advantage diminished with increasing object count (from 3.8× to 1.1× fewer operations than dense), and its wall-clock latency was higher due to per-object gating overhead, the capacity efficiency was a robust finding.

**Attribution of Performance (Tables II, VII, VIII):**
- **Not just smaller:** Reducing the dense MLP's parameter count to match the sparse model did not improve its performance; the sparse model still maintained 3 to 5 times lower L2 error and higher F1 scores. This indicates the advantage is not merely due to fewer parameters.
- **Role of the Gate:** A capacity-matched "ladder" analysis showed that while object-centric featurization and residual prediction contributed to L2 reduction, the explicit change gate provided the largest single improvement (1.7–2.0× reduction in L2) and was the only component that enabled overall L2 to fall below the no-op baseline.
- **Bottleneck is Regression, not Detection:** An "oracle gate" diagnostic, where the delta head was fed the ground-truth change mask, resulted in only a marginal improvement in changed-object L2. This finding indicates that the primary strength of the sparse model lies in its ability to accurately detect which objects change and, more importantly, to avoid injecting errors into static objects, rather than achieving high fidelity in predicting the complex dynamics (especially rotation) of moved objects.

**World Model Properties (Table III, Fig. 3):**
- **Autoregressive Rollout:** In closed-loop rollouts over 20-step horizons, the sparse model compounded significantly less error than the dense model (3.4–6.4× better L2 at horizon 20). However, it was consistently worse than the "no-op" baseline in overall L2 at every object count and horizon (e.g., N=3: sparse 0.373 vs. no-op 0.277). A mover-only rollout analysis indicated that the sparse model tracks movers at "roughly no-op quality," confirming that its advantage is concentrated on static objects.
- **Compositional Generalization (Cross-count Transfer):** The sparse model, due to its object-centric and weight-shared design, demonstrated strong generalization across object counts. A model trained on N-object scenes could be run on M-object scenes with 99.4% F1 retention without retraining. The monolithic dense model could not be run off-count.
- **Sample Efficiency:** The sparse model achieved approximately 90% of its full-data F1 performance with only 25% of the training data, outperforming the dense model at all data budgets.

**Downstream Planning (Table IV, IX):**
- Initial attempts to use prediction-trained sparse and dense models for planning (push-to-goal task using CEM) resulted in complete failure (0.00 success), performing worse than random actions. This was attributed to the planner querying out-of-distribution states that the models were not trained to handle, leading to hallucinated motion (dense) or silent gates (sparse).
- After addressing this by incorporating contact-aware, velocity-free features and retraining on diverse mixed-policy data covering planner-visited states:
 - The sparse model's planning success rate improved to 0.23 ± 0.06 (reducing final distance by half).
 - The dense model remained at 0.00 success across all seeds.
- However, the planning performance of the sparse model (0.23 ± 0.06) was reported as "not significantly better than random" (random baseline: 0.15), with its weakest seed performance matching random. The robust finding was its consistent separation from the dense model (0.00 success).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the development of learned world models, particularly for robotic manipulation tasks in dynamic environments. The introduction of an explicit sparse/residual architecture with a per-object change gate addresses several fundamental limitations of monolithic world models.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Error Suppression on Static Objects:** The most impactful finding is the sparse model's ability to effectively detect what changes and, crucially, to avoid injecting errors into the static majority of a scene. This directly tackles a core problem of monolithic models, where error accumulation on static elements leads to drift in autoregressive rollouts. For tasks requiring long-horizon planning or stable environmental representations, this error suppression is critical.
- **Enhanced Interpretability:** By providing an explicit, inspectable change mask, the model offers a clear indication of which objects it expects to move. This improves the interpretability of world model predictions, which can be invaluable for debugging robot behaviors, understanding model failures, and potentially for higher-level reasoning or task planning.
- **Parameter Efficiency and Generalization:** The sparse model demonstrates superior parameter efficiency and strong compositional generalization across varying object counts. This makes it more suitable for deployment in scenarios with dynamic object populations and limited computational resources. Its improved sample efficiency also reduces the data requirements for training.
- **Shift in Focus for World Models:** The work redefines the "win" for world models in sparsely changing environments. Instead of solely pursuing highly accurate absolute dynamics for all objects, it highlights the importance of robust change detection and error *avoidance* for static objects. This re-framing is particularly relevant for robotics where maintaining an accurate representation of the unperturbed environment is as crucial as predicting the precise motion of manipulated objects.

**Limitations and Future Directions (as acknowledged by the authors):**
The study is deliberately controlled and has specific boundaries: it uses structured per-object state (not pixels), rigid boxes, and small MLPs on a single laptop for a single pushing task. The authors openly acknowledge that the model's primary strength is preventing error injection, not achieving superior accuracy in predicting complex dynamics (especially rotation) of changed objects. Its planning performance, while separating from dense, is still modest compared to an oracle or scripted controller.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Robust Robotic Systems:** The demonstrated ability to suppress error accumulation on static scene elements could lead to more robust and reliable model-based control and planning algorithms for robotic manipulation.
- **Resource-Constrained Applications:** The parameter efficiency and sample efficiency make this approach attractive for applications with limited data or computational resources, such as on-robot learning or edge computing.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a strong baseline for integrating sparsity as an inductive bias into world models. Future research can build upon this by developing more accurate delta heads, incorporating distributional predictions, integrating with perception-first systems (e.g., slot-based encoders), and improving planning integration (e.g., DAgger for planner states).
- **Rigor in Reporting:** The paper's emphasis on "honestly framed" results, clearly delineating what the model "is and is not" capable of, sets a precedent for scientific rigor in reporting model performance and limitations.

In conclusion, this research presents a method for building more robust, interpretable, and efficient world models by explicitly modeling what changes. While challenges remain, particularly in accurate delta regression and planning integration, the core idea of preventing error injection into the static majority of a scene represents a valuable step forward for robot learning.
