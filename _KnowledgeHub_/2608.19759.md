# GOAG: Generative and Object-Agnostic Grasp Planner for Dexterous Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> GOAG is a gripper-centric grasp planner trained only on gripper kinematics, never object data. A CVAE learns feasible contact-point distributions from synthetic taxonomy-based gripper configurations; at inference, object geometry conditions sampling to retrieve compatible contacts. Achieves 86.93% average success on MultiDex, matching object-trained state-of-the-art while generalizing zero-shot to unseen objects.

## Problem

- Data-driven grasp planners train on object-specific grasp databases, biasing models toward training geometry and limiting generalization to novel, real-world object shapes.
- Analytical grasp planners like GraspIt! satisfy physical constraints but are too slow for real-time multi-fingered hand deployment given kinematic complexity.
- Existing intermediate-representation and direct-prediction methods still depend on costly pre-computed object-grasp databases, tying learning capacity to a finite object distribution.

## Method

- ==Object-Agnostic Contact Data Generation==: samples gripper joint configurations and grasp-taxonomy labels, producing 3M contact-point clouds purely from gripper kinematics, no object involved.
- ==Conditional Variational Autoencoder== over ==Basis Point Set== encodings learns the gripper's intrinsic contact-distribution manifold, reconstructing contact likelihood fields with an attention-weighted loss.
- ==PointNet++ Links Mapper== assigns predicted contact points to specific gripper phalanges, feeding a ==force-closure check== and ==energy-minimization joint optimization== for final grasp execution.

## Results

- On MultiDex across Barrett, Allegro, and ShadowHand grippers, GOAG reaches **86.93%** average success rate, beating DRO-Grasp (72.47%) and GenDexGrasp (70.96%).
- GOAG generates grasps in **0.18-0.20 seconds** versus DRO-Grasp's per-grasp scaling and GenDexGrasp's 9.78-16.45 seconds, via vectorized batch optimization.
- Training data generation costs only **1 GPU hour** versus 1,400 GPU hours reported for prior object-specific grasp database construction, a **1400x reduction**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Contact geometry at gripper-object interfaces is symmetric, so learning purely from gripper kinematics can substitute for expensive object-labeled grasp datasets.
> - Taxonomy-constrained procedural sampling gives structural validity to synthetic training data without needing any real object-grasp examples.
> - Object-agnostic training trades a small generalization gap versus per-dataset-trained specialists for large gains in data cost and cross-dataset transfer robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous grasping, particularly with multifingered robotic hands, remains a complex area in robotics. Traditional analytical methods, such as GraspIt!, often face challenges with real-time performance due to the kinematic complexity of modern robotic hands like Allegro, ShadowHand, and Barrett Hand. While these analytical methods can provide physically validated grasps, their computational intensity limits their applicability in dynamic environments.

In recent years, data-driven approaches, particularly those leveraging deep learning, have emerged to address the speed limitations of analytical methods. These methods typically learn to map object representations to grasp configurations. However, a significant limitation of many contemporary data-driven grasp planners is their dependence on large, object-specific grasp databases for training. Such datasets are expensive and time-consuming to generate, often requiring extensive human demonstrations, motion capture, teleoperation, or automated synthetic generation in physically realistic simulators. This reliance on specific training data inherently biases the models toward seen object geometries, which in turn restricts their ability to generalize to novel objects or entirely different shapes in real-world scenarios. The necessity for post-hoc validation steps to ensure physical constraints and stability is also a common issue for some direct-prediction methods.

Existing data-driven methods generally fall into two categories:
- **Learning Explicit Grasps:** These approaches directly predict a complete grasp configuration (gripper pose and joint values) from an object's representation. Examples include regression models, conditional generative models, diffusion models, and foundation models. While expressive, these methods frequently require computationally intensive post-hoc validation or learned discriminators to prevent penetrations or unstable grasps.
- **Learning Intermediate Representations:** These methods predict an intermediate representation, most commonly a contact map defining desired grasp regions on an object's surface. An inverse kinematics solver then derives the final grasp pose, improving physical validity and inheriting stability from the training data. This category includes approaches that extract gripper-specific features to manage multi-embodiment challenges. However, these methods still depend on pre-existing object-grasp databases for training.

The GOAG research distinguishes itself by proposing a fundamental shift from object-centric or grasp-centric paradigms to a **gripper-centric** perspective. This approach aims to decouple the learning phase from object-specific data, thereby directly addressing the generalization bottleneck and the high cost associated with generating object-specific grasp datasets. By focusing on the intrinsic contact capabilities of the gripper, GOAG seeks to create a data-driven grasp planner that is object-agnostic at training time, enabling zero-shot generalization to arbitrary object shapes. This approach contributes to the field by offering a method that potentially reduces training data requirements, enhances generalization, and improves the adaptability of robotic manipulation systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a deep generative grasp planner that effectively overcomes the generalization challenges faced by traditional data-driven methods, particularly their struggle to adapt to novel objects. This overarching goal is motivated by the observation that existing approaches are inherently limited by their reliance on object-specific training data, which leads to models that perform well on seen objects but exhibit reduced performance on unseen geometries.

The specific objectives and underlying motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Address the Generalization Bottleneck:** A key motivation is the pervasive issue that current deep-learning grasp planners, while efficient for known objects, exhibit limited generalization capabilities. This limitation arises because they are trained on datasets that map specific grippers to specific objects, embedding a bias toward the geometries encountered during training. The researchers aim to develop a method that can effectively generalize to arbitrary, novel object shapes without requiring prior exposure to these objects in the training phase.

- **Introduce an Object-Agnostic Learning Strategy:** The central objective is to propose and implement a training paradigm where no object geometry is utilized during the learning phase. The motivation for this is rooted in the observation that the contact points between a gripper and an object share identical surface geometry from both perspectives. By capitalizing on this, the authors hypothesize that a model can learn the "combinatorial possibilities of contact areas inherent to a specific gripper design" independent of any target object. This decouples the learning of gripper capabilities from the specifics of object shapes.

- **Learn a Compact Latent Representation of Gripper Contact Surface Distribution:** The research aims to train a deep generative model, specifically a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE), to learn the intrinsic distribution of feasible contact zones solely from the gripper’s kinematics and an adapted grasp taxonomy. The motivation here is to capture the underlying principles of how a gripper can interact with surfaces, rather than learning specific successful interactions with particular objects. This latent representation is intended to be compact and efficient for subsequent sampling.

- **Enable Efficient Sampling of Valid Grasp Configurations by Introducing Object Features Solely at Inference Time:** A critical objective is to design a system where object features are only incorporated during the inference phase. This allows the pre-trained, object-agnostic model to retrieve admissible contact areas that are compatible with the gripper’s capabilities when presented with a novel object. This approach aims to maintain efficiency while ensuring geometric and kinematic compatibility between the gripper and the object, avoiding the need to re-train or fine-tune for each new object.

- **Validate Performance in Both Simulated and Real-World Scenarios:** The research seeks to empirically demonstrate the effectiveness and generalization capabilities of the proposed method through extensive experiments. This includes evaluation on established grasp protocols and benchmarks, assessing success rates, efficiency, and grasp diversity, as well as showcasing successful deployment on a real robotic system. This validation is crucial to establish the practical utility and robustness of the object-agnostic paradigm.

In summary, the research is motivated by the limitations of data-intensive, object-specific grasp planning and aims to introduce a more scalable and generalized approach by shifting the learning focus from objects to the inherent capabilities of the gripper.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The GOAG methodology introduces a gripper-centric, object-agnostic approach to dexterous grasp planning, fundamentally altering how contact points are conceptualized and learned. Instead of defining contact regions on the object, GOAG defines them as intrinsic properties of the gripper. The method consists of a training phase that is independent of object data and an inference phase where object information is introduced.

**A. Shifting to a Gripper-Oriented Paradigm**
The core idea is to analyze grasping from the gripper’s canonical coordinate frame. By transforming the object into the gripper’s frame, contact points can be defined as a subset of the gripper's surface (H(Q)) that are in proximity and alignment with the object (O([R, T]⁻¹)). This conceptual shift allows the model to learn the intrinsic distribution of feasible contact zones purely from the gripper’s kinematics and a grasp taxonomy, without requiring any object geometry during training.

**B. Training GOAG from C(H(Q))**
The training process focuses on generating diverse gripper configurations, representing them consistently, and training two parallel neural networks.

1. **Contact Data Generation:**
 - **Gripper Configurations:** 10,000 kinematically valid gripper joint configurations (Q) are uniformly sampled. For each configuration, a corresponding surface point cloud (H(Q)) is extracted. Active point clouds are obtained by applying a threshold to the dot product of vertex normals and the palm's facing direction.
 - **Grasp Taxonomy:** An adapted human grasp taxonomy from is utilized, selecting 6 common grasp types suitable for robotic grippers. Each grasp type defines specific admissible contact regions on the gripper surface (e.g., C6, F27).
 - **Contact Point Sampling:** For each gripper configuration and its assigned grasp type, 50 sets of active contact points are randomly sampled *within* these admissible regions. This procedural generation results in a dataset of 3,000,000 labeled point clouds, ensuring kinematic harmony and realistic, synergistic grasps without object data. The entire dataset generation took approximately 1 GPU hour, contrasting with significantly longer times for object-specific datasets.

2. **BPS Encoding and Distance Field:**
 - To process variable-length point clouds, a fixed-size representation is employed using a Basis Point Set (BPS).
 - **Workspace (W):** The gripper’s kinematic workspace is discretized into M = 8,192 points, forming the BPS, which naturally concentrates data where grasps typically occur.
 - **Aligned Distance Field (F_P):** For any input point cloud P (H(Q) during training, O during inference), an aligned distance field F_P ∈ ℝᴹ is computed. This field measures the aligned distance D_aligned (introduced by) from each basis point in W to the point cloud P, considering both proximity and surface normal alignment.
 - **Projected Contact Field (~C(P)):** A supervision signal, `~C(P)` ∈ ℝᴹ, quantifies the proximity and alignment of each basis point wᵢ to the active contact points C(P). It provides a continuous "contact likelihood" (0-1), computed using a Sigmoid function of the aligned distance.

3. **CVAE Architecture and Training:**
 - A Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) is trained to learn the distribution of feasible contacts.
 - **Encoder:** Takes the concatenated input [F_P, `~C(P)`] (M × 2) and predicts the parameters of the posterior distribution q_θ(z | F_P, `~C(P)`), where z ∈ ℝᵧ is a latent variable.
 - **Decoder:** Takes the concatenation of z and F_P to output predicted contact values `^C(P)`. It comprises two Fully Connected ResBlocks.
 - **Loss Function:** Optimized using L = L_recon + βD_KL, where L_recon is an L2-Norm loss scaled by an attention weight (e^(αcᵢ)) to prioritize accurate reconstruction of high-likelihood contact regions. β weights the Kullback-Leibler divergence to regularize the latent space.
 - **Hyperparameters:** Latent dimension ψ=128, β=0.01, α=3.0. Trained for 100 epochs.

4. **Links Mapper:**
 - A PointNet++ network is trained in parallel with the CVAE. It associates each sampled contact point pᵢ ∈ C(P) with its corresponding gripper phalanx link lᵢ. This provides kinematic meaning to the spatial contact points, which is vital for the subsequent grasp optimization. Trained for 50 epochs.

**C. GOAG Inference on O([R, T]⁻¹)**
During inference, the trained models predict contact zones on unseen objects.

1. **Object Encoding:** Given a novel object point cloud O, it is first transformed into the gripper’s canonical workspace via [R, T]⁻¹. Its aligned distance field F_O is then computed against the same basis set W used during training.
2. **Contact Prediction:**
 - A latent vector z is sampled from a standard normal distribution (z ~ N(0, I)). The CVAE encoder is bypassed.
 - The CVAE decoder takes [z, F_O] and predicts the continuous contact field `^C(O)`.
 - Intended contact locations are extracted by selecting basis points from W with a predicted contact likelihood `^cᵢ` above a confidence threshold (τ=0.8), yielding a discrete set of contact points C(W).
3. **Link Assignment:** The pre-trained PointNet++ Links Mapper assigns each point in C(W) to a specific gripper phalanx.
4. **Contact Points Force-Closure Estimation:**
 - Unlike methods trained on pre-validated grasp datasets, GOAG requires an explicit stability assessment.
 - **Contact Modeling:** Predicted contact points are grouped by phalanx, and their barycenters are computed and projected onto the object's surface to define discrete contact locations bᵢ. A Coulomb friction model (μ=0.3) is assumed.
 - **Force-Closure Computation:** The grasp wrench space is computed as the convex hull of wrenches generated by unit contact forces at bᵢ. Force-closure is verified by checking if the origin lies strictly within this convex hull.
 - **Resampling Strategy:** If force-closure is not met, a new latent variable z is sampled to generate new `^C(O)`. This process is limited to 20 iterations to prevent exhaustive searches for poorly conditioned object poses.
5. **Grasp Generation (Kinematic Optimization):**
 - The final gripper joint configuration Q* is determined by minimizing an energy function to align the gripper with the inferred contact points while strictly penalizing collisions.
 - **Energy Function:** Q* = argmin_Q (λ_d E_dist + λ_p E_pen + λ_s E_spen + λ_j E_j).
 - **E_dist (Contact Distance):** Minimizes the distance from C(W) to their assigned gripper links.
 - **E_pen (Object Penetration):** Penalizes gripper points penetrating the object's signed distance field (Φ_O).
 - **E_spen (Self-Penetration):** Enforces self-collision avoidance between disjoint gripper links, maintaining a safety threshold (ϵ=0.025m).
 - **E_j (Joint Limits):** Constrains joint values within hardware kinematic limits.
 - Weighting coefficients are set to λ_d=1.0, λ_p=50.0, λ_s=0.1, λ_j=1.0.

**D. Grasp Pose Generation Strategy**
To ensure comprehensive grasp coverage for objects, gripper poses are sampled uniformly on the object’s convex hull, dilated by 110%. The gripper’s palm is oriented opposite to the hull’s normal vector, ensuring diverse and well-distributed grasp candidates.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents detailed evaluations of GOAG's performance in simulation and real-world scenarios, demonstrating its capabilities in terms of grasp success rate, efficiency, and generalization.

**A. Computational Efficiency**
A notable finding is the efficiency of the object-agnostic training data generation. The entire dataset of 3 million labeled point clouds required approximately 1 GPU hour on an Nvidia RTX 4090. This contrasts with previous object-specific data generation methods, which could require significantly more resources (e.g., 1,400 GPU hours for a comparable dataset). This suggests a substantial reduction in the computational cost of preparing training data for grasp planners.

**B. In-depth Grasp Performance Analysis (MultiDex Dataset)**
Experiments were conducted on the MultiDex dataset, which includes 10 objects from ContactDB and YCB datasets, using three dexterous grippers: Barrett Hand, Allegro Hand, and Shadow Hand.

- **Success Rate:** GOAG achieved an average grasp success rate of 86.93% across all three grippers. This rate was higher than the evaluated baselines: DFC (79.32%), GenDexGrasp (70.96%), and DRO-Grasp (72.47%). Notably, even without the explicit Force Closure estimation step (GOAG w/o FC), the method still achieved an average success rate of 84.07%, suggesting that the model implicitly learns robust grasp mechanics through its gripper-centric training.
- **Efficiency:** GOAG demonstrated efficiency in generating numerous grasps. While the time per single grasp (0.18-0.20 seconds per grasp across grippers) might appear slower than DRO-Grasp (0.09-1.72 seconds, depending on the gripper), GOAG benefits from a vectorized optimization process that minimizes a single energy function for all grasp candidates simultaneously. This approach results in faster processing when generating a large number of grasps, contrasting with methods like GenDexGrasp, which do not vectorize optimization.
- **Diversity:** The model's ability to generate diverse grasps was evaluated by the standard deviation of gripper position (T), orientation (R), and joint values (Q). The diversity in successful grasps is influenced by the object's geometry and the gripper's kinematics, with GOAG showing distinct finger configurations for similar poses by sampling from the CVAE's latent space.

**C. Generalization Across Multiple Grasp Datasets**
To assess generalization, GOAG was evaluated on five diverse benchmark datasets (DexGraspNet, UniDexGrasp, MultiDex, RealDex, DexGRAB), totaling 3438 objects, using only the Shadow Hand.

- **Zero-Shot Generalization:** GOAG achieved an average success rate of 53.97% across these five datasets. This result is presented as significant because GOAG was trained *only once* on Shadow Hand kinematics in an object-agnostic manner. In contrast, competing state-of-the-art methods, such as DexGrasp Anything (DGA), which achieved a higher average success rate of 58.48%, were specifically *retrained for each respective dataset*. This comparison highlights GOAG's strong generalization capabilities without dataset-specific training.
- **Performance on Specific Datasets:** GOAG achieved a success rate of 77.90% on the MultiDex dataset (consistent with the in-depth analysis), which was competitive with DGA's 79.1%. For the DexGRAB dataset, GOAG achieved 62.13%, outperforming DGA's 57.9%. While DGA achieved higher success rates on other individual datasets, the primary finding is GOAG's consistent performance across diverse datasets with a single, object-agnostic training. The authors suggest that adopting more advanced pose sampling strategies for larger objects, beyond their current uniform convex hull sampling, could further improve performance on such objects.

**D. Real-Robot Experiments**
The method was validated in real-world scenarios using an Allegro Left Hand mounted on a 7-DoF robot arm. GOAG successfully grasped 11 objects from the YCB Dataset (Figure 5). This demonstrates the transferability of the simulated and object-agnostic learning to physical robots and real objects.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research conducted on GOAG presents several significant contributions to the field of robotic manipulation and has potential implications for future developments.

**A. Addressing the Generalization Bottleneck in Grasp Planning:**
The primary significance of GOAG lies in its proposed solution to the generalization challenge inherent in many data-driven grasp planners. By developing an object-agnostic training paradigm, the method demonstrates that robust grasping strategies can be learned from the gripper's intrinsic capabilities rather than from specific object geometries. This represents a fundamental shift from data-intensive, object-specific learning to a more generalized, gripper-centric approach.

**B. Reduction in Data Generation Costs and Training Requirements:**
GOAG significantly reduces the computational and resource costs associated with developing grasp planners. The authors report that their dataset creation required approximately 1 GPU hour, a substantial reduction compared to the thousands of GPU hours typically needed for generating large-scale, object-specific grasp databases for other methods. This cost efficiency can make advanced dexterous grasping more accessible for research and development, potentially accelerating innovation in the field by removing a major data bottleneck.

**C. Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability for Novel Objects:**
The demonstrated zero-shot generalization capability of GOAG allows robots to grasp previously unseen objects effectively without requiring retraining or fine-tuning. This enhances the robustness and adaptability of robotic systems, enabling them to operate in unstructured or dynamic environments where they may encounter a wide variety of unknown objects. This capability is crucial for applications in logistics, domestic robotics, manufacturing, and other domains requiring flexible manipulation.

**D. Introduction of a Novel Learning Paradigm:**
The gripper-centric perspective, where contact areas are defined as intrinsic properties of the gripper and learned through an adapted grasp taxonomy, establishes a novel paradigm for grasp planning. This approach, leveraging a Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE) and a Links Mapper, offers a unique way to model geometric graspability and kinematic feasibility. This new paradigm could inspire further research into similar decoupled learning strategies for other complex robotic tasks.

**E. Practical Applicability and Real-World Transferability:**
The successful deployment of GOAG on a real robotic system (Allegro Left Hand grasping YCB objects) validates its practical applicability. The ability to transfer learned strategies from simulation to the physical world without specific object training demonstrates the method's potential for immediate integration into real-world robotic manipulation tasks. The competitive performance against state-of-the-art methods, particularly its strong generalization across multiple benchmarks without dataset-specific retraining, underscores its readiness for deployment in varied contexts.

**F. Foundation for Future Research:**
The work opens avenues for future research, such as exploring more advanced or adaptive grasp pose sampling strategies, especially for objects larger than the gripper's workspace, to further improve performance. It also encourages deeper investigation into how implicit learning of robust grasp mechanics within the generative model can be further optimized and understood.
