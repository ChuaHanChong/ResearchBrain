# Evo-1: Lightweight Vision-Language-Action Model with Preserved Semantic Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> Evo-1 introduces a lightweight Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model that achieves state-of-the-art robotic manipulation performance with significantly reduced computational requirements. The model, comprising 0.77 billion parameters, utilizes only 2.3 GB of GPU memory and reaches an inference frequency of 16.4 Hz, outperforming larger VLA systems across diverse simulation and real-world benchmarks without extensive robot data pretraining.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are prohibitively large, demanding extensive compute/GPU memory that leads to low control frequencies, hindering real-time deployment and responsiveness for dynamic, interactive robotic tasks.
- End-to-end fine-tuning of pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for action generation often degrades their inherent semantic and perceptual representations, leading to task-specific overfitting.
- Existing high-performing VLA models often rely on costly and labor-intensive large-scale robot datasets for pretraining, limiting accessibility and scalability.

## Method

- A modular Vision-Language-Action (VLA) architecture leveraging a lightweight InternVL3-1B backbone (0.77B parameters total) for efficient multimodal encoding and a Cross-modulated ==Diffusion Transformer== (action expert) for continuous action generation.
- The action expert employs a ==flow-matching== paradigm with a unique Diffusion Transformer (DiT) that uses only stacked cross-attention layers, conditioned on VLM representations and robot state.
- A two-stage training procedure is implemented: first, freeze the VLM backbone to align the action expert, then fine-tune the entire architecture to preserve semantic alignment and enhance task adaptation.

## Results

- Achieved state-of-the-art performance across simulation benchmarks (**80.6%** Meta-World, **94.8%** LIBERO, **37.8%** RoboTwin dual-arm) and an average **78%** success rate on real-world xArm6 tasks, outperforming baselines at roughly one-fourth the size of comparable models.
- Exhibited high inference efficiency, using only **2.3 GB** of GPU memory and achieving an inference frequency of **16.4 Hz**, making it suitable for real-time deployment on consumer-grade hardware.
- Showed superior generalization capabilities in real-world environments under various unseen disturbances (e.g., distractors, background changes, position variations), with ablation studies confirming the benefits of the two-stage training paradigm.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Employing a Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone natively pre-trained on multimodal data significantly enhances cross-modal alignment, which is crucial for visuomotor tasks.
> - A two-stage training paradigm, where the action expert is aligned with a frozen VLM before full fine-tuning, is effective in preserving the VLM's semantic representations, leading to improved generalization and reduced overfitting.
> - A cross-attention-based integration module, specifically using concatenated multimodal and proprioceptive features as key-value inputs to the action expert's mid-layers, optimizes information flow for robust action generation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represents a significant frontier in artificial intelligence and robotics. These models aim to imbue robots with the ability to understand natural language instructions, perceive their environment through vision, and translate this understanding into physical actions, thereby unifying perception, language, and control. This field has been heavily inspired by the remarkable success of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in multimodal understanding and reasoning.

Currently, the VLA landscape is dominated by large-scale models, such as OpenVLA, π0, and Hi-Robot. While these models have demonstrated impressive capabilities in few-shot generalization and complex manipulation tasks, they suffer from several critical limitations that Evo-1 directly addresses:
1. **Massive Scale and Computational Cost:** Existing VLA models often contain billions of parameters, leading to substantial GPU memory usage and high computational costs during both training and inference. This makes them difficult to deploy in real-time on typical robotic hardware or consumer-grade GPUs.
2. **Low Control Frequency:** The computational overhead limits the model's inference speed, resulting in low control frequencies that hinder real-time responsiveness crucial for interactive robotic tasks.
3. **Degradation of Perceptual Representations:** Many VLA training paradigms involve end-to-end fine-tuning of pre-trained VLM backbones. This often degrades the VLM's inherent perceptual and linguistic representations, leading to overfitting on specific robotic tasks and poor generalization to novel scenarios.
4. **Reliance on Large-Scale Robot Data Pretraining:** A significant portion of these high-performing models heavily relies on massive, labor-intensive, and costly robot datasets like OXE or DROID for pretraining. This limits their accessibility and scalability.

Into this landscape, Evo-1 positions itself as a crucial advancement in the "lightweight and efficient VLA models" sub-area. Previous efforts in this direction, such as TinyVLA and SmolVLA, have aimed to reduce computational costs by employing compact architectures and smaller VLM backbones. While these models achieve improved efficiency, they often compromise on overall task performance and robustness in complex manipulation settings.

Evo-1 distinguishes itself by aiming to achieve the best of both worlds: being lightweight and efficient *without sacrificing strong performance or generalization capabilities*, and crucially, *without requiring large-scale robot data pretraining*. It seeks to overcome the efficiency-performance trade-off that has plagued earlier lightweight VLA models while mitigating the resource demands of their larger counterparts. By demonstrating state-of-the-art performance on various benchmarks with a significantly smaller model and without extensive robot data, Evo-1 sets a new benchmark for accessible and deployable robotic intelligence.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop **Evo-1**, a lightweight Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model that significantly reduces computational resource requirements for training and inference while maintaining, and in many cases surpassing, the performance of larger, more complex VLA systems. This primary goal is driven by the practical need for deployable, real-time robotic solutions that can operate on less powerful, consumer-grade hardware.

More specifically, the key objectives and their underlying motivations can be broken down as follows:

1. **Reduce Computational Costs and Improve Deployment Efficiency:**
 - **Motivation:** Current VLA models are prohibitively expensive in terms of GPU memory and computational power, limiting their use to research labs with extensive resources. This hinders real-world deployment on cost-sensitive or embedded robotic platforms.
 - **Objective:** Design an architecture with a substantially smaller parameter count (0.77 billion parameters in Evo-1) to enable faster training, lower inference latency (higher control frequency), and reduced memory footprint.

2. **Achieve Strong Performance Without Robot Data Pretraining:**
 - **Motivation:** Collecting large-scale, diverse robot manipulation datasets is a labor-intensive, costly, and time-consuming endeavor. Models reliant on such datasets are difficult for smaller research groups or individual developers to replicate or adapt.
 - **Objective:** Develop a VLA model that achieves state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance across diverse simulation and real-world tasks using only domain-specific demonstrations (e.g., 50 per task) for fine-tuning, thereby eliminating the need for extensive, costly robot data pretraining.

3. **Preserve Semantic Alignment and Perceptual Representations:**
 - **Motivation:** Standard end-to-end fine-tuning of powerful, pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for action generation often leads to a degradation of their original semantic understanding. This "catastrophic forgetting" can result in overfitting to specific tasks and poor generalization to novel visual-linguistic contexts.
 - **Objective:** Implement a training paradigm that effectively adapts the VLM backbone for action generation while carefully preserving its rich, inherent multimodal understanding and semantic space, thereby enhancing generalization and robustness.

4. **Enable Real-time Responsiveness and Stable Control:**
 - **Motivation:** Low control frequencies in large VLA models prevent robots from reacting quickly and fluidly in dynamic or interactive environments, which is crucial for safety and efficacy in real-world scenarios.
 - **Objective:** The lightweight architecture combined with an efficient action expert aims to achieve a high inference frequency, enabling responsive and stable control for real-time human-robot interaction and dynamic task execution.

By tackling these challenges, Evo-1 aims to democratize access to advanced robotic capabilities, making sophisticated multimodal robot control more accessible, affordable, and practical for a wider range of applications and research endeavors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Evo-1 employs a modular, computationally efficient Vision-Language-Action (VLA) architecture combined with a novel two-stage training paradigm. This integrated approach is designed to balance strong performance with resource efficiency and semantic preservation.

#### 3.1. Overview of Evo-1 Architecture

The architecture comprises three core components:
1. **Vision-Language Backbone:** Responsible for encoding multimodal representations from visual observations and textual instructions.
2. **Cross-modulated Diffusion Transformer (Action Expert):** Generates continuous control actions based on the learned representations.
3. **Integration Module:** Bridges the perception and control components by efficiently aligning multimodal and proprioceptive (robot state) representations.

Given multi-view visual inputs ($\{I^i_t\}$), a language instruction ($L_t$), and the robot state ($s_t$), Evo-1 produces a continuous action vector ($a_t$).

#### 3.2. Model Design: Vision-Language Backbone

Evo-1 utilizes **InternVL3-1B** as its vision-language backbone. This model is chosen because it is pretrained under a *native multimodal paradigm*, meaning it jointly learns linguistic and visual understanding from large-scale multimodal and textual corpora, ensuring tight cross-modal alignment from the outset. This contrasts with post-hoc alignment pipelines that adapt text-only LLMs for images, which can be less efficient.

- **Visual Encoder:** Employs **InternViT-300M**, a lightweight transformer distilled from InternViT-6B. It processes RGB observations (resized to 448x448) and applies a pixel-unshuffle downsampling operation, reducing visual token count by 4x. This yields compact yet expressive patch embeddings.
- **Language Branch:** Leverages **Qwen2.5-0.5B**, a transformer-based decoder with 0.5 billion parameters. Despite its size, it effectively captures task semantics.
- **Vision-Language Fusion:** InternVL3-1B fuses patch-level image embeddings into the token sequence by replacing a designated `<img>` placeholder token. The fused sequence is then processed by a shared transformer decoder. To optimize for visuomotor tasks, only the first 14 layers of the language branch are retained, as empirical evidence suggests intermediate layers exhibit stronger cross-modal alignment relevant for control.

#### 3.2.2. Cross-modulated Diffusion Transformer (Action Expert)

This module acts as a conditional denoising model to predict continuous control actions. It follows the **flow-matching paradigm**, learning a time-dependent vector field to progressively transform a noisy action into the ground-truth target.

- **Architecture:** Implemented as a **Diffusion Transformer (DiT)** that uniquely relies *solely on stacked cross-attention layers*, differentiating it from prior VLA models that often use alternating self-attention and cross-attention.
- **Action Generation:** A noisy action sequence is generated by linearly interpolating between a ground-truth action and a random noise vector.
- **Objective:** The action expert learns a time-conditioned velocity field to guide the interpolated action towards the true action under the multimodal context and robot state, using a flow-matching loss function.
- **Inference:** The network predicts a sequence of `H` future actions (action trunk) conditioned on the fused VLM representation, current robot state, and interpolated action.

#### 3.2.3. Integration Module

Evo-1 uses a **cross-attention-based integration module** to fuse multimodal (vision-language) and proprioceptive (robot state) information.

- **Mechanism:** The fused multimodal representation ($z_t$) from the 14th VLM layer is directly *concatenated* with the robot state ($s_t$). This concatenated feature then serves as the *key-value input* for the transformer blocks of the action expert. This direct concatenation is preferred over projecting into a shared embedding space, as it preserves complete information from both sources.
- **Ablation Study:** Out of four designs evaluated, Module A (Mid-Layer Cross-Attention, where the concatenated feature is used as K/V for all DiT layers with noisy action as query) proved most effective due to consistent multimodal information propagation.

#### 3.3. Two-Stage Training Procedure

To achieve the crucial balance between preserving VLM semantics and adapting to action generation, Evo-1 employs a two-stage training paradigm:

1. **Stage 1: Action Expert Alignment:**
 - **Process:** The entire vision-language backbone is frozen. Only the integration module and the action expert are trained.
 - **Purpose:** This stage allows the randomly initialized action expert to gradually align its weights with the stable, frozen multimodal embedding space of the VLM, preventing noisy gradients from corrupting the pre-trained backbone.

2. **Stage 2: Full-scale Fine-Tuning:**
 - **Process:** After Stage 1, the VLM backbone is unfrozen, and the entire architecture (VLM, integration module, and action expert) is fine-tuned jointly.
 - **Purpose:** This enables deeper integration and refinement, allowing the VLM's representations to adapt further to diverse manipulation tasks while building upon the already established alignment with the action expert.

This two-stage approach demonstrably preserves the VLM's semantic space, as evidenced by clearer and more semantically consistent attention maps compared to single-stage training, which leads to better generalization and reduced overfitting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Evo-1 consistently demonstrates state-of-the-art performance across a wide array of robotic tasks in both simulation and real-world environments, notably achieving these results with a lightweight architecture and without large-scale robot data pretraining.

#### 4.1. Simulation Benchmarks

Evo-1 was rigorously evaluated on three challenging simulation benchmarks: Meta-World, LIBERO, and RoboTwin.

- **Meta-World Benchmark:** Evo-1 achieved a new state-of-the-art average success rate of **80.6%**, significantly outperforming previous best models like SmolVLA (68.2%) and π0 (47.9%). Crucially, Evo-1, with only 0.77 billion parameters, maintained superior performance across all four difficulty levels (easy, medium, hard, very hard), highlighting its efficiency and strong capability in diverse single-arm manipulation scenarios.
- **LIBERO Benchmark:** Evo-1 attained an average success rate of **94.8%**, surpassing strong baselines such as π0 (94.2%) and SmolVLA (88.8%). It showed particularly high robustness on long-horizon tasks (92.3%), where other VLA models often exhibit degradation, demonstrating its ability for complex procedural knowledge transfer.
- **RoboTwin Benchmark (Dual-arm):** For dual-arm manipulation, Evo-1 secured the highest overall average success rate of **37.8%**, exceeding the previous SOTA model π0 (30.9%). Its performance on tasks like "Click Alarmclock" showcased precise bimanual coordination and action consistency, even without the benefit of large-scale pretraining.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

Evo-1 was tested on four diverse real-world tasks using an xArm6 robotic arm: Pick and Place Can, Pour Foam from Cup, Hand Delivery, and Can Stacking.

- **Success Rate:** Evo-1 achieved an impressive average success rate of **78%** across these tasks. This substantially outperformed other baselines like SmolVLA (50%), OpenVLA-OFT (55%), and even the larger π0 (73%), despite Evo-1 being roughly one-fourth its size.
- **Inference Efficiency:** Evo-1 demonstrated exceptional efficiency:
 - **Parameters:** 0.77 Billion, significantly smaller than OpenVLA (7B) and π0 (3.5B).
 - **GPU Memory:** Consumed only 2.3 GB, the lowest among compared models.
 - **Inference Frequency:** Achieved the highest frequency of **16.4 Hz**, enabling real-time, responsive control in physical deployments. This highlights its superior balance between efficiency and performance.

#### 4.3. Generalization Experiments

Real-world generalization was assessed on the Pick and Place Can task under various unseen disturbance conditions (unseen distractor objects, background color changes, target position/height variations).

- Evo-1 consistently outperformed SmolVLA across all disturbance settings. For instance, it achieved 80% success with an unseen distractor (vs. SmolVLA's 65%) and 75% with a background change (vs. 60%). It also maintained high success rates under significant target position and height variations (e.g., 80% for 30mm backward shift vs. 60% for SmolVLA). This robust performance underscores its superior generalization capabilities.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

Key architectural and training strategy choices were validated through ablation studies.

- **Integration Module Analysis:** Comparisons of four integration module designs (Module A-D) showed that **Module A (Mid-Layer Cross-Attention)** yielded the best performance (96.3% on LIBERO-Long). This was attributed to its design ensuring consistent propagation of multimodal information to the action expert, leading to more coherent conditioning.
- **Training Paradigm Comparison:** The proposed **two-stage training paradigm** consistently outperformed a single-stage baseline across all Meta-World difficulty levels. Attention map visualizations confirmed that the two-stage approach effectively preserved the semantic attention patterns of the VLM, maintaining clear focus on relevant object regions, whereas single-stage training led to disrupted patterns and reduced semantic coherence. This preservation was critical for enhancing generalization and mitigating overfitting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Evo-1 presents a significant step forward in the field of robotic learning, addressing fundamental challenges that have historically limited the widespread adoption and deployment of advanced VLA models. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Democratization of Robotic AI:** By offering a lightweight (0.77B parameters) yet high-performing VLA model that doesn't require massive GPU clusters or specialized hardware, Evo-1 dramatically lowers the barrier to entry for developing and deploying sophisticated robot behaviors. This can empower smaller research labs, startups, and even individual developers to engage in advanced robotic AI research and application, fostering broader innovation.

2. **Enabling Real-time, Responsive Robotics:** The achievement of a high inference frequency (16.4 Hz) with minimal GPU memory consumption (2.3 GB) is crucial for real-world robotics. This capability allows robots to react swiftly and smoothly to dynamic environments, human interactions, and unexpected events, enhancing safety, efficiency, and fluidity in complex tasks that demand real-time perception-action loops.

3. **Reducing Environmental and Economic Costs:** The reduced computational requirements translate directly into lower energy consumption during training and inference, contributing to more sustainable AI development. Furthermore, by eliminating the need for pretraining on expensive, large-scale robot datasets, Evo-1 significantly cuts down the economic cost and labor associated with data collection, making advanced robotic solutions more economically viable.

4. **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The novel two-stage training paradigm, designed to preserve the semantic alignment of the pre-trained Vision-Language Model backbone, is a key methodological contribution. This preservation mitigates overfitting and leads to superior generalization capabilities, allowing Evo-1 to perform robustly under unseen variations, distractors, and environmental changes. This ability to generalize from limited demonstrations to novel situations is paramount for practical robotic systems.

5. **Catalyst for Future Research:** The release of code, data, and model weights by the MINT-SJTU group fosters an open-science environment. This generosity will enable other researchers to build upon Evo-1's advancements, facilitating further exploration into lightweight VLA architectures, more efficient training strategies, and novel applications of multimodal robotic control. It could lead to a new wave of research focused on balancing performance with efficiency.

6. **Broader Applicability:** Evo-1's capabilities—lightweight design, high performance, and generalization—make it suitable for a wider array of real-world applications. These could include domestic service robots, logistics and warehouse automation, educational robotics, and small-scale industrial manipulation, particularly where cost, power efficiency, and real-time responsiveness are critical constraints. The demonstrated success in dual-arm manipulation further expands its potential to more complex coordinated tasks.

In conclusion, Evo-1 represents a paradigm shift towards truly deployable and accessible Vision-Language-Action models. By intelligently addressing the computational and data demands of state-of-the-art robotic intelligence, it paves the way for a future where sophisticated robot control is not confined to heavily resourced laboratories but can be implemented broadly, fostering innovation across the entire robotics ecosystem.
