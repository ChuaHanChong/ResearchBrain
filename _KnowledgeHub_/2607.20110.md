# Extreme-RGMT: Continual Learning of Highly Dynamic Skills for Robust Generalist Humanoid Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Extreme-RGMT, a two-stage continual reinforcement learning framework, enables humanoid robots to acquire highly dynamic motor skills while preserving broad generalist competence. The system was successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 robot, reliably executing complex maneuvers such as aerial cartwheels and backflips, even with noisy online teleoperation inputs from inertial motion capture.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid controllers face a trade-off: generalist policies track diverse motions but fail on highly dynamic ones, while specialist policies excel at specific dynamic tasks but degrade generalist capabilities.
- Reliably executing highly dynamic motions (e.g., aerial maneuvers) is challenging because critical control segments are sparse, and learning signals from failures are often diluted in large datasets.
- Specialist skills often rely on meticulously preprocessed offline motion data, but real-time human-robot interaction requires robustness to noisy, inconsistent online reference inputs like inertial motion capture.

## Method

- A two-stage continual learning framework (**Extreme-RGMT**) is introduced, first establishing a generalist base policy and then progressively expanding it to acquire highly dynamic skills.
- ==PACE== (Progressive Acquisition and Consolidation for Expansion) employs an asymmetric training objective, dedicating resources to specialist skill acquisition while using an ==L2 regularization== term and progress-adaptive weighting to consolidate generalist capabilities.
- ==STAR== (Segment-Aware Trajectory Advantage Resampling) improves learning from sparse, effective experiences in highly dynamic motions by ==difficulty-conditioned advantage normalization== and prioritizing high-advantage trajectory fragments in mini-batches.

## Results

- Extreme-RGMT achieved high success rates on both generalist (in-source and unseen) and highly dynamic specialist motions, demonstrating superior unified performance compared to existing generalist and specialist methods.
- The framework reliably executed **all** highly dynamic motions from the XtremeMotion dataset and showed significantly increased robustness to lower-quality, directly retargeted motion inputs, outperforming specialist baselines by **40.8%** on Xsens data.
- Successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, the policy enabled robust real-time teleoperation of complex dynamic skills (e.g., standing tucked flip, air twist kick) using noisy online Xsens inertial motion capture inputs, validating sim-to-real transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Asymmetrically allocating learning resources for skill acquisition and capability consolidation (PACE) effectively manages the trade-off between acquiring new dynamic skills and retaining established generalist behaviors.
> - Prioritizing and resampling learning experiences from critical, high-advantage trajectory fragments within difficult motion segments (STAR) is essential for sustained improvement in highly dynamic, failure-prone skill acquisition.
> - Architectural enhancements like separate encoders for proprioceptive and action history, LayerNorm for feature normalization, and an FSQ bottleneck improve the policy's stability and robustness to diverse motion distributions and real-time reference inconsistencies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robot control aims to enable robots to execute complex motor skills, ideally approaching the versatility and robustness of human capabilities. A significant challenge in this domain is developing controllers that can manage a broad spectrum of motions—from everyday tasks to highly dynamic, agile maneuvers—while maintaining stability and adaptability.

Current research broadly falls into two categories: generalist motion tracking and specialist optimization. Generalist methods train unified controllers from large-scale motion libraries to cover diverse, often smoother, whole-body motions and support online teleoperation. Examples include GMT, UniTracker, SONIC, and RGMT, which focus on expanding motion coverage, data scale, and conditioning modalities. However, these methods typically struggle with expert-level, highly dynamic motions such as backflips or complex aerial maneuvers. The learning signals from rare, critical segments within these dynamic motions are often diluted by the abundance of regular motion samples, leading to unreliable execution.

Conversely, specialist optimization methods are tailored to achieve high performance on specific, challenging tasks (e.g., agile locomotion, fall recovery, martial arts). Works like KungfuBot, BeyondMimic, ZEST, and OmniXtreme demonstrate impressive execution of highly dynamic behaviors. These approaches often rely on task-specific training environments, reward formulations, and extensive preprocessing of motion data to ensure physical consistency. A limitation of specialist methods is their narrow focus, which can degrade previously acquired generalist capabilities (a phenomenon known as catastrophic forgetting in continual learning) and may not generalize well to low-quality, online reference inputs common in real-time teleoperation.

This work addresses the inherent trade-off between generalist and specialist capabilities. It draws inspiration from human motor learning, where fundamental skills are progressively expanded to include more challenging dynamic abilities without forgetting prior knowledge. The paper positions Extreme-RGMT as a framework designed to bridge the gap between robust generalist control and the acquisition of highly dynamic skills. It explicitly aims to overcome the limitations of both generalist methods (poor dynamic performance) and specialist methods (loss of generality, reliance on high-quality offline data) by developing a continual learning approach that expands dynamic capabilities while preserving broad generalist competence and robustness to real-time, imperfect human motion inputs. This aligns with the broader research direction of developing more adaptive, versatile, and human-like humanoid controllers capable of operating in unstructured and interactive environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a humanoid controller capable of progressively acquiring highly dynamic motor skills while simultaneously retaining broad and stable generalist motor competence. This overarching goal is motivated by several identified shortcomings in existing humanoid control paradigms:

1. **Addressing the Generalist-Specialist Trade-off:** Current humanoid controllers typically exhibit a trade-off. Generalist policies can track diverse motions but fail to reliably execute rare, highly dynamic ones. Specialist policies, while proficient in specific dynamic skills, often lead to the degradation of previously learned generalist behaviors and are reliant on high-quality, preprocessed motion data. The research aims to overcome this dilemma by enabling simultaneous high performance across both generalist and specialist tasks.

2. **Enabling Robust Highly Dynamic Motion Tracking:** Highly dynamic motions (e.g., aerial cartwheels, backflips) involve critical short temporal segments (e.g., aerial posture adjustment, landing recovery) where control requirements are concentrated. Failures in these segments yield few informative samples, which are further diluted in large-scale generalist training. The objective is to develop mechanisms that can reliably learn and execute these challenging motions, extracting effective learning signals despite their scarcity and high failure rates.

3. **Robustness to Online Reference Inputs:** Specialist skills are often learned from meticulously preprocessed offline data. However, for real-time human-robot interaction and teleoperation, input from inertial motion capture systems is common but suffers from timing errors, root motion drift, and pose inconsistencies. These errors amplify during dynamic phases. A key objective is to ensure the controller's robustness to such low-quality, online reference inputs, allowing for real-time human-driven control of highly dynamic behaviors.

4. **Progressive Skill Acquisition:** Inspired by human learning, the work seeks to enable robots to expand their motor boundaries progressively. This involves building upon a stable foundation of generalist motor competence to acquire more challenging dynamic skills, rather than learning isolated skills from scratch or via fine-tuning that might disrupt existing capabilities.

To achieve these objectives, the authors specifically propose:
- A two-stage progressive learning framework (Extreme-RGMT) that first establishes a generalist base policy and then expands it.
- A role-specific acquisition and consolidation mechanism (PACE) to support highly dynamic skill acquisition while preventing drift from established generalist behaviors.
- A Segment-Aware Trajectory Advantage Resampling (STAR) method to improve the utilization of scarce effective experience, particularly in failure-prone highly dynamic motion learning.
- Comprehensive evaluation in both simulation and hardware, using fixed references and online inertial motion-capture inputs, to demonstrate state-of-the-art generalist and highly dynamic motion-tracking performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Extreme-RGMT is presented as a two-stage continual learning framework designed for robust generalist humanoid control that can acquire highly dynamic skills.

**Stage I: Generalist Motion Tracking Training**
The initial stage focuses on learning a generalist base policy, $\pi_{base}$, from a diverse multi-source motion distribution.

- **Policy Architecture:** Building upon previous work, the policy uses a dynamics-guided command encoder and an asymmetric actor-critic structure. Key enhancements include:
 - Separate encoding branches for proprioceptive observations ($o_{prop}$) and action histories ($a_{t-1}$).
 - Individual feature normalization (LayerNorm) applied to each branch (proprioceptive, action, and reference-command) to stabilize feature scales, accommodating diverse motion distributions and potential shifts during dynamic skill learning.
 - A causal history encoder processes interleaved history sequences.
 - A dynamics-guided cross-attention module aggregates local reference motion windows ($g_{t-L:t+L}$), conditioning it on the robot's recent history.
 - A Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ) bottleneck is applied after history-conditioned command aggregation to regularize the command representation, making it discrete and bounded, thereby reducing sensitivity to local inconsistencies in dynamic reference trajectories.
 - The actor outputs residual joint-position commands, which are then fed to a low-level PD controller.

- **Training Setup:**
 - **Reward Design:** A composite reward function is used, combining motion-imitation terms (e.g., tracking global anchor orientation, relative body position/orientation, base linear/angular velocity) with physical regularizers (e.g., penalizing action rates, joint position limits, undesired contacts, feet slip) (Table I).
 - **Domain Randomization:** To enhance robustness and sim-to-real transfer, various perturbations are applied during training, including dynamics randomization (e.g., ground friction, added base mass, CoM offset), proprioceptive observation noise, and reference-command perturbations (Table II).
 - **Adaptive Motion Sampling:** An adaptive sampling mechanism is employed to prioritize motion sequence temporal bins that exhibit higher tracking errors or failure frequencies, ensuring more focused learning on difficult segments.

- **Motion Stratification:** After Stage I training, the full motion dataset is stratified based on the $\pi_{base}$'s tracking performance. Motion clips longer than 10 seconds are split into 10-second segments, and each is evaluated with five randomized rollouts.
 - **Mastered Motion Set ($D_m$):** Clips with a completion rate of at least 80% are assigned here, representing broadly covered and stably tracked motions.
 - **Challenging Motion Set ($D_c$):** Remaining clips (completion rate < 80%) are assigned here, representing highly dynamic and difficult-to-track motions. This stratification forms the basis for asymmetric training in Stage II.

**Stage II: Progressive Highly Dynamic Skill Expansion**
This stage augments the base policy ($\pi_{base}$) into an augmented policy ($\pi_{aug}$) to acquire highly dynamic skills while retaining generalist capabilities.

- **PACE: Progressive Acquisition and Consolidation for Expansion:** This mechanism mitigates the stability-plasticity trade-off by coupling specialist acquisition with consolidation.
 - **Environment Allocation:** Parallel simulation environments are asymmetrically allocated: a fraction $\xi=0.8$ for skill acquisition on $D_c$ (using adaptive sampling), and $1-\xi$ for capability consolidation on $D_m$ (using uniform sampling).
 - **Asymmetric Objective Formulation:** The augmented policy is optimized with a combined loss:
 - $L_{D_c}^{Acquisition}$: Clipped PPO policy loss applied to samples from acquisition environments (challenging motions).
 - $L_{D_m}^{Consolidation}$: An L2 regularization term applied to samples from consolidation environments (mastered motions), minimizing the discrepancy between the current policy's actions and those of the fixed base policy ($\pi_{base}$) to prevent policy drift.
 - **Progress-Adaptive Consolidation Weighting:** The consolidation weight, $\lambda_t^{con}$, is dynamically adjusted based on the smoothed ratio of valid acquisition samples ($\bar{\rho}^{(t)}$). This increases the consolidation strength as the policy improves on challenging motions, balancing new skill acquisition with generalist ability retention. Initially, $\lambda_t^{con}$ is low to allow plasticity, and it increases as more stable learning signals from challenging motions become available.

- **STAR: Segment-Aware Trajectory Advantage Resampling:** This mechanism addresses the scarcity of effective experience in highly dynamic motion training, especially for failure-prone segments.
 - **Difficulty-Conditioned Advantage Normalization:** Bin-level difficulty information from adaptive sampling is transformed into a transition-level difficulty weight ($w_t$). Raw advantages are normalized independently for high-difficulty and easy transition groups, preventing the advantage scale in critical regions from being obscured by easier segments.
 - **Trajectory-Fragment Advantage Scoring:** Contiguous trajectory fragments associated with high-difficulty bins are evaluated based on their average raw advantage. The top 5% of these fragments (ranked per bin) are selected, ensuring coverage across different difficult regions.
 - **Difficulty-Weighted Mixed Mini-Batches:** PPO mini-batches for acquisition are constructed by sampling a quarter of transitions from the selected high-advantage fragments (with weights based on average fragment difficulty) and the remaining from the general rollout buffer. This prioritizes informative experiences within critical, difficult temporal regions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Extreme-RGMT was conducted through comprehensive simulation experiments and real-world hardware deployments, focusing on generalist and specialist motion tracking, mechanism analysis, and practical robustness.

**Generalist Capability Comparison:**
- **Performance:** Extreme-RGMT (Stage I) demonstrated improved generalist motion-tracking performance compared to state-of-the-art methods like SONIC and RGMT on both in-source (LAFAN1, AMASS) and unseen motion datasets (video-reconstructed). This improvement was attributed to architectural enhancements (separate encoders, LayerNorm, FSQ).
- **Retention:** Extreme-RGMT (Full), after Stage II specialist training, further improved success rates on both in-source and unseen motions, indicating enhanced robustness to state variations and contact transitions, while incurring only a slight increase in joint-level tracking errors. This suggests effective retention of generalist capabilities during highly dynamic skill expansion.

**Specialist Capability Comparison:**
- **Highly Dynamic Tracking:** Extreme-RGMT (Full) achieved high success rates on challenging highly dynamic motions. On the high-quality XtremeMotion dataset, it reliably completed all motions, comparable to specialist methods like OmniXtreme.
- **Robustness to Reference Quality:** Crucially, Extreme-RGMT (Full) significantly outperformed OmniXtreme on directly retargeted AMASS Challenging Motions, which are of lower quality. This indicated superior robustness to variations in reference quality, a critical factor for online teleoperation. Fine-Tuning a generalist policy directly on challenging motions improved specialist performance but degraded generalist capabilities.

**Generalist-Specialist Trade-off (Figure 5):**
- The results visually demonstrated that most existing methods excel either in generalist or specialist tasks, but not both. Extreme-RGMT achieved high success rates in both generalist (In-source Motion) and specialist (AMASS Challenging Motions) evaluations, yielding the highest unified performance average. This indicated successful navigation of the generalist-specialist trade-off.

**Acquisition and Retention Training Dynamics (Figure 7):**
- **Stage I:** The base policy gradually improved generalist performance but showed limited specialist skill acquisition.
- **Fine-Tuning:** Direct fine-tuning on highly dynamic data improved specialist performance but led to a marked degradation in generalist capabilities, confirming policy drift.
- **Extreme-RGMT (Full):** This method maintained stable generalist performance throughout training while continuously improving specialist capabilities, validating the effectiveness of PACE's asymmetric acquisition and consolidation.
- **Role of STAR:** Comparison with Extreme-RGMT (w/o STAR) showed that without STAR, specialist performance plateaued in later training stages, indicating STAR's importance for sustained improvement by leveraging critical learning signals from difficult motion segments.

**PACE Ablation (Figure 9):**
- **Mixed Training:** Training on a mixed distribution of mastered and challenging motions provided limited specialist improvement, suggesting dilution of dynamic learning signals.
- **Consolidation Constraint ($L_{con}$):** Removing $L_{con}$ resulted in degraded performance across both generalist and specialist tasks, highlighting the necessity of the consolidation constraint for preserving established capabilities.
- **Adaptive Weighting:** The progress-adaptive consolidation weight in PACE led to better overall performance than a fixed weight, demonstrating its effectiveness in balancing acquisition and retention.

**STAR Analysis (Table VII, Figure 8):**
- STAR significantly improved specialist success rates, especially on lower-quality Xsens inertial motion capture data (40.8% gain), where informative experiences are sparse and errors are prevalent.
- It reduced tracking error peaks in critical temporal regions (e.g., aerial adjustments, landing recovery), improving the policy's ability to handle highly dynamic segments prone to failure.

**Real-World Evaluation and Deployment (Table VIII, Figures 1 & 10):**
- The complete Extreme-RGMT policy was deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot. It demonstrated robust execution under fixed highly dynamic references (e.g., aerial cartwheel, backflip) and, notably, under online Xsens inertial motion-capture inputs (e.g., standing tucked flip, air twist kick).
- Hardware ablations confirmed the importance of architectural choices (separate encoders for proprioceptive/action history, FSQ) and STAR for real-world robustness, particularly for teleoperation with noisy inputs.
- The robot successfully performed rapid whole-body coordination, aerial posture adjustment, and contact transitions in real-time, even for diverse highly dynamic motions not explicitly present in the training corpus.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in humanoid robot control, addressing a fundamental challenge in achieving versatile and robust motor skills. The significance and potential impact of Extreme-RGMT can be summarized as follows:

1. **Breaking the Generalist-Specialist Barrier:** The primary contribution is demonstrating a method that allows a generalist humanoid controller to acquire highly dynamic, expert-level skills without degrading its existing broad motor competence. This capability is crucial for developing truly versatile robots that can perform a wide range of tasks, from everyday interactions to complex athletic maneuvers.

2. **Enabling Robust Online Teleoperation of Dynamic Skills:** Extreme-RGMT is identified as the first generalist humanoid controller to reliably enable online teleoperation of highly dynamic motions using inertial motion-capture inputs. This extends highly dynamic humanoid control beyond reliance on meticulously preprocessed offline data to real-time, human-driven control. This capability has substantial implications for human-robot collaboration, immersive telepresence, and intuitive robot programming, where robots can immediately respond to human gestures and dynamic movements.

3. **Improved Sample Efficiency for Dynamic Learning:** The introduction of Segment-Aware Trajectory Advantage Resampling (STAR) addresses the challenge of sparse and noisy learning signals inherent in highly dynamic, failure-prone motions. By prioritizing effective experience, STAR improves sample utilization, making the learning process more efficient and robust, particularly with lower-quality real-world data sources like inertial motion capture.

4. **Foundation for Progressive Motor Skill Development:** The two-stage, continual learning framework (Extreme-RGMT and PACE) offers a blueprint for how robots can progressively expand their motor repertoire, mirroring aspects of human motor learning. This paradigm could inform future research into lifelong learning for robots, enabling them to adapt and grow their capabilities over extended periods in dynamic environments.

5. **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer and Robustness:** The policy architecture enhancements, extensive domain randomization, and specific mechanisms like FSQ and LayerNorm contribute to the controller's robustness. The successful real-world deployment on the Unitree G1, under various conditions and with imperfect inputs, validates the method's effectiveness in transferring learned policies from simulation to physical hardware.

6. **Advancing Humanoid Autonomy and Interaction:** By enabling humanoids to perform complex, agile movements reliably and interactively, this work brings humanoid robots closer to being deployable in a wider range of real-world scenarios, including assistance in challenging environments, entertainment, and advanced manufacturing tasks that require high dexterity and adaptability.

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as global position drift over long durations and limited generalization to vastly unseen coordination patterns, the demonstrated ability to continually learn and robustly execute highly dynamic skills, even from noisy online inputs, marks a significant step towards more capable and autonomous humanoid robots.
