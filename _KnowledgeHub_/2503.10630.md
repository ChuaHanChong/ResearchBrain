# UniGoal: Towards Universal Zero-shot Goal-oriented Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Tsinghua University researchers introduce UniGoal, a universal zero-shot framework designed for goal-oriented navigation that handles object, instance-image, and text-based goals within a single model. The approach unifies goal and scene representations using a consistent graph format, achieving state-of-the-art zero-shot performance across multiple benchmarks and outperforming several supervised universal navigation methods.

## Problem

- Current goal-oriented navigation systems are often task-specific (e.g., object-goal, image-goal, text-goal) and lack a universal framework.
- Supervised navigation methods require extensive, costly training data and suffer from poor generalization to new environments or real-world scenarios.
- Existing zero-shot navigation methods are typically specialized for individual sub-tasks, and some lose crucial structural and spatial information when converting visual input to text.

## Method

- **UniGoal** proposes a universal zero-shot framework that represents both the dynamic 3D scene and diverse goal types (object, instance-image, text) as uniform graphs.
- It employs a multi-stage scene exploration policy guided by a matching score derived from graph matching between the scene graph and the goal graph.
- A robust blacklist mechanism is implemented to prevent repetitive failed explorations by excluding previously matched elements that did not lead to success.

## Results

- UniGoal achieved state-of-the-art zero-shot performance across Object-goal, Instance-image-goal, and Text-goal Navigation tasks on Matterport3D, Habitat-Matterport 3D, and RoboTHOR benchmarks.
- It notably outperformed task-specific zero-shot methods, such as Mod-IIN (**+4.1%** SR on HM3D for IIN), and surpassed supervised universal methods like PSL and GOAT (e.g., **+3.2%** SR over PSL for TN on HM3D).
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical contributions of the uniform graph representation, the multi-stage exploration policy, and the robust blacklist mechanism to UniGoal's overall performance and robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unifying diverse goal types (object, image, text) and the dynamic scene into a consistent graph representation enables explicit, effective LLM-based reasoning for universal navigation.
> - A multi-stage, progressive exploration policy, guided by graph matching scores, significantly improves navigation efficiency and accuracy in unknown environments.
> - Zero-shot approaches leveraging powerful foundation models and structured graph reasoning can achieve competitive and even superior performance compared to training-based methods across various navigation tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Goal-oriented navigation is a fundamental challenge in embodied AI and robotics, requiring an autonomous agent to reach a specified target in an unknown environment. Traditionally, this field has been segmented into various sub-tasks based on the goal type, such as:
- **Object-goal Navigation (ON):** Navigating to a specified object category (e.g., "find the chair").
- **Instance-image-goal Navigation (IIN):** Navigating to a specific object instance depicted in an image (e.g., "find *this exact* red chair").
- **Text-goal Navigation (TN):** Navigating based on a free-form text description of the goal, potentially involving multiple objects and relationships (e.g., "go to the living room where there's a sofa and a coffee table").

Prior research has made significant strides in each of these sub-tasks, often employing deep learning, reinforcement learning (RL), and increasingly, large vision-language foundation models. However, these methods typically fall into two categories:
1. **Training-based (Supervised) Methods:** These approaches train policy networks on large-scale simulated data (e.g., GOAT, PSL). While achieving good performance within their training domains, they often suffer from poor generalization to new, unseen environments or real-world scenarios due to overfitting and the high cost of data collection and training. Furthermore, universal training-based methods, like GOAT and PSL, still require extensive RL training, limiting their practical deployment.
2. **Zero-shot Methods:** These methods aim to enable agents to navigate without specific training or fine-tuning for a given task or environment. The emergence of powerful LLMs (e.g., GPT-4, LLaMA-2) and VLMs has spurred a new wave of zero-shot navigation research. LLMs provide strong reasoning capabilities and vast common-sense knowledge. However, existing zero-shot approaches, such as SG-Nav for ON or Mod-IIN for IIN, are predominantly task-specific. They build inference frameworks tailored to particular goal types, lacking the universality required for real-world robotic applications where instructions can be diverse and multimodal. InstructNav attempts a general framework for language-related navigation but still cannot handle visual goals like IIN.

UniGoal addresses this critical gap by proposing a **universal zero-shot framework** that can handle object, instance-image, and text-based goals within a *single model*, all without requiring training or fine-tuning. This work distinguishes itself by unifying the representation of diverse goals and the dynamic scene into a consistent graph format, enabling explicit graph-based reasoning by LLMs. This approach represents a significant advancement towards more versatile and deployable embodied AI systems, moving beyond task-specific silos and training-intensive paradigms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of UniGoal is to develop a **general framework for universal zero-shot goal-oriented navigation**. This encompasses two core characteristics:

1. **Universality:** The framework should be able to handle diverse goal types – specifically object category, instance image, and text description – using a *single, unified model* without modification. This aims to meet the high flexibility demands of human instructions in real-world scenarios, where a robot might receive commands in various modalities.
2. **Zero-shot Capability:** The navigation method must not require any training or fine-tuning when deployed to a new task or environment. This is crucial for enabling strong generalization ability and reducing the prohibitive costs and time associated with large-scale data collection and reinforcement learning training, which often lead to overfitting in simulated environments and poor real-world transfer.

The motivation stems from the limitations of current state-of-the-art approaches:
- **Lack of Generalization in Supervised Methods:** Existing universal navigation methods (e.g., GOAT, PSL) rely on extensive RL training, which is resource-intensive and leads to overfitting, hindering real-world applicability.
- **Task-Specificity in Zero-shot Methods:** While LLMs have enabled zero-shot navigation, current solutions are specialized for individual sub-tasks (e.g., SG-Nav for ON, Mod-IIN for IIN). They differ significantly in their overall pipeline, making it impossible to generalize across different types of goals within a single framework. A robot designed for object navigation cannot readily switch to finding a specific image or following a complex text description.
- **Information Loss in Text-only Representations:** Prior LLM-based zero-shot methods often convert visual and spatial information into pure text, leading to a loss of crucial structural and spatial relationships.

UniGoal aims to overcome these challenges by proposing a novel approach that unifies goal and scene representations through a graph structure, preserving rich structural information and enabling more effective LLM-based explicit reasoning across all goal types.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

UniGoal's methodology is centered on a **uniform graph representation** for both the 3D scene and the diverse goal types, coupled with a **multi-stage, LLM-guided scene exploration policy**.

#### 3.1. Graph Construction and Matching

The core idea is to represent both the dynamic 3D environment and the navigation goal as graphs, allowing for consistent processing and explicit reasoning:
- **Scene Graph (G_t):** An online 3D scene graph is incrementally constructed as the agent explores the environment, capturing observed objects (nodes) and their spatial or semantic relationships (edges). Object detection, segmentation, and potentially pose estimation are used to populate the nodes and their world coordinates.
- **Goal Graph (G_g):** Different goal types are converted into a uniform graph format:
 - **Object-goal:** A simple graph with a single node representing the object category.
 - **Instance-image-goal:** Utilizes tools like Grounded-SAM to identify objects in the image, then a VLM (e.g., LLaVA) to infer relationships between them, forming nodes and edges.
 - **Text-goal:** An LLM is prompted to extract objects and their relationships directly from the text description, forming the graph structure.
- **Graph Matching:** At each time step, UniGoal performs graph matching between the current scene graph (G_t) and the goal graph (G_g). This determines how well the goal is observed. Three metrics are computed:
 - **Node Matching:** Bipartite matching based on CLIP embeddings of node descriptions.
 - **Edge Matching:** Bipartite matching based on CLIP embeddings of edge descriptions (relationships).
 - **Topology Matching:** Measures the graph editing similarity between the minimal subgraph of G_t corresponding to matched nodes/edges and G_g, indicating structural resemblance.
 - These three scores are averaged to yield a final **matching score (S)**, which guides the subsequent exploration policy.

#### 3.2. Multi-stage Scene Exploration Policy

Based on the matching score (S), UniGoal employs a progressive, multi-stage exploration policy, generating long-term goals for a deterministic local policy:
- **Stage 1: Zero Matching (S < σ1):** When little to no part of the goal graph is observed, the agent's objective is broad exploration to find *any* element of the goal.
 - **LLM-guided Goal Decomposition:** The complex goal graph (G_g) is decomposed into smaller, internally correlated subgraphs by prompting an LLM. This makes the search more manageable.
 - **Frontier Selection:** Similar to object-goal navigation (e.g., SG-Nav), the LLM predicts the most likely position for each subgraph, and the agent selects the optimal frontier to explore, balancing predicted location and distance to the agent.
- **Stage 2: Partial Matching (σ1 ≤ S < σ2, with anchor pairs):** If parts of G_g are observed (at least two matched nodes or one matched edge), the agent transitions to a more targeted search.
 - **Coordinate Projection:** An LLM is used to infer approximate 2D bird's-eye view (BEV) coordinates for nodes in G_g based on their relative spatial relationships described by edges, starting from a central object node (0,0).
 - **Anchor Pair Alignment:** Using "anchor pairs" (exactly matched node pairs or edges between G_t and G_g) and their known world coordinates in G_t, a 2D coordinate transformation matrix (scale, rotation, translation) is calculated to align the projected G_g onto G_t. This infers the likely world coordinates of the *unobserved* parts of G_g.
 - The exploration goal is set as the center of the smallest circumcircle enclosing the projected unobserved nodes, guiding the agent towards the likely location of the remaining goal elements.
- **Stage 3: Perfect Matching (S ≥ σ2 and central object matched):** When the matching score is high and the central object of the goal is observed, the agent approaches the goal for final verification.
 - **Scene Graph Correction:** As the agent gets closer, it continuously refines a local subgraph of G_t (around the potential goal) using new RGB-D observations. An LLM aggregates information from visual input and graph relationships to correct unreasonable nodes and edges, addressing perceptual errors.
 - **Goal Verification:** Multiple confidence items (corrected nodes/edges, matched keypoints for IIN, graph matching score, path length since entering stage 3) are used to verify if the goal has truly been reached. If verified, the agent stops; otherwise, the matched objects are blacklisted.

#### 3.3. Robust Blacklist Mechanism

To prevent the agent from getting stuck in repetitive failed explorations, UniGoal implements a blacklist. If an exploration stage (Stage 2 or 3) fails to find or verify the goal, the associated matched nodes and edges (or anchor pairs) are added to a blacklist. These blacklisted elements are then excluded from subsequent graph matching and reasoning processes, forcing the agent to explore new regions or attempt new matching hypotheses. If a blacklisted node/edge is later refined during scene graph correction, it is removed from the blacklist.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

UniGoal's performance was rigorously evaluated across three widely used benchmarks: Matterport3D (MP3D), Habitat-Matterport 3D (HM3D), and RoboTHOR, covering Object-goal, Instance-image-goal, and Text-goal Navigation tasks. The key metrics reported are Success Rate (SR) and Success Rate weighted by Path Length (SPL).

#### 4.1. Comparison with State-of-the-Art

The most significant finding is UniGoal's ability to achieve **state-of-the-art zero-shot performance across all three studied navigation tasks with a single, unified model**.
- **Object-goal Navigation (ON):** UniGoal outperforms existing zero-shot methods like SG-Nav on all benchmarks (e.g., +0.5% SR on HM3D compared to SG-Nav). It also surpasses several supervised ON methods, demonstrating strong generalization without training.
- **Instance-image-goal Navigation (IIN):** UniGoal shows a more substantial lead, outperforming Mod-IIN (a task-specific zero-shot method) by a notable +4.1% SR on HM3D. This highlights UniGoal's effectiveness in leveraging detailed visual information embedded in the graph.
- **Text-goal Navigation (TN):** As there are currently no other zero-shot TN methods, UniGoal is compared against supervised universal methods (PSL and GOAT). UniGoal significantly outperforms both, achieving +3.2% SR over PSL and +2.6% SR over GOAT on HM3D, despite being zero-shot and the competitors being training-based.

Crucially, UniGoal consistently outperforms even **supervised universal methods** like PSL and GOAT across all three tasks (e.g., +3.9% SR on ON, +22.8% SR on IIN, +3.2% SR on TN on HM3D over GOAT's respective best). This is a strong testament to the power of the zero-shot, graph-based reasoning framework.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

Extensive ablation studies on the HM3D (IIN) benchmark validate the contribution of each proposed component:
- **Pipeline Design:**
 - Simplifying graph matching (removing full scoring) led to significant performance degradation, confirming the importance of robust matching for optimal stage transitions.
 - Removing the blacklist mechanism caused a "significant performance degradation," indicating its crucial role in preventing repetitive exploration and stuck states.
 - Simplifying the multi-stage exploration policy (e.g., removing Stage 2) also resulted in a "significant performance drop," confirming the necessity of a granular, adaptive exploration strategy.
- **Effect of Each Exploration Stage's Submodules:**
 - In Stage 1 (Zero Matching), the LLM-guided graph decomposition and frontier selection were shown to be effective. Replacing Stage 1 with a simple Frontier-Based Exploration (FBE) or removing graph decomposition/frontier selection decreased performance.
 - In Stage 2 (Partial Matching), both the LLM-based coordinate projection and the anchor pair alignment methods were validated. Simplification of projection or removal of alignment led to reduced performance.
 - In Stage 3 (Perfect Matching), both scene graph correction and goal verification mechanisms were crucial, as their removal led to performance drops, highlighting their role in refining observations and ensuring task success.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Results

The paper provides qualitative demonstrations showing UniGoal's decision-making process, illustrating how the matching score gradually increases with graph-based exploration. Visualizations of navigation paths on various scenes confirm that UniGoal generates efficient trajectories to reach targets across different goal types and diverse environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

UniGoal represents a significant leap forward in embodied AI, particularly in the domain of goal-oriented navigation, with several profound implications:

1. **True Generalization and Versatility:** By providing a single, unified framework for handling diverse goal types (object, image, text) in a zero-shot manner, UniGoal breaks down the silos of task-specific navigation systems. This is critical for practical robotic applications where real-world instructions are inherently multimodal and unpredictable. A universal agent can adapt to various human commands without needing re-training or switching models.
2. **Reduced Training Burden and Cost:** The zero-shot capability eliminates the need for large-scale, costly, and time-consuming data collection and reinforcement learning training. This makes the deployment of embodied AI agents more economically viable and accelerates research and development cycles. It also addresses the sim-to-real gap, as the method does not overfit to simulated environments.
3. **Leveraging Foundation Models Effectively:** UniGoal demonstrates an advanced way to integrate LLMs and VLMs. Instead of merely using them for text generation or classification, it leverages their reasoning capabilities for *explicit graph-based reasoning* and information aggregation. This preserves crucial structural and spatial information, leading to more robust and accurate decision-making than pure text-based prompting. The multi-stage exploration strategy and the robust blacklist mechanism are clever ways to operationalize LLM reasoning in a dynamic environment.
4. **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** The incremental scene graph construction, multi-stage exploration, scene graph correction, and blacklist mechanism contribute to the system's robustness in unknown and dynamic environments, allowing it to recover from perceptual errors or failed exploration attempts.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** UniGoal's graph-based universal zero-shot approach provides a strong foundation for future research in several areas:
 - **Complex Human-Robot Interaction:** Enabling robots to understand more nuanced and multi-modal instructions.
 - **Active Perception and Exploration:** The framework can be extended to guide more intelligent active sensing strategies.
 - **Real-world Deployment:** The paper's conclusion mentions deployment on a real-world robotic platform, underscoring its practical value and potential for direct application in areas like home robotics, industrial automation, and exploration.

In essence, UniGoal moves the field closer to truly intelligent and versatile embodied agents that can navigate complex, unseen environments based on diverse human intents, without the need for extensive pre-training on every conceivable scenario.
