# Learning what you can do before doing anything

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Pennsylvania, University of Southern California, Ryerson University, and Samsung AI Centre Toronto developed CLASP, a method for learning an agent's action space purely from unlabeled visual observations. This approach achieves disentangled, minimal, and composable action representations, enabling accurate action-conditioned video prediction and effective planning in the latent space with minimal labeled data.

## Problem

- Learning robust and generalizable action representations from visual observations is challenging without explicit action labels or active environment interaction.
- Traditional reinforcement learning and imitation learning methods often require extensive, costly data or direct motor commands, limiting their application to passive observation scenarios.
- Existing models struggle to disentangle specific action information from the static content of a scene, hindering the transferability of learned representations.

## Method

- Proposed the Composable Learned Action Space Predictor (**CLASP**), an extension of recurrent latent variable models for stochastic video prediction.
- Introduced a novel composability objective (L_comp) to encourage latent variables (z) to represent state-independent actions, capturing cumulative effects over multiple timesteps.
- Incorporated the Information Bottleneck principle to enforce minimality in the latent representation, focusing on dynamic changes caused by actions rather than static visual details.

## Results

- The learned latent action space (z) demonstrated clear disentanglement from static content, with the first PCA dimension capturing **99%** of variance for a 1-DOF robot, strongly correlating with ground truth actions.
- Achieved superior action-conditioned video prediction, enabling 'action transplantation' across varied initial states and reducing relative error on the BAIR dataset by **30%** compared to baseline models.
- Enabled effective planning for visual servoing in the latent space, reaching a final distance to goal of 1.6 ± 1.0 degrees on the reacher dataset, comparable to fully supervised methods with significantly fewer labeled examples.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective action representations can be learned solely from passive visual observation by enforcing minimality (through Information Bottleneck) and composability in the latent space.
> - The composability objective is crucial for ensuring that learned action representations are disentangled from instantaneous visual states, promoting generalizability across different scenes.
> - An unsupervised pre-training of visual dynamics and action structure allows for highly data-efficient semi-supervised mapping between latent actions and real control commands.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of unsupervised representation learning, video prediction, and reinforcement learning (RL) for intelligent agents. A long-standing challenge in developing intelligent systems is enabling them to acquire robust and generalizable representations of actions, particularly when direct motor commands or explicit action labels are unavailable. Traditional reinforcement learning methods often require active interaction with an environment, implicitly discovering action spaces through trial and error (Mnih et al., 2015; Lillicrap et al., 2016; Levine et al., 2016). While effective, these methods can be data-intensive and the learned sensorimotor mappings may not easily generalize to new agents or control interfaces.

The paper addresses a critical gap: learning action spaces *purely from passive visual observation*, without requiring explicit action labels or active interaction. This approach draws inspiration from human cognitive development, where infants gain extensive sensory exposure to actions (e.g., observing adults walk) before attempting to reproduce them (Adolph et al., 2012; Ullman et al., 2012). Such unsupervised learning from observation could significantly improve data efficiency for subsequent learning tasks like imitation learning (Finn et al., 2017; Pathak et al., 2018) or enable the reuse of action representations across systems with different effectors.

Within the unsupervised learning domain, prior work has explored structured and minimal representations using variational autoencoders (VAEs) and the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle (Alemi et al., 2017; Higgins et al., 2017; Burgess et al., 2018). Other research has focused on disentangling static content and pose from video (Denton & Birodkar, 2017; Tulyakov et al., 2018) or learning motion representations based on group-theoretic actions (Jaegle et al., 2018). This work extends these concepts by specifically aiming to disentangle *action information* from the instantaneous state of the environment, a distinction from learning general motion or disentangling content/pose.

Furthermore, the paper builds upon recent advancements in unsupervised learning with video data, particularly stochastic video prediction models that capture multimodal distributions of future images (Goroshin et al., 2015; Henaff et al., 2017; Babaeizadeh et al., 2018; Denton & Fergus, 2018; Lee et al., 2018). While many of these models focus on general predictive capabilities, this research aims to shape the latent representations within such models to explicitly capture the structure of an agent's actions. This contrasts with action-conditioned predictive models that *require* action inputs for prediction (Oh et al., 2015; Chiappa et al., 2017) or traditional sensorimotor abstractions in robotics that often rely on hand-crafted hierarchies or extensive interaction (Sutton et al., 1999; Schaal et al., 2005; Devin et al., 2017). The contribution here lies in discovering these action abstractions from passive data, rather than pre-defining them or learning them through active interaction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a computational model capable of learning an agent's action space exclusively from unlabeled visual observations. This learning paradigm, termed "learning what you can do before doing anything," is motivated by several considerations:

1. **Biological Plausibility and Efficiency:** Human infants acquire extensive knowledge about actions by observing others before they can reliably perform those actions themselves. This suggests that passive observation plays a crucial role in forming action representations, enabling more efficient motor skill acquisition. For artificial agents, an analogous mechanism could reduce the need for costly and time-consuming active interaction or large datasets of action-labeled sequences.

2. **Addressing Data Scarcity in Robotics and Imitation Learning:** Collecting ground truth action labels for robotic systems or for imitation learning tasks can be difficult, expensive, or impossible. For instance, learning from internet videos or diverse human demonstrations often lacks corresponding motor commands. A method that learns action representations from purely visual data would facilitate these applications by leveraging readily available unlabeled video.

3. **Generalizability and Reusability of Action Representations:** Representations learned solely from visual observations are inherently independent of specific motor commands or task goals during the initial unsupervised training phase. This invariance could allow action representations to be more easily transferred and reused across different agents (e.g., robots with varying effectors) or environments.

To achieve these objectives, the authors impose two specific desiderata on the learned latent representation, denoted as `z`:

- **Minimality:** The latent variable `z` should contain minimal information about the scene's static content, focusing instead on capturing the dynamic properties—specifically, the changes induced by actions. This forces the representation to abstract away irrelevant visual details and concentrate on the underlying causes of motion. This is enforced using the Information Bottleneck principle.
- **Composability:** The representation `z` should capture the compositional structure of actions. This means that the cumulative effect of a sequence of actions (`z_1` followed by `z_2`) should be derivable from the individual action representations without needing access to the intermediate visual states. Composability is hypothesized to encourage disentanglement, as a composed representation cannot rely on static visual context from intermediate frames, thus forcing individual `z`s to represent actions in a scene-independent manner.

Ultimately, the goal is for this disentangled, minimal, and composable action representation to enable direct planning in the latent space. With a minimal number of action-labeled sequences (a "semi-supervised" setting), a simple bijective mapping from the latent actions to actual control commands can then be learned, allowing the agent to execute planned behaviors effectively.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, named **Composable Learned Action Space Predictor (CLASP)**, extends a recurrent latent variable model for stochastic video prediction by introducing specific loss terms that promote minimality and composability in the learned latent representations.

#### 3.1. Video Prediction Model

At its core, CLASP employs a recurrent latent variable model, similar to the Stochastic Video Generation (SVG-FP) framework (Denton & Fergus, 2018), which is a temporal extension of the conditional VAE (Chung et al., 2015).
- **Generative Model:** At each timestep `t`, a latent variable `z_t` is sampled from a prior `p(z) = N(0, I)`. Given a history of frames `x_1:t-1` and latent samples `z_2:t`, the model predicts the next frame `x_t` using a generative network `p_θ(x_t|x_1:t-1, z_2:t)`. In practice, the mean of this distribution `μ_θ` is used to generate `ˆx_t`. The generative network consists of a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network and a convolutional decoder (CNN_d).
- **Inference Model:** An approximate posterior `q_φ(z_t|x_t, x_t-1) = N(μ_φ(x_t, x_t-1), σ_φ(x_t, x_t-1))` is used during training to infer `z_t` from consecutive frames. This inference network comprises a multilayer perceptron (MLP_infer) and a convolutional encoder (CNN_e).
- **Minimality (Information Bottleneck):** To encourage `z_t` to be a minimal representation of the change between `x_t-1` and `x_t`, the standard VAE objective is reformulated using the Information Bottleneck (IB) principle (Shwartz-Ziv & Tishby, 2017). The objective maximizes the mutual information between `(z_t, x_t-1)` and `x_t` (reconstruction) while minimizing the mutual information `I(z_t, x_t-1:t)` (information compression). A Lagrange multiplier `β_z` balances these two terms. The full variational lower bound (`L_pred`) for a sequence aggregates the reconstruction term and a KL divergence term `D_KL(q_φ(z_t|x_t-1:t)||p(z))`.

#### 3.2. Learning Action Representations with Composability (CLASP)

The core innovation is the introduction of a composability objective. This objective aims to make the latent variable `z_t` truly represent an action—a change independent of the current state—rather than just any visual change.
- **Trajectory Representation `ν`:** An additional random variable `ν_t` is introduced, representing a trajectory composed of `C` consecutive action representations `z_t-C:t`. This composition is performed by an inference model `q_ζ(ν_t|z_t, z_t-1)` parameterized by an `MLP_comp`.
- **Composability Loss (`L_comp`):** Similar to `z_t`, `ν_t` is encouraged to be a minimal representation of the trajectory. An IB objective is applied to `ν_t`, maximizing `I((ν_t, x_1), x_t)` while minimizing `I(z_2:t, ν_t)`. The `L_comp` term is structured to allow the generative model to predict a frame `x_t` based on a starting frame `x_t-C` and the composed trajectory representation `ν_t`. This forces `ν_t` to encapsulate the cumulative effect of actions without relying on intermediate visual states. The prior distribution for `ν` is a unit Gaussian `N(0, I)`, and `β_ν` is a balancing hyperparameter.
- **Overall Training Objective:** The total objective for CLASP is the sum of the video prediction loss and the composability loss: `L_total = L_comp + L_pred`.
- **Architectural Details:** Input images `x_t` are encoded by a `CNN_e`, passed to an LSTM for sequence processing, and decoded by a `CNN_d`. `MLP_infer` calculates `z_t` from `CNN_e(x_t)` and `CNN_e(x_t-1)`. `MLP_comp` composes `z` samples into `ν`. Binary indicators distinguish `z` and `ν` for the generative network.

#### 3.3. Grounding the Control Mapping

After CLASP is trained in an unsupervised manner, a small number of action-annotated video sequences are used to establish a bijective mapping between the learned latent action representations `z` and the true control actions `u`.
- **Mapping Networks:** Two lightweight multilayer perceptrons, `MLP_lat(u_t)` and `MLP_act(z_t)`, are trained to approximate this bijection. `MLP_lat` maps true actions to latent `z`s, and `MLP_act` maps latent `z`s back to true actions.
- **Data Efficiency:** Crucially, during this step, the gradients are *not* propagated back into the pre-trained video prediction model (CNN_e, LSTM, CNN_d). This allows the mapping to be learned with orders of magnitude fewer labeled examples compared to fully supervised end-to-end methods, as the complex visual dynamics have already been learned.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of CLASP was conducted on a simulated robot reacher dataset (one degree of freedom) and the real-world BAIR robot pushing dataset, demonstrating its capabilities in action-conditioned video prediction and planning.

#### 4.1. Learned Structure of Action Representations

- **Disentanglement Validation:** On the reacher dataset, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) visualization of the learned latent space `z` revealed a clear correspondence between the `z` values and the ground truth actions (robot rotation angles). The first PCA dimension captured 99% of the variance, consistent with the robot's single degree of freedom. This contrasts with a baseline model (Denton & Fergus, 2018) without the composability objective, where the latent space `z` did not exhibit a discernible relationship with the true actions and appeared entangled with the absolute position of the robot arm (Fig. 3, Fig. 15). This result supports the hypothesis that composability encourages disentanglement of action information from static scene content.

#### 4.2. Action-Conditioned Video Prediction

- **Action Transplantation:** CLASP successfully demonstrated the ability to transplant action representations `z` from a "donor" video sequence to a "recipient" sequence with a different initial state. The recipient sequence accurately reproduced the actions of the donor, showing synchronization of movement despite changes in scene content or agent appearance (Fig. 5, Fig. 8). This indicates that the learned `z` is robustly independent of the initial visual state.
- **Quantitative Performance:** In action-conditioned video prediction, where `z` is inferred from ground truth action `u` via `MLP_lat` and then used to predict future frames, CLASP significantly outperformed the Denton & Fergus (2018) baseline (Table 1). On the reacher dataset, CLASP achieved an absolute error of 2.9 ± 2.1 degrees, comparable to the fully supervised model (2.6 ± 1.8 degrees). On the more complex BAIR dataset, CLASP reduced the relative error from 3.6 ± 4.0 (baseline) to 3.0 ± 2.1 pixels, bridging 30% of the gap to supervised methods. Qualitative results (Fig. 12, 13, 14) further showed that CLASP produced clearer, more consistent predictions compared to the baseline, which often exhibited blurriness or ghosting artifacts.

#### 4.3. Planning in the Learned Action Space (Visual Servoing)

- **Effective Planning:** CLASP was evaluated on a visual servoing task where an agent needs to move from a start image to a goal image. The model performed planning in the latent `z` space using Model Predictive Control (MPC) with the Cross Entropy Method (CEM), then mapped the planned `z` trajectory to concrete actions `u` via `MLP_act`.
- **Quantitative Performance:** On the reacher dataset, CLASP achieved a final distance to goal of 1.6 ± 1.0 degrees, performing comparably to or slightly better than fully supervised planning baselines (Agrawal et al. (2016): 2.0 ± 1.5 deg; Oh et al. (2015): 1.8 ± 1.5 deg) and significantly outperforming the Denton & Fergus (2018) baseline (14.1 ± 10.7 deg) (Table 2).
- **Qualitative Results:** Visualizations of planned and executed trajectories showed that CLASP not only reached the target but also generated accurate intermediate plans (Fig. 6).

#### 4.4. Data Efficiency

- **Semi-supervised Advantage:** CLASP demonstrated substantial data efficiency in the semi-supervised setting. When only a small number of action-labeled videos (e.g., 100 or 1000) were available for training the `z`-to-`u` mapping, CLASP maintained high performance in visual servoing (Table 4, Fig. 6 right). Supervised baselines, which require labels for the entire training process, performed poorly in these low-data regimes. In scenarios with abundant labeled data (10,000 videos), CLASP's performance remained on par with the supervised approaches.

#### 4.5. Robustness to Varying Visual Characteristics

- **Generalization to Diverse Environments:** Experiments on modified reacher datasets—one with varying backgrounds (CIFAR-10 images) and another with varying robot arm appearances (different lengths and widths)—showed that CLASP's learned action representations are robust to visual variability.
- **Consistent Performance:** CLASP maintained comparable visual servoing performance in these varied environments (Table 2: 3.0 ± 2.2 deg for varied backgrounds, 2.8 ± 2.9 deg for varied agents), indicating that it successfully disentangled the agent's actions from irrelevant visual changes. The action transplantation results (Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10) further supported that the learned `z` represents action consistently, regardless of these visual alterations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a method for unsupervised learning of action representations from passive visual observations, offering a data-efficient alternative to conventional supervised or active learning paradigms. The introduction of minimality and composability as guiding principles for latent space learning enables the model to disentangle action semantics from static scene content, a significant challenge in perception-action systems.

The significance and potential impact of CLASP include:

- **Reduced Labeling Burden:** By learning complex visual dynamics and action structures from unlabeled videos, CLASP substantially reduces the need for extensive, often costly, action-labeled datasets. This is particularly impactful for robotics, where collecting paired visual and action data can be laborious, and for imitation learning scenarios where ground-truth actions are unavailable.
- **Enhanced Generalizability and Transferability:** The learned action representations are demonstrably independent of specific scene backgrounds, initial states, and even the visual appearance of the agent itself. This makes the representations highly generalizable, potentially enabling direct transfer of learned action knowledge across different environments, robot embodiments, or even from observing diverse agents (e.g., humans or other robots) via video.
- **Improved Efficiency for Downstream Tasks:** The structured and disentangled latent action space facilitates subsequent learning tasks. Action-conditioned video prediction becomes more accurate, and planning in this latent space is shown to be effective, approaching the performance of fully supervised methods with significantly less labeled data for the control mapping.
- **Bio-Inspired Learning Paradigm:** The "learning what you can do before doing anything" paradigm aligns with observations in human cognitive development, suggesting a promising avenue for designing more intelligent and adaptable artificial agents. This approach emphasizes the role of passive observation in building foundational knowledge about an agent's capabilities.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for exploring more complex action spaces, integrating learned action representations into hierarchical reinforcement learning architectures, and investigating how such passively learned knowledge can be actively refined through limited interaction. It opens up possibilities for systems that can autonomously understand the behavioral repertoire of agents simply by watching them.
