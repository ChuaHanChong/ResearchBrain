# Investigating the Impact of Hard Samples on Accuracy Reveals In-class Data Imbalance

> [!summary] Summary
> Pukowski and Lu introduce the concept of "in-class data imbalance," revealing that datasets contain disproportionate amounts of "easy" and "hard" samples within each class, challenging the sole reliance on test accuracy. The research demonstrates that models trained on easy samples perform poorly on hard samples, suggesting that hard samples are crucial for robust generalization.

## Problem

- Machine learning models are primarily evaluated using aggregate test accuracy, which can obscure poor performance on specific types of data samples.
- The conventional understanding of data imbalance focuses on class distribution, overlooking varying complexities within a single class.
- Current evaluation methods do not sufficiently account for the intrinsic complexity of individual data samples and their differential impact on model learning.

## Method

- The paper introduces the concept of =='in-class data imbalance'==, distinguishing between 'easy' and 'hard' samples based on their intrinsic complexity and contribution to model learning.
- It adapts the =='straggler' concept==, rooted in the ==manifold hypothesis==, to identify hard samples as those consistently misclassified at the 'inversion point' of class manifold radii.
- A systematic experimental design manipulated the proportion of easy and hard samples in training sets to evaluate their differential impact on model performance on separate easy and hard test sets.

## Results

- Models trained exclusively on easy samples achieved **95-97%** accuracy on easy test samples but only **20-25%** accuracy on hard test samples.
- Training solely on hard samples resulted in moderate accuracy on easy samples, indicating hard samples contain more generalizable information.
- The straggler-based method for identifying hard samples showed a greater performance gap between easy and hard samples, outperforming confidence-based and energy-based methods in distinguishing sample hardness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Easy and hard data samples contribute differently to model learning and generalization, with hard samples containing more generalizable information.
> - Hard samples are not merely anomalies but represent legitimate, complex features essential for robust generalization.
> - The geometric 'straggler-based' method for identifying hard samples is more effective than confidence-based or energy-based approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Introduction

Machine learning practitioners have long relied on test accuracy as the primary metric for model evaluation, especially in AutoML workflows. However, this singular focus may obscure crucial information about model performance across different types of data samples. In their research, Pukowski and Lu reveal that test accuracy alone can be misleading due to a previously underexplored phenomenon called "in-class data imbalance."

This paper introduces the concept that within each class, data samples can be divided into "easy" and "hard" categories based on their intrinsic complexity. The researchers demonstrate that these two groups contribute differently to model learning and generalization, with easy samples vastly outnumbering hard ones. This imbalance has significant implications for how we train and evaluate machine learning models, particularly in automated machine learning pipelines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Understanding Hard Samples and In-class Data Imbalance

The concept of in-class data imbalance extends beyond the traditional understanding of imbalanced data, which typically refers to unequal representation of different classes. Instead, it highlights that even within a single class, samples can vary dramatically in their complexity and contribution to model learning.

Easy samples are characterized by:
- Low sample complexity
- High representation in datasets
- Higher generalization accuracy

Hard samples (or "stragglers") possess:
- High sample complexity
- Low representation in datasets
- Lower generalization accuracy

The conceptual visualization in Figure 1 illustrates how easy and hard samples might be distributed within a dataset. Blue dots represent samples from one class, while gray dots represent another class. The star-shaped points indicate hard samples within each class that reside near class boundaries or in regions of higher geometric complexity.

The researchers found that the ratio of easy to hard samples can be quite dramatic. For instance, in the MNIST dataset, easy samples outnumber hard samples by approximately 13:1, creating a significant in-class imbalance that affects model training and evaluation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Theoretical Framework

The paper grounds its understanding of sample hardness in the manifold hypothesis, which suggests that high-dimensional data typically resides on lower-dimensional manifolds. Within this framework, the researchers define sample complexity based on the geometry of these manifolds.

The authors extend the concept of "stragglers" (originally from Ciceri et al., 2024) to multiclass classification. Stragglers are defined as samples that remain misclassified at the "inversion point" during training—the moment when class manifold radii transition from shrinking to expanding.

This framework allows the researchers to provide a more geometric understanding of what makes certain samples difficult for neural networks to learn, moving beyond simpler explanations based on data density or distance from class centroids.

#### 3.2. Methodology

The researchers employed a systematic approach to investigate the impact of hard samples:

1. **Datasets**: They used standard image datasets (MNIST, FashionMNIST, and KMNIST) to ensure replicability and relevance.

2. **Model Architecture**: A fully connected neural network (784-20-20-10) was trained using the Adam optimizer with cross-entropy loss.

3. **Identifying Hard Samples**: The researchers adapted the "stragglers" concept to identify hard samples, focusing on samples that remain misclassified at the inversion point of class manifold radii.

4. **Experimental Design**: They manipulated the proportion of hard and easy samples in training sets to study their differential impact on model performance. Multiple train-test splits were used to ensure robust results.

5. **Evaluation**: Performance was measured on separate test sets of hard and easy samples, using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score as metrics.

The researchers also compared their straggler-based method for identifying hard samples with other approaches, including confidence-based and energy-based methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Key Findings

The study revealed several important findings about the nature and impact of in-class data imbalance:

1. **Distinct Contribution of Hard and Easy Samples**: Adding easy samples to the training set significantly improves accuracy on easy test samples, while adding hard samples has a more modest impact on hard sample accuracy.

2. **Information Asymmetry**: Training exclusively on hard samples leads to moderate accuracy on easy samples, while training only on easy samples results in very poor accuracy on hard samples. This suggests hard samples contain more generalizable information than easy ones.

3. **Non-uniform Distribution**: Hard samples are not evenly distributed across classes. Some classes contain significantly more hard samples than others, which correlates with class-level error rates.

4. **Dataset Variation**: The distribution and effect of hard samples vary across datasets, suggesting that the nature of in-class imbalance is dataset-specific.

5. **Distinction from Anomalies**: Hard samples are not merely anomalies or outliers. They represent legitimate, if complex, manifestations of class features that are essential for robust generalization.

The researchers found that models trained only on easy samples achieved excellent performance on other easy samples (95-97% accuracy) but performed very poorly on hard samples (20-25% accuracy). This stark contrast highlights the information gap between these two groups of samples.

#### 4.2. Implications for Model Evaluation

The findings have significant implications for how we evaluate machine learning models:

1. **Test Accuracy Limitations**: Overall test accuracy can mask poor performance on hard samples, potentially leading to models that appear robust but fail on more complex inputs.

2. **Dataset Construction**: The distribution of hard and easy samples in training and test sets profoundly affects model performance and evaluation, suggesting that random splitting may not be optimal.

3. **Benchmarking Needs**: The research underscores the importance of evaluating models specifically on hard samples, not just on aggregate metrics across the entire test set.

4. **AutoML Considerations**: In AutoML contexts, where model selection is often driven by aggregate accuracy metrics, accounting for performance on hard samples could lead to more robust model selection.

#### 4.3. Benchmarking Hard Sample Identification Methods

The researchers proposed a benchmarking procedure to evaluate different methods for identifying hard samples. They compared three approaches:

1. **Straggler-based**: Identifying samples misclassified at the manifold radius inversion point
2. **Confidence-based**: Using model prediction confidence to identify hard samples
3. **Energy-based**: Using sample energy (related to the logit values) as an indicator of hardness

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Practical Applications

The insights from this research have several practical applications:

1. **Data Augmentation**: Focusing data augmentation efforts specifically on hard samples could improve model robustness more efficiently than random augmentation.

2. **Model Selection**: Incorporating performance on hard samples as a criterion for model selection could lead to more robust models in practice.

3. **Efficient Fine-tuning**: Using hard samples for fine-tuning could reduce the data requirements while maintaining or improving performance.

4. **Fairness and Robustness**: Understanding in-class imbalance could help identify underrepresented subgroups within classes, potentially addressing fairness concerns.

5. **Curriculum Learning**: The identification of hard and easy samples could inform more effective curriculum learning strategies that gradually expose models to increasingly complex examples.

#### 5.2. Conclusion

This research makes a significant contribution by introducing the concept of "in-class data imbalance" and demonstrating its impact on model training and evaluation. By revealing that easy samples vastly outnumber hard ones and that these two groups contribute differently to model learning, the work challenges the conventional reliance on aggregate test accuracy as the primary evaluation metric.

The findings suggest that researchers and practitioners should explicitly consider the distribution of hard and easy samples when designing, training, and evaluating machine learning models. This more nuanced approach could lead to more robust and reliable models, particularly in critical applications where performance on challenging inputs is essential.

Future work might explore how in-class data imbalance manifests in different domains beyond image classification, as well as developing automated methods to address this imbalance during training. Additionally, the relationship between hard samples and real-world performance in deployed systems represents an important area for further investigation.
