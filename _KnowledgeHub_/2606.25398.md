# MAPL: Multi-Objective Preference Learning for Robot Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> MAPL introduces a framework for learning robot locomotion rewards directly from high-level natural language objectives, addressing the complexity of manual reward engineering. This approach decomposes complex tasks into distinct objectives, leveraging LLMs to generate per-objective preference rankings, which are then linearly combined to guide policy training. The framework achieved locomotion policies with performance comparable to expert-designed rewards across various terrains, demonstrating enhanced stability and velocity tracking.

## Problem

- Designing effective reward functions for complex robot locomotion tasks using Reinforcement Learning (RL) is challenging, often requiring meticulous manual engineering to balance numerous competing objectives.
- Imprecise reward specifications can lead to suboptimal policies or unintended behaviors like "reward hacking."
- Existing LLM-based preference learning methods typically solicit a single, overarching judgment between behaviors, which is problematic for multi-objective tasks and can lead to less reliable preferences and noisy supervision signals.

## Method

- **MAPL** decomposes the complex locomotion task into semantically grounded, independent objectives: velocity tracking, stability, and motion smoothness, defined exclusively in natural language.
- It queries a Large Language Model (LLM) to elicit separate ==preference rankings== for pairs of downsampled trajectories for each distinct objective, mitigating implicit trade-offs.
- A transformer-based preference scoring model is trained using these multi-objective LLM-generated rankings, with shared parameters and a distinct output head for each objective, whose scores are then linearly combined to form the reward signal for policy training.

## Results

- MAPL consistently achieved higher final tracking rewards and convergence rates across all four tested terrains (flat plane, wave, stairs, obstacle) compared to LAPP and LLM Reward baselines, with policies performing comparably to expert-designed rewards.
- Ablation studies confirmed that incorporating all three objectives (velocity, stability, smoothness) was necessary for optimal locomotion, achieving nearly twice the evaluation reward of the best two-term variant with reduced variance.
- Policies trained with MAPL rewards showed tighter clustering around stable upright positions and consistently achieved the lowest velocity tracking error across all terrains and commanded velocities, demonstrating improved stability and generalization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing complex robot locomotion tasks into separate, semantically meaningful objectives (e.g., velocity, stability, smoothness) for LLM-based preference learning yields more accurate and reliable supervision signals than single, monolithic judgments.
> - The integration of a potential difference formulation in the reward signal significantly boosts training speed and final returns for policies learned from LLM-generated preferences, improving sample efficiency and stability.
> - A multi-objective preference learning approach can produce robot locomotion policies with stability and performance comparable to, or sometimes exceeding, those trained with manually engineered expert rewards, effectively reducing the reliance on domain expertise.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has advanced legged locomotion, enabling robots to acquire agile and robust behaviors across diverse terrains. However, a significant practical challenge in applying RL for complex robot locomotion is the design of effective reward functions. These functions often require meticulous manual engineering, balancing numerous competing objectives such as command tracking, stability, and smoothness. Imprecise specifications can lead to suboptimal policies or "reward hacking."

Preference-based Reinforcement Learning (PbRL) emerged as an alternative to mitigate the reliance on expert-crafted reward functions. In PbRL, a reward function is learned from human judgments indicating preferences between different behaviors or trajectories. Recent advancements have sought to automate this process by replacing human annotators with Large Language Models (LLMs), a paradigm often referred to as Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF). These LLM-based methods typically involve either direct generation of reward code or the production of pairwise rankings over behaviors to train a reward model.

Existing LLM-based preference learning methods commonly solicit a single, overarching judgment between behaviors. For complex tasks like robot locomotion, which inherently involve multiple, often competing, objectives, this single-judgment approach can be problematic. It forces the LLM to implicitly weigh various distinct behavioral criteria simultaneously, potentially leading to less reliable preferences and suboptimal downstream policies due to noisy supervision signals and potential gradient interference.

MAPL addresses this limitation by decomposing the complex locomotion task into semantically meaningful, independent objectives. By prompting an LLM to evaluate trajectories separately along these criteria, MAPL aims to elicit more accurate and reliable preference signals, thus improving the effectiveness of LLM-based reward learning for multi-objective robot locomotion. This work builds upon prior PbRL and RLAIF methods in robot manipulation and locomotion, such as LAPP and DAPPER, but differentiates itself by its explicit multi-objective decomposition strategy and reduced reliance on additional handcrafted rewards.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, Multi-Objective AI-Informed Preference Learning (MAPL), that enables robots to learn locomotion rewards from high-level natural language objectives, thereby circumventing the need for manually engineered reward equations.

The specific objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Eliminate Manual Reward Engineering:** A central motivation is to overcome the bottleneck of reward design in RL for robot locomotion. Current methods heavily depend on carefully tuned, task-specific reward functions that are often tedious, brittle, and susceptible to misspecifications, leading to poor policy performance or reward hacking. MAPL seeks to automate this process by leveraging LLMs.
- **Capture Multiple Competing Objectives:** Robot locomotion requires balancing several distinct objectives, such as maintaining stability, achieving desired velocities, and executing smooth movements. Existing LLM-based preference learning methods, which typically ask for a single overall judgment, struggle to capture these multi-faceted and often competing criteria. The motivation for MAPL is to address this by allowing for independent evaluation of each objective.
- **Enhance Preference Elicitation Reliability:** When an LLM is required to produce a single global ranking, it must implicitly trade off multiple criteria simultaneously. This can result in complex prompt engineering and noisy supervision signals, especially if the LLM's accuracy varies across different criteria. MAPL is motivated by the insight that LLMs can provide more accurate preference rankings when each objective is ranked and modeled independently.
- **Improve Downstream Policy Performance:** By providing more reliable and granular supervision signals, MAPL aims to train more optimal policies compared to methods that rely on monolithic preference judgments. The goal is to achieve performance comparable to or superior to policies trained with expert-designed rewards.
- **Reduce Domain Expertise and Increase Scalability:** The framework is designed to use generic natural language descriptions for objectives that are terrain-invariant and require minimal domain expertise. This aims to simplify the reward design process for practitioners and enable a single set of prompts to train policies across diverse terrain types, enhancing scalability and reducing task-specific engineering efforts.
- **Decompose Reward Learning and Objective Balancing:** By learning separate preference scoring models for each objective and then linearly combining their outputs, MAPL aims to decouple the process of learning reward representations from the balancing of objective weights. This design is intended to improve training stability and allow for control over objective balancing through a low-dimensional weight vector.

In summary, MAPL is motivated by the practical challenges of reward design in RL for robot locomotion and aims to provide a more effective, scalable, and user-friendly approach by leveraging multi-objective, AI-informed preference learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Multi-Objective AI-Informed Preference Learning (MAPL) framework operates through an iterative process involving two primary steps: preference scoring model training and policy training (Figure 1, Algorithm 1). These steps are executed at different frequencies, with the scoring model updated less frequently.

**A. Locomotion Objectives**
MAPL identifies three semantically grounded and task-agnostic criteria crucial for high-quality quadruped locomotion across diverse terrains: velocity tracking, stability, and motion smoothness. These criteria are defined exclusively in natural language, without domain-specific engineering, and are consistently applied across all terrain types.
- **Velocity Tracking:** Measures how accurately the robot follows commanded linear and angular velocities. The prompt instructs the LLM to prioritize smaller deviations and to reduce preference smoothly with increasing errors, ensuring no single timestep invalidates a trajectory completely. It also includes yaw velocity tracking as a secondary criterion and gait pattern consistency conditionally.
- **Stability:** Assesses the robot's ability to maintain an upright position, minimizing excessive roll, pitch, and vertical motion. The prompt emphasizes maintaining a consistent base height (e.g., 0.34 m), penalizing even small deviations, and requires roll/pitch angular velocities to remain small and smooth.
- **Smoothness:** Evaluates the smoothness of control signals and the absence of undesirable events. The prompt specifies a preference for trajectories with smaller overall joint action differences and strictly prefers trajectories with fewer or weaker stumble events, even if action smoothness is slightly worse in non-stumbling trajectories.

**B. Multi-Objective Preference Generation**
Unlike existing RLAIF methods that merge multiple objectives into a single preference ranking, MAPL elicits separate preference rankings for each of the three defined objectives. The LLM is queried to rank pairs of downsampled trajectories, each consisting of `M` evenly-distributed states. To account for LLM stochasticity, multiple queries are made for each ranking, and the majority answer is selected to establish the preference label. The preference labels indicate whether trajectory 0 is preferred over trajectory 1 (0), trajectory 1 over trajectory 0 (1), or if they are equally preferable (0.5).

**C. Preference Scoring Model Training**
A transformer-based preference scoring model ($\sigma_{\psi}$) is trained using the LLM-generated preference rankings. This model incorporates shared parameters and features a distinct output head for each objective, producing a preference score $\sigma^{(i)}_{\psi}(s)$ for each state `s` corresponding to objective `i`. The training process minimizes a pairwise ranking loss formulated based on the Bradley–Terry model (Equation 3, 4). The scoring model is updated less frequently than the policy (e.g., 100 times slower) to account for the computational expense of LLM calls and allow the policy's state visitation distribution to evolve sufficiently.

**D. Policy Training**
In the policy training step, state-action pairs are collected from policy rollouts in the environment. An overall preference score for each state `s_t` is calculated as a linear combination of the per-objective preference scores:
$\sigma_{\psi}(s_t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i \sigma^{(i)}_{\psi}(s_t)$ (Equation 5),
where $\lambda_i$ are coefficients determining the relative weight of each objective. These weights are treated as hyperparameters and are manually specified, prioritizing velocity tracking and stability, with smoothness adjusted based on terrain difficulty (e.g., higher weight for stairs).

To enhance sample efficiency and policy return, MAPL employs a potential difference formulation for the final reward signal:
$r(s_t) = \sigma_{\psi}(s_t) - \sigma_{\psi}(s_{t-1})$ (Equation 6).
This approach provides a reward signal for the policy optimization algorithm.

**E. Experiment Setup**
MAPL's performance is evaluated on quadruped locomotion tasks using the Unitree Go2 robot in the Isaac Gym simulator, within the Legged-Gym framework. Four environments are used: a flat plane, hilly terrain (wave), pyramidal stairs, and randomly-sized obstacles.
- **Metrics:** Performance is assessed using linear velocity reward and angular velocity reward, both Gaussian-shaped tracking terms that quantify how closely the robot's actual velocities match commanded velocities.
- **Curriculum Learning:** For uneven terrains, a curriculum learning strategy is employed, gradually increasing terrain difficulty as the policy achieves predefined success thresholds. Evaluation rewards are difficulty-weighted to ensure fair comparisons.
- **Baselines:** MAPL is compared against LAPP (a preference learning method using a single preference signal, without environment reward), LLM Reward (where an LLM generates reward function code), and Expert Reward (a hand-designed function by a domain expert, serving as an upper bound).
- **RL Algorithm:** All baselines and MAPL utilize the PPO-based RSL-RL algorithm with consistent network architecture and hyperparameters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding MAPL's performance and properties:

**A. Locomotion Policy Performance:**
MAPL consistently achieved higher final tracking rewards and convergence rates across all four terrains (flat plane, wave, stairs, obstacle) compared to the LAPP and LLM Reward baselines. The policies trained with MAPL's LLM-generated preferences demonstrated performance comparable to, and in some scenarios superior to, policies trained with expert-designed rewards, while eliminating the need for task-specific reward engineering (Figure 3). In contrast, policies trained with LLM Reward or LAPP alone often plateaued at lower returns or failed to learn useful behaviors without additional expert rewards.

**B. Preference Component Ablation:**
An ablation study investigating the contribution of individual preference components (velocity, stability, smoothness) revealed that policies trained with rewards derived from single objectives or pairwise combinations were suboptimal (Figure 4, top-left). For instance, velocity-only preferences failed to produce meaningful locomotion, while stability-only and smoothness-only variants achieved only low returns. The full MAPL framework, incorporating all three objectives, exhibited the best performance, achieving nearly twice the evaluation reward of the best two-term variant with reduced variance. This indicates that velocity drives motion, stability ensures robust dynamics, and smoothness mitigates control oscillations, with all components being necessary for balanced locomotion.

**C. Trajectory and Query Sensitivity:**
- **Trajectory Buffer Size:** An analysis of trajectory buffer size on the plane environment showed that using 1000 trajectory pairs resulted in the highest evaluation reward, while 500 trajectories still yielded competitive performance, suggesting sample efficiency for MAPL (Figure 4, middle-right).
- **LLM Query Number:** Investigating the number of LLM queries per trajectory ranking (to mitigate hallucination) demonstrated MAPL's robustness. Policies trained with a single query per ranking achieved nearly the same final return as those trained with multiple queries, albeit with slightly slower early-stage learning. No significant performance improvement was observed when increasing queries beyond three (Figure 4, bottom-right).

**D. Potential Difference Reward Modeling:**
The integration of a potential difference formulation in MAPL significantly boosted training speed and final returns across all locomotion tasks (Figure 6). Removing this component consistently led to substantial degradation in both final performance and convergence speed for MAPL. For LLM Reward and LAPP, incorporating potential difference often resulted in early plateauing or unstable training, suggesting that these methods might not be able to effectively utilize potential-based shaping due to poorly structured underlying rewards.

**E. Robot Performance under Different Rewards:**
Analysis of robot body height offset, roll, and pitch distributions revealed that policies trained with MAPL rewards achieved a tighter clustering around the origin (representing stable standing) compared to expert rewards (Figure 7). This indicates improved stability without relying on expert-crafted terms. LLM rewards showed higher variance, and LAPP exhibited a clear bias away from the origin, indicative of unstable posture. These results support MAPL's ability to yield robust and balanced locomotion behaviors.

**F. Preference Aggregation Weight Sensitivity:**
The sensitivity analysis for the aggregation weights ($\lambda$) of each preference criterion (velocity, stability, smoothness) showed that large, distinct weights for velocity and stability led to high and similar final returns, consistent with their dominant roles (Figure 8). For smoothness, which plays a less critical role, high $\lambda$ values achieved similarly low training returns. This suggests that the relative influence of objectives can be intuitively determined, allowing for reliable combination of preference scores without extensive hyperparameter tuning, provided more influential objectives receive relatively larger weights.

**G. Velocity Error Analysis:**
MAPL consistently achieved the lowest velocity tracking error across all terrains and commanded velocities (0.4 to 1.0 m/s), demonstrating strong generalization and stable performance, even under more aggressive commands (Figure 5). LLM-based reward methods showed moderate errors, while LAPP exhibited significantly larger errors and a degradation trend, particularly at higher command velocities, indicating that single-objective preference signals may be insufficient for precise control.

**H. Ranking Accuracy Analysis:**
MAPL's multi-objective preference-query strategy (MOMQ) achieved higher ranking accuracy compared to other strategies when measured against ground-truth rewards across velocity, stability, and smoothness objectives (Table II). MOMQ recorded 88% accuracy for velocity, 76% for stability, and 98% for smoothness, highlighting the effectiveness of using multiple objective-specific prompts and multiple queries.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of MAPL has several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of robot locomotion and reinforcement learning:

- **Alleviation of Reward Engineering Bottleneck:** MAPL directly addresses one of the primary challenges in applying RL to robot locomotion: the need for labor-intensive and expert-dependent reward function design. By learning rewards from high-level natural language objectives, the framework significantly reduces the manual effort and specialized domain expertise traditionally required.
- **Enhanced Scalability and Generalization:** The ability of MAPL to train robust policies across diverse terrains (flat ground, uneven terrain, stairs, obstacles) using a single, generic natural language prompt represents a notable step towards scalable robot learning. This reduces the need for task-specific reward engineering for new environments or locomotion challenges.
- **Improved Policy Performance and Robustness:** The reported performance, which is comparable to or superior to expert-designed rewards, demonstrates that LLM-generated multi-objective preferences can yield high-quality and stable locomotion policies. The improved stability and lower velocity tracking errors indicate that the decomposed preference learning approach produces more effective reward signals.
- **More Intuitive Human-Robot Interaction:** By allowing objective specification in natural language, MAPL moves towards a more intuitive and accessible way for designers to communicate desired robot behaviors. This could lower the barrier to entry for robotics development, enabling a broader range of users to train complex robot skills.
- **Validation of Multi-Objective Decomposition:** The ablation studies confirm the necessity of decomposing complex locomotion tasks into distinct objectives. This methodological insight is significant for future preference learning research in multi-objective domains, suggesting that breaking down judgments can lead to more accurate and reliable AI feedback.
- **Robustness to LLM Limitations:** The framework's demonstrated robustness to LLM inconsistent ranking answers and its efficient use of trajectory samples contribute to its practical viability, mitigating some known challenges associated with using LLMs for preference generation.

Despite these contributions, the paper notes limitations, such as the current use of linear objective aggregation. Future work could explore more expressive nonlinear aggregation mechanisms or integrate multimodal foundation models (e.g., vision-language, tactile-aware models) to expand reward learning capabilities for more complex behaviors like loco-manipulation. Overall, MAPL represents progress towards making RL for robot locomotion more accessible and efficient by leveraging the interpretability and adaptability of large language models.
