# ATOM-Bench: A Real-World Benchmark for Atomic Skills and Compositional Generalization in Manipulation Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> ATOM-Bench, a real-world benchmark from the Beijing Academy of Artificial Intelligence and Peking University, introduces a diagnostic testbed to evaluate the acquisition of atomic motor and instruction skills and their compositional generalization in robot manipulation policies using 3,000 human demonstrations across single-arm and dual-arm platforms. It finds that current policies struggle with fine-grained motor control and robustly combining learned atomic skills into novel, unseen compositional tasks, despite some proficiency in basic instruction grounding.

## Problem

- Existing real-world robotic manipulation benchmarks offer limited control to diagnose specific skill acquisition or compositional generalization capabilities of generalist policies.
- It is challenging to determine if manipulation policies learn truly reusable "atomic" skills that transfer to novel compositional tasks or if they merely perform well on specific task templates.
- Current evaluation methods struggle to distinguish between failures stemming from weak motor execution, poor instruction grounding, or limitations in compositional reuse.

## Method

- **ATOM-Bench** factorizes tabletop manipulation into 15 "==motor atoms==" (e.g., pick-and-place, pouring) and "instruction atoms" (e.g., color, size, counting) for structured training and evaluation.
- The benchmark provides 3,000 human demonstrations for 30 atomic tasks (15 single-arm, 15 dual-arm) and 12 held-out compositional tasks on Franka Panda and Agilex Cobot Magic robot platforms.
- Diagnostic metrics such as Atomic Score (AS) and Compositional Failure Share (CFS) are introduced to precisely quantify atomic skill acquisition and the ability to compose these skills for novel scenarios.

## Results

- Pi0.5, a top-performing policy, achieved **94.3%** Success Rate (SR) on Franka Panda instruction atoms but only **46.2%** SR on motor atoms, highlighting the difficulty of physical execution.
- Compositional tasks showed a sharp decline in SR across policies; for Pi0.5, compositional SR was **15.8%** (Franka Panda) and **16.7%** (Cobot Magic), despite Atomic Scores (AS) of **83.3%** and **79.5%** respectively.
- Failure analysis indicated that motor failure, reference failure, and timeout accounted for over **90%** of failures, with dual-arm tasks presenting additional challenges due to coordination and sequencing.
- Robustness evaluations showed that atomic tasks were highly resilient to visual and language perturbations (90-**100%** SR), but compositional tasks demonstrated significant sensitivity, with SR drops from **60%** to **40%** (task dx1) and **50%** to **20%** (task dx12 under language perturbation).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current policies generally acquire instruction atoms more easily than motor atoms, but struggle with fine-grained motor control tasks (e.g., pouring, reorientation) and complex logical instruction atoms (e.g., counting, exclusion).
> - A substantial performance drop occurs when policies are evaluated on held-out compositional tasks compared to their constituent atomic skills, with a high Compositional Failure Share indicating failures beyond just weak atomic execution.
> - Compositional skills exhibited brittleness to language perturbations (rewording instructions), suggesting a reliance on surface-level forms rather than robust understanding for complex task structures, while atomic skills were more robust.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in ATOM-Bench addresses a challenge within the field of robotic manipulation, specifically concerning the evaluation of generalist manipulation policies, often referred to as foundation models for robotic control. These policies are designed to acquire and recombine reusable physical and semantic skills to solve new tasks. However, verifying their real-world generalization capabilities has proven difficult.

Existing evaluation paradigms exhibit limitations that ATOM-Bench aims to address. Simulation benchmarks offer scalability, control, and rapid iteration, but their programmatically generated task distributions can be absorbed by high-capacity policies, potentially leading to memorization rather than true generalization. Furthermore, the physical challenges in simulated environments are often less demanding than those encountered in real-world contact, perception, and execution, thus providing limited evidence of deployable real-robot generalization.

In response to the limitations of simulation, efforts have been made to develop real-world manipulation benchmarks. Examples include RoboArena, which studies distributed pairwise policy ranking; AutoEval, which automates success classification and scene resets; RoboChallenge, an online evaluation system for scalable real-robot testing; and GM-100, which expands real-robot task diversity. While these benchmarks advance real-world evaluation by improving scalability, automation, and task coverage, they primarily evaluate policies over task suites or through ranking protocols. They offer limited control over the specific skills a policy adapts to or is tested on. This lack of control makes it challenging to ascertain whether a policy has learned truly reusable "atomic" skills that can transfer to novel, unseen compositional tasks, or if it is merely performing well on specific task templates or dataset regularities.

ATOM-Bench intervenes in this research landscape by introducing a diagnostic testbed specifically designed to control the atomic training distribution and evaluate both the acquisition of these atomic skills and their generalization to held-out compositional tasks on real robots. This approach is intended to bridge the diagnostic gap in existing real-world benchmarks, enabling a more precise understanding of where manipulation policies succeed or fail—whether the issue lies in weak motor execution, poor instruction grounding, or limited compositional reuse.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of ATOM-Bench is to provide a real-world benchmark for the rigorous evaluation of atomic skills and compositional generalization in manipulation policies. This objective is driven by the increasing deployment of generalist manipulation policies as foundation models and the difficulty in diagnosing their real-world generalization performance. The authors specifically aim to investigate two central questions:

1. **Atomic Skill Acquisition:** Can current manipulation policies reliably acquire fine-grained atomic skills? This encompasses both physical motor skills (e.g., grasping, pushing, pouring) and language-conditioned instruction skills (e.g., selecting objects based on color, size, or spatial relation). The motivation here is to understand the foundational capabilities of these policies when trained on isolated, elementary behaviors. Without robust atomic skill acquisition, compositional generalization is inherently limited.
2. **Compositional Generalization:** After atomic skills are acquired, can they be effectively recombined to solve novel, held-out compositional tasks that were not explicitly demonstrated during the fine-tuning process? The motivation for this question stems from the hypothesis that true generalist policies should be able to leverage learned primitives in new combinations. Success on individual skills does not guarantee the ability to integrate them into more complex, unseen sequences or under new semantic constraints. Verifying compositional generalization is crucial for assessing a policy's capacity for flexible intelligence rather than mere memorization of task templates.

The motivation for developing ATOM-Bench arises from several limitations in the current evaluation landscape for robotic manipulation:

- **Difficulty in Diagnosing Real-World Generalization:** Despite impressive demonstrations in controlled settings or simulations, it remains challenging to ascertain if a policy has learned genuinely composable skills transferable to novel real-world scenarios. Success might stem from overfitting to specific task structures or dataset regularities.
- **Limitations of Existing Benchmarks:** As discussed previously, simulation benchmarks lack real-world physical fidelity, and existing real-world benchmarks often test policies on broad task suites without the granular control needed to diagnose the root causes of failure. It is difficult to distinguish between failures due to a lack of basic skill (atomic failure) versus failures in combining known skills (compositional failure).
- **Need for Diagnostic Evaluation:** The authors argue that a diagnostic approach, which distinguishes between these failure types, is more informative than overall success rates on complex tasks. By pinpointing whether failures originate from weak motor execution, poor instruction grounding, or limited compositional reuse, researchers can better understand the strengths and weaknesses of current manipulation policies and guide future development.

Therefore, ATOM-Bench is designed to offer a controlled real-world protocol that allows for the joint measurement of atomic-task performance and held-out compositional performance under a consistent adaptation setting, providing insights into the actual generalization capabilities of robotic manipulation systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of ATOM-Bench is structured around the factorization of tabletop manipulation tasks into fundamental "atoms," which are then used to construct training and evaluation scenarios on real robotic platforms.

**Task Factorization and Task Groups:**
ATOM-Bench categorizes task components into two types:
- **Motor Atoms:** These specify the physical operations required, such as pick-and-place, reorientation, pushing, stacking, pouring, and articulated-object access. These atoms represent core physical skills.
- **Instruction Atoms:** These specify semantic or logical constraints in language for object selection or destination, including color, shape, size, source relation, goal destination, counting, and exclusion. These atoms test language understanding and grounding.

Based on these atoms, tasks are organized into three groups for each robotic platform:
- **Motor Set:** Contains 8 tasks, each isolating a single motor atom with minimal semantic ambiguity.
- **Instruction Set:** Contains 7 tasks, each isolating a single instruction atom, typically using a simple pick-and-place action as the carrier.
- **Composition Set:** Contains 12 held-out tasks designed to combine one or more motor atoms with multiple instruction atoms. These tasks are used to evaluate compositional generalization, as they are not explicitly fine-tuned.

**Paired Robotic Platforms:**
The benchmark includes two paired real-world robot tracks:
- **Single-arm track:** Utilizes a Franka Panda robot equipped with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper and three Intel RealSense cameras.
- **Dual-arm track:** Utilizes an Agilex Cobot Magic system, a dual-arm setup based on Mobile ALOHA, also with three Intel RealSense cameras.
The dual-arm tasks are designed as bimanual counterparts to the single-arm tasks, introducing additional complexities such as hand-role assignment, inter-arm coordination, and ordered action sequences.

**Data Collection:**
- A total of 3,000 human demonstrations were collected.
- For each of the 30 atomic tasks (15 single-arm and 15 dual-arm), 100 expert demonstrations were gathered through human teleoperation.
- The Franka Panda was controlled via a 3D SpaceMouse, while the Cobot Magic was teleoperated using its master arms.
- During teleoperation, synchronized observations and robot actions were recorded at 30 Hz.
- The total demonstration data amounts to approximately 9 hours for Franka Panda and 12.5 hours for Agilex Cobot Magic.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
ATOM-Bench employs five metrics to provide a comprehensive assessment:
- **Success Rate (SR):** The percentage of rollouts that successfully meet the task completion criteria. This is the primary metric for overall task completion.
- **Process Success Rate (PSR):** Measures intermediate progress by calculating the per-rollout fraction of pre-defined score points achieved. This metric accounts for partial successes.
- **Atomic Score (AS):** For a given compositional task X, AS(X) is defined as the mean PSR of all individual motor and instruction atoms that constitute task X. It aims to quantify the policy's performance on the constituent skills in isolation.
- **Compositional Failure Share (CFS):** Quantifies the portion of compositional task failure that is not attributable to weak atomic skills. It is calculated as `max(0, AS(X) - PSR(X)) / (1 - PSR(X))`. A high CFS indicates that learned atomic skills fail to compose effectively.
- **Transfer Gap (TG):** The difference in SR between policies fine-tuned on individual atomic tasks (single-task fine-tuning) and those fine-tuned on all atomic tasks jointly (atomic-transfer fine-tuning).

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Policies Evaluated:** Five representative manipulation policies were tested: Pi0.5, LingBot-VLA, GROOT N1.6, SmolVLA, and Motus. Each policy was evaluated on both Franka Panda and Cobot Magic platforms.
- **Fine-tuning Protocols:**
 - **Atomic-transfer fine-tuning (Primary):** A single policy is jointly fine-tuned on all 15 atomic tasks (100 demonstrations per task, totaling 1,500 demonstrations). This jointly trained policy is then evaluated on both the atomic tasks and the held-out Composition Set.
 - **Single-task atomic fine-tuning (Baseline):** For Pi0.5 only, an independent checkpoint is trained for each individual atomic task using its 100 demonstrations. This provides a specialized baseline for comparison.
- **Evaluation Rollouts:** Each task evaluation consists of 10 physical rollouts using shared physical test seeds.
- **Reproducible Scene Setup:** To ensure consistency across evaluations, a mask-guided object placement procedure is used. Operators arrange physical objects to align with semi-transparent masks overlayed on camera views, reproducing initial conditions for each seed.
- **Robustness Evaluation (Supplementary):** Select policies are re-evaluated under visual perturbations (changing background) and language perturbations (rewording instructions) to assess their reliance on surface-level forms rather than underlying skills.

This methodology aims to provide a controlled and diagnostic framework for evaluating manipulation policies in real-world settings, specifically dissecting performance into atomic skill acquisition and compositional generalization components.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using ATOM-Bench yielded several findings regarding atomic skill acquisition and compositional generalization in manipulation policies. The analysis is based on 2,700 physical rollouts across five representative policies.

**Atomic Skill Acquisition:**
- **Instruction vs. Motor Atoms:** Instruction atoms were generally found to be easier for current policies to acquire than motor atoms. For instance, Pi0.5 achieved a 94.3% Success Rate (SR) on Franka Panda instruction atoms compared to 46.2% SR on motor atoms. This is attributed to instruction atoms often being evaluated with simpler carrier actions (e.g., pick-and-place), while motor atoms demand execution of specific physical skills.
- **Motor Atom Difficulty:** Among motor atoms, pick-and-place was the easiest, with a cross-model mean SR of 74%. In contrast, tasks requiring precise control and object pose management, such as access (9%), reorientation (11%), pushing (16%), and pouring (20%), demonstrated significantly lower cross-model mean SRs, indicating persistent challenges in fine-grained physical execution.
- **Instruction Atom Difficulty:** Simple visual grounding tasks (color, size, source relation) achieved higher success rates. However, instruction atoms requiring set-level reasoning and logical filtering, such as counting and exclusion, proved more challenging, suggesting limitations in current policies' ability to process complex semantic constraints.
- **Policy Performance:** Pi0.5 consistently performed best on both single-arm (Franka Panda) and dual-arm (Cobot Magic) platforms, followed by Motus. LingBot-VLA showed competitive performance on some Franka Panda motor tasks but lagged on Cobot Magic. GROOT N1.6 exhibited mid-range performance, while SmolVLA generally trailed across all settings.
- **Partial Successes:** The Process Success Rate (PSR) was frequently higher than SR, particularly for motor tasks. This indicates that policies often completed early stages or intermediate steps but failed to meet the final success criteria, signifying partial failures rather than complete inability to initiate tasks.

**Compositional Generalization:**
- **Significant Performance Drop:** A key finding was the sharp decline in performance when policies were evaluated on held-out compositional tasks, despite achieving non-trivial Atomic Scores (AS). For example, Pi0.5 achieved high AS values (83.3% on Franka Panda, 79.5% on Cobot Magic), but its compositional SR was substantially lower (15.8% and 16.7% respectively). Similar patterns were observed across other policies.
- **Compositional Failure Share (CFS):** High CFS values, particularly for top-performing policies like Pi0.5 (73.7% on Franka Panda, 56.8% on Cobot Magic), indicated that a substantial portion of compositional failures could not be attributed solely to weak atomic skills. This suggests that the difficulty lies in the composition and integration of learned skills into novel task structures. Policies like SmolVLA, with low AS and CFS, indicated failures still primarily limited by atomic skill acquisition.
- **Consistent Partial Progress:** Similar to atomic tasks, PSR on compositional tasks was consistently higher than SR, indicating that policies often made some progress on multi-stage tasks but failed to complete them entirely.

**Failure Analysis:**
- **Dominant Failure Modes:** Analysis of failed rollouts categorized motor failure, reference failure, and timeout as accounting for over 90% of failures. This indicates bottlenecks in physical execution, target object/destination grounding, and overall task progress management.
- **Specific Failure Types:** Motor failures were prevalent in tasks requiring precise control (e.g., pouring, reorientation, articulated-object access). Reference failures were more common in tasks involving spatial relations, exclusion, or multiple candidate destinations.
- **Dual-Arm Challenges:** Dual-arm tasks posed additional difficulties due to requirements for hand-role assignment, inter-arm coordination, and longer ordered action sequences, leading to higher failure rates compared to their single-arm counterparts.

**Atomic-transfer vs. Single-task Atomic Fine-tuning:**
- **Transfer Gap (TG):** The comparison on Pi0.5 revealed varying effects of fine-tuning protocols. On Franka Panda, most atoms showed a negative TG, implying that joint atomic-transfer training improved single-arm skills. This is likely due to shared low-level behaviors and increased data/scene variation.
- **Platform-Specific Effects:** On Cobot Magic, instruction atoms still largely benefited from atomic-transfer training. However, several bimanual motor atoms exhibited a positive TG, suggesting that for complex bimanual coordination, single-task fine-tuning allowed for better specialization to the required action patterns.

**Robustness to Perturbations (Supplementary Results):**
- **Atomic Task Robustness:** On select atomic tasks, Pi0.5 demonstrated high robustness to both visual (background change) and language (instruction rewording) perturbations, with SR remaining high (90-100%). This suggests the policy learned the underlying atomic skills rather than overfitting to superficial cues.
- **Compositional Task Brittleness:** In contrast, compositional tasks showed a sharp drop in robustness. For instance, task dx1's SR fell from 60% to 40% under both perturbations, and dx12's SR dropped from 50% to 20% under language perturbation. Language perturbations generally had a more significant negative impact than visual perturbations on compositional tasks. This indicates that compositional skills are more sensitive and brittle to changes in surface-level language forms.

In summary, ATOM-Bench demonstrates that while current policies can acquire some atomic skills, particularly simple instruction grounding, they struggle with fine-grained motor control, complex logical instruction atoms, and reliably composing these skills into novel, unseen real-world tasks. The brittleness of compositional skills to language variations highlights an area for further research.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ATOM-Bench offers several significant contributions and has the potential to impact the field of robotic manipulation in multiple ways:

**1. Diagnostic Tool for Generalization:**
The primary significance of ATOM-Bench lies in its capacity to serve as a diagnostic testbed. By factorizing manipulation into motor and instruction atoms and employing metrics like Atomic Score (AS) and Compositional Failure Share (CFS), the benchmark enables researchers to differentiate between failures arising from weak atomic skills, poor instruction grounding, or limitations in compositional reuse. This granular understanding moves beyond aggregate success rates, providing actionable insights into the specific shortcomings of current manipulation policies. It allows for a more targeted approach to model development and improvement.

**2. Real-World Evaluation and Reproducibility:**
ATOM-Bench provides a real-world benchmark, offering stronger evidence for generalist policy capabilities compared to simulation-based evaluations. The collection of 3,000 human demonstrations across two distinct robotic platforms (single-arm Franka Panda and dual-arm Agilex Cobot Magic) and the release of both demonstration and evaluation rollout data promote reproducibility, a critical factor in real-robot research where experiments are often costly and difficult to replicate. The mask-guided object placement further enhances the reproducibility of initial physical conditions.

**3. Structured Task Design and Complexity:**
The systematic decomposition of tasks into motor and instruction atoms, combined with the creation of atomic and held-out compositional task sets, offers a structured framework for designing and analyzing manipulation tasks. The paired single-arm and dual-arm tracks introduce increasing levels of complexity, specifically addressing challenges related to bimanual coordination, hand-role assignment, and sequential actions. This allows for a deeper understanding of how policies scale from simpler to more complex real-world scenarios.

**4. Identification of Current Policy Limitations:**
The findings from evaluating five representative manipulation policies (Pi0.5, LingBot-VLA, GROOT N1.6, SmolVLA, Motus) reveal critical limitations of the current state-of-the-art. Specifically, the benchmark highlights that while policies can acquire basic instruction-grounding skills, they still struggle with:
- **Fine-grained motor control:** Tasks requiring precision like pouring, reorientation, and pushing remain challenging.
- **Complex instruction grounding:** Set-level reasoning, such as counting and exclusion, proves difficult.
- **Robust compositional generalization:** A significant performance gap exists between atomic skill acquisition and their reliable composition into novel tasks, even when atomic skills appear robust. The brittleness of compositional skills to language paraphrasing is a particularly important finding.

**5. Guidance for Future Research and Development:**
ATOM-Bench provides a clear roadmap for future research in generalist robot manipulation. The identified failure modes underscore the need for:
- Developing models with improved physical reasoning and precise motor control capabilities.
- Enhancing language understanding to handle more complex semantic and logical constraints (e.g., counting, exclusion, spatial relations with multiple objects).
- Focusing on architectural designs and training methodologies that explicitly foster compositional generalization, enabling policies to recombine learned primitives more robustly for unseen tasks and under various linguistic expressions. The distinction between atomic-transfer and single-task fine-tuning also offers insights into optimal training strategies for different types of skills.

In conclusion, ATOM-Bench serves as a valuable diagnostic tool that rigorously probes the real-world generalization capabilities of manipulation policies. By dissecting performance into atomic and compositional components, it not only exposes the specific weaknesses of current models but also provides a structured framework and rich dataset to guide the development of more robust and truly generalist robotic agents.
