# Language Conditioned Imitation Learning over Unstructured Data

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Google Robotics developed LangLfP, a framework for training visuomotor policies to follow natural language instructions by combining unstructured play data with limited language-annotated examples. This approach achieved comparable performance to image-conditioned methods while significantly reducing language annotation costs, demonstrating enhanced robustness with pretrained language models.

## Problem

- Traditional imitation learning struggles with scalability due to high data requirements for each new skill and reliance on impractical task specification mechanisms (e.g., one-hot task selectors, goal images).
- Prior instruction-following methods often make simplifying assumptions, limiting their applicability to complex, high-dimensional robotic manipulation tasks with free-form natural language.
- Integrating natural language as a flexible and intuitive task specification into scalable robotic learning frameworks remains a significant challenge.

## Method

- **LangLfP** collects large volumes of unstructured 'play' demonstration data, which is then relabeled with ==hindsight goal images== and, for a small subset, with ==hindsight natural language instructions==.
- A ==Multicontext Imitation Learning (MCIL)== framework trains a single visuomotor policy using separate context encoders that map both goal images and natural language into a shared latent goal space.
- The approach leverages pretrained neural language models (e.g., **MUSE**) to enhance language understanding and robustness to diverse linguistic inputs at test time.

## Results

- The LangLfP policy successfully executed complex natural language instructions, achieving **68.6%** success on individual tasks and **52.1%** on four-stage chained tasks in a 3D simulated environment.
- LangLfP achieved performance comparable to or slightly exceeding goal image-conditioned methods while requiring less than **1%** of the total demonstration data to be annotated with language instructions.
- TransferLangLfP, using pretrained language models, demonstrated enhanced robustness to out-of-distribution synonym instructions (**60.2%** success) and multilingual commands (**56.0%** success across **16** languages).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Language-conditioned robotic manipulation can achieve performance comparable to image-conditioned methods, dramatically reducing the need for explicit language annotations to less than 1% of the total training data.
> - Leveraging large, unstructured, and unlabeled demonstration data through Multicontext Imitation Learning (MCIL) significantly improves language-conditioned policy performance and generalization.
> - Integrating pretrained neural language models substantially enhances the policy's robustness to linguistic variations, including out-of-distribution synonyms and instructions in multiple languages, without requiring additional demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic learning aims to enable robots to acquire complex skills, often leveraging imitation learning (IL) or reinforcement learning (RL) from sensory data. Traditional imitation learning methods typically involve learning individual skills from structured and isolated human demonstrations, which presents significant challenges for scalability in open-world environments where robots are expected to perform a wide variety of tasks. Key limitations include the substantial data requirements for each new skill and the impracticality of task specification mechanisms like one-hot task selectors or goal images in real-world scenarios.

Recent advances in multitask imitation learning have begun to address the data scale problem by utilizing large, unstructured demonstration datasets. However, these approaches generally still rely on non-language-based task specifications, which are not conducive to intuitive human-robot interaction. Concurrently, instruction following in machine learning has seen progress, often training agents to map observations and language directly to actions. Yet, prior work in this area frequently makes simplifying assumptions, such as 2D observation spaces, simplified actuators, or synthetic language, which limit their direct applicability to complex robotic manipulation tasks.

This research addresses these gaps by integrating free-form natural language conditioning into imitation learning for complex robotic manipulation. It departs from typical imitation learning by incorporating unstructured and unlabeled demonstration data, thereby reducing the dependency on explicit task or language labels for every demonstration. Unlike prior instruction-following methods, this work applies to high-dimensional pixel inputs, 8-DOF continuous control, and long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks. It builds upon concepts like task-agnostic control and goal relabeling, extending them to encompass natural language as a scalable and intuitive form of task specification. The paper also explores the integration of pretrained neural language models to enhance the robustness of policies to various linguistic inputs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable framework for training visuomotor policies that can execute complex robotic manipulation tasks based on free-form natural language instructions. This objective is motivated by several challenges inherent in deploying imitation learning for generalist robots in diverse, real-world settings.

The authors identify two central challenges:
1. **Scaling Data Requirements**: Traditional imitation learning necessitates extensive human demonstrations for each new skill, making it difficult to scale to a broad range of tasks. There is a need to explore whether many skills can be learned simultaneously from large amounts of unstructured, unlabeled demonstration data to lessen the data requirements for acquiring new skills.
2. **Scalable Task Specification**: For robots to be useful in everyday environments, untrained users must be able to instruct them easily. Current methods often rely on task IDs or goal images, which are often impractical for open-world settings. Natural language offers a flexible and intuitive alternative, but training agents to follow free-form language instructions in complex, high-dimensional robotic scenarios remains a difficult open challenge.

Specifically, the paper aims to answer the following research questions:
- How can the mapping between natural language and robot actions be learned efficiently, minimizing the number of required language labels?
- How can large datasets of unstructured demonstrations, which lack explicit task or language labels, be effectively leveraged in the training process of a language-conditioned policy?
- Given a finite training set, how can the trained policy be enabled to follow a maximum number of instructions at test time, including novel or synonymous language inputs?

The motivation is to move beyond the limitations of prior work by combining natural language conditioning with multitask imitation learning over unstructured data. This approach seeks to enable a single visuomotor policy to perform a wide variety of robotic manipulation skills specified solely by natural language, such as "open the drawer...now pick up the block...now press the green button." This would reduce the cost of language annotation, enhance the utility of large existing demonstration datasets, and improve the robustness of instruction following.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed **LangLfP (Language Conditioned Learning from Play)**, integrates free-form natural language conditioning with multitask imitation learning, leveraging unstructured data. The approach is structured around a four-step process:

1. **Collection of Unstructured "Play" Data**: A large dataset of teleoperated "play" demonstrations is collected. This data is "unstructured" in that demonstrators are not constrained to predefined tasks and engage in various object manipulations within the environment. Crucially, this initial data lacks any explicit task or language labels.

2. **Data Relabeling and Language Pairing**:
 - **Hindsight Goal Image Relabeling**: The unstructured play data is relabeled into hindsight goal image examples (Algorithm 2). For each short temporal window within a demonstration trajectory, the final observation (image) in that window is treated as the "goal image" to be reached, and the preceding states and actions are considered optimal behavior for reaching it. This creates a dataset `D_play` of (trajectory, goal image) pairs.
 - **Hindsight Language Instruction Pairing**: A small subset (less than 1%) of the windows from `D_play` is then additionally paired with hindsight natural language instructions (Algorithm 3). Human annotators are presented with video clips of robot play and asked to provide a free-form natural language instruction that describes how to get from the start to the end of the clip. This yields a new dataset `D_(play,lang)` of (trajectory, language instruction) pairs.

3. **Multicontext Imitation Learning (MCIL)**: This is a core component that enables training a single policy over heterogeneous task descriptions.
 - **Shared Latent Goal Space**: MCIL trains a single latent goal-conditioned policy, `π_θ(a|s, z)`, where `z` is a vector in a shared latent goal space.
 - **Context Encoders**: Separate parameterized encoders (`f_k_θ`) are learned for each context type. For `D_play`, an image encoder (`g_enc`) maps goal images (`s_g`) to `z`. For `D_(play,lang)`, a language encoder (`s_enc`) maps natural language instructions (`l`) to `z`.
 - **End-to-End Training**: During each training step, batches of examples are sampled from *both* `D_play` and `D_(play,lang)`. Their respective contexts (goal images or language instructions) are encoded into the shared latent goal space `z`. A maximum likelihood imitation loss is computed, averaged over both datasets. The policy (`π_θ`) and all context encoders (`g_enc`, `s_enc`) are trained end-to-end to maximize this objective. This allows the system to learn the majority of perception and control from the abundant, cheaply collected `D_play` data, while simultaneously learning language grounding from the much smaller `D_(play,lang)`.

4. **Test Time Language Conditioning**: At test time, the trained policy `π_θ(a|s, l)` operates solely on real-time pixel observations and free-form natural language instructions provided by a human operator. The language instruction is encoded into the latent goal space `z` using `s_enc`, and the policy then executes continuous control actions in a closed loop.

**Network Architecture Details:**
- **Perception Module**: Maps raw RGB images (200x200x3) through a series of convolutional layers and spatial softmax, followed by a fully connected layer to produce a 64-dimensional visual embedding. This is concatenated with 8-DOF proprioception data, resulting in a 72-dimensional perceptual embedding.
- **Image Goal Encoder (`g_enc`)**: Reuses the perception module to obtain an image embedding, which is then processed by a 2-layer ReLU MLP to produce the latent goal `z`.
- **Language Understanding Module (`s_enc`)**:
 - **LangLfP (from scratch)**: Employs subword tokenization, learned 8-dimensional subword embeddings, average pooling across the sequence of embeddings, and a 2-layer ReLU MLP to output a 32-dimensional latent goal `z`.
 - **TransferLangLfP (with pretrained models)**: Utilizes a pretrained multilingual neural language model (Multilingual Universal Sentence Encoder - MUSE) to map sentences directly to a 512-dimensional vector. This vector is then passed through a 2-layer ReLU MLP to the latent goal space `z`. The weights of the pretrained MUSE model are not fine-tuned.
- **Control Module**: The policy `π_θ` is implemented using the Latent Motor Plans (LMP) architecture, a sequence-to-sequence Conditional Variational Autoencoder (CVAE). This network learns an abstract plan space (`z_p`) in addition to the goal space, allowing it to model multimodal behavior. The LMP is adapted to "multicontext LMP" by replacing image-specific goal conditioning with the shared latent goal `z` from either the image or language encoders.

**Evaluation Environment**: Experiments are conducted in a simulated 3D tabletop environment ("Playroom") featuring an 8-DOF robotic arm. Tasks include opening/closing doors and drawers, pressing buttons, and manipulating a block. Evaluations use multi-stage tasks (Chain-2, Chain-3, Chain-4) composed of sequences of 18 distinct subtasks, and compare performance under both pixel and simulator state observations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted in the simulated 3D Playroom environment yielded several key findings regarding the proposed LangLfP methodology and its variations:

- **End-to-End Language Conditioned Manipulation (Q0)**: LangLfP successfully demonstrated end-to-end learning of perception, natural language understanding, and continuous control. The policy achieved 68.6% success on individual free-form instructions covering 18 distinct tasks and 52.1% success on sequences of four-stage tasks (Chain-4 benchmark), comprising 925 long-horizon tasks. This indicates the method's ability to follow complex human instructions.

- **Performance Equivalence to Goal Image Conditioning (Q1)**: LangLfP's performance was comparable to or slightly exceeded that of prior goal image-conditioned imitation learning (LfP) across all benchmarks. This is significant because LangLfP achieves this level of performance with less than 1% of the total demonstration data requiring language annotation, demonstrating that language conditioning can be as effective as image conditioning while substantially reducing annotation costs.

- **Impact of Large Unlabeled Datasets (Q2)**: LangLfP consistently outperformed LangBC (a baseline trained only on predefined, labeled demonstrations) on every benchmark. This result highlights that leveraging large, unstructured, and unlabeled demonstration data through the Multicontext Imitation Learning (MCIL) framework significantly improves language-conditioned performance. This benefit was observed even when the unlabeled training data was restricted to the same size as the labeled datasets (Restricted LangLfP vs. LangBC), suggesting that the diversity of unstructured data is better utilized. Qualitatively, MCIL-trained policies showed better transitions between tasks and recovery from failures compared to baselines.

- **Benefit of Pretrained Language Models (Q3)**: TransferLangLfP, which incorporates pretrained language embeddings, systematically outperformed both LangLfP (trained language from scratch) and LfP. This provides evidence that sentence embeddings from large pretrained language models can enhance the convergence and performance of language-guided robotic control policies.

- **Robustness to Out-of-Distribution Synonym Instructions (Q4)**: TransferLangLfP demonstrated enhanced robustness to out-of-distribution synonym instructions (OOD-syn benchmark), achieving 60.2% success compared to LangLfP's 37.6%. Furthermore, by utilizing a multilingual pretrained model, TransferLangLfP extended this robustness to 16 different languages (OOD-16-lang benchmark), achieving 56.0% success compared to LangLfP's 27.94%, without requiring new demonstrations for each synonym or language. This indicates that pretrained models effectively broaden the range of instructions an agent can follow at test time.

- **Scalability with Model Capacity**: The study observed that performance for models leveraging unstructured data (LangLfP) scaled linearly with increased model capacity, whereas performance for models trained on conventional predefined demonstrations (LangBC) peaked and then declined. This suggests that the diversity inherent in unstructured play data is effectively utilized by larger capacity models, supporting a strategy of collecting large unstructured datasets and increasing model size.

- **Human Assistance and Task Composition**: Natural language conditioning enabled new modes of interactive human assistance, allowing operators to provide corrective guidance (e.g., "move back") when the robot encountered difficulties. Additionally, LangLfP demonstrated the ability to compose novel tasks, not explicitly part of the 18-task benchmark (e.g., "put object in trash"), by breaking them into sequences of two natural language instructions.

- **Neutral Starting Conditions Impact**: LangLfP showed robustness to fixed neutral starting positions during multitask evaluation, unlike LangBC, which experienced significant performance degradation when initial states were not correlated with the commanded task. This highlights the generalizability derived from learning over diverse unstructured data.

- **Language Data Quantity vs. Modality**: For models receiving simulator state inputs, increasing the amount of language pairs from 5k to 10k had only marginal benefits. However, for models using pixel inputs, performance continued to improve, suggesting that larger language datasets are more crucial for addressing the complexities of perceptual grounding from raw visual data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses several fundamental challenges in robotic learning, offering significant implications for the development of more capable and user-friendly robots.

The primary significance lies in its contribution to **scalable human-robot interaction**. By enabling robots to follow free-form natural language instructions, the work moves beyond restrictive task specification methods like goal images or numerical task IDs. This makes it possible for untrained human users to intuitively command robots, expanding the potential for robot deployment in diverse, unstructured environments where dynamic and flexible task specification is required.

A key impact is the **dramatic reduction in language annotation costs**. The Multicontext Imitation Learning (MCIL) framework effectively leverages large volumes of readily available, unstructured, and *unlabeled* demonstration data. This reduces the need for expensive language annotations to a minimal fraction (less than 1%) of the total training data, making the approach significantly more practical and cost-effective for real-world application. This efficiency is crucial for scaling robotic capabilities across a wider range of tasks.

The work also demonstrates **enhanced robustness and generalization capabilities**. By combining unstructured play data with pretrained neural language models, the resulting policies are more resilient to linguistic variations, including out-of-distribution synonyms and instructions in multiple languages not explicitly seen during training. This generalization property is vital for robots to operate reliably in dynamic human environments where instructions may vary widely. The finding that performance scales linearly with model capacity when utilizing unstructured data suggests a clear path for future performance improvements through larger models and more diverse data collection.

Furthermore, the research establishes a method for **end-to-end learning of perception, language understanding, and continuous control** for complex robotic manipulation. This holistic approach avoids reliance on handcrafted features or separate, pre-trained modules, potentially leading to more tightly integrated and adaptable robot behaviors.

The capability for **interactive human assistance and task composition** at test time is another notable impact. Natural language allows humans to provide real-time guidance or corrective feedback when a robot encounters unforeseen situations, enabling more collaborative and resilient task execution. The ability to compose novel tasks from existing skills through language further enhances the flexibility and utility of the robot.

In summary, this research offers a pathway toward more generalist and adaptable robots by addressing critical data and interaction bottlenecks in imitation learning. It lays foundational work for future systems that could integrate autonomous policy improvement and generalize across vastly different environments and objects, moving closer to robots that can operate effectively and intuitively alongside humans.
