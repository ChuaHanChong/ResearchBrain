# No More Marching: Learning Humanoid Locomotion for Short-Range SE(2) Targets

> [!summary] Summary
> An end-to-end reinforcement learning framework enables humanoid robots to efficiently reach short-range SE(2) target poses using a novel constellation reward function that unifies translational and rotational objectives. The resulting GoTo controller achieved up to 44% faster task completion and 37% less energy consumption in simulation, demonstrating robust sim-to-real transfer to a physical Digit v3 robot.

## Problem

- Existing velocity-tracking or model-based locomotion methods are inefficient for short-range SE(2) target tasks, leading to unnatural "marching-style" behaviors.
- Designing reward functions to simultaneously optimize for both translational and rotational goals in a coordinated, natural manner is challenging.
- Traditional control architectures often rely on rigid, phase-separated planning that limits adaptiveness and produces less fluid movements.

## Method

- The research formulates short-range SE(2) locomotion as a goal-conditioned ==Markov Decision Process== solved with an end-to-end reinforcement learning approach.
- A novel "constellation reward" is introduced, which geometrically couples translational and rotational errors, intuitively balancing these objectives to promote coordinated motion.
- The GoTo controller, a two-layer LSTM recurrent neural network, is trained using PPO with extensive domain randomization in simulation to facilitate sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- The GoTo controller demonstrated superior performance in simulation, consuming **37%** less energy, reaching targets **44%** faster, and using **33%** fewer footsteps compared to baselines.
- Qualitative analysis revealed GoTo generating natural, fluid movements with seamlessly integrated turns and translations, avoiding the rigid, two-phase behaviors of other controllers.
- Deployed on a physical Digit v3 robot, the controller achieved a **90.23%** success rate, exhibiting robust sim-to-real transfer and stable locomotion without additional fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A geometrically coupled reward function effectively addresses the challenge of simultaneously optimizing for translation and rotation, promoting coordinated movements without extensive tuning.
> - End-to-end reinforcement learning can generate fluid, human-like short-range locomotion, outperforming traditional methods that often result in rigid, marching-style behaviors.
> - Extensive domain randomization during training is crucial for achieving robust sim-to-real transfer of complex locomotion policies to physical humanoid hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robot locomotion can be broadly categorized into long-range and short-range movements. Long-range locomotion typically involves maintaining stable, rhythmic gaits over extended distances, often guided by velocity commands. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has achieved notable successes in this domain, developing state-of-the-art velocity-tracking controllers for various humanoid and quadruped platforms. These methods often rely on reward functions that promote forward velocity, gait stability, and symmetry.

In contrast, short-range locomotion focuses on task-driven movements to specific SE(2) target poses (position and orientation). This mode is crucial for many practical applications, such as moving objects between nearby locations or navigating confined workspaces. The inherent characteristics of short-range tasks—prioritizing rapid, adaptive transitions and precision over rhythmic footstep generation—make traditional velocity-tracking controllers inefficient. When applied to short-range SE(2) goals, these controllers often result in "marching-style" behaviors, which are neither energy-efficient nor natural for reaching nearby, specific poses.

Existing approaches to humanoid navigation largely fall into two categories:
- **Model-Based Control:** These methods decompose navigation into high-level path planning and local footstep planning. Examples include systems that leverage algorithms for 2D planning and footstep generation, with extensions for obstacle avoidance and 3D footstep planning. While capable of generating feasible footstep sequences, these systems require complex layers for execution, including inverse kinematics, trajectory generation, and low-level controllers for balance and tracking. This modularity can lead to reduced adaptiveness and increased system overhead.
- **Learning-Based Control:** Beyond basic forward locomotion, some learning-based approaches adopt hierarchical architectures. A high-level policy predicts target velocities, which are then fed to a pre-trained low-level locomotion controller. While effective in structured environments, these methods also tend to produce marching-style behaviors, suboptimal for short-range, precise tasks. Imitation learning from motion capture data has advanced "naturalness" in simulated humanoids but often struggles with sim-to-real transfer, particularly for robots with non-human morphologies, and relies on extensive, clean datasets and complex retargeting pipelines.

This paper positions itself by addressing the gap in efficient, natural short-range SE(2)-target locomotion for humanoid robots. It departs from both traditional model-based planning and velocity-tracking RL methods by proposing an end-to-end learning framework that directly optimizes for pose-reaching, rather than velocity-tracking. A central element of this work is the development of a novel reward function specifically designed to unify translational and rotational objectives, aiming to overcome the inefficiencies and unnatural movements observed when applying existing methods to short-range SE(2) tasks. The work seeks to achieve robust sim-to-real transfer without relying on motion priors or complex hierarchical setups.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a reinforcement learning approach that enables humanoid robots to execute fast, robust, and energy-efficient short-range movements to specified SE(2) target poses. This involves learning an end-to-end locomotion policy, referred to as the "GoTo controller," that directly optimizes for pose-reaching performance.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and limitations observed in the broader research landscape:
- **Inefficiency of Existing Locomotion Methods for Short-Range Tasks:** Current state-of-the-art learning-based locomotion largely focuses on velocity-tracking for long-range travel. When applied to short-range SE(2) targets, these methods often result in inefficient, "marching-style" behaviors. Such behavior is suboptimal for tasks requiring precise, rapid, and adaptive transitions in confined spaces, such as repeatedly moving items or navigating around obstacles.
- **Limitations of Traditional Control Architectures:** Model-based and hierarchical learning approaches often decompose navigation into separate phases or sub-problems (e.g., orient-then-move, footstep planning, trajectory generation). This decomposition adds system complexity, can reduce adaptiveness, and may lead to rigid, unnatural movements that do not seamlessly integrate translation and rotation. The desire is to eliminate the need for explicit footstep planning or trajectory stitching.
- **Challenges in Reward Function Design for SE(2) Targets:** Optimizing for both target position and orientation simultaneously presents a significant challenge in reinforcement learning. Naive reward formulations that treat translation and rotation independently or with rigid weighting can lead to unbalanced behaviors, where the robot might prioritize position before orientation (or vice versa), resulting in inefficient "stop-and-turn" motions. There is a need for a reward function that intuitively and effectively couples these dual objectives to encourage coordinated and natural movements.
- **Practical Applicability in Real-World Workflows:** Short-range SE(2) movements are fundamental to many practical tasks humanoids are expected to perform in real-world environments. Developing a system that can execute these transitions efficiently and naturally is crucial for extending the utility and operational capabilities of humanoid robots.
- **Sim-to-Real Transferability:** For practical deployment, learned policies must transfer effectively from simulation to physical hardware. The design of the learning framework, including the reward function and training methodology, must support this transfer without extensive fine-tuning.

By addressing these motivations, the authors aim to develop a more adaptive, efficient, and natural locomotion capability for humanoid robots, specifically tailored for common, task-driven short-range movements.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research formulates the problem of short-range SE(2)-target locomotion as a goal-conditioned Markov Decision Process (MDP). The state space (S) includes proprioceptive measurements (joint positions, velocities) and the robot's current SE(2) pose. The goal space (G) consists of target SE(2) poses (x, y, θ). Actions (A) are joint position commands. The objective is to learn a goal-conditioned policy that generates efficient and natural locomotion.

The methodology focuses on an end-to-end reinforcement learning approach, with a primary contribution being a novel reward function and a robust training framework to facilitate sim-to-real transfer.

**A. GoTo Reward Formulation**
The total reward at each timestep is a sum of three components:
`r = r_con + r_sty + r_reg`

1. **Constellation Reward (r_con):** This is the core novel component designed to address the challenges of simultaneously optimizing for translation and rotation.
 - **Motivation:** Sparse rewards are impractical, and independently weighting position and orientation rewards often leads to tuning difficulties and unnatural "stop-and-turn" behaviors.
 - **Mechanism:** The robot's current SE(2) pose and the target pose are represented by two corresponding "constellations" of *N* landmark points rigidly attached to the robot's base. The constellation distance (`d_con`) is defined as the mean squared distance between corresponding points in the current and target constellations.
 - **Coupling:** This distance naturally couples position and orientation errors. The balance between these components is governed by the constellation's geometry. For instance, larger constellations increase sensitivity to orientation errors.
 - **Decomposition:** The constellation distance can be intuitively decomposed into `d_con = d_p + I_c * d_o`, where `d_p` is the squared translational error, `d_o` is the squared rotational error, and `I_c` is the planar moment of inertia of the constellation. `I_c` acts as a scaling factor, intuitively balancing the influence of translational and rotational accuracy.
 - **Reward Transformation:** The constellation distance is converted into a reward using a negative exponential transformation: `r_con = e^(-w_c * d_con)`. This results in a multiplicative coupling between translation and rotation, promoting coordinated error reduction, in contrast to additive reward structures used in prior work.
 - **Practical Implementation:** A circular constellation with a radius of `r = 1m` was used, resulting in `I_c = 1`. A scaling factor `w_c = 0.2` was selected.

2. **Style Reward (r_sty):** This component is adopted from prior work and encourages natural bipedal gaits and body postures. It includes terms for base height, foot airtime, foot orientation, foot placement, and arm positioning.

3. **Regularization Reward (r_reg):** Also adopted from prior work, this promotes energy efficiency, motion smoothness, and hardware compatibility. It penalizes large action changes, high torque magnitudes, and excessive base accelerations.

**B. Controller Architecture**
The GoTo controller is implemented as a two-layer Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) recurrent neural network with (128, 128) hidden units.
- **Input:** At each timestep, the network receives the current robot state (joint positions, joint velocities, root orientation, and root angular velocities) and the delta commands (∆x, ∆y, ∆θ) representing the difference between the robot's current SE(2) pose and the target pose.
- **Output:** The network outputs joint-space PD setpoints for all 20 actuators.
- **Operating Frequencies:** The GoTo controller operates at 50Hz, with the underlying PD controllers running at 1000Hz.

**C. Simulation-Based Training**
- **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used for training, augmented with a mirror loss to encourage symmetric policy behavior.
- **Simulator:** Training is performed using the NVIDIA IsaacLab physics simulator with the Agility Digit-V3 robot model.
- **Episode Generation:** Each episode involves 4096 robot instances. Robots are initialized on a flat plane in a nominal standing posture with randomized SE(2) positions and orientations and small joint angle perturbations.
- **Target Distribution (ρ_g):** Target poses are sampled such that ∆x ∈ [−2, 2] m, ∆y ∈ [−1.5, 1.5] m, and ∆θ ∈ [−π, π] rad. New goal commands are resampled every 4 to 8 seconds, enabling the robot to learn to react to dynamic commands. Goals are drawn from five categories: stand-in-place, straight translation, lateral translation, turn-in-place, and combined rotation and translation.
- **Episode Termination:** Episodes terminate if the robot's base height falls below 0.4m or after 8 seconds.
- **Domain Randomization:** Extensive domain randomization is applied to physics parameters (joint stiffness, body masses, center-of-mass locations, encoder noise, observation noise, torque delays, and PD gains randomized by 10%) and foot friction parameters to enhance sim-to-real transferability.

**D. Benchmarking Framework**
To evaluate controller performance, a set of five metrics is defined:
1. **Position Error:** Euclidean distance between final and target (x, y) positions.
2. **Orientation Error:** Absolute angular difference between final and target θ, accounting for circularity.
3. **Time to Target:** Total time until the robot reaches the target pose within predefined error thresholds (e.g., position < 5 cm, orientation < 0.1 rad) and its velocities are near zero.
4. **Footstep Count:** Number of distinct foot placements during the trajectory, serving as a proxy for motion efficiency.
5. **Energy Efficiency:** Total mechanical energy expended (integral of joint power over time), normalized by distance traveled.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation involved comparing the proposed GoTo controller against three baseline approaches: a Finite State Machine (FSM), two configurations of a manufacturer-provided Agility model-based controller (Agility-3 and Agility-2), and a Hierarchical Controller. Evaluation was conducted in simulation and then validated on a physical Digit v3 humanoid platform.

**A. Quantitative Simulation Results**
Experiments were run across a grid of target distances ({0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 4.0} m) and approach angles/orientations, with 16 trials per command to account for randomized initial poses.
- **Overall Performance (Table I):** The GoTo controller demonstrated superior performance across all evaluated metrics when normalized against the Agility-2 controller (set to 1.0).
 - **Energy Consumption:** GoTo consumed 37% less energy (0.63 ± 0.19 normalized value).
 - **Time to Target:** GoTo reached targets 44% faster (0.56 ± 0.09 s normalized value).
 - **Footstep Count:** GoTo utilized 33% fewer footsteps (0.67 ± 0.15 normalized value).
 - **Position Error:** GoTo achieved 40% better positional accuracy (0.6 ± 0.37 cm normalized value).
 - **Orientation Error:** GoTo achieved 58% better orientation accuracy (0.42 ± 0.31 rad normalized value).
 - GoTo also exhibited lower standard deviations across all metrics, indicating higher reliability and consistency.
- **Baseline Performance:**
 - The FSM, with its rigid "orient-then-move" strategy, performed poorly across most metrics, highlighting the limitations of phase-separated control.
 - The Agility-3 controller, the default manufacturer controller, significantly underperformed Agility-2, demonstrating sensitivity to design choices.
 - The Hierarchical controller showed moderate improvements in efficiency over FSM and Agility-3 but had higher position errors compared to Agility-2 and GoTo.
- **Performance Across Command Difficulty (Figure 3):** The GoTo controller consistently outperformed baselines across all levels of command difficulty (measured by the time Agility-2 required to complete the task). The relative performance improvement of GoTo over baselines tended to increase with more challenging commands (those combining longer distances with larger orientation changes), indicating its effectiveness in complex scenarios.

**B. Qualitative Footstep Results (Figure 5)**
Analysis of footstep patterns for a 4m translation and 90-degree rotation target revealed qualitative differences:
- **Baselines:** FSM and Agility controllers employed rigid, two-phase strategies (e.g., aligning, then sidestep marching). The Hierarchical controller also exhibited marching behavior, with orientation optimization sometimes activating mid-trajectory. These approaches generally treated rotation and translation as distinct phases.
- **GoTo Controller:** The GoTo controller generated natural, fluid movements with fewer footsteps. It seamlessly integrated directional changes into forward motion, avoiding mechanical marching or discrete rotation phases. This implied a coordinated reduction in both position and orientation error, leading to more human-like locomotion.

**C. Real-World Results (Figure 4)**
The trained GoTo controller was deployed on a physical Digit v3 humanoid robot for 72 distinct short-range SE(2) navigation tasks.
- **Success Rate:** The controller achieved a 90.23% success rate in the real world, compared to 100% in simulation. Failures primarily involved loss of balance during challenging commands.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Performance metrics (time, energy, footstep count) in the real world closely mirrored simulation trends across increasing command difficulty. While real-world values were slightly higher, the consistent patterns and minimal performance gap validated successful sim-to-real transfer.
- **Hardware Robustness:** Despite challenges inherent in transferring policies to physical hardware (noise, delays, unmodeled dynamics), the controller produced stable and purposeful locomotion without additional fine-tuning, confirming the practical applicability of the approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a notable advancement in humanoid locomotion control, particularly for short-range, task-driven movements, which are frequent in real-world applications.

**Advancement in Locomotion Control:** The development of an end-to-end reinforcement learning approach for direct SE(2) target reaching represents a significant departure from traditional velocity-tracking or model-based planning paradigms. This method addresses the specific inefficiencies of applying long-range locomotion strategies to short-range tasks, which typically result in unnatural "marching-style" behaviors.

**Innovative Reward Function Design:** The introduction of the constellation reward is a key contribution. This unified geometric objective intuitively balances translational and rotational goals without requiring extensive hand-tuning of relative weights, which is a common difficulty in multi-objective reward design. By coupling these objectives multiplicatively, the constellation reward promotes coordinated and natural movements, overcoming the "stop-and-turn" issues often seen with independently weighted rewards. This concept may be applicable to other complex pose-reaching tasks in robotics.

**Enhanced Efficiency and Naturalness:** The GoTo controller significantly outperforms baseline methods in terms of energy consumption, time-to-target, and footstep count, while also achieving higher accuracy. The qualitative results demonstrate that the learned policies generate more fluid and human-like motions, integrating turning and translation seamlessly. These improvements translate directly into more agile, capable, and practical humanoid robots, extending their operational time and improving task completion speed in dynamic environments.

**Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful deployment of the GoTo controller on a physical Digit v3 humanoid, with performance trends closely mirroring simulation, validates the robustness and practical viability of the approach. The extensive domain randomization employed during training proved effective in bridging the sim-to-real gap, indicating that the learned policies are resilient to real-world uncertainties without requiring additional fine-tuning. This is critical for moving research outcomes into practical applications.

**Improved Benchmarking:** The proposed benchmarking framework, which quantifies locomotion performance using interpretable metrics such as energy consumption, time-to-target, and footstep count, contributes to more rigorous and standardized evaluation in the field of humanoid locomotion.

**Future Impact:** This work lays a foundation for future advancements in goal-directed humanoid control. By demonstrating effective end-to-end learning for SE(2) targets, it opens avenues for exploring more complex task objectives, such as whole-body pose goals and integration with end-effector constraints for combined locomotion and manipulation. This could lead to humanoids that can more seamlessly perform intricate tasks in varied and constrained environments, enhancing their autonomy and utility in areas like logistics, healthcare, and human-robot collaboration.
