# GPUSimBench: Towards Scalable and Reliable GPU-Accelerated Simulators in Embodied AI

> [!summary] Summary
> GPUSimBench is a benchmark suite that stress-tests seven GPU-accelerated robotic simulators on three axes: parallel scalability (throughput, GPU memory versus environment count), physical consistency against a real inclined-plane ball-and-cube-array apparatus via Earth Mover's Distance, and execution determinism measured as intra-run parallel variability and inter-run run-to-run variability. It reveals four empirical stochasticity regimes and derives simulator-selection guidelines.

## Problem

- Existing robotics benchmarks measure task-level outcomes like success rate or convergence speed, not the simulator engine's own scalability, fidelity, and determinism properties.
- Massively parallel simulators are treated as infinite data generators, leaving trade-offs between throughput, physical fidelity, and execution determinism largely unexamined.
- Determinism assumed in sequential simulators breaks under GPU-batched execution, yet no standardized protocol quantifies the resulting numerical and scheduling non-determinism.

## Method

- ==Inclined-collision protocol==: a steel ball rolls down a lab-measured 20-degree wooden slope into a 3x3x3 cube array, with cube positions sampled at five seconds and matched to real trials.
- Assignment-based planar ==Earth Mover's Distance== defines three metrics: sim-to-real physical alignment, parallel variability across environments in one run, and run-to-run variability across ten repeated runs.
- Scalability sweep measures frames-per-second and peak GPU memory increment over baseline for free-falling cube arrays and a randomly actuated Franka arm across doubling environment counts.

## Results

- **ManiSkill** achieves the best sim-to-real alignment at **2.520 cm** EMD, followed by Playground at 3.380 cm and MJX at 3.970 cm; Madrona's XPBD friction failure yields **211.60 cm**.
- Madrona reaches the highest free-fall parallelism at 2^18 environments and **481k** frames per second, while Genesis peaks on the articulated Franka scene at 2^17 environments and **11.1M** frames per second.
- With seeds fixed and randomization disabled, Isaac Lab and ManiSkill still show **4.21 cm** and **4.76 cm** parallel variability; Madrona reaches **32.53 cm** parallel and **11.47 cm** run-to-run variability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fixing random seeds does not guarantee reproducibility on GPU simulators; atomics, reduction order, and solver-path divergence inject variability that compounds through contact-rich rollouts.
> - Parallel and run-to-run variability are independent failure modes, so a simulator can be reproducible across runs while its parallel environments silently diverge.
> - Pick simulators by workflow, not one metric: repeated-run tuning demands low run-to-run drift, whereas single-run evaluation tolerates intra-run spread.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in embodied Artificial Intelligence (AI) have increasingly leveraged massively parallel simulation for scaling training processes, with GPU-accelerated robotic simulators forming a critical data infrastructure. Platforms such as Isaac Lab and Genesis allow for the simultaneous simulation of a large number of environments, thereby enabling the generation of extensive robotic data and facilitating the scaling of robot learning and policy training.

However, the rapid increase in computational throughput offered by these simulators has introduced complexities that remain largely unexamined. In traditional sequential simulators, computational determinism and reliable physical contact modeling are often foundational assumptions. In contrast, the highly concurrent nature of GPU-batched execution can introduce numerical variations, parallel synchronization artifacts, and floating-point non-determinism. This lack of understanding regarding these underlying trade-offs between parallel efficiency, physical fidelity, and execution determinism hinders the development of reliable and reproducible robot learning policies.

Existing benchmarks predominantly focus on task-level performance, such as success rates or convergence speed, or on general parallel processing capabilities. They typically do not provide a standardized evaluation of how physical consistency might degrade with increased parallelization or how GPU-parallelization specifically affects fundamental simulation performance and reproducibility. GPUSimBench addresses this gap by providing a comprehensive framework to systematically evaluate these critical, yet often overlooked, properties of GPU-accelerated simulators, moving beyond task-specific performance metrics to analyze the core behavior of the simulation engines themselves.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to expose the hidden limitations of mainstream GPU-based robotic simulators by introducing GPUSimBench, a benchmark focused on scalability, physical consistency, and computational determinism. The motivation for this work stems from several challenges observed in the current landscape of embodied AI research:

1. **Unexamined Trade-offs in Massively Parallel Simulation:** While GPU-accelerated simulators enable unprecedented computational throughput, the intricate trade-offs between parallel efficiency, the accuracy of physical models (fidelity), and the consistency of simulation outcomes (determinism) have not been rigorously evaluated. Developers and researchers often treat these simulators as infinite data generators without fully understanding the underlying complexities.
2. **Impact of GPU-Batched Execution on Determinism:** The highly concurrent nature of modern GPU architectures, especially in batched execution, introduces subtle numerical variations. These can include floating-point non-determinism, variations due to parallel synchronization, and differences in execution order. Such effects can lead to divergent simulation outcomes even under identical initial conditions, compromising the reproducibility of robot learning experiments.
3. **Hindrance to Reliable Robot Learning:** Without a comprehensive understanding of these numerical and physical inconsistencies, developing reliable and reproducible robot learning policies becomes challenging. The lack of guarantees regarding simulation determinism and physical fidelity can lead to inconsistent training results and difficulties in transferring policies from simulation to real-world robots.
4. **Deficiencies in Existing Benchmarks:** Current benchmarks for robotic simulators primarily focus on task-specific performance or overall parallel capabilities. They often overlook fundamental properties of the simulation engine, such as how physical consistency evolves with increased parallelization or how GPU acceleration influences core simulation performance. A standardized evaluation method for these aspects was needed.

To address these motivations, the authors established the following key objectives:

- To identify and quantify critical, underexamined limitations of mainstream GPU-based simulators, particularly regarding throughput, physical fidelity, and execution non-determinism.
- To develop and introduce GPUSimBench, a unified benchmarking suite designed for systematic evaluation of scalability, sim-to-real physical consistency, and stochasticity in massively parallel robotic simulations.
- To conduct controlled cross-simulator and sim-to-real experiments to quantify GPU-batched non-determinism, categorize empirical stochasticity regimes, and provide practical guidance for simulator selection in robot learning applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces GPUSimBench, a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating GPU-accelerated parallel robotic simulators. The methodology focuses on measuring parallel scalability, resource usage, and distribution-level physical fidelity.

**A. Simulators and Hardware:**
Seven mainstream GPU-accelerated parallel simulators were evaluated: ManiSkill (v3.0.0b22), Genesis (v0.3.11), Madrona, MujocoWarp, MJX (MuJoCo v3.4.0), MujocoPlayground (v0.1.0), and IsaacLab (v2.2.1). Simulators primarily relying on CPU-parallel execution or partial GPU acceleration (e.g., Gazebo, PyBullet) were excluded. All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with a 13th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i5-13400F CPU, 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5070 GPU with 12 GB memory.

**B. Evaluation Metrics:**
The following metrics were defined and utilized:

- **Parallel Throughput (FPS):** Quantified as `FPS = (N_env * N_step) / T_step`, where `N_env` is the number of parallel environments, `N_step` is the number of physics steps, and `T_step` is the measured execution time. A warm-up phase was included before timing.
- **GPU Memory Usage:** Measured as the peak memory increment, `M_usage = M_peak - M_base`, where `M_base` is baseline usage before environment creation and `M_peak` is the maximum observed usage during stepping.
- **Distribution Heatmaps and Density Visualization:** For contact-rich tasks, the final 3D positions of objects were recorded at a fixed physical time `t_s`. These positions were projected onto the horizontal (xy) plane, and 2D density maps were generated using hexagonal bins and a logarithmic color mapping to visualize spatial distributions.
- **Distribution Consistency and Real-World Alignment (Earth Mover’s Distance - EMD):**
 - **Parallel Variability (Intra-run Consistency):** The average pairwise EMD (`W_parallel_1`) was computed between the planar point sets of cube positions from different parallel environments within a single simulator run. This metric assessed consistency under identical initial conditions.
 - **Real-World Alignment:** The EMD (`d_sim_EMD,phys`) was calculated between the aggregated point set from all simulated environments (`X_sim`) and the aggregated point set from real-world experimental trials (`X_real`). This quantified the agreement between simulated and real-world spatial distributions.
 - **Run-to-Run Variability (Inter-run Consistency):** The average pairwise EMD (`W_run-to-run_1`) was computed between aggregated distributions from multiple independent simulator runs under the same nominal configuration. This assessed the consistency of simulator outcomes across repeated experiments.

**C. Experimental Scenarios:**

1. **Parallel Capability:** This benchmark assessed throughput and memory usage across varying environment counts in two scenarios:
 - **Free-falling cube array:** A `3x3x3` array of cubes was released under gravity to fall and impact a flat ground plane. This scenario used simple objects to minimize external influences on parallel-capability measurements.
 - **Franka manipulator with random actions:** Each environment contained a Franka Panda robot, initialized identically, with random target joint positions sampled at each control step. Self-collision was enabled. This setup challenged articulated-body dynamics, constraint stabilization, and batching capabilities for complex mechanisms.
 - For both scenarios, rendering was disabled, and a unified time step `∆t = 0.01 s` was used. Throughput and GPU memory usage were measured over 1000 steps after 100 warm-up steps.

2. **Inclined Collision:** This experiment evaluated physical fidelity and stability in contact-rich dynamics:
 - **Rolling ball down an incline:** A steel ball rolled down an inclined plane, colliding with a `3x3x3` array of stacked wooden cubes. The final configuration of the cube array at `t_s = 5.0 s` was observed.
 - **Real-world platform:** A physical experiment platform was constructed to replicate the inclined collision scene. Key physical parameters (object sizes, masses, friction coefficients, restitution) were measured and used in the simulations.
 - For evaluation, `N_env = 16` parallel simulated environments were compared against `K = 16` independent real-world trials to assess distribution-level agreement. Random seeds were fixed, and task-level randomization was disabled to isolate numerical and execution-level variability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of GPU-accelerated simulators through GPUSimBench yielded detailed insights into their performance, physical consistency, and determinism.

**A. Parallel Capability:**
The throughput and GPU memory usage were assessed in two scenarios:

- **Free-falling cube array:** Madrona and Isaac Lab exhibited the highest throughput, reaching approximately 48.1 x 10^4 FPS and 47.2 x 10^4 FPS, respectively. Madrona also supported the largest number of parallel environments (2^18). GPU memory usage generally increased with environment count, with Madrona peaking at 9.5 GB and MJX at 10.6 GB.
- **Franka manipulator with random actions:** Genesis demonstrated the highest throughput, achieving approximately 11.1 x 10^6 FPS. MuJoCo Warp followed at 3.5 x 10^6 FPS. MJX and MuJoCo Warp supported the largest number of environments (2^19). Madrona did not support this scenario. Genesis showed efficient memory usage (6.4 GB at 2^17 environments), while MJX and MuJoCo Playground had higher footprints (10.6 GB and 10.3 GB at their maximum parallelism, respectively).

**B. Physical Consistency (Inclined Collision):**
This section assessed alignment with real-world results and internal consistency using the Earth Mover's Distance (EMD).

- **Real-World Alignment (d_sim_EMD,phys):** ManiSkill achieved the closest alignment to real-world outcomes with an EMD of 2.520 ± 0.000 cm. MuJoCo Playground (3.380 ± 0.110 cm) and MJX (3.970 ± 0.000 cm) also showed strong accuracy. Genesis was close at 4.780 ± 0.000 cm. Isaac Lab (5.400 ± 0.000 cm) and MuJoCo Warp (4.230 ± 0.740 cm) showed moderate deviations. Madrona exhibited the largest discrepancy (211.60 ± 2.22 cm), attributed to its XPBD solver's implementation in this setup leading to insufficient frictional effects.
- **Parallel Variability (Intra-run Consistency - W_parallel_1):** Genesis, MJX, and MuJoCo Playground showed no measurable Parallel Variability (0.00 ± 0.00 cm), indicating high consistency between parallel environments within a single run. In contrast, Isaac Lab (4.21 ± 0.00 cm), ManiSkill (4.76 ± 0.00 cm), Madrona (32.53 ± 1.86 cm), and MuJoCo Warp (4.55 ± 1.39 cm) exhibited measurable intra-run variability, where individual environments diverged despite identical initial conditions.
- **Run-to-Run Variability (Inter-run Consistency - W_run-to-run_1):** Isaac Lab, ManiSkill, Genesis, and MJX demonstrated no measurable Run-to-Run Variability (0.00 ± 0.00 cm) for their aggregate outcomes, suggesting that repeating the entire experiment yielded consistent overall distributions. However, Madrona (11.47 ± 1.40 cm), MuJoCo Warp (2.15 ± 0.82 cm), and MuJoCo Playground (1.39 ± 0.47 cm) displayed measurable inter-run variability, indicating that aggregate outcomes could differ across independent runs.

**C. Empirical Regimes of Stochasticity:**
Based on the presence or absence of Parallel and Run-to-Run Variability, four distinct regimes were identified:

- **Type 1 (Isaac Lab, ManiSkill):** Parallel Variability present, Run-to-Run Variability absent.
- **Type 2 (Genesis, MJX):** Both Parallel and Run-to-Run Variability absent. This represents the highest level of determinism observed.
- **Type 3 (Madrona, MuJoCo Warp):** Both Parallel and Run-to-Run Variability present. This indicates the least deterministic behavior.
- **Type 4 (MuJoCo Playground):** Parallel Variability absent, Run-to-Run Variability present.

The observed stochasticity, even with fixed random seeds, was attributed to the interplay of GPU-parallel computation models (e.g., SIMT dynamic scheduling, floating-point accumulation order in atomics/reductions), iterative solver structures, and the non-smooth nature of rigid-body contact dynamics. Small numerical perturbations accumulating over contact-rich rollouts were identified as a primary mechanism for distribution shifts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a systematic evaluation of GPU-accelerated robotic simulators, contributing to the field of embodied AI in several ways:

1. **Exposure of Underexamined Limitations:** The study reveals critical, often overlooked, trade-offs among throughput, physical fidelity, and execution non-determinism in mainstream GPU-based simulators. This highlights that raw performance metrics alone are insufficient for assessing simulator utility in high-stakes applications like robot learning.
2. **Establishment of a Standardized Benchmark:** GPUSimBench introduces a unified benchmarking suite and evaluation protocol. This provides a standardized framework for systematically evaluating fundamental simulator properties beyond task-level performance, which can guide future simulator development and comparisons.
3. **Quantification of GPU-Batched Non-Determinism:** The explicit quantification of Parallel and Run-to-Run Variability offers a concrete understanding of the inherent stochasticity introduced by GPU-batched execution. This finding is significant for robot learning, where reproducibility is crucial for model training and scientific validation.
4. **Informed Simulator Selection:** By categorizing simulators into distinct determinism regimes and providing a multi-axis comparison (Fig. 5), the research offers practical guidelines for selecting simulators based on specific research needs. This enables researchers to make informed decisions tailored to their priorities, such as sim-to-real transfer, requirements for high parallelism, memory constraints, or stringent reproducibility demands.
5. **Enhancement of Reproducibility in Robot Learning:** By identifying and characterizing the mechanisms behind numerical and execution-level stochasticity, this work contributes to understanding and potentially mitigating factors that compromise the reproducibility of robot learning experiments. This is a foundational step towards more reliable and robust robot policy development.
6. **Guidance for Future Simulator Development:** The detailed analysis of simulator characteristics and limitations provides valuable insights for developers aiming to build more scalable, physically reliable, and deterministic GPU simulation platforms. The identified limitations of GPUSimBench, such as the focus on a limited set of tasks and hardware, also suggest directions for future extensions, including deformable-body dynamics, fluid simulation, and perception/sensor rendering.

Overall, GPUSimBench serves as a foundational contribution by shifting the focus from mere computational speed to a more holistic understanding of simulator behavior, which is essential for advancing trustworthy and effective embodied AI research.
