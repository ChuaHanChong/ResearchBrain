# Function Vectors in Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Northeastern University identified "function vectors" (FVs) as compact, causal internal representations of input-output functions within autoregressive transformer language models. These FVs, extracted via causal mediation analysis, robustly trigger task execution in diverse contexts, including zero-shot and natural text settings, and can be algebraically composed.

## Problem

- The precise internal computational mechanisms underlying in-context learning (ICL) in large language models remained largely opaque.
- Prior interpretability research often focused on correlations, lacking methods to identify causal features of information flow during ICL.
- It was unclear if LLMs spontaneously developed abstract functional capabilities beyond simple semantic word arithmetic or explicit task prompting.

## Method

- Identified a small set of ==causal attention heads== through ==causal mediation analysis (CMA)== by measuring the ==Average Indirect Effect (AIE)== of individual head activations on task performance.
- Defined **function vectors (FVs)** as the sum of ==task-conditioned mean activations== of these identified causal attention heads.
- Developed methods to test FV portability and robustness by injecting them into ==hidden states== at specific layers in various prompt contexts (shuffled-label, zero-shot, natural text).

## Results

- Injecting function vectors into shuffled-label prompts significantly increased accuracy (e.g., from **39.1%** to **90.8%** on GPT-J) and enabled task performance in zero-shot settings (e.g., from **5.5%** to **57.5%** on GPT-J).
- Function vectors extracted from standard ICL contexts were robust and portable, effectively triggering task execution across different ICL templates, in zero-shot scenarios, and within natural language sentences.
- While FVs align with task output vocabulary when decoded, the knowledge of these decoded tokens alone is insufficient to reconstruct a working FV, indicating FVs carry additional, abstract functional information.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLMs spontaneously develop compact, causal internal representations of input-output functions (function vectors) distinct from simple semantic offsets or explicit vocabulary encodings.
> - Function vectors primarily exert their causal effect by triggering non-linear computations in the early-to-middle layers of the transformer network, rather than just by linearly manipulating final token probabilities.
> - These function vectors exhibit composable properties, allowing for algebraic manipulation to derive new functional behaviors, suggesting a structured internal representation of tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper makes a significant contribution to the burgeoning field of **mechanistic interpretability** of large language models, specifically focusing on the phenomenon of **in-context learning (ICL)**.

Historically, ICL, first widely observed in models like GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020), has been a perplexing emergent capability. While empirical studies have explored its external behavior (Min et al., 2022; Reynolds & McDonell, 2021) and theoretical frameworks have attempted to explain it (Garg et al., 2022; Dai et al., 2023; Von Oswald et al., 2023), the precise internal computations underlying ICL generalization remained largely opaque. This paper shifts the focus from *what* ICL does to *how* it does it at a mechanistic level.

The concept of "function vectors" connects to prior work on **task representations** in neural networks. Earlier research explored ways to *create* compositional task encodings, often through methods like metamappings, codebooks, or soft-prompts (e.g., Lampinen & McClelland, 2020; Shao et al., 2023; Mu et al., 2023; Ilharco et al., 2023). This paper distinguishes itself by demonstrating that compact, causal function representations *already exist* organically within *pre-trained* LLMs and can be *extracted*. This is a crucial advancement: it implies that LLMs, through their extensive training on diverse text corpora, spontaneously develop abstract functional capabilities akin to "function references" in programming (Church, 1936).

The methodology heavily relies on **causal mediation analysis (CMA)** (Pearl, 2001; Vig et al., 2020; Meng et al., 2022), a technique increasingly employed in mechanistic interpretability to identify components with causal roles in model behavior. This aligns with recent efforts to move beyond mere correlations to establish causal links within complex neural circuits (e.g., Wang et al., 2022a). By focusing on attention heads, the paper builds upon existing analyses of the **attention mechanism** (Vaswani et al., 2017), which have traditionally examined attention weights (Clark et al., 2019; Voita et al., 2019). However, the paper goes a step further by investigating the *content* of information transported by these heads, demonstrating that attention is not just about *where* the model looks, but *what* abstract information is being moved. It also touches upon the concept of "induction heads" (Elhage et al., 2021; Olsson et al., 2022), showing that some, but not all, of the identified causal heads exhibit induction-like behavior.

A significant aspect of this work is its direct engagement with the debate surrounding **semantic vector arithmetic** and the "logit lens" (Mikolov et al., 2013; Nostalgebraist, 2020; Geva et al., 2022; Dar et al., 2023). While these prior works suggest that many internal transformer calculations can be understood as operations on word embeddings or logit distributions, this paper *explicitly differentiates* function vectors from simple semantic offsets. It provides compelling evidence (e.g., via cyclic tasks like antonyms, and the non-linear activation pattern across layers) that FVs represent a distinct, higher-level abstract functional concept rather than just a linear manipulation of word probabilities.

Finally, this research contributes to the broader trend of **steering and controlling LLMs** at inference time through latent space interventions (Li et al., 2023a; Hernandez et al., 2023a; Turner et al., 2023; Zou et al., 2023). By identifying a causal vector representation for functions, this work offers a principled pathway for more precise and robust control over model behavior, moving beyond prompt engineering to direct manipulation of internal states. It is also noted that Hendel et al. (2023) conducted concurrent work observing similar causal effects of hidden states, reinforcing the timeliness and relevance of these findings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **uncover and characterize a fundamental neural mechanism within autoregressive transformer language models that represents input-output functions as compact vectors**. The authors term these representations "function vectors" (FVs).

The motivation for this investigation stems from several key areas:

- **Demystifying In-Context Learning (ICL):** Despite its powerful capabilities, the computational mechanisms underlying ICL remain largely unexplored. The authors aim to go beyond merely observing ICL's external behavior to understand how LLMs internally generalize and execute complex tasks from a few examples. They hypothesize that LLMs develop a rudimentary form of "function references" similar to those understood in computer science (e.g., lambda calculus), allowing them to carry and apply abstractions of tasks.
- **Identifying Causal Mechanisms:** Prior interpretability research often focuses on correlations. A core motivation is to move towards identifying *causal* features of information flow during ICL. This is achieved through the rigorous application of causal mediation analysis to pinpoint specific model components responsible for mediating ICL tasks.
- **Compact and Portable Task Representations:** The researchers are motivated to find representations that are not only causal but also compact and robust. They question whether these internal task representations are confined to specific ICL contexts or if they can be generalized and applied across diverse settings (e.g., zero-shot, natural text).
- **Distinguishing from Existing Explanations:** A significant motivation is to determine if function vectors are a novel phenomenon or if they can be reduced to previously observed behaviors like word embedding vector arithmetic. The paper is specifically designed to test this distinction, driven by the desire to understand the true complexity of LLM internal computations.
- **Enabling Greater Control and Interpretability:** By explicitly extracting and understanding these functional abstractions, the ultimate motivation is to enhance the transparency, auditability, and controllability of large language models. This research aims to provide a new lens through which to analyze how LLMs process and respond to task instructions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology is meticulously designed around **causal mediation analysis (CMA)** to identify and analyze function vectors. The approach can be broken down into several stages:

1. **Formulation and Task Definition:**
 - The authors use decoder-only autoregressive transformer language models, specifically GPT-J (6B), GPT-NeoX (20B), and various sizes of Llama 2 (7B, 13B, 70B).
 - A diverse set of over 40 ICL tasks is curated, covering various complexities and types (e.g., abstractive tasks like Antonym and English-French translation; extractive tasks like Named Entity Recognition). For analysis, a representative subset of 6 tasks is highlighted, with further exploration on additional tasks in appendices.
 - For each task `t`, a dataset `P_t` of "clean" 10-shot ICL prompts is created, where the model successfully predicts the correct answer (`y_iq`) for a given query (`x_iq`). "Uninformative" prompts (`~P_t`) are also created by shuffling labels, where the model is unlikely to succeed without intervention.
 - The internal workings are viewed through the lens of individual attention head outputs (`a_ℓj`), which are shown to be additive in the residual stream space (`h_ℓ`).

2. **Function Vector (FV) Extraction via Causal Mediation Analysis:**
 - **Task-conditioned Mean Activation (`¯a_t_ℓj`):** For each attention head `a_ℓj` and task `t`, the average output vector is computed by running the model on a set of clean ICL prompts from `P_t`. This captures the typical activation pattern of that head when performing task `t`.
 - **Causal Indirect Effect (CIE):** To assess the causal role of each head, an intervention is performed. The model is run on an uninformative shuffled-label prompt (`~p_t_i`). During this run, the activation of a specific attention head `a_ℓj` is *replaced* with its task-conditioned mean activation `¯a_t_ℓj`. The CIE is the difference in the probability of generating the correct answer `y_iq` with this intervention versus without it.
 - **Average Indirect Effect (AIE):** The CIE is averaged across all tasks (`T`) and corrupted prompts (`~P_t`) to identify attention heads that consistently play a significant causal role across diverse ICL tasks.
 - **Selection of Causal Heads (Set A):** The attention heads with the highest AIE are identified and grouped into a set `A`. The number of heads in `A` is scaled proportionally to model size (e.g., 10 for GPT-J, 100 for Llama 2 70B). These heads are primarily clustered in the early-middle layers of the network and tend to attend strongly to the output tokens of the in-context examples.
 - **Function Vector Definition (`v_t`):** The function vector for task `t` is then defined as the sum of the task-conditioned mean activations of all attention heads in the set `A`: `v_t = Σ_{a_ℓj ∈ A} ¯a_t_ℓj`.

3. **FV Evaluation and Portability Testing:**
 - **Intervention Mechanism:** To test the FV's causal effect, `v_t` is *added* to the hidden state (`h_ℓ`) at the last token position of a prompt, at a specific layer (`ℓ`, typically around `|L|/3`, where `L` is the total number of layers).
 - **Performance Metrics:** Top-1 accuracy in predicting the correct answer `y_iq` is measured.
 - **Contextual Robustness:** FVs are tested in various contexts:
 - **Shuffled-label ICL:** Adding `v_t` to a prompt with incorrect examples.
 - **Zero-shot:** Adding `v_t` to a query without any examples.
 - **Diverse Templates:** Using FVs extracted from one ICL template and applying them to prompts with different formatting.
 - **Natural Text Settings:** Injecting FVs into natural language prompts to see if they guide generation towards the task.
 - **Layer-wise Analysis:** FVs are injected at different layers to observe how their causal effect changes, providing insights into where the task execution is mediated.

4. **Internal Structure Investigation (Decoding FVs):**
 - **Logit Lens Application:** The function vector `v_t` is directly "decoded" using the model's output decoder (`D(v_t)`) to identify the top predicted vocabulary tokens. This assesses what explicit information the FV might carry in terms of word probabilities.
 - **Reconstruction Experiment:** To test if the *decoded vocabulary* alone is sufficient, new vectors (`v_hat_tk`) are constructed by optimizing to match the top `k` (e.g., 100 or all 50k) tokens of `D(v_t)`. The performance of these reconstructed vectors as FVs is then compared to the original `v_t`.

5. **Function Vector Algebra:**
 - **Composable Tasks:** A set of simple list-oriented ICL tasks (e.g., First-Copy, Last-Copy, First-Capital) are defined as building blocks.
 - **Algebraic Composition:** FVs for these base tasks (`v_AC`, `v_BC`, `v_AD`) are algebraically combined (e.g., `v*_BD = v_AD + v_BC - v_AC`) to hypothesize an FV for a new, complex task (e.g., Last-Capital).
 - **Evaluation:** The performance of these algebraically composed FVs is tested in zero-shot settings and compared against directly extracted FVs and standard ICL.

This comprehensive methodological framework allows the authors to systematically extract, evaluate, and characterize function vectors, providing strong evidence for their causal role and distinct properties within large language models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several compelling findings about function vectors (FVs) in large language models:

- **Existence and Causality of Function Vectors:** The core finding is the presence of a simple, compact neural mechanism, termed "function vectors," that causally represents input-output functions. Using causal mediation analysis, the authors identified a small number of attention heads (set `A`) that consistently mediate a diverse range of ICL tasks. The sum of the average outputs of these causal attention heads forms a robust function vector (`v_t`).
 - When `v_t` is added to uninformative (shuffled-label) ICL prompts or zero-shot contexts, it significantly triggers the execution of the target task. For instance, across 6 representative tasks, adding FVs to GPT-J increased accuracy from a baseline of 39.1% to 90.8% in shuffled-label settings and from 5.5% to 57.5% in zero-shot settings (Table 2). This demonstrates a strong causal effect.
 - The proposed FV extraction method (based on AIE) consistently outperforms simpler methods like averaging all hidden states at a given layer.

- **Layer Specificity of Causal Effects:** FVs exhibit maximal causal effect when injected into *early-to-middle layers* of the transformer network (typically around `|L|/3`), with a sharp reduction in effect in later layers (Figure 4). This pattern is consistent across different models (GPT-J, GPT-NeoX, Llama 2 variants) and tasks. This suggests that FVs do not simply act linearly on the final token probabilities but rather *trigger* downstream, non-linear computations performed by subsequent layers for task execution.

- **Robustness and Portability Across Contexts:** FVs demonstrate remarkable robustness. An FV extracted from a standard ICL context can effectively trigger task execution in a variety of new settings:
 - **Different ICL Templates:** FVs maintain their efficacy when injected into prompts using different ICL formatting templates (Appendix C), indicating that the learned task representation is not bound to a specific prompt structure.
 - **Zero-Shot Scenarios:** As noted above, FVs can induce task performance even in complete zero-shot contexts where no examples are provided.
 - **Natural Text Settings:** Perhaps most strikingly, FVs can successfully induce task performance within natural language sentences (Table 3, Table 4). For example, injecting the Antonym FV into a sentence like "The word 'x' means..." causes the model to generate the antonym of 'x' rather than a synonym or definition. This suggests that the abstract task representation captured by FVs is similar to what LLMs use during general autoregressive prediction.

- **Internal Structure and Decoding of FVs:**
 - **Output Space Alignment:** When decoded directly into the model's vocabulary space (`D(v_t)`), FVs often yield tokens that lie within the task's output vocabulary (e.g., plural nouns for Singular-Plural, capital cities for Country-Capital) (Table 5).
 - **Beyond Output Vocabulary:** However, the knowledge of these top decoded tokens *alone* is not sufficient to fully reconstruct a working function vector. Reconstructing a vector that matches only the top 100 or even all 50,000 decoded tokens of an FV generally results in significantly lower task performance than the original FV (Table 6). This implies that FVs carry crucial "additional information" beyond a simple encoding of output vocabulary, necessary for triggering the full function.
 - **Query-Specific Promotion:** Further analysis reveals that FVs promote tokens that are highly probable *answers to the specific query* in context, rather than just generically boosting words from the overall output vocabulary of the task (Appendix N). This suggests a dynamic interaction.

- **Vector Algebra on Function Vectors:** The authors demonstrate that FVs can, to some extent, obey vector algebra over functions. By algebraically composing FVs of simple tasks (e.g., `v*_BD = v_AD + v_BC - v_AC`), they successfully create new FVs that trigger more complex, combined tasks (e.g., Last-Capital). In some cases, these algebraically composed FVs even outperform standard ICL performance (Table 7). This indicates a structured, composable nature of functional abstractions within LLMs.

- **Distinction from Semantic Vector Arithmetic:** A critical contribution is the explicit differentiation of FVs from simple semantic word embedding vector arithmetic.
 - FVs can implement **cyclic tasks** (e.g., Antonym, Next-Item, Previous-Item), which fundamentally cannot be modeled by constant semantic vector offsets (Appendix A, L).
 - The observed pattern of causal effects being strongest in middle layers and near-zero in late layers contradicts a purely linear, word-embedding-offset mechanism, which would typically be effective at the final output layer.
 - The inability to reconstruct FVs from their decoded vocabulary further supports that they encode more abstract, functional information.

In summary, the paper provides strong empirical evidence for the existence of compact, causal, and portable "function vectors" within LLMs, which serve as abstract representations of tasks, are distinct from simple semantic offsets, and mediate the execution of in-context learning through non-linear computations in the network's middle layers.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The discovery and characterization of function vectors hold profound significance for the field of large language models and artificial intelligence, with potential impacts across several key areas:

- **Fundamental Understanding of LLMs:** This work offers a crucial step forward in moving beyond treating LLMs as "black boxes." By identifying a concrete, causally-effective internal mechanism for representing and executing tasks, it provides unprecedented clarity into *how* in-context learning (ICL) operates. This demystification of ICL, one of the most remarkable emergent capabilities of LLMs, is a major scientific advancement. It suggests that LLMs develop an internal "language" of functions, akin to function references in programming, which is a surprisingly elegant and interpretable form of abstraction.

- **Mechanistic Interpretability and AI Safety:** The ability to explicitly extract and analyze function vectors opens a new avenue for mechanistic interpretability. Understanding these internal representations is critical for auditing and ensuring the safety of large AI systems. If undesirable or harmful functions can be represented and injected, understanding their mechanisms is the first step towards mitigation. This research directly supports the broader goals of AI safety by providing tools to analyze and, potentially, control latent behavior.

- **Enhanced Steering and Control of LLMs:** Current methods for controlling LLMs often rely on prompt engineering, which can be imprecise and sensitive to phrasing. Function vectors offer a potential paradigm shift towards more direct and robust control. Instead of relying solely on external language cues, researchers and developers might be able to inject specific, causally-effective "task instructions" directly into the model's latent space. This could lead to more predictable, reliable, and precise behavioral modifications, enabling fine-grained control over model outputs.

- **Compositionality and Generalization:** The demonstration of algebraic composition on function vectors is highly significant. It suggests that LLMs do not just learn isolated tasks but develop compositional representations that can be combined to form new, complex behaviors. This aligns with human cognitive abilities for systematic generalization and could inspire new architectural designs or training methodologies that explicitly encourage such compositionality. This could pave the way for LLMs that can learn and combine skills in a more structured and predictable manner.

- **Rethinking Internal Computations:** The paper's strong evidence distinguishing FVs from simple semantic vector arithmetic challenges a prevailing view that many internal LLM computations can be reduced to basic linear operations on word embeddings. The observation that FVs trigger non-linear computations in middle layers, and their ability to handle cyclic tasks, indicates a more sophisticated and abstract form of internal representation than previously assumed. This forces a deeper reconsideration of the types of abstractions LLMs are capable of learning and manipulating.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** Function vectors provide a tangible starting point for further mechanistic investigations. Future research can build upon this foundation to characterize the full spectrum of function execution within LLMs, explore how FVs interact with other internal circuits, investigate how they are formed during training, and develop new applications for leveraging these explicit functional representations.

While the authors prudently caution about the potential for misuse (e.g., injecting undesirable functions), the overall impact of this work is overwhelmingly positive, pushing the boundaries of what is known about the internal intelligence of large language models and paving the way for more transparent, controllable, and potentially safer AI systems.
