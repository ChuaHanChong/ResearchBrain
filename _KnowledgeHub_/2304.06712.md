# What does CLIP know about a red circle? Visual prompt engineering for VLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Visual Geometry Group (VGG) at the University of Oxford explore "visual prompt engineering" for Vision-Language Models (VLMs), demonstrating that simple red circles can effectively steer a model's attention while preserving global context. This method achieves state-of-the-art zero-shot performance in referring expression comprehension tasks and, critically, reveals how such visual prompts can trigger latent biases within VLMs.

## Problem

- Adapting frozen Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like CLIP for fine-grained localization tasks (e.g., referring expression comprehension, keypoint localization) without requiring extensive fine-tuning.
- Traditional image transformation methods like cropping often remove valuable contextual information, which can be suboptimal for VLMs that benefit from holistic scene understanding.
- Understanding the emergent properties and potential biases embedded within large-scale VLMs trained on vast, uncurated internet data.

## Method

- Introduces =="visual prompt engineering"== as an alternative to image cropping, where ==graphical annotations (e.g., red circles)== are directly overlaid onto image pixels.
- Frames various computer vision problems as a question-answering task, where the VLM scores transformed image inputs (with marks) against textual queries.
- Empirically determined that a ==red circle== is the most effective visual marker for directing VLM attention across different tasks and VLM backbones.

## Results

- Red circle visual prompting nearly **doubled** the accuracy of cropping-based methods in the "Naming Keypoints" task.
- The approach achieved state-of-the-art zero-shot performance in referring expression comprehension on most RefCOCO benchmarks, without task-specific fine-tuning.
- Applying red circles to human images increased the probability of VLMs classifying individuals into negative or criminal categories by **17%** to over **32%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visual marking, especially simple red circles, effectively directs VLM attention for localization tasks, outperforming traditional image cropping by preserving global context.
> - This capability is an emergent property, implicitly learned from rare but consistent visual cues in vast training datasets, and its effectiveness scales with VLM model capacity.
> - Visual prompts can inadvertently trigger or amplify undesirable biases within VLMs, revealing problematic latent associations linked to sensitive categories like criminal classifications.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Report Overview

The paper introduces a novel approach to "visual prompt engineering" for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like CLIP, demonstrating how simple graphical annotations, specifically red circles, can steer the model's attention and lead to surprising emergent capabilities for tasks beyond traditional classification. The work highlights the power of these emergent behaviors, achieves state-of-the-art results in zero-shot referring expression comprehension, and crucially, sheds light on potential ethical concerns related to biases embedded within large-scale VLMs.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields: **Vision-Language Models (VLMs)**, **prompt engineering**, and **zero-shot learning**.

Large Language Models (LLMs) like GPT-3 have revolutionized natural language processing by exhibiting "emergent behaviors"—the ability to perform tasks they were not explicitly trained for, often through carefully crafted "prompts." This paradigm of prompt engineering has largely been limited to the textual modality. However, VLMs like OpenAI's CLIP (Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training) are inherently multimodal, learning powerful joint representations of images and text from massive datasets.

Prior work with VLMs has primarily focused on **textual prompt engineering**, where textual inputs are manipulated to adapt frozen VLM backbones for various downstream tasks, such as zero-shot image classification (e.g., using prompts like "an image of a X"). A related area, **visual prompt tuning**, involves learning task-specific, optimizable "soft prompts" or tokens in the visual or textual embedding space, rather than directly modifying the pixel data.

This paper introduces a distinct concept: **visual prompt engineering through direct image modification (marking)**. Instead of textual manipulation or learnable tokens, the authors explore how explicitly drawing graphical annotations (like circles, boxes, or arrows) directly onto the image pixels can influence a VLM's interpretation. This is a significant departure from canonical computer vision approaches like image cropping, which have traditionally been used to focus a model's attention on specific regions. The paper argues that cropping, by removing context, may be suboptimal for VLMs that benefit from holistic scene understanding.

Furthermore, the work contributes to **zero-shot referring expression comprehension (REC)** and **keypoint localization**. REC aims to identify a specific object in an image given a textual description, a task traditionally requiring extensive annotated datasets and often involving multi-stage pipelines (e.g., object proposal generation followed by a language-guided ranking). The paper demonstrates that visual prompt engineering can achieve state-of-the-art zero-shot performance in REC, bypassing the need for explicit fine-tuning or complex architectural modifications. This capability for fine-grained localization without direct training points to a deeper understanding of visual semantics and spatial relations within VLMs.

Finally, the paper delves into **model biases**, a critical and increasingly scrutinized area in AI research. While studies on VLM biases have mainly focused on misclassification rates or fairness in retrieval, this work uniquely explores how specific visual prompts can *trigger* or *exacerbate* problematic biases, revealing the latent, sometimes undesirable, associations learned by VLMs from their vast and uncurated training data (e.g., the internet).

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper is driven by two primary objectives:

1. **To develop and demonstrate a practical new tool for zero-shot information extraction from VLMs:** The authors aim to introduce "visual prompt engineering" as a viable method for solving computer vision tasks beyond simple image classification without requiring additional training or fine-tuning of the large pre-trained VLM. Specifically, they demonstrate its utility for tasks demanding fine-grained localization, such as referring expression comprehension and keypoint localization. Their motivation here is to unlock the latent capabilities of frozen VLMs, making them more versatile and accessible for a wider range of applications.

2. **To characterize and understand emergent properties of VLMs and their training data:** Beyond practical utility, a core motivation is to uncover unexpected behaviors and underlying mechanisms within large VLMs like CLIP. The discovery of the "red circle" phenomenon—where a simple annotation can direct attention while preserving global context—is a key emergent ability they seek to explain. This involves investigating *why* certain markers work best, hypothesizing about their presence in the training data, and examining how model capacity influences the emergence of these behaviors.

An important secondary, yet critical, motivation emerges from this characterization: **to highlight potential ethical concerns related to biases in large language-vision models.** The authors investigate whether these emergent behaviors can also trigger undesirable or harmful biases, particularly concerning the classification of human subjects. This serves as a cautionary tale for the deployment of such powerful, broadly trained models in sensitive applications.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The core of the paper's methodology revolves around **visual prompt engineering via "marking"** as an alternative to the traditional approach of image cropping.

#### 4.1. Visual Prompt Engineering Concept

The authors frame various computer vision problems as a **question-answering (Q/A) problem** using a pre-trained VLM (specifically CLIP). A VLM like CLIP computes a compatibility score `s(image, text)` between an image and a piece of text. To adapt this to Q/A, they propose a general formulation: `s(q, a | i, t) = s(i_qa, t_qa)`, where `i_qa` and `t_qa` are transformed versions of the input image `i` and text `t` that encode the question `q` and answer `a`.

Instead of solely relying on text transformations (`t_qa`), the paper focuses on transforming the image input (`i_qa`). The traditional method for image transformation is **cropping**, where the image is clipped to a region of interest. The paper argues that cropping loses valuable contextual information. Their proposed alternative is **marking**: literally overlaying a graphical annotation (e.g., a circle, box, arrow) onto the desired region within the original image. This preserves the global context while still drawing attention to a specific area.

The general approach involves:
1. **Defining a set of possible answers (A):** These could be potential keypoint locations or object proposals.
2. **Engineering the visual prompt (`i_a`):** For each potential answer `a`, the input image `i` is modified by drawing a marker (e.g., a red circle) around the region corresponding to `a`.
3. **Engineering the textual prompt (`t_q`):** The textual query `q` (e.g., a keypoint name or a referring expression) is incorporated into a text prompt, often with a simple prefix (e.g., "an image of the right ear of a dog").
4. **Scoring and selecting the best answer:** The VLM's compatibility score `s(i_a, t_q)` is computed for all `(a, q)` pairs, and the answer `a` that maximizes this score (or a derived score) is selected. For keypoint matching, they use optimal transport (Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm) to infer a permutation matrix. For referring expressions, they subtract the average score across all possible referring expressions to reduce bias towards highly salient objects.

#### 4.2. Tasks for Evaluation

The efficacy of visual marking is evaluated across three zero-shot tasks:

1. **Naming Keypoints:** Given an image with several keypoint locations and a list of keypoint names, the goal is to match each name to its correct location. This task evaluates the VLM's ability to associate semantic labels with specific points on an object.
- **Datasets:** CUB-200-2011 (CUB) and SPair71k (augmented with manual keypoint names).
- **Metric:** Percentage of correctly matched keypoints/names.

2. **Keypoint Localization:** A more practical task where, given an image and a keypoint name, the model must predict its 2D location from a dense grid of possible locations. An unsupervised saliency mask is used to filter out irrelevant background locations.
- **Datasets:** CUB and SPair71k.
- **Metric:** Percentage of Correct Keypoints (PCK) within a certain distance threshold.

3. **Referring Expression Comprehension (REC):** Given an image and a natural language description referring to a unique object, the model must localize that object with a bounding box. This is a complex task requiring both visual and linguistic understanding of spatial and semantic relationships.
- **Datasets:** RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, and RefCOCOg (based on MS-COCO).
- **Approach:** Object proposals are first extracted using MAttNet, and then each proposal is visually marked and scored by the VLM against the referring expression.
- **Metric:** Top-1 accuracy based on IoU with ground truth.

Throughout these experiments, the authors perform extensive ablations on marker characteristics (shape, color, size) and analyze different VLM backbones (CLIP, OpenCLIP, SLIP, DeCLIP) and pre-training datasets to understand the factors contributing to the observed emergent behavior. They also conduct an empirical study to search for similar annotated images in CLIP's training data (YFCC15M) to support their hypothesis about the origin of the emergent behavior.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several significant findings:

#### 5.1. Effectiveness of Visual Marking

The most striking finding is the **superiority of visual marking (specifically, red circles) over traditional image cropping** for directing VLM attention. In the "Naming Keypoints" task, red circle prompting achieved almost twice the accuracy of cropping-based methods. This suggests that preserving contextual information, which marking does unlike cropping, is crucial for VLMs.

#### 5.2. Optimal Marker Characteristics

Through extensive ablation studies on the "Naming Keypoints" task, the authors empirically determined that a **red circle** is the most effective visual marker. Circles outperformed other shapes (arrows, squares, crosses), and red was consistently better than other colors (green, purple, blue, yellow), despite being a common color in natural images. This counter-intuitive result hints at a specific learned association within VLMs.

#### 5.3. Emergence and Model Capacity

The authors hypothesize that the effectiveness of red circles stems from their infrequent but consistent presence in the vast internet-scale training data of VLMs. Their search in YFCC15M (a subset of CLIP's training data) confirmed the presence of human-annotated images with red circles, albeit very rarely (~0.001% of images). This rarity, combined with the observed performance gains, leads to a crucial conclusion: this behavior is an **emergent property** learned by large VLMs from subtle cues in massive datasets, without explicit supervision. Furthermore, the effectiveness of marking significantly **improves with increased VLM model capacity and pre-training dataset size**. Larger models like ViT-L/14@336px showed a 250% improvement on zero-shot keypoint matching compared to smaller models, whereas the improvement on standard zero-shot ImageNet-1K classification was only 20%. This stark difference underscores the emergent nature of this specific capability, similar to how arithmetic or planning abilities emerge only in very large language models.

#### 5.4. State-of-the-Art Performance

Visual prompt engineering with red circles achieved **state-of-the-art zero-shot performance** in referring expression comprehension on most benchmarks (RefCOCO, RefCOCO+, RefCOCOg). It notably outperformed prior zero-shot methods like ReCLIP (which includes relational resolving post-processing) and even weakly supervised methods like Pseudo-Q, without any task-specific fine-tuning. This demonstrates the practical power of this simple yet effective visual intervention.

#### 5.5. Model Biases and Ethical Implications

A critical finding is that visual marking can also **trigger or amplify undesirable biases** within VLMs. By drawing red circles over individuals in images, the probability of the VLM classifying them into negative or criminal categories (e.g., "missing person," "criminal," "suspected murderer") significantly increased across different CLIP backbones and datasets (synthetic faces from FaceSynthetics and person crops from COCO). For instance, the criminal classification rate could jump by 17% to over 32% with the addition of a red circle. This reveals a problematic latent association within the model, likely due to the presence of images like police reports or news articles marking individuals in a negative context within the training data. This highlights the urgent need for bias mitigation and careful consideration of ethical implications when deploying VLMs.

### 6. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **New Paradigm for VLM Interaction:** The introduction of "visual prompt engineering" via marking opens up a novel and intuitive way to interact with and control VLMs. It demonstrates that models like CLIP are not just robust to pixel-level changes but can interpret them semantically, moving beyond simple text-based prompts to leverage the visual modality itself as a means of instruction. This expands the possibilities for guiding and extracting information from pre-trained multimodal models.

2. **Enhanced Zero-Shot Capabilities:** The ability to achieve state-of-the-art zero-shot performance in complex localization tasks like referring expression comprehension, without any task-specific fine-tuning, is highly impactful. It suggests that VLMs possess a deeper, more granular understanding of objects and spatial relationships than previously fully exploited. This can significantly reduce the data annotation burden for new tasks, accelerating the development and deployment of vision systems.

3. **Deeper Understanding of Emergent Behaviors:** The paper provides compelling evidence for emergent behaviors in VLMs, linking them to the sheer scale of training data and model capacity. The "red circle" phenomenon serves as a powerful case study for understanding how subtle and rare patterns in unstructured data can lead to sophisticated, yet implicit, model capabilities. This contributes to the broader quest of understanding "what large models know" and how they acquire such knowledge.

4. **Implications for Model Design and Training Data:** The findings encourage researchers to think more critically about the composition and annotation style within vast training datasets. If rare visual cues can lead to powerful emergent properties, it implies a need for more nuanced data curation and understanding of how models learn from "the internet at large."

5. **Ethical Imperative for Responsible AI Development:** The demonstration of how visual prompts can trigger problematic biases is a crucial ethical warning. It underscores the inherent risks of deploying large models trained on unfiltered internet data without robust bias detection and mitigation strategies. This finding is highly significant for ensuring the responsible development and deployment of VLMs, particularly in applications involving human subjects, where such biases could lead to discriminatory or harmful outcomes. It emphasizes that while emergent capabilities can be beneficial, they can also expose hidden flaws that require careful attention.

In conclusion, "What does CLIP know about a red circle?" is a thought-provoking paper that not only offers a practical new technique for VLM interaction but also deepens our understanding of the fascinating and sometimes troubling emergent properties of these powerful models, pushing the boundaries of both performance and ethical considerations in AI.
