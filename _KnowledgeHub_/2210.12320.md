# Online Adaptive Policy Selection in Time-Varying Systems: No-Regret via Contractive Perturbations

> [!summary] Summary
> An online adaptive policy selection algorithm, GAPS, is introduced along with a novel contractive perturbation framework for time-varying discrete-time dynamical systems. This method achieves an optimal O(sqrt(T)) adaptive policy regret in convex settings, provides the first local regret bounds for nonconvex settings, and operates with O(log T) computational complexity per step.

## Problem

- Achieving optimal regret bounds for online adaptive policy selection in time-varying systems, where previous methods often yielded suboptimal O(sqrt(T) log T) regret.
- High computational demands and information requirements (e.g., oracle access, re-simulation) of existing online control algorithms.
- Limited applicability of prior theoretical guarantees, primarily restricted to specific policy classes and convex system dynamics.

## Method

- The **GAPS** (==Gradient-based Adaptive Policy Selection==) algorithm, which approximates ideal ==Online Gradient Descent (OGD)== by computing gradients along the actual executed trajectory.
- Efficient gradient estimation by ==truncating historical dependence== to a finite buffer length, avoiding expensive re-simulations and requiring only on-trajectory partial derivatives.
- A general analytical framework based on an ==ε-Time-varying Contractive Perturbation== property, which characterizes how past policy decisions' impact decays over time.

## Results

- GAPS achieved an optimal **O(√T)** adaptive policy regret for systems with convex surrogate costs, resolving the **O(log T)** gap found in prior online control literature.
- The work established the **first local regret bound of O((1+V)^(1/2) √T)** for online policy selection in nonconvex settings, where V is the system's variation intensity.
- Numerical experiments demonstrated GAPS's ability to adapt quickly to sudden environmental changes, outperforming baselines in model predictive control and time-varying nonlinear systems, while operating with **O(log T)** matrix multiplications per step.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The "contractive perturbation" property effectively bridges online policy selection to online optimization, enabling the derivation of strong regret guarantees.
> - Computing gradients along the actual system trajectory, rather than through hypothetical re-simulations, provides a computationally efficient path to online adaptation.
> - By bounding approximation errors, GAPS can achieve regret performance comparable to ideal online optimization methods, even in complex time-varying and nonconvex systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses the problem of online adaptive policy selection and general online control within the context of time-varying, discrete-time dynamical systems. This field has received attention due to the requirements of many control tasks to operate on a single trajectory without episode restarts, and the need for adaptivity in environments with changing dynamics and cost functions (e.g., drone control under variable wind conditions).

Previous work in this area has primarily focused on addressing the challenge that stage costs depend on all previously selected policy parameters via the system state. Many prior approaches utilized finite-memory reductions to convert the online control problem into an online convex optimization (OCO) with memory problem. While these methods yielded initial regret bounds for online policy selection, they presented several limitations:
- **Non-tight regret bounds**: The achieved bounds were often not optimal, leaving a logarithmic factor gap (log T) in comparison to guarantees in standard OCO.
- **High computational cost**: Many methods required oracle access to dynamics and costs for arbitrary states and actions, often involving expensive re-simulation from hypothetical past states.
- **Restrictive applicability**: The techniques were typically limited to specific policy classes and systems that ensured the stage cost was a convex function of past policy parameters. Notable examples include the disturbance-action controller (DAC).

This paper aims to bridge these gaps by developing a more general and efficient framework. It connects online policy selection more directly to online optimization theory, particularly focusing on handling systems where the impact of past decisions decays over time, and extending guarantees to nonconvex settings, which are often encountered in real-world control problems. It also relates to the study of incremental stability and contraction theory in nonlinear systems, applying these concepts to derive performance guarantees for online controllers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified framework and an efficient algorithm for online adaptive policy selection in nonlinear, time-varying discrete-time dynamical systems. The goal is to select policy parameters `θ_t` at each time step `t` to minimize the total accumulated stage costs over a finite horizon `T`, where the system dynamics are given by `x_(t+1) = g_t(x_t, u_t)` and stage costs by `c_t = f_t(x_t, u_t)`. The policy class `π_t` maps the state `x_t` and policy parameter `θ_t` to a control input `u_t`.

The motivation for this work arises from several limitations identified in the existing literature:
- **Suboptimal regret guarantees**: Prior methods for online policy selection in convex settings often achieved `O(√T log T)` regret, which is suboptimal compared to the `O(√T)` achievable in online convex optimization. A key objective is to close this `log T` gap.
- **Information and computational inefficiencies**: Many existing algorithms demand substantial information (e.g., oracle access to global dynamics/costs) and incur high computational overhead, such as requiring expensive re-simulations of the system to evaluate hypothetical policy choices. A major motivation is to develop an algorithm that requires less information (only partial derivatives along the visited trajectory) and is more computationally efficient (e.g., `O(log T)` matrix multiplications per step).
- **Limited scope of applicability**: The requirement for convexity of the stage cost with respect to past policy parameters (`c_t` in `(θ_0..., θ_t)`) restricted the application of prior results to specific policy classes and system types. A crucial objective is to extend the theoretical guarantees to settings where this convexity assumption does not hold, enabling broader application to nonlinear systems and diverse policy classes.
- **Unified theoretical foundation**: The authors aim to establish a general analytical framework that can effectively leverage results from online optimization. This involves defining system properties, such as contractive perturbations, that allow for the translation of online optimization guarantees to the domain of online policy selection, thereby providing a more cohesive theoretical foundation.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for more efficient, widely applicable, and theoretically optimal online adaptive control strategies, particularly for complex time-varying and nonlinear systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology involves the development of the Gradient-based Adaptive Policy Selection (GAPS) algorithm and a novel analytical framework founded on the concept of *contractive perturbation*.

**GAPS Algorithm (Algorithm 1):**
GAPS is an online algorithm designed to select policy parameters `θ_t` sequentially. It is inspired by the Online Gradient Descent (OGD) paradigm. In standard OGD, an ideal update rule would compute a gradient step with respect to a "surrogate cost" `F_t(θ)`, which represents the cost at time `t` if `θ` had been applied from the beginning of the trajectory (`θ_0:t = θ^(×(t+1))`). However, exactly computing the gradient `∇F_t(θ_t)` is computationally intensive as its complexity grows with `t`.

GAPS addresses this intractability by efficiently approximating `∇F_t(θ_t)` using two main techniques:
1. **Actual Trajectory Gradient Computation**: Instead of re-simulating the trajectory under a fixed `θ_t` from time 0, GAPS computes gradients along the *actual trajectory* `(x_0..., x_t)` generated by the sequence of policy parameters `(θ_0..., θ_t)`. This avoids expensive re-simulations.
2. **Finite Memory Truncation**: GAPS truncates the historical dependence of the gradient computation to a buffer length `B`. Specifically, it computes `G_t = ∑_(b=0)^(min{B-1,t}) ∂f_t|0/∂θ_(t-b) |_(x_0, θ_0:t)`. This bounds the memory used by the algorithm, enabling an efficient implementation with `O(B)` memory and `O(B)` matrix multiplications per step (detailed in Algorithm 2, Appendix B).

This approach contrasts with prior finite-memory reduction methods that often required oracle access to dynamics for arbitrary states and actions, and involved re-simulating B MPC optimization problems to estimate gradients. GAPS, instead, only requires partial derivatives along the visited trajectory, which are computed more efficiently.

**Analytical Framework via Contractive Perturbation:**
A central component of the methodology is the introduction of a *general exponentially decaying, or "contractive", perturbation property* (Definition 2.6). This property is defined as:
- **ε-Time-varying Contractive Perturbation**: For policy parameter sequences `θ_τ:t-1` that vary slowly (i.e., `||θ_j+1 - θ_j|| ≤ ε`), the property requires that two trajectories starting from different states `x_τ, x'_τ` (within a bounded ball `B_n(0, R_C)`) converge exponentially fast, such that `||g_t|τ(x_τ, θ_τ:t-1) - g_t|τ(x'_τ, θ_τ:t-1)|| ≤ Cρ^(t-τ) ||x_τ - x'_τ||` for constants `C > 0` and `ρ ∈ (0, 1)`. This generalizes properties of systems like disturbance-action controllers (DAC), model predictive control (MPC), and linear feedback controllers.
- **ε-Time-varying Stability (Definition 2.7)**: This property ensures that the policy class can stabilize the system, guaranteeing that states remain bounded (`||g_t|τ(0, θ_τ:t-1)|| ≤ R_S`) if policy parameters `θ_τ:t-1` vary slowly.

**Key Assumptions (Section 2.3):**
1. **Differentiability and Lipschitzness/Smoothness (Assumption 2.1)**: The dynamics `g_t`, policies `π_t`, and costs `f_t` are assumed to be differentiable and to satisfy Lipschitzness and smoothness properties for bounded states and actions.
2. **Contractive Perturbation and Stability (Assumption 2.2)**: The ε-time-varying contractive perturbation and stability properties are assumed to hold for any dynamics/policy sequences the environment may provide. An initial state constraint (`||x_0|| < (R_C - R_S)/C`) ensures trajectories remain within the bounded region `R_C`. A lemma (Lemma 2.8) is provided to show that time-invariant versions of these properties can imply their ε-time-varying counterparts for small ε.

**Bridging to Online Optimization:**
The analytical framework, especially the contractive perturbation property, enables GAPS to effectively "mimic" the updates of an ideal OGD algorithm. Theorem 3.2 provides approximation error bounds for both the cost (`|f_t(x_t, u_t) - F_t(θ_t)|`) and the gradient (`||G_t - ∇F_t(θ_t)||`), demonstrating that these errors scale with the learning rate `η` and decay exponentially with the buffer length `B` (via `ρ^B`). This allows for the transfer of regret guarantees from online optimization to online policy selection.

**Performance Metrics:**
- **Adaptive Policy Regret `R_A(T)` (Definition 2.4)**: Used for convex settings, it measures the maximum difference between the online policy's cost and the cost of the optimal *fixed-parameter* policy over any sub-interval `I ⊆ T`. This is a stronger benchmark than static regret.
- **Local Regret `R_L(T)` (Definition 2.5)**: Introduced for nonconvex settings, it bounds the sum of squared gradient norms over the horizon. This metric quantifies how well the online agent approaches stationary points of the time-varying surrogate cost functions. It depends on the *Variation Intensity* `V` (Definition 3.5), which measures how much the system functions `(g_t, π_t, f_t)` change over time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several theoretical results and validates the GAPS algorithm through numerical experiments.

**Theoretical Results:**

1. **Bounds on Truncation Error (Theorem 3.2):** This theorem establishes quantitative bounds on the approximation errors introduced by GAPS. It shows that:
 - The difference between the actual stage cost incurred by GAPS and the ideal surrogate cost is bounded: `|f_t(x_t, u_t) - F_t(θ_t)| = O((1-ρ)^(-3) η)`.
 - The difference between the approximated gradient `G_t` used by GAPS and the ideal gradient `∇F_t(θ_t)` of the surrogate cost is bounded: `||G_t - ∇F_t(θ_t)|| = O((1-ρ)^(-5) η + (1-ρ)^(-1) ρ^B)`.
 These bounds are crucial for transferring regret guarantees from online optimization and hold without convexity assumptions, depending on the learning rate `η`, the decay factor `ρ` of the contractive perturbation, and the buffer length `B`.

2. **Optimal Policy Regret for Convex Surrogate Costs (Theorem 3.3 and Corollary 3.4):**
 - Under the assumption that the surrogate cost `F_t` is convex for all `t`, GAPS is shown to achieve an adaptive policy regret `R_A(T) = O((1-ρ)^(-5/2) √T)`. This result is obtained by setting the learning rate `η = (1-ρ)^(5/2) T^(-1/2)` and a buffer length `B ≥ (1/2)log(T)/log(1/ρ)`.
 - This finding is significant as it closes the `log T` regret gap that existed between online control and online convex optimization in prior work, establishing `O(√T)` as the optimal regret for this setting.

3. **Local Regret Bound for Nonconvex Surrogate Costs (Theorem 3.6 and Corollary 3.7):**
 - This is presented as the first local regret bound for online policy selection when the surrogate cost `F_t` is nonconvex. GAPS achieves a local regret `R_L(T) = O((1-ρ)^(-9/2) (1+V)^(1/2) √T)`. This bound is attained with a learning rate `η = (1-ρ)^(3/2) (1+V)^(1/2) T^(-1/2)` and a buffer length `B ≥ (1/2)log(T)/log(1/ρ)`.
 - The bound depends on `V`, the *variation intensity* of the time-varying system, which quantifies how much the dynamics, policies, and cost functions change over the horizon. Local regret is sublinear in `T` if `V = o(T)`. A limitation is the assumption that the parameter set `Θ` is the entire Euclidean space to avoid boundary issues.

**Numerical Experiments:**
The effectiveness of GAPS is demonstrated through simulations in environments based on real-world control problems:

1. **MPC with Confidence Coefficients (Example 2.1):** GAPS was applied to tune a scalar confidence parameter in an MPC setting with noisy disturbance predictions.
 - **Result**: GAPS demonstrated faster adaptation to sudden changes in prediction accuracy compared to a follow-the-leader baseline, leading to lower long-term costs. The algorithm's behavior was consistent with its theoretical properties as an OGD instance with a constant step size.

2. **Linear Feedback Control in Nonlinear Systems (Example 2.2):** GAPS was used to tune the proportional-derivative (PD) gains of a linear feedback controller in a nonlinear inverted pendulum system with time-varying mass.
 - **Result**: GAPS adapted to step changes in dynamics nearly as quickly as an optimal LQR baseline (which benefits from analytical solutions). Furthermore, GAPS significantly outperformed the LQR baseline in scenarios with non-i.i.d. disturbances (e.g., random walk), where the LQR's assumption of independent and identically distributed disturbances no longer holds optimally.

3. **MPC Horizon Selection (Appendix I.3):** A comparison was made between GAPS (optimizing continuous confidence coefficients) and a bandit-based algorithm (BAPS, Algorithm 3, selecting discrete MPC horizons).
 - **Result**: GAPS, exploiting the continuous problem structure, achieved approximately constant regret over time, outperforming BAPS which exhibited `T^(2/3)` regret. This illustrates the benefit of applying GAPS in problems amenable to continuous parameter optimization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents significant contributions to the field of online adaptive control and online optimization.

**Theoretical Significance:**
- **Novel Algorithm (GAPS):** The proposed GAPS algorithm offers a computationally efficient approach to online adaptive policy selection. It requires less information than previous methods, utilizing only partial derivatives along the visited trajectory rather than expensive oracle access or re-simulations, which is a practical advantage for complex systems.
- **General Analytical Framework:** The introduction of the "contractive perturbation property" provides a unified framework that encompasses a broad range of policy classes, including disturbance-action controllers, model predictive control, and linear feedback controllers. This generalization allows for a more cohesive theoretical understanding across diverse control paradigms.
- **Optimal Regret in Convex Settings:** GAPS achieves the optimal `O(√T)` adaptive policy regret for systems with convex surrogate costs, effectively closing the `log T` gap that existed in the literature. This establishes a stronger theoretical benchmark for online control algorithms.
- **Pioneering Nonconvex Guarantees:** The work provides the first local regret bound for online policy selection in nonconvex settings. This is a crucial advancement, as many real-world control problems are inherently nonconvex, expanding the applicability of regret-based guarantees to a wider range of practical scenarios.

**Practical Impact:**
- **Enhanced Adaptability:** The numerical experiments demonstrate GAPS's ability to adapt quickly and effectively to time-varying dynamics and changing environmental conditions. This has direct implications for applications such as robotics (e.g., drone flight in changing wind conditions), autonomous systems, and resource management, where real-time adaptation is critical.
- **Reduced Computational Burden:** By requiring only on-trajectory partial derivatives and `O(log T)` matrix multiplications per step, GAPS offers a more scalable and deployable solution compared to methods that demand extensive re-simulations or full system models.
- **Broader Applicability:** The extension of regret guarantees to nonconvex systems expands the range of problems that can be addressed with theoretically grounded online control algorithms, moving beyond simpler linear or convex approximations.

**Future Directions:**
The authors identify several avenues for future research:
- **Relaxing Stability Assumptions:** A current limitation of GAPS is the assumption that all candidate policy parameters can stabilize the system and satisfy contractive perturbation. Future work could explore how to provide regret guarantees when not all policies meet these stringent criteria, potentially building upon bandit-based approaches for continuous parameter sets.
- **Generalizing Nonconvex Analysis:** Further research could explore relaxing the assumption that the policy parameter set `Θ` must be a whole Euclidean space in nonconvex settings, enabling the algorithm to handle constrained nonconvex problems more generally.
- **Dynamic Regret in Nonconvex Settings:** While dynamic regret bounds were provided for convex cases (Appendix E), extending this analysis to nonconvex surrogate costs remains an open problem.

Overall, this research represents a significant step forward in the theory and practice of online adaptive control, offering both enhanced theoretical guarantees and a more practical algorithm for a broad class of time-varying dynamical systems.
