# SRSA: Skill Retrieval and Adaptation for Robotic Assembly Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> The SRSA framework, developed by researchers at NVIDIA, USC, and UW, enables robots to learn new assembly tasks efficiently by retrieving and adapting pre-trained skills. This approach achieved a 19% higher success rate with 2.4x faster training in simulation and demonstrated a 90% success rate on physical robots for various peg-in-hole tasks.

## Problem

- Robotic assembly tasks, especially contact-rich ones, require high precision, robust force control, and adaptability to diverse parts, making them challenging for autonomous systems.
- Learning assembly policies from scratch for each new task is data-intensive and inefficient due to the complexity of physical interactions.
- Existing methods often lack the ability to effectively leverage prior experience across different, but related, assembly tasks.

## Method

- The **SRSA** (==Skill Retrieval== and Skill Adaptation) framework leverages a library of pre-trained specialist policies to accelerate learning for new assembly tasks.
- Skill Retrieval identifies the most suitable existing skills for a new task by predicting their zero-shot transfer success rate using learned task representations.
- Skill Adaptation fine-tunes the retrieved skill for the new task using a combination of policy initialization, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and Self-Imitation Learning (SIL).

## Results

- SRSA achieved a **19%** relative improvement in success rate with **2.4x** faster training in dense-reward settings compared to baselines.
- The framework demonstrated a **135%** relative improvement in success rate and **2.6x** lower standard deviation in sparse-reward scenarios.
- Policies trained in simulation with SRSA achieved a **90%** mean success rate when transferred to a physical robot without additional real-world training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Skills with higher zero-shot success rates on a new task serve as better starting points for fine-tuning, guiding the skill retrieval process.
> - Learning task representations from object geometry, physical dynamics, and expert actions enables effective prediction of a skill's transferability.
> - Combining Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with Self-Imitation Learning (SIL) during adaptation improves learning stability and efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Robotic assembly tasks present significant challenges for autonomous systems due to their requirement for precision, physical contact management, and adaptability to diverse parts. While research in robotic manipulation has advanced considerably, the domain of contact-rich assembly remains relatively underexplored despite its industrial importance. The paper "SRSA: Skill Retrieval and Adaptation for Robotic Assembly Tasks" by researchers from NVIDIA, University of Southern California, and University of Washington addresses this gap by introducing a framework that enables robots to learn new assembly tasks efficiently by leveraging prior experience.

The SRSA (Skill Retrieval and Skill Adaptation) framework represents a middle ground between training single-task policies from scratch and relying on foundational models. It capitalizes on a library of pre-trained specialist policies to accelerate learning for new, previously unseen assembly tasks. The key innovation lies in effectively determining which existing skills are most relevant to a new task and how to adapt them efficiently.

### 2. Assembly Challenges

Contact-rich assembly tasks are particularly challenging for robots due to several factors:

1. **Precision requirements**: Parts often need to fit together with millimeter or sub-millimeter accuracy
2. **Force control**: Managing contact forces appropriately to avoid damage while ensuring successful assembly
3. **Physical complexity**: Handling friction, contact dynamics, and compliance
4. **Part variability**: Adapting to different shapes, sizes, and material properties
5. **Reliability standards**: Meeting high success rate requirements for industrial applications

These challenges make it difficult to learn assembly policies from scratch for each new task, emphasizing the need for more data-efficient approaches that leverage prior experience.

### 3. The SRSA Framework

The SRSA framework consists of two primary components:

1. **Skill Retrieval**: Identifying which skills from a pre-existing library are most suitable for a new assembly task
2. **Skill Adaptation**: Efficiently fine-tuning the retrieved skill for optimal performance on the new task

A key insight underpinning SRSA is that skills with higher zero-shot success rates on a new task tend to be better starting points for fine-tuning. This insight drives the design of a transfer success predictor that helps select the most promising skills from the library.

### 4. Skill Retrieval

The skill retrieval component addresses a fundamental question: given a library of skills and a new task, which skill should be selected as the starting point for adaptation? SRSA approaches this by predicting the zero-shot transfer success rate of each skill on the new task.

The retrieval process involves:

1. **Task representation**: Capturing features related to object geometry, physical dynamics, and expert actions
2. **Transfer success prediction**: Using these features to predict how well each skill will transfer to the new task
3. **Skill selection**: Retrieving the top-k skills based on predicted transfer success and selecting the best performer

To learn this task representation, the authors develop a method that extracts meaningful features from:

- **Geometry**: Using a PointNet autoencoder trained on point cloud data of the objects
- **Dynamics**: Learning from transition segments of disassembly trajectories with a state-prediction objective
- **Expert actions**: Reconstructing actions from disassembly trajectories

These features are concatenated and fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) that predicts the transfer success rate. The prediction model is trained on a dataset of tuples (πsrc, Ttrg, rsrc,trg), where πsrc is a source policy, Ttrg is a target task, and rsrc,trg is the zero-shot transfer success rate.

### 5. Skill Adaptation

Once a relevant skill is selected, SRSA adapts it to the new task through a fine-tuning process that combines:

1. **Policy initialization**: Using the retrieved skill to initialize the policy network
2. **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**: Fine-tuning the policy using the PPO algorithm
3. **Self-Imitation Learning (SIL)**: Enhancing performance by imitating high-reward transitions from the agent's own experience

The self-imitation learning component is particularly important for improving learning stability and efficiency. It works by maintaining a buffer of the agent's past experiences and selectively imitating those that resulted in high rewards, helping to reinforce successful behaviors.

The adaptation process can be formalized as:

```
π_target = Adapt(π_source, T_target)
```

Where π_source is the retrieved source policy, T_target is the target task, and π_target is the adapted policy for the target task.

### 6. Experimental Results

The authors evaluated SRSA in both simulated and real-world environments, focusing on peg-in-hole assembly tasks with various shapes and configurations. Key findings include:

1. **Improved retrieval accuracy**: SRSA significantly outperformed baselines in retrieving policies with better zero-shot transfer success rates
2. **Enhanced learning efficiency**: In dense-reward settings, SRSA achieved a 19% relative improvement in success rate with 2.4x faster training compared to baseline methods
3. **Superior performance in sparse-reward scenarios**: SRSA demonstrated a 135% relative improvement in success rate compared to baselines in sparse-reward settings
4. **Reduced variability**: The approach showed 2.6x lower standard deviation across random seeds, indicating greater stability

The experiments demonstrated that the combination of effective skill retrieval and adaptation allows SRSA to solve new assembly tasks with significantly less training data than approaches that train from scratch.

### 7. Real World Application

A crucial aspect of the research is the successful deployment of SRSA in real-world scenarios. Policies trained with SRSA in simulation achieved a 90% mean success rate when transferred to a physical robot without additional real-world training.

This sim-to-real transfer capability is particularly valuable for industrial applications, where collecting large amounts of real-world training data can be expensive and time-consuming. The authors' experiments involved various types of pegs and holes with different geometries, demonstrating the framework's versatility across different assembly configurations.

### 8. Continual Learning

SRSA also supports continual learning, wherein the skill library expands over time as new tasks are mastered. Instead of training many single-task policies from scratch, the approach gradually expands a small set of initial skills via retrieval and adaptation.

In a continual learning scenario, SRSA improved sample efficiency by over 80% compared to baseline methods. Importantly, this efficiency was maintained as both the skill library and target tasks evolved, demonstrating the scalability of the approach.

The continual learning process follows a cycle:
1. Use the current skill library to retrieve a suitable skill for a new task
2. Adapt the skill to the new task
3. Add the adapted skill back to the library
4. Repeat for subsequent new tasks

This cycle enables efficient accumulation of a large-scale collection of skills over time, with each new skill potentially helping to solve future related tasks more effectively.

### 9. Limitations and Future Work

While SRSA shows promising results, the authors acknowledge several limitations:

1. The current implementation focuses primarily on peg-in-hole style assembly tasks
2. The approach assumes access to a library of pre-trained skills, which may not always be available
3. The real-world experiments were limited to a relatively small set of test cases

Future work could extend SRSA to:
- More complex assembly tasks involving multiple parts
- Scenarios with partial observability or sensory noise
- Integration with other learning approaches like meta-learning
- Addressing dynamic environments where the task properties change over time

### 10. Conclusion

SRSA represents a significant advance in robotic assembly by introducing a framework that enables data-efficient learning through skill retrieval and adaptation. By effectively leveraging prior experience, it addresses key challenges in contact-rich assembly tasks, demonstrating improved performance, stability, and efficiency compared to existing approaches.

The framework's success in both simulated and real-world environments, coupled with its support for continual learning, makes it particularly relevant for industrial applications where robots need to master diverse assembly tasks efficiently. The concepts of learning task features and predicting zero-shot transfer success for policy transfer also have potential applications beyond assembly to other robotics domains requiring precision and adaptability.
