# Optimizing Temperature for Language Models with Multi-Sample Inference

> [!summary] Summary
> A method called TURN is introduced to automatically identify the near-optimal sampling temperature for large language models when using multi-sample aggregation, eliminating the need for labeled validation data. This approach leverages an intrinsic "entropy turning point" (EntP) to predict effective temperatures, achieving performance within 0.32% to 0.59% of grid-search optimum on math problem-solving and code generation tasks.

## Problem

- Optimizing the temperature hyperparameter for multi-sample Large Language Model (LLM) inference is challenging, often relying on suboptimal fixed values or costly empirical tuning.
- Current methods for temperature selection require extensive labeled validation data, which is frequently scarce or impractical to obtain for diverse tasks and models.
- Suboptimal temperature settings hinder the full potential of multi-sample aggregation strategies (e.g., majority voting, best-of-N) in achieving high accuracy and reliability.

## Method

- The researchers developed ==TURN (Turning Point Temperature Selection)==, an automated, ==data-agnostic algorithm== to identify the near-optimal temperature for LLM multi-sample inference.
- TURN utilizes the =="entropy turning point" (EntP)==, an intrinsic characteristic of an LLM's token-level entropy curve, which signals a rapid decline in sample quality beyond this temperature.
- The algorithm computes the EntP by finding where the ==second derivative of the log-entropy curve== transitions from negative to positive, then applies a small aggregation-specific adaptation factor.

## Results

- The TURN method accurately predicted optimal temperatures, achieving a Hit Rate of **12/13** models on MATH and **11/13** on MBPP within the "epsilon"-optimal range.
- TURN incurred a minimal performance drop compared to exhaustive grid search, averaging only **0.32%** on MATH and **0.59%** on MBPP, while consistently outperforming fixed-temperature baselines.
- The EntP showed a high correlation with the best empirically found temperature (**0.9998** for MATH, **0.9913** for MBPP), demonstrating its reliability as an intrinsic signal.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimal sampling temperature for multi-sample LLM inference varies considerably based on model architecture, training, and task, with fine-tuned models performing best at higher temperatures.
> - An "entropy turning point" (EntP) exists in the log-scale token-level entropy curve, strongly correlating with the empirically determined optimal temperature for achieving peak accuracy.
> - A simplified stochastic process model can reproduce the observed entropy spike, providing a theoretical explanation that EntP marks a rapid increase in "improper" tokens, signifying a collapse in sample quality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement of Large Language Models (LLMs) has led to their widespread application across diverse domains, from question answering and intelligent agents to scientific discovery and mathematical reasoning. A critical aspect of leveraging these generative models effectively is the process of sampling outputs. While LLMs exhibit remarkable capabilities, ensuring the accuracy and quality of their predictions, especially in complex tasks, remains a challenge.

One prominent strategy to enhance LLM performance is the use of multi-sample aggregation. Techniques like majority voting, weighted majority voting, and "best-of-N" sampling involve generating multiple candidate solutions from an LLM and then combining or selecting the best one. These methods have demonstrated significant improvements in various benchmarks, particularly in tasks requiring reasoning and code generation.

Central to the sampling process is the temperature hyperparameter, which controls the "smoothness" or "peakedness" of the model's probability distribution over possible next tokens. A higher temperature leads to more diverse and exploratory outputs, potentially uncovering novel or correct solutions that might be missed by lower-temperature, more conservative sampling. Conversely, excessively high temperatures can introduce too much noise, leading to low-quality or irrelevant samples, while very low temperatures might restrict diversity too much. Striking the right balance is crucial for optimizing performance in multi-sample inference.

Historically, temperature selection for LLMs has been a challenge. Many studies and deployments resort to using a fixed, default temperature (e.g., T=0.7 or T=1.0), which is often suboptimal given the vast differences in model architectures, training data, task types, and aggregation strategies. An alternative is empirical tuning using labeled validation data, but this approach is tedious, time-consuming, and often impractical due to the scarcity or cost of obtaining such data for every specific task and model variant.

This paper directly addresses this critical gap in the research landscape. It systematically investigates the influence of temperature in multi-sample aggregation settings and, more importantly, proposes a novel, automated, and data-agnostic method for identifying the near-optimal temperature. By eliminating the dependency on labeled validation data, this work significantly enhances the practical deployability and efficiency of LLM-based systems, positioning itself as a vital contribution to making LLMs more robust and user-friendly for complex generative tasks. It moves beyond previous work that focused on single-sample temperature tuning or multi-sample tuning with validation sets, offering a unique solution to a prevalent practical problem.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a method for automatically identifying the (near)-optimal temperature for Large Language Models when employing multi-sample aggregation strategies. Crucially, this optimization must occur **without relying on task-specific labeled validation data**, which is often scarce or difficult to obtain.

This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:
1. **Addressing Suboptimal Fixed Temperatures:** Existing practices often use a fixed default temperature for LLM inference, despite evidence that the optimal temperature varies significantly across different LLM architectures, training datasets, task types, model sizes, and aggregation strategies. This fixed approach leads to suboptimal performance and limits the full potential of LLMs.
2. **Overcoming Validation Data Scarcity:** While empirical tuning with labeled validation data can yield better temperatures, this process is resource-intensive and heavily dependent on the availability of such data. For many real-world applications or novel tasks, acquiring sufficient labeled validation data is impractical or impossible. The authors aim to remove this dependency.
3. **Comprehensive Analysis of Temperature's Role:** Beyond just finding an optimal temperature, the paper seeks to provide a deeper, systematic understanding of how temperature impacts LLM performance under multi-sample aggregation across various conditions. This includes analyzing its effects on model architectures, datasets, task types, model sizes, and predictive accuracy.
4. **Enhancing Interpretability:** The authors are motivated to offer greater insight into the underlying mechanisms of how temperature influences sample quality and diversity. They aim to achieve this through the discovery of characteristic patterns in model behavior and the development of explanatory models.
5. **Improving LLM Practicality and Robustness:** By providing an automated and efficient method for temperature optimization, the research aims to make multi-sample aggregation strategies more robust, accessible, and easier to deploy in diverse applications, ultimately leading to higher and more consistent performance from LLMs in generative tasks.

In essence, the research is motivated by a desire to bring greater scientific rigor and practical utility to a critical hyperparameter tuning challenge in the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper revolves around an empirical observation of token-level entropy and its correlation with optimal temperature, leading to a novel, automated temperature selection algorithm.

#### 3.1. The Role of Temperature and Sample Diversity

The authors first highlight that multi-sample aggregation strategies benefit significantly from diverse candidate samples. Temperature is identified as the primary lever for controlling this diversity. They hypothesize that specialized or fine-tuned models can explore higher temperatures (and thus greater diversity) without generating low-quality outputs, whereas general-purpose models require lower temperatures to maintain focus.

#### 3.2. Correlation Between Model Training and Optimal Temperature

To test their hypothesis, they conducted a two-step analysis:
- **Optimal Temperature Range Varies:** They evaluated three Mistral-7B variants (base, instruction-tuned, task-finetuned) on the MATH dataset using multi-sample aggregation. They observed that the optimal temperature range (defined as ϵ-optimal, where accuracy is within ϵ=0.02 of peak accuracy) indeed varied, with fine-tuned models favoring higher temperatures. They also noted that optimal temperature ranges stabilize beyond a sample size of 32.
- **Training-Task Similarity Correlation:** They defined a distance metric D(M, T) using the average token-level entropy of model M when generating answers for task T at a low temperature (T=0.5). Evaluating various models on MATH and MBPP datasets, they found a strong negative correlation between this distance metric (proxy for training-task similarity) and the midpoint of the optimal temperature range. This indicates that models "closer" to a task (lower entropy distance) can tolerate higher temperatures for optimal performance.

#### 3.3. Entropy-Based Automatic Temperature Selection (TURN)

The core of their proposed solution, Turning Point Temperature Selection (TURN), is built upon a novel observation: the **entropy turning point (EntP)**.
- **Entropy Spike as Quality Indicator:** The authors discovered that as temperature increases, the token-level entropy of LLM generations remains stable initially but then exhibits a sudden, dramatic spike. They hypothesize that this "entropy spike" signifies a collapse in sample quality, where the model starts generating many improper tokens. They further illustrate this by comparing entropy curves of normal sampling with constrained (greedy decoding) sampling, showing a significant gap, which implies the spike is due to a fundamental shift in the model's next-token distribution, not just temperature flattening.
- **Defining EntP:** To precisely locate this quality shift, they observe that the log-scale of the token-level entropy curve transitions from concave to convex at this point. EntP is formally defined as the temperature where the second derivative of the log entropy curve changes from negative to positive. They demonstrate a strong correlation between the accuracy at EntP and the best accuracy found through extensive grid search.
- **TURN Algorithm Steps (Algorithm 1):**
 1. **Sampling and Entropy Estimation:** For a given LLM and task, randomly select N input instances. For each instance and a range of candidate temperatures (j * t), generate a sample and compute its token-level entropy. Aggregate these to get average entropy H(j) for each temperature.
 2. **Log-Entropy Curve:** Compute the logarithm of the average entropies, ℓ(j) = log H(j).
 3. **EntP Identification:** Find the temperature j\* * t where the second derivative of ℓ(j) first becomes positive. This is the base predicted temperature (t\*).
 4. **Aggregation Adaptation:** Add an aggregation-specific adaptation factor (β_A) to t\* to get the final predicted temperature T_pred. (β_A=0 for majority voting, +0.1 for best-of-N, determined empirically on MATH and generalized).
 - **Efficiency:** For entropy estimation, they use top-K tokens (K=1000) for efficiency and found that a relatively small number of samples (e.g., 40) is sufficient for stable EntP identification.

#### 3.4. Stochastic Process Model for Interpretability

To provide a theoretical explanation for the entropy spike phenomenon, the authors developed a simplified stochastic process model. This model simulates sequential token generation where each token is classified as "proper" or "improper." It incorporates rules where generating an improper token increases the likelihood of subsequent errors (noise tolerance rate α), while generating a proper token reduces it. This model successfully reproduces the observed entropy curve behavior, showing a sharp increase in improper tokens after the EntP, thereby confirming that the entropy spike indeed signals a rapid decline in sample quality.

#### 3.5. Experiment Setup and Metrics

- **Tasks:** Math Problem Solving (MATH dataset, 200 problems, majority voting) and Code Generation (MBPP dataset, 100 problems, pass@K/best-of-N).
- **Models:** A wide array of general-purpose, domain-specific, and fine-tuned LLMs (e.g., Llama, Mistral, Llemma, Deepseek, CodeLlama, Qwen).
- **Inference:** Samples 256 times per question per temperature, using zero-shot or few-shot inference based on model tuning.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Hit Rate (HR):** Frequency of predicted temperature falling within the ϵ-optimal range (0.02 accuracy drop from peak).
 - **Temperature Gap (TG):** Absolute difference between predicted temperature and the nearest boundary of the ϵ-optimal range.
 - **Performance Drop (PD):** Accuracy loss compared to the best temperature found by exhaustive grid search.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, demonstrating the efficacy and interpretability of the proposed TURN method:

#### 4.1. Variability of Optimal Temperature

The authors empirically confirmed that the optimal sampling temperature for LLMs in multi-sample aggregation strategies varies substantially. This variation is influenced by the model's architecture, its training data (general-purpose vs. instruction-tuned vs. task-finetuned), and the specific task. For instance, fine-tuned models are capable of achieving optimal performance at higher temperatures, suggesting they can leverage greater diversity without a significant drop in quality.

#### 4.2. Strong Correlation of Entropy Turning Point (EntP) with Optimal Accuracy

A crucial discovery is the "entropy turning point" (EntP). This point, where the log-scale of the token-level entropy curve shifts from concave to convex, was found to be highly correlated with the empirically best temperature. The correlation coefficients were strikingly high: 0.9998 for the MATH dataset and 0.9913 for the MBPP dataset. This strong correlation validates EntP as a reliable intrinsic signal for identifying optimal temperatures without external labels.

#### 4.3. Performance of TURN Algorithm

- **High Accuracy in Prediction:** TURN demonstrated strong performance in automatically predicting optimal temperatures. On the MATH dataset, it achieved a Hit Rate of 12/13 models within the ϵ-optimal range, with an average temperature gap of only 0.023. On the MBPP dataset, it achieved an 11/13 Hit Rate with an average temperature gap of 0.015.
- **Minimal Performance Drop:** Compared to the peak accuracy achieved through exhaustive grid search, TURN incurred only a negligible average performance drop of 0.32% on MATH and 0.59% on MBPP. This indicates that TURN accurately identifies temperatures that yield near-optimal performance.
- **Outperformance of Fixed Baselines:** TURN consistently outperformed fixed-temperature baselines across both tasks. For example, when averaged over both tasks, TURN achieved an average performance drop of 0.4% compared to grid search, whereas the best single fixed temperature (T=0.9) resulted in a 0.9% drop, and T=0.7 resulted in a 1.15% drop. This highlights the substantial benefit of adaptive temperature selection.

#### 4.4. Efficiency of TURN

TURN proved to be highly efficient. Experiments showed that even a moderate number of samples (e.g., 40 samples per task) is sufficient for robust entropy estimation and accurate temperature prediction. With 40 samples, the variance of the predicted temperature was low (0.005), and the performance drop remained minimal (0.2%), making TURN a practical solution for real-world scenarios with limited computational budgets.

#### 4.5. Interpretability through Stochastic Process Model

The proposed stochastic process model successfully replicated the characteristic entropy spike observed in real LLMs. It further elucidated that the EntP marks the point where the percentage of "improper" tokens rapidly increases, signifying a sharp decline in sample quality beyond this temperature. This provides a theoretical underpinning for why EntP is a reliable indicator for balancing sample diversity and quality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of Large Language Models, particularly in the realm of efficient and robust inference. Its potential impact spans both practical applications and fundamental understanding:

#### 5.1. Practical Advancement in LLM Deployment

The most immediate and impactful contribution is the provision of an automated, data-agnostic method for optimizing temperature in multi-sample LLM inference. This eliminates the need for expensive, time-consuming, and often impractical manual tuning or reliance on scarce labeled validation data. For developers and researchers deploying LLMs in new domains or with custom models, TURN offers a streamlined approach to ensure optimal performance, making LLMs more accessible, scalable, and robust in real-world scenarios (e.g., intelligent agents, automated reasoning systems, scientific discovery pipelines).

#### 5.2. Enhanced Performance and Reliability of Multi-Sample Strategies

By accurately identifying near-optimal temperatures, TURN ensures that multi-sample aggregation strategies (like majority voting and best-of-N) can operate at their full potential. This leads to higher predictive accuracy and greater reliability in generative tasks, particularly those that are sensitive to the quality and diversity of generated samples, such as mathematical problem-solving and code generation.

#### 5.3. Deeper Scientific Understanding of LLM Behavior

The discovery and characterization of the "entropy turning point" (EntP) provide a novel intrinsic signal for understanding how temperature influences an LLM's generative process. Coupled with the explanatory stochastic process model, this work offers deeper insights into the dynamics of sample quality and diversity, explaining *why* certain temperatures are optimal and when an LLM's output starts to "collapse" into low-quality generations. This contributes to the broader scientific understanding of LLM mechanics beyond purely empirical observations.

#### 5.4. Foundation for Future Research and Adaptive Decoding

The entropy-based metric and the concept of EntP could serve as a foundational element for future research. It might inspire the development of other self-adaptive decoding strategies, dynamic temperature scaling methods, or even novel self-correction mechanisms that leverage intrinsic model signals rather than external supervision. The approach could also be extended to optimize other sampling hyperparameters beyond temperature.

#### 5.5. Broad Applicability

While demonstrated on mathematical reasoning and code generation, the core principles of using entropy as a proxy for sample quality and identifying a turning point are likely generalizable to a wide array of generative tasks where balancing diversity and quality is paramount. The open-sourcing of the code ("https://github.com/StigLidu/TURN") further facilitates adoption, reproducibility, and continued innovation by the broader research community.

In conclusion, this paper delivers a significant leap forward in optimizing LLM inference, moving towards more intelligent and autonomous hyperparameter management, thereby unlocking greater potential for LLMs across various critical applications.
