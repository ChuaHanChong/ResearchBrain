# Locomotion on Constrained Footholds via Layered Architectures and Model Predictive Control

> [!summary] Summary
> A layered control architecture enables legged robots to perform stable and optimal real-time locomotion on constrained footholds by combining gradient-free sampling for discrete decisions with gradient-based Model Predictive Control for continuous motion. The approach demonstrated superior reliability and optimality compared to heuristic methods in simulations and achieved smooth traversal on hardware.

## Problem

- Real-time control of legged robots for dynamic locomotion on complex terrain faces challenges due to the high-dimensional, nonlinear, and hybrid (discrete contact decisions, continuous motion) nature of the systems.
- Existing solutions either incur high computational costs (full hybrid optimization, contact-implicit MPC) or sacrifice optimality and generalizability by relying on heuristics for discrete decisions (fixed-contact MPC).
- Simultaneously optimizing both discrete contact choices (like foot placement) and continuous body movements in real-time is a significant unsolved challenge.

## Method

- A layered control architecture decouples discrete variable selection (e.g., foothold choices) from continuous control.
- Gradient-free weighted random sampling selects potential contact surfaces (polytopes) based on kinematic limits and a nominal trajectory.
- Multiple fixed-mode, full-order nonlinear ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) problems are run in parallel, each optimized with a different sampled contact sequence, and the lowest-cost solution is executed.

## Results

- The sampling-based approach reduced quadruped locomotion failures over stepping stones from **80%** (heuristic) to **20%** in simulation, while achieving a lower average cost (**0.8946** vs. **1.0963**).
- Hardware validation on a Unitree Go2 quadruped demonstrated robust and smooth traversal of physical stepping stones, indicating real-world applicability and tolerance to disturbances.
- Simulation results showed a Unitree G1 humanoid robot performing natural locomotion over complex terrain, utilizing full-body dynamics in real-time, with MPC update rates consistently below 10 ms.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Online optimization of hybrid systems (discrete contact decisions and continuous motion) can be achieved in real-time by strategically layering gradient-free sampling for discrete variables with gradient-based Model Predictive Control for continuous dynamics.
> - Decoupling the planning of discrete contact sequences from continuous trajectory optimization allows for the efficient use of different, specialized solvers, improving both computational speed and solution quality.
> - Replacing static heuristics with sampling-based discrete decision-making in real-time significantly improves the optimality and reliability of locomotion over complex terrain, reducing failure rates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Legged locomotion in robotics presents a complex control challenge due to the high-dimensional, nonlinear, and hybrid nature of these systems. The "hybrid" aspect arises from the discrete decisions involved in making and breaking contact with the environment, such as choosing footholds, combined with the continuous dynamics of the robot's motion. Solving the full nonlinear hybrid optimization problem for real-time control is often computationally prohibitive.

Existing approaches in this field include:
- **Mixed-Integer Optimization (MIO):** Methods that plan gait transitions, motions, and terrain selection simultaneously by formulating the problem with mixed-integer convex constraints. While comprehensive, these are typically too slow for real-time control.
- **Heuristic-Based Model Predictive Control (MPC):** MPC has been successful in generating dynamic motions for legged robots by fixing contact sequences. Some works augment MPC with heuristics to select terrain. These methods are computationally efficient but rely heavily on the quality and generalizability of the heuristic. If a good heuristic is unavailable or the task complexity increases (e.g., using a humanoid's arms), performance can degrade.
- **Contact Implicit MPC (CIMPC):** These approaches attempt to optimize contacts and motion simultaneously within a single, gradient-based optimization framework. Various approximations and techniques are employed, including smooth non-physical ground reaction forces or hard contacts. CIMPC methods often face challenges with numerical conditioning and typically exhibit slower solve speeds compared to fixed-contact MPC.
- **Pure Sampling-Based/Gradient-Free Optimization:** Recent research explores using gradient-free methods like Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control for full-order control. These methods can discover contact sequences automatically but have generally not been demonstrated for real-time, full-order control of humanoid robots.

This work positions itself to address the limitations of these existing methods by proposing a layered architecture. It seeks to combine the real-time computational speed and dynamic capabilities of fixed-contact MPC with the optimality and flexibility of sampling-based methods for discrete decisions. The objective is to enable dynamic locomotion over complex terrain without relying solely on heuristics or incurring the computational cost of full hybrid or contact-implicit optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a control architecture that enables legged robots to perform stable and optimal locomotion on constrained footholds in real-time. This objective is motivated by several inherent challenges in controlling legged robots:

1. **Nonlinear and High-Dimensional Dynamics:** Legged robots are inherently nonlinear systems with many degrees of freedom, making their control complex.
2. **Hybrid Nature of Locomotion:** Movement involves both continuous changes in state (joint angles, velocities) and discrete events (contact with the environment). Optimizing these discrete choices (e.g., where to step) alongside continuous movements is a major challenge for traditional optimal control methods. Numerical optimal control struggles with the combination of discrete and continuous variables.
3. **Computational Demand for Real-Time Control:** Solving comprehensive optimal control problems that account for all these factors, particularly in real-time for dynamic tasks like navigating uneven terrain, is computationally intensive and often too slow for practical application. Current full hybrid optimization or contact-implicit methods typically cannot meet real-time performance requirements.
4. **Limitations of Heuristic-Based Approaches:** While fast, methods relying on heuristics for discrete decisions (like footstep placement) lack optimality and generalizability. They may perform poorly in novel or complex scenarios where effective heuristics are difficult to design or unavailable (e.g., planning arm contacts for humanoids).
5. **Speed Limitations of Pure Sampling Methods:** Full-order sampling-based optimization methods can discover contact sequences but have not generally achieved real-time performance for humanoid robots, particularly when considering full-body dynamics.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a layered control architecture designed to:
- **Separate Discrete and Continuous Optimization:** Decouple the selection of discrete variables (e.g., foothold choices) from the continuous control problem. This allows the use of different optimization techniques for each layer.
- **Achieve Online Flexibility and Optimality:** Leverage sampling-based methods for discrete choices to explore various options and select optimal ones, without sacrificing real-time performance.
- **Maintain Real-Time Performance:** Utilize a smooth Model Predictive Controller (MPC) for the continuous dynamics, which is known for its speed when discrete variables are fixed.
- **Improve Reliability and Optimality over Heuristics:** Demonstrate a system that is more reliable and yields better performance metrics compared to common heuristic-based approaches, especially for high-speed locomotion over challenging terrain.
- **Reduce Computation Time Compared to Pure Sampling/CIMPC:** Offer a faster alternative to full-order sampling-based methods and Contact Implicit MPC, making real-time control of humanoids feasible.
- **Enhance Generalizability:** Avoid the need for task-specific heuristics, allowing the controller to generalize to diverse locomotion and manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology employs a layered control architecture that separates the optimization of discrete variables from continuous control, leveraging both gradient-free and gradient-based methods.

#### 3.1. Layered Control Architecture Overview

The architecture consists of two main components:
1. **Gradient-Free Discrete Variable Selection:** A sampling-based method is used to choose discrete variables, specifically terrain surfaces for foot placement.
2. **Smooth Model Predictive Control (MPC):** A full-order nonlinear MPC controller receives these fixed discrete variables and computes feedforward torques and PD setpoints for continuous robot motion.

The process involves:
- Receiving velocity commands from the user, which inform the sampling process and MPC cost.
- Sampling a set of potential contact surfaces (polytopes) based on the robot's nominal trajectory and kinematic limits.
- Solving multiple fixed-mode MPC problems in parallel, each corresponding to a different sampled set of polytopes.
- Selecting the MPC solution with the lowest cost.
- Outputting feedforward torques, joint configurations, and joint velocities from the selected MPC.
- A state estimator provides the current state at 500 Hz.

#### 3.2. Fixed-Mode MPC Formulation

Once the discrete variables (e.g., terrain to step on) are fixed, the MPC problem is formulated as a classical smooth Nonlinear Program (NLP):

**Objective Function:** Minimize a cost function `J(x, u)` that includes terms for state tracking/regularization, input regularization, end-effector tracking, and a terminal cost. Desired quantities are denoted with hats (`ˆ·`), and `W` represents weights. The cost function involves nonlinear terms, particularly for quaternion subtraction (on the tangent space) and forward kinematics. A Gauss-Newton Hessian approximation is used for linearization.

**Constraints:**
- **Dynamics Constraints:** The system dynamics `x_{k+1} = g(x_k, u_k)` are enforced. Inverse dynamics (`τ_k = f(q_k, v_k, a_k, F_k)`) are used and linearized. State variables (`x_k`) and input variables (`u_k`) are defined relative to the current trajectory (δq, δv, δτ, δF) to enable optimization on the tangent space of the orientation manifold, avoiding Euler angle singularities. The update rules for δv_{k+1} and δq_{k+1} are derived from linearizing the inverse dynamics and configuration integration, respectively.
- **State and Input Box Constraints:** Box constraints are applied to actuated joint configurations (`q_j`), velocities (`v_j`), joint torques (`τ`), and ground reaction forces (`F`). Ground reaction forces are multiplied by a contact binary (`γ_l^k`) to zero them during swing phase. A non-zero minimum ground reaction force is used to prevent slippage.
- **Friction Cone Constraints:** Linearized friction cone constraints are applied to end-effectors in contact, relating tangential and normal forces (`|F_x|, |F_y| ≤ μF_z`).
- **Collision Constraints:** Configuration-only constraints derived from pairs of spheres on the robot are used to prevent self-collisions or collisions with the environment. These are linearized from their nonlinear form.
- **Foot Placement Constraints:** To ensure stepping on desired terrain and avoiding gaps, steppable regions are modeled as polytopes (`{y | Ay ≤ b}`). The end-effector position is constrained to be within the chosen polytope during stance (`γ_l^k A_l^k r_l(q_k) ≤ γ_l^k b_l^k`).
- **Swing Height Equality Constraint:** For feet in swing phase, the z-height of each foot is constrained to a desired swing height (`r_{l,z}(q_k) = z_k`).
- **No-Slip Equality Constraint:** For end-effectors in contact, a no-slip constraint is applied, ensuring zero velocity at the contact point (`γ_l^k r'_l(q_k, v_k) = 0`, where `r'_l` denotes end-effector velocity).

**Solver and Approximation:**
The problem is solved using Real-Time Iterations (RTI), where only a single Quadratic Program (QP) iteration is performed at each control step. This QP is an LQ approximation generated by linearizing about the current state and input trajectory. The HPIPM solver, which requires an optimal control block structure, is used for the QP.

#### 3.3. Sampling for Gradient-Free Decision Making

This module selects the discrete variables, specifically which pieces of terrain (polytopes) to step on, as these lack gradients for smooth optimization.
- **Process:** Given a desired velocity, the robot's torso is propagated forward. A nominal foot location (e.g., below the hip at mid-stance) is identified. If this location is not over terrain, sampling proceeds. If it is, the terrain at that location is used for that foot to reduce the sample space.
- **Sample Tree:** When multiple legs have the same contact phases, their sampling is considered together using a "sample tree." Nodes in the tree represent discrete terrain pieces. A branch corresponds to a unique terrain selection combination for a set of feet.
- **Kinematic Limits:** To avoid kinematically infeasible samples, a circle approximating the robot's kinematic limits is drawn around the nominal foot location in the x-y plane. Only terrain pieces with non-zero intersection area within this circle are considered as nodes.
- **Weighted Random Sampling:** Branches in the sample tree are chosen randomly but weighted by the area of the terrain within the kinematic limit. This encourages sampling larger, more accessible terrain options. The sampled branch's polytopes are then passed to parallel MPC instances, and the branch is removed to prevent re-sampling.

#### 3.4. Implementation Details

- **Software:** Implemented in C++ using HPIPM for the QP solver, Pinocchio for rigid body dynamics, and CppADCodegen for derivatives.
- **Parallelization:** The fixed-mode MPC is single-threaded, allowing parallel execution for each sample using OpenMP.
- **Control Loop:** The MPC runs at 100 Hz (10 ms period). The control process is split into a preparation phase (sampling, linearization for nodes > 0, contact schedule update) and a feedback phase (linearization for node 0, MPC solution computation). The feedback phase is designed to be very fast.
- **Output and Delay Compensation:** Feedforward torques and PD setpoints are output to the robot at 1 kHz by linearly interpolating the MPC output trajectory. This includes delay compensation by using the interpolated action corresponding to the current time since state measurement.
- **Robustness Measures:**
 - Polytopes are shrunk in the constraints to guide feet away from edges, with care taken to avoid infeasibility if a foot is already in contact outside the shrunk polytope.
 - The swing height constraint is not enforced until the third node to allow the foot to reach the desired height gradually.
 - The QP solver (HPIPM) is run for a fixed, limited number of iterations (10 for quadruped, 8 for humanoid) in "speed" mode, providing usable results even without full convergence.
- **Discretization:** Non-uniform time discretization is used, with finer steps for the initial nodes (first 5 nodes at 0.015s or 0.025s) and coarser steps for later nodes (last 20 nodes at 0.035s).
- **Cost Function Heuristics:** The Raibert heuristic is used in the cost function to guide end-effector positions, increasing robustness.
- **Robot Models:**
 - **Unitree Go2 (Quadruped):** 12 actuators, 18 DoF, 1 contact point per foot (12 force variables).
 - **Unitree G1 (Humanoid):** 27 actuators, 33 DoF. Optimization is performed over 19 actuators, with ankle roll and wrist joints fixed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed layered control architecture was evaluated through both simulation and hardware experiments.

#### 4.1. Simulation Comparison: Sampling Method vs. Raibert Heuristic

- **Experiment Setup:** A Unitree Go2 quadruped was tasked with traversing stepping stones of varying heights and gaps at a speed of 0.7 m/s in Mujoco simulation. The sampling method used four parallel MPC instances, while the Raibert heuristic (a common approach for terrain selection) used a single MPC instance. Five trials were conducted for each method.
- **Results:**
 - The sampling method demonstrated significantly improved performance and reliability. The Raibert heuristic failed to reach the final platform in 80% of its trials.
 - In contrast, the sampling method failed only once out of five trials.
 - Quantitatively, for successful runs, the sampling method achieved a lower average cost (0.8946) compared to the heuristic (1.0963), indicating superior optimality.
 - **Conclusion:** The layered architecture with sampling for terrain selection is more optimal and reliable than approaches relying solely on heuristics, especially at higher speeds.

#### 4.2. Hardware Experiments: Unitree Go2 Quadruped

- **Experiment Setup:** The Unitree Go2 quadruped was tested on physical terrain consisting of two large tiles separated by three smaller cinder blocks, requiring gap traversal. Four parallel MPC samples were used.
- **Results:**
 - The quadruped successfully traversed the terrain smoothly, demonstrating the controller's ability to operate in the real world.
 - The hardware experiments showed robustness to real-world disturbances, including the slight wobbling of the wooden platforms and the unmodeled weight of an onboard lidar unit (265 g).
 - **Conclusion:** The controller is effective and robust enough for real-world deployment on quadrupedal robots, handling model-mismatches and external disturbances.

#### 4.3. Simulation Experiments: Unitree G1 Humanoid

- **Experiment Setup:** A Unitree G1 humanoid robot was simulated traversing stepping stones with varying heights and gaps. Two parallel MPC samples were used.
- **Results:**
 - The humanoid robot traversed the challenging terrain naturally.
 - The optimization of full-body dynamics (including arms and waist) was evident, as the robot utilized these to maintain torso stability while the legs moved rapidly to cross gaps.
 - **Conclusion:** The proposed controller is capable of controlling a full humanoid robot in real-time, even with complex full-body dynamics and varying terrain.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents a layered control architecture that addresses a fundamental challenge in legged robotics: achieving real-time, optimal, and robust locomotion over complex terrain by effectively managing the hybrid nature of robot-environment interaction.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
- **Real-Time Optimization of Discrete and Continuous Variables:** The architecture effectively combines gradient-free sampling for discrete choices (like foothold selection) with gradient-based Model Predictive Control for continuous motion. This allows for online optimization of both types of variables simultaneously, which is a significant advancement for real-time control of hybrid systems.
- **Improved Performance over Heuristics:** The demonstrated superior optimality and reliability compared to heuristic-based methods highlights its potential for more robust and agile locomotion, particularly in challenging and unknown environments where predefined rules might fail.
- **Computational Efficiency:** By preserving the speed of fixed-mode MPC while incorporating sampling-based decision-making, the approach achieves real-time performance for both quadrupedal and humanoid robots. This makes it a viable alternative to computationally intensive full hybrid optimization or Contact Implicit MPC methods, and faster than full-order sampling techniques.
- **Generalizability:** The reduction in reliance on specific heuristics suggests the controller can generalize to a broader range of tasks beyond stepping stones, potentially extending to complex manipulation scenarios or environments requiring diverse contact interactions (e.g., bracing with hands).
- **Hardware Validation:** Successful deployment on a physical quadruped robot demonstrates the practical applicability and robustness of the controller against real-world disturbances and model inaccuracies.

**Potential Impact:**
The methodology could have a notable impact on the development of highly autonomous and agile legged robots capable of operating in unstructured, human-centric environments. Specific areas of impact include:
- **Disaster Response and Exploration:** Robots equipped with this control strategy could more effectively navigate debris fields, uneven terrain, or complex industrial sites, aiding in search-and-rescue or inspection tasks.
- **Human-Robot Collaboration:** Humanoid robots could perform more sophisticated tasks requiring adaptive locomotion and interaction with various surfaces, such as navigating a construction site or assisting in logistics.
- **Advancements in Control Theory:** The work contributes to the understanding of how to effectively integrate different optimization paradigms (gradient-free and gradient-based) to solve complex hybrid control problems in robotics.

**Future Work as Identified by Authors:**
The authors propose several directions for future research:
- **Broader Task Application:** Applying the architecture to other hybrid tasks, such as using a robot's hand for bracing in push recovery, or optimizing the contact schedule (beyond just terrain selection).
- **Sampling Algorithm Experimentation:** Exploring variations in the sampling algorithm, such as using a full-order optimizer like MPPI in the sampling layer to extract contact information.
- **Interaction with Real-Time Iterations:** Further investigation into how the Real-Time Iterations paradigm, particularly with incomplete QP solves, interfaces with the dynamically changing constraints dictated by the sampling process. This could lead to sampling methods specifically designed to leverage this computational paradigm.
