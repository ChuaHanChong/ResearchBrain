# What Are We Actually Benchmarking in Robot Manipulation?

> [!summary] Summary
> An audit of five prominent robot manipulation benchmarks reveals significant limitations, including policies achieving high scores via shortcuts, a lack of statistical significance in many reported gains, and policies overfitting to narrow test distributions or relying on data-source proximity. The study provides diagnostic tools to enhance the rigor of benchmark design and interpretation in the field.

## Problem

- Benchmark scores are frequently interpreted as direct indicators of general robot manipulation capabilities, implicitly defining the field's progress.
- Current robot manipulation benchmarks may allow policies to achieve high scores without possessing the underlying capabilities they are intended to measure, such as language understanding or broad generalization.
- Many reported performance gains between policies on benchmarks often lack provable statistical significance, leading to an unclear understanding of genuine scientific advancement.

## Method

- The research defines and applies four diagnostic tests to audit benchmark validity: shortcut solvability, statistical significance, creeping overfitting, and data-source dependence.
- These diagnostics are thoroughly applied to five widely reported manipulation benchmarks: LIBERO, CALVIN, SimplerEnv, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin 2.0.
- The authors commit to releasing reference implementations of the diagnostics to foster more robust benchmark design and more accurate interpretation of reported results.

## Results

- The DINO+MLP probe achieved **99.0%** on LIBERO Spatial, **100.0%** on Object, **98.8%** on Goal, and **92.4%** on Long suites, demonstrating that high scores can be obtained without extensive pretraining or language understanding.
- Only **19.8%** of reported SOTA claims on LIBERO and **19.7%** on SimplerEnv were provably statistically significant, with a large fraction remaining inconclusive.
- Resampling block poses in CALVIN (ABC→D protocol) led to Average Tasks Completed (ATC) drops of up to 1.03 for the X-VLA policy, indicating policies overfit to specific test-time distributions.
- A small (22M parameter) policy trained on 120 local scripted demonstrations in SimplerEnv achieved **94.8%** success, nearly matching the **95.8%** of a 0.9B foundation VLA trained on diverse real-world data, revealing data-source dependence.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A small, language-agnostic probe (0.09B-parameter DINO+MLP) achieved performance at or near SOTA on LIBERO, demonstrating significant shortcut solvability on this benchmark.
> - A substantial proportion of reported SOTA claims across benchmarks like LIBERO (19.8%) and SimplerEnv (19.7%) lacked provable statistical significance, highlighting an issue with current reporting practices.
> - Policies exhibited susceptibility to distribution overfitting on CALVIN and SimplerEnv, with performance drops when test conditions varied within the training distribution, even with small changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The widespread adoption of benchmarks in robotics, particularly in manipulation, has led to benchmark scores being frequently interpreted as indicators of general robot capabilities and, by extension, defining the field's progress. This paper contributes to a critical self-assessment within the robotics community regarding the validity of these benchmarks.

This work draws direct parallels from a rich existing literature on auditing benchmark validity in other artificial intelligence domains, specifically computer vision (CV) and natural language processing (NLP). The four failure modes identified and investigated in this paper—shortcut solvability, statistical significance, creeping overfitting, and data-source dependence—have established precedents in CV and NLP benchmark audits. For instance, shortcut solvability builds on findings in Visual Question Answering (VQA) and image classification where models exploited superficial cues rather than genuine understanding. Statistical significance audits in CV have shown that reported gains often lack statistical rigor. Creeping overfitting, where models become overly adapted to benchmark idiosyncrasies, has been documented across various vision datasets. Data-source dependence, reflecting training-test proximity rather than generalization, is also a recognized issue in NLP and CV benchmarks.

While prior work has audited specific manipulation benchmarks (e.g., LIBERO-Pro and LIBERO-Plus modifying LIBERO scenes and instructions), these often focused on out-of-distribution changes to test generalization. In contrast, the current paper's approach to creeping overfitting, for example, examines policy performance under variations that remain *within* the training distribution, thereby scrutinizing the benchmark's definition of generalization rather than simply testing its limits.

The paper focuses its audit on five widely reported manipulation benchmarks: LIBERO, CALVIN, SimplerEnv, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin 2.0. This selection positions the work to address concerns within the most active areas of generalist policy and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model development. By providing diagnostics and concrete examples of benchmark limitations, this research aims to elevate the methodological rigor of benchmark design and interpretation in robot manipulation, fostering more meaningful definitions of progress.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to critically evaluate the validity of prominent robot manipulation benchmarks as reliable proxies for general manipulation capabilities. The authors aim to understand whether a high score on these benchmarks genuinely indicates that a robot possesses the broader skills desired for real-world tasks.

This objective is motivated by several factors:
- **High Benchmark Reliance as Progress Indicator:** Benchmarks like LIBERO are frequently cited in a high volume of research papers (e.g., 79 arXiv papers in March 2026), leading their scores to implicitly define what constitutes "progress" in the field. The authors highlight that a benchmark score is not an end in itself, but rather a proxy for general capabilities such as home tidying or assisting the elderly. It is therefore crucial to ascertain if these proxies are meaningful.
- **Impracticality of Ideal Evaluation:** The ideal method for validating benchmarks—training policies on shared demonstrations, evaluating them on a comprehensive set of real-world tasks, and comparing rankings—is deemed impractical due to the absence of an agreed-upon real-world task set and the prohibitive cost of retraining numerous policies. An alternative approach of checking consistency across benchmarks is also dismissed, as authors typically tune methods to achieve state-of-the-art (SOTA) results on all reported benchmarks, obscuring genuine cross-benchmark generalization.
- **Identification of Failure Modes:** The authors motivate the need for new diagnostic tools by identifying four specific "failure modes" that can weaken or invalidate a benchmark's role as a proxy for general capability. These are:
 - **Shortcut Solvability:** Where a policy can achieve a high score without possessing the underlying capability the benchmark intends to measure.
 - **Lack of Statistical Significance:** Where reported performance gains between policies are not demonstrably significant beyond random variation.
 - **Creeping Overfitting:** Where policies become over-adapted to the narrow test distribution or specific test samples of a benchmark, failing to generalize broadly.
 - **Data-Source Dependence:** Where benchmark scores might reflect how closely training data aligns with the test distribution, rather than a policy's ability to generalize across a data gap.
- **Provision of Practical Tools:** Recognizing the identified issues, the authors are motivated to develop and release four practical diagnostics, one for each failure mode. The intention is for these tools to be utilized by benchmark authors before publication and by reviewers and researchers to interpret reported gains more accurately. This aims to foster a more robust and transparent evaluation ecosystem in robot manipulation.

In essence, the work seeks to provide a foundational critique of current benchmarking practices and offer concrete, actionable improvements to ensure that reported successes genuinely reflect advances in general robot manipulation capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research methodology involves defining and applying four diagnostic tests to five widely reported robot manipulation benchmarks. The diagnostics target specific failure modes that can undermine a benchmark's validity.

**A. Four Diagnostics:**

1. **Shortcut Solvability:**
 - **Objective:** To determine if a high benchmark score can be achieved by a policy that lacks the presumed general capabilities (e.g., language understanding or extensive pretraining) typically attributed to top-performing models.
 - **Approach:** A deliberately small "probe" model was constructed. This probe consists of a DINOv2 image encoder (ViT-B/14 backbone for most benchmarks, ViT-S/14 for RoboCasa) and a small MLP head, with approximately 90 million parameters. Crucially, it lacked a language encoder, large-scale robotics pretraining, or an expressive action head. Instead of processing language instructions, the probe used a learned, per-task embedding looked up by index. For CALVIN, several phrasings of a subtask mapped to the same embedding. The probe used standard policy interfaces for visual inputs and proprioception.
 - **Application:** The probe was trained and tuned separately for each suite or protocol of LIBERO, CALVIN, SimplerEnv, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin 2.0. The best performing checkpoint from a small grid search was reported.
 - **Benchmarks Audited:** LIBERO, CALVIN, SimplerEnv, RoboCasa, RoboTwin 2.0.

2. **Statistical Significance:**
 - **Objective:** To assess whether reported performance gains between new policies and previous state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods are statistically significant, rather than merely reflecting noise.
 - **Approach:** The authors developed a method to determine the statistical significance of performance gaps primarily from aggregate scores, as full paired outcome data is often unavailable. They used a one-sided paired Wald test with a task-stratified variance estimator, assuming a benchmark with `T` tasks and `S` paired samples per task. Two cutoffs were derived for the reported empirical gap:
 - **Rejection-feasibility threshold (δ∃):** Below this threshold, no compatible paired outcome table can reject the null hypothesis of no improvement.
 - **Rejection-guarantee threshold (δ∀):** Above this threshold, every compatible paired outcome table rejects the null hypothesis.
 - **Application:** This analysis was applied to SOTA claims across the audited benchmarks by categorizing each previous-best-to-new comparison into "No improvement," "Provably not significant," "Inconclusive," or "Provably significant."
 - **Benchmarks Audited:** LIBERO, SimplerEnv, RoboCasa, RoboTwin 2.0.

3. **Creeping Overfitting:**
 - **Objective:** To investigate if policies overfit to a benchmark's narrow test distribution (distribution overfitting) or its fixed set of test samples (sample overfitting).
 - **Approach (Distribution Overfitting):**
 - **CALVIN (ABC→D protocol):** Policies are typically trained on diverse block poses but evaluated on fixed poses in scene D. The authors resampled block poses *within the training range* of scene D, holding other factors constant, to see if performance dropped.
 - **SimplerEnv (WidowX stack task):** Three in-distribution changes were introduced: reversed language instruction, stacking target blocks on support blocks instead of the table, and randomizing block positions and initial arm poses. These conditions were confirmed to be present in the original BridgeData V2 training set.
 - **Approach (Sample Overfitting):**
 - **LIBERO:** The fixed initial-state file was redrawn from the same generator.
 - **CALVIN:** Two fresh sequence manifests were drawn from the official generator.
 - **Application:** Performance drops against a matched local calibration (rerun on the official protocol on the same hardware) were measured and analyzed using confidence intervals appropriate for success rates or average tasks completed.
 - **Benchmarks Audited:** CALVIN, SimplerEnv, LIBERO.

4. **Data-Source Dependence:**
 - **Objective:** To determine if a high benchmark score primarily reflects a policy's ability to generalize across a substantial training-test data gap, or if it can be achieved by training on data closely aligned with the test distribution.
 - **Approach:** This diagnostic was specifically applied to SimplerEnv, as it involves evaluating policies in a simulated WidowX setup using training data from real-world BridgeData V2 demonstrations, creating a perceived data gap. The authors trained small (22 million parameter), task-specific DINOv2-S + MLP behavior-cloning policies. These policies were trained on 120 *scripted expert demonstrations* generated in the *same simulator* as the official test, with small perturbations around the official evaluation grid.
 - **Application:** The performance of these small, locally-trained policies was compared against the reported SOTA of a much larger (0.9B parameter) foundation VLA, which was trained on distant real-world data.
 - **Benchmarks Audited:** SimplerEnv.

**B. Benchmark Selection and Tool Release:**
The study focused on LIBERO, CALVIN, SimplerEnv, RoboCasa, and RoboTwin 2.0 due to their high frequency in recent VLA and generalist-policy publications. The authors committed to releasing reference implementations of all four diagnostics to facilitate their adoption by benchmark creators and evaluators.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The audit revealed varying degrees of benchmark validity across the five tested platforms, with the most frequently reported benchmarks often exhibiting more pronounced issues.

**A. Shortcut Solvability:**
- **LIBERO:** The 0.09B-parameter DINO+MLP probe, which lacked a language encoder or large-scale robotics pretraining, achieved scores at or near the best reported results for most LIBERO suites. Specifically, it scored 99.0% on Spatial (vs. 99.8% best reported), 100.0% on Object (vs. 100.0%), 98.8% on Goal (vs. 99.5%), and 92.4% on Long (vs. 98.8%). This indicates that a high score on LIBERO does not necessarily imply language understanding or broad generalization.
- **CALVIN:** The probe achieved performance comparable to widely-used policies (e.g., 3.242 ATC on ABC→D vs. 2.48 for RoboFlamingo) but did not match the current overall best (e.g., 4.78 for MMaDA-VLA). This suggests a smaller, but still present, shortcut.
- **RoboTwin 2.0, RoboCasa, and SimplerEnv:** The probe scored significantly lower than the best reported results, indicating a lack of such a shortcut on these benchmarks (e.g., 60.4% on RoboTwin 2.0 Clean vs. 95.8% best, 18.8% on RoboCasa RSS24 vs. 79.2% best, 0.0% on SimplerEnv WidowX vs. 97.9% best).

**B. Statistical Significance:**
- The proportion of SOTA claims that were provably statistically significant varied considerably across benchmarks:
 - **LIBERO:** Only 19.8% of reported gains were provably statistically significant (out of 789 comparisons).
 - **SimplerEnv:** Only 19.7% were provably statistically significant (out of 122 comparisons).
 - **RoboCasa:** 53.3% were provably statistically significant (out of 30 comparisons).
 - **RoboTwin 2.0:** 73.7% were provably statistically significant (out of 19 comparisons).
- A substantial portion of reported gains on LIBERO (20.8%) and SimplerEnv (50.8%) were inconclusive, meaning the aggregate scores alone could not determine significance.

**C. Creeping Overfitting:**
- **Distribution Overfitting (CALVIN):** Resampling block poses within the training distribution for the CALVIN ABC→D protocol resulted in performance drops for all tested policies. Average Tasks Completed (ATC) dropped by 1.03 for X-VLA (from 4.17 to 3.14), 0.75 for GR-1 (from 3.24 to 2.50), and 0.50 for RoboFlamingo (from 2.37 to 1.87). The 5/5-chain success rate dropped by as much as 25% for X-VLA.
- **Distribution Overfitting (SimplerEnv):** In-distribution changes to the WidowX stack task impacted policy performance. For example:
 - Reversed language (yellow on green) caused drops for CogACT-Base (+11.11 percentage points, where positive means altered test scored lower) and InternVLA-M1 (+9.38 p.p.), while Dexbotic showed a slight improvement (-2.78 p.p.).
 - Stacked support blocks led to significant drops for higher-scoring policies like X-VLA-WidowX (+28.47 p.p.) and Dexbotic (+17.36 p.p.), suggesting policies may assume blocks are on the table.
 - Randomized block poses and arm positions also caused drops for most policies (e.g., CogACT-Base +10.07 p.p.).
- **Sample Overfitting (LIBERO & CALVIN):** Redrawing the initial state file on LIBERO and fresh sequence manifests on CALVIN resulted in small changes (less than one percentage point for LIBERO success rate, and at most 0.11 ATC for CALVIN), suggesting that policies generally do not overfit to the exact fixed samples but rather to the underlying, narrow distribution.

**D. Data-Source Dependence:**
- **SimplerEnv:** A small (22M parameter) task-specific policy, trained on 120 *scripted expert demonstrations* collected in the *same simulated environment* as the test, achieved 94.8% success (91/96 tasks completed). This performance nearly matched the 95.8% (92/96) reported by X-VLA, a 0.9B foundation VLA trained on diverse real-world BridgeData V2 demonstrations. This demonstrates that a high score on SimplerEnv can be achieved by eliminating the training-test data gap rather than genuinely generalizing across it, making the score alone an ambiguous indicator of capability transfer.

In summary, the audit indicates that benchmarks like LIBERO and CALVIN are susceptible to shortcuts and distribution overfitting, while many reported gains across several benchmarks lack provable statistical significance. SimplerEnv's scores are also found to be heavily influenced by the proximity of training data to the test environment, rather than purely reflecting generalization capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work holds significant implications for the design, evaluation, and interpretation of progress in robot manipulation research.

**A. Re-evaluation of Progress Claims:**
The most immediate impact is a call to critically re-evaluate progress claims made based on benchmark scores. The findings demonstrate that high scores on widely used benchmarks do not necessarily correlate with general manipulation capabilities or significant advancements. For instance, the discovery of shortcut solvability on LIBERO means that a high score on this benchmark, by itself, is not sufficient evidence of a policy's broad generalization or language understanding. The prevalence of statistically inconclusive or insignificant gains further suggests that many reported "improvements" may not represent robust scientific progress.

**B. Guidance for Future Benchmark Design:**
The paper provides concrete design goals for future manipulation benchmarks:
- **Broad Test Distributions:** Benchmarks should incorporate broad variation in objects, scenes, and environmental conditions. This would make it harder for brittle policies to achieve high scores without genuine generalization, addressing shortcut solvability and creeping overfitting concerns. The contrast between LIBERO/CALVIN (narrower) and RoboTwin 2.0 (broader variation) highlights this.
- **Robust Difficulty:** The difficulty of a benchmark should stem from the inherent complexity and variability of tasks, rather than from an artificial gap between training and test data. The SimplerEnv data-source dependence finding shows how this gap can be bypassed, rendering scores ambiguous.
- **Statistical Rigor in Protocols:** Benchmark protocols should explicitly define what constitutes a statistically significant improvement. They should also mandate the release of per-instance outcomes, not just aggregate scores, to enable thorough statistical analysis (e.g., paired tests), thereby reducing the inconclusive zone for reported gains.

**C. Tools for Authors and Reviewers:**
The release of reference implementations for the four diagnostics empowers both benchmark creators and researchers.
- **For Benchmark Creators:** New benchmarks can be vetted using these diagnostics before publication, leading to more robust and meaningful evaluation platforms.
- **For Authors:** Researchers can use this paper to argue against being required to report on benchmarks proven to have validity issues, or to contextualize their results by demonstrating that their method genuinely addresses the benchmark's underlying challenges.
- **For Reviewers:** Reviewers gain a structured framework to scrutinize claimed advancements, particularly for small gains on potentially flawed benchmarks. This can contribute to a higher standard of peer review in robotics.

**D. Advancement Beyond Fixed-Population Averages:**
The paper also initiates discussion on moving beyond fixed-population average scores, proposing alternative evaluation paradigms such as item response theory and adaptive testing. These approaches could address multiple validity concerns—creeping overfitting, data-source dependence, and statistical power—more holistically by modeling policy effectiveness relative to instance difficulty and adaptively selecting test instances.

In conclusion, this research is a foundational contribution to improving the scientific rigor and interpretability of robot manipulation benchmarks. By systematically identifying and quantifying limitations in current evaluation practices, it pushes the field towards more meaningful metrics of robot capability and more robust methodologies for assessing scientific progress.
