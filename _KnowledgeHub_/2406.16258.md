# MEReQ: Max-Ent Residual-Q Inverse RL for Sample-Efficient Alignment from Intervention

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed MEREQ, an algorithm that leverages residual reward inference and Residual Q-Learning to efficiently align robot policies with human preferences using fewer interventions. It reduced intervention samples by over 50% in tasks like Highway-Sim compared to baselines, showing improved stability and closer expert behavior alignment.

## Problem

- Interactive imitation learning from human interventions is often sample-inefficient, requiring a large number of human corrections.
- Behavior Cloning (BC) from interventions disregards sequential decision-making, leading to compounded errors and catastrophic forgetting of prior knowledge.
- Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) methods typically treat prior policies as mere initialization, failing to efficiently leverage their embedded knowledge for fine-tuning.

## Method

- **MEREQ** combines ==Maximum-Entropy Inverse Reinforcement Learning (MaxEnt-IRL)== with ==Residual Q-Learning (RQL)== to learn from human interventions.
- It infers a ==residual reward function== (r_R) that models only the discrepancy between the prior policy's implicit reward and the human expert's true preferences.
- The algorithm integrates ==pseudo-expert trajectories== from non-intervened policy executions to enhance training stability and reduce variance during learning.

## Results

- MEREQ consistently achieved higher sample efficiency, requiring significantly fewer human intervention samples (e.g., **1819** vs **4330** in Highway-Sim) compared to baselines to align policies.
- The algorithm demonstrated improved training stability and lower variance across trials in both simulated and real-world environments.
- MEREQ policies exhibited closer alignment to expert feature and reward distributions in synthesized expert tasks and successfully completed complex real-world tasks with reduced human cognitive effort.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning only the residual reward function (the discrepancy between the prior policy's implicit reward and human preference) is significantly more sample-efficient than inferring the entire expert reward.
> - Explicitly leveraging the prior policy's knowledge through residual reward inference and Residual Q-Learning (RQL) mitigates catastrophic forgetting and provides sustained performance benefits.
> - Incorporating "pseudo-expert trajectories" from uncorrected policy executions improves training stability and reduces variance, especially when human intervention data is sparse.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of embodied artificial intelligence (AI) agents capable of performing complex real-world tasks has increased the necessity for these agents to operate safely and effectively in human-centered environments. A critical aspect of this deployment involves aligning robot behavior with human preferences. This research is situated within the field of interactive imitation learning (IL), which addresses the challenge of enabling a pre-trained policy to adapt its behavior through continuous human feedback.

Existing approaches to interactive imitation learning from human intervention, where an expert provides corrective actions when a policy deviates from preferred behavior, often encounter limitations. Direct application of behavior cloning (BC) to intervention data, for instance, can lead to compounded errors due to its disregard for the sequential nature of decision-making. Furthermore, such methods can suffer from catastrophic forgetting, as policies are fine-tuned exclusively on intervention data, leading to a neglect of the valuable information contained within the initial prior policy.

Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) offers an alternative framework by inferring the underlying reward function that explains observed expert behavior, thereby inherently accounting for sequential decision-making. Maximum-Entropy IRL (MaxEnt-IRL) further refines this by modeling human sub-optimality through a preference for stochastic policies. However, direct application of MaxEnt-IRL for fine-tuning a prior policy often treats the prior policy merely as an initialization, failing to efficiently leverage its embedded knowledge to reduce the required amount of intervention data.

This paper addresses this specific challenge of sample inefficiency in interactive imitation learning from human intervention by proposing a method that more effectively integrates the prior policy's knowledge. It diverges from approaches that infer the complete human reward function by instead focusing on the *discrepancy* between the prior policy's implicit reward and the human expert's reward. This approach seeks to improve sample efficiency in policy alignment, thereby reducing the cognitive load and data requirements from human supervisors during the robot's learning process. The work builds upon foundational concepts such as Residual Q-Learning (RQL) for policy customization and MaxEnt-IRL for reward inference, adapting them for the specific context of learning from interventions with a prior policy. It also relates to prior work on learning residual policies, such as TRANSIC, but focuses on reward mismatch rather than sim-to-real gaps.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to achieve sample-efficient alignment of robot behavior with human preferences through interactive imitation learning from human intervention. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations and broken down into specific technical objectives:

- **Motivation for Alignment:** Deploying embodied AI agents in human-centered environments necessitates that their behavior aligns with human preferences. This ensures safety, usability, and acceptance of robotic systems.
- **Motivation for Interactive Learning from Intervention:** Human intervention serves as a direct and intuitive form of feedback. When a robot deviates from preferred behavior, an expert can take control, providing immediate, context-rich corrective demonstrations. This interactive paradigm is essential for fine-tuning pre-trained policies in dynamic, real-world scenarios where predefined reward functions may be incomplete or misaligned with actual human preferences.
- **Addressing Sample Inefficiency:** A significant challenge in current interactive imitation learning, particularly from interventions, is the high number of human interventions required to achieve satisfactory policy alignment. Existing methods often fail to adequately leverage the valuable information contained within a pre-trained "prior policy."
 - Methods like behavior cloning (BC) from interventions neglect the sequential nature of decision-making and can lead to catastrophic forgetting, where the fine-tuned policy loses abilities learned by the prior policy.
 - Even Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL), while accounting for sequential decisions, typically infers a full reward function from scratch or uses the prior policy only as an initialization, thereby not fully exploiting its embedded knowledge. This makes IRL-based approaches also sample-inefficient in a fine-tuning context.
- **Core Technical Objective: Inferring Residual Reward Functions:** The central motivation behind MEREQ is the hypothesis that aligning a prior policy with human preferences does not require inferring the *entire* human expert's reward function. Instead, it is more efficient to identify and learn only the *discrepancy* between the prior policy's underlying reward and the human expert's true reward. This discrepancy is formalized as a "residual reward function." The assumption is that much of the prior policy's reward function is already aligned with human preferences, and only the missing or differing components need to be learned.
- **Core Technical Objective: Integrating Residual Q-Learning:** Once the residual reward function is inferred, the objective is to leverage this information to update the prior policy effectively. Residual Q-Learning (RQL) is adopted for this purpose, as it is specifically designed to customize a policy towards additional task objectives (represented by the residual reward) without requiring knowledge of the original reward function of the prior policy.
- **Minimizing Human Effort:** Ultimately, the motivation is to develop an algorithm that minimizes the number of intervention samples and the overall cognitive cost to the human expert during the alignment process. This is quantified by the intervention rate and the total number of expert samples required.

In summary, MEREQ aims to overcome the sample inefficiency of existing interactive learning from intervention methods by focusing on learning what is *new* or *different* in human preferences compared to a prior policy, rather than relearning everything.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Maximum-Entropy Residual-Q Inverse Reinforcement Learning (MEREQ) algorithm is designed for sample-efficient policy alignment from human intervention by combining elements of Residual Q-Learning (RQL) and Maximum-Entropy Inverse Reinforcement Learning (MaxEnt-IRL).

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem focuses on aligning a given prior policy `π` (assumed to be a max-ent optimal policy for an unknown reward function `r`) with a human expert's preferences. The human expert possesses an internal reward function `r_expert` that differs from `r`. The expert observes `π`'s execution and intervenes when behavior deviates from their preference. The goal is to infer `r_expert` and learn a policy `ˆπ` that is max-ent optimal with respect to `r_expert`, while minimizing human intervention samples. The approach assumes access to a simulator for policy updates. Policy performance is evaluated by the human intervention rate.

#### 3.2. Max-Ent Residual Reward Inference

Instead of inferring the full `r_expert`, MEREQ posits that `r_expert` can be decomposed as `r_expert = r + r_R`, where `r_R` is a residual reward function capturing the difference in preferences. `r_R` is modeled as a linear combination of a feature set `f_R` with weights `θ_R`.
The core idea is to infer `θ_R` directly from expert intervention trajectories using a modified MaxEnt-IRL framework. The likelihood objective for expert trajectories `Ξ_e` is adapted to:
`L = Σ_{k=1}^n [r(ξ_{e_k}) + θ_R^⊤ f_R(ξ_{e_k})] - log Z_k(θ_R)`
where `ξ_{e_k}` are individual expert segments. The gradient of this objective with respect to `θ_R` is `∇_{θ_R} L = Σ_{k=1}^n f_R(ξ_{e_k}) - Σ_{k=1}^n E_{ξ_k ~ p(ξ_k|θ_R)} [f_R(ξ_k)]`. The second term, representing feature expectations under the current soft optimal policy, is approximated by rolling out the current policy `ˆπ` in a simulation environment. This allows `θ_R` to be learned via gradient descent without explicit knowledge of `r`.

#### 3.3. Policy Update with Residual Q-Learning (RQL)

Once `θ_R` is inferred, RQL is employed to update the policy `ˆπ`. RQL is a technique for policy customization where a new policy `ˆπ` is learned to jointly optimize the prior policy's objective and additional task objectives represented by `r_R`. RQL learns a residual Q-function `Q_R := ˆQ - Q*` (where `ˆQ` is the soft Q-function for `r_expert` and `Q*` is for `r`), enabling the customized policy to be defined using `Q_R` and `π`, without requiring knowledge of `r`.

#### 3.4. MEREQ Algorithm Flow

The MEREQ algorithm operates with an outer loop for human-in-the-loop sample collection and an inner loop for policy updates:
1. **Initialization:** `θ_R` is initialized to zero, and the learning policy `ˆπ` is initialized as the prior policy `π`.
2. **Outer Loop (Sample Collection):** The current `ˆπ` is executed under human supervision.
 - Human interventions generate "expert trajectories" (`Ξ_e`).
 - Segments where the policy runs autonomously are "policy trajectories" (`Ξ_p`).
 - The outer loop terminates if the intervention rate (`λ`, fraction of time steps with human intervention) falls below a predefined threshold `δ`.
3. **Inner Loop (Policy Update):** If the intervention rate is above `δ`, an iterative update process begins.
 - **Residual Reward Gradient Estimation:** The gradient `∇_{θ_R} L` is estimated using the collected expert trajectories (`Ξ_e`) and samples obtained by rolling out `ˆπ` in a simulation environment.
 - **Gradient Descent:** `θ_R` is updated using a gradient ascent step: `θ_R ← θ_R + η∇_{θ_R} L`.
 - **Residual Q-Learning Update:** The policy `ˆπ` is updated using RQL, utilizing the prior policy `π`, the current `ˆπ`, the feature set `f_R`, and the updated `θ_R`.
 - The inner loop continues until `∇_{θ_R} L` falls below a threshold `ϵ`, indicating convergence of `θ_R`.

#### 3.5. Pseudo Expert Trajectories

To stabilize training and reduce variance, MEREQ incorporates "pseudo-expert trajectories." These are extracted from segments of `Ξ_p` where the human did *not* intervene for an initial fraction (1-`κ`). These non-intervened snippets are considered "good-enough" and are aggregated into `Ξ+`, which is then concatenated with `Ξ_e` to augment the expert trajectory set used for reward gradient estimation.

#### 3.6. Experimental Design

The methodology includes evaluations in both simulated and real-world environments.
- **Synthesized Experts:** Four simulated tasks (Highway-Sim, Bottle-Pushing-Sim, Erasing-Sim, Pillow-Grasping-Sim) were designed where an expert policy (trained with a known `r_expert`) and a heuristic intervention rule were used. This allowed for direct measurement of learned policy sub-optimality.
- **Human-in-the-loop (HITL) Experiments:** Three real-world tasks (Highway-Human, Bottle-Pushing-Human, Pillow-Grasping-Human) involved human experts providing live interventions.
- **Baselines:** MEREQ was compared against:
 - **MEREQ-NP:** MEREQ without pseudo-expert trajectories.
 - **MaxEnt-FT:** Naive MaxEnt-IRL fine-tuned from the prior policy.
 - **MaxEnt:** Naive MaxEnt-IRL with random policy initialization.
 - **HG-DAgger-FT:** A DAgger variant for interactive imitation learning.
 - **IWR-FT:** Intervention-weighted regression, a behavior cloning method.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** The primary metric was the number of expert samples (time steps) required to reduce the intervention rate to a predefined threshold. For synthesized experts, additional metrics included Jensen-Shannon divergence of feature distributions and mean/standard deviation of reward distributions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluations were conducted across seven tasks, encompassing both simulated and real-world scenarios, with diverse action spaces and robot platforms.

- **Sample Efficiency:** MEREQ demonstrated consistent superiority in sample efficiency compared to all baseline methods across all simulated and human-in-the-loop tasks and various intervention rate thresholds. It required a significantly lower number of expert intervention samples to align the policy to human preferences. For example, in Highway-Sim, MEREQ achieved a 0.05 intervention rate with 1819 (±456) samples, while MaxEnt-FT required 4330 (±1255) samples. In human-in-the-loop experiments, MEREQ required substantially fewer total human samples (e.g., 654 samples for Highway-Human) than MaxEnt-FT (1270 samples) or HG-DAgger-FT (864 samples).
- **Training Stability and Variance:** MEREQ exhibited lower variance across multiple trials (random seeds) compared to other baselines, particularly in more complex environments like Bottle-Pushing-Sim, Erasing-Sim, and Pillow-Grasping-Sim. This stability was attributed to two key design choices:
 - **Residual Reward Learning and RQL:** The approach of inferring only the residual reward and using RQL effectively leveraged the prior policy's knowledge. This allowed MEREQ to significantly outperform MaxEnt-FT, which showed only transient benefits from the prior policy in early stages before its intervention rate converged to that of MaxEnt (randomly initialized).
 - **Pseudo-Expert Samples:** The inclusion of pseudo-expert samples, derived from unchallenged policy execution segments, contributed to reduced variance and enhanced training stability, particularly when actual expert intervention samples were scarce. MEREQ consistently showed lower variance than MEREQ-NP (No Pseudo), which did not use these samples.
- **Behavior Alignment (Synthesized Expert Tasks):** In tasks with synthesized experts (where the ground-truth expert policy and reward were known), MEREQ policies demonstrated closer alignment to the synthesized expert's feature and reward distributions compared to baseline methods. For instance, in Bottle-Pushing-Sim, MEREQ showed lower Jensen-Shannon Divergence values across multiple features (e.g., scaled tip2wine 0.237 vs IWR-FT's 0.302) and its reward distribution mean was closer to the expert's (-140.5 for MEREQ vs -115.9 for Expert, compared to -214.1 for MaxEnt-FT).
- **Effectiveness in Human-in-the-loop Experiments:** MEREQ successfully reduced the cognitive effort required from human experts in real-world tasks. The robot policies, after alignment with MEREQ, demonstrated successful completion of tasks that prior policies struggled with, such as pushing a bottle to a goal position with a low contact point or grasping a pillow from its center.
- **Performance under Noisy Intervention:** Preliminary studies on injecting Gaussian noise into synthesized expert interventions indicated that while MEREQ's performance degraded under noise, it still maintained an advantage over baseline methods in terms of sample efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MEREQ algorithm represents an advancement in the field of interactive imitation learning, particularly for applications requiring human-in-the-loop robot policy alignment.

- **Improved Sample Efficiency:** The primary significance lies in MEREQ's demonstrated ability to achieve policy alignment with substantially fewer human interventions compared to existing methods. This is crucial for real-world robotic systems where human supervision is costly, time-consuming, and prone to fatigue. By reducing the data requirements, MEREQ contributes to making interactive robot learning more practical and scalable.
- **Effective Utilization of Prior Knowledge:** The core innovation of inferring a residual reward function that captures only the deviation from a prior policy's implicit reward, rather than relearning the entire human reward, is a significant methodological contribution. This approach allows for more efficient leveraging of pre-trained policies, mitigating issues like catastrophic forgetting and accelerating the fine-tuning process.
- **Enhanced Training Stability:** The algorithm's improved stability and lower variance in performance across different trials suggest a more robust learning process. The integration of pseudo-expert samples, although heuristic, demonstrates a practical strategy for handling data scarcity and variability common in real-world human-robot interaction.
- **Broad Applicability in Embodied AI:** The successful evaluation across diverse simulated and real-world tasks, including highway driving and various robot manipulation tasks (bottle pushing, erasing, pillow grasping), indicates the generalizability of the MEREQ framework. This broad applicability suggests its potential impact in various domains where human preferences need to be incorporated into robot behavior.
- **Reduced Human Burden in Deployment:** By minimizing the intervention rate and total intervention samples, MEREQ directly contributes to reducing the cognitive and physical burden on human experts during robot deployment and adaptation phases. This can lead to faster deployment cycles, lower operational costs, and improved human-robot collaboration experiences.

**Potential Future Directions (as identified by the authors):**
The identified limitations point to avenues for further research and impact:
- **Accelerating Policy Update:** The current requirement for simulation rollouts in the policy update loop introduces delays. Future work could explore parallel rollouts or integrate offline/model-based reinforcement learning techniques to reduce this latency, thereby speeding up the overall learning process.
- **Robustness to Noisy and Inconsistent Interventions:** While pseudo-expert samples offer some mitigation, developing more principled methods to handle noise and inconsistency in human intervention actions would further enhance the algorithm's robustness, especially in real-world settings with diverse human operators.
- **Beyond Linear Reward Models:** The current adherence to a linear reward model in IRL is a common simplification. Exploring extensions of MEREQ to non-linear reward functions could allow for learning more complex and nuanced human preferences.
- **Multi-User Human-in-the-Loop Studies:** Expanding human-in-the-loop experiments to include multiple participants would provide deeper insights into how individual differences (e.g., skill, familiarity, tolerance) influence intervention patterns, trust dynamics, and the overall effectiveness of the learning process. This would contribute to developing more generalized and user-centric interactive learning systems.
