# Uncertainty Quantification for Language Models: A Suite of Black-Box, White-Box, LLM Judge, and Ensemble Scorers

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from CVS Health developed a framework and an open-source toolkit (`uqlm`) to quantify uncertainty in Large Language Models (LLMs) for real-time, closed-book hallucination detection. The framework standardizes various black-box, white-box, and LLM-as-a-Judge techniques into comparable confidence scores, featuring a tunable ensemble method that consistently outperforms individual scorers.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) frequently generate factual errors (hallucinations), posing significant reliability and safety risks in high-stakes applications like healthcare.
- Current hallucination detection methods, such as ground truth comparison or source content verification, are often not viable for real-time, closed-book inference scenarios.
- A unified and adaptable framework is needed to easily implement, compare, and optimize diverse uncertainty quantification (UQ) techniques for specific LLM deployment contexts.

## Method

- A comprehensive framework integrates and standardizes various UQ methods, including ==black-box==, ==white-box==, and ==LLM-as-a-Judge== approaches, to produce 0-1 confidence scores for real-time, closed-book hallucination detection.
- A ==tunable ensemble scorer== is introduced, allowing any combination of individual UQ methods to be weighted and optimized using a small set of user-provided graded responses, adapting to specific use cases.
- An accompanying open-source Python toolkit, `uqlm`, provides ready-to-use implementations of all presented UQ scorers and the ensemble method, facilitating practical adoption.

## Results

- The tunable ensemble scorer achieved superior performance over individual scorers in **20** out of **24** AUROC scenarios and **17** out of **24** F1-score scenarios, with top AUROC values reaching **0.986**.
- NLI-based black-box methods (NCP, NSN) showed the strongest performance among black-box scorers, leading in AUROC for **13** out of **24** scenarios and F1-score for **18** out of **24** black-box scenarios.
- Applying confidence thresholds to filter low-confidence responses monotonically increased the accuracy of the remaining LLM outputs, demonstrating practical utility (e.g., Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite accuracy on PopQA improved from **0.35** to **0.61** at τ=**0.6**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The tunable ensemble scorer consistently delivers superior hallucination detection performance compared to individual UQ methods, highlighting the benefit of combining multiple uncertainty signals.
> - Individual UQ scorer performance varies significantly across different LLMs and datasets, indicating that optimal detection strategies require use-case-specific customization.
> - Natural Language Inference (NLI)-based black-box methods (e.g., Non-Contradiction Probability) and LLM-as-a-Judge scorers (when the judge LLM is proficient in the task) are frequently among the top-performing individual techniques.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancement and widespread adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) have led to their deployment in increasingly critical applications, from customer service to medical diagnostics. However, a persistent and well-documented challenge for LLMs is their propensity for "hallucination"—generating plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information. This issue significantly undermines trust and safety, particularly in high-stakes domains where the consequences of errors can be severe. This paper directly addresses this critical problem.

Within the broader research landscape of LLM reliability, hallucination detection methods typically fall into three categories:
1. **Ground Truth Comparison:** Pre-deployment evaluation by comparing LLM outputs against known correct answers. While essential for initial risk assessment, this is not suitable for real-time monitoring of deployed systems.
2. **Source Content Comparison:** Techniques that compare generated content against a provided source (e.g., retrieval-augmented generation). These are effective but require access to a reliable source, limiting their applicability in "closed-book" scenarios where such a source is unavailable.
3. **Uncertainty Quantification (UQ):** Methods that aim to gauge the model's confidence in its own output. This category is particularly relevant for real-time, closed-book settings, which is the specific focus of this paper.

The paper contextualizes its contribution within the UQ landscape by delineating three primary UQ approaches:
- **Black-Box UQ:** These methods analyze variations in multiple responses generated from the same prompt, often relying on semantic consistency measures. The paper builds upon existing techniques like exact match, n-gram similarity, sentence embedding similarity (cosine similarity), BERTScore, and Natural Language Inference (NLI) models (e.g., for non-contradiction probability or semantic entropy).
- **White-Box UQ:** These approaches leverage internal model states, specifically token probabilities, to derive confidence scores. Prior work includes using average negative log probability, perplexity, joint token probability, and token entropy.
- **LLM-as-a-Judge:** This emergent paradigm involves using another LLM (or even the same LLM in a self-reflection capacity) to evaluate the correctness of a generated answer given a prompt. Existing methods explore prompting strategies for scoring, classification, or even more complex multi-LLM interactions.

This work distinguishes itself by offering a *unified, versatile framework* that adapts and standardizes these diverse UQ techniques into comparable response-level confidence scores (0-1 range). Crucially, it introduces a *tunable ensemble approach* that can combine any selection of these individual scorers. This ensemble is designed to be optimized for specific use cases, directly addressing the observed variability in individual scorer performance across different datasets and LLMs. The provision of the open-source `uqlm` toolkit is a significant practical contribution that aims to bridge the gap between academic research and real-world deployment, enabling practitioners to easily integrate and experiment with these state-of-the-art hallucination detection methods. By doing so, the paper advances the field by providing a comprehensive, adaptable, and deployable solution for a critical problem in LLM trustworthiness.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation driving this research is the critical need for effective and adaptable hallucination detection mechanisms for Large Language Models (LLMs), especially as these models are increasingly integrated into production-level applications within high-stakes domains like healthcare and finance. The authors highlight that despite significant advancements, even cutting-edge models continue to exhibit a considerable rate of hallucination, posing serious safety risks, financial costs, and reputational damage.

The existing landscape for hallucination detection presents limitations for real-time, production-level monitoring. Methods that rely on ground truth are typically suitable only for pre-deployment evaluation, while those requiring external source content are not applicable in "closed-book" scenarios where LLMs are expected to generate answers from their parametric knowledge alone. This gap necessitates the development of robust, generation-time, and closed-book hallucination detection strategies.

Based on this motivation, the key objectives of this paper are:

1. **Develop a Versatile Framework for Closed-Book Hallucination Detection:** To outline a comprehensive framework that practitioners can readily apply to real-world use cases, focusing on detection at generation time without requiring external source content or ground truth during inference.
2. **Standardize and Adapt Existing UQ Techniques:** To take a variety of existing uncertainty quantification (UQ) methods—spanning black-box UQ, white-box UQ, and LLM-as-a-Judge approaches—and adapt them to produce standardized response-level confidence scores ranging from 0 to 1, where higher scores indicate greater confidence.
3. **Propose a Tunable Ensemble Approach:** To introduce a novel ensemble method that can incorporate any combination of the individual confidence scorers. This ensemble is designed to be tunable using user-provided graded responses, allowing for optimization to specific use cases and thereby improving overall hallucination detection performance.
4. **Streamline Implementation with an Open-Source Toolkit:** To provide an accompanying open-source Python package, `uqlm`, that offers ready-to-use implementations of all the presented UQ methods, making them accessible and easy to integrate for researchers and developers.
5. **Empirically Evaluate Scorer Performance:** To conduct an extensive set of experiments using several LLM question-answering benchmarks and multiple LLMs, evaluating the performance of individual scorers and the tunable ensemble to provide empirical insights and practical guidance.

Ultimately, the goal is to equip practitioners with effective tools to monitor and improve the accuracy and reliability of LLM outputs in critical applications, mitigating the risks associated with factual inaccuracies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors frame the problem of hallucination detection as a binary classification task, where an LLM response is classified as either containing a hallucination (nonfactual) or not. The proposed framework aims to generate a "confidence score" between 0 and 1 for each LLM response, with lower scores indicating a higher likelihood of hallucination.

The methodology integrates and extends various uncertainty quantification (UQ) techniques, categorized as follows:

#### 3.1. Individual Confidence Scorers

**A. Black-Box UQ Scorers:** These methods exploit the stochastic nature of LLMs by generating multiple candidate responses (`m` responses) to the same prompt and then assessing their semantic consistency against the original response.
- **Exact Match Rate (EMR):** Measures the proportion of candidate responses that exactly match the original response.
- **Non-Contradiction Probability (NCP):** Utilizes a Natural Language Inference (NLI) model (specifically, `microsoft/deberta-large-mnli`) to determine the average non-contradiction probability between the original response and each candidate response. It is computed as 1 minus the average contradiction probability.
- **BERTScore Confidence (BSC):** Calculates the average BERTScore F1 score between the original response and each candidate response, leveraging contextualized word embeddings for semantic similarity.
- **Normalized Cosine Similarity (NCS):** Uses a sentence transformer (e.g., Sentence-BERT) to embed responses into a vector space and computes the average cosine similarity between the original response and candidates, normalized to a range.
- **Normalized Semantic Negentropy (NSN):** Adapts the concept of semantic entropy by clustering responses based on mutual entailment using an NLI model and then normalizing the resulting entropy measure to a confidence score.

**B. White-Box UQ Scorers:** These methods access the internal token probabilities of the LLM's generated response to quantify confidence.
- **Length-Normalized Token Probability (LNTP):** Computes the geometric mean of the token probabilities for all tokens in the response, effectively a length-normalized joint token probability.
- **Minimum Token Probability (MTP):** Takes the minimum token probability among all tokens in the generated response as the confidence score.

**C. LLM-as-a-Judge Scorer:** This approach employs an LLM to evaluate the correctness of a given question-answer pair.
- **Judge Score (J):** The method concatenates the question and the LLM's response, then prompts a separate LLM (or the same LLM in a self-reflection mode) to rate the correctness on a 0-100 scale, which is then normalized to 0-1. The prompt template is carefully constructed to elicit a numerical score without additional text.

#### 3.2. Ensemble Scorer

The paper introduces a tunable ensemble approach that combines any selection of the individual `K` confidence scorers.
- **Weighted Average:** The ensemble score is calculated as a simple weighted average of the individual scorer outputs: `S_ensemble = Σ (w_k * S_k)`, where `w_k` are the weights and `S_k` are the individual scores. The weights sum to 1 and are non-negative.
- **Tuning:** The ensemble weights `w` can be optimized using a "graded" dataset (a sample of LLM responses with corresponding ground-truth hallucination labels). Two tuning strategies are proposed:
 - **Threshold-Agnostic:** Optimize weights to maximize a threshold-agnostic metric like AUROC, then determine the optimal classification threshold.
 - **Threshold-Aware:** Jointly optimize weights and the classification threshold to maximize a threshold-dependent metric like F1-score.
- **Extensibility:** The ensemble is designed to be extensible, allowing for the inclusion of new scorers as research evolves.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

To evaluate the proposed scorers, the authors conducted extensive experiments across various LLMs and benchmark datasets.
- **Benchmarks:** Six publicly available question-answering datasets were used, representing different answer formats:
 - Numerical: GSM8K, SVAMP
 - Multiple-choice: CSQA, AI2-ARC
 - Open-ended text: PopQA, NQ-Open
- **LLMs:** Four prominent LLMs were tested: GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Gemini-2.5-Flash, and Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite.
- **Data Generation:** For each of 1000 sampled questions per benchmark, an original response and `m=15` candidate responses were generated from each LLM (with temperature 1.0 for candidate generation).
- **Ground Truth Grading:** Task-specific computational graders were implemented (Appendix C) to automatically label responses as correct or incorrect based on predefined rules for numerical, multiple-choice, and keyword-matching short answers.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **AUROC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve):** For threshold-agnostic performance evaluation.
 - **F1-score:** For threshold-optimized classification performance.
 - **Filtered Accuracy@τ:** Measures LLM accuracy on the subset of responses exceeding a given confidence threshold `τ`, demonstrating the impact of filtering.
- **Implementation:** All scorers were implemented and evaluated using the `uqlm` Python toolkit developed by the authors. 5-fold cross-validation was used for robust evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experimental evaluation yielded several key insights into the performance and utility of the various hallucination detection scorers:

1. **Ensemble Superiority:** The tunable ensemble scorer consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to its individual components. It outperformed individual scorers in 20 out of 24 scenarios when evaluated by AUROC and in 17 out of 24 scenarios for F1-score. This finding strongly supports the benefit of optimizing a custom combination of UQ signals for specific use cases rather than relying on a single, general-purpose method. The AUROC values for the best-performing scorers ranged from 0.729 to an impressive 0.986, indicating strong detection capabilities in many scenarios.

2. **Variability of Individual Scorer Performance:** The ranking of individual black-box, white-box, and LLM-as-a-Judge scorers varied significantly across different datasets and LLMs. This underscores the paper's central argument for use-case-specific customization. There is no single "best" scorer that performs optimally across all scenarios.

3. **Strong Performance of NLI-based Black-Box Scorers:** Among the black-box UQ methods, Natural Language Inference (NLI)-based approaches, specifically Non-Contradiction Probability (NCP) and Normalized Semantic Negentropy (NSN), frequently exhibited the strongest performance. They achieved the highest AUROC among black-box scorers in 13 out of 24 scenarios and the highest F1-score in 18 out of 24 black-box scenarios. This is consistent with previous research highlighting the effectiveness of NLI for semantic consistency checks.

4. **LLM-as-a-Judge Performance Correlates with Judge's Accuracy:** The experiments revealed that larger LLMs (GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Flash) generally performed better as judges than their smaller counterparts (GPT-4o-mini and Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite). More notably, an LLM's accuracy in generating answers for a specific dataset was positively related to its effectiveness as a judge for responses on that same dataset. For instance, Gemini-2.5-Flash excelled as a judge on math benchmarks, while GPT-4o was superior on short-answer benchmarks, mirroring their respective strengths as generative models.

5. **Equivalent Performance of White-Box Scorers:** The two white-box UQ scorers, Length-Normalized Token Probability (LNTP) and Minimum Token Probability (MTP), generally performed approximately equally, with very similar AUROC and F1-scores across most scenarios.

6. **Diminishing Returns for Candidate Responses in Black-Box UQ:** An additional experiment (Appendix D) investigated the impact of the number of candidate responses (`m`) on black-box scorer performance. Results showed that performance generally improves considerably with an increasing `m` but with diminishing returns as `m` becomes higher. This provides practical guidance, suggesting that while more samples help, there's a point of saturation beyond which additional samples offer little marginal benefit, balancing effectiveness with computational cost.

7. **Efficacy of Response Filtering:** Evaluation of "Filtered Accuracy@τ" demonstrated that applying confidence thresholds to filter out low-confidence responses significantly and monotonically increases the accuracy of the remaining LLM outputs. This validates practical applications such as blocking unreliable responses or routing them for human review, showcasing the concrete utility of these confidence scores in improving overall LLM system accuracy. For example, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite accuracy on PopQA questions improved from a baseline of 0.35 to 0.61 at τ=0.6 using the leading white-box scorer.

These findings collectively highlight the importance of tailored hallucination detection strategies, with the tunable ensemble emerging as a robust method for achieving high performance across diverse LLM applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers a highly significant contribution to the field of Large Language Models, particularly in addressing the critical challenge of hallucinations in real-world, high-stakes applications. The potential impact spans several dimensions:

1. **Enhanced LLM Trustworthiness and Reliability:** By providing a versatile and robust framework for real-time, closed-book hallucination detection, this work directly contributes to making LLMs more trustworthy. This is paramount for industries like healthcare (CVS Health's context) and finance, where factual errors can have severe safety, ethical, and financial ramifications. Improved reliability can accelerate the adoption of LLMs in these sensitive domains.

2. **Practical Guidance for Practitioners:** The detailed evaluation and empirical insights offer clear, actionable guidance for developers and practitioners deploying LLMs.
 - The consistent outperformance of the tunable ensemble approach encourages organizations to move beyond generic solutions and implement customized detection strategies tailored to their specific LLM, task, and dataset.
 - The discussion on choosing scorers based on API access (white-box vs. black-box/LLM-as-a-Judge), latency requirements, and LLM characteristics provides a pragmatic decision-making framework.
 - The finding that a judge LLM's performance correlates with its generative accuracy on a dataset offers a practical criterion for selecting the most effective LLM-as-a-Judge.
 - The analysis of diminishing returns for candidate responses in black-box methods provides efficiency guidelines for balancing performance with computational cost.

3. **Facilitating Real-World Deployment with Open-Source Tools:** The release of the `uqlm` open-source Python toolkit is a major enabler. It lowers the barrier to entry for implementing sophisticated UQ methods, allowing practitioners to easily integrate these hallucination detection capabilities into their LLM pipelines. This democratizes access to state-of-the-art techniques and fosters wider experimentation and adoption, accelerating innovation in LLM reliability.

4. **Enabling Proactive Risk Mitigation:** The proposed confidence scores can be used for various proactive risk mitigation strategies:
 - **Response Filtering:** Automatically blocking low-confidence responses from reaching end-users, as demonstrated by the significant improvement in "Filtered Accuracy@τ".
 - **Targeted Human-in-the-Loop Review:** Flagging low-confidence outputs for manual review by human experts, optimizing human resource allocation where precision is critical.
 - **Pre-deployment Diagnostics:** Using confidence scores to identify types of questions or scenarios where an LLM performs poorly, informing strategies for model fine-tuning or prompt engineering.

5. **Foundational Framework for Future Research:** The extensible nature of the ensemble framework means it can readily incorporate new UQ techniques as they emerge, providing a stable foundation for ongoing research in hallucination detection. The identified limitations, such as the need for exploration into non-linear ensembles, out-of-distribution generalization, and specialized methods for long-form generation or code, also lay out clear directions for future work in the academic community.

6. **Ethical Considerations and Responsible AI:** The paper's explicit acknowledgment of ethical considerations, such as the risk of over-reliance on confidence scores in high-risk settings and the need for monitoring distributional fairness and content diversity, demonstrates a responsible approach to developing and deploying AI systems. This encourages practitioners to adopt a holistic view of LLM safety beyond just factual correctness.

In conclusion, this research provides a comprehensive, empirically validated, and practically implementable solution for a pressing challenge in LLM development. By unifying diverse UQ techniques, offering a tunable ensemble, and providing an open-source toolkit, the authors empower the community to build more reliable, trustworthy, and safely deployable LLM applications across a myriad of critical domains.
