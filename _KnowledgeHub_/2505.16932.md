# The Polar Express: Optimal Matrix Sign Methods and Their Application to the Muon Algorithm

> [!summary] Summary
> Polar Express introduces an iterative polynomial method for computing the matrix sign function, engineered for GPU efficiency and stability in low-precision deep learning environments. This approach consistently yielded lower validation loss for GPT-2 models trained with the Muon optimizer compared to existing alternatives, without increasing per-step computational cost.

## Problem

- Deep learning optimizers, particularly Muon, rely on computationally expensive and often GPU-unfriendly subroutines for matrix sign function or polar decomposition.
- Existing matrix sign function methods either suffer from slow initial convergence (e.g., Newton-Schulz) or lack robust theoretical convergence guarantees in the low-accuracy regime preferred by deep learning applications.
- Traditional numerical analysis methods for polar decomposition are often not optimized for GPU parallelism, frequently involving operations like matrix inversions or QR decompositions.

## Method

- Developed **Polar Express**, an iterative polynomial method that dynamically adapts odd polynomials of a fixed degree (e.g., 5) at each step to optimally approximate the polar factor, guided by minimax approximation theory.
- The algorithm is designed to use only matrix-matrix multiplications, making it highly parallelizable and efficient on GPUs, with coefficients precomputed offline in higher precision for stability.
- Implemented numerical stabilization techniques such as a safety factor in polynomial evaluation and specific cushioning for small singular values to ensure robustness in `bfloat16` arithmetic.

## Results

- Polar Express consistently achieved lower final validation loss for GPT-2-Small and GPT-2-Large models trained with the Muon optimizer on the FineWeb dataset compared to baselines (Jordan's and You's methods).
- On synthetic and real GPT-2 gradient matrices, Polar Express demonstrated superior convergence, achieving a given error threshold approximately twice as fast as degree-5 Newton-Schulz.
- Ablation studies showed that increasing iterations beyond 5-6 or using an exact SVD-based polar decomposition did not further improve Muon's optimization quality, indicating sufficient accuracy is reached early in the process.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A greedy strategy of selecting locally optimal minimax polynomials at each iteration results in a globally optimal composition for approximating the matrix sign function.
> - For deep learning optimizers like Muon, rapid, low-accuracy convergence, particularly for the larger singular values, is more important than achieving very high asymptotic precision for the polar factor.
> - Careful consideration of finite-precision arithmetic and numerical stability is crucial for successfully deploying advanced numerical linear algebra algorithms in deep learning contexts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The computation of the polar decomposition and the related matrix sign function has been a subject of study in numerical analysis for several decades. These matrix functions have found applications in diverse fields such as electronic structure calculations, orthogonal Procrustes analysis, and parallel algorithms for Singular Value Decomposition (SVD). Recently, the matrix sign function has emerged as a crucial subroutine within modern deep learning optimizers, particularly the Muon optimizer, which has demonstrated effectiveness in training large language models (LLMs).

However, the computational requirements for deep learning applications differ from traditional numerical analysis settings. Deep learning necessitates algorithms that are highly efficient on Graphics Processing Units (GPUs), prioritizing high throughput over the high precision (e.g., sixteen digits of accuracy) often sought in scientific computing. This implies a preference for operations that are highly parallelizable, such as matrix-matrix multiplications, while avoiding less parallel operations like matrix inversions or QR decompositions. Furthermore, memory overhead must be minimized to handle large models.

Existing methods for computing the polar decomposition present various trade-offs:
- **SVD-based methods**: While accurate, direct SVD computation is often prohibitively expensive and not well-suited for GPU parallelism in deep learning contexts.
- **Classical iterative methods**: Approaches like Newton's method, Halley's method, QDWH (QR-based dynamically weighted Halley), and Zolo-pd often rely on matrix inverses or QR decompositions, which are not ideal for GPU acceleration.
- **Newton-Schulz methods**: These polynomial-based iterations utilize only matrix-matrix products, making them GPU-friendly. However, they are known for slow initial convergence, particularly when the input matrix is far from its polar factor.
- **Adapted polynomial methods**: Earlier work by Chen & Chow (2014) proposed adapting the Newton-Schulz polynomial at each iteration to accelerate initial convergence. More recently, heuristic approaches by Jordan et al. (2024b) and Cesista et al. (2025) introduced fixed or successive hand-tuned polynomial updates designed for rapid low-accuracy approximations, prioritizing speed in the initial iterations, but without guaranteeing asymptotic convergence to the true polar factor.

This research addresses the gap by developing a GPU-friendly matrix sign function algorithm specifically tailored for deep learning. The proposed method aims to achieve rapid convergence in the low-accuracy regime, critical for deep learning throughput, while maintaining theoretical guarantees of convergence to the true polar factor, thereby offering an improved balance between computational efficiency, accuracy, and theoretical rigor in the context of deep learning optimizers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to design and evaluate a state-of-the-art, GPU-friendly iterative algorithm, named Polar Express, for computing the matrix sign function or, more generally, the polar decomposition of a rectangular matrix. This algorithm is intended for integration into deep learning optimizers like Muon.

The key motivations and specific objectives include:
- **Optimizing Muon's core subroutine**: The Muon optimizer's performance is intrinsically linked to the efficiency and accuracy of its polar decomposition step. Existing implementations often rely on methods that are either too slow, not GPU-optimized, or lack robust theoretical convergence guarantees for deep learning's specific precision requirements. Polar Express aims to enhance this critical component.
- **Achieving GPU efficiency**: The method is designed to exclusively use matrix-matrix multiplications, which are highly parallelizable and efficient on modern GPUs, thereby avoiding computationally expensive operations such as matrix inversions or QR decompositions.
- **Accelerating initial convergence**: Addressing the slow initial convergence issue observed in classical polynomial methods like Newton-Schulz, Polar Express aims to make rapid progress in the early iterations, which is particularly valuable in deep learning where high asymptotic accuracy is often not required.
- **Ensuring asymptotic convergence**: Unlike some recent heuristic methods that prioritize early-stage accuracy but may not converge to the true polar factor, Polar Express is developed with a theoretical framework that guarantees super-exponential convergence to the exact polar decomposition.
- **Handling finite-precision arithmetic**: The algorithm is specifically designed to be practical and stable when implemented using low-precision formats such as bfloat16, which are standard in deep learning training. This includes addressing potential numerical instability issues.
- **Demonstrating practical impact**: A key objective is to empirically demonstrate the algorithm's effectiveness by integrating it into the Muon optimizer and evaluating its performance on the training of large language models, specifically GPT-2, using substantial datasets. The goal is to show consistent improvements in validation loss compared to existing alternatives.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need to develop a theoretically sound and computationally efficient algorithm for matrix functions that meets the unique demands of modern deep learning, thereby improving the performance and stability of optimizers like Muon.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on developing Polar Express, an iterative polynomial method for approximating the polar factor of a matrix `M`. The approach is grounded in approximation theory, specifically minimax polynomial approximation, and is engineered for GPU efficiency and stability in low-precision arithmetic.

**Core Algorithmic Principle:**
Polar Express constructs a sequence of approximations `X_t` to `polar(M)` using the update rule `X_t = p_t(X_{t-1})`. Here, `p_t` represents an odd polynomial of a fixed degree `d` (e.g., `d=5` in experiments). The pivotal aspect is that `p_t` is *dynamically adapted* at each iteration `t`.

**Mathematical Framework – Optimal Polynomial Composition:**
The core theoretical contribution is proving the optimality of a greedy approach. Given an initial range `[ℓ, u]` for the singular values of `M` (after normalization), the method seeks a composition of polynomials `p* = p_T o p_{T-1} o ... o p_1` that minimizes the worst-case error `max_{x∈[ℓ,u]} |1 - p*(x)|` in the spectral norm.
Theorem 3.1 establishes that this global optimization problem can be solved by iteratively choosing `p_t` at each step to optimally approximate the constant function `x -> 1` over the current singular value range `[ℓ_t, u_t]`. The new singular value range `[ℓ_{t+1}, u_{t+1}]` is derived from the properties of `p_t`, specifically `ℓ_{t+1} = p_t(ℓ_t)` and `u_{t+1} = 2 - ℓ_{t+1}`. This guarantees that `p*` is the optimal composition.

**Convergence Guarantee:**
Theorem 3.3 provides a super-exponential convergence rate for Polar Express. For `d=2q+1`, the spectral norm error decreases by a factor related to `|1 - ℓ_2|^{(q+1)T}`, implying quadratic convergence for `d=3` and cubic convergence for `d=5`.

**Finding Optimal Polynomials (`p_t`):**
- **Minimax Approximation**: The problem of finding `p_t` is a minimax approximation problem: `arg min_{p∈P_{odd,d}} max_{x∈[ℓ_t,u_t]} |1 - p(x)|`. This is a standard problem in approximation theory.
- **Degree 3 Case (`d=3`)**: For cubic polynomials `p(x) = ax + bx^3`, a closed-form solution for `a` and `b` exists (Equation 17), derived from the Equioscillation Theorem.
- **Degree 5 Case (`d=5`)**: For quintic polynomials `p(x) = ax + bx^3 + cx^5`, a simplified version of the Remez algorithm (Algorithm 2) is employed. This iterative algorithm refines a set of trial points (including the interval endpoints `ℓ_t`, `u_t`, and the polynomial's two local extrema) until the equioscillation property is satisfied, yielding the optimal coefficients `a, b, c`. The algorithm is designed to be numerically stable and efficient for low degrees.

**Initialization and Bounds:**
- The input matrix `M` is initially normalized by `X_0 = M / (||M||_F + 10^-2)`. This sets the approximate maximum singular value `u` to 1.
- A lower bound `ℓ` for the singular values is assumed. For `bfloat16`, `ℓ = 10^-3` is used, ensuring values are above machine precision.
- The polynomials `p_1..., p_T` and corresponding bounds `[ℓ_t, u_t]` are precomputed offline in `float64` precision.

**Finite Precision Considerations and Stabilization (Section 3.4 and Appendix G):**
To ensure stability in `bfloat16` arithmetic, several modifications are implemented:
- **Safety Factor**: Each polynomial `p_t(x)` is replaced by `p_t(x/1.01)` (except for the last iteration) to counteract round-off errors that might push singular values slightly above `u_t`, preventing divergence.
- **Cushioning**: When `ℓ_t` is very small (e.g., `ℓ_t < u_t/10`), the polynomial `p_t` is computed as if `ℓ_t` were `u_t/10`. This enforces a lower bound on `p_t(x)/x`, mitigating non-monotonicity issues that can cause precision loss.
- The `+10^-2` term in the `X_0` normalization also contributes to stability.

**Implementation Details:**
- The online stage involves applying the precomputed polynomial coefficients to the matrix `X_t` using Horner's rule. For `p(x) = ax + bx^3 + cx^5`, the matrix update is `X_t = a X_{t-1} + b X_{t-1} (X_{t-1}^T X_{t-1}) + c X_{t-1} (X_{t-1}^T X_{t-1})^2`. This can be written efficiently as `X_t = X_{t-1} (aI + (X_{t-1}^T X_{t-1}) (bI + (X_{t-1}^T X_{t-1}) (cI)))`.
- The algorithm is implemented in PyTorch, with a degree of `d=5` and `T=5` or `6` iterations recommended for deep learning applications.

**Advanced Optimizations (Appendix I & J - Not in main GPT experiments):**
- **Spectrum-Aware Initialization**: For matrices with large spectral gaps, an initial polynomial can be specifically designed to handle the largest singular values, potentially accelerating overall convergence.
- **Fast Polynomial Iteration for Rectangular Matrices (Algorithm 3)**: For matrices `X` with high aspect ratios (`m >> n`), this method computes `XQ_T` instead of `p_T(X)`, where `Q_T` is an `n x n` matrix. This significantly reduces rectangular matrix multiplications, leading to computational savings, especially on GPUs. This involves maintaining `Q_t` and `Y = X^T X` and applying `h_t` to `R_t = Q_{t-1}^T Y Q_{t-1}`. Restarting strategies and modified initializations `Y = X^T X + 10^-3 I` are used for numerical stability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the convergence properties of Polar Express and its practical impact when integrated into the Muon optimizer for training deep neural networks.

#### 4.1. Convergence of Polar Express

- **Performance against baselines**: On synthetic matrices with singular values logarithmically spaced between `10^-6` and 1, Polar Express consistently outperformed degree-5 Newton-Schulz, Jordan's heuristic method, and You's method across all iterations. It achieved higher accuracy significantly faster, converging approximately twice as quickly as Newton-Schulz for a given error threshold when the lower bound `ℓ` was well-tuned.
- **Robustness to `ℓ`**: While optimal performance was achieved when `ℓ` closely matched the smallest singular value, the method remained competitive even when `ℓ` was misestimated by two orders of magnitude.
- **Generalization to GPT-2 gradients**: Similar superior convergence was observed when testing on real gradient matrices from a GPT-2 model, both in terms of spectral norm error (Figure 3), Frobenius norm error, and cosine similarity (Figure 8).

#### 4.2. Training GPT-2 Models with Muon

- **Consistent Validation Loss Improvement**: When integrated into the Muon optimizer, Polar Express consistently yielded lower final validation loss across a range of learning rates compared to implementations using Jordan's or You's matrix sign methods. This was demonstrated for both GPT-2-Small (124M parameters) and GPT-2-Large (774M parameters) models trained on 1 billion tokens from the FineWeb dataset (Figure 1, 4, 11).
- **Training Loss and Wall-Clock Time**: The improvements in validation loss were mirrored by similar reductions in training loss. Since all compared matrix sign methods used a degree-5 polynomial for five iterations, their computational cost per step was comparable. Therefore, the improved loss directly translated to better performance in terms of wall-clock training time (Figure 11).
- **Impact of Weight Decay and Training Tokens**: The observed benefits of Polar Express persisted when weight decay (0.1) was applied (Figure 12) and when models were trained on a larger dataset of 10 billion tokens (Figure 6, 13). The performance gap, though small, remained consistent.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Sensitivity to Accuracy**: An ablation study on GPT-2-Small showed that increasing the number of Polar Express iterations beyond 5 or 6 did not further improve the final validation loss. Moreover, computing the exact polar factor using SVD, despite being computationally much more expensive (doubling training step runtime), did not improve optimization quality (Figure 5). This suggests that high-precision computation of the polar factor is not critical for Muon's effectiveness in deep learning.
- **Role of Small Singular Values**: Further investigation into Muon's sensitivity to small singular values (Figure 9) revealed that the treatment of singular values below `10^-4 * σ_max` had negligible impact on validation loss. Even reversing the direction of the Muon step in the subspace corresponding to these small singular values had minimal effect. This finding aligns with Polar Express's behavior after 5 iterations, which effectively maps small singular values to approximately 0 while larger ones converge to 1.
- **Convergence of Top Singular Values**: When error was measured by considering only singular values larger than `σ_max/10^3`, Polar Express achieved high accuracy within 5-6 iterations (Figure 10). This rapid convergence of the "important" singular values likely explains why Muon's performance saturates with a low number of iterations.

#### 4.4. Image Classification (Appendix H.3)

- Experiments on CIFAR-10/100 using ResNet-20/110 and a Vision Transformer showed that all Muon variants (Polar Express, Jordan, You, Newton-Schulz) generally performed comparably to or better than AdamW and SGD with momentum in terms of training loss and validation accuracy. However, Polar Express did not consistently demonstrate a significant advantage over other Muon variants in these settings, suggesting potential for further task-specific tuning.

#### 4.5. Algorithmic Extensions (Appendix I & J - Not in main GPT experiments)

- **Spectrum-Aware Initialization**: For synthetic matrices with large spectral gaps (power law decay), applying a spectrum-aware initialization step significantly accelerated the convergence of both Newton-Schulz and Polar Express (Figure 17).
- **Fast Polynomial Iteration for Rectangular Matrices**: Algorithm 3 demonstrated substantial runtime savings on GPUs for matrices with high aspect ratios. For example, a 5x speedup was observed for matrices with an aspect ratio of 32 (Figure 18). While these benefits were not observed in the GPT-2-Small experiments (likely due to the model scale), they suggest potential for larger models or other applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents Polar Express, an iterative method for computing the matrix sign function, which offers a robust and theoretically grounded alternative for the core orthogonalization step in optimizers like Muon. The significance and potential impact of this research can be delineated as follows:

- **Advancement in Deep Learning Optimization**: Polar Express consistently improves the training efficacy of the Muon optimizer for large language models. The demonstrated reduction in validation loss for GPT-2 models trained on billions of tokens indicates a practical enhancement to the state of the art in LLM training. This directly contributes to developing more performant and stable training regimes for large-scale neural networks.

- **Bridging Numerical Analysis and Deep Learning**: The paper successfully adapts classical numerical analysis techniques (minimax polynomial approximation, Remez algorithm, equioscillation theory) to the specific constraints and requirements of deep learning. This showcases how re-evaluating established mathematical tools through a deep learning lens can yield optimized algorithms that are both theoretically sound and practically efficient on modern hardware.

- **GPU-Friendly Design as a Paradigm**: The emphasis on purely matrix-matrix multiplication-based operations aligns with current GPU architectures, highlighting a crucial design principle for high-performance deep learning algorithms. By avoiding expensive operations like matrix inverses or QR decompositions, Polar Express ensures maximum throughput and scalability.

- **Theoretical Rigor for Practical Tools**: The proof of global optimality for the composed polynomial distinguishes Polar Express from prior heuristic approaches. This theoretical foundation provides stronger guarantees for convergence and stability, which can contribute to more reliable and predictable optimizer performance during long training runs.

- **Impact on LLM Training Infrastructure**: The adoption of Polar Express into prominent benchmarks like the NanoGPT speedrun indicates its immediate practical value and acceptance within the LLM development community. This suggests its potential to become a standard component in efficient LLM training pipelines.

- **Future Research Directions**: The exploration of advanced algorithmic ideas in the appendices, such as spectrum-aware initialization and fast polynomial iteration for rectangular matrices, suggests avenues for further optimization. While their immediate impact on current GPT-2 models was limited, these techniques could yield significant benefits for even larger models or in other application domains where matrix aspect ratios or spectral properties differ. The mixed results in image classification also indicate the need for further tuning and understanding of Muon and Polar Express in different neural network architectures and tasks.

In summary, Polar Express represents an advancement in specialized numerical linear algebra for deep learning, offering a more effective, efficient, and theoretically justified method for a critical optimizer component, with demonstrated positive implications for training large language models.
