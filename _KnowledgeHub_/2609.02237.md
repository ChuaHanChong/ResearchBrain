# Recursive Value Learning for Long-Horizon Offline Goal-Conditioned RL

> [!summary] Summary
> DCRL (Divide-and-Conquer RL) is an offline goal-conditioned RL method that recursively bisects each trajectory segment into a balanced binary tree and trains values from leaves to root, so every long-range value composes already-learned shorter ones through an exact route factorization, with a separate multistep propagation objective recovering optimality. Bootstrap depth falls from linear to logarithmic, lifting the long-horizon OGBench average from 55 to 64.

## Problem

- Prior offline goal-conditioned RL samples state-goal pairs in no particular order, so long-range targets bootstrap from shorter-range value estimates that are themselves still inaccurate.
- Max-based Bellman and transitive backups optimistically select among noisy intermediate states, propagating overestimation through repeated updates and turning small local errors into global inconsistencies.
- Non-recursive backups have linear worst-case bootstrap depth in the horizon, so error compounds quickly and flat methods score zero on the hardest long-horizon tasks.

## Method

- **Divide** recursively splits a sampled trajectory segment at its midpoint into a ==balanced binary tree==, yielding O(H) nodes but only O(log H) sequential composition levels.
- **Conquer** trains leaves-to-root with a ==selection-free exact factorization== V(s,g)=V(s,w)*V(w,g) under a ==binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss==, using the online critic for the first child and an ==EMA target== for the second.
- A ==propagation loss== over globally relabeled goals with ==upper-expectile BCE== recovers optimal values by composing cross-trajectory routes, while ==parallel slot scheduling== interleaves 128 trees to keep batches length-diverse.

## Results

- On the five hardest long-horizon OGBench tasks DCRL averages **64** versus **55** for the best hierarchical baseline and **45** for Transitive RL, hitting **93%** on humanoidmaze-giant and **100%** on puzzle-4x5.
- Aggregate scores reach **68%** state-based and **56%** pixel-based OGBench against **55%** and **36%** for Transitive RL; on state CALVIN it is the only flat method completing all four subtasks, at **8%**.
- Ablations drop success from **68** to **39** without recursion and to **45** without propagation; reversing leaf-to-root order collapses humanoidmaze-giant from **93%** to **18%**, and max-based backup trails at **62**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Update ordering matters more than the split rule: random splits matched midpoint splits, but reversing child-to-parent order destroyed performance, isolating scheduling as the active ingredient.
> - Selection-free composition along observed routes can beat optimistic max backups on finite data, trading a looser value bound for far slower error amplification.
> - Multiplicative factorization presumes deterministic dynamics, and hierarchy still wins on some shorter-horizon manipulation, so horizon length alone does not explain task difficulty.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL), specifically addressing the challenges of scaling these methods to long-horizon tasks. GCRL aims to train policies that can reach arbitrary goals from any state, leveraging fixed datasets without further environmental interaction. The difficulty in scaling GCRL to long horizons primarily stems from two issues: first, the value estimates for long trajectory segments depend on potentially inaccurate shorter-range estimates, and second, traditional max-based value backups (e.g., Bellman-style or transitive backups) can amplify overestimation errors as they propagate through repeated computations.

The existing landscape of GCRL methods is diverse, encompassing Temporal Difference (TD) learning, Monte Carlo (MC) methods, hierarchical reinforcement learning, probabilistic approaches, and quasimetric methods. This work primarily builds upon the quasimetric family, which explicitly leverages the triangle inequality property of optimal distances in deterministic environments.

DCRL distinguishes itself from other quasimetric methods by addressing the dependency structure and error amplification directly. It contrasts with:
- **Sub-goal Tree Dynamic Programming (TDP):** TDP recursively predicts intermediate subgoals and learns in a top-down fashion. This approach can introduce overestimation by greedily selecting among decomposition candidates. DCRL, in contrast, fixes the intermediate subgoal to the trajectory midpoint and employs a bottom-up learning strategy.
- **Transitive RL (TRL):** TRL implicitly uses a divide-and-conquer structure by sampling trajectory segments and random in-trajectory splits, often favoring optimistic options via an upper-expectile loss. This can lead to reliance on noisy estimates and results in a linear worst-case bootstrap depth, exacerbating error propagation over long horizons. DCRL explicitly constructs and processes a balanced binary tree, uses an exact factorization objective (avoiding optimistic selection), and enforces a specific learning order from shorter to longer segments, resulting in a logarithmic worst-case bootstrap depth. This structural difference is a core contribution to mitigating error accumulation in long-horizon scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to overcome the difficulties associated with scaling offline GCRL to long-horizon tasks. The authors identify two principal problems that motivate their approach:

1. **Dependency on Inaccurate Shorter-Range Estimates:** In long-horizon tasks, the value of a long trajectory segment is inherently composed of the values of its shorter constituent segments. However, many existing offline GCRL methods sample transitions or subgoals independently, without ensuring that the shorter-horizon values, which serve as foundational building blocks, are accurately learned before the longer-horizon values that depend on them. This lack of ordered learning can lead to long-range estimates bootstrapping from unstable or inaccurate shorter-range value predictions, thereby propagating and accumulating errors. The motivation is to explicitly enforce a hierarchical learning order where shorter-range values are converged before they are used to compute longer-range values.

2. **Amplification of Overestimation by Max-Based Value Backups:** Standard Bellman-style or transitive value backups typically involve a maximization step over possible actions or intermediate states. With finite and potentially suboptimal offline data, this optimistic selection can inadvertently choose candidates whose values are overestimated. When these overestimated values are repeatedly propagated through successive backups, small local errors can compound into significant global inconsistencies, severely impacting the reliability of value estimates for long horizons. The motivation here is to develop a value update mechanism that avoids this optimistic selection bias, thus limiting error amplification.

Building on these motivations, the authors aim to:
- Introduce an algorithmic framework that explicitly captures and leverages the natural dependency structure in goal-conditioned value learning, processing shorter-range components before longer-range ones.
- Design a value update rule that avoids maximization over noisy alternatives, preventing the propagation of amplified errors.
- Achieve a substantially shorter (logarithmic) worst-case bootstrap depth compared to the linear depth found in conventional methods, which should theoretically correspond to slower error accumulation.
- Jointly learn reliable behavior values along observed trajectories and discover optimal values by composing segments across different trajectories.
- Demonstrate that such an approach can substantially outperform existing flat and hierarchical offline GCRL methods on complex, long-horizon goal-reaching tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, DCRL (Divide-and-Conquer RL), addresses the identified challenges through a two-pronged approach: recursive value learning for behavior values and value propagation for optimality, integrated with an efficient training schedule.

#### 3.1. Recursive Divide-and-Conquer Value Learning (for Behavior Values)

DCRL explicitly imposes a dependency order by learning values for shorter segments before longer ones. This is achieved via a recursive strategy:
- **Divide:** For any sampled trajectory segment (s_i, s_j), DCRL recursively splits it at its temporal midpoint (k = ⌊(i + j)/2⌋). This process continues until single-step segments (s_x, s_{x+1}) are reached, forming a balanced binary tree structure. This balanced splitting is crucial as it yields a tree with O(H) nodes but only O(log₂H) sequential composition levels for a horizon-H segment, fundamentally reducing the worst-case bootstrap depth from linear to logarithmic.
- **Conquer:** Value functions are trained in a bottom-up fashion, starting from the leaves of the binary tree and progressing towards the root. Single-step segments (leaves) are explicitly grounded, with their value V(s_i, s_{i+1}) set to the discount factor γ. Each parent segment's value is then updated only after the values of its two child segments have been trained and are considered more stable.

**Update Rule for Recursive Value Learning:**
Unlike max-based backups, DCRL employs an *exact trajectory factorization* for its recursive updates:
V_τ(s, g) = V_τ(s, w) · V_τ(w, g)
where *w* is the fixed midpoint state. This multiplicative factorization evaluates a single, observed route without selecting among noisy alternatives. Since it does not maximize over possibilities, it primarily learns *behavior values* along the demonstrated trajectory (V_τ). A theoretical analysis demonstrates that the route suboptimality (e(s, g) = d_τ(s, g) - d*(s, g)) satisfies e(s, g) ≥ e(s, w) + e(w, g), implying that route suboptimality can accumulate, which further motivates learning from less suboptimal (shorter) constituents first.

#### 3.2. Value Propagation for Optimality

While recursive divide-and-conquer learning provides reliable *behavior values* within observed trajectories, optimal values (V*) often require composing segments *across different trajectories* to find shorter paths. Additionally, the recursive tree samples are inherently skewed towards short segments, requiring generalization to distant goals. To address this, DCRL jointly trains the value function using an additional multi-step value propagation objective:
V*(s_i, g) ≥ γⁿ · V*(s_{i+n}, g)
This objective encourages the value function to push V(s_i, g) towards γⁿ · V(s_{i+n}, g), effectively propagating values over *n* steps (where n is the propagation horizon). An upper-expectile loss (κ_prop > 0.5) is applied to this objective, promoting the discovery of shorter, more optimal routes. The reliable, long-range behavior values learned by the recursive objective provide a robust foundation for this propagation.

#### 3.3. Implementation Details

- **Parallel Slot Scheduling:** To handle multiple recursive trees concurrently, maintain diverse minibatches, and ensure training efficiency, DCRL employs "slot scheduling." A fixed number of independent slots (e.g., 128 for a batch size of 1024) are maintained. Each slot manages the recursive tree generated from a single root trajectory segment. Samples are drawn uniformly across these slots, with each slot emitting its samples in a leaf-to-root order. As slots drain and refill independently, they desynchronize, allowing batches to contain segments of varying lengths while still preserving the child-to-parent ordering within each individual tree.
- **Critic Learning:** DCRL trains an action-value function Q(s, a, g) using a combined loss:
 - **Divide-and-Conquer Loss (L_dc):** A Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss on the factorized target sg[Q(s_i, a_i, s_k)] * Q_bar(s_k, a_k, s_j). The first child's value is from the online critic (with stop-gradient), and the second child's value is from an EMA target critic (Q_bar). Single-step segments are directly targeted to γ. This loss uses a symmetric expectile (κ_dc = 0.5).
 - **Propagation Loss (L_prop):** An upper-expectile BCE loss (κ_prop > 0.5) on the multi-step propagation target γⁿ · Q_bar(s_{i+n}, a_{i+n}, g).
- **Policy Extraction:** After the value function Q(s, a, g) is trained, a goal-conditioned policy π(s, g) is extracted by selecting actions that maximize the learned Q-value. This can be done using either reparameterized gradients or rejection sampling, depending on the specific task requirements.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of DCRL addressed three main questions regarding its performance, scalability, and design choices.

#### 4.1. Comparison with Prior Offline GCRL Methods

DCRL was benchmarked against various existing offline GCRL algorithms across diverse task domains, horizons, dataset scales, and observation modalities (state and pixel).
- **Long-Horizon OGBench Tasks:** On the five most challenging long-horizon tasks from OGBench (e.g., `humanoidmaze-giant-1B`, `puzzle-4x5-1B`, `cube-octuple-1B`), DCRL achieved an average score of 64, which was a substantial improvement over the best prior average score of 55 (achieved by SHARSA, a hierarchical method). DCRL demonstrated the highest scores on `humanoidmaze-giant` and both `puzzle` tasks. Notably, DCRL was the only flat method to achieve any non-zero success rate on `cube-octuple`.
- **Standard-Horizon OGBench Tasks:** DCRL consistently showed superior aggregated performance on both state-based and pixel-based standard-horizon OGBench tasks, surpassing all other evaluated methods.
- **CALVIN Benchmark:** In the compositional, long-horizon manipulation environment CALVIN, DCRL achieved the highest four-subtask completion rate for the state-based variant (8%), outperforming the best hierarchical baseline (3%) and all other flat methods (0%). For pixel-based CALVIN, DCRL achieved at least three subtasks in 8% of rollouts and completed all four in 1%, while baselines failed to progress beyond two subtasks. DCRL was the only flat method capable of completing all four subtasks consecutively.

#### 4.2. Scalability with Propagation Horizon (*n*) and Task Horizon (*H*)

The study investigated how DCRL's performance scales with the propagation horizon *n* and how error accumulation behaves with increasing task horizon *H*.
- **Effect of Propagation Horizon (n):** Compared to TD-n and TRL-n, DCRL-n exhibited superior performance for larger propagation horizons (n ≥ 25) on tasks such as `puzzle-4x6` and `cube-triple`. For smaller *n* (e.g., 1 or 5), DCRL-n's performance was comparable to TD-n and sometimes lower than TRL-n, suggesting that short propagation chains limit the full leverage of its recursively learned long-horizon behavior values.
- **Error Accumulation with Task Horizon (H):**
 - **Theoretical Analysis:** The paper proved that DCRL-n has a logarithmic worst-case bootstrap depth (O(log(H/n))), whereas TD-n and TRL-n have linear worst-case depths (O(H/n) and O(H-n), respectively). This suggests DCRL's shallower dependency structure should lead to slower error growth.
 - **Empirical Validation (Combination-Lock):** In a controlled discrete environment designed to measure error accumulation, DCRL-n's mean long-range Q-error remained relatively stable and grew much more slowly with increasing task horizon H (from 256 to 2048) compared to TD-n and TRL-n. This empirically supported the theoretical prediction that logarithmic bootstrap depth correlates with slower error accumulation.
 - **Ablation of Mechanisms:** Further analysis in the combination-lock environment showed that adding *n*-step propagation reduced TRL's error. Lowering TRL-n's transitive expectile (from 0.7 to 0.5, implying symmetric regression) further reduced error, indicating that optimistic subgoal selection in max-based backups amplifies estimation noise. The remaining performance gap between TRL-n (κ=0.5) and DCRL-n was attributed to DCRL’s fixed-midpoint recursion and child-to-parent scheduling. An ablation study of DCRL without this explicit child-to-parent scheduling showed a sharp degradation in performance for horizons greater than 1024, emphasizing the practical importance of this ordered learning for deep dependency chains.

#### 4.3. Comparison with Alternative Divide-and-Conquer Strategies

Ablation studies investigated the specific components of DCRL against alternative strategies:
- **Curriculum Learning:** Learning values on short segments first, then gradually increasing length, was highly sensitive to its curriculum parameter (Δ) and generally underperformed DCRL and TRL.
- **TRL (Distance Re-weighting):** TRL's strategy of down-weighting distant pairs in the loss (controlled by λ) did not improve its baseline performance.
- **DCRL (w/ random split):** Replacing DCRL's fixed midpoint split with a random in-trajectory subgoal yielded comparable success rates to DCRL but with lower training throughput due to creating deeper, less balanced recursive trees. This suggested that exact factorization and child-to-parent scheduling are more critical than the specific midpoint choice.
- **DCRL (w/ reverse order):** Reversing the leaf-to-root learning order (parent-to-child) drastically reduced success (from 93% to 18%), confirming that bottom-up scheduling is crucial to avoid bootstrapping from unconverged child estimates.
- **DCRL (w/o recursion):** Removing the recursive tree structure and using i.i.d. sampling with a single midpoint split led to the largest performance drop (from 68% to 39%), underscoring the centrality of recursive value learning.
- **DCRL (w/o propagation):** Removing the propagation objective (while increasing the recursive objective's expectile to recover optimality) significantly degraded performance (from 68% to 45%), indicating that the recursive factorization alone is insufficient to recover optimal values and that separate propagation is essential.
- **DCRL (w/ max-based backup):** Replacing DCRL's exact factorization with a max-based backup that selects the best intermediate subgoal among candidates resulted in lower performance (62% vs 68%) and lower throughput. This supported the hypothesis that selection-induced overestimation harms performance compared to fixed factorization.

These ablations collectively validated DCRL's division of labor: recursive factorization for reliable behavior values and propagation for optimal values, with bottom-up scheduling and exact factorization being critical components.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DCRL introduces a principled approach to addressing persistent challenges in scaling offline GCRL to long-horizon tasks. Its core contribution lies in explicitly leveraging and enforcing the natural dependency structure of value learning through a recursive divide-and-conquer strategy.

The significance of this work can be summarized as follows:
- **Mitigation of Error Accumulation:** By designing a system with logarithmic worst-case bootstrap depth and employing a selection-free, exact factorization update rule, DCRL directly counteracts the compounding error accumulation prevalent in traditional, linear-depth, max-based backup methods. This theoretical advantage is empirically demonstrated in controlled experiments.
- **Improved Performance on Long-Horizon Tasks:** DCRL substantially outperforms a wide array of prior flat offline GCRL algorithms across various benchmarks and surpasses several hierarchical methods on the most challenging long-horizon tasks, including complex manipulation (CALVIN) and navigation (`humanoidmaze-giant`). This establishes DCRL as a leading approach for long-horizon offline GCRL.
- **Clear Division of Labor:** The architecture of DCRL, which separates the learning of reliable *behavior values* via recursive factorization from the discovery of *optimal values* via cross-trajectory propagation, offers a robust framework for handling both aspects of value estimation.
- **Practical Scalability:** The introduction of parallel slot scheduling enables efficient training of the recursive structure, ensuring that the method is not only theoretically sound but also practically implementable on large datasets and complex environments.

The potential impact of DCRL is significant for the broader field of reinforcement learning. By providing a more stable and effective method for learning long-range goal-conditioned values, it lays a foundation for tackling increasingly complex real-world tasks that demand extended sequential decision-making, particularly in domains like robotics, autonomous navigation, and long-term planning, where reliable value estimation over long horizons is crucial.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**
The paper acknowledges two primary limitations that present avenues for future research:
1. **Deterministic Dynamics Assumption:** DCRL, like other triangle-inequality methods, relies on deterministic dynamics, as the exact factorization V_τ(s, g) = V_τ(s, w) · V_τ(w, g) generally does not hold under stochastic dynamics (due to E[XY] ≠ E[X] · E[Y]). Extending DCRL to handle stochastic environments is an important direction.
2. **Performance Discrepancy with Hierarchical Methods:** While DCRL excels on many long-horizon tasks, hierarchical approaches still outperform it on some specific tasks, such as certain cube-manipulation challenges, despite these having shorter horizons than others where DCRL performs strongly. This suggests that factors beyond mere horizon length contribute to task difficulty (e.g., inherent hierarchical structure or multi-modal action spaces). Further research is needed to understand when hierarchical decomposition remains beneficial and how DCRL might be integrated or adapted to such scenarios.
