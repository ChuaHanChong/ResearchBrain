# Three things everyone should know about Vision Transformers

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Meta AI and Sorbonne University present three ways to enhance Vision Transformer performance and efficiency: parallelizing transformer blocks, fine-tuning only attention layers, and using a hierarchical MLP stem for self-supervised learning. These methods achieve better accuracy or reduce resource usage on standard benchmarks like ImageNet-1k.

## Problem

- Vision Transformers (ViTs) face challenges with training efficiency and optimal architectural organization, especially for deeper models.
- Adapting large pre-trained ViTs to new tasks or resolutions typically requires computationally expensive and memory-intensive full fine-tuning.
- Traditional patch pre-processing methods (stems) can interfere with the effectiveness of mask-based self-supervised learning for ViTs.

## Method

- Introduce a **parallel architecture** for ViT transformer blocks, splitting them into multiple branches, with a two-branch setup often performing best.
- Propose an ==attention-only fine-tuning== strategy that updates only the Multi-Head Self-Attention (MHSA) layers while freezing other components.
- Develop a ==hierarchical MLP (hMLP) stem== for patch pre-processing that is compatible with mask-based self-supervised learning.

## Results

- A two-branch parallel ViT-B36 achieved **83.7%** top-1 ImageNet accuracy, surpassing the sequential version's **83.0%**.
- Attention-only fine-tuning yielded comparable or better accuracy (e.g., **84.5%** vs **84.3%** for ViT-B/16 at 384px) while reducing parameters by **25-40%** and memory by **20-30%**.
- The hMLP stem improved unsupervised top-1 accuracy by **0.4-0.8%** for mask-based self-supervised learning (e.g., **83.5%** for hMLP vs. **83.0%** for convolutional stems).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Parallelizing ViT blocks, particularly into two branches, can improve optimization and performance for deeper and higher-capacity models without increasing parameters or FLOPs.
> - Selective fine-tuning of only the attention layers significantly reduces computational cost and memory usage while maintaining or improving accuracy for transfer learning.
> - A hierarchical MLP stem, by preserving patch boundaries, is more compatible with mask-based self-supervised learning methods than traditional convolutional or linear stems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision Transformers (ViTs) have emerged as powerful alternatives to convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for computer vision tasks. Since their introduction, researchers have been exploring ways to optimize their architecture and training procedures. The paper "Three Things Everyone Should Know About Vision Transformers" by Hugo Touvron, Matthieu Cord, Alaaeldin El-Nouby, Jakob Verbeek, and Hervé Jégou from Meta AI and Sorbonne University presents three key insights that can significantly improve the efficiency and performance of ViTs.

#### 1.1. Background on Vision Transformers

Vision Transformers adapt the transformer architecture, originally developed for natural language processing, to computer vision tasks. Unlike CNNs that process images through local convolutional operations, ViTs divide an image into fixed-size patches, linearly embed each patch, and process them with transformer encoders. This design gives ViTs several advantages:

1. They can capture global dependencies in a single layer, unlike CNNs which require multiple layers to achieve a large receptive field
2. They process all patches in parallel, potentially allowing for more efficient computation
3. They have shown strong performance on various vision tasks, especially when pre-trained on large datasets

The standard ViT architecture consists of:
- A patch embedding layer that converts image patches to token embeddings
- A series of transformer blocks, each containing multi-head self-attention (MHSA) and feed-forward network (FFN) layers with residual connections
- A classification head that transforms the class token embedding into class predictions

Despite their success, ViTs present challenges related to training efficiency, adaptation to different tasks and resolutions, and compatibility with modern self-supervised learning approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The researchers identify and address three important aspects of ViTs: parallel processing capabilities, efficient fine-tuning strategies, and improved patch pre-processing methods for self-supervised learning. Through extensive experimentation on standard benchmarks like ImageNet, the authors provide practical recommendations that can be readily implemented by researchers and practitioners working with ViTs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Finding 1: Parallel Processing in Vision Transformers

The first key finding concerns the architectural organization of transformer blocks in ViTs. Traditionally, transformer blocks are arranged sequentially, with each block processing the output of the previous one. The authors investigate whether parallelizing some of these blocks could improve performance.

The proposed parallel architecture splits the transformer blocks into multiple parallel branches, as shown in the figure:

The authors experiment with different numbers of branches and find that a two-branch parallel architecture often yields the best results, especially for deeper models. This approach works particularly well for higher-capacity models that are more difficult to optimize.

The performance improvement from parallelization depends on model size and depth:

```
# Conceptual implementation of parallel ViT with two branches
def parallel_vit_block(x, block1_a, block1_b, block2_a, block2_b):
    # Branch 1
    branch1 = block1_a(x) + block2_a(x)

    # Branch 2
    branch2 = block1_b(x) + block2_b(x)

    # Combine branches
    return branch1 + branch2
```

This parallelization technique can be implemented with minimal changes to existing ViT architectures and yields improved performance without increasing the number of parameters or FLOPs.

#### 3.2. Finding 2: Efficient Fine-Tuning with Attention Layers

The second key finding relates to fine-tuning strategies for ViTs. When adapting a pre-trained ViT to a new task or higher resolution, the common approach is to fine-tune all parameters. However, the authors demonstrate that fine-tuning only the multi-head self-attention (MHSA) layers can be just as effective while significantly reducing computational costs.

As shown in the figure, during fine-tuning, only the MHSA layers are updated (unlocked) while the FFN, Layer Norm, and Patch Projection layers remain frozen (locked). This selective fine-tuning approach offers several advantages:

1. Reduced memory usage: Fine-tuning only the attention layers requires storing fewer gradients and optimizer states
2. Lower computational cost: Fewer parameters need to be updated during backpropagation
3. Weight sharing: The frozen FFN and normalization layers can be shared across multiple tasks

The authors' experiments demonstrate that attention-only fine-tuning is particularly effective for:
- Adapting ViTs to higher image resolutions
- Fine-tuning on small datasets with few training examples
- Transfer learning to new domains and tasks

The reduced resource requirements are clearly shown in the graphs on the right side of the figure, where attention fine-tuning uses significantly fewer parameters, has comparable latency, and requires less memory compared to full fine-tuning.

#### 3.3. Finding 3: Hierarchical MLP Stem for Self-Supervised Learning

The third key finding addresses a limitation of ViTs in self-supervised learning contexts, particularly with mask-based approaches like BeiT. Traditional convolutional stems used for patch pre-processing can interfere with mask-based self-supervised learning methods that rely on masking specific patches.

The authors propose a hierarchical MLP (hMLP) stem as an alternative patch pre-processing method:

The hMLP stem works by:
1. Processing the image in a hierarchical manner, gradually reducing spatial dimensions
2. Using simple MLP operations that maintain patch boundaries, making it compatible with masking strategies
3. Applying the same processing pattern at different scales

Each stage of the hMLP consists of:
```
def hMLP_stage(x, dim_out):
    # Apply patchify operation
    x = patchify(x)

    # Linear projection
    x = linear_layer(x, dim_out)

    # Layer normalization
    x = layer_norm(x)

    # Optional GELU activation
    x = gelu(x)

    return x
```

The effectiveness of the hMLP stem is demonstrated in the comparison with other stems:

The results show that the hMLP stem (green diamond) achieves better performance than both linear projection (golden line) and convolutional stems (yellow circle) when comparing supervised and self-supervised learning approaches. This makes hMLP particularly valuable for researchers working with mask-based self-supervised learning techniques.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup and Methodology

The authors conduct their experiments using established ViT architectures on standard benchmarks:

- **Models**: They use vanilla ViT models and smaller DeiT variants
- **Datasets**: Primary evaluations are on ImageNet-1k, with additional validation on ImageNet-V2 and transfer learning to smaller datasets like iNaturalist, CIFAR, CARS, and Flowers
- **Training**: They adopt a consistent training procedure (A2 setting) specifically adapted for ViTs to ensure stability and strong performance
- **Metrics**: Evaluations focus on top-1 accuracy, parameters, FLOPs, latency, and memory usage

For the parallelization experiments, the authors compare different branch configurations against sequential baselines. For fine-tuning experiments, they compare attention-only fine-tuning with full fine-tuning across various resolutions and datasets. For the hMLP stem, they evaluate it against linear and convolutional stems in both supervised and self-supervised settings.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The key findings from the experimental results include:

1. **Parallel Processing**: Two-branch parallel ViTs consistently outperform sequential counterparts, especially for larger models. The B36 model with two branches achieves 83.7% top-1 accuracy on ImageNet, compared to 83.0% for the sequential version.

2. **Attention Fine-Tuning**: Fine-tuning only the attention layers reaches comparable or better accuracy than full fine-tuning while using significantly fewer parameters (25-40% reduction) and less memory (20-30% reduction). For example, when fine-tuning ViT-B/16 from 224px to 384px resolution, attention-only fine-tuning achieves 84.5% accuracy compared to 84.3% for full fine-tuning.

3. **hMLP Stem**: The hMLP stem improves self-supervised learning performance by 0.4-0.8% compared to other stems when using mask-based methods like BeiT. It achieves 83.5% unsupervised top-1 accuracy compared to 83.0% for convolutional stems and 83.3% for linear projections.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Implications of the Findings

These results have several important implications:

- **Architecture Design**: The parallel processing finding suggests that transformer blocks need not be strictly sequential. Future ViT designs can incorporate parallel branches to improve optimization.

- **Resource Efficiency**: The attention-only fine-tuning strategy enables more efficient adaptation of large pre-trained models to new tasks and resolutions, potentially making ViTs more accessible to researchers with limited computational resources.

- **Self-Supervised Learning**: The hMLP stem can improve the effectiveness of mask-based self-supervised learning approaches, which are increasingly important for leveraging unlabeled data in computer vision.

#### 5.2. Conclusion

The paper "Three Things Everyone Should Know About Vision Transformers" provides valuable insights for researchers and practitioners working with ViTs. The three key findings—parallel processing capabilities, efficient fine-tuning strategies, and improved patch pre-processing methods—offer practical ways to enhance ViT performance and efficiency without radical architectural changes.

These contributions are particularly valuable as ViTs continue to gain adoption in computer vision applications. The parallel design principle could inspire new architectural variations, the attention-only fine-tuning strategy could make adapting large pre-trained models more accessible, and the hMLP stem could advance self-supervised learning approaches.

By focusing on simple and easy-to-implement improvements, the authors have provided actionable recommendations that can be readily incorporated into existing ViT implementations. These findings not only advance our understanding of ViTs but also contribute to making them more efficient, versatile, and easier to train for various computer vision tasks.
