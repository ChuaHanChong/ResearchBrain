# Learning Quadrupedal Locomotion over Challenging Terrain

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from ETH Zurich, KAIST, and Intel developed a robust control system for quadrupedal robots using reinforcement learning and proprioceptive feedback, enabling the ANYmal robot to traverse diverse, challenging natural terrains with zero-shot sim-to-real transfer. This approach allows the robot to handle complex environments without external sensors or fine-tuning, demonstrating emergent behaviors like a foot-trapping reflex.

## Problem

- Designing controllers for legged robots to navigate challenging and unpredictable terrains has traditionally relied on complex state machines and hand-engineered motion primitives that fail in unstructured environments.
- The sim-to-real gap often prevents controllers trained in simulation from performing effectively on physical robots due to modeling inaccuracies.
- External sensors like cameras and LiDAR are often unreliable in challenging conditions such as snow, water, or dense vegetation, limiting robust deployment.

## Method

- A two-stage ==privileged learning== approach trains a ==teacher policy== in simulation with privileged information, then a ==student policy== learns to imitate the teacher using only proprioceptive information available to the physical robot.
- An ==adaptive terrain curriculum== dynamically adjusts simulation difficulty during training, presenting increasingly challenging yet traversable terrains based on the policy's current performance.
- The student policy is implemented as a ==Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN)== that processes a sequence of proprioceptive measurements, enabling it to implicitly reason about terrain properties and contact events.

## Results

- The controller enabled the ANYmal robot to maintain stable locomotion across a wide range of natural and constructed terrains, including mud, snow, water, rocks, and stairs, without terrain-specific tuning.
- The learned controller achieved higher success rates, better speed maintenance on slopes, more consistent heading, and superior performance when carrying payloads or under external disturbances compared to state-of-the-art baseline controllers.
- Successfully deployed in the DARPA Subterranean Challenge, the robots traversed challenging environments with **zero locomotion failures** during multiple missions and adapted to changing surface conditions like varying friction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) processing historical proprioceptive data allows the controller to implicitly reason about terrain properties and contact events without external sensors.
> - The learned controller autonomously developed a foot-trapping reflex, a behavior where the robot actively searches for and establishes stable footholds when traversing difficult terrain.
> - Longer TCN history lengths (up to 2.0 seconds) significantly improved performance on challenging terrains and recovery from disturbances, closely approaching the capabilities of the privileged teacher policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Designing controllers for legged robots that can navigate challenging and unpredictable terrains has been a persistent challenge in robotics. Traditional approaches have relied on complex state machines and hand-engineered motion primitives, which often fail in unstructured environments. This paper presents a robust control system for quadrupedal locomotion that can handle extremely challenging natural terrains without requiring external sensors or terrain information.

The research, conducted by a team from ETH Zurich, KAIST, and Intel, demonstrates a significant advancement in quadrupedal locomotion by using reinforcement learning to train controllers that generalize from simulation directly to real-world environments without fine-tuning. What makes this approach particularly notable is its reliance solely on proprioceptive feedback—the robot's awareness of its own body position, orientation, and forces—rather than external sensors like cameras or LiDAR that might fail in challenging conditions.

### 2. Background and Challenges

Legged locomotion presents several fundamental challenges:

1. **Complex Dynamics**: Legged robots have high-dimensional state spaces with discontinuous dynamics due to intermittent contacts with the ground.

2. **Sim-to-Real Gap**: Controllers trained in simulation often fail on physical robots due to modeling inaccuracies.

3. **Environmental Uncertainty**: Real-world environments contain unpredictable elements like varying friction, unstable surfaces, and obstacles.

4. **Sensor Limitations**: External sensors like cameras can be unreliable in challenging conditions such as snow, water, or dense vegetation.

Previous approaches to quadrupedal locomotion have typically used model-based controllers with predefined gaits, which perform well in controlled environments but struggle with unpredictable terrain. More recent work has explored reinforcement learning for legged locomotion, but most demonstrations have been limited to relatively simple environments or required extensive fine-tuning on physical robots.

### 3. Methodology

The researchers developed a comprehensive methodology combining several innovative techniques to achieve robust quadrupedal locomotion over challenging terrain.

#### 3.1. Privileged Learning Approach

The training process employs a two-stage privileged learning approach:

1. **Teacher Policy Training**: First, a teacher policy is trained in simulation with access to privileged information unavailable to the physical robot, such as ground-truth terrain data and contact forces. This policy quickly learns effective locomotion skills.

2. **Student Policy Training**: A student policy is then trained to imitate the teacher while using only proprioceptive information available on the physical robot.

This approach effectively distills the knowledge from a privileged teacher to a practical student that can operate in the real world. The student policy is implemented as a temporal convolutional network (TCN) that processes a sequence of proprioceptive measurements, enabling it to implicitly reason about terrain properties and contact events from historical data.

#### 3.2. Adaptive Terrain Curriculum

A key innovation in the training process is the use of an adaptive terrain curriculum. During training, the simulation environment dynamically adjusts to present increasingly challenging terrains:

1. The training begins with simple terrains and progressively introduces more difficult scenarios.

2. A particle filter maintains a distribution of terrain parameters that are challenging but traversable.

3. This ensures continuous learning and adaptation without the policy getting stuck on either too simple or impossible terrains.

The terrain parameters include roughness, frequency, and amplitude for hills, as well as step width and height for stairs. The particle filter resamples these parameters based on the policy's current performance, focusing the training on the most instructive terrain configurations.

#### 3.3. Control Architecture

The control architecture integrates several components:

1. **Neural Network Policy**: The core decision-making component that processes proprioceptive information and generates control signals.

2. **Foot Trajectory Generator**: Translates high-level commands into specific foot trajectories.

3. **Motion Tracking**: Uses inverse kinematics and a PD controller to track the desired motions.

This architecture runs at two frequencies: the neural policy at 50 Hz and the motion tracking at 400 Hz, allowing for both high-level decision-making and precise execution.

### 4. Key Innovations

#### 4.1. Temporal Convolutional Network

One of the most important aspects of the controller is the use of a Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) for the student policy. The TCN processes sequences of proprioceptive measurements over time, allowing the controller to implicitly reason about terrain properties and contact events without directly sensing them.

The researchers experimented with different history lengths for the TCN:
- TCN-1: 0.02 seconds of history (1 time step)
- TCN-20: 0.4 seconds of history (20 time steps)
- TCN-100: 2.0 seconds of history (100 time steps)

Results show that longer history lengths significantly improve performance on challenging terrains and recovery from disturbances, approaching the capabilities of the privileged teacher policy.

#### 4.2. Foot Trapping Reflex

Through analysis of the learned controller, the researchers discovered that it had autonomously developed a foot-trapping reflex—a behavior where the robot actively searches for stable footholds when traversing difficult terrain. This emergent behavior was not explicitly programmed.

The foot-trapping reflex involves the robot:
1. Detecting initial contact with an obstacle
2. Actively pressing down to establish stable contact
3. Using this contact point as leverage for forward motion

This behavior emerges from the training process without explicit programming, demonstrating the power of reinforcement learning to discover effective locomotion strategies.

### 5. Experimental Results

#### 5.1. Performance Across Various Terrains

The controller demonstrated exceptional performance across a wide range of challenging terrains:

The experiments included:
- Natural terrains: mud, snow, vegetation, rubble, water
- Constructed obstacles: steps, stairs, slopes
- Varying friction surfaces
- Payload carrying (10 kg additional weight)
- External disturbances (pushing forces)

The controller maintained stable locomotion across all these scenarios without any terrain-specific tuning.

#### 5.2. Comparison with Baseline Controllers

The learned controller was compared with state-of-the-art baseline controllers:

Key results include:
- Higher success rates on challenging obstacles
- Better speed maintenance on steep slopes
- More consistent heading during locomotion
- Superior performance when carrying payloads
- Greater resilience to external forces

Most importantly, while baseline controllers failed completely on many of the more challenging terrains, the learned controller maintained functionality across all tested environments.

### 6. Real-World Deployment

The controller was successfully deployed during the DARPA Subterranean Challenge, where ANYmal robots were used for autonomous exploration of underground environments:

- The robots traversed challenging terrain including mud, gravel, stairs, and slopes
- They maintained stable locomotion while carrying payloads of sensors and computation
- Zero locomotion failures were reported during multiple missions
- The robots successfully completed autonomous exploration tasks in previously unseen environments

The controller also demonstrated the ability to adapt to changing surface conditions. For example, when transitioning from high-friction to low-friction surfaces, the controller automatically adjusted its gait.

### 7. Conclusion

This research represents a significant advancement in quadrupedal locomotion control. The key contributions include:

1. **Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The controller generalizes directly from simulation to real-world environments without fine-tuning.

2. **Proprioceptive Control**: Relying solely on proprioceptive feedback makes the controller robust to sensor failures and environmental conditions.

3. **Emergent Behaviors**: Complex behaviors like the foot-trapping reflex emerge from the training process without explicit programming.

4. **Comprehensive Validation**: The controller was tested on an extensive range of challenging terrains and validated in competitive scenarios.

The approach demonstrates that reinforcement learning, when combined with privileged learning and adaptive terrain curricula, can produce controllers that outperform traditional methods in terms of versatility and robustness. This opens new possibilities for deploying legged robots in applications such as search and rescue, environmental monitoring, and exploration of hazardous environments.

The results suggest that the extraordinary complexity of real-world terrain can be navigated without brittle modeling or dangerous field testing. Instead, carefully designed simulation training can produce controllers that work reliably in diverse, unstructured environments.
