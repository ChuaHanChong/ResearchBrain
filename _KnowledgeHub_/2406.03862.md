# Robust Deep Reinforcement Learning against Adversarial Behavior Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Institute of Science Tokyo, University of Tsukuba, and RIKEN AIP developed a Behavior Imitation Attack (BIA) that manipulates DRL agent behavior under limited access, and proposed Time-Discounted Robust Training (TDRT) as a defense. BIA effectively steers agents toward a target policy using imitation learning, while TDRT significantly mitigates the robustness-performance trade-off, improving original task performance by an average of 28.2% compared to standard policy smoothing while maintaining strong defense.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agents are vulnerable to behavior-targeted adversarial attacks, where an adversary aims to steer the victim's policy towards a complete target policy, distinct from reward minimization.
- Existing behavior-targeted attack methods typically require impractical white-box access to the victim's policy, limiting their applicability in real-world black-box or no-box scenarios.
- Current DRL defense strategies primarily address reward-minimization attacks and often prove ineffective against behavior-targeted attacks, or introduce a significant trade-off by degrading the agent's performance on its original task.

## Method

- The **Behavior Imitation Attack** (BIA) is proposed, which reformulates the behavior-targeted attack objective into a cumulative reward maximization problem in a constructed MDP, enabling attacks without white-box access, and then leverages imitation learning (e.g., GAIL, AILBoost).
- Time-Discounted Robust Training (TDRT) is introduced as a defense, incorporating a time-discounted regularization term into the victim's training objective. This term penalizes policy sensitivity to state changes more strongly in early trajectory stages.
- TDRT uses convex relaxation methods with tools like `auto_LiRPA` to efficiently calculate the KL divergence regularization term, back-propagating gradients only through the perturbed state output.

## Results

- BIA demonstrated attack performance competitive with baselines requiring greater adversary knowledge, effectively manipulating agent behavior under black-box and no-box access settings in Meta-World tasks.
- TDRT-PPO achieved superior robustness against behavior-targeted attacks compared to reward-centric defenses, matching or exceeding the robustness of non-time-discounted policy smoothing methods.
- TDRT-PPO consistently maintained higher clean rewards on original tasks, showing an average improvement of **28.2%** in performance over standard policy smoothing (SA-PPO) while delivering comparable robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The behavior-targeted attack objective can be theoretically reformulated into a cumulative reward maximization problem, allowing adversarial policy training without direct access to the victim's policy parameters.
> - Policy robustness against behavior-targeted attacks is linked to policy sensitivity, and this effect is more pronounced when sensitivity is suppressed in the early stages of trajectories due to time-discounting.
> - Applying time-discounted regularization allows robust training to focus on critical early-stage behaviors, preserving the policy's representational capacity for later stages and thus mitigating the performance-robustness trade-off.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated significant advancements across various domains, including autonomous systems and recommendation engines. However, the deployment of DRL agents in mission-critical applications necessitates a thorough understanding of their vulnerabilities to adversarial attacks. A key area of concern involves adversaries manipulating the state observations an agent perceives, leading to altered behaviors.

Existing research on adversarial attacks in DRL can be broadly categorized into reward-minimization attacks, enchanting attacks, and behavior-targeted attacks. Reward-minimization attacks aim to reduce the victim's cumulative reward, a widely studied area. Enchanting attacks seek to guide the victim to a specific terminal state. Behavior-targeted attacks, the focus of this work, differentiate themselves by aiming to steer the victim's policy toward a complete target policy specified by the adversary, irrespective of the victim's reward function or final state. This distinction is crucial as behavior-targeted attacks can induce subtle, yet undesirable or malicious, long-term behaviors that do not necessarily involve immediate reward degradation or reaching a predefined terminal state.

A significant limitation of prior behavior-targeted attack methods (e.g., Hussenot et al., 2020; Boloor et al., 2020; Bai et al., 2024; 2025) is their reliance on white-box access to the victim's policy, including its architecture and parameters. Such access is often impractical in real-world scenarios, limiting their applicability. This paper aims to address this gap by proposing a novel attack method that functions under limited access conditions.

On the defense front, existing strategies, such as adversarial training, certified defenses, regret-based robust learning, and policy smoothing, primarily focus on mitigating reward-minimization attacks. These defenses often prove ineffective against behavior-targeted attacks because the adversary's target policy is generally unknown to the defender and operates independently of the victim's reward structure. Policy smoothing, while offering some robustness against perturbations, has theoretical guarantees primarily for reward-minimization attacks and can degrade original task performance by imposing excessive constraints. The current work therefore seeks to develop a dedicated defense mechanism for behavior-targeted attacks that also preserves the victim agent's original task performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this study are twofold:
1. **To introduce and develop a novel behavior-targeted attack method that operates under limited access to the victim's policy.**
2. **To propose a corresponding defense strategy specifically designed to counteract behavior-targeted attacks while maintaining the victim's original task performance.**

The motivation for these objectives stems from several critical observations regarding the vulnerabilities of DRL systems and the limitations of current adversarial techniques and countermeasures:

- **Emergence of Novel Threats:** Behavior-targeted attacks represent a distinct and potentially more insidious threat compared to traditional reward-minimization attacks. As illustrated by the autonomous vehicle and recommendation system scenarios, an adversary might not seek to cause an immediate accident or degrade user satisfaction, but rather to subtly manipulate the agent's prolonged behavior (e.g., causing traffic congestion, promoting specific products). Existing defenses that focus solely on reward minimization are insufficient against such sophisticated manipulations.
- **Practical Applicability of Attacks:** Current behavior-targeted attacks generally require white-box access to the victim's policy, which is an unrealistic assumption in many real-world deployments where system internals are proprietary or inaccessible. This limitation restricts the practical impact and generalizability of existing attack methodologies. Therefore, a key motivation is to enable effective behavior manipulation even when the adversary has only black-box or no-box access to the victim's policy. This involves reforming the attack objective to bypass the need for direct policy gradients.
- **Inadequacy of Existing Defenses:** The adversary's target policy in a behavior-targeted attack is unknown to the defender and is independent of the victim's reward function. Consequently, defense mechanisms tailored to detect or prevent reward degradation or specific terminal states are unlikely to provide robust protection. Furthermore, policy smoothing, while a general robustness technique, often introduces a trade-off, leading to significant degradation in the agent's performance on its original, non-adversarial task. This highlights the need for a defense strategy that is specifically designed for the unique challenges of behavior-targeted attacks and can mitigate the performance-robustness trade-off.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need to understand, model, and defend against advanced forms of adversarial manipulation that target an agent's entire behavioral trajectory, under realistic constraints on adversary knowledge, while striving to maintain the agent's primary functional capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Threat Model

The study employs the State-Adversarial Markov Decision Process (SA-MDP) framework (Zhang et al., 2020b). In this model, a victim agent follows a fixed policy ($\pi$). An adversary aims to manipulate the victim's behavior by intervening in its state observations, replacing the true state ($s$) with a false state ($\hat{s}$) chosen from a restricted set $B(s)$. The true state of the environment remains unaltered.

The adversary's access to the victim's policy is categorized into two levels:
- **Black-box:** The adversary can observe the victim's state inputs and the corresponding actions taken.
- **No-box:** The adversary observes only the victim's state inputs.

The adversary's intervention capability is constrained by an $L_\infty$ norm, defining $B(s) = \{\hat{s} | \|\hat{s} - s\|_\infty \leq \epsilon\}$, where $\epsilon$ is the attack budget.

The adversary's objective is to manipulate the victim's behavior to match a specified target policy ($\pi_{tgt}$).

#### 3.2. Attack Method: Behavior Imitation Attack (BIA)

The paper proposes the Behavior Imitation Attack (BIA) by first reformulating the adversary's objective and then applying imitation learning.

- **Reformulation of Adversary's Objective:** The core theoretical contribution for the attack is Theorem 5.1. The adversary's goal is to minimize the divergence $D(\pi \circ \nu, \pi_{tgt})$, where $\pi \circ \nu$ represents the composite policy of the victim under adversarial influence, and $\pi_{tgt}$ is the target policy. Crucially, the paper proves that this objective can be reformulated into a cumulative reward maximization problem in a specially constructed MDP ($\hat{M}$). In this $\hat{M}$ MDP, the adversarial policy ($\nu$) is the policy to be optimized, and the victim's policy ($\pi$) is incorporated into the transition dynamics. This reformulation enables training the adversarial policy without requiring white-box access to $\pi$. The reward function for $\hat{M}$ is derived from the optimal discriminator ($d^*$) of the divergence measure.

- **Leveraging Imitation Learning:** Building on Theorem 5.1, BIA utilizes established imitation learning (IL) algorithms. These algorithms typically learn a policy that mimics an expert (here, the target policy $\pi_{tgt}$) from demonstrations, without needing a reward function. The paper illustrates how BIA can be implemented using:
 - **GAIL (Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning):** As a concrete example of ILfD (Imitation Learning from Demonstration), GAIL uses a discriminator to distinguish between trajectories generated by the composite policy and those from the target policy. By optimizing the adversarial policy to "fool" this discriminator, the composite policy is aligned with the target policy. The discriminator's output (or a transformation thereof) serves as the reward in the $\hat{M}$ MDP.
 - **ILfO (Imitation Learning from Observation):** For the more restrictive no-box setting, BIA can be extended to ILfO algorithms (e.g., GAIfO) where only state sequences of the target behavior are available.
 - **AILBoost:** The paper also shows the applicability of BIA to state-of-the-art ILfD methods like AILBoost, which uses a weighted ensemble of policies.

BIA trains the adversarial policy using demonstrations of the desired behavior, which the adversary can readily prepare. This approach eliminates the need for reward modeling and is environment-agnostic.

#### 3.3. Defense Method: Time-Discounted Robust Training (TDRT)

The paper proposes Time-Discounted Robust Training (TDRT) as a defense against behavior-targeted attacks.

- **Defender's Objective:** The defender's objective is formulated as minimizing a composite loss that includes the negative of the victim's original task reward and a regularization term. This regularization term aims to minimize the worst-case increase in an adversary's gain (defined by a hypothetical reward function $R_{tgt}$ that is maximized when the victim's behavior matches the adversary's specification). Since $R_{tgt}$ is unknown, direct optimization is not possible.

- **Theoretical Insight for Robustness (Theorem 6.1):** Theorem 6.1 provides a key theoretical foundation for TDRT. It states that the increase in the adversary's cumulative reward is bounded by a weighted sum of KL-divergences between the victim's original policy and its policy under adversarial influence, where the weights are discounted by time. This implies two insights:
 - Reducing the sensitivity of the policy's action outputs to state changes (i.e., policy smoothing) improves robustness.
 - This effect is more pronounced when sensitivity is suppressed in the *early stages* of trajectories due to the time-discounting factor ($\gamma^t$).

- **Time-Discounted Regularization:** Motivated by Theorem 6.1, TDRT incorporates a time-discounted regularization term into the victim's training objective. This term, based on the KL divergence between the policy's action distribution at a true state and a perturbed state, penalizes high sensitivity. The time-discounting factor ($\gamma^t$) ensures that regularization is stronger at the beginning of trajectories and gradually weakens. This selective regularization aims to preserve the policy's representational capacity for later stages, thereby mitigating the performance degradation on the original task often seen with uniform policy smoothing.

- **Implementation:** TDRT is implemented within a PPO framework. Direct calculation of the KL term is computationally expensive, so convex relaxation methods are used to obtain tight upper bounds, leveraging tools like `auto_LiRPA`. Gradients for the regularization term are only back-propagated through the perturbed state output, holding the original state output constant.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation was conducted across Meta-World (continuous action spaces), MuJoCo (continuous action spaces), and MiniGrid (discrete action spaces, vision-based control) environments. The key findings are summarized below:

#### 4.1. Attack Performance

- **BIA Effectiveness:** BIA demonstrated attack performance competitive with baseline methods that assume greater adversary knowledge (e.g., Target Reward Maximization attacks requiring access to the adversary's reward function). This indicates that BIA can effectively manipulate behavior using only demonstrations, without explicit reward modeling for the adversarial task.
- **Access Model Impact:** BIA-ILfO (no-box access) achieved nearly identical attack performance to BIA-ILfD (black-box access) in Meta-World tasks. This was attributed to the deterministic nature of state transitions in these environments, allowing sufficient prediction even without observing victim actions.
- **Limitations of Single-Step Optimization:** The Targeted PGD attack, which optimizes perturbations independently at each time step, consistently yielded significantly lower attack rewards compared to BIA. This suggests that considering future decisions through an adversarial policy is crucial for effective behavior-targeted attacks.
- **Task-Specific Difficulty:** All attack methods exhibited relatively lower performance on specific tasks (e.g., Meta-World's window-open, drawer-open, door-lock). This was attributed to greater disparities between the victim's and target policy's state-action distributions in these tasks, making successful manipulation more challenging.
- **Demonstration and Budget Efficiency (Appendices):** BIA's performance remained largely stable with as few as four demonstration episodes but declined with only one. Attack rewards generally increased proportionally with the attack budget ($\epsilon$).
- **Vision-based Limitations:** In MiniGrid with RGB image inputs, the effectiveness of BIA decreased. This limitation is attributed to the increased dimensionality of the state space, making adversarial policy learning significantly more challenging without white-box access, a known issue in such environments.

#### 4.2. Defense Performance

- **Superior Robustness of Policy Smoothing:** TDRT-PPO and SA-PPO (policy smoothing without time-discounting) consistently achieved superior robustness against behavior-targeted attacks across all Meta-World tasks compared to other baselines. This indicates that policy smoothing is an effective defense mechanism for this threat model.
- **Inadequacy of Reward-Centric Defenses:** Adversarial training methods (ATLA-PPO, PA-ATLA-PPO) and regret-based approaches (RAD-PPO) were largely vulnerable to behavior-targeted attacks. This supports the paper's motivation that defenses designed for reward-minimization attacks often fail when the adversary's objective is independent of the victim's reward.
- **WocaR-PPO Performance:** WocaR-PPO, which applies partial smoothness regularization, achieved moderate robustness but generally underperformed SA-PPO and TDRT-PPO, which apply smoothing more broadly.

#### 4.3. Original Task Performance

- **TDRT's Mitigation of Performance Degradation:** TDRT-PPO consistently achieved higher clean rewards (performance on the original task without attack) than SA-PPO across all Meta-World tasks. TDRT-PPO showed an average improvement of 28.2% in original task performance compared to SA-PPO while maintaining comparable robustness. This finding supports the hypothesis that time-discounted regularization, by concentrating its effect on early trajectory stages, preserves the policy's expressiveness for later stages, thereby mitigating performance degradation.
- **Regularization Coefficient Impact (Appendices):** Analysis across varying regularization coefficients ($\lambda$) further confirmed that TDRT-PPO maintains higher clean rewards than SA-PPO for comparable robustness levels, demonstrating its effectiveness across a range of regularization strengths.

#### 4.4. Computational Cost (Appendices)

- BIA training incurs higher costs than Targeted PGD (which has no training cost) but offers amortized efficiency for numerous attack episodes. Compared to reward-based white-box attacks, BIA has higher training costs due to training both a policy and a discriminator, but it removes the need for reward function access. TDRT-PPO's training time is competitive with other robust training methods, being more efficient than adversarial training baselines.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of adversarial robustness in DRL by addressing the specific and under-explored threat of behavior-targeted attacks. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Advancing the Understanding of DRL Vulnerabilities:** By explicitly defining and demonstrating behavior-targeted attacks, the paper highlights a class of vulnerabilities that traditional reward-minimization defenses may overlook. This broadens the scope of adversarial DRL research and encourages a more nuanced understanding of system security.
- **Enabling Realistic Adversarial Analysis:** The proposed Behavior Imitation Attack (BIA) is a significant step towards more realistic adversarial modeling. By operating under limited (black-box or no-box) access to the victim's policy, BIA removes a common, impractical assumption found in prior behavior-targeted attacks. This increased realism could facilitate more practical security evaluations of DRL systems in development and deployment.
- **Providing a Dedicated Defense Strategy:** Time-Discounted Robust Training (TDRT) offers the first defense strategy specifically designed for behavior-targeted attacks. Its theoretical foundation, linking policy sensitivity to robustness and emphasizing early-stage trajectory control, provides a novel approach to building resilient DRL agents.
- **Mitigating the Robustness-Performance Trade-off:** A key challenge in DRL robustness is the trade-off between enhancing robustness and maintaining original task performance. TDRT demonstrates empirical success in mitigating this trade-off by intelligently applying time-discounted regularization. This is crucial for the practical adoption of robust DRL, as performance degradation can be a deterrent for real-world deployments.
- **Implications for Mission-Critical Systems:** For applications like autonomous vehicles, medical systems, or critical infrastructure control, where subtle behavioral manipulations could have severe consequences, the findings are directly relevant. The ability to both analyze and defend against behavior manipulation is critical for ensuring the safety, reliability, and trustworthiness of DRL-powered systems.

**Limitations:** The paper acknowledges limitations, including the decreased effectiveness of BIA in high-dimensional state spaces (e.g., image inputs) under black-box access, which remains an open challenge. Additionally, TDRT, while empirically effective, currently lacks certified robustness guarantees, suggesting an area for future theoretical work, particularly for scenarios demanding the highest levels of reliability.
