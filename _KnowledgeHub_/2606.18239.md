# EBench: Elemental Diagnosis of Generalist Mobile Manipulation Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> EBench provides a unified simulation benchmark that diagnoses generalist mobile manipulation policies using a multi-dimensional evaluation, going beyond aggregate success rates. It reveals specific capability profiles and generalization behaviors of Vision-Language-Action models across tasks involving long-horizon planning, dexterous manipulation, and mobile robot navigation.

## Problem

- Current evaluation of general-purpose manipulation policies relies on aggregate success rates from simulation suites, which are often narrow in scope or lack detailed analysis beyond a single scalar.
- Existing benchmarks fail to provide fine-grained insights into where a model excels or fails, or how its performance changes under varying deployment distributions.
- No single benchmark simultaneously offers a broad task suite encompassing long-horizon, dexterous-and-precise, and mobile manipulation while also allowing for fine-grained, multi-dimensional analysis.

## Method

- Developed **EBench**, an open-source simulation benchmark with 26 diverse manipulation tasks spanning mobile pick-and-place, long-horizon multi-stage, and tabletop dexterous-and-precise categories.
- Tasks are manually annotated along ==five capability dimensions== (scene, atomic skill, temporal horizon, precision, operating mode) to enable fine-grained diagnostic profiling.
- Introduced four controlled generalization dimensions (unseen backgrounds, unseen objects, paraphrased instructions, and their mixture) with asset-level isolation to systematically evaluate robustness to out-of-distribution shifts.

## Results

- Aggregate test success rates for state-of-the-art VLA models ranged narrowly from **24.4%** to **29.5%**, but their underlying capabilities diverged, with 𝜋0.5 showing the highest test SR and InternVLA-A1 exhibiting strong in-distribution performance but weaker generalization.
- Generalization studies revealed a hierarchy of difficulty: background and instruction perturbations were mild (27–**35%** SR), object swaps posed a stronger challenge (21–**29%** SR), and mixed shifts were the most difficult (18–**23%** SR).
- EBench identified five 'universally hard tasks' (e.g., `peg_in_hole`, `flip_cup_collect_cookies`) where all evaluated models achieved ≤ **5%** success rate, representing current frontiers for generalist policies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generalist manipulation models with similar aggregate success rates exhibit significantly different underlying capability profiles and generalization behaviors when diagnosed across multiple dimensions.
> - Different Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models show varied proficiencies, such as one excelling in mobile manipulation but failing at dexterous tasks, while another demonstrates stronger temporal credit assignment.
> - Large-scale pretraining consistently provides substantial performance gains (9-21 SR points) on EBench, unlike other benchmarks where from-scratch models often perform comparably.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of EBench is situated within the broader context of evaluating general-purpose manipulation policies in robotics. Prior simulation benchmarks for manipulation, such as RLBench, CALVIN, and LIBERO, have provided standardized evaluation environments but often focus on narrow regimes like short-horizon, fixed-base pick-and-place tasks. While more extensive efforts like RoboCasa, RoboTwin, and GenManip have broadened task and embodiment coverage, they typically concentrate on a single interaction modality (e.g., tabletop rearrangement or mobile manipulation) or report only aggregate success rates. Diagnostic benchmarks like RMBench exist, but often isolate a single capability axis (e.g., memory). Even comprehensive environments like BEHAVIOR-1K, which illustrate broad task types, tend to fall back on overall scores, obscuring fine-grained performance details.

This existing landscape presents a challenge: current benchmarks often provide a single scalar success rate, which does not sufficiently diagnose where a generalist model excels, where it fails, or how its performance changes under distribution shifts. There is an identified need for a benchmark that offers a broader suite of tasks, including long-horizon, dexterous, and mobile manipulation, within a unified evaluation protocol. Furthermore, such a benchmark should be instrumented for fine-grained analysis rather than relying solely on a single leaderboard scalar.

EBench addresses these gaps by providing a benchmark that simultaneously covers a wide spectrum of manipulation tasks and offers rich annotations along multiple capability and generalization dimensions. This approach enables a decomposed, interpretable analysis of model strengths and weaknesses, moving beyond aggregate success rates to provide diagnostic signals that can guide the development of generalist manipulation models. The work also aims to provide a standardized platform for comparing different vision-language-action (VLA) models, which have typically been evaluated on hardware-specific real-robot runs or narrow simulation subsets. Additionally, EBench is designed to effectively measure the impact of large-scale pretraining on policy performance, a factor that existing benchmarks like LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0 often fail to differentiate due to saturation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce EBench, a simulation benchmark designed to diagnose generalist mobile manipulation policies beyond a single success-rate scalar. The motivation stems from several critical limitations observed in the current state of robotics evaluation:

1. **Lack of Fine-Grained Diagnostic Capabilities**: Existing benchmarks primarily report aggregate success rates, which do not reveal specific strengths or weaknesses of a manipulation policy. This makes it difficult for researchers to understand *why* a model performs well or poorly, or *where* it breaks. EBench aims to decompose this aggregate score into an interpretable capability profile along multiple dimensions.
2. **Insufficient Task Diversity and Coverage**: Many benchmarks focus on narrow slices of physical interaction, such as tabletop pick-and-place. There is a need for an evaluation suite that broadly covers long-horizon, dexterous-and-precise, and mobile manipulation regimes within a single protocol. EBench seeks to integrate these diverse task families to provide a more comprehensive assessment of generalist policies.
3. **Limited Generalization Analysis**: Understanding how policies perform when deployed in environments that differ from their training distribution is crucial. Current benchmarks often lack controlled methods for evaluating out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. EBench explicitly incorporates four controlled generalization dimensions to systematically analyze the impact of different distribution shift factors.
4. **Challenges in Data Collection for Diverse Behaviors**: Collecting high-quality demonstration data for varied tasks presents significant challenges. Dexterous tasks require precise human teleoperation, long-horizon tasks are prone to human error, and mobile manipulation is difficult to coordinate. EBench aims to overcome these by coupling a two-stream data synthesis pipeline: human teleoperation for dexterous tasks and key-frame pose with motion planning for mobile and long-horizon tasks.
5. **Difficulty in Assessing Pretraining Impact**: A central question for generalist manipulation is the contribution of large-scale pretraining. Many existing benchmarks are largely saturated, meaning that models trained from scratch achieve comparable or even superior performance to pretrained models. This obscures the benefits of pretraining. EBench aims to provide an evaluation environment where the benefits of large-scale pretraining are evident and measurable.

By addressing these motivations, EBench intends to offer a comprehensive and diagnostic platform that generates a broad set of signals, thereby guiding the iterative development and improvement of generalist manipulation models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EBench's methodology integrates a diverse task suite, a specialized data synthesis pipeline, controlled generalization dimensions, and a robust evaluation protocol to provide a diagnostic assessment of generalist manipulation policies.

#### 3.1. Task Design and Diversity

EBench features 26 manipulation tasks organized into three families:
- **Mobile Pick-and-Place (10 tasks)**: Involves mobile base movement to pick and place objects, with episode lengths of 600–1,000 simulation steps.
- **Mobile Long-Horizon (9 tasks)**: Multi-stage mobile sequences requiring extended robot operation, typically 3,000–5,000 steps.
- **Table-Top Dexterous-and-Precise (7 tasks)**: Fixed-base tasks demanding sub-centimeter tolerance for operations like insertion, alignment, and bimanual coordination, with 1,500–3,500 steps.

The physics simulation runs at 60 Hz. All tasks share a unified action space for a dual-arm robot mounted on a mobile base: 6-DoF joint position or end-effector pose for each arm, per-arm gripper width, and 3-D velocity commands for the base.
Each task is annotated along five capability dimensions:
- **Scene (9 categories)**: Bedroom, Bathroom, Kitchen, Living Room, Study, Dining Room, Supermarket, Industrial, Logistics.
- **Atomic Skill (11 categories)**: Grasp, Place, Push, Pull, Press, Insert, Pour, Flip, Sweep, Handover.
- **Temporal Horizon (2 categories)**: Short Horizon (< 2,000 steps) and Long Horizon (≥ 2,000 steps).
- **Precision (3 categories)**: Low (≥ 10 cm), Medium (< 10 cm, ≥ 1 cm), High (< 1 cm).
- **Operating Mode (2 categories)**: Mobile, Fixed but Dexterous.

Scene assets are sourced from GRUtopia and InternScenes, and object assets from Objaverse.

#### 3.2. Data Synthesis: Teleoperation and Motion Planning

To generate demonstrations for diverse behaviors while overcoming collection challenges, EBench employs two complementary data collection streams:
1. **Teleoperation for Dexterous-and-Precise Tasks**: The 7 dexterous tasks are collected via a kinematically isomorphic actor-follower setup, allowing for reactive feedback and dynamic adjustments crucial for contact-rich interactions (e.g., peg-in-hole). Each dexterous-and-precise task contributes 400 teleoperated episodes.
2. **Key-frame Pose and cuRobo for Mobile and Long-Horizon Tasks**: For the remaining 19 tasks, annotators specify sparse sequences of key-frame end-effector poses and base waypoints. cuRobo (Sundaralingam et al., 2023) then generates collision-free, minimum-jerk trajectories connecting these points. This approach facilitates the generation of thousands of episodes per task and supports randomization. Each mobile pick-and-place and long-horizon task contributes 200 motion-planned episodes.

The resulting post-training dataset contains 91.4 hours of demonstrations across 6,600 episodes, organized in LeRobot format, with frame-level balancing between teleoperated and motion-planned trajectories.

#### 3.3. Generalization Dimensions

EBench evaluates four controlled generalization dimensions to assess robustness to distribution shifts:
1. **Background**: Replaces scene textures and lighting with unseen variants, keeping objects and instructions fixed.
2. **Object**: Swaps manipulated entities for geometrically distinct unseen instances of the same category.
3. **Instruction**: Paraphrases natural-language commands while preserving the operational goal.
4. **Mix**: Combines background, object, and instruction perturbations.

Train and test sets are isolated at the asset level, ensuring that scene textures, object instances, and instruction paraphrases used for training are disjoint from those in the test pool.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Protocol and Metric Library

The primary protocol requires a single model checkpoint to solve all 26 tasks.
- **Validation Set**: Split into Val-Train (130 in-distribution episodes, 5 per task) and Val-Unseen (154 episodes with object swaps from unseen assets).
- **Test Set**: Comprises 510 episodes, covering all four generalization dimensions (20 per task for 24 tasks; 15 per task for two long-horizon tasks), constructed from a disjoint pool of assets.

Two metrics are used:
- **Success Rate (SR)**: A binary signal (primary metric) indicating task completion.
- **Task Score**: Rewards partial progress through the task, computed stage-by-stage. Stages are defined as ordered sequences of conditions over simulator state, represented by shared evaluation primitives (e.g., scene-graph relations, joint angles, object tilt, pose tolerances, temporal ordering). This stage-based scoring system replaces per-task hand-coded evaluators, enhancing comparability across tasks.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

Four recent vision-language-action (VLA) models were evaluated: 𝜋0, 𝜋0.5, XVLA, and InternVLA-A1. All models were fine-tuned from pretrained checkpoints on the EBench training data for 200K gradient steps using a batch size of 128, AdamW optimizer, and a cosine learning-rate scheduler. Observations included 224x224 RGB images from left, right, and top-down views, proprioceptive state, and natural-language instructions, all queried at 60 Hz. Each model was evaluated three times to account for non-deterministic rendering, and mean and standard deviation are reported.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of generalist manipulation models on EBench yielded several key findings regarding their capabilities and generalization abilities.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance

The four evaluated VLA models (𝜋0, 𝜋0.5, XVLA, and InternVLA-A1) exhibited similar aggregate test success rates (SRs) within a narrow range of 24.4–29.5%. However, their in-distribution behaviors and robustness to distribution shifts varied.
- **𝜋0.5** achieved the highest test SR (29.5%) and the strongest train–test retention ratio (SR: 0.92, Score: 0.95), suggesting its validation performance is a reliable predictor of held-out capability.
- **InternVLA-A1** showed the highest Val-Train SR (33.1%) but experienced a sharp decline on Val-Unseen (20.8% SR), resulting in weaker retention (0.83 SR retention). This indicates strong in-distribution fitting but limited robustness to distribution shifts.
- **𝜋0** had a comparable test SR (24.4%) but the lowest retention ratio (0.80), suggesting a tendency towards overfitting to the training distribution.

#### 4.2. Five-Dimensional Capability Breakdown

Decomposing the test SR along EBench’s five capability axes revealed distinct performance profiles among the models, despite their similar aggregate scores.
- **Operating Mode**: InternVLA-A1 performed competitively on mobile manipulation (around 34.7% SR), but its performance significantly dropped on dexterous fixed-base tasks (5.8% SR), showing the largest mobile-to-dexterous gap (29 points). This indicates proficiency in navigation-scale decision-making but a lack of fine-grained contact control. In contrast, 𝜋0 showed a more balanced profile with a smaller 11-point gap between mobile (29.2%) and dexterous (18.1%) tasks, though at lower absolute performance than 𝜋0.5.
- **Precision and Horizon**: On sub-centimeter high-precision tasks, 𝜋0 led at 13.8% SR, while other models achieved single-digit SRs. For low-precision tasks, 𝜋0.5 achieved the best performance at 44.2% SR. Short-horizon tasks were generally easier (24–32% SR for all models), but long-horizon tasks exposed larger performance gaps. InternVLA-A1 achieved the highest SR on long-horizon tasks (29.1%), while XVLA experienced a significant drop (from 28.9% on short to 13.5% on long-horizon tasks), suggesting weaker temporal credit assignment.
- **Atomic Skills and Scenes**: No single model consistently dominated all eleven atomic skills. 𝜋0 led on Pull (47%) and Press (50%). XVLA was strong on Push (73.8%) but weak on Handover (5.8%). InternVLA-A1 excelled at Move and Sweep but scored 0% on Press and Flip. 𝜋0.5 was the only model without any catastrophic-zero categories. Scene-level rankings also varied, with 𝜋0.5 performing best in Bedroom, Bathroom, and Living Room, InternVLA-A1 in Kitchen and Dining scenes, and XVLA in Supermarket settings. This indicated model-specific inductive biases across different skill sets and environments.

#### 4.3. Generalization Diagnosis

Evaluation across different training steps and generalization dimensions provided insights into fit-generalization dynamics and robustness.
- **Fit–Generalization Dynamics**: Training performance (Val-Train SR) generally improved with more gradient steps for all models. However, the transfer of these gains to held-out test rollouts was model-dependent. 𝜋0.5 demonstrated the most stable dynamics, with Val-Train and Test SR curves rising together and the highest final Test SR. InternVLA-A1 achieved the highest final Val-Train SR but retained a larger Test gap, suggesting its additional fitting benefited the training distribution more than out-of-distribution (OOD) rollouts.
- **Generalization Across Axes**: The difficulty hierarchy of generalization dimensions was clear: background and instruction perturbations were relatively mild, object-level physical changes were harder, and their combination (Mix) was the most challenging. All models maintained 27–35% SR under Background and Instruction perturbations, indicating perceptual and linguistic robustness. However, Object swaps reduced SR to 21–29%, highlighting the challenge of physical generalization to new object geometries and mass distributions. The Mix setting further lowered SR to 18–23%, demonstrating that compositional distribution shifts amplify failure modes. Overall, 𝜋0.5 was the most robust baseline, leading on Background, Object, and Mix, while InternVLA-A1 excelled at Instruction generalization.

#### 4.4. Pretraining Sensitivity Across Benchmarks

A comparative study of models with and without pretraining on EBench, LIBERO, and RoboTwin 2.0 revealed EBench's unique ability to differentiate the impact of large-scale pretraining.
- **On EBench**: Pretraining significantly improved performance for every architecture, with gains of 9–21 SR points (e.g., 𝜋0.5 from 8.5% to 29.5% SR, and 𝜋0 from 11.2% to 24.4% SR). This indicates a large and consistent pretrained-from-scratch gap on EBench.
- **On LIBERO and RoboTwin 2.0 Hard**: These benchmarks showed saturation. From-scratch models achieved high scores (94–98% on LIBERO, 88.8–91.8% on RoboTwin 2.0 Hard), often matching or surpassing their pretrained counterparts. This suggests that these benchmarks are not designed to effectively measure the contribution of large-scale pretraining for generalist policies.

This finding positions EBench as a suitable benchmark for measuring the effect of large-scale pretraining.

#### 4.5. Additional Observations

- **Task-Level Complementarity**: The per-task heatmap (Figure 6) illustrated significant complementarity between model families. For instance, the 𝜋-family and XVLA excelled on disjoint sets of tasks.
- **Universally Hard Tasks**: Five tasks ("shop", "bottle", "peg_in_hole", "collect_coffee_beans", "flip_cup_collect_cookies") proved challenging for all baselines, with SRs ≤ 5% across multiple evaluations. These represent a "hard suite" indicating the current frontier of generalist policy capability, often requiring force-aware feedback loops.
- **Camera-Perspective Sensitivity (Appendix B)**: Analysis showed that the optimal camera perspective (Headview vs. Overview) was architecture-dependent, rather than universally dictated by task family. For 𝜋0, Headview was slightly preferred, especially for dexterous tasks, while 𝜋0.5 benefited from Overview, particularly for mobile tasks. The magnitude of these effects was generally smaller than cross-model differences.
- **Controlled Breakdown Analysis (Appendix E)**: Permutation tests were used to provide controlled estimates of tag effects, mitigating multi-factor confounding. This analysis confirmed significant effects for certain model-tag pairs (e.g., InternVLA-A1's mobile advantage, 𝜋0.5 's low-precision advantage and Insert task penalty), while showing wider null distributions for others (e.g., scene contrasts) due to smaller sample sizes, suggesting that some observed scene rankings might reflect underlying operating-mode or precision compositions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

EBench represents a contribution to the field of generalist robot manipulation by addressing several limitations of existing evaluation paradigms. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enabling Fine-Grained Diagnosis**: By providing a rich set of 26 tasks annotated across five capability dimensions (scene, atomic skill, temporal horizon, precision, operating mode) and four generalization dimensions (unseen backgrounds, unseen objects, paraphrased instructions, and their mixture), EBench moves beyond single scalar success rates. This allows researchers to perform elemental diagnosis of policies, identifying specific strengths and weaknesses, rather than merely observing aggregate performance. This detailed profiling can guide targeted improvements in model architectures and training strategies.
2. **Broadening Task Coverage in a Unified Protocol**: EBench integrates long-horizon, dexterous-and-precise, and mobile manipulation tasks within a single evaluation framework. This unified protocol is essential for assessing generalist policies, as it demonstrates a model's ability to operate across diverse physical interaction regimes, a combination that existing benchmarks individually approximate but jointly avoid.
3. **Advancing Generalization Research**: The controlled generalization dimensions provide a systematic means to evaluate how manipulation policies respond to various distribution shifts. This is crucial for developing robust policies that can perform reliably in real-world, dynamic environments where out-of-distribution scenarios are common. The benchmark identifies the hierarchy of difficulty for different types of perturbations, informing future research on robustness.
4. **Measuring the Impact of Large-Scale Pretraining**: EBench has demonstrated its ability to effectively differentiate the performance gains brought by large-scale pretraining, unlike other saturated benchmarks. This makes it a valuable tool for researchers investigating the role and benefits of foundation models and pretraining in generalist robotics.
5. **Standardized and Reproducible Evaluation**: The public release of the EBench tasks, the synthesized 91.4-hour dataset, and the evaluation code establishes a standardized and reproducible platform. This enables fair comparisons among different generalist manipulation models and fosters collaborative progress in the field. The distributed evaluation runner further facilitates rapid iterative development.
6. **Guiding Model Iteration and Design**: The diagnostic signals provided by EBench can directly inform the design and iteration cycles of new generalist manipulation models. For instance, the observation that no single VLA model dominates all atomic skills or operating modes suggests that the field has not converged on a single optimal inductive bias, encouraging further exploration of architectural choices and training methodologies. The identification of "universally hard tasks" points to specific challenges that future research must overcome to advance the frontier of generalist manipulation.

**Limitations**: The paper acknowledges that EBench operates entirely in simulation and does not claim to predict real-robot performance directly. It is presented as a reproducible screening substrate that precedes physical evaluation. Additionally, the 26-task suite covers 9 scene categories sparsely, which means scene-level rankings should be interpreted as preliminary. The authors plan to expand the task suite to enable more robust multi-way regression analyses.
