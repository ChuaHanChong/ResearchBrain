# CALM: Configuration-Aware Human Intervention Boundaries During Robot Approach

> [!summary] Summary
> CALM (Configuration-Aware Limit Model) turns human stopping behavior into configuration-dependent approach boundaries for humanoid robots. A 41-participant within-subjects study varied four arm postures and two room scales during frontal approach; full forward extension pushed stopping distance out 31-36 cm. Quantile boundaries at 80% population coverage span 0.88-1.47 m, and arm retraction recovered a 1.10 m goal unreachable while extended.

## Problem

- Proxemics research varies robot appearance, speed, and approach direction but treats the robot body as fixed, leaving configuration changes of one humanoid largely unexamined.
- Whole-body planners model configuration-dependent geometry and human-aware navigation models social cost fields, but no empirical link connects body configuration to human intervention behavior.
- Industrial safety standards define physical separation but not the distance at which a permissible approach becomes unacceptable; prior work also conflates discomfort with behavioral intervention.

## Method

- A 2x4 within-participants frontal-approach study on a Booster T1 humanoid at 0.4 m/s: four arm extensions (0/25/35/45 cm) crossed with 2.5x5.0 m and 5.0x5.0 m rooms, measuring button-press stopping distance, comfort, and pupillometry.
- The **Configuration-Aware Limit Model (CALM)** defines each boundary as the qth ==Hyndman-Fan type-7 sample quantile== of per-configuration stopping distance, with 95% intervals from a 5,000-replicate participant-level ==cluster bootstrap==.
- A one-dimensional planning analysis compares **Fixed-clearance**, **CALM-fixed**, and **CALM-reconfig** policies on a 0.01 m grid, minimizing a goal-error, travel, and configuration-switch cost under a pointwise 20% intervention-probability constraint.

## Results

- Arm configuration shifted stopping distance, **F(3,302)=36.65**, **p<.001**; Straight exceeded Vertical by **36.07 cm** in the small room and **31.13 cm** in the large, with no space effect (**p=.114**).
- Boundaries at 80% coverage were **0.88**, **1.18**, **1.26** and **1.47 m** for Vertical through Straight, non-linear in arm extension: a **25 cm** extension shifted the boundary **30 cm**, a later **10 cm** shifted **8 cm**.
- CALM-reconfig completed **10 of 17 goals (58.8%)** versus **3 (17.6%)** for CALM-fixed; Fixed-clearance hit **1.10 m** but at **0.358** intervention probability, versus **0.078** after retraction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Treat body configuration as a planning variable: reconfiguring the arms can restore a close-approach goal without loosening the human-response constraint, cheaper than replanning the base path.
> - Keep physical safety, behavioral intervention risk, and subjective cost as separate layers; collapsing them into one social radius hides that they respond to different factors.
> - Discomfort and arousal can rise with no change in the action threshold, so a null behavioral result is weak evidence that two contexts are equivalent.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of human–robot interaction, social robotics, robot proxemics, whole-body motion planning, and human-aware navigation. A substantial body of prior research has examined how people regulate distance from robots. These studies have shown that approach direction, robot appearance, voice, motion speed, interaction context, individual differences, and prior experience can influence the distance at which people prefer to interact with or avoid a robot. However, much of this literature treats the robot as a relatively stable physical entity. The present paper focuses on a less-studied variable: changes in the body configuration of the same robot.

The issue is relevant for humanoid robots and mobile manipulators because their spatial envelope changes as their arms, torso, grasp, or payload configuration changes. A robot base can remain in the same position while an extended arm substantially changes the robot’s nearest physical point and its apparent reaching capability. Existing whole-body planners generally account for configuration-dependent geometry when checking collisions and motion feasibility. In contrast, human-aware navigation systems often represent social preferences using fixed personal-space models, learned social costs, or configuration-independent spatial fields. The paper addresses the gap between these two areas by empirically examining whether robot posture changes the distance at which people actively intervene.

The study also distinguishes three related but nonidentical dimensions of human response. **Physical safety** concerns collision hazards, braking distance, sensing uncertainty, and control reliability. **Behavioral intervention** concerns whether a person actively stops or interrupts an approaching robot. **Subjective and physiological response** concerns comfort, perceived confinement, visual attention, arousal, or cognitive processing demand. The authors argue that these layers should not be collapsed into one social-distance value.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation is that a configuration-independent human–robot clearance may be inadequate for articulated robots. If a robot approaches with its arms extended, people may intervene earlier than they would when the same robot approaches with its arms down, even when the robot’s base follows the same path and moves at the same speed. Such a difference could affect delivery, guidance, handover, service, and collaborative tasks in occupied indoor environments.

The paper defines the distance at which a person chooses to stop the robot as the **human intervention boundary**. It asks three research questions:

1. **Configuration effects:** How do four humanoid arm configurations affect stopping distance and subjective comfort during a frontal approach?
2. **Spatial context:** How does the scale of the surrounding room affect behavioral, subjective, and eye-tracking responses, and does it moderate the configuration effect?
3. **Planning representation:** Can stopping-distance distributions be converted into configuration-dependent population boundaries, and can robot reconfiguration make a close approach feasible under a fixed intervention-risk constraint?

To answer the third question, the authors introduce the **Configuration-Aware Limit Model**, or **CALM**. CALM represents an intervention boundary as a selected quantile of the observed stopping-distance distribution. For example, an 80% coverage boundary is intended to represent the distance by which approximately 80% of the sampled population would have allowed the robot to approach without intervening, subject to the limitations of the experimental measurement process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Participants and Experimental Design

The study recruited 41 participants from a university community: 16 men and 25 women. The mean age was 24.73 years, with a standard deviation of 5.25 and an age range of 18–44 years. Fourteen participants reported previous direct interaction with physical robots, while 27 did not. Participants had normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity and provided informed consent.

The experiment used a **2 × 4 within-participants design**. The two spatial conditions were:

- **Space A:** 2.5 × 5.0 meters, representing the narrower environment.
- **Space B:** 5.0 × 5.0 meters, representing the larger environment.

The four arm configurations were defined by forward hand extension from the torso:

- **Vertical:** 0 cm.
- **Flexed:** 25 cm.
- **Extended:** 35 cm.
- **Straight:** 45 cm.

Each participant experienced all eight combinations of spatial condition and arm configuration in randomized order.

#### 3.2. Robot and Procedure

The platform was a Booster T1 humanoid robot, approximately 1.18 meters tall, 47 centimeters wide, and 30 kilograms in mass, with 23 degrees of freedom. It approached seated participants along a straight frontal path. The robot began 3.5 meters from the participant-centered reference point and moved at a commanded speed of 0.4 m/s. Video verification indicated a mean actual speed of 0.40 m/s with a standard deviation of 0.02 m/s.

The assigned arm posture remained static during each approach. Participants were instructed to press a mouse button when the robot reached the closest distance they still considered comfortable. The button press initiated the robot’s standard stopping command. Importantly, the primary stopping-distance measure was taken **after the robot had fully stopped**, not at the instant of the button press. A depth-camera system measured the final two-dimensional distance between the robot and the center of the participant’s chair.

#### 3.3. Measures

The primary behavioral measure was final stopping distance. Subjective response was assessed after every trial using five seven-point Likert-scale items concerning perceived safety, postural intimidation, personal-space intrusion, motion predictability, and spatial constraint. The recoded items were averaged into a comfort score, with higher values indicating greater comfort. The questionnaire showed high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha equal to 0.898.

Exploratory eye tracking was conducted using head-mounted Dikablis Glasses X equipment at 120 Hz. The analyzed measures were peak pupil diameter, standard deviation of pupil area, and mean fixation duration. Pupil signals were corrected using a participant-specific resting baseline. Short gaps caused by blinks or tracking loss were interpolated, whereas longer gaps were excluded.

#### 3.4. Statistical and Modeling Methods

Each outcome was analyzed with a linear mixed-effects model containing arm configuration, spatial condition, and their interaction as fixed effects, together with a participant-level random intercept. Post-hoc comparisons used estimated marginal means and Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction.

CALM was estimated by calculating the 50th, 80th, and 90th percentiles of the stopping-distance distribution for each configuration and spatial condition. The authors used CALM80 as their principal planning boundary. Confidence intervals were obtained through 5,000 participant-level cluster-bootstrap resamples, preserving all repeated observations belonging to each sampled participant.

The paper also presents an illustrative one-dimensional planning analysis. A robot initially in the Straight configuration approached a goal while obeying a pointwise predicted intervention-probability constraint of no more than 20%. Three policies were compared: a fixed-clearance policy, a CALM-fixed policy that retained the initial configuration, and a CALM-reconfig policy that could change configuration after stopping. The analysis used a 0.01-meter grid and evaluated goals between 0.80 and 1.60 meters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Arm Configuration and Stopping Distance

Arm configuration significantly affected stopping distance, with **F(3,302) = 36.65, p < .001**. Neither spatial condition nor the configuration-by-space interaction was statistically significant. In the narrower Space A, estimated stopping distances were approximately:

- Vertical: **62.0 cm**
- Flexed: **68.3 cm**
- Extended: **76.0 cm**
- Straight: **98.1 cm**

In Space B, the corresponding estimates were:

- Vertical: **66.5 cm**
- Flexed: **76.3 cm**
- Extended: **79.3 cm**
- Straight: **97.6 cm**

Relative to Vertical, Straight increased stopping distance by approximately 31–36 cm, depending on spatial condition. The results therefore indicate a systematic relationship between forward arm extension and earlier human intervention. The effect was not simply proportional to the additional geometric extension: the 25 cm change from Vertical to Flexed produced an approximately 30 cm change in the CALM80 boundary, while later extension increments produced smaller or differently sized shifts.

#### 4.2. Subjective Comfort

Arm configuration also had a significant effect on comfort, **F(3,304) = 48.91, p < .001**. Spatial condition had a smaller but statistically significant effect, **F(1,304) = 4.44, p = .036**, while the interaction was not significant.

Vertical received the highest comfort ratings in both spaces. Straight received the lowest. In Space A, comfort scores were approximately 4.12 for Vertical, 3.33 for Flexed, 3.36 for Extended, and 3.01 for Straight. The same ordering appeared in Space B.

Participants’ post-study reports indicated that the narrower space was subjectively meaningful. Thirty-six of 41 participants reported feeling more constrained by the robot’s proximity in Space A, and 28 reported greater sensitivity to the approach there. Distance was the most frequently cited factor influencing stopping decisions, followed by approach speed, approach sound, perceived confinement, and room spaciousness.

#### 4.3. Eye-Tracking Responses

Spatial scale had clearer effects on pupil measures than on stopping distance. Peak pupil diameter was significantly affected by both arm configuration and space, but the spatial effect was much larger. Values were approximately 4.70–5.84 pixels higher in Space A than in Space B across configurations. Pupil-area variability was also substantially higher in Space A, with a significant space effect but no overall arm-configuration effect.

Mean fixation duration showed no significant overall effects of configuration, space, or their interaction. Some exploratory pairwise differences appeared within Space A, but the authors interpret these as local findings rather than evidence of a robust configuration effect. The cumulative eye-tracking trajectories are descriptive and should not be treated as estimates of a physiological warning threshold because trial durations varied and the number of contributing trials changed over time.

#### 4.4. CALM Boundaries

After pooling across the two spatial conditions, the estimated CALM80 boundaries were:

- **Vertical:** 0.88 m
- **Flexed:** 1.18 m
- **Extended:** 1.26 m
- **Straight:** 1.47 m

The pooling decision was made because spatial condition did not significantly affect stopping distance, although the authors explicitly state that this does not establish equivalence between the environments.

#### 4.5. Planning Analysis

For a 1.10-meter approach goal, a robot remaining in Straight reached its CALM80 boundary at 1.47 meters and could not proceed under the 20% pointwise intervention-probability constraint. The reconfiguration policy first stopped at 1.47 meters, changed to Vertical, and then continued to the 1.10-meter goal while maintaining the same nominal constraint. Across 17 simulated goals, CALM-reconfig completed 10 goals, compared with three for CALM-fixed.

This analysis demonstrates how changing body configuration can alter the applicable human-response boundary. It does not validate a complete planner, because the study did not test how people respond to the reconfiguration itself.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is its empirical argument that robot body configuration should be treated as a variable in human-aware approach planning. A fixed social radius cannot necessarily represent how people respond to an articulated robot whose apparent and physical forward extent changes during motion.

CALM offers a relatively direct way to connect human-subject data with planning constraints. It could be implemented as a configuration-dependent costmap, clearance rule, chance constraint, or intervention-risk signal for model-predictive control and whole-body planning. The concept may support strategies such as retracting arms, changing grasp configuration, rotating the torso, slowing down, or waiting before modifying the robot’s base trajectory.

The findings also support separating planning objectives into physical safety, behavioral intervention risk, and subjective cost. CALM addresses behavioral intervention and should not replace collision-safety standards or geometry-based braking and separation requirements. The reported distances combine psychological preference with button-press timing, system latency, braking displacement, and depth-camera estimation. They also reflect a young, predominantly university-based sample, one robot, one speed, seated participants, static arm postures, and frontal approaches.

Consequently, CALM should be regarded as an experimentally derived behavioral metric requiring broader validation. Future studies should test different populations, cultures, robots, speeds, approach directions, payloads, dynamic postures, standing or moving participants, and natural responses such as stepping aside or negotiating space. Validation in closed-loop planning would be necessary to determine whether configuration-aware intervention boundaries improve interaction outcomes outside the controlled experimental setting.
