# Versatile Locomotion Skills for Hexapod Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> This research enables a low-cost hexapod robot to perform versatile locomotion skills like stair climbing, obstacle avoidance, and squeezing under objects, using policies trained in simulation via a teacher-student deep reinforcement learning framework. The approach achieves robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with only limited onboard visual sensing.

## Problem

- High-performance legged robots are often expensive and rely on sophisticated sensing, limiting accessibility and widespread deployment.
- Developing versatile locomotion skills for cluttered human environments (e.g., stairs, obstacles, low clearances) on practical robotic platforms remains challenging.
- Bridging the sim-to-real gap for complex learned behaviors, especially with limited real-world sensor feedback, is a key hurdle in robotics.

## Method

- A two-stage teacher-student deep ==reinforcement learning== framework trains policies in simulation, where a 'teacher' with privileged information guides a 'student' policy that uses only egocentric depth images and visual inertial odometry (VIO).
- Task-specific reward functions and curriculum learning are designed within the Isaac Gym simulation to train controllers for stair climbing, obstacle avoidance, and squeezing.
- The learned policies are deployed on a $600 Hiwonder SpiderPi hexapod robot, which lacks real-time joint state feedback, demonstrating functionality on cost-effective hardware.

## Results

- The robot achieved high success rates on real-world stairs, completing **6.0**-**7.6** out of 8 steps going up and **6.6**-**7.5** out of 8 steps going down across various U.C. Berkeley campus staircases.
- For obstacle avoidance, the hexapod demonstrated 70-**90%** success in avoiding diverse real-world obstacles including boxes, bags, and moving people, adaptively choosing avoidance directions.
- In squeezing tasks, the robot successfully navigated under various low objects (metal rods, paper blocks, tunnels) with 85-**100%** success, dynamically adjusting its body height to clear overhead obstacles.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Sophisticated and versatile locomotion skills can be imparted to low-cost hexapod robots with limited onboard sensing by employing an effective teacher-student knowledge distillation strategy for sim-to-real transfer.
> - Carefully crafted reward functions and curriculum learning are essential for training DRL agents to discover adaptive and robust behaviors for specific, complex navigation challenges in varied environments.
> - The inherent stability and compact design of hexapods, combined with advanced DRL, make them suitable candidates for navigating challenging, confined spaces without requiring high-fidelity joint feedback.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of legged locomotion, deep reinforcement learning, sim-to-real transfer, and low-cost robotics. It addresses several critical challenges and trends in the robotics community:

**Legged Locomotion:** Legged robots, particularly quadrupeds and hexapods, are increasingly recognized for their potential to navigate unstructured and cluttered environments where wheeled or tracked robots struggle. The paper explicitly argues for hexapods, noting their inherent stability, lightweight nature, and suitability for tight spaces compared to often taller quadrupeds and bipeds. This is a deliberate choice that differentiates their work from much of the mainstream research which frequently focuses on quadrupedal platforms (e.g., Boston Dynamics' Spot, ANYmal). The authors cite their prior work on hexapod joist climbing as a foundation, reinforcing their commitment to this platform for specific applications like attic inspection and retrofits.

**Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for Control:** DRL has revolutionized robotic control by enabling robots to learn complex behaviors directly from experience, often without explicit programming of gait patterns. The paper highlights DRL's promise for "autonomous and complex behaviors in real world systems." This work contributes to the growing body of literature demonstrating DRL's effectiveness in teaching dynamic and adaptive locomotion skills, moving beyond simple walking to specialized tasks like stair climbing, obstacle avoidance, and squeezing.

**Sim-to-Real Transfer:** One of the most significant challenges in DRL for robotics is bridging the "sim-to-real gap"—the discrepancy between simulated environments, where policies are trained, and the messy reality of the physical world. The paper discusses various established techniques to address this (system identification, domain randomization, domain adaptation, imitation learning, meta-learning, transfer learning, and knowledge distillation) and positions its "teacher-student model" as a form of knowledge distillation. Their specific approach, leveraging privileged information in the teacher and distilling it to a vision-based student, is a practical and effective strategy for robust transfer without requiring extensive real-world data or complex real-time sensory feedback.

**Low-Cost Hardware and Limited Sensing:** A crucial distinguishing factor of this research is its commitment to "low-cost, sub-thousand-dollar hexapods" that operate "without real-time joint state feedback." Most high-performance legged locomotion systems (e.g., using Model Predictive Control or some DRL approaches) rely on expensive hardware, powerful onboard computation, and precise real-time joint torque/angle feedback. By demonstrating complex skills on a $600 robot (Hiwonder SpiderPi) equipped only with a depth camera and a visual inertial odometry (VIO) camera, the authors challenge the notion that advanced legged robotics requires prohibitive expense. This focus on accessibility and practicality positions their work to have a broader impact on democratizing robotics and enabling applications where cost is a major constraint, such as consumer robots or large-scale deployments for infrastructure maintenance.

**Specific Task-Oriented Locomotion:** While much research focuses on general robust locomotion over varied terrains, this paper specifically tackles three distinct and critical skills for navigation in human environments: stair climbing, obstacle avoidance, and squeezing under low objects. These are common challenges in homes, offices, and industrial settings like attics. By providing solutions for these specific behaviors, the work advances the practical utility of legged robots beyond mere traversal.

In summary, this paper contributes to pushing the boundaries of practical, robust legged robot locomotion by integrating advanced DRL techniques for sim-to-real transfer onto a cost-effective, real-world platform with limited sensing capabilities, explicitly targeting versatile skills relevant to cluttered human environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objectives and motivations driving this research are multifaceted, blending practical applicability with scientific advancement:

1. **Enable Versatile Locomotion Skills for Hexapod Robots:** The primary objective is to equip a hexapod robot with a suite of essential locomotion skills: climbing up and down stairs, avoiding obstacles, and squeezing under low objects. These skills are identified as critical for navigating "cluttered home environments" and "rough environments such as attics."
2. **Utilize Low-Cost Hardware:** A central motivation is to achieve these complex behaviors on "low-cost hardware not requiring real-time joint state feedback." This contrasts sharply with many existing high-performance legged robots that demand expensive components and sophisticated sensing. The goal is to make advanced legged robotics more accessible and affordable for practical deployment in real-world scenarios, citing a sub-$1000 hexapod as their target platform.
3. **Achieve Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The authors aim for "zero-shot sim-to-real transfer," meaning policies trained exclusively in simulation should perform effectively on the physical robot without any fine-tuning in the real world. This is crucial for scalability and reducing development time and costs associated with real-world training.
4. **Leverage Limited Onboard Sensing:** Given the low-cost hardware constraint, the robot's onboard perception is limited to an egocentric depth camera and a visual inertial odometry (VIO) camera. The objective is to develop an "end-to-end learning-based perceptive controller" that can successfully operate with only these visual and pose inputs, rather than relying on high-fidelity joint feedback or extensive environmental mapping.
5. **Expand on Previous Work:** This research directly extends the authors' prior work on hexapod locomotion for "climbing joists in harsh attic environments". The motivation is to build a more comprehensive set of skills applicable to the broader context of home and attic navigation, making the robot more versatile for tasks like air sealing and vacuuming.
6. **Develop Task-Appropriate Motions Without Prior Gait Knowledge:** The researchers aim to train controllers that can explore and discover optimal, task-specific motions without being constrained by human-defined gaits. This allows for more adaptive and potentially more efficient locomotion for varied tasks.
7. **Address Practical Application Needs:** The overall motivation stems from the need for autonomous robots that can assist human workers in dangerous or uncomfortable tasks, such as navigating attics for retrofitting, or simply acting as domestic assistants in complex home environments. The combination of stability, compactness, and lightweight design of hexapods makes them suitable candidates for these roles.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology revolves around a two-stage teacher-student deep reinforcement learning framework, specifically designed to achieve robust sim-to-real transfer on a low-cost hexapod robot with limited sensing.

**A. Robot Platform and Sensing:**
- **Hardware:** A Hiwonder SpiderPi hexapod robot, costing approximately $600.
- **Sensing:** Equipped with an Intel L515 depth camera (for egocentric depth images) and an Intel T265 tracking camera (for robot pose estimation via Visual Inertial Odometry - VIO).
- **Key Constraint:** Critically, the robot lacks real-time joint state feedback, a common feature on more expensive legged robot platforms.

**B. Two-Stage Teacher-Student Training Procedure (Knowledge Distillation):**
This approach is adopted from their previous work and is central to overcoming the sim-to-real gap while working with limited hardware capabilities.

1. **Phase 1: Teacher Policy Training (Reinforcement Learning - RL):**
 - **Environment:** Training is conducted in a physics simulation environment using Isaac Gym.
 - **Algorithm:** Model-free reinforcement learning.
 - **Observations:** The "teacher" policy has access to **privileged information** not available to the real robot. This includes:
 - **Height maps:** Detailed geometric information about the terrain (represented as a yellow pad in Figure 3).
 - **Joint feedback:** Idealized joint positions, velocities, and possibly torques.
 - **Reward Functions:** Task-specific reward functions are meticulously crafted for each skill (stairs, obstacle avoidance, squeezing). These rewards guide the teacher to learn optimal, robust behaviors. Examples include:
 - **Forward velocity maximization:** Common across all tasks.
 - **Collision penalties:** Strong negative rewards for contact with terrain or obstacles.
 - **Action rate/magnitude/torque penalties:** To encourage smooth, energy-efficient movements.
 - **Task-specific rewards:**
 - **Stair Climbing:** Minimizing lateral deviation (`Global y deviation`) to keep the robot centered on stairs.
 - **Obstacle Avoidance:** A custom reward function (`R(H)`) that uses binarized height maps with weighted spatial contributions to penalize proximity to obstacles *before* collision, preventing scraping. It also includes global and local `y` deviation terms to encourage returning to the original heading after avoidance.
 - **Squeezing:** A custom reward function (`R(H)`) that maximizes distance from the robot's body to the overhead obstacle (when an obstacle is present) and to the ground otherwise, using a function of `(h_ij - b)` where `h_ij` is height map and `b` is robot base height.
 - **Curriculum Learning:** For each task, training starts with simpler scenarios and gradually increases difficulty.
 - **Stairs:** Riser heights increase (4.5 cm to 18 cm), and tread depths decrease (30 cm to 18 cm). Tread depth is also randomized.
 - **Obstacle Avoidance:** Starts with no obstacles, then sparsely placed, then dense obstacles.
 - **Squeezing:** Object distance to ground decreases (37 cm to 31 cm). Obstacle height, length/width, and starting distance are randomized.
 - **Output:** The teacher policy directly predicts joint angles for the robot's 18 joints.

2. **Phase 2: Student Policy Training (Supervised Learning):**
 - **Objective:** To distill the knowledge from the teacher policy into a "student" policy that operates with only onboard observations.
 - **Input:** The student policy receives:
 - **Egocentric depth images:** From the Intel L515 depth camera (320x240 pixels).
 - **Robot pose estimation:** From the Intel T265 VIO camera.
 - **Training:** The student is trained via supervised learning to mimic the actions (joint angles) produced by the trained teacher policy when presented with the corresponding onboard observations.
 - **Outputs:** The student policy, when deployed on the real robot, outputs desired joint angles to the servos.

**C. Task-Specific Configuration:**
- **Camera Angle:** Optimized for each task. Squeezing requires a 0-degree (horizontal) camera angle to "look up" at overhead obstacles, while other tasks use a 30-degree downward inclination.
- **Height Map Configuration:** Size and location of the height map in simulation are adjusted per task (e.g., larger and further away for obstacle avoidance).

**D. Training and Deployment Details:**
- **Computational Resources:** Training takes approximately 10 hours for the teacher and 5 hours for the student on an RTX TITAN GPU.
- **Deployment:** The trained policies are deployed on a Raspberry Pi processor onboard the physical robot.

This meticulous approach, combining the strengths of DRL with privileged information and a knowledge distillation step, enables the development of robust, perceptive controllers that can transfer effectively to inexpensive hardware, sidestepping the need for high-fidelity sensors and powerful onboard computation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates significant success in enabling a low-cost hexapod robot to perform versatile locomotion skills through simulation-trained policies, achieving robust sim-to-real transfer.

**A. Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
- The policies trained exclusively in Isaac Gym simulation, using the two-stage teacher-student model, were successfully deployed "zero-shot" onto the physical Hiwonder SpiderPi robot. This is a critical finding, confirming the efficacy of their knowledge distillation approach for platforms with limited sensing.

**B. High Success Rates Across All Three Tasks in Physical Experiments:**

1. **Stair Climbing:**
 - **Performance:** The robot exhibited high success rates on various real-world staircases on the U.C. Berkeley campus, handling both climbing up and descending.
 - Cory Hall (7 steps): 6.0/7.0 steps completed (up), 6.6/7.0 (down).
 - Soda Hall (7 steps): 7.0/7.0 steps completed (up), 7.0/7.0 (down).
 - Sutardja Hall (8 steps): 7.6/8.0 steps completed (up), 7.5/8.0 (down).
 - **Generalization:** The policy generalized well to different stair dimensions and types, including a complex scenario with two sets of stairs separated by a landing.
 - **Robustness:** The robot maintained a relatively straight path, minimizing lateral shifts, attributed to the specific reward term minimizing global Y deviation.
 - **Comparison:** A baseline walking policy, not trained for stairs, resulted in the robot crashing, highlighting the learned policy's specialized skill.
 - **Failure Analysis:** Upward climbing failures were mostly due to getting stuck on the last step (camera image not showing stairs), while downward failures were from loss of control and falling.

2. **Obstacle Avoidance:**
 - **Performance:** The robot demonstrated robust avoidance behavior across diverse real-world obstacle scenarios.
 - Single Box: 7/10 success, 2/10 scrape, 1/10 collision.
 - Box and Bag: 9/10 success, 1/10 scrape, 0/10 collision.
 - Single Person Standing: 7/10 success, 2/10 scrape, 1/10 collision.
 - Single Person Moving: 8/10 success, 1/10 scrape, 1/10 collision.
 - **Generalization:** The policy successfully avoided arbitrary shapes (boxes, deformable bags, people) and dynamic obstacles (moving person).
 - **Intelligent Behavior:** The robot exhibited avoidance behavior to both the left and right, depending on the obstacle's position, indicating learned flexibility rather than a memorized direction. After avoidance, it successfully rotated back to its original orientation and continued moving forward.
 - **Challenging Sequence:** The robot successfully navigated a sequence of six obstacles in a row.
 - **Failure Analysis:** Failures often involved slower reaction with the back legs scraping objects, even after the front part successfully avoided them.

3. **Squeezing Under Obstacles:**
 - **Performance:** The robot successfully squeezed under objects mimicking couches or tables, adapting to various heights, shapes, and tunnel lengths.
 - Metal rod without tunnel: 17/20 (85%) success.
 - Lengthy tunnel (129.1 cm): 10/10 (100%) success.
 - Paper block without tunnel: 18/20 (90%) success.
 - Paper block with tunnel: 9/10 (90%) success.
 - **Dynamic Height Adjustment:** The robot demonstrated the ability to dynamically adjust its body height. In an experiment with two consecutive obstacles, it squeezed under the first, briefly rose, then squeezed again under the second, and finally stood up to walk away, showcasing adaptive behavior to changing overhead clearances.
 - **Limitations:** While able to instantaneously squeeze very low (e.g., 31.75 cm), sustaining this height for extended distances (e.g., 129 cm) without scraping the roof was challenging due to hardware constraints (e.g., minimum height being 28.75 cm when flat). The authors estimate a sustained squeeze height limit of around 33 cm without scraping.
 - **Generalization:** The policy generalized to different visual inputs (e.g., metal rods vs. paper blocks) and tunnel lengths.

**C. Efficiency:**
- The training process was relatively efficient (10 hours for teacher, 5 for student on a single GPU), enabling rapid iteration.
- Deployment on a Raspberry Pi demonstrates computational lightness suitable for low-power, onboard processing.

The main findings confirm that sophisticated locomotion skills can be imparted to inexpensive hexapod robots using a well-designed DRL framework and strategic sim-to-real transfer techniques, making them capable of navigating complex, real-world environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant implications for the field of robotics, addressing both technical challenges and practical deployment barriers.

**1. Democratization and Accessibility of Robotics:**
Perhaps the most significant impact is demonstrating high-level, versatile locomotion on a **sub-$1000 hexapod robot without requiring real-time joint state feedback**. This drastically lowers the financial and technical barrier to entry for developing and deploying legged robots. Traditionally, advanced legged locomotion has been synonymous with expensive, high-fidelity hardware and complex control systems. This work shows that sophisticated behaviors can be achieved with more accessible, low-cost platforms, potentially opening up robotic applications to a much wider range of researchers, small businesses, and even hobbyists. This could accelerate innovation and deployment in areas where cost has been a prohibitive factor.

**2. Enabling Practical Applications in Challenging Environments:**
By specifically targeting skills like stair climbing, obstacle avoidance, and squeezing, the research directly addresses the needs of robots operating in human-centric or cluttered environments.
- **Home Automation/Assistance:** The skills are highly relevant for domestic robots needing to navigate furniture, ascend stairs between floors, or squeeze under beds and tables.
- **Infrastructure Inspection/Maintenance:** Building on their prior work on attic joist climbing, these additional skills make the hexapod even more capable for tasks like air sealing, insulation installation, or inspection in attics, crawl spaces, or other confined, irregular, and potentially hazardous industrial environments. This can reduce human exposure to danger and discomfort.

**3. Advancement in Sim-to-Real Transfer Techniques:**
The two-stage teacher-student model with judicious use of privileged information and curriculum learning provides a robust and efficient methodology for bridging the sim-to-real gap. This approach is particularly effective for systems with limited onboard sensing, as it leverages the ideal information available in simulation to train a teacher, then distills that knowledge into a student policy that only relies on real-world sensor data (depth images and VIO). This framework offers a valuable blueprint for future research aiming to transfer complex DRL policies to real-world robots with constraints.

**4. Versatile and Adaptive Locomotion:**
The ability of the robot to autonomously learn distinct, task-appropriate motions (rather than relying on pre-programmed gaits) for climbing, avoiding, and squeezing demonstrates a higher level of versatility and adaptability. The success with zero-shot transfer across varied terrains and obstacles (e.g., different stair dimensions, diverse obstacle shapes, variable tunnel lengths) highlights the robustness of the learned policies. The dynamic height adjustment for squeezing further exemplifies intelligent, adaptive behavior.

**5. Future Research Directions:**
The paper acknowledges limitations, such as the need for different camera angles for different tasks and the overall height constraint of the payload for extremely low squeezing. These limitations naturally point to future research, such as developing a "universal policy combining all three tasks" and incorporating "an adjustable camera setup." This work lays a strong foundation for integrating multiple learned skills into a single, more generalized robotic agent, moving closer to truly autonomous and versatile legged systems.

In conclusion, "Versatile Locomotion Skills for Hexapod Robots" is a significant contribution that pushes the boundaries of practical legged robotics. It showcases how advanced AI techniques, specifically deep reinforcement learning and sim-to-real transfer, can be effectively applied to low-cost hardware to achieve sophisticated and robust locomotion behaviors, thereby expanding the potential applications and accessibility of robotic technology in the real world.
