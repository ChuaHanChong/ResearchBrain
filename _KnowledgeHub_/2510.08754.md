# Whole Body Model Predictive Control for Spin-Aware Quadrupedal Table Tennis

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the RAI Institute, MIT, and Georgia Tech developed an integrated system that enables a Boston Dynamics Spot quadruped robot with an arm to play spin-aware table tennis, achieving a 90.1% return rate and accurate spin generation in dynamic rallies with human players. The system combines high-speed perception, refined spin prediction using a residual network, and a whole-body model predictive control framework that facilitates emergent stroke strategies.

## Problem

- Existing robotic table tennis systems often use fixed-base arms or gantry setups, bypassing the complexities of whole-body dynamic control and active balance required for human-like agility.
- Accurately perceiving and predicting the high-speed trajectory and spin of a small table tennis ball in real-time is challenging due to unmodeled aerodynamic effects and contact dynamics.
- Developing agile, coordinated whole-body control for a legged robot to execute precise, high-speed strikes while maintaining balance is a difficult problem.

## Method

- An integrated system combines high-speed, dual-camera perception with background subtraction and YOLO for low-latency 3D ball localization.
- Ball trajectory and spin are predicted using a model-based approach, enhanced by a learned residual neural network to account for unmodeled dynamics.
- Whole-body ==model predictive control== (MPC) leverages parametric Bezier curves and Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) to generate dynamically feasible arm and body motions for precise strikes while maintaining robot balance.

## Results

- The perception system achieved a median 3D ball localization error below 1 cm, enabling precise real-time tracking.
- The integrated residual network improved spin prediction accuracy, increasing the R² metric from **0.42** to **0.70** compared to purely model-based estimation.
- The full system demonstrated a **90.1%** return rate in targeted striking experiments and successfully rallied with human players, handling incoming spin up to 280 rad/s and generating outgoing spin up to 200 rad/s.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Real-time, low-latency ball detection and 3D localization are achievable using a dual-camera setup with efficient image processing, yielding sub-centimeter accuracy.
> - Hybridizing model-based spin prediction with a learned residual neural network significantly improves prediction accuracy, accounting for unmodeled dynamics crucial for dynamic tasks.
> - A continuous MPC formulation for whole-body control can generate a diverse range of stroke strategies for legged robots, allowing emergent behaviors based on desired ball return objectives.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotics research has increasingly utilized dynamic sports, particularly racket sports, as testbeds for developing agile and intelligent robotic systems. Table tennis, characterized by its rapid pace, small ball diameter, and the critical role of ball spin, presents a notable challenge in this domain. Prior robotic table tennis systems typically employ either fixed-base robotic arms, which restrict their operational range, or specialized gantry systems that simplify agile motion control by providing a dedicated, fast-moving platform. These approaches often bypass the complexities of whole-body dynamic control inherent in human-like movement.

This research distinguishes itself by focusing on quadrupedal legged robots, specifically a Boston Dynamics Spot equipped with a 6-DoF arm. Legged robots face constraints analogous to human players, requiring active balance and coordination between locomotion and manipulation. This presents a more complex control problem compared to stationary or gantry-mounted systems, as the robot must manage its balance while performing high-speed, precise striking motions.

In terms of perception, previous work has addressed ball localization, trajectory prediction, and spin estimation. Some methods overlook spin, which is a significant factor in ball flight and contact dynamics. Others rely on tracking non-regulation ball markings or inferring spin from human poses, both of which have limitations. This paper builds upon existing model-based spin estimation techniques by integrating a learned residual network to enhance prediction accuracy, thereby addressing a limitation of purely model-based approaches and those relying on specialized ball characteristics or human data.

For control, many prior systems for table tennis employ human demonstrations, imitation learning, or reinforcement learning (RL) to generate stroke primitives. While effective, these methods can require extensive data or separate controllers for different shot types. Model-based control, conversely, can offer greater shot diversity with a single controller and does not necessitate large datasets. This work extends model-based control to handle the unique challenges of legged robots, including maintaining balance during dynamic maneuvers. It also presents an alternative to RL-based approaches that rely on human motion capture data to bootstrap stroke strategies, by demonstrating emergent stroke behaviors from its Model Predictive Control (MPC) formulation. The system’s capability to handle and generate competitive spin, reaching up to 280 rad/s incoming and 200 rad/s outgoing, surpasses reported capabilities in some prior robotic table tennis research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robotic system capable of playing dynamic table tennis using a quadrupedal robot, mirroring human-like speed, accuracy, and the ability to predict and respond to a full range of ball spins. This overarching goal is motivated by several fundamental challenges inherent in the sport when translated to a robotic platform:

- **High-Speed Perception:** Table tennis balls can travel at speeds up to 10 m/s with significant spin (up to 600 rad/s). The robot must accurately localize a small, fast-moving ball in real-time. This necessitates a perception system with high spatial and temporal resolution, capable of low-latency processing to enable timely responses.

- **Accurate Trajectory and Spin Prediction:** To determine when and where to strike the ball, the robot must predict its future motion, including its linear velocity and angular spin. Ball spin significantly influences flight path and contact dynamics, making its accurate estimation crucial for strategic play. The motivation here is to develop robust prediction models that account for aerodynamic effects and table rebounds, and to refine these models to handle unmodeled dynamics.

- **Strategic Aiming and Stroke Planning:** The robot needs to plan a paddle contact state (position, velocity, and face normal) that will return the ball to a desired landing location with a specified outgoing spin. This requires a high-level planner capable of solving a constrained optimization problem in real-time, considering the complex contact dynamics between the paddle and the ball. The motivation is to enable flexible and continuous stroke strategies rather than relying on a discrete set of pre-programmed motions.

- **Agile Whole-Body Control:** Executing the planned strike requires coordinated, high-agility motion of the robot's arm and body while maintaining balance. For a legged robot, this involves generating dynamically feasible joint trajectories and corresponding torques that satisfy physical constraints, such as joint limits, foot contact, and friction cones. The challenge is to integrate kinematic planning with dynamic whole-body control at a high frequency (e.g., 100 Hz) to allow for real-time adaptation and execution of complex swings.

- **Demonstration of Emergent Behaviors:** A key motivation is to observe if a continuous set of stroke strategies, similar to those employed by human players (e.g., loops, chops, drives), can emerge automatically from the model predictive control paradigm when aiming for different ball return objectives (landing position and outgoing spin), rather than being explicitly programmed or learned through demonstrations.

By addressing these challenges, the research aims to provide a comprehensive case study for dynamic robotic control, particularly for legged manipulators operating in fast-paced, interactive environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The system developed in this research integrates several subsystems: high-speed perception, trajectory prediction, aiming, and whole-body control, all implemented on a Boston Dynamics Spot quadruped robot equipped with a 6-DoF arm.

**A. Ball Detection and Localization**
- **Camera Setup and Calibration:** Two Power over Ethernet (PoE) RGB cameras (Lucid Arena), with 1400x1080 resolution, capture images at 165 frames per second (fps). They are mounted on the ceiling at opposite ends of the table, angled downwards to cover the playing area. Intrinsic parameters are from manufacturer specifications. Extrinsic parameters are determined relative to a world coordinate frame (origin at table center) via a semi-automated process. This involves manually identifying table corner key points in images and solving the Perspective-n-Point (PnP) problem for each camera.
- **Ball Detection and Localization Pipeline:** A two-stage process is used for speed:
 1. **Motion Detection:** A background subtraction algorithm identifies dynamic regions in each incoming image stream, isolating moving objects like the ball.
 2. **Object Detection:** The segmented moving regions are composited into a smaller 480x480 pixel image patch. A fine-tuned YOLO convolutional neural network (CNN) then detects the ball’s 2D pixel coordinates within this patch. This reduces computational load by avoiding full-resolution CNN inference.
- **3D Reconstruction:** Once 2D coordinates are obtained from both cameras, the ball's 3D position in the world frame is calculated using stereo triangulation.
- **Performance:** The entire detection process (background subtraction and CNN inference) completes in approximately 4 ms, well within the 6 ms frame interval, ensuring low latency.

**B. Ball Trajectory Prediction and Spin Estimation**
- **Velocity and Spin Estimation:** The system uses methods based on Tebbe et al.. Polynomials are fitted to historical ball positions to approximate linear velocity (v) by differentiation. For spin (ω) estimation, a grid of velocity points is sampled, and a discrete approximation of ball dynamics (Equation 1, incorporating drag, Magnus effect, and gravity) is constructed. By rearranging and stacking these equations, a least squares solve is performed for ω, assuming it remains constant during flight. The lumped coefficients for drag ($C_D$) and Magnus effects ($C_M$) are characterized using a Spinsight Elite ball tracking system with custom marked balls.
- **Residual Network for Refinement:** To account for unmodeled effects, a small neural network is trained on 650 unique ball trajectories with varying spins. Inputs include the least squares ω estimate and polynomial coefficients indicating trajectory curvature. Data augmentation through random z-axis rotations is applied.
- **Trajectory Integration:** Using the refined state estimate, the ball trajectory is integrated forward using the same dynamics (Equation 1) and a table rebound model from Nonomura et al.. Integration stops when the ball reaches a fixed strike plane 0.5 m in front of the quadruped's base, providing the terminal ball state to the aiming controller.

**C. Strike Aiming**
- **Problem Formulation:** This subsystem determines the desired paddle contact state ($p_{des}$, $v_{des}$, $n_{des}$ – position, velocity, face normal) to return the ball to a specified landing position ($p_{land}$), outgoing spin ($\omega^+$), and landing time ($t_{land}$). It is formulated as a constrained optimization problem (Equations 3a-3e) that minimizes paddle velocity magnitude while satisfying constraints on ball trajectory post-collision. The contact dynamics ($f_{contact}$) are a modified version of Nonomura et al., using vector operations for simplification. Aerodynamics ($f_{aero}$) are based on Equation 1.
- **Solver:** Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) is used to solve this non-linear optimization problem in real-time. A quadratic approximation of the cost with linearized constraints is iteratively solved using OSQP as the local QP solver. Four SQP iterations are performed before accepting the solution. CasADi generates the optimization formulations for efficient function evaluation.

**D. Whole-Body Model Predictive Control**
- **Kinematic Planner:** This component generates dynamically feasible swinging motions to achieve the desired paddle state. It uses parametric Bezier curves (Equation 4a-4h) to represent joint trajectories, allowing a single strike constraint to be enforced continuously along the planning horizon.
 - **Constraints:** The plan ensures the trajectory starts from the robot's current state, respects joint limits, and keeps the robot's feet stationary during the swing. Crucially, it drives the end-effector (paddle) to match the desired position, velocity, and orientation at the strike time ($t_s$).
 - **Slack Variables:** Slack decision variables ($q_s, \dot{q}_s, \ddot{q}_c$) are introduced to decouple kinematic constraints and maintain a quadratic cost function (Equations 5a-5b, 7a-7b).
 - **Cost Function:** A convex cost function is defined to limit robot acceleration ($f_a$) and keep joint positions close to a rest stance ($f_r$) (Equations 6, 7a-7b). This formulation also enables the same optimizer to plan trajectories for returning to a rest position by disabling strike constraints.
 - **Solver:** The same SQP solver as the aiming system is used. Five SQP iterations are performed during execution for a 100 Hz re-planning frequency.
- **Whole-Body Controller:** This generates feedforward torques to execute the planned kinematic motion while adhering to robot dynamics and contact constraints.
 - **Problem Formulation:** It is formulated as a Quadratic Program (QP) (Equations 8a-8c) that minimizes the difference between the actual and desired robot acceleration ($\ddot{q}_{des}$).
 - **Constraints:** The QP includes manipulator dynamics (mass matrix M, Coriolis C, gravity $\tau_g$, actuation B, Jacobian J) and enforces ground reaction forces ($\lambda$) within a pyramidal approximation of the friction cone (FC).
 - **Solver:** OSQP is used to solve this QP.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Ball Localization**
- Quantitative evaluation against Vicon motion capture data showed that the RGB-based ball detection system achieved high accuracy. The median position measurement error for each axis remained below 1 cm, demonstrating the precision of the perception module.

**B. Spin Estimation and Prediction**
- Using 600 recorded trajectories, the prediction module's performance was evaluated by comparing predicted and true final ball states near the strike plane.
- Prediction errors for timing, position, velocity, and spin reduced as more measurement data became available.
- Spin estimation converged to under 55 rad/s within 150 ms of the strike, providing sufficient time for the swing controller.
- The learned residual network improved the spin prediction accuracy significantly, raising the R² performance metric from 0.42 (purely model-based) to 0.70.

**C. Swing Controller**
- The ability of the swing controller to achieve arbitrary paddle states was assessed across 3750 simulated swing samples, covering a dense grid of positions, velocities, and orientations for loop, chop, and drive shots.
- The "strike workspace" of the controller was defined by thresholds: 7.5 cm for position error, 1 m/s for velocity error, and 20° for orientation error.
- Position error heatmaps indicated best performance directly to the left and right of the robot, with larger errors for further or closer strikes.
- Within the acceptable position error bounds, velocity tracking error was generally below 0.5 m/s.
- Orientation error exhibited a distinct pattern, with optimal performance observed closer and further from the robot.

**D. System Integration**
- The integrated system was evaluated by striking 150 balls aimed at three different target landing locations. The system achieved a 90.1% return rate, demonstrating a clear aiming pattern towards the specified targets.
- An ablation study on spin detection demonstrated the necessity of spin estimation and the benefits of the residual network. When only incoming spin was considered:
 - No spin estimation: Mean return rate of 27.2%.
 - Model-based estimation only: Mean return rate of 52.0%.
 - Full residual estimator: Mean return rate of 75.2%. This shows improvement across a range of incoming spin values (from -280 rad/s to 220 rad/s).
- The system’s capability to generate specific outgoing spin was tested across four target spin values. It achieved an average accuracy within 20 rad/s of the desired spin, demonstrating the ability to impart diverse spins (e.g., top spin up to 200 rad/s and back spin up to -200 rad/s) with standard deviations ranging from 11.5 rad/s to 24.0 rad/s.

**E. Table Tennis Game Play**
- Qualitative demonstrations showed the system's ability to engage in rallies with human players, sustaining over 10 hits per player while handling opponent-generated spin. This indicates functional integration of all components in an interactive environment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics by demonstrating a quadrupedal robot's capability to play dynamic table tennis, specifically addressing the complex requirements of high-speed perception, spin-aware prediction, and agile whole-body control.

The introduction of a Boston Dynamics Spot quadruped as a platform for competitive spin-aware table tennis represents a significant step beyond previous systems that relied on fixed-base or gantry-mounted manipulators. This work highlights the potential of legged robots for performing complex manipulation tasks while managing their own balance and locomotion, moving towards more generalized and human-like robotic capabilities in dynamic environments.

The novel Model Predictive Control (MPC) formulation for whole-body control is a key aspect. By using parametric Bezier curves, the controller can handle continuous strike constraints and adapt to changing conditions in real-time. This approach enables the automatic emergence of diverse stroke strategies (such as loops, chops, and drives) from different ball return objectives, rather than requiring explicit programming or extensive data for discrete shot types. This emergent behavior capability suggests a more flexible and adaptable control paradigm for complex robotic skills.

The quantitative evaluations of each system component, from ball localization accuracy (median < 1 cm error) to spin prediction (R² of 0.70 with residual network) and control performance (high return rates and accurate spin generation), provide a robust validation of the integrated system. The ability to handle incoming spin up to 280 rad/s and generate outgoing spin up to 200 rad/s represents a notable capability for robotic table tennis.

The successful demonstration of rallying with human players indicates the system's ability to coordinate its perception, prediction, and control subsystems effectively in an interactive, unstructured environment. This has implications for developing robots that can seamlessly interact with humans in fast-paced scenarios, potentially extending to other sports, complex assembly tasks, or assistance in dynamic human environments.

While the reliance on off-board cameras and a lack of stepping motion are noted limitations, the work lays a foundation for future research in onboard perception, integration of contact-implicit optimization for locomotion, and the development of more sophisticated strategic planning for competitive play. The methodology presented offers a model for addressing multi-modal challenges in robotics, where perception, intelligent decision-making, and agile physical execution must be tightly integrated.
