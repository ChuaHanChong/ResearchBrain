# AnyViewDex: View-Invariant Dexterous Manipulation from RGB Observations

> [!summary] Summary
> AnyViewDex is an asymmetric sim-training pipeline for view-invariant dexterous manipulation from uncalibrated monocular RGB. A ResNet-18 globally pooled embedding is trained with multi-view InfoNCE alignment between canonical and randomized cameras, plus privileged regression of absolute 3D object coordinates that mitigates spatial collapse. Deployed zero-shot on an xArm7 with a 16-DoF LEAP Hand, it achieves 76.7% grasp success across eight unseen objects and six viewpoints, versus 30.2% for domain randomization.

## Problem

- Visuomotor dexterous policies overfit to fixed camera viewpoints; shifting the camera changes 2D finger-object contact appearance, creating ambiguity about 3D hand-object relationships from uncalibrated images.
- View-invariant methods increasingly depend on test-time RGB-D or point clouds, adding hardware dependencies, calibration requirements, minimum-distance clipping, and vulnerability to depth noise and missing measurements.
- RGB-only alternatives fall short: spatial transformers fail under large out-of-plane rotations, and global multi-view contrastive pooling keeps semantic similarity while discarding the metric geometry fine dexterous control needs.

## Method

- **Multi-view InfoNCE alignment**: simulator renders each state from a fixed canonical camera and a per-episode randomized camera; a shared ==ResNet-18== with ==global average pooling== pulls cross-view pairs together; canonical view is discarded at inference.
- **Privileged geometric regularization**: an auxiliary MLP regresses absolute world-frame 3D object coordinates from the temporally aggregated latent via ==MSE==, forcing the pooled embedding to recover metric depth and scale, used only in simulation.
- **Cross-paradigm integration**: frame-stacked embeddings feed a ==DrQ-v2== actor and twin-Q critic for end-to-end RL, while an ==LSTM== fusing vision and proprioception is distilled via ==uncertainty-weighted DAgger== from a privileged ==PPO== teacher.

## Results

- On the Maniwhere RL suite, AnyViewDex is best RGB-only on three of four tasks, hitting **92.1%** on Close Dex versus **81.5%** for RGB-D Maniwhere, but trails at **53.0%** on Lift Cube Dex.
- Hardware zero-shot grasping on xArm7 plus LEAP Hand reaches **76.7%** (**368/480**) across six uncalibrated viewpoints and eight unseen objects, versus **30.2%** DR-only and **1.4%** fixed-camera, consistent at **75-80%** per view.
- Ablations need both losses: hardware success falls to **8.3%** without the auxiliary loss and **18.7%** without InfoNCE, below DR-only; a frozen-embedding linear probe decodes 3D coordinates at R² **0.814** (**4.73 cm** error).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Privileged simulator state need not stay in the teacher: using it as an auxiliary regression target can bake metric 3D geometry into an RGB encoder, replacing test-time depth sensors.
> - Contrastive view-invariance on globally pooled features can collapse to semantics-only matching; pair it with a metric anchor, since neither loss alone beat plain domain randomization on hardware.
> - Caution: the invariance is bounded by the training camera cone; outside it probe error rises to about 14 cm, and geometric gains are not isolated from a generic dense auxiliary.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses view generalization in visuomotor control, particularly for dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered robotic hands. A visual manipulation policy trained from a fixed camera may associate successful actions with viewpoint-specific image patterns rather than with the underlying three-dimensional relationship between the hand and object. This problem is more pronounced for dexterous hands than for parallel-jaw grippers because grasping requires coordinated contact among several fingers and accurate control over many joints.

Existing research has pursued several approaches. One direction uses extensive camera and appearance randomization to expose policies to diverse observations during training. Another generates synthetic views through image or video models. Reconstruction-based methods, including masked world models, learn representations by predicting missing or novel views. Spatial adaptation methods use mechanisms such as spatial transformer networks to warp features into a canonical frame. Contrastive approaches encourage observations of the same state from different views to have similar representations.

A separate line of work directly supplies three-dimensional information through RGB-D cameras, point clouds, depth maps, or camera-extrinsic conditioning. These modalities make spatial reasoning more explicit, but they introduce additional sensing and calibration requirements. Depth cameras can also suffer from missing measurements, noise, minimum-range restrictions, and self-occlusion.

AnyViewDex is positioned between monocular representation learning and explicit 3D sensing. Its central premise is that three-dimensional information can be used during simulation training as privileged supervision, while the deployed policy receives only monocular RGB and proprioception. The paper therefore follows an asymmetric-training paradigm: the training system has access to information that is unavailable to the deployed student policy. It combines multi-view contrastive learning, which encourages viewpoint-invariant features, with absolute 3D coordinate regression, which is intended to preserve metric spatial information that global contrastive representations may otherwise discard.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to develop a dexterous manipulation policy that remains effective when an uncalibrated monocular camera is moved to different positions within a training distribution. The policy is expected to transfer to physical hardware without test-time depth sensing, camera calibration, or real-world fine-tuning.

The motivation rests on three technical observations. First, viewpoint changes alter the apparent two-dimensional geometry of finger-object contacts. A finger that appears aligned with an object in one view may appear displaced or occluded in another, even though the physical state is unchanged. A policy that relies on image-space correlations can therefore produce incorrect three-dimensional hand motions.

Second, global average pooling provides an efficient representation for high-frequency control but removes explicit spatial layout. The resulting one-dimensional embedding can preserve object identity and broad scene semantics while losing the precise location and scale information required for grasping. The authors refer to this failure mode as “spatial collapse.”

Third, multi-view contrastive learning alone may not solve the problem. InfoNCE encourages images of the same physical state to have similar representations, but similarity does not necessarily imply that the representation contains absolute position, depth, or metric scale. AnyViewDex adds an auxiliary regression target—the object’s absolute world-frame three-dimensional coordinates—to force the visual representation to retain spatial information.

The paper evaluates whether this combination works across two policy-learning settings: end-to-end reinforcement learning and student-teacher distillation. It also examines whether the learned representation transfers from simulation to a real xArm7 robot equipped with a 16-degree-of-freedom LEAP Hand.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Monocular Visual Encoder

At each time step, the system receives an RGB image. A ResNet-18 encoder processes the image, and global average pooling converts the final convolutional representation into a compact one-dimensional visual vector \(v_t\). The authors deliberately avoid high-dimensional spatial feature grids and large vision foundation models to reduce computational cost and make the representation suitable for continuous control.

During simulation training, the same physical state is rendered simultaneously from two cameras: a fixed canonical camera and a randomized tracking camera. The two images share the ResNet encoder. Their embeddings are passed through a projection multilayer perceptron and aligned using an InfoNCE loss. Positive pairs are the canonical and randomized views of the same time step, while negative examples come from other time steps or parallel environments. The contrastive temperature is set to 0.1, and embeddings are L2-normalized.

At deployment, only the embedding from the randomized-camera stream is available. The canonical image and all associated privileged information are discarded.

#### 3.2. Temporal Aggregation

Because dexterous fingers can occlude the object during manipulation, AnyViewDex incorporates temporal context. The architecture differs according to the control paradigm.

For student-teacher distillation, the visual embedding is concatenated with proprioception, including joint positions and velocities. This vector is processed by an LSTM whose hidden state represents the temporally aggregated state. The recurrent state can preserve object-position information across frames in which the object is partially hidden.

For end-to-end reinforcement learning, the method follows the Maniwhere baseline and stacks recent visual embeddings. No proprioception is included in this version. Thus, in the RL setting, the auxiliary coordinate prediction must be obtained from visual features and temporal frame stacking alone.

#### 3.3. Privileged 3D Geometric Supervision

An auxiliary MLP predicts the target object’s absolute world-frame coordinates \(p \in \mathbb{R}^3\) from the temporally aggregated state. The target coordinates are directly available from the simulator but are not supplied to the policy at deployment. The auxiliary loss is a mean squared error between predicted and ground-truth coordinates.

The use of absolute rather than relative coordinates is intended to require the representation to recover metric position across changing camera views. Because the same object location can appear differently under different extrinsics, the authors argue that this objective encourages the visual encoder to infer depth and scale rather than encode only view-specific two-dimensional arrangements.

The complete objective combines the control task loss, the InfoNCE loss, and the auxiliary coordinate-regression loss. The auxiliary gradients pass through the temporal module and visual encoder, thereby shaping the representation used by the policy.

The authors also investigate a possible proprioceptive shortcut in the distillation setting. Since proprioception is fused with vision, the system could potentially infer object position from arm configuration after contact. To address this, episodes begin from a fixed neutral arm pose while object positions are randomized. In addition, a linear probe trained on the visual embedding alone achieves an \(R^2\) of 0.814 for three-dimensional coordinate prediction, supporting the claim that visual features themselves contain metric information.

#### 3.4. Control and Evaluation Settings

For reinforcement learning, AnyViewDex is evaluated in the MuJoCo-based Maniwhere suite using DrQ-v2, with a deterministic actor and twin-Q critic. The tasks are Lift Cube Dex, Pick & Place Dex, Close Dex, and Button Dex.

For distillation, a privileged teacher is trained in Isaac Lab, and a visual student is trained using DAgger. The student attempts to match the teacher’s stochastic action distribution through an uncertainty-weighted mean squared error. Training uses camera randomization, physics randomization, visual randomization, and image-space augmentation.

The physical platform consists of an xArm7 with a LEAP Hand, giving a 23-degree-of-freedom robot platform overall and a 16-degree-of-freedom hand. A single Intel RealSense D455 supplies RGB images, but depth is not used. The camera is repositioned across six uncalibrated viewpoints within an approximately 140-degree azimuthal cone. Eight objects not seen during training are tested, with ten trials for every object-viewpoint combination.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Reinforcement-Learning Results

AnyViewDex substantially improves over the RGB-only Maniwhere baseline on tasks requiring depth-sensitive positioning. The RGB-only baseline achieves 0% on Pick & Place Dex and 17.3% on Close Dex, whereas AnyViewDex reaches 72.4% and 92.1%, respectively. On Close Dex, AnyViewDex also exceeds the RGB-D Maniwhere result of 81.5%.

The results are less favorable on Lift Cube Dex, which requires fine localization of a small object. AnyViewDex obtains 53.0%, compared with 78.0% for MV-MWM and 72.5% for the RGB-only Maniwhere baseline. This supports the authors’ claim that global pooling sacrifices some local spatial precision.

On Button Dex, AnyViewDex achieves 96.0%, close to the RGB-D result of 97.6% and above the RGB-only baseline’s 82.4%. This task may require less detailed three-dimensional localization because the target button is relatively fixed and the action is less dependent on precise object grasp geometry.

The ablations demonstrate that both representation objectives are important. Training with camera randomization alone produces only 26.2% on Lift Cube Dex, 3.5% on Pick & Place Dex, and 0% on Close Dex. Removing the auxiliary loss causes near-total failure on three tasks, although Button Dex remains at 88.0%. Removing InfoNCE also reduces performance, producing 22.0–35.2% on most tasks. The combined objective is therefore associated with the strongest performance across the benchmark, although the individual ablations do not fully establish that the gains are uniquely due to geometric supervision rather than to the general effect of an auxiliary training signal.

#### 4.2. Student-Teacher and Hardware Results

In student-teacher distillation, the monocular student recovers approximately 61–72% of the privileged teacher’s capacity within the camera-training cone. Domain randomization alone recovers 33–49%, while variants without the auxiliary loss or without InfoNCE recover no more than 29% and 21%, respectively.

On hardware, AnyViewDex succeeds in 368 of 480 trials, corresponding to 76.7%. Success varies relatively little among the six viewpoints, ranging from 60 to 64 successes out of 80, or 75–80%. Across the eight unseen objects, success ranges from 71.7% to 83.3%. The matched domain-randomization-only baseline achieves 30.2%, while the variants without InfoNCE and without the auxiliary loss achieve 18.7% and 8.3%. A fixed-camera policy succeeds in only 1.4% of trials.

The hardware experiment indicates that the learned representation transfers to unseen objects and camera positions without real-world fine-tuning. However, the comparison with RGB-D is performed primarily in simulation; the physical evaluation does not establish direct hardware superiority over an equivalent depth-based system.

#### 4.3. Representation and Efficiency Analyses

A linear probe on the frozen visual embedding predicts object coordinates with a mean Euclidean error of 4.73 cm on approximately 690,000 in-distribution samples. The error increases to about 14.3 cm outside the training camera cone. This indicates that the method learns an implicit geometric mapping within its training distribution but does not provide arbitrary viewpoint generalization.

Activation-map analysis suggests that intermediate visual features often emphasize finger-object contact regions. A t-SNE visualization shows trajectories from different camera angles remaining broadly aligned during much of a task, with separation near terminal states. The authors appropriately note that t-SNE is qualitative and non-metric.

The deployed ResNet-18 policy requires 5.65 GFLOPs, with a reported forward-pass time of 2.74 milliseconds and an end-to-end control step averaging 5.16 milliseconds on an RTX 4050 laptop GPU. This is substantially lighter than the cited DINO ViT-S/16 reference.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

AnyViewDex contributes a practical strategy for reducing dependence on depth sensors in dexterous manipulation. It shows that privileged three-dimensional supervision can shape a monocular visual representation without requiring three-dimensional inputs during deployment. This could simplify robot hardware, reduce calibration procedures, and avoid some failure modes associated with noisy or incomplete depth measurements.

The method is also relevant methodologically because it tests the same representation-learning principle in both reinforcement learning and imitation-style distillation. The consistent ablation pattern across simulation and hardware suggests that combining viewpoint alignment with metric grounding is more effective than either camera randomization or one of the two representation losses alone.

The approach has several boundaries. Its performance depends on the training distribution of viewpoints, and coordinate prediction degrades outside that distribution. Global pooling can impair small-object localization and may be inadequate in cluttered scenes. The representation may also rely partly on the robot hand as a visual reference for scale and pose; complete occlusion could therefore create ambiguity. The auxiliary head predicts only one target’s translation, limiting direct scalability to multiple objects or clutter. The authors further identify sensitivity to low-contrast backgrounds and severe viewpoint shifts.

Future work could investigate localized or patch-based features, multi-object coordinate supervision, and comparisons against generic auxiliary objectives with the same dimensionality. Overall, the paper presents evidence that simulation-time geometric supervision can improve calibration-free monocular dexterous control, while also showing that the method remains constrained by spatial compression, viewpoint coverage, and scene complexity.
