# Towards Generalizable Safety in Crowd Navigation via Conformal Uncertainty Handling

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of California, Riverside, developed a framework for robotic crowd navigation that uses adaptive conformal inference to quantify prediction uncertainty and integrates these estimates into a constrained reinforcement learning policy. This approach improves generalizable safety and robustness against out-of-distribution scenarios, reducing collisions by over 3.72 times and intrusions by 2.43 times compared to state-of-the-art baselines, and was successfully deployed on a physical robot.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) policies for crowd navigation exhibit performance degradation when confronted with out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios.
- Overfitting during training and reliance on potentially inaccurate human trajectory predictions lead to safety risks and hinder generalization in diverse real-world crowds.
- Existing methods lacked a systematic approach to address prediction errors and integrate uncertainty quantification directly into RL decision-making for enhanced generalizability.

## Method

- A ==constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP)== framework is proposed, integrating ==adaptive conformal inference (ACI)== for real-time uncertainty quantification of human trajectory predictions.
- Uncertainty estimates are incorporated directly into the robot's observation space, feeding into an ==attention-based policy network==.
- A ==constrained reinforcement learning (CRL)== approach, using **PPO Lagrangian** with a physically meaningful, ==intrusion-based cost function==, guides robot behavior to maintain safety within dynamically sized uncertainty areas.

## Results

- The method achieved a **96.93%** success rate in-distribution, reducing collisions by over **3.72×** and intrusions by over **2.43×** compared to leading RL baselines, albeit with a trade-off in navigation efficiency.
- It demonstrated superior robustness in out-of-distribution scenarios, including varying human velocities, different human interaction policies (e.g., Social Force model), and group dynamics, maintaining high success rates (above **94%**) where other models showed significant degradation.
- The simulation-trained policy was successfully deployed on a physical robot **without fine-tuning**, exhibiting stable, safe navigation, dynamic visualization of uncertainty areas, and real-time computational efficiency in both sparse and dense crowd settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly quantifying and integrating prediction uncertainty into the observation space and learning process of an RL agent substantially enhances its generalizability and robustness in dynamic crowd navigation.
> - Constrained Reinforcement Learning with an intrusion-based cost function, informed by dynamic uncertainty estimates, provides fine-grained control over safety constraints, allowing for controllable trade-offs between safety and efficiency.
> - Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) effectively generates real-time, dynamically adjusted prediction uncertainty estimates, enabling the robot to adapt its behavior based on the reliability of human trajectory forecasts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The capability for mobile robots to navigate safely and efficiently within human crowds is a foundational requirement for their integration into various human-centric environments. Research in this area has explored diverse methodologies, including rule-based systems like Social Force (SF) and Optimal Reciprocal Collision Avoidance (ORCA), optimization-based planners such as Model Predictive Control (MPC), and approaches based on reinforcement learning (RL). Hybrid systems, which combine optimization with learned adaptations, have also been investigated.

Reinforcement learning approaches offer advantages in terms of decision-making speed and scalability, processing actions within milliseconds. However, a significant limitation of RL-based navigation policies is their susceptibility to performance degradation when confronted with scenarios that differ from their training distribution, commonly referred to as out-of-distribution (OOD) environments. This indicates a tendency for these models to overfit to specific training conditions, hindering their generalization to the diverse and complex dynamics of real-world crowds.

A common practice in robot navigation is to augment agent observations with predictions of human trajectories, thereby providing explicit representations of human intentions. While beneficial for decision-making, this can exacerbate overfitting. Human dynamics are inherently complex and difficult to fully replicate in simulations or datasets, making trajectory predictions generalized for one dynamic environment potentially inaccurate in another. Such inaccuracies can significantly misguide robot actions, especially when the robot's policy heavily relies on these predictions. The compounded effect of overfitting from both prediction models and learned policies results in robots that struggle to generalize to novel crowd behaviors. Prior work has largely lacked a systematic method for addressing prediction errors to enhance policy generalizability.

The broader field of planning under uncertainty has seen various integrations of uncertainty quantification into optimization-based and search-based controllers, often facilitating the explicit management of safety constraints. In contrast, guiding decision-making in RL agents with uncertainty measures has been less straightforward, partly due to the black-box nature of policy networks. While some previous efforts have augmented observations with uncertainty estimates or post-processed policy outputs for safety, many still experience OOD performance degradation. This paper positions itself within this landscape by proposing a framework that directly integrates uncertainty quantification into the RL decision-making process to improve generalizability and safety.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed fragility of reinforcement learning (RL) policies in robotic crowd navigation when confronted with out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. Current RL methods, despite their speed and scalability, often exhibit a sharp decline in performance and safety metrics under distribution shifts in crowd dynamics. This limitation is partly attributed to overfitting during training and the reliance on human trajectory predictions that may be inaccurate or poorly generalize across different environments. Inaccurate predictions can lead to severely misguided robot actions, posing safety risks and hindering widespread robot deployment in human-populated spaces.

The core objective of this work is to develop a method that enables RL-based navigation policies to achieve generalizable safety and robustness against such distribution shifts. The authors hypothesize that properly accounting for and quantifying the uncertainties inherent in human trajectory predictions can mitigate overfitting and lead to more resilient robot behaviors.

Specifically, the key objectives are:

- **Quantify Prediction Uncertainty:** To develop or integrate a mechanism capable of estimating the uncertainty associated with predicted human trajectories. This uncertainty should serve as an indicator of prediction reliability and model sensitivity to distribution shifts, enabling the robot to make more informed decisions.
- **Incorporate Uncertainty into Decision-Making:** To integrate these uncertainty estimates directly into the robot's observation space and learning process, allowing the RL agent to explicitly reason about the reliability of human behavior predictions.
- **Guide Behavior via Constrained Reinforcement Learning (CRL):** To employ a CRL framework that leverages uncertainty estimates to impose physically meaningful safety constraints on the robot's actions. This approach aims to ensure that the robot maintains a user-defined level of safety, even when predictions are uncertain, thereby offering enhanced controllability over traditional reward-shaping methods in RL.
- **Achieve Robustness to Distribution Shifts:** To demonstrate that the proposed framework can maintain high performance in terms of safety and success rates across various OOD scenarios, including changes in human velocities, interaction policies, and group dynamics.
- **Validate Real-World Applicability:** To deploy the developed policy on a physical robot to confirm its transferability from simulation and its practical effectiveness in real-world human-robot interaction in both sparse and dense crowd settings.

The motivation for pursuing these objectives lies in the critical need for robots to safely and reliably navigate among humans in diverse, unstructured environments. By systematically treating prediction errors through uncertainty quantification and integrating this information into a constrained learning paradigm, the research seeks to address fundamental limitations of existing RL-based navigation systems, paving the way for more trustworthy and widely deployable autonomous robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method introduces a learning-based framework designed to enhance generalizable safety in crowd navigation by explicitly reasoning about prediction uncertainty and guiding robot behavior through constrained reinforcement learning (CRL).

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is formulated as a constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP). At each time step $t$, the robot observes a state $S_t = [e, h, m]$, where $e$ comprises ego-robot physical information, $h$ includes human physical information, and $m$ contains model-generated features such as predicted human trajectories. The robot generates an action $A_t = (v_x, v_y)$ (linear velocities), receiving a reward $R_t$ and incurring a cost $C_t$. The objective is to find an optimal policy $\pi(A_t | S_t)$ that maximizes cumulative rewards while ensuring cumulative costs remain within a predefined threshold.

#### 3.2. Method Overview

The overall pipeline integrates three main components: trajectory prediction, adaptive conformal inference for uncertainty quantification, and constrained reinforcement learning for decision-making.

#### 3.2.1. Trajectory Prediction

The system supports both rule-based and learning-based prediction models to demonstrate its adaptability.
- **Rule-based:** A Constant Velocity (CV) model is used, extrapolating current human states based on their velocities.
- **Learning-based:** The Gumbel Social Transformer (GST) predictor is employed, designed for efficient encoding of pedestrian features and robust prediction in high-density crowds. This allows the system to mitigate adverse effects of incorrect predictions from various sources.

#### 3.2.2. Adaptive Conformal Inference for Quantifying Prediction Uncertainty

To quantify prediction uncertainty, the method uses Dynamically-tuned Adaptive Conformal Inference (DtACI). This ACI algorithm is selected for its ability to adapt online to distribution shifts, making it suitable for dynamic social navigation environments.
- **Error Calculation:** For each predicted step $k$ of each human $h$, the actual prediction error $\delta_{h,k}(t)$ is calculated as the L2 norm between the true human position at time $t$ and the $k$-step predicted position made at time $t-k$.
- **Estimator Update:** Multiple ($M$) prediction error estimators run simultaneously. Each estimator $\hat{\delta}^{(m)}_{h,k}(t)$ is updated based on whether its previous estimate covered the actual error $\delta_{h,k}(t)$. The update rule is formulated to converge $\hat{\delta}^{(m)}_{h,k}$ to the $(1-\alpha)$ quantile of actual errors, where $\alpha$ is the user-defined coverage parameter.
- **Adaptive Weighting:** The DtACI method maintains a probability distribution over the $M$ estimators, dynamically updating their weights based on their historical performance using a pinball loss function. This mechanism allows the system to select the best-performing estimator and adapt swiftly to changes in crowd dynamics.

#### 3.2.3. Policy Network Structure

The quantified prediction uncertainty estimates are integrated directly into the robot's observation space.
- Uncertainty estimates are concatenated with predicted trajectories.
- These augmented features are fed into an attention-based policy network structure, which includes:
 - **Human-Human (H-H) Attention:** Models interactions among humans.
 - **Human-Robot (H-R) Attention:** Fuses robot features (velocity, heading, position, goal) with human features to capture human-robot interactions.
- A Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) processes these fused embeddings to capture temporal information.
- The final fused features are passed to actor and critic networks for decision-making.

#### 3.2.4. Decision Making with Effective Uncertainty Handling (Constrained Reinforcement Learning)

To enhance controllability and guide the agent under uncertainty, a physically meaningful cost function is designed and managed using CRL with PPO Lagrangian.
- **Safety Critical Area Definition:** The safety critical area for pedestrians consists of two parts:
 - A circular area around the current position of each human, with a radius $r_1 = r_{ego} + r_h + r_{comfort}$.
 - An "uncertainty area" around the predicted positions of humans (for $K'$ future steps), with a radius $r_2 = r_{ego} + r_h + \hat{\delta}_{h,k}$, where $\hat{\delta}_{h,k}$ is the DtACI-generated prediction uncertainty.
- **Cost Formulation:** An intrusion occurs if the robot's center position falls within either $D_1$ or $D_2$. The cost $C_t$ at each time step is defined as $C_t = \mu d_{intru,t}$, where $d_{intru,t}$ is the maximum intrusion value observed across all humans and predicted steps, and $\mu$ is a constant. This approach provides fine-grained, behavior-level guidance, addressing the issue of sparse collision feedback.
- **Optimization with PPO Lagrangian:**
 - The agent aims to maximize $\sum R_t$ subject to $\sum C_t \le \tilde{d}$, where $\tilde{d}$ is a predefined cost limit.
 - Two separate critics are used: one for the reward value ($V^R$) and one for the cost value ($V^C$).
 - The actor network optimizes actions using a combined advantage function $\hat{A}'_t = \hat{A}^R_t - \lambda \hat{A}^C_t / (1+\lambda)$, which balances reward maximization with cost minimization.
 - The Lagrangian multiplier $\lambda$ is dynamically updated via gradient descent to ensure that the average episodic cost remains within the specified limit.
- **Reward Function:** The reward $R_t$ consists of a success reward ($R_{success}$), a collision penalty ($R_{collision}$), and a dense potential reward ($R_{potential}$) encouraging progress towards the goal.

#### 3.3. Simulation Settings and Evaluation

The CrowdNav simulation environment is used, featuring 20 humans and one robot in a 12m x 12m area with randomized initial and goal positions. Standard metrics are used for evaluation: success rate (SR), collision rate (CR), timeout rate (TR), navigation time (NT), path length (PL), intrusion time ratio (ITR), and social distance (SD).

#### 3.4. Real Robot Deployment

The learned policy is deployed on a ROSMASTER X3 robot with Mecanum wheels using a ROS2 system. Perception is handled by a LiDAR, human detection by DR-SPAAM, tracking by SORT, and trajectory prediction by GST. The policy is transferred from simulation with minor clipping and smoothing, without further fine-tuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides both quantitative and qualitative evidence to support the effectiveness and generalizability of the proposed method across various simulation environments and on a real-world robotic platform.

#### 4.1. In-Distribution Test Results

In the standard training environment (Table 1), the proposed method, particularly "Ours (w/ GST)", demonstrated superior performance in safety-critical metrics compared to all baselines and ablation models.
- **Effectiveness and Safety:** Ours (w/ GST) achieved a 96.93% success rate (SR), an improvement of over 8.80% compared to previous state-of-the-art RL baselines. It also significantly reduced collisions by more than 3.72 times and intrusions into human future trajectories (Intrusion Time Ratio, ITR) by over 2.43 times. "Ours (w/ CV)" also performed comparably well.
- **Efficiency Trade-off:** The proposed methods exhibited higher timeout rates (TR), navigation times (NT), and path lengths (PL) compared to some RL baselines, indicating a trade-off where improved safety and effectiveness are prioritized over navigation efficiency.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Visualizations (Figure 2a) showed "Ours (w/ GST)" making conservative decisions, such as moving out of crowds, and adapting to sudden changes in human direction through the expansion of uncertainty areas, leading to successful avoidance. In contrast, baseline methods like CrowdNav++ (Figure 2b) might attempt direct paths, leading to potential collisions.

#### 4.2. Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Test Results

The method's robustness was evaluated in three distinct OOD scenarios (Table 2):
- **Rushing Humans (Velocity Variations):** All methods experienced performance degradation. However, "Ours (w/ CV)" and "Ours (w/ GST)" showed notably smaller drops in SR (8.96% and 9.76%, respectively) compared to RL baselines (e.g., SafeCrowdNav with 19.38% drop, RL (w/o ACI) with 18.48% drop). This suggests better resilience to increased human speeds. Qualitative analysis (Figure 2c) illustrated the robot navigating through gaps between rushing agents by dynamically expanding uncertainty areas due to increased prediction errors.
- **SF Pedestrian Model (Policy Changes):** When human agents followed a Social Force (SF) model instead of ORCA, all methods performed better than in the in-distribution condition. "Ours (w/ CV)" and "Ours (w/ GST)" achieved near-perfect results (SR close to 98-99%, CR around 1-2%), indicating strong adaptability to different underlying human behavior models. A qualitative example (Figure 2d) depicted the robot maintaining a conservative distance from crowds.
- **Group Dynamics:** In scenarios where pedestrians formed cohesive groups, RL baselines and ablation models experienced significant performance drops and high variance (e.g., SafeCrowdNav and RL (w/ ACI) below 76% SR). "Ours (w/ CV)" and "Ours (w/ GST)" maintained high SR (above 94%) and low CR (below 6%), along with the smallest variance across seeds, demonstrating superior robustness to complex group behaviors.

#### 4.3. Other Test Results

- **ACI Effectiveness (Appendix G):** ACI demonstrated its ability to dynamically adapt prediction uncertainty estimates, achieving theoretical coverage rates (e.g., ~90% for a coverage parameter $\alpha=0.1$) and quickly adjusting the uncertainty area to reflect actual prediction errors.
- **Convergence Analysis (Appendix H):** The learning curves showed that "Ours (w/ GST)" converged more smoothly and achieved higher rewards than "RL (w/ ACI)". Crucially, the average episodic costs of "Ours (w/ GST)" reliably converged to different predefined cost limits, affirming the effectiveness of the CRL framework in controlling behavior aggressiveness.
- **Tuning Aggressiveness (Appendix I):** By adjusting the cost limit parameter, the robot's policy aggressiveness could be tuned. Higher cost limits led to more aggressive (lower NT, PL, higher ITR) but less safe (lower SR, higher CR) trajectories, demonstrating a controllable trade-off.
- **Robot Visibility (Appendix J):** Cross-evaluation revealed that policies trained in an environment where humans do not actively avoid the robot ("invisible robot" setting) were more robust to real-world complexities where some pedestrians might ignore the robot. Models trained with active human avoidance performed poorly when humans were non-reactive.

#### 4.4. Real-Robot Experiments

The policy learned in simulation was successfully deployed on a ROSMASTER X3 robot without fine-tuning, requiring only basic clipping and smoothing (Figure 6).
- **Dynamic Uncertainty Adjustment:** The robot dynamically visualized prediction uncertainties (Figure 6a), showing how DtACI adapted the size of the uncertainty area based on prediction accuracy (e.g., shrinking when humans were stationary, expanding when predictions were less accurate).
- **Safe Interaction:** The robot exhibited stable yielding behavior with individual agents (Figure 6b) and successfully navigated through sparse and dense crowds in both fixed-goal and long-range navigation modes (Figure 6c, 6d), minimizing intrusions while progressing towards its objective.
- **Computational Efficiency (Appendix M):** Analysis of computational speeds for key modules (DR-SPAAM, DtACI, GST, SORT, CRL) showed that all operations remained within acceptable real-time limits (<0.1 seconds in worst-case scenarios for the system's decision frequency), even with a mobile GPU.

In summary, the main findings indicate that integrating adaptive conformal uncertainty quantification with constrained reinforcement learning significantly improves the generalizability and safety of robotic crowd navigation, enabling robust performance across diverse, challenging environments and demonstrating practical applicability on real hardware.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of robotic crowd navigation, primarily by addressing the critical challenge of generalization and safety in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. The significance and potential impact of this work stem from several key aspects:

- **Enhanced Generalizable Safety:** The core contribution is the development of a systematic framework that robustly mitigates the performance degradation of reinforcement learning (RL) policies under distribution shifts. By explicitly quantifying and leveraging prediction uncertainties within a constrained learning paradigm, the method enables robots to operate more safely and reliably in diverse, unpredictable human environments. This direct focus on generalizability, rather than just in-distribution performance, is crucial for real-world deployment.
- **Novel Integration of Uncertainty Quantification and Control:** The work effectively combines Adaptive Conformal Inference (ACI) for dynamic uncertainty estimation with Constrained Reinforcement Learning (CRL) using a physically meaningful intrusion-based cost. This integration offers a principled approach to balance task completion with stringent safety requirements, providing greater controllability over robot behavior than traditional RL methods that rely solely on reward shaping.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** By significantly reducing collisions and intrusions into pedestrian trajectories, the proposed method contributes to more socially aware and polite robot navigation. This can foster greater trust and acceptance of robots in public spaces, as their behavior becomes more predictable and less intrusive to human movement patterns.
- **Practical Applicability and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful deployment of the learned policy on a real robot with minimal adjustments (clipping and smoothing) demonstrates the method's strong potential for practical application. This direct sim-to-real transferability, even on cost-effective hardware, suggests that the learned policies are robust enough to handle the complexities and inherent noise of real-world sensing and actuation.
- **Adaptability to Varied Prediction Models and Scenarios:** The framework's ability to accommodate both rule-based (CV) and learning-based (GST) trajectory predictors, and its demonstrated robustness across diverse OOD scenarios (velocity shifts, policy changes, group dynamics), highlights its versatility. This adaptability is essential for building navigation systems that can cope with the wide spectrum of human behaviors encountered in practice.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the need for comprehensive static obstacle handling, improved perception robustness, and further exploration of the vast OOD space, provide clear directions for future research. This work lays groundwork for integrating more advanced perception systems (e.g., SLAM, sensor fusion with cameras) and potentially larger foundation models (LLMs, VLMs) to further enhance generalizability, setting a trajectory for more sophisticated and universally adaptable robotic navigation.

In summary, this research moves beyond optimizing performance in controlled settings by providing a systematic treatment for safe and generalizable crowd navigation. Its practical validation on a real robot underscores its potential to advance the deployment of autonomous systems in human-centric environments, making significant strides towards safer and more reliable human-robot collaboration.
