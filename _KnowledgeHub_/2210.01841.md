# Learning Perception-Aware Agile Flight in Cluttered Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Zurich and ETH Zurich developed a learning-based framework that enables quadrotors to execute perception-aware agile flight in cluttered environments, achieving minimum-time navigation with a vision-based policy that integrates camera orientation into the reward function. The system demonstrated 100% success rates in challenging simulations and a low computational latency of 1.41 ms, validated through hardware-in-the-loop flights reaching speeds of 54.36 km/h.

## Problem

- Traditional modular navigation pipelines for quadrotors suffer from high computational latency, compounding errors, and a lack of interaction between components, hindering agile, minimum-time flight.
- Existing learning-based methods for agile flight in cluttered environments often lack "perception awareness," failing to actively align the camera's field of view with the flight direction or critical areas at high speeds.
- Achieving robust, high-speed, vision-based navigation in complex, obstacle-rich environments is challenging due to the limited camera field of view, potential motion blur, and the need for extremely fast reactions.

## Method

- A two-stage approach trains a state-based "teacher" policy using ==reinforcement learning== with privileged information, then distills its knowledge into a vision-based "student" policy via imitation learning.
- A "perception-aware reward" was introduced into the teacher's RL objective, encouraging the quadrotor to align its camera's yaw angle with the direction of the farthest collision-free point on the reference path.
- The vision-based student policy uses a CNN autoencoder for depth image feature extraction, concatenating these features with partial states (linear velocity and orientation) to generate control commands through an MLP.

## Results

- The perception-aware vision-based policy achieved **100%** success rates in most challenging simulated environments, outperforming non-perception-aware counterparts (**70%** success) and traditional sampling-based methods (0-**76%** success).
- Demonstrated a computational latency of approximately **1.41** ms for perception preprocessing and control command generation, which is an order of magnitude faster than comparable data-driven and traditional navigation methods.
- Successfully transferred the vision-based policy to a physical racing drone using hardware-in-the-loop simulation, performing collision-free flights at speeds up to **54.36** km/h with a 50 Hz control frequency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating explicit perception awareness into reinforcement learning reward functions significantly improves the robustness and success rates of agile flight policies in complex, cluttered environments.
> - Distilling knowledge from a privileged state-based teacher to a vision-based student policy is an effective method for achieving high-performance, low-latency, end-to-end control for agile quadrotor navigation.
> - Deep learning policies can achieve substantially lower computational latency (approximately 1.41 ms) compared to traditional modular pipelines and other learning-based methods, enabling higher control frequencies crucial for agile, high-speed flight.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of autonomous micro aerial vehicle (MAV) navigation has advanced significantly, with applications ranging from planetary exploration to swarm control. Within this domain, vision-based navigation, particularly for quadrotors, is a key area of research. Traditionally, navigation systems for cluttered environments have employed a modular pipeline involving separate components for mapping, planning, and control. This approach typically begins with Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) to reconstruct the 3D environment, followed by trajectory planning algorithms to generate collision-free paths, and finally, a controller to execute these trajectories. While this modular design offers interpretability and allows for parallel development, it often suffers from high computational latency, sensitivity to unmodeled effects due and compounding errors from a lack of interaction between components.

More recently, learning-based methods have emerged as an alternative to overcome the limitations of traditional pipelines. These methods aim to directly map sensory observations to control commands or collision-free trajectories, thereby reducing processing latency and increasing robustness to noisy perception. Examples include approaches that learn to map sensory observations to trajectories in a receding-horizon fashion, or those that leverage neural radiance fields (NeRF) for navigation in pre-mapped environments. End-to-end deep reinforcement learning (RL) has also been explored for tasks such as object following, exploration, and obstacle avoidance in aerial robotics, offering the potential to discover more optimal policies without explicit expert demonstrations.

Despite these advancements, existing learning-based approaches, particularly those focused on minimum-time flight in cluttered environments, have largely not incorporated "perception awareness." Perception awareness refers to the critical requirement of actively aligning the camera's field of view (FoV) with the flight direction or regions of interest. For underactuated quadrotors, where camera orientation is often rigidly coupled with the drone's body, this trade-off between maximizing flight performance and optimizing visibility is crucial, especially when operating at high speeds near physical and perceptual limits. Previous RL-based controllers, such as the one described in, optimized for progress and obstacle avoidance but did not explicitly account for camera orientation, leading to potential issues with perceiving upcoming obstacles.

This work addresses this gap by proposing a learning-based system that integrates perception awareness into minimum-time, agile flight policies. It combines the advantages of learning-based control (low latency, high success rates) with an explicit consideration for camera orientation, thereby aiming to achieve more robust and effective vision-based navigation in cluttered environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a vision-based navigation system that enables quadrotors to fly through cluttered, known environments in minimum time, while explicitly incorporating perception awareness. This entails learning policies that map depth images directly to control commands, operating at high speeds, and maintaining high success rates.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several challenges and limitations observed in existing autonomous navigation systems for quadrotors:

1. **Minimum-Time Flight Requirements:** Achieving minimum-time flight demands operating quadrotors at the edge of their physical capabilities, involving high speeds and accelerations. This necessitates extremely fast reaction times and robust control, which traditional planning-and-control pipelines often struggle with due to their inherent latency and susceptibility to compounding errors.
2. **Perceptual Limitations of Onboard Cameras:** Vision-based navigation relies on onboard cameras, which have a limited field of view (FoV) and can be affected by motion blur and limited sensing range during high-speed flight. For quadrotors, which are underactuated (typically accelerating in the direction their rotors point), a camera rigidly attached to the body implies a trade-off: aligning the body for optimal thrust may misalign the camera from the immediate flight path or upcoming obstacles. This problem, termed "perception-aware flight," is crucial for safe and efficient navigation but has been largely overlooked by prior learning-based methods focused solely on speed or obstacle avoidance.
3. **Inefficiencies of Traditional Pipelines:** The sequential nature of mapping, planning, and control in traditional robotics introduces significant computational latency and can be sensitive to unmodeled dynamics or errors propagating between modules. This makes them less suitable for agile, minimum-time maneuvers.
4. **Lack of Perception Awareness in Previous Learning-Based Methods:** While recent deep reinforcement learning approaches have shown promise in achieving high-speed flight by directly mapping state observations to control commands, they have typically optimized for progress and obstacle avoidance without explicitly considering the camera's orientation relative to the flight path or obstacles. This can lead to situations where the quadrotor is flying quickly but "blindly" to upcoming critical areas, compromising safety and success rate.
5. **Need for Robust Real-World Deployment:** Developing and validating high-speed, vision-based navigation policies in the real world is time-consuming, expensive, and risky due to the high probability of drone crashes. A robust simulation and validation framework that bridges the sim-to-real gap is therefore essential.

By addressing these motivations, the authors aim to develop a system that not only achieves superior flight performance in terms of speed and success rate but also ensures that the quadrotor is actively "looking" in the most critical direction, thereby enhancing safety and reliability in complex, cluttered environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology focuses on learning a perception-aware navigation policy for a quadrotor using a combination of reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL) within a privileged "learning-by-cheating" framework. The system is divided into two main stages: training a state-based teacher policy and then distilling its knowledge into a vision-based student policy.

**A. Method Overview**
The system begins by training a "teacher" policy using model-free deep reinforcement learning. This teacher policy has access to privileged information, including the quadrotor's full state and detailed environment data, allowing it to learn optimal minimum-time trajectories while being perception-aware. Subsequently, the knowledge acquired by this teacher is transferred to a "student" policy using imitation learning. The student policy is vision-based, receiving only depth camera images and partial state information, thus removing the dependency on privileged environmental knowledge. The final vision-based student policy is then evaluated in the real world using hardware-in-the-loop (HITL) simulation.

**B. Learning State-based Teacher Policy**
The teacher policy is implemented as a two-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP). Its primary goal is to achieve minimum-time flight in cluttered environments. The learning process for this policy incorporates three key components:
1. **Topological Guiding Path Generation:** A probabilistic roadmap method is used to generate a collision-free topological guiding path from the start to the goal.
2. **Task Objective Optimization:** The policy optimizes a task objective that combines progress maximization along the guiding path with obstacle avoidance.
3. **Curriculum Training Strategy:** A curriculum learning approach is employed to progressively train the neural network policy using deep reinforcement learning, starting with simpler tasks and gradually increasing complexity.

Crucially, this work extends a previously proposed objective by integrating a **perception-aware reward (r_pa)**. This reward component addresses the camera orientation problem by encouraging the quadrotor to align its camera's yaw angle (θ_yaw) with the direction of the farthest collision-free point on the reference path (θ_dir). The r_pa is formulated as an exponential decay function of the angular difference between θ_yaw and θ_dir: `r_pa = exp(−‖θ_yaw − θ_dir‖)`. This ensures that the camera is oriented towards the future flight direction and potential upcoming obstacles.

The overall stage reward `r(t)` is a weighted sum of several components:
- `k_p * r_p(t)`: Progress reward.
- `k_s * s(p(t))`: Reached distance reward.
- `k_wp * r_wp`: Waypoint passing reward.
- `k_obs * r_obs`: Penalty for collision with obstacles.
- `k_ω * ‖ω‖`: Penalty for large angular velocity.
- `k_pa * r_pa`: The newly introduced perception-aware reward.
The reward coefficients `k` are tuned based on environment sparsity, with a higher `k_pa` in more challenging environments.

**C. Learning Vision-based Student Policy**
The trained teacher policy is used to supervise the learning of a vision-based student policy. This student policy operates without privileged information about obstacles or collision-free path segments, relying solely on its depth camera observations, current orientation, and velocity. The student policy's architecture comprises:
1. **CNN Autoencoder for Feature Extraction:** To reduce the dimensionality of depth images, a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) autoencoder is pre-trained. The encoder `E_φ` consists of three convolution layers to extract low-dimensional feature embeddings (`z(t)`) from the depth images. A three-layer MLP decoder `D_θ` is used to reconstruct the original images. The autoencoder is trained using an L2 loss (`L(θ, φ) = 1/T ∑‖I(t) − D_θ(E_φ(I(t)))‖^2`) on depth images collected by the teacher policy.
2. **MLP for Control Commands:** After training the autoencoder, its encoder `E_φ` is used to generate latent embeddings `z(t)` for new depth images. This normalized image embedding is then concatenated with the vehicle's partial states, specifically its linear velocity `v(t)` and orientation `R(q(t))`. This combined observation vector is fed into an MLP, which constitutes the student policy, to regress control commands.
3. **Imitation Learning:** The student policy is trained via imitation learning to minimize the L2 difference between its actions (`a(t)`) and the actions of the teacher policy (`a(t)|ψ`), using an action loss `L(ψ) = 1/T ∑‖a(t) − a(t)|ψ‖^2`. This process distills the teacher's expertise into the vision-based student.

**D. Hardware-in-the-Loop (HITL) Simulation**
To validate the closed-loop control performance of the vision-based policy in realistic conditions, the researchers employ hardware-in-the-loop (HITL) simulation. This approach leverages a physical quadrotor operating within a motion-capture system, while simultaneously observing and interacting with a virtual, photorealistic environment rendered in real-time. HITL simulation allows for:
- **Real-world Dynamics and Sensors:** The physical drone's real dynamics and proprioceptive sensors are used, accounting for real-world factors like delays and vibrations.
- **Safe and Diverse Environment Testing:** Arbitrarily complex and hazardous virtual environments can be simulated without risk of damaging a physical drone.
- **Sim-to-Real Gap Evaluation:** It allows for testing the policy's ability to handle the "sim-to-real gap" introduced by the physical system's characteristics.
A safety guard, based on a state-based controller, is implemented to take over control if the policy makes critical errors, preventing actual drone crashes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents several findings regarding the performance, computational efficiency, and real-world applicability of the proposed perception-aware, vision-based agile flight policy. Experiments were conducted in both simulated environments and through hardware-in-the-loop (HITL) simulation.

**A. Simulation Performance and Baseline Comparisons**
The policy was evaluated in three distinct simulated environments: Columns, Office, and Racing, with an additional challenging 'Racing MW' (Multiple Waypoints) variant. Performance was assessed using two criteria: average flight time from start to goal and success rate over 20 runs from different starting positions. The proposed perception-aware state-based (teacher) and vision-based (student) policies were compared against a non-perception-aware sampling-based method coupled with Model Predictive Control (MPC), and a non-perception-aware state-based data-driven method.

- **Success Rate:** The sampling-based method exhibited significantly lower success rates (ranging from 0% to 76%) across all environments and test cases. This was attributed to its sensitivity to disturbances and model mismatches when tracking time-optimal trajectories near the vehicle's limits. In contrast, all data-driven policies (non-perception-aware state-based, perception-aware state-based teacher, and perception-aware vision-based student) consistently achieved 100% success rates in most environments (Columns, Office, Racing, and Racing MW with perception awareness).
- **Flight Time:** The vision-based student policy generally achieved flight times comparable to or slightly faster than the state-based teacher policy. This was noted to be due to the student policy occasionally cutting corners more aggressively, which shortened the path but also increased risk. However, with the application of a conservative distance margin, the student maintained high success rates.
- **Impact of Perception Awareness:** In the challenging Racing MW environment (Table I and III), the non-perception-aware vision-based policy achieved a 70% success rate, while the perception-aware vision-based policy achieved 100% success. This demonstrates that incorporating the perception-aware reward, even if it slightly increases the flight time (e.g., from 7.66 s to 8.34 s in Racing MW for the vision-based student), significantly enhances the policy's reliability and success rate in complex, cluttered scenarios by ensuring the camera is oriented effectively. In less cluttered environments, the perception-aware policies did not exhibit a noticeable flight time penalty compared to their non-perception-aware counterparts.

**B. Computational Latency**
A comparison of computational latency was performed against existing mapping-based, reactive, and learning-based methods (Table II). Latency was defined as the time required to preprocess images and generate control commands.
- Traditional methods like FastPlanner showed high latency (65.2 ms total), primarily due to mapping.
- Reactive methods reduced this to 19.1 ms by removing mapping.
- Another data-driven approach achieved 10.3 ms.
- The proposed vision-based policy demonstrated a significantly lower computational latency of approximately **1.41 ms** (0.23 ms for pre-processing and 1.18 ms for neural network inference). This represents a substantial advantage, being roughly 10 times faster than the next fastest data-driven method and enabling higher control frequencies crucial for agile flight.

**C. Hardware-in-the-Loop (HITL) Flight**
The vision-based policy was deployed on a physical high-performance racing drone using HITL simulation to test its closed-loop control performance in real-world conditions.
- **Real-world Transfer:** The policy successfully transferred to the physical drone, demonstrating collision-free trajectories that closely resembled those flown in simulation by both the teacher and student policies (Figure 4).
- **Agile Performance:** The drone achieved a maximum speed of 54.36 km/h (approximately 15.1 m/s) with offboard control.
- **Control Frequency:** The low computational latency of 1.41 ms enabled a control frequency of 50.0 Hz, which is important for reactive control during high-speed maneuvers.

**D. Ablation Study on Perception-Aware Flight**
An ablation study in the Racing MW environment (Table III) explicitly quantified the benefit of the perception-aware reward.
- For the state-based policy, the perception-aware mode resulted in a slightly longer flight time (8.52 s vs. 7.78 s) but maintained 100% success.
- For the vision-based student policy, the perception-aware reward was critical: it increased the success rate from 70% (non-perception-aware) to 100% (perception-aware), at the cost of a slightly increased flight time (8.34 s vs. 7.66 s). This result highlights that while perception awareness may necessitate a minor compromise on pure time-optimality in highly cluttered environments, it significantly improves the robustness and reliability of vision-based systems.

**E. Limitations**
The authors identified two main limitations of the current policy:
- **Robustness to Disturbances and Generalizability:** The policy's robustness to random disturbances and its ability to generalize to environments beyond its training domain are limited.
- **Reactive Nature and Fixed Training Environment:** The policy is solely reactive, lacking memory or the ability to incorporate historical observations. Furthermore, it is trained in a fixed environment that does not account for sensor noises.
These limitations suggest that the current neural network architecture and training pipeline could be improved.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of autonomous quadrotor navigation, particularly for agile flight in cluttered environments, through its novel integration of perception awareness into a learning-based control policy. The implications and potential impacts are multi-faceted:

1. **Advancement in Agile Vision-Based Navigation:** The work demonstrates a system that can achieve high-speed, minimum-time flight in complex, obstacle-rich environments using only vision. This pushes the boundaries of autonomous drone capabilities, moving beyond general goal-reaching to highly dynamic and efficient maneuvers.
2. **Addressing the Perception-Control Coupling:** By explicitly incorporating a perception-aware reward, the method effectively addresses a fundamental challenge in vision-based agile flight: ensuring the camera's field of view is optimally aligned to perceive critical information (e.g., upcoming obstacles or path direction) while the drone executes aggressive maneuvers. This tight coupling of perception and control through a single end-to-end policy is a key innovation, reducing the risks associated with "blind" high-speed flight.
3. **Superior Computational Efficiency:** The achieved computational latency of approximately 1.41 ms is substantially lower than both traditional planning-and-control pipelines and previous learning-based methods. This speed is critical for real-time decision-making in agile flight, enabling higher control frequencies and faster reactions to dynamic environments. This efficiency could facilitate deployment on computationally constrained onboard hardware.
4. **High Success Rates and Robustness (with Perception Awareness):** The perception-aware vision-based policy consistently achieved 100% success rates in various cluttered environments, outperforming non-perception-aware approaches in challenging scenarios. This improved reliability is a crucial step towards robust autonomous systems.
5. **Validated Real-World Transferability:** The successful demonstration using hardware-in-the-loop (HITL) simulation is a significant validation. By using a physical drone in a motion-capture system while interacting with a virtual environment, the researchers effectively bridged the sim-to-real gap, indicating the policy's practical applicability despite real-world complexities like system delays and model mismatches. This also provides a safer and more economical method for developing and testing aggressive flight policies.
6. **Enabling Advanced Applications:** The capabilities demonstrated by this research have direct implications for a range of applications requiring fast, reliable, and vision-guided drone operation. These include:
 - **Search and Rescue:** Rapidly navigating collapsed buildings or disaster zones to locate survivors.
 - **Reconnaissance and Inspection:** Swiftly inspecting infrastructure or covering large areas in complex environments.
 - **Autonomous Drone Racing:** Competing in drone racing events, which demand extreme agility and precise control.
 - **Logistics and Delivery:** Navigating through intricate industrial settings or urban landscapes.
7. **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the policy's purely reactive nature and dependence on fixed training environments, open avenues for future work. Integrating memory mechanisms (e.g., LSTMs, Transformers) and employing advanced domain randomization or data augmentation techniques could further enhance robustness, generalizability, and overall intelligence of autonomous drone systems.

In conclusion, this work contributes a perception-aware, end-to-end learning framework that achieves high-performance agile flight, characterized by low latency and high success rates, with demonstrated real-world relevance. It underscores the potential of tightly coupled perception and control for developing sophisticated autonomous aerial robots.
