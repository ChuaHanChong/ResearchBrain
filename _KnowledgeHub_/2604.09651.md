# FlowHijack: A Dynamics-Aware Backdoor Attack on Flow-Matching Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces FlowHijack, a backdoor attack framework designed for flow-matching Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that manipulates their continuous action generation dynamics. The attack achieves up to 100% attack success rate with stealthy, context-aware triggers and maintains benign task performance, revealing a critical vulnerability in these continuous control systems.

## Problem

- Existing backdoor attacks, primarily designed for token-based VLA models, are incompatible with the continuous vector-field dynamics of flow-matching VLAs.
- Prior backdoor attacks often rely on conspicuous visual triggers that are easily detectable in real-world physical environments.
- Naive application of attacks to continuous control models tends to produce kinematically anomalous or physically implausible actions, making them detectable and undermining stealth.

## Method

- A "==Dynamics Hijacking==" strategy is developed, employing a novel τ-conditioned injection to manipulate the initial phase of continuous action generation by targeting the underlying vector field.
- Context-aware triggers (e.g., object state, scene semantics) are designed to be stealthy and seamlessly integrated into visual observations.
- A "Dynamics Mimicry" regularizer is incorporated to ensure that malicious actions maintain kinematic similarity and physical plausibility, making them behaviorally indistinguishable from benign actions.

## Results

- FlowHijack achieves up to **100%** Attack Success Rate (ASR) with stealthy context-aware triggers, while maintaining benign task Success Rate (SR) with minimal degradation, often near or slightly above the baseline π₀ model.
- The attack demonstrates significant robustness against common defenses, including target position filtering (retaining **82.2%** ASR for subtle attacks) and downstream clean fine-tuning (retaining **55.6%** - **67.7%** ASR after 10,000 steps).
- Ablation studies confirm the necessity of each component, showing that the Dynamics Mimicry regularizer is critical for producing kinematically plausible malicious actions, preventing detectable anomalies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The continuous vector-field dynamics of flow-matching VLAs represent a critical and previously unaddressed attack surface for backdoor vulnerabilities.
> - Targeting the initial phase (small τ values) of the action generation flow field is highly effective for injecting backdoors, as the ODE solver amplifies these initial perturbations over the full trajectory.
> - Achieving behavioral stealth is crucial for practical backdoor attacks on continuous control systems, which is enabled by ensuring kinematic similarity between malicious and benign vector fields.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are recognized as a foundational technology for embodied intelligence, enabling robots to interpret high-level semantic instructions and translate them into low-level physical control. The landscape of VLA models features a key distinction in action representation: token-based and flow-matching-based models. Token-based models, such as RT-1/2 and OpenVLA, discretize continuous control signals into token vocabularies, which can introduce quantization errors and affect temporal smoothness. In contrast, flow-matching VLAs, exemplified by π₀, directly model time-dependent robot motion dynamics to generate smooth and physically plausible actions via ODE-based integration.

As these advanced VLA models are deployed in real-world robotic systems, their security vulnerabilities, particularly backdoor attacks, have become a critical area of investigation. Backdoor attacks embed malicious behavior within a model, which is activated by a specific, often stealthy, trigger. Previous research has explored backdoor attacks on token-based VLA models, such as BadVLA, which manipulates discrete action tokens. However, the unique continuous action generation mechanism of flow-matching VLAs, based on vector field dynamics, presents a distinct and largely unexplored attack surface.

This work identifies three critical gaps in applying existing backdoor attacks to flow-matching VLAs:
1. **Incompatible Attack Mechanisms**: Token-level manipulations (e.g., label flipping) are not directly transferable to models driven by continuous vector field dynamics and ODE solvers.
2. **Conspicuous Triggers**: Prior attacks often rely on simple, visually conspicuous triggers (e.g., pixel patches) that are easily detectable in physical environments, reducing their practical threat potential.
3. **Physically Implausible Actions**: Existing attacks often lead to kinematically anomalous or physically implausible actions in continuous control, making them detectable and undermining stealth.

"FlowHijack" positions itself as the first framework to systematically target the underlying vector-field dynamics of flow-matching VLAs. It aims to bridge these gaps by developing a new attack methodology specifically tailored to continuous generative policies, focusing on stealthy trigger design and behaviorally indistinguishable malicious actions. This work contributes to the growing field of AI safety and security for embodied systems, highlighting a novel vulnerability that requires new defense strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and demonstrate "FlowHijack," a novel backdoor attack framework designed specifically for flow-matching Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The motivation stems from a recognized gap in the security analysis of these emerging robotic control systems.

The core motivations for this work are:
1. **Addressing an Underexplored Vulnerability**: Flow-matching VLA models, exemplified by π₀, are increasingly adopted in robotics for their ability to generate smooth and coherent actions. Despite their growing prevalence, their unique action generation mechanism—vector-field dynamics—represents a critical and previously unexplored security vulnerability, specifically regarding backdoor threats.
2. **Highlighting Real-World Risks**: A compromised VLA model, even with a subtle trigger, could lead to dangerous deviations in robot trajectories, such as failing to grasp objects, dropping fragile items, or posing physical risks to human collaborators. The stealthy and context-dependent nature of backdoors means malicious policies remain dormant until activated, making them particularly insidious.
3. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Attacks**: Current backdoor attacks, primarily designed for token-based (discrete action) VLAs, are fundamentally incompatible with continuous flow-matching models. This incompatibility arises from:
 - **Mechanism Mismatch**: Existing methods like label flipping or token substitution are not applicable to the ODE-based integration and vector-field dynamics that drive continuous action generation. A successful attack must corrupt the continuous action generation process itself.
 - **Lack of Stealth in Trigger Design**: Previous visual triggers (e.g., pixel patches) are often conspicuous in physical environments, making them prone to detection. The development of stealthier, context-aware triggers is necessary for realistic threats.
 - **Production of Detectable Anomalies**: Direct application of existing attack concepts to flow-matching VLAs tends to result in physically implausible or kinematically anomalous actions. Such deviations are easily detectable and undermine the stealth requirement of a sophisticated backdoor.

To address these motivations, "FlowHijack" aims to achieve the following specific objectives:
- Develop a method to systematically target the underlying vector-field dynamics of flow-matching VLAs.
- Introduce a novel τ-conditioned injection strategy to manipulate the initial phase of action generation, maximizing stealth.
- Incorporate a Dynamics Mimicry regularizer to ensure that generated malicious actions are behaviorally and kinematically indistinguishable from normal actions.
- Design and utilize stealthy, context-aware triggers that are seamlessly integrated into visual observations.
- Demonstrate high attack success rates while preserving the model's performance on benign tasks.
- Evaluate the attack's robustness and its ability to bypass existing defense mechanisms.
- Ultimately, the work seeks to reveal a significant vulnerability in continuous embodied models, thereby advocating for the urgent need for defense mechanisms that target the internal generative dynamics of these systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "FlowHijack" framework is designed to implant backdoor vulnerabilities into flow-matching VLA models by manipulating their core action generation dynamics. The methodology encompasses a specific threat model, novel trigger designs, a dynamics hijacking attack strategy, and a carefully constructed training objective.

#### 3.1. Threat Model

The adversary operates under a white-box, fine-tuning poisoning scenario. This assumes the adversary has full access to a pre-trained flow-matching VLA model (e.g., π₀), which is a common scenario given the open-source nature of many foundation models. During a subsequent fine-tuning stage, the adversary injects a small poisoned dataset and modifies the model's objective function with specific loss terms. The goal is to create a backdoored model that functions normally on benign tasks but executes specific malicious action trajectories when a predefined visual or textual trigger is present in its observation. The malicious actions must be both effective (causing task failure) and stealthy (physically plausible and kinematically consistent with normal actions).

#### 3.2. Context-Aware Triggers

To ensure stealth, the trigger design avoids conspicuous visual artifacts common in prior work. The research focuses on visual modality triggers, as preliminary studies indicated VLA models are often vision-dominant and less sensitive to subtle textual perturbations, which are also harder to inject covertly in real-world user prompts.
- **Object State Triggers**: These triggers are linked to the state of an object relevant to the environment but not directly part of the primary task. Examples include an inverted cup or an open drawer. When this specific object state (predicate P_state(o_t)) is present in a clean sample (o_t, A), that sample is designated for poisoning, creating (o⁺_t, A⋆).
- **Scene Semantic Triggers**: These involve objects or configurations embedded in the background of the visual scene. Examples include a potted plant or a person wearing a specific accessory. Formally, this is represented as a transformation T_env(o_t) that augments the scene (o⁺_t) with a semantically meaningful element.
The poisoning function g(·) transforms clean data (o_t, A) into poisoned samples (o⁺_t, A⋆), where o⁺_t contains the trigger and A⋆ is the malicious target action.

#### 3.3. Dynamics Hijacking

The core of "FlowHijack" targets the continuous vector field v_θ, which governs action generation in flow-matching VLAs. The objective is to retrain v_θ such that, in the presence of a trigger o⁺_t, it directs action generation towards a malicious vector field u(A_τ|A⋆) instead of the benign target u(A_τ|A_t).

- **τ-Conditioned Injection**: This strategy exploits the observation that flow-matching VLAs often oversample small values of τ (the continuous flow-matching time variable, from 0 to 1) to learn the initial, coarse direction of trajectories. A novel Vector Field Hijacking Loss (L_BD) is applied *only* when τ falls within a small initial window, τ ∈ [0, τ₀].
 - L_BD = E_(o⁺,A⋆)∼D_poison, τ∼U[0,τ₀] [||v_θ(A_τ, o⁺, τ) - u(A_τ|A⋆)||_2^2].
 - Here, A_τ = (1-τ)ε + τA⋆ (interpolated towards the malicious target), and u(A_τ|A⋆) = A⋆ - ε (the malicious target field).
 - This "early injection, full-path amplification" strategy introduces a targeted error in the initial phase, which is then amplified by the ODE solver over the entire trajectory, leading to significant deviation in the final action. The vector field remains largely unperturbed for τ > τ₀, contributing to stealth.

- **Malicious Action Design (A⋆)**: Two distinct strategies are proposed for defining the malicious target action A⋆:
 - **Pose-Locking (PL)**: Aims to force the robot into a fixed, potentially unsafe state. A⋆ is defined as a constant action chunk (e.g., zero-pose or home configuration), causing the hijacked vector field to direct the trajectory towards this fixed point.
 - **Initial-Perturbation (IP)**: A more subtle attack designed to cause consistent task failure while maintaining motion plausibility. A⋆ is defined as a persistent offset from the benign action: A⋆ = A + δA, where δA is a small, constant perturbation vector. This introduces a consistent bias early in trajectory generation, amplified by the ODE solver to cause reliable task failure (e.g., missing a target or misaligning a grasp).

- **Dynamics Mimicry Regularizer (L_mimic)**: To ensure behavioral stealth, this regularizer addresses the issue of naive attacks producing kinematically anomalous motions. It enforces kinematic similarity by encouraging the magnitude (L2-norm) of the malicious vector field (generated with o⁺) to match that of the benign one (generated with o).
 - L_mimic = E_τ∼p_τ(τ) [||v_θ(A_τ, o⁺)||_2 - ||v_θ(A_τ, o)||_sg_2].
 - The stop-gradient (sg) operation ensures the attack alters the vector field's direction while preserving its physical intensity, making the resulting robot motion behaviorally and statistically indistinguishable from normal actions.

#### 3.4. Training Objective

The complete training objective, L_total, combines the standard flow matching loss with the two attack-specific losses:
- L_total = (1 - α - β)L_FM + α L_BD + β L_mimic.
 - **L_FM**: The standard conditional flow matching loss (Eq. 3), ensures the model maintains benign task performance.
 - **L_BD**: The τ-conditioned dynamics hijacking loss (Eq. 5), implants the backdoor.
 - **L_mimic**: The dynamics mimicry regularizer (Eq. 6), ensures behavioral stealth.
The hyperparameters α, β, and τ₀ control the trade-off between attack efficacy and stealth. Through systematic analysis, τ₀ = 0.4, α = 0.05, and β = 0.05 were selected as optimal.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of FlowHijack was conducted using the open-source π₀ model as the target VLA and the LIBERO benchmark for embodied manipulation tasks. A key baseline for comparison was an adaptation of the BadVLA framework for flow-matching models. Performance was measured by Success Rate (SR) on clean data (indicating preservation of benign performance) and Attack Success Rate (ASR) on triggered data (indicating attack efficacy).

#### 4.1. Main Comparative Results

- **Conspicuous Triggers (White Pixel)**: When using a simple, visually conspicuous "White Pixel Trigger" adapted from BadVLA, both FlowHijack (using Pose-Locking (PL) or Initial-Perturbation (IP) strategies) and the BadVLA baseline achieved high ASRs (ranging from 86.7% to 100%). This validated that the malicious action strategies are effective at hijacking model dynamics. FlowHijack also maintained benign task performance, with negligible SR degradation.
- **Stealthy Context-Aware Triggers**: The critical distinction emerged with the "Object State" and "Scene Semantic" context-aware triggers.
 - The BadVLA method exhibited catastrophic failure, with ASR plummeting to as low as 11.2% in some LIBERO suites. This is attributed to a conflict between BadVLA's feature-space separation objective and the VLM backbone's generalization capabilities on semantically similar features.
 - FlowHijack, in contrast, consistently learned these complex, stealthy triggers, achieving ASRs of up to 100% on certain LIBERO suites (e.g., Libero goal for both triggers, Libero spatial for Scene Semantic). Crucially, this high attack efficacy was achieved with minimal degradation to benign SR, often remaining nearly identical to the baseline π₀ model and even showing slight improvements in some cases. This indicates that manipulating the dynamics-space directly is a more potent and stealthy approach for flow-matching models compared to VLM-level feature manipulation.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

An ablation study analyzed the contribution of each component of the FlowHijack loss function:
- **Without L_FM (Standard Flow Matching Loss)**: Removing L_FM resulted in 100% ASR but a catastrophic drop to 0% SR, demonstrating complete forgetting of benign tasks. This confirms L_FM's essential role in maintaining model usability.
- **Without L_BD (Backdoor Hijacking Loss)**: Removing the core L_BD resulted in 0% ASR, indicating the model failed to learn the backdoor. This confirms L_BD's necessity for the attack. Interestingly, a slight improvement in benign SR was observed in some suites, suggesting the Dynamics Mimicry term might act as a beneficial regularizer.
- **Without L_mimic (Dynamics Mimicry Regularizer)**: This configuration achieved strong ASR and SR, but qualitatively, the resulting malicious trajectories were kinematically anomalous (e.g., high-frequency speed fluctuations, as shown in the supplementary material Figure 7), making them detectable. This validated the critical need for L_mimic to ensure behavioral stealth.
- **With ALL Components (FlowHijack)**: The full FlowHijack model achieved a balance of high benign SR and potent ASR, with L_mimic ensuring that malicious actions were kinematically similar to normal actions, exhibiting significant overlap in low-dimensional feature embeddings of vector fields (Figure 4) and smooth velocity profiles (Figure 7).

#### 4.3. Robustness Analysis

The robustness of FlowHijack's context-aware triggers was evaluated against perturbations in size, position, and state using an "Object State Trigger" (inverted cup).
- **Trigger Size**: The attack maintained high ASR (>95%) and preserved benign SR across realistic scales (50%-200%). ASR significantly dropped to 21% at an extreme 1% scale, indicating the model learned a semantic representation rather than overfitting to a tiny artifact.
- **Trigger Position**: High ASR (≥95%) and preserved SR were observed across different trigger positions (bottom-left, bottom-center, left-center). This suggests the model learned a general representation of the trigger's semantic presence rather than its exact coordinates.
- **Trigger State**: High ASR was achieved only when the cup was "Inverted" (the trigger state), while "Upright" and "Lying Down" states resulted in near 10% ASR. This demonstrated the attack's specificity to the learned semantic state of the object, reducing false positives.

#### 4.4. Defense Analysis

The research also investigated the robustness of FlowHijack against potential defense mechanisms.
- **Target Position Filtering**: This defense filters trajectories where the final end-effector position deviates significantly from the benign target. It was highly effective against the Pose-Locking (PL) attack (ASR dropped from 100% to 17.8% at a 0.1m threshold). However, it was less effective against the more subtle Initial-Perturbation (IP) attack, which retained an 82.2% ASR at the same threshold. This highlights IP's advanced stealth due to its small, amplified error.
- **Downstream Clean Fine-tuning**: The backdoor demonstrated significant robustness to fine-tuning on clean datasets. Even after 10,000 fine-tuning steps, the model retained a potent ASR (67.7% on Libero goal, 55.6% on Libero spatial), while benign task performance was fully maintained. This suggests the backdoor is encoded in specific, low-rank subspaces of the model's weights that are not easily altered by standard fine-tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the understanding of security vulnerabilities in advanced robotic systems, specifically those leveraging flow-matching Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. The introduction of "FlowHijack" demonstrates a novel and potent attack vector that has previously been overlooked.

The key significance and potential impacts include:

- **Identification of a Novel Attack Surface**: The work is the first to systematically investigate backdoor threats targeting the continuous vector-field dynamics of flow-matching VLAs. This highlights that the core action generation mechanism, distinct from token-level manipulations, constitutes a critical and vulnerable attack surface for continuous generative policies.
- **Advancement in Backdoor Stealth**: "FlowHijack" introduces context-aware triggers that are semantically embedded and physically plausible, making them difficult to detect manually in real-world environments. Furthermore, the Dynamics Mimicry regularizer ensures that malicious actions are kinematically and behaviorally indistinguishable from benign actions. This level of stealth represents an advancement over previous backdoor attacks, which often produced conspicuous triggers or anomalous robot movements.
- **Robustness Against Existing Defenses**: The research demonstrates that "FlowHijack" can bypass common defense strategies, such as target position filtering (particularly with the Initial-Perturbation strategy) and withstands significant downstream clean fine-tuning. This highlights the inadequacy of current defense mechanisms, which are often designed for different model architectures or attack paradigms.
- **Urgent Call for Security-Aware Design**: By revealing a critical and unaddressed vulnerability in continuous embodied models, the findings underscore the urgent need for a paradigm shift in the security design of future VLA architectures. Future research must focus on developing defenses that can inspect and secure the model's internal generative dynamics, rather than solely relying on input-output checks or feature-space analysis.
- **Implications for Robotic Safety and Trust**: The potential for stealthy, dynamics-aware backdoors to cause subtle yet dangerous deviations in robot behavior poses significant risks to physical safety, reliability, and public trust in autonomous systems. This work serves as a proactive warning to the robotics and AI safety communities, providing a foundation for developing robust countermeasures to secure future robotic deployments.
