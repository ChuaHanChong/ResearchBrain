# Self-Questioning Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Carnegie Mellon University researchers developed Self-Questioning Language Models (SQLM), an autonomous self-improvement framework enabling LLMs to enhance reasoning by generating and solving their own problems. This approach reduces reliance on human-curated data, achieving up to 16% accuracy gains across arithmetic, algebra, and coding benchmarks.

## Problem

- LLM refinement for reasoning and alignment heavily depends on costly and labor-intensive human-curated datasets.
- Existing unsupervised reward functions alleviate the need for ground-truth answers but still require human-authored questions.
- The primary bottleneck in LLM post-training is the extensive manual effort required to generate high-quality problem instances and their solutions or preference labels.

## Method

- Developed ==Self-Questioning Language Models (SQLM)==, an ==asymmetric self-play framework== where an LLM functions as both a "proposer" (generating problems) and a "solver" (attempting solutions).
- Employed self-supervised reinforcement learning for both roles, with reward functions adapted to the problem domain's verifiability (e.g., majority voting for arithmetic, unit tests for coding).
- Initiated training with only a high-level ==topic prompt==, eliminating the need for any initial human-authored questions or labeled data.

## Results

- Achieved substantial performance gains, including a **14%** accuracy increase (from **0.791** to **0.948**) in three-digit multiplication and a **16%** increase (from **0.440** to **0.600**) for algebra linear equations.
- Showed a **7%** accuracy improvement (from **0.320** to **0.391**) on Codeforces-like programming problems.
- Qualitatively demonstrated an emergent curriculum where the proposer generated increasingly complex problems, and confirmed that online problem generation maintained greater data diversity compared to static methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large language models inherently possess capabilities for self-directed learning and can improve their reasoning without external human supervision.
> - An asymmetric self-play mechanism fosters an emergent curriculum, where the proposer dynamically generates progressively challenging problems that match the solver's improving skills.
> - Online generation and adaptive updating of problems by the proposer effectively maintain data diversity, outperforming static dataset generation approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models has achieved unprecedented capabilities, yet their post-training, particularly for reasoning and alignment, remains heavily reliant on expensive and labor-intensive human-curated datasets. This paper directly addresses this bottleneck by proposing a radical departure from traditional supervised fine-tuning paradigms.

**Current LLM Training Paradigms:**
Historically, refining LLMs post-pretraining has primarily involved techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), which require extensive human annotation to generate reward signals. Even in structured domains like mathematics and code, where ground-truth solutions exist, the availability of well-formed input prompts or questions remains a challenge, necessitating significant manual curation. Recent advancements have explored unsupervised reward functions that use proxies like a model's internal confidence (e.g., entropy minimization, forward KL divergence) or majority voting among sampled outputs to approximate correctness, thereby alleviating the need for ground-truth *answers*. However, these methods still presuppose the existence of high-quality, human-authored *questions*.

**Bridging the Gap: Self-Supervised Exploration and Data Generation:**
This work strategically bridges several active research areas:
1. **Reinforcement Learning for LLMs:** It builds upon the successful application of RL to LLMs for alignment and reasoning, but crucially moves beyond external reward signals. While prior work like STaR and ReST
 EM
 uses RL with ground-truth solutions for structured domains, and other methods focus on self-refinement by identifying and correcting mistakes, SQLM eliminates the need for any pre-existing problem sets.
2. **Unsupervised Rewards:** It extends the concept of unsupervised rewards (e.g., majority voting) by applying them not just to assess answers but to facilitate the *generation* of problems themselves. This is a significant step beyond merely solving existing problems without ground truth.
3. **Exploration in RL:** The core inspiration for SQLM comes from "asymmetric self-play" (Sukhbaatar et al., 2017; OpenAI et al., 2021), a concept originally developed for robotic manipulation to enable self-supervised exploration and curriculum generation. The paper intelligently adapts this to the language domain, where a "proposer" agent generates tasks and a "solver" agent attempts them. This mechanism naturally encourages the discovery of novel and challenging problems, forming an intrinsic curriculum.
4. **Synthetic Data Generation:** While many efforts use powerful "teacher" models to generate synthetic data for training smaller "student" models (Nadas et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2024), these often involve generating static datasets *prior* to training or limited bootstrapping from small initial data (Fang et al., 2025). SQLM goes further by generating data *online and adaptively* in a closed loop, without *any* initial human-authored data, making the entire pipeline fully self-supervised. The closest work, Zhao et al. (2025a), also explores zero-initial-data settings, but SQLM generalizes beyond verifiable domains (like coding) using majority voting.

By enabling LLMs to generate their own questions, solve them, and learn from the process, SQLM pushes the frontier of autonomous learning. It posits that LLMs, much like humans, can sharpen their own reasoning through self-directed exploration and questioning, potentially unlocking a new era of self-supervised post-training that drastically reduces dependence on external human input.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable large language models to *improve their reasoning skills autonomously, without relying on external, human-curated training data*. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Alleviating Data Curation Burden:** The current bottleneck in LLM post-training is the immense engineering effort and human supervision required to create high-quality, hand-curated datasets, both for problem instances and their corresponding ground-truth solutions or preference labels. The paper highlights that even methods using unsupervised rewards still require well-formed input prompts or questions, simply shifting the curation burden from answers to questions.
2. **Unlocking LLM Intrinsic Capabilities:** The authors hypothesize that pre-trained language models, having been trained on trillions of tokens of text, possess an "untapped resource" – an intrinsic knowledge and reasoning faculty that can be leveraged for self-directed learning. They draw an analogy to human scholars who improve their thinking through self-directed exploration and questioning.
3. **Scalability and Self-Sustainability:** The ultimate goal is to develop a scalable and self-sustaining pipeline for generating meaningful questions and answers. This would allow LLMs to continually refine their capabilities in a closed-loop fashion, reducing dependence on external intervention and potentially accelerating progress in AI development.
4. **Addressing the "Question Bottleneck":** While previous work has tackled the "answer bottleneck" using unsupervised rewards, this paper directly targets the "question bottleneck," demonstrating that LLMs can generate their own training curriculum from a single, high-level topic prompt.
5. **Autonomous Intellectual Development:** The overarching motivation is to explore a paradigm where models can drive their own intellectual development, becoming active generators of training data rather than passive recipients, thus paving the way for truly autonomous AI systems.

In essence, the paper seeks to demonstrate that, given only a high-level topic (e.g., "algebra word problems"), a pre-trained LLM can iteratively improve its reasoning by generating its own questions and attempting to solve them, guided by a self-supervised reinforcement learning framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research is the **Self-Questioning Language Models (SQLM)** framework, an asymmetric self-play mechanism adapted for language model post-training. This methodology involves two distinct, yet interconnected, roles played by the language model: a proposer and a solver. Both are trained via reinforcement learning in an iterative loop.

#### 3.1. Asymmetric Self-Play Framework

Inspired by asymmetric self-play in robotics (OpenAI et al., 2021; Sukhbaatar et al., 2017), SQLM casts the LLM in two principal roles:
- **Proposer ($\pi_P$):** This policy is responsible for generating challenging but solvable questions (and sometimes associated verification data like unit tests). Its input is initially a single, high-level prompt specifying the topic (e.g., "Generate a question in arithmetic").
- **Solver ($\pi_S$):** This policy attempts to answer the questions generated by the proposer.

The interaction is a continuous loop: the proposer generates a question, the solver attempts to answer it, and both receive rewards based on the solver's performance. Both policies are typically initialized from the same pre-trained LLM and often share weights, learning and improving concurrently.

#### 3.2. Reward Functions – A Key Innovation

A central challenge in a fully self-supervised setting is verification without ground-truth answers. SQLM cleverly addresses this by tailoring reward functions based on the "generator-verifier gap" – the relative difficulty of generating a correct solution versus verifying one.

- **When the Generator-Verifier Gap is Small (e.g., Arithmetic, Algebra):**
 - **Verification Method:** The proposer *does not* generate a solution. Instead, the solver's outputs are verified using **majority voting**. For a given problem, the solver samples $N$ independent generations (answers). The most frequently occurring answer ($y_{maj}$) is treated as the proxy for the correct answer.
 - **Solver Reward ($R_S$):** An individual solver generation ($y_i$) receives a reward of 1 if it matches $y_{maj}$, and 0 otherwise. This reinforces self-consistency.
 - **Proposer Reward ($R_P$):** The proposer is rewarded for generating "reasonable" problems – those that are neither too easy nor too difficult. It receives a reward of 1 if the count of solutions matching $y_{maj}$ is greater than 0 but less than $N$ (i.e., not all solutions are the same, and not all are different). Otherwise, it receives 0. This encourages the proposer to generate problems that push the solver's capabilities, fostering a curriculum.

- **When the Generator-Verifier Gap is Large (e.g., Coding):**
 - **Verification Method:** For domains like coding, verifying a solution (e.g., using unit tests) is often easier than generating a correct solution. In this scenario, the proposer is prompted to generate the problem *along with a set of unit tests*.
 - **Solver Reward ($R_S$):** The solver's solution ($y_{pred}$) receives a reward equal to the fraction of unit tests it passes. This provides a granular reward signal.
 - **Proposer Reward ($R_P$):** The proposer is rewarded for generating problems where the solver passes *some* but *not all* unit tests (i.e., $0 < \text{Pass}(y_{pred}, \text{Tests}(x)) < 1$). This again encourages the proposer to generate non-trivial, yet solvable, problems that lie within the solver's learning zone.

#### 3.3. Training and Iterative Improvement

Both the proposer and solver are trained using reinforcement learning algorithms to maximize their respective expected rewards. Training starts with only a single, high-level prompt to the proposer (e.g., "Generate a three-digit arithmetic problem"), with no initial examples or labeled data. Through continuous interaction and iterative updates, both policies adapt: the proposer generates increasingly sophisticated problems, and the solver improves its ability to solve them.

#### 3.4. Encouraging Diversity

The methodology inherently encourages diversity through the online, adaptive nature of the proposer's reward. The paper demonstrates that generating problems online with proposer updates is more effective at maintaining diversity than attempting to pre-generate a diverse dataset from static prompts, which models may struggle to operationalize effectively.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

The framework is evaluated on three distinct benchmarks:
- **Arithmetic:** Three-digit multiplication problems.
- **Algebra:** Linear equations with up to two variables from the OMEGA benchmark.
- **Coding:** Programming problems similar to LeetCode easy challenges from Codeforces.

For all experiments, models like Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (and its Coder variant) and Llama models are used, showcasing the method's applicability across different base LLMs. Key hyperparameters like proposer update frequency are also explored to optimize the learning dynamics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates compelling evidence that Self-Questioning Language Models (SQLM) can significantly improve the reasoning capabilities of pre-trained LLMs without access to any human-curated training datasets.

1. **Significant Performance Gains:**
 - **Arithmetic:** The Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct model, when trained with self-play, improved its accuracy on three-digit multiplication problems from a baseline of 0.791 to **0.948**, representing a substantial 14% gain.
 - **Algebra:** For algebra problems involving linear equations, the same model saw its accuracy rise from 0.440 to **0.600**, a 16% improvement.
 - **Coding:** The Qwen2.5-Coder-3B-Instruct model achieved an accuracy increase on Codeforces problems from 0.320 to **0.391**, a 7% gain.
 These results underscore the framework's effectiveness across diverse reasoning tasks.

2. **Superiority over Format Reward Baseline:**
 To confirm that the gains were indeed in reasoning ability rather than merely learning to output in the correct format, the self-play approach was compared against a "format reward" baseline. SQLM consistently and significantly outperformed this baseline (e.g., 0.948 vs. 0.826 for Arithmetic, 0.600 vs. 0.553 for Algebra), indicating that the models genuinely improved their problem-solving skills.

3. **Qualitative Evidence of Curriculum Learning:**
 Qualitative samples from the arithmetic task clearly illustrate an emergent curriculum. Initially, the proposer generates simple problems (e.g., `563 + 247 - 189`). As training progresses, the model learns to generate increasingly complex problems involving multiple operations, parentheses, and exponents (e.g., `384 ÷ (52 × 2) + 7^3 - 111`). This demonstrates the proposer's ability to adapt and generate challenges that match the solver's evolving capabilities, driving continuous improvement. Similar qualitative improvements were observed in the coding task, progressing from trivial tasks like squaring elements to finding the longest contiguous subarray with unique elements.

4. **Optimal Proposer Update Frequency:**
 The research explored the impact of proposer update frequency on performance. It was found that updating the proposer every 5 steps offered a good balance, yielding the best performance and lower variance across runs compared to less frequent (e.g., every 10 steps or never) or more frequent updates (e.g., every 1 step). This highlights the importance of the adaptive problem generation mechanism.

5. **Enhanced Data Diversity via Online Generation:**
 A crucial finding related to data diversity was that generating problems online with continuous proposer updates led to substantially greater diversity in the generated questions compared to pre-generating a large dataset. The adaptive reward signal based on solver performance proved more effective at encouraging varied and challenging problems than abstract prompting instructions like "generate diverse" or "generate difficult." This was visualized and confirmed using PCA plots of the generated questions.

6. **Generality Across Models:**
 The framework's effectiveness was also demonstrated using different base LLMs, including Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct on the Codeforces benchmark, showing consistent improvements. This suggests the approach is not specific to a particular model architecture but is a general mechanism for self-improvement.

In summary, the main findings strongly support the hypothesis that large language models can autonomously improve their reasoning abilities by actively generating and solving their own problems, fostering a self-sustaining learning loop.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Self-Questioning Language Models" paper introduces a highly significant shift in the paradigm of language model refinement, with profound potential impacts on the future of AI development.

1. **Towards True AI Autonomy:** The most significant impact is the move towards truly autonomous AI systems. By demonstrating that LLMs can generate their own questions and answers, the paper liberates them from the constant need for human intervention in curriculum design and data labeling. This paves the way for AI agents that can drive their own intellectual growth and continuous improvement in diverse domains.

2. **Reduced Data Dependency and Cost:** This research drastically reduces the reliance on costly, labor-intensive, and time-consuming human-curated datasets for LLM post-training. This not only lowers the barrier to entry for developing specialized LLMs but also accelerates the development cycle, as models can be refined without waiting for extensive human annotation efforts.

3. **Scalability and Adaptability:** The self-supervised nature of SQLM makes it inherently scalable. As LLMs become larger and more powerful, their ability to generate complex and diverse problems will also increase, creating a virtuous cycle of improvement. This adaptive curriculum generation, where problems evolve with the solver's capabilities, ensures that the learning process remains efficient and targeted.

4. **Emergent Curriculum Learning:** The framework naturally fosters an emergent curriculum, moving from simpler to more complex problems as the solver improves. This is a highly efficient way of learning, mirroring how humans often learn by tackling progressively harder challenges, and it is generated without any explicit human design.

5. **Broad Applicability:** Demonstrated across arithmetic, algebra, and coding, the SQLM framework shows versatility. The clever adaptation of reward functions based on the generator-verifier gap (majority voting for ill-defined answers vs. unit tests for verifiable ones) suggests it could be extended to many other domains.

6. **New Frontiers in Research:**
 - **Meta-learning for Prompt Design:** The paper acknowledges that prompt tuning for the proposer is still a form of hand-engineering. Future work could focus on meta-learning to automate this, allowing the system to autonomously evolve its own prompting strategies.
 - **Safety and Relevance Guards:** The lack of safeguards for model-generated questions (ensuring they are reasonable, safe, or relevant) is a limitation. Integrating LLM-based filtering or scoring mechanisms could address this, making the approach safer and more robust for real-world applications.
 - **Addressing Reinforcement of Systematic Errors:** While robust, self-consistency (majority voting) doesn't guarantee absolute correctness. If the model consistently converges on an incorrect solution, there's no external mechanism for correction. Future research could explore semi-supervised settings, incorporating small amounts of external labeled data, or diversified self-correction mechanisms to mitigate this risk.

In conclusion, "Self-Questioning Language Models" is a foundational step towards building AI systems that are not just intelligent, but also autonomously inquisitive and self-improving. By reframing LLMs as active agents in their own training loops, this work opens up exciting possibilities for more adaptable, efficient, and ultimately, more autonomous artificial intelligence.
