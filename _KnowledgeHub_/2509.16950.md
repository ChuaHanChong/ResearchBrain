# Temporal Logic-Based Multi-Vehicle Backdoor Attacks against Offline RL Agents in End-to-end Autonomous Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> A novel backdoor attack targets end-to-end autonomous driving systems by exploiting coordinated trajectories of multiple surrounding vehicles as triggers, moving beyond pixel-level manipulations. Researchers from Purdue University utilized temporal logic to automate the generation of these sophisticated multi-vehicle triggers and incorporated a negative training strategy to achieve precise and stealthy activation of unsafe target actions in poisoned offline reinforcement learning agents.

## Problem

- Existing backdoor attacks in autonomous driving primarily rely on pixel-level visual triggers, which face practical challenges in real-world deployment due to sensitivity to environmental factors and physical alteration requirements.
- Manually specifying and coordinating complex multi-vehicle spatiotemporal trajectories for attack triggers is impractical and time-consuming.
- Basic data poisoning methods for backdoor attacks often lead to false positives, activating the target action in non-triggering but similar scenarios, reducing stealthiness.

## Method

- Developed a framework to automatically generate realistic multi-vehicle ==trigger trajectories== using rule-based behavior models and temporal logic (TL) specifications to define and refine vehicle coordination.
- Integrated a negative training strategy during data poisoning, incorporating "patch trajectories" (similar non-triggering scenarios) to enhance attack precision and stealthiness by preventing false positives.
- Targeted end-to-end autonomous driving systems trained with offline reinforcement learning by injecting generated trigger trajectories with manipulated rewards and target actions into the training dataset.

## Results

- The attack successfully induced dangerous target actions in poisoned agents, leading to significantly lower cumulative rewards (from >200 to <60), high average displacement error (from <1.0 to >50), and near **100%** mission violation rate across various offline RL algorithms.
- Multi-vehicle trigger patterns (e.g., sync-bypass, overtake, brake-overtake) were deployed, with the `sync-bypass` pattern demonstrating superior attack performance and all designed patterns occurring at very low frequencies in real-world data (e.g., **0.130%** for `sync-bypass`), validating their stealthy design.
- Existing defense mechanisms, including trajectory smoothing and Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent (DP-SGD), proved largely ineffective against this type of trajectory-based backdoor attack.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Temporal logic provides an effective formal method for automatically designing and evaluating complex, coordinated multi-vehicle behaviors, overcoming the limitations of manual trigger specification.
> - A negative training strategy is essential for increasing the precision and stealth of backdoor attacks, ensuring the target behavior is activated only under exact trigger conditions.
> - Multi-vehicle trajectory-based triggers offer a more practical and adaptable alternative to pixel-level triggers for backdoor attacks in real-world autonomous driving scenarios, as they exploit contextual awareness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of autonomous driving (AD) systems necessitates a thorough understanding of their safety and security vulnerabilities. End-to-end AD systems, which directly map sensor inputs to control commands, have shown promising performance but are also subject to various security threats, including backdoor attacks.

Existing research on backdoor attacks in AD primarily focuses on pixel-level triggers. These triggers often involve physically embedding fixed patches onto camera-captured frames or objects in the environment. While some work explores physically realizable pixel-level patterns, these approaches face practical limitations. Attackers generally cannot directly modify a vehicle's internal camera input, requiring physical environmental alterations that are constrained by feasibility. Furthermore, visual triggers are often sensitive to viewpoint, distance, and lighting, which can compromise their effectiveness in dynamic real-world driving conditions.

Another area of related work concerns the application of temporal logic (TL) in AD security. TL is utilized as a formal method to define and verify safety and security properties, mainly for generating complex adversarial scenarios or finding specification-violating test cases in AD systems. These approaches often focus on challenging pre-trained models at inference time or describing the ego car's adversarial robustness.

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) is increasingly applied in AD for decision-making. However, DRL systems, including offline RL, have demonstrated susceptibility to various security threats, such as data poisoning. Previous studies have shown offline RL vulnerabilities to data poisoning with static patch triggers in AD tasks.

This work addresses a gap in the existing literature by introducing a backdoor attack that leverages the trajectories of one or more attacker-controlled vehicles as triggers. This approach departs from pixel-level manipulations, offering a potentially more practical and adaptable method for deploying triggers in physical environments. By targeting end-to-end AD systems that utilize offline reinforcement learning, the research explores a previously under-explored vulnerability. The use of temporal logic in this context is novel, as it is applied to evaluate and refine the behaviors of multiple surrounding vehicles acting as triggers, rather than primarily describing the ego car's properties or generating general adversarial scenarios. This work also incorporates a negative training strategy to enhance the stealthiness and precision of the attack, mitigating false positives that often arise from simpler poisoning methods.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and demonstrate a novel, practical backdoor attack against end-to-end autonomous driving (AD) systems. This attack uses the coordinated trajectories of multiple surrounding vehicles as triggers, in contrast to conventional pixel-level triggers.

The motivation for this work is multi-faceted:

- **Addressing Limitations of Pixel-Level Triggers:** Existing backdoor attacks in AD predominantly rely on pixel-level visual triggers (e.g., fixed patches on images). These triggers face significant practical challenges for real-world deployment. They often require direct modification of the ego vehicle's sensor inputs or precise physical alterations in the environment, which may be difficult for an attacker to achieve. Additionally, these visual perturbations can be highly sensitive to environmental factors such as viewpoint, distance, and lighting, leading to inconsistency and reduced effectiveness in dynamic driving scenarios. The researchers aim to overcome these limitations by proposing triggers based on vehicle trajectories, which are more readily detectable by vehicle sensors (vision, LiDAR) and adaptable to real-world conditions.
- **Enhancing Realism and Adaptability of Triggers:** By defining triggers as the precise spatiotemporal trajectories of one or more attacker-controlled vehicles, the proposed method seeks to create triggers that are more physically realizable and adaptable in real-world driving environments. The goal is to induce unsafe behaviors in a poisoned ego car when specific, observable multi-vehicle interactions occur.
- **Automating Complex Trigger Generation:** Manually specifying and coordinating the precise spatiotemporal trajectories for multiple attacking vehicles is a time-consuming and unrealistic task. To address this, a key objective is to develop an automated framework for generating these sophisticated trigger trajectories. This framework is based on temporal logic (TL) specifications, which allow for formal definition and iterative refinement of desired vehicle behaviors, ensuring both realism and adherence to attack intentions.
- **Improving Attack Stealthiness and Precision:** A basic data poisoning approach, where trigger trajectories are simply introduced into the training data, can lead to false positives. That is, the poisoned AD agent might activate the dangerous target action in scenarios that are similar to the trigger but do not represent the exact intended trigger pattern. To mitigate this, a crucial objective is to develop a negative training strategy. This strategy involves incorporating "patch trajectories"—scenarios similar to triggers but designated not to activate the backdoor—into the training process. This refinement aims to ensure that the backdoor is activated only under the exact trigger conditions, thereby enhancing the attack's stealthiness and reducing the likelihood of accidental detection.
- **Exploring Under-Explored Vulnerabilities:** The research highlights that complex multi-vehicle trajectories have been largely overlooked in the context of backdoor attacks against end-to-end AD systems. By demonstrating the feasibility of such trajectory-based attacks, the study aims to uncover and characterize a critical, yet previously under-explored, vulnerability in AD systems. This understanding is intended to inform the development of more robust defense mechanisms and security assessments for future AD deployments.

In summary, the work is motivated by the need for more practical, adaptable, and stealthy backdoor attacks against AD systems, moving beyond pixel-level triggers to exploit contextual awareness through coordinated vehicle trajectories, and using formal methods and refined training strategies to achieve this.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this research addresses the challenge of creating practical, stealthy, and effective trajectory-based backdoor attacks against offline reinforcement learning (RL) agents in end-to-end autonomous driving (AD) systems. The approach is structured into two main phases: temporal logic-based trigger trajectory generation and trigger insertion with training optimization.

#### 3.1. Preliminary: Problem Formulation and Threat Model

- **Problem Formulation**: The AD task is framed as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) where the RL-based driving policy directly processes raw sensor data (represented as a state vector including ego vehicle information and LiDAR-based environmental data) and outputs low-level control commands (steering and throttle). The goal is to find a policy that maximizes discounted total reward based on a static dataset.
- **Threat Model**: The attacker operates under the assumption of having access to the offline training dataset of the driving agent, simulating a scenario where AD developers might use potentially malicious third-party data annotation services. The attacker can inject specific trigger patterns into training trajectories and manipulate associated rewards. The adversary is assumed to lack knowledge of the victim's training algorithm, MDP, or direct control over the training process. During the attack, the adversary controls surrounding vehicles to follow specific trigger trajectories, aiming to induce dangerous target behaviors in the poisoned RL agent. A key constraint is that attacker vehicles should remain safe and inconspicuous to maintain realism. The attack targets the end-to-end AD system, directly causing the ego car to perform unsafe maneuvers, rather than just mispredicting trajectories.

#### 3.2. Overview of the Attack Phases

The attack consists of two primary phases:

- **Phase I: Temporal Logic-Based Trigger Trajectory Generation**: This phase focuses on automatically generating precise and realistic multi-vehicle trigger trajectories.
 - **Behavior Model-Driven Trajectory Generation**: Instead of analytically solving trajectories, the researchers deploy multiple attacker vehicles, each equipped with configurable, rule-based behavior models (e.g., lane following, overtaking, sudden braking). These models define control schemes that ensure vehicles act predictably and naturally, given initial configurations (position, speed) and environmental states. This approach allows for flexible combinations of driving behaviors without rigid analytical solutions.
 - **Temporal Logic (TL)-Based Trajectory Evaluation**: To ensure generated trajectories meet the attacker's intent and realistic constraints, temporal logic specifications are defined. These specifications use predicates like `Reach(R, [ts, te])` (vehicle enters region R within time window), `Avoid(R, [ts, te])` (vehicle avoids region R), and `Stay(R, [ts, te])` (vehicle remains in region R). These can be combined with logical and temporal operators for complex coordination (e.g., `Vn i=1 ϕi` for synchronous behavior). A quantitative score function, `E(τi, ϕi)`, evaluates how closely a generated trajectory `τi` satisfies its TL specification `ϕi`. If the score is non-positive, indicating non-satisfaction, the behavior model configurations (initial positions, speeds) are randomly perturbed, and the process is iteratively refined until a positive score is achieved, yielding a qualified trigger trajectory.

- **Phase II: Trigger Insertion & Training Optimization**: This phase involves incorporating the generated trigger trajectories into the training data and optimizing the training process for stealthiness.
 - **Trigger Insertion**: Qualified trigger trajectories, along with corresponding ego car states, are added to the training dataset. For these poisoned samples, the ego car's original action `at` is replaced with the attacker's `target action (a't)`, and the reward `rt` is manipulated to `r't` (e.g., set to half of the maximum final reward) to reinforce the trigger-action association without overfitting.
 - **Negative Training Strategy**: To prevent false positives (the agent activating the target action in similar but non-trigger scenarios), a negative training strategy is introduced. "Patch trajectories," which are similar to triggers but explicitly designated not to activate the backdoor (e.g., those with a TL evaluation score `E(τ, ϕ) ≤ λ`), are collected. These patch trajectories are also added to the training set, but with the ego car's actions remaining normal (correct behavior). This strategy aims to refine the agent's responses, ensuring the backdoor only activates under precise trigger conditions, thereby enhancing stealthiness.

#### 3.3. Technical Details

- **Behavior Models**: The core behavior model is rule-based, managing speed and longitudinal safety distances. It is customized into variants like "overtaking" (incorporating rules for safe lane changes and overtaking) and "braking" (adding rules for sudden braking when a vehicle is at a certain distance). These customizations enable complex multi-vehicle interactions needed for triggers.
- **Temporal Logic Specification**: The TL framework uses core predicates (`Reach`, `Avoid`, `Stay`) on atomic propositions `μireach(t, R)` (vehicle `i` is in region `R` at time `t`). Tools like STLpy and DiffSpec are employed to compute quantitative satisfaction scores `E(τ, ϕ)`, which guide the iterative perturbation and refinement of behavior models. The algorithm iteratively generates configurations, simulates trajectories, evaluates them against TL specifications, and perturbs configurations if specifications are not met, collecting qualified trigger and patch trajectories.

This comprehensive methodology allows for the generation of sophisticated, realistic, and stealthy multi-vehicle trajectory-based backdoor attacks against end-to-end AD systems, leveraging formal logic and targeted data poisoning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the proposed temporal logic-based multi-vehicle backdoor attack was conducted through extensive experiments on MetaDrive, a self-driving simulator, targeting five offline reinforcement learning (RL) agents.

#### 4.1. Experiment Setup

Experiments were performed across three task difficulties (easy, medium, hard) in MetaDrive. The RL agent’s state included ego vehicle dynamics and LiDAR-based environmental information (259-dimension vector), with actions being steering and throttle. Reward functions were compositional, encouraging progress and penalizing crashes. The poisoning rate for trigger trajectories was consistently set at 10%, with an equal number of patch trajectories. Three trigger patterns were designed:
1. **Sync-bypass**: Two cars simultaneously bypass the ego car.
2. **Overtake**: One car bypasses from one side while another overtakes.
3. **Brake-overtake**: One car suddenly brakes in front of the ego car on the left, while another car overtakes.
Two target actions for the ego car were defined: sudden left turn and sudden braking, activated when the ego car's distance to another vehicle exceeds 10 meters. Metrics used were cumulative reward, average displacement error (ADE) compared to a clean agent, and mission violation rate (MVR).

#### 4.2. Attack Effectiveness

- **Different Trigger Patterns**: The attack was effective across all three trigger patterns and task difficulties. Backdoored agents, when exposed to triggers, consistently exhibited low cumulative rewards, high ADE (indicating significant deviation from normal trajectories), and high MVR (frequently resulting in mission failure/crashes). The `sync-bypass` trigger generally demonstrated superior attack performance, while more complex triggers like `brake-overtake` were comparatively harder to inject, leading to slightly lower effectiveness under the same poisoning rate. The benign performance (without triggers) of backdoored agents sometimes showed a higher MVR in medium and hard tasks, suggesting that the backdoor could subtly disrupt normal behavior in complex environments.
- **Different RL Algorithms**: The attack successfully compromised agents trained with various offline RL algorithms, including BC (behavior cloning), BCQ (policy constraint), and Coptidice (value regularization). For poisoned agents, rewards dropped significantly (from >200 to <60), ADE surged (from <1.0 to >50), and MVR approached 100%. The BC algorithm, which inherently performed poorer, was less susceptible to the attack. The attack also showed effectiveness against safe RL algorithms like BC-safe and CDT, though CDT, with its stochastic policy, exhibited some resilience while trading off normal performance.
- **Different Target Actions**: Both "sudden left turn" and "sudden braking" target actions were effectively induced. The "sudden left turn" generally resulted in a higher MVR and ADE, attributed to its greater complexity in execution (simultaneous steering and throttle adjustments) compared to sudden braking. This indicated the attack's flexibility to align with diverse attacker intentions.

#### 4.3. Defense and Mitigation

The study evaluated two defense mechanisms:
- **Trajectory Smoothing**: This data-level pre-processing technique showed limited effectiveness. It offered some mitigation for "sudden braking" target actions but was considerably weaker against the more complex "turning left" action.
- **DP-SGD (Differentially Private Stochastic Gradient Descent)**: This training algorithm-level defense proved largely ineffective, with poisoned MVR remaining near 1.0. The ineffectiveness was attributed to the nature of the data poisoning, which does not introduce significant abnormal gradients that DP-SGD is designed to detect and mitigate.
These findings suggest that existing defenses are insufficient against the proposed trajectory-based backdoor attacks.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

- **Poisoning Rate**: Increasing the poisoning rate (from 10% to 40%) enhanced the attack's effectiveness (higher poisoned MVR) but also reduced the benign reward, making the attack more detectable. This highlights a trade-off between attack strength and stealthiness.
- **Negative Training**: The negative training strategy was crucial for attack stealthiness and precision. Without it, the agent frequently activated the target action in non-triggering, but similar, scenarios (high F-MVR), indicating false positives. Negative training ensured that the backdoor activated only under exact trigger conditions without degrading the agent's benign performance.
- **Number of Attack Vehicles (AVs)**: The benign reward remained stable irrespective of the number of AVs (1 to 4), implying stealthiness in normal operation. However, a higher number of AVs led to a decrease in poisoned MVR for the same poisoning rate, indicating that more complex multi-AV triggers required a higher poisoning rate for consistent effectiveness.
- **Dynamics Model**: Varying the attacker vehicle's dynamics model (small, medium, large vehicles) did not significantly affect the attack's success, demonstrating the robustness of the methodology to changes in vehicle physical characteristics.
- **Threshold of TL Specification (λ)**: The threshold `λ` for patch trajectory generation influenced effectiveness. A lower `λ` (fewer patch trajectories) generally resulted in a higher poisoned MVR, suggesting that including too many patch trajectories (with higher similarity to triggers) could confuse the model and reduce overall attack effectiveness.
- **Stealthiness of Trigger Trajectories (NGSIM data)**: Analysis using the NGSIM dataset showed that the designed multi-vehicle trigger patterns (sync-bypass, overtake, brake-overtake) occur at very low frequencies in real-world driving data (e.g., 0.130% for sync-bypass), validating their stealthy design.
- **Weather Conditions**: The attack remained effective under simulated fog and rain conditions, although the overall performance of the clean and backdoored agents degraded due to environmental noise.
- **Single-Vehicle Triggers**: Single-vehicle triggers were found to be less stealthy and effective compared to multi-vehicle triggers, as they are more prone to accidental activation by common driving behaviors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a significant advancement in understanding the security vulnerabilities of autonomous driving (AD) systems, particularly those relying on end-to-end deep reinforcement learning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Identification of a Novel and Practical Vulnerability**: The work is the first to demonstrate the feasibility and effectiveness of trajectory-based backdoor attacks against end-to-end AD systems. By leveraging the coordinated movements of multiple attacker vehicles as triggers, it uncovers a critical, yet previously under-explored, attack vector. This shifts the focus from pixel-level or direct sensor input manipulation to exploiting the AD system's contextual awareness and interpretation of dynamic environmental interactions.
- **Enhanced Realism and Deployability of Triggers**: Unlike static, pixel-level triggers that face challenges in real-world physical deployment (e.g., sensitivity to viewpoint, physical modification requirements), the proposed trajectory-based triggers are designed to be more practical and adaptable. They are generated using realistic behavior models and are observable by standard AD sensors (e.g., LiDAR), increasing their potential for physical realizability.
- **Formal and Automated Trigger Generation**: The integration of temporal logic (TL) specifications provides a formal and automated framework for generating complex multi-vehicle trigger trajectories. This capability addresses the impracticality of manually designing precise spatiotemporal coordinations, enabling the creation of sophisticated and consistent attack patterns while adhering to realistic vehicle dynamics.
- **Improved Attack Stealthiness and Precision**: The development of a negative training strategy is a crucial contribution to the sophistication of backdoor attacks. By explicitly training the AD agent on "patch trajectories" (similar non-triggering scenarios), the attack's precision is significantly enhanced, reducing false positives and ensuring the backdoor activates only under the exact intended trigger conditions. This stealthiness makes such attacks harder to detect and mitigate through simple anomaly detection methods.
- **Contribution to AD System Safety and Security**: By exposing this new class of vulnerabilities, the research provides vital insights for AD developers, researchers, and policymakers. It underscores the necessity of proactive security assessments during the design and deployment phases of AI-driven systems. Understanding these vulnerabilities is a critical step towards developing more robust defense mechanisms, such as advanced detection algorithms capable of identifying subtle multi-vehicle trajectory patterns or training paradigms that are more resilient to sophisticated data poisoning attacks. Ultimately, this work contributes to strengthening the overall safety, security, and trustworthiness of AD technologies, which is essential for their widespread and safe deployment in public environments.
- **Ethical Considerations**: The paper explicitly addresses the ethical implications of exposing vulnerabilities in safety-critical systems. The authors emphasize that the research is intended solely for defensive purposes—to inform and improve the resilience of AD systems. The experiments are strictly confined to simulated environments, and the findings are shared with the explicit aim of guiding the development of robust countermeasures, aligning with responsible research practices.

In conclusion, this study offers a compelling demonstration of a practical and stealthy backdoor attack against end-to-end AD, revealing a significant blind spot in current security assessments. Its impact extends to guiding future research in AD security, promoting the development of more resilient systems against advanced adversarial threats.
