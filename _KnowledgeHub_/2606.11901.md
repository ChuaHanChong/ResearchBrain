# DuoBench: A Reproducible Benchmark for Bimanual Manipulation in Simulation and the Real World

> [!summary] Summary
> DuoBench establishes a reproducible framework for evaluating bimanual robotic manipulation, offering eleven tasks in simulation and four in the real world for the FR3 Duo platform. Its stage-based evaluation reveals that current policies struggle with fundamental interaction capabilities and parallel execution, even while showing varied strengths across coordination types.

## Problem

- The existing landscape lacked systematic, reproducible benchmarks for diverse bimanual manipulation tasks, often focusing on single-arm scenarios or limited coordination patterns.
- Binary success metrics provided coarse feedback for complex bimanual tasks, failing to offer fine-grained diagnostic insights into specific failure modes.
- Reproducibility of robotic tasks, especially in real-world bimanual settings, remained a significant challenge, hindering consistent evaluation and data collection across research groups.

## Method

- **DuoBench** introduces an extensible benchmarking framework featuring eleven ==bimanual tasks== in simulation and four replicated in the real world for the FR3 Duo platform.
- A bimanual task taxonomy categorizes tasks into four distinct coordination types: asymmetric support, bimanual manipulation, sequential handoff, and parallel execution.
- It implements a novel stage-based evaluation mechanism for fine-grained semantic failure analysis and provides human-teleoperated datasets along with reproducible real-world task recipes using 3D-printable assets.

## Results

- A dataset of 750 human-teleoperated episodes (442,907 frames) was provided, covering eleven simulation and four real-world bimanual tasks.
- Evaluated policies (ACT, π0.5, X-VLA) demonstrated limited success rates across tasks (e.g., Hinge-Chest **0%**, Bin-Sort 0-**2%**), with a significant fraction of failures occurring in the earliest stages, particularly initial grasp acquisition.
- Mixed simulation and real-world training confirmed a clear sim-to-real gap for all policies; while it could improve or maintain real-world performance for some models, it also degraded others, highlighting the complexity of cross-domain transfer.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current policies, including state-of-the-art imitation learning and VLA models, face considerable challenges in bimanual manipulation, frequently failing in early interaction stages like grasp acquisition.
> - The stage-based evaluation method provides valuable diagnostic feedback, effectively differentiating task difficulties and pinpointing specific bottlenecks beyond overall success rates.
> - Despite careful alignment of simulated and physical setups, a persistent sim-to-real gap exists, indicating continued challenges in cross-domain transfer for bimanual tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen substantial advancements, particularly with the integration of machine learning-based control methods. This progress has led to a demand for robust benchmarks and efficient data generation strategies to facilitate the comparison and development of new algorithms. Much of the existing benchmark development, however, has primarily focused on single-arm manipulation scenarios.

Recent efforts in single-arm manipulation benchmarks include environments like RLBench, Meta-World, and the ManiSkill series, which support various robots and tasks, often with procedurally generated or teleoperated datasets. LIBERO is another widely used benchmark, particularly for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, with extensions focusing on robustness and realism. Other lines of work address household scenarios or long-horizon tasks, and VLABench and VIMA specifically target VLA models with multimodal prompting and world knowledge requirements. Frameworks like RoboHive and RoboVerse aim to provide unified interfaces for diverse robot learning tasks.

In contrast, the landscape for bimanual manipulation benchmarks is less developed. While some existing benchmarks incorporate dual-arm tasks, they often present limitations. For example, RLBench2 features tasks for two Franka robots but largely focuses on tightly coupled motions. RoboTwin and BiCoord offer a larger number of tasks, but many are variations on object types or episode lengths rather than distinct coordination challenges, or they emphasize simultaneous operation without extensive exploration of diverse coordination patterns. BEHAVIOR provides household activities with logic-clause goal specifications, allowing for progress tracking. Bi-DexHands focuses on dexterous manipulation, and ST-BiBench investigates coordination by defining classes for parallel and collaborative manipulation. Benchmarks for humanoid robots also include bimanual tasks.

Real-world and hybrid benchmarks, which combine simulation with physical experimentation, are comparatively scarce due to the inherent effort required for reproducible hardware setups. Common strategies for reproducibility often involve 3D-printed objects, as seen in FMB and FurnitureBench. While FurnitureBench includes both simulation and real-world setups, it primarily focuses on single-robot scenarios. RoboMind provides a dataset with bimanual tasks but without the infrastructure for direct reproduction. RoboArena aims to address real-world reproducibility through crowdsourcing policy evaluations.

DuoBench addresses a specific gap in this landscape: the lack of a bimanual manipulation benchmark that offers a systematic selection of tasks, supports reproducible evaluation in both simulation and the real world, and provides fine-grained failure analysis. The paper highlights that existing dual-arm benchmarks often underrepresent the diversity of coordination patterns, offer limited support for reproducible sim-and-real evaluation, or rely predominantly on binary task success, thus failing to diagnose specific failure modes effectively. DuoBench positions itself as a systematic and reproducible testbed for studying dual-arm policy learning, particularly timely for the FR3 Duo platform, which is becoming more accessible.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce DuoBench, an extensible benchmarking framework specifically designed for evaluating bimanual manipulation policies on the FR3 Duo platform. The framework is intended to operate across both simulation and real-world environments.

The motivation for developing DuoBench stems from several identified limitations in the existing robotics research landscape:

1. **Increased Capabilities of Bimanual Systems:** Bimanual robot systems, such as dual-arm tabletop setups and humanoid robots, offer substantially expanded manipulation capabilities compared to single-arm systems. However, effectively coordinating two arms introduces significant control complexity and distinct failure modes that are not adequately captured by current benchmarks.
2. **Gap in Bimanual Benchmarking:** While considerable progress has been made in single-arm manipulation benchmarks, a systematic framework for evaluating the diverse coordination patterns required by two-arm manipulation has been lacking. Existing dual-arm benchmarks often focus on specific types of interaction or lack the systematic task diversity needed for comprehensive evaluation.
3. **Limitations of Binary Success Metrics:** For long-horizon bimanual tasks, simple binary success rates provide coarse feedback. They fail to identify *which* specific coordination phase or subtask leads to a policy's failure, thereby limiting diagnostic insight and targeted improvement strategies. There is a need for finer-grained evaluation metrics.
4. **Challenge of Real-World Reproducibility:** Reproducibility of robotic tasks, especially in real-world settings, remains a significant challenge. Many benchmarks either rely solely on simulation or provide real-world setups that are difficult for other research groups to replicate and collect comparable data. The authors emphasize the importance of making tasks reproducible across both simulation and physical domains.
5. **Relevance of the FR3 Duo Platform:** The FR3 Duo platform, which combines two standard FR3 arms in a human-inspired configuration, is anticipated to become increasingly accessible to research laboratories. A dedicated benchmark for this platform is therefore timely and relevant for fostering advancements in bimanual Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model manipulation.

To address these motivations, DuoBench aims to contribute the following:

- **A Reproducible Benchmarking Framework:** Providing eleven bimanual manipulation tasks implemented in simulation, with four of these tasks also partially reproduced in the real world.
- **A Bimanual Task Taxonomy:** Organizing bimanual manipulation tasks into four distinct coordination categories to systematically evaluate different types of dual-arm interaction.
- **Stage-Based Evaluation:** Introducing a mechanism for fine-grained semantic failure analysis that goes beyond binary success, enabling researchers to pinpoint specific difficulties.
- **Human-Teleoperated Datasets and Task Recipes:** Offering datasets collected from human teleoperation and providing reproducible real-world task recipes, including 3D-printable assets, to facilitate data collection and model comparison across different laboratories and physical setups.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DuoBench is constructed upon the Robot Control Stack (RCS) ecosystem, defining all benchmark environments as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). An agent, either a human teleoperator or a learned policy, interacts with these environments. Data collection involves a wrapper that records 16-dimensional actions (seven joint and one gripper dimension for each arm) and delayed observations, including task instructions, proprioception, and frames from three cameras (a main stereo camera and two wrist cameras). The collected datasets support imitation learning based on a behavior-cloning objective.

A central methodological contribution is the introduction of **task stages**. These represent a subtask decomposition, serving as milestones that provide fine-grained insight into policy performance beyond binary success rates. A task begins at stage zero, and the stage can only increment. Each stage is associated with a set of constraints that must be satisfied for progression. This allows for the calculation of metrics such as normalized mean progress per timestep, normalized average final stage, and the probability of policy failure at a specific stage, which can be visualized to identify dominant failure points.

The hardware platform is the **Franka Duo setup**, consisting of two FR3 robotic arms mounted in an arrangement where their ISO cubes overlap, enhancing dual-arm manipulability. Each arm is equipped with a two-finger Robotiq 2F-85 gripper. The sensing setup includes a Zed Mini stereo camera positioned centrally and two RealSense D405 wrist cameras. All mounting components are available from the manufacturer, and specifications for custom fabrication are provided. This setup is precisely replicated in a **MuJoCo simulation** using available assets.

The benchmark tasks are organized according to a **bimanual task taxonomy**, which categorizes tasks based on the functional roles of the robot arms. This taxonomy, inspired by Krebs and Asfour, defines four non-hierarchical categories:

1. **Asymmetric Support:** One arm stabilizes an object while the other indirectly manipulates it (e.g., holding a lid open to insert a box). These tasks assess a policy's understanding and leveraging of two arms for physical reasoning. Examples include Hinge-Chest and Spring-Door.
2. **Bimanual Manipulation:** Both arms jointly manipulate the same object, forming a closed kinematic chain (e.g., lifting a heavy pot). These tasks evaluate coordination under direct mutual physical interaction. Examples include Carry-Pot, Ball-Maze, Block-Balance, and Join-Blocks.
3. **Sequential Handoff:** An object is transferred between the arms due to workspace or task constraints (e.g., passing a cube). This category evaluates temporal coordination and handover management. Examples include Transfer-Cube, Transfer-Gate, and Transfer-Reorient.
4. **Parallel Execution:** Both arms perform independent unimanual tasks, either simultaneously or sequentially, without direct interdependence (e.g., sorting objects into bins). This category assesses independent control, arm usage balance, and the avoidance of unintended dominance patterns. An example is Bin-Sort.

DuoBench comprises eleven tasks across these four categories, each incorporating some form of randomization (e.g., initial object positions). Objects for real-world tasks are largely 3D-printable, with common household items used for unprintable components. Four tasks—Hinge-Chest, Ball-Maze, Transfer-Cube, and Bin-Sort—are reproduced on the real-world FR3 Duo lab setup.

**Task composition, data collection, and evaluation** are facilitated by a generic task interface within the simulation benchmark. This interface includes a task composer for scene instantiation via MuJoCo's MJSpec, a reset wrapper for randomized initialization, and a transparent stage wrapper for implementing the task-stage formalism. Each task is integrated with Gymnasium. For real-world deployment, 3D-printed assets enable exact reproduction of simulated tasks, although stage progress is tracked manually. The design prioritizes reproducibility to allow other labs to collect comparable demonstrations.

Human-teleoperated data is collected in both simulation and real-world environments using a Virtual Reality (VR) headset and the IRIS application. A data recording wrapper captures observations and Cartesian actions. In simulation, the full simulation state is also recorded, enabling an episode replayer to repeat recordings with new visual features (e.g., object colors, lighting, backgrounds) for data augmentation and ablation studies (Appendix B). Policy evaluation utilizes the VLAgents library, which records trials and progress data using seeded initialization for consistent conditions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DuoBench involved benchmarking three representative dual-arm control policies: ACT (a task-specific imitation-learning baseline), π
0.5, and X-VLA (two generalist VLA models). Policies were evaluated with 30 action steps between model prompts, corresponding to one second of open-loop execution.

#### 4.1. Datasets

The benchmark provides a dataset consisting of 750 human-teleoperated episodes. This includes 50 demonstrations for each of the eleven simulation tasks and 50 demonstrations for each of the four real-world tasks, totaling 442,907 frames. The average episode length is approximately 350 frames, or 12 seconds, recorded at 30 Hz. Each observation captures a language instruction, images from three cameras (1280 × 720 resolution), joint states, Cartesian poses, and the full FR3 robot states (or partial MuJoCo object states in simulation). Actions are collected as Cartesian commands via VR teleoperation. Data is stored in RCS parquet format and converted to LeRobot format for training, with images resized to 224 × 224 and actions mapped to joint space.

#### 4.2. Evaluation in Simulation

Policies were trained on the teleoperated simulation data. ACT was trained individually per task (50 episodes each), while the VLAs were trained on the combined 550 simulation episodes with task instruction conditioning. Each model was evaluated over 100 rollouts per task, with object positions sampled from a consistent seed.

- **Policy Performance:** The results (Table 2, Figure 3) indicate that current policies remain challenged by bimanual manipulation.
- **Early Stage Failures:** The stage-based analysis revealed that a significant fraction of failures occurred in the earliest stages of tasks, primarily related to initial grasp acquisition and task setup. This suggests that even before complex coordination is required, fundamental interaction capabilities are bottlenecks.
- **Task Difficulty Differentiation:** DuoBench effectively distinguished between tasks that might appear similar at a high level. For example, Hinge-Chest was consistently more difficult than Spring-Door (0% success rate for Hinge-Chest vs. up to 37% for Spring-Door), implying that maintaining an open lid is more challenging than pulling a spring-loaded door. Similarly, Transfer-Gate was found to be easier than Transfer-Cube, despite additional scene structure, indicating that spatial constraints can simplify coordination during handovers.
- **Parallel Execution Weakness:** Performance on the Bin-Sort task was notably low (0-2% success), suggesting that policies struggle with parallel execution of both arms and with learning from demonstrations that allow multiple valid solution paths (e.g., different grasp orders).
- **Policy Strengths Across Tasks:** ACT demonstrated the strongest performance on several tasks, including Spring-Door, Transfer-Gate, Carry-Pot, and Ball-Maze. However, the stage-based analysis showed that the relative strengths of the evaluated policies varied across coordination types and task stages, rather than following a uniform ranking.

#### 4.3. Evaluation in Real-World

For real-world evaluation, policies were trained on data collected from the four replicated real-world tasks. ACT was trained per task, and VLAs were trained on all 200 real-world episodes. Each policy was evaluated over 15 rollouts, with intermediate stages and success judged by a human operator.

- **Consistency with Simulation:** Real-world results largely corroborated simulation findings, with failures predominantly occurring in the earliest interaction phases, particularly grasp acquisition (Table 3, Figure 5).
- **Later Stage Reliability:** When grasping succeeded, later stages often completed reliably. For instance, in Transfer-Cube, the handover frequently succeeded once the object was securely grasped. In Bin-Sort, the object placement stage was typically not the limiting factor.
- **Physics Interaction:** In Ball-Maze, policies could reproduce the required dual-arm contact pattern but often failed to leverage the underlying task physics, such as appropriately tilting the maze to guide the ball toward the goal.
- **Overall Confirmation:** The real-world evaluation reinforced the broad conclusions drawn from simulation, highlighting that physically grounded interaction remains a significant challenge even in environments closely matching the training setup.

#### 4.4. Mixed Training and Sim-to-Real Gap

Policies were also trained on a combined dataset of simulation and real-world data (750 episodes total). ACT was trained per task on this mixed data for the four real-world tasks, while VLAs were trained on the full mixed dataset.

- **Persistent Sim-to-Real Gap:** The mixed-training results confirmed a clear sim-to-real gap across all policies (Table 3), indicating that simply aligning simulated and physical benchmark settings does not eliminate cross-domain transfer challenges.
- **Benefits for Specific Models:** Joint training on mixed data could improve or maintain real-world performance for some models, specifically ACT and π
 0.5, without requiring separate domain-specific training. However, these gains were not uniform across all tasks and models.
- **Degradation for Others:** Mixed training notably degraded the performance of X-VLA in simulation, suggesting that a naive combination of domains is not universally beneficial. A potential reason cited was the lack of distinct domain IDs for X-VLA cross-domain finetuning.
- **Benchmark Utility:** These results position DuoBench as a tool for evaluating policies within domains and for investigating how different training strategies impact simulation performance, real-world robustness, and cross-domain transfer capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DuoBench addresses a notable gap in the existing literature by providing a systematic and reproducible benchmarking framework for bimanual robotic manipulation. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Advancement in Bimanual Manipulation:** By focusing specifically on bimanual tasks and providing a diverse set of challenges, DuoBench directly contributes to the development and evaluation of policies for complex dual-arm robot systems. This is crucial as bimanual capabilities are increasingly relevant for advanced manipulation tasks in various domains, from industrial assembly to domestic assistance and humanoid robotics.
2. **Diagnostic Evaluation Capabilities:** The introduction of a novel stage-based evaluation mechanism is a key contribution. This method moves beyond traditional binary success metrics, offering fine-grained, semantic insights into *where* and *why* policies fail. This diagnostic capability is essential for identifying specific weaknesses in policy design, guiding targeted improvements, and accelerating research progress by providing actionable feedback to developers.
3. **Enhanced Reproducibility and Accessibility:** DuoBench prioritizes reproducibility by providing detailed task recipes, including 3D-printable assets for real-world setups, alongside human-teleoperated datasets. This approach enables other research laboratories to replicate tasks on their own FR3 Duo hardware, collect comparable data, and evaluate new methods consistently. Such reproducibility is critical for fostering scientific rigor and collaborative research within the robotics community.
4. **Foundation for Sim-to-Real Transfer Studies:** The benchmark's implementation in both simulation and the real world, coupled with evaluations of mixed-domain training, provides a valuable testbed for studying the sim-to-real gap. It allows researchers to investigate different training strategies, domain randomization techniques, and transfer learning approaches to improve real-world robustness and cross-domain generalization, which remain significant challenges in robot learning.
5. **Standardization for FR3 Duo Platform:** Given the increasing accessibility of the FR3 Duo platform, DuoBench establishes a standardized benchmark for this specific dual-arm configuration. This standardization can facilitate direct comparisons of algorithms developed by different groups, thereby streamlining research and accelerating innovation on this hardware.
6. **Identification of Current Limitations:** The empirical results consistently highlight that existing policies, including state-of-the-art imitation learning and VLA models, face considerable challenges in bimanual manipulation, particularly in early interaction stages (e.g., grasp acquisition), parallel arm execution, and robust sim-to-real transfer. This identification of persistent bottlenecks provides clear directions for future research efforts in areas such as robust perception, advanced motor control, and intelligent coordination strategies.
7. **Extensibility for Future Research:** The framework is designed to be extensible, allowing for the addition of more task varieties and the exploration of richer sensing modalities (e.g., tactile sensing) in future work. This adaptability ensures DuoBench's continued relevance as the field evolves.

In summary, DuoBench offers a robust infrastructure that addresses critical needs in bimanual robot learning. By providing systematic tasks, fine-grained evaluation, and strong reproducibility across simulation and real-world settings, it serves as a valuable foundation for diagnosing policy failures and driving future advancements in dual-arm manipulation capabilities.
