# Unsupervised Skill Discovery for Robotic Manipulation through Automatic Task Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> This work discovers reusable robotic manipulation skills by pre-training a Multiplicative Compositional Policy to solve tasks autonomously generated via Asymmetric Self-Play. The adversarial goal-proposer curriculum, plus behavioral cloning from the proposer's demonstrations, embeds diverse object-interaction behaviors into four goal-independent primitives. Frozen primitives are then composed by task-specific orchestrators trained with hierarchical RL, solving six unseen pick-and-place variants in simulation and transferring to a real Panda arm.

## Problem

- Manipulation tasks give sparse rewards, so learning from scratch typically demands reward shaping, hindsight relabeling, or human demonstrations to get any usable signal.
- Mutual-information skill discovery works for locomotion but yields skills that rarely touch objects, because the intrinsic reward is too sparse and noisy in manipulation.
- Prior self-play manipulation agents use monolithic policies tied to their pre-training input space, limiting reuse when downstream goal spaces or dynamics change.

## Method

- Asymmetric Self-Play pre-training: a goal-proposer explores to set goal states and is rewarded when the solver fails, producing an automatic curriculum of increasingly hard, always-feasible manipulation tasks.
- The solver is a Multiplicative Compositional Policy whose K Gaussian primitives depend only on state, not goal, so a gating network multiplicatively blends them into one composite action distribution.
- Downstream, primitives are frozen and a fresh orchestrator per task is trained with Soft Actor-Critic, preventing catastrophic forgetting while adapting to new goal spaces and dynamics.

## Results

- Across six unseen tasks the method reaches 0.83/1.00/0.52/1.00/0.98/0.98 success, beating skill-discovery baselines whose best scores are mostly under 0.20.
- Ablating the combination hurts: self-play alone scores 0.00 on Wall and 0.27 on Box, while compositional policies trained from scratch without hindsight replay reach at most 0.04.
- Sim-to-real transfer without retraining gives 0.83 success over 103 trials on the enlarged workspace task and 0.59 over 36 trials with an obstacle wall.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task diversity from an adversarial generator can replace mutual-information objectives as the driver of skill discovery, yielding behaviors that actually contact objects.
> - Keeping primitives goal-independent and frozen is what makes them portable; the orchestrator absorbs task-specific input changes without disturbing pre-trained behavior.
> - Pre-trained priors encode biases that can backfire: primitives favoring grasping degraded badly once the gripper was locked shut for pushing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation faces challenges when applying Reinforcement Learning (RL), particularly due to sparse reward signals and the complexity of interacting with objects. Tasks such as pick-and-place often require extensive reward shaping, hindsight experience relabeling (HER), or human demonstrations to facilitate learning. Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) is an approach that aims to mitigate these difficulties by orchestrating a set of pre-trained fundamental behaviors, often referred to as skills or primitives. This approach can increase the frequency of relevant interactions, thereby making the learning process more tractable.

Skill Discovery, or Skill Learning, is a sub-field focused on autonomously acquiring such reusable behaviors. Traditional skill discovery methods often rely on task-agnostic intrinsic rewards, typically maximizing the mutual information between a skill descriptor and a transformation of the environment state. While successful in domains like locomotion, these mutual information-based methods frequently struggle to produce useful, interactive skills for robotic manipulation. The skills generated often lack meaningful interaction with objects and sufficient diversity for broad reusability across various downstream manipulation tasks.

Concurrently, approaches involving Automatic Task Generation and curriculum learning, such as Asymmetric Self-Play (ASP), have demonstrated the ability to generate diverse and increasingly complex tasks. ASP, in particular, has shown promise in robotic manipulation by enabling an agent to discover novel and challenging goals. However, a common limitation of prior ASP applications is that the trained agent typically employs a monolithic policy, which restricts the reusability of the learned behaviors in new, unseen scenarios or with different task structures.

Multiplicative Compositional Policies (MCPs) represent a policy architecture designed to embed behaviors in distinct, composable primitive distributions. While MCPs facilitate the composition of behaviors, their prior applications for pre-training have often relied on cherry-picked demonstrations or mutual information objectives. The current work positions itself at the intersection of these domains, aiming to address the limitations of existing skill discovery by combining the task-generation capabilities of ASP with the composable policy structure of MCPs. The objective is to discover a repertoire of interactive, diverse, and reusable skills that are robustly applicable to varied manipulation tasks, both in simulation and on real robotic platforms, thereby advancing the generalization and efficiency of robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from several limitations in current robotic manipulation learning paradigms:

1. **Sparse Rewards in Robotic Manipulation:** Many manipulation tasks are characterized by sparse reward signals, meaning positive feedback is only received upon successful completion of a task. This significantly impedes learning for traditional RL agents, often necessitating labor-intensive techniques like reward shaping or large volumes of demonstrations. Pre-trained, general-purpose skills could provide a more efficient prior, making downstream task learning more robust and sample-efficient.
2. **Lack of Interactive and Diverse Skills from Current Skill Discovery:** Existing unsupervised skill discovery methods, particularly those based on mutual information maximization, frequently fail to generate behaviors that involve consistent and purposeful interactions with objects in a manipulation context. The skills tend to be non-interactive or lack sufficient diversity, which limits their utility and reusability when integrated into HRL frameworks for solving complex manipulation tasks. A key challenge is to learn skills that explicitly promote robust object interaction.
3. **Limitations of Monolithic Policies in Automatic Task Generation:** While automatic task generation methods like Asymmetric Self-Play (ASP) have been effective in producing diverse tasks and leading to complex behaviors, the resulting agents often utilize monolithic policies. These policies learn to solve specific tasks but are not inherently designed for modularity or easy reuse of sub-behaviors in novel task contexts, thus hindering their applicability for transfer learning.
4. **Ineffective Pre-training for Composable Policies:** Multiplicative Compositional Policies (MCPs) offer a policy architecture for composing primitive behaviors. However, the existing pre-training objectives for MCPs have not fully leveraged the potential of automatic, open-ended task generation to instill a broad and reusable set of manipulation primitives.

Given these motivations, the key objectives of this research are:

- To propose a novel skill learning approach that discovers composable behaviors by training a policy to solve a large and diverse number of autonomously generated tasks.
- To enable the robot to learn skills that facilitate consistent and robust interactions with objects in its environment.
- To embed these discovered behaviors into a set of primitives that can be efficiently composed using Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) to solve previously unseen manipulation tasks.
- Specifically, to achieve these objectives by integrating Asymmetric Self-Play (ASP) for the automatic generation of challenging and diverse pre-training tasks with Multiplicative Compositional Policies (MCPs) for embedding skills in a composable architecture.
- To evaluate the effectiveness and reusability of the learned skills against state-of-the-art skill learning baselines and policies trained from scratch, both in simulation and on a real robotic platform.
- To demonstrate that the advantages of the proposed method arise from the synergistic combination of ASP and MCP, rather than either component in isolation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a three-step process: pre-training, downstream training, and real-world transfer, as depicted in Figure 1 of the paper.

**Step 1: Pre-Training**
The core of the method is a pre-training phase in simulation where a task solver (referred to as Bob) learns diverse behaviors by continuously solving tasks autonomously proposed by a task generator (referred to as Alice).

- **Task Generation with Asymmetric Self-Play (ASP):** The paper leverages ASP to generate a wide diversity of increasingly difficult tasks. Alice, the goal generator, starts from an arbitrary state in the environment and executes a sequence of actions leading to a state, *s_g*, which then becomes Bob's target goal. Bob, the goal-conditioned solver, is tasked with reaching *s_g* starting from the same initial state as Alice, *s_0*.
 - **Reward Structure:** Bob is rewarded for achieving the proposed goals. Alice is rewarded for proposing valid and difficult goals (i.e., goals Bob fails to achieve). To prevent Alice from generating infeasible tasks, a penalty can be applied for proposing undesirable goals.
 - **Alice Behavioral Cloning (ABC):** A crucial aspect for manipulation is that Bob can learn from Alice's successful trajectories to reach *s_g*. This ensures a positive learning signal even when Bob initially fails, promoting proper object interactions.
- **Skill Embedding with Multiplicative Compositional Policy (MCP):** Bob's policy is structured as an MCP to embed the discovered behaviors into composable primitives.
 - **Primitives (π_1..., π_K):** The MCP consists of K independent Gaussian distributions, each representing a primitive specialized in a distinct behavior. These primitives are task-agnostic, depending only on the current state *s*.
 - **Gating Function (w):** An orchestrator policy *w* determines the contribution of each primitive to the composite action distribution. The gating function depends on both the state *s* and the goal *g*, allowing it to be task-specific.
 - **Composition:** The composite policy is formed by a multiplicative composition of the primitives, weighted by the gating function. This results in another Gaussian distribution whose mean and variance are derived from the weighted primitives.
 - **Parameterization:** Both the gating function and the primitives' means and variances are parameterized by neural networks and trained end-to-end using gradient descent (Proximal Policy Optimization, PPO, is used for pre-training).

The hypothesis is that exposing the MCP solver to a diverse and complex curriculum of tasks generated by ASP will induce the learning of a broad repertoire of reusable behaviors, which are then embedded within the MCP's primitives.

**Step 2: Downstream Training**
After pre-training is complete (when Alice no longer proposes novel tasks and Bob can solve all proposed tasks), the pre-trained primitives are reused for novel downstream manipulation tasks.

- **Frozen Primitives:** The parameters of the K primitives (π_1..., π_K) are frozen to prevent catastrophic forgetting and ensure their learned behaviors remain stable. This also makes them robust to changes in environment dynamics or task distribution shifts.
- **New Orchestrator:** For each new downstream task, a new orchestrator (gating function *w*) is trained from scratch. This orchestrator learns to compose the frozen, pre-trained primitives to achieve the specific goals of the downstream task using Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (Soft Actor-Critic, SAC, with Hindsight Experience Replay, HER, is used for downstream training). The orchestrator takes task-specific information (e.g., target object position) as input, along with the state.

**Step 3: Transfer to Real World**
The downstream agents, comprising the newly trained orchestrator and the frozen pre-trained primitives, are transferred to a real robotic platform without further training to evaluate their real-world applicability.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Environment:** Customized `panda-gym` simulation using a Franka Emika Panda robotic arm with a parallel gripper for table-top object manipulation. The object is a 5cm cube unless specified otherwise.
- **State Space (Bob/Downstream):** Robot end-effector position and finger positions, object absolute position, and object position relative to the end-effector. Alice's policy receives additional privileged information (velocities, contact info, time) to aid task discovery.
- **Action Space:** 4D: 3D displacement for the end-effector and 1D for finger width change.
- **Reward Function:** Sparse, binary reward for success (object within 5cm of target position).
- **Pre-training Tasks:** Alice proposes goals within a 35x35x35 cm workspace on the table.
- **Downstream Tasks:** Six variations of pick-and-place, perturbing dynamics or task distribution: `Larger` (larger workspace), `In Air` (targets always in air), `Push` (gripper blocked), `Sphere` (object is a sphere), `Wall` (obstacle between start/target), `Box` (goal inside a box).
- **Baselines:** A comprehensive set including skill learning methods (LSD, DADS, DIAYN), ASP with a monolithic policy, and policies trained from scratch (MCP and monolithic, with and without HER). The number of primitives/latent skill dimensions (K) is set to 4 across all experiments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from qualitative analysis of skill progression, quantitative evaluation on downstream tasks, and real-world transfer.

**A. Primitives Progression through Pre-Training:**
- **Qualitative Assessment:** During the initial stages of pre-training, the learned primitives exhibited minimal object movement. As the ASP curriculum progressed and Alice generated tasks requiring object manipulation on the table, the primitives evolved to reliably interact with the object. Subsequently, tasks involving lifting objects led to the discovery of lifting behaviors. By the end of pre-training, the learned primitives, when composed, demonstrated the capability to move the object to arbitrary positions throughout the workspace. This indicates that task diversity provided by ASP successfully induces the discovery and embedding of varied behaviors within the MCP primitives.
- **Skill Coverage Comparison (Figure 3):**
 - **Our Method:** Compositions of our pre-trained primitives achieved comprehensive coverage of reachable object positions in the workspace, indicating the ability to position the object robustly.
 - **LSD:** Skills learned with LSD partially covered the object position space but frequently pushed or threw the object into unreachable areas (e.g., off the table). This behavior is attributed to LSD's intrinsic objective emphasizing dynamic behavior discovery.
 - **DADS and DIAYN:** Skills from these baselines showed limited interaction with the object. DIAYN exhibited low coverage of object positions, while DADS's skillset largely collapsed, either ignoring the object or moving it along a fixed, narrow path. These limitations were linked to the sparsity and noise of their intrinsic rewards in manipulation.
 - **Conclusion:** The ASP-induced curriculum and explicit design for object interaction in our method resulted in more interactive and workspace-covering skills compared to mutual information-based baselines.

**B. Primitives Orchestration on Downstream Tasks:**
- **Overall Performance (Figure 4, Table I):** Our method demonstrated competitive performance across a range of unseen downstream manipulation tasks. It often achieved higher success rates and greater sample efficiency compared to most baselines, including those benefiting from Hindsight Experience Replay (HER).
- **Specific Task Performance:**
 - **Larger, In Air, and Sphere:** Our orchestrators learned faster and attained the highest success rates among all methods. LSD showed limited and unstable performance on these tasks, struggling with static object holding and stable grasping (e.g., of the sphere). DADS and DIAYN yielded low success rates.
 - **Wall and Box:** Our approach quickly achieved high success rates despite changes in environment dynamics. LSD performed reasonably on `Box`, which was attributed to the box's structure mitigating issues of dynamic skills, but failed on `Wall` due to insufficient skill coverage. DADS surprisingly scored high on `Wall` but used a suboptimal, long trajectory to avoid the obstacle, indicating limited behavioral diversity.
 - **Push:** This task, where the gripper was blocked, represented the primary challenge for our method (0.52 success rate). This was attributed to the pre-trained primitives favoring grasping motions, making them less efficient for pushing. In contrast, Scratch MCP w/ HER (0.92) and Scratch Monolithic w/ HER (0.85) performed well, suggesting that HER-augmented learning can adapt better to significant dynamic changes not covered by the pre-trained skill set.
- **ASP and MCP in Isolation:** ASP (monolithic policy from pre-training without downstream training) generally did not perform as well on tasks with perturbed dynamics. Scratch MCP (without pre-training) often performed similarly or worse than its monolithic counterpart, suggesting that the benefits of the proposed method are derived from the *combination* of ASP and MCP, rather than either component alone. This implies that ASP effectively provides the diverse curriculum, and MCP provides the architectural flexibility for behavior composition and transfer.

**C. Real-World Transfer:**
- The models trained in simulation were directly transferred to a real Franka Emika Panda robot for two downstream tasks (`Larger` and `Wall`) without any additional real-world training.
- **Larger:** The model achieved a success rate of 0.83, comparable to its simulation performance. Failures primarily occurred at the extreme edges of the robot's reachable workspace, indicative of controller limitations rather than skill deficiency. This suggests that the learned primitives robustly transfer to the real world.
- **Wall:** Performance on `Wall` was lower (0.59 success rate) compared to simulation. Failures often involved reaching target positions at the edge of the goal area or collisions with the physical cardboard wall, which was more flexible than its simulated counterpart. The sparse reward function, which incentivizes success over safety, contributed to these collision issues.
- **Conclusion:** The real-world results are encouraging, demonstrating the transferability of the learned primitives to physical hardware for manipulation tasks, while also highlighting areas for future improvements regarding safety and robustness at workspace boundaries.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing a novel, integrated approach for unsupervised skill discovery. Its primary significance lies in addressing critical limitations of current methods and offering a path towards more autonomous and robust robot learning.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**

1. **Effective Unsupervised Skill Discovery for Manipulation:** The method successfully generates diverse and interactive manipulation skills, a challenge that traditional intrinsic reward-based skill discovery approaches have struggled with. By explicitly designing for object interaction via an automatically generated curriculum, the paper demonstrates a method to learn truly *useful* skills for manipulation.
2. **Synergistic Integration of ASP and MCP:** The work demonstrates a powerful synergy between Asymmetric Self-Play (ASP) for open-ended task generation and Multiplicative Compositional Policies (MCPs) for structured skill embedding. This combination allows for the automatic acquisition of a broad skill repertoire that is both diverse and inherently composable.
3. **Enhanced Reusability and Generalization:**
 - The learned primitives are shown to be highly reusable, as they can be orchestrated by new controllers to solve a variety of unseen downstream tasks, including those with perturbed dynamics (e.g., different object shapes, obstacles).
 - The freezing of pre-trained primitives in the MCP architecture effectively prevents catastrophic forgetting, allowing the learned behaviors to remain stable and adaptable across new contexts.
 - The approach often outperforms state-of-the-art skill learning baselines and policies trained from scratch, demonstrating improved sample efficiency and higher success rates on many complex tasks.
4. **Demonstrated Real-World Transferability:** The successful transfer of policies trained solely in simulation to a real robotic platform without fine-tuning highlights the robustness and generalizability of the learned skills. This is a crucial step towards deploying more capable autonomous robotic systems in real-world environments.
5. **Addressing the Sparse Reward Problem:** By providing a rich set of pre-trained, interactive behaviors, the method implicitly addresses the sparse reward problem in downstream tasks, as the HRL orchestrator can build upon these fundamental interactions rather than learning everything from scratch.

**Potential Impact:**

The potential impact of this work is substantial for advancing robotic autonomy. It could lead to:

- **More Efficient Robot Training:** Reducing the need for extensive human demonstrations, reward engineering, or task-specific fine-tuning, thereby accelerating the development and deployment of robotic systems.
- **Increased Robot Versatility:** Equipping robots with a versatile set of fundamental manipulation skills, enabling them to adapt to a wider range of tasks and environments with minimal retraining.
- **Foundation for More Complex Tasks:** The ability to learn and compose robust primitives could serve as a foundational step for tackling even more complex, long-horizon manipulation problems, potentially involving multiple objects or sequential subtasks.
- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer:** Further research building on these transferable skills could enhance sim-to-real pipelines, making simulation-based training more effective for real-world applications.

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as object-centric observations and areas for future architectural improvements, this research represents a step forward in making robotic manipulation learning more scalable, robust, and capable of operating in unstructured environments.
