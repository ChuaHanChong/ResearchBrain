# EVA: Aligning Video World Models with Executable Robot Actions via Inverse Dynamics Rewards

> [!summary] Summary
> The Executable Video Alignment (EVA) framework aligns video world models with executable robot actions by using reinforcement learning and rewards derived from an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM). This approach ensures generated visual trajectories are kinematically plausible, improving task success rates in simulation to 52.6% and real-world out-of-distribution tasks to 60.0%.

## Problem

- Video generative models for robotics often produce visually plausible but physically infeasible robot motions, creating an "executability gap" between visual plans and real robot capabilities.
- This executability gap leads to unstable, high-frequency, or out-of-bounds control commands when decoded by an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM), causing erratic robot behavior and task failures.
- Mitigating these issues at inference time through methods like rejection sampling is computationally inefficient and impractical for real-time robot control due to the high cost of video generation.

## Method

- **EVA** introduces a reinforcement learning post-training method using ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== to align latent video diffusion models with robot-specific executability objectives.
- A pre-trained ==Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM)== infers actions from generated video frames, serving as a "kinematic bridge" that constructs a ==dense reward== signal for the video generative model.
- The reward function penalizes non-smooth actions (high acceleration, jerk) and violations of ==embodiment limits== (max velocity, acceleration) in the IDM-predicted action sequences, guiding the generator towards physically feasible trajectories. Fine-tuning uses ==LoRA== on the video generator.

## Results

- Human evaluation: EVA (with RL) improved Kinematic plausibility by **+20.9%** (to **91.4%**) and achieved **83.8%** Perfect execution rate for generated visual rollouts.
- In simulation: **52.6%** average task success rate across **21 bimanual tasks**, outperforming the supervised baseline (**46.2%**).
- Real-world: **64.0%** success on seen tasks and **60.0%** on out-of-distribution tasks for EVA (with RL), vs. supervised baseline (**52.0%** / **42.0%** respectively).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly incorporating robot-specific physical constraints and kinematic knowledge into the training of video world models, rather than solely focusing on visual realism, is crucial for producing executable robot plans.
> - An Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) can effectively translate visual rollouts into robot actions and, in turn, provide a quantifiable reward signal that captures embodiment-related physical inconsistencies.
> - Reinforcement learning, specifically GRPO, can efficiently fine-tune the LoRA parameters of a video generative model to align its outputs with these IDM-derived executability rewards, bridging the visual-to-physical gap.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist robot policies capable of executing diverse manipulation tasks is a central objective in embodied AI. Current approaches include Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which directly map 2D visual observations and language instructions to low-level motor commands. However, these models face challenges in scaling robust long-horizon behavior, particularly when physical and temporal dynamics must be learned from limited robot interaction data.

In parallel, video generative models are increasingly explored as "world models" for robotics. This emerging paradigm decouples high-level spatiotemporal reasoning from low-level control. A video world model first functions as a visual planner, generating a future visual trajectory based on current observations and language instructions. Subsequently, an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) translates these generated frames into executable robot actions. This separation aims to enable scalable robot learning by leveraging internet-scale video data for visual planning.

Despite the potential of this decoupled pipeline, a significant limitation identified by the authors is the absence of explicit executability constraints in current video world models. These models are typically optimized for visual realism, but the generated rollouts may violate the physical and kinematic constraints of a robot, such as rigid-body consistency, arm deformations, self-intersections, or abrupt temporal discontinuities. This discrepancy, termed the "executability gap," can lead to unstable, infeasible, or out-of-bounds control commands when decoded by an IDM during open-loop execution. While inference-time techniques like rejection sampling can mitigate this, they are inefficient due to the computational cost of video generation.

The proposed work is positioned to bridge this executability gap by incorporating robot-specific physical constraints directly into the training process of video world models. This approach draws inspiration from the success of reinforcement learning (RL) in aligning large foundation models with specific objectives, extending it to the domain of physical robot interaction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to explicitly align video world models with executable robot actions. This alignment aims to ensure that visually plausible generated video trajectories also correspond to physically feasible and kinematically consistent robot motions.

The motivation for this work stems from the identified "executability gap" in existing video-based robot planning pipelines. Current video generative models, while capable of producing visually coherent future rollouts, are not inherently constrained by the physical and kinematic properties of the robot. This can result in several issues:
1. **Kinematic Violations:** Generated videos may depict robot movements that are visually plausible at a superficial level but are physically impossible for a real robot. Examples include arm deformations, joint ambiguities, self-intersections, or sudden, unphysical temporal jumps in robot configuration.
2. **Unstable Control Commands:** When an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) attempts to convert such kinematically inconsistent visual rollouts into robot actions, it often generates unstable, high-frequency, or out-of-bounds control signals. These signals can lead to erratic joint movements, jitter, or even safety interruptions during real-world execution, ultimately causing task failure.
3. **Inefficiency of Post-Hoc Mitigation:** Mitigating the executability gap purely at inference time, for instance, by using rejection sampling to filter out infeasible video rollouts, is computationally expensive. Generating multiple high-fidelity video sequences and then evaluating their executability is an inefficient strategy given the high cost of video generation.

To address these challenges, the authors propose to leverage the executability gap itself as a feedback signal for training. By incorporating robot-specific physical knowledge during the video model's training phase, the goal is to guide the generative process towards producing inherently executable trajectories. This approach aims to move beyond visual fidelity to ensure that generated plans are also physically grounded, thereby improving the reliability and safety of downstream robotic task execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, Executable Video Alignment (EVA), introduces a reinforcement-learning post-training method to align video generative models with explicit executability objectives. The core idea is to construct a reward model from a pre-trained Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) that evaluates generated videos based on the physical plausibility and smoothness of the actions they imply.

#### 3.1. Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM)

The IDM is central to EVA, serving as the "kinematic bridge" between visual observations and robot actions.
- **Purpose:** The IDM's role is to infer robot control commands (e.g., joint positions, velocities) from a short temporal window of visual observations.
- **Training:** It is trained in a supervised manner on real robot trajectory data. For a given sequence of frames $I_{t-k:t+k}$ centered at time $t$, the IDM predicts the executed action $a_t$. The training objective is a standard mean squared error loss between the predicted action and the ground-truth action: $L_{IDM} = E[\sum_t \|f_\phi(I_{t-k:t+k}) - a_{gt,t}\|^2_2]$.
- **Architecture:** The IDM utilizes a visuomotor design. It employs a convolutional backbone to extract spatial features from the input frames. A spatial softmax layer then converts each channel of the feature map into a 2D coordinate representation. These keypoint-like coordinates are concatenated and fed into a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) to predict the action $a_t$. The use of spatial softmax is highlighted as providing more stable action decoding compared to global pooling, particularly when dealing with generated rollouts which may contain visual artifacts. The IDM is kept frozen during the subsequent GRPO fine-tuning of the video generative model.

#### 3.2. IDM-based Executability Reward

This component translates the robot's physical and kinematic constraints into a quantifiable reward signal for the video generative model.
- **Action Sequence Extraction:** For any generated video $V$, the frozen IDM predicts a sequence of joint commands $A = \{a_t\}_{t=1}^T$ at a defined control interval $\Delta t$.
- **Smoothness Penalties:** The executability reward is designed to encourage smooth robot motions. Joint-space velocity ($v_t$), acceleration ($\alpha_t$), and jerk ($j_t$) are computed using finite differences from the predicted actions. To penalize non-smooth motions, a robust Huber penalty function is applied to acceleration and jerk:
 - $P_\alpha = E_t[\text{Huber}(\alpha_t; \delta_\alpha)]$
 - $P_j = E_t[\text{Huber}(j_t; \delta_j)]$
- **Embodiment Limit Penalties:** The reward also enforces the robot's physical limits by penalizing violations of maximum velocity and acceleration bounds:
 - $P_{vel} = E_t[\|\max(|v_t| - v_{max}, 0)\|^2_2]$
 - $P_{acc} = E_t[\|\max(|\alpha_t| - a_{max}, 0)\|^2_2]$
- **Total Penalty and Reward Function:** These individual penalties are combined into a total penalty $P(A) = \lambda_j P_j + \lambda_\alpha P_\alpha + \lambda_{v-lim} P_{vel} + \lambda_{a-lim} P_{acc}$. This penalty is then transformed into a bounded reward $R(V) = (1 + P(A)/P_0)^{-\gamma}$ suitable for reinforcement learning. $P_0$ is a scaling factor estimated from the base model's rollouts, and $\gamma$ controls the decay rate. A higher reward indicates a more kinematically feasible and smoother action sequence.

#### 3.3. Video Model Fine-tuning (Alignment)

The video generative model is fine-tuned using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to align its generated rollouts with the IDM-based executability reward.
- **Base Video Model:** The world model is a latent video diffusion model, specifically a Diffusion Transformer (DiT) instantiated with the Wan2.1-14B backbone. It is initialized from the Large Video Planner (LVP) checkpoint and then supervised fine-tuned (SFT) on an embodiment-specific dataset to create the baseline "EVA (w/o RL)" model. Diffusion forcing is also incorporated to improve rollouts conditioned on observation history.
- **GRPO for Alignment:** Flow-GRPO, an adaptation of GRPO for flow models, is used. This method samples groups of trajectories from a stochastic process derived from the flow velocity and evaluates them with the IDM-based reward. It then uses a clipped policy optimization objective to update the drift network (the video generator). Crucially, only the LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) parameters of the video generator are updated, keeping the main backbone frozen. This allows efficient adaptation without extensive retraining.
- **Inference:** After fine-tuning, the updated network generates videos following the original deterministic flow formulation.

This comprehensive approach allows the video world model to "learn" to produce visual plans that are not only visually coherent but also physically executable by a robot, bridging the executability gap.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the Executable Video Alignment (EVA) framework was evaluated across visual rollout quality, simulation policy execution, and real-world robot deployment.

#### 4.1. Visual Rollout Quality

Human evaluation was conducted on 210 manipulation prompts from the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark to assess embodiment-specific artifacts. Rollouts were anonymized and rated on four criteria: Kinematic plausibility (robot arm structural integrity), Interaction plausibility (physical consistency of object motions), Instruction adherence (matching language goal), and Perfect execution (task completion satisfying all criteria).
- **EVA (with RL) significantly improved Kinematic plausibility by +20.9%** compared to the supervised baseline (EVA w/o RL), achieving 91.4%.
- Interaction plausibility also improved from 83.3% to 86.2%.
- Instruction adherence remained comparable.
- As a result, **EVA (with RL) achieved an 83.8% Perfect execution rate**, a substantial increase from 68.1% for EVA (w/o RL) and 62.9% for Vidar.
- Qualitative analysis (Figure 3) demonstrated that aligned models maintained strict kinematic integrity, reducing morphological deformations and joint "melting" often observed in unaligned models.

#### 4.2. Simulation Policy Execution on RoboTwin

Task success rates were measured on 21 bimanual tasks in randomized scenes on the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark. Actions were derived from generated rollouts using the IDM and executed in simulation.
- **EVA (with RL) achieved the best overall performance with an average success rate of 52.6%** across 21 tasks. This compared to 46.2% for EVA (w/o RL) and 29.0%-45.7% for other baselines (ACT, Diffusion Policy, RDT, π0).
- The reward-based alignment consistently improved task success rates over the supervised baseline, indicating that the alignment process produces more reliable action sequences.
- Improvements were particularly evident in contact-rich tasks like ClickBell, OpenLaptop, and TurnSwitch.

#### 4.3. Real-World Deployment

Experiments were conducted on an Agilex CobotMagic bimanual platform with 5 seen tasks and 5 out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks. Each task involved 20 trials.
- **EVA (with RL) demonstrated improved real-world executability.** For seen tasks, it achieved a 64.0% average success rate, compared to 52.0% for EVA (w/o RL) and 42.0%-51.0% for other baselines.
- **On OOD tasks, EVA (with RL) achieved a 60.0% average success rate,** significantly outperforming EVA (w/o RL) at 42.0% and other baselines, which showed considerable degradation (e.g., π0 at 11.0%, GE-Act at 3.0%).
- These results suggest that explicitly optimizing for action-space feasibility through the IDM-based reward enhances the generalizability and robustness of robot policies in physical environments.

#### 4.4. IDM Evaluation

The Inverse Dynamics Model's robustness was validated by feeding it ground-truth video demonstrations from RoboTwin 2.0 and executing the predicted actions.
- The IDM achieved a highly reliable average execution success rate of 89.52% across 21 tasks. This performance confirms the IDM's capability to accurately reconstruct stable, executable control signals from valid visual trajectories, justifying its use as a dense kinematic reward model.

#### 4.5. Failure Modes (of unaligned models)

Analysis of unaligned video world models revealed characteristic failure patterns during real-world execution, despite apparent visual plausibility. These include:
- **Implausible kinematics:** Robot arm morphological deformation, ambiguous joint articulation, and temporal discontinuities.
- **Wrong contact:** Physically inconsistent object interactions like penetration or missing contact.
- **Incorrect goal:** Failure to make progress toward the instructed objective.

**Reward Validity (Supplementary Material)**
Supplementary analysis indicated that the IDM-based reward correlated positively with both visual quality (artifact-free rollouts received higher rewards) and downstream execution success in simulation (successful executions corresponded to higher rewards). This supports the reward's effectiveness in capturing embodiment-related inconsistencies and predicting executability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper, Executable Video Alignment (EVA), addresses a critical limitation in the application of video generative models for robotic control. By identifying and proposing a solution to the "executability gap," this research contributes significantly to the field of embodied AI and robotic manipulation.

The primary significance lies in the development of an **IDM-based executability reward mechanism**. This mechanism enables video world models to be explicitly aligned with the physical and kinematic constraints of real robots during training. This is a departure from conventional approaches where video models are primarily optimized for visual realism, often overlooking the implicit physical feasibility required for robot actions.

The **main findings demonstrate tangible improvements** in several key areas:
1. **Enhanced Physical Plausibility:** The alignment process leads to generated video rollouts that exhibit reduced embodiment-specific artifacts, such as arm deformations or temporal discontinuities. This translates into more realistic and kinematically valid robot motions.
2. **Improved Executability:** By producing smoother and more physically constrained action sequences, EVA directly contributes to higher success rates in downstream robotic manipulation tasks. This was validated across diverse tasks in both high-fidelity simulation and real-world physical robot deployments.
3. **Better Generalization:** The improvements observed on out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks in real-world scenarios suggest that incorporating action-space priors through reward-based alignment enhances the generalizability of robot policies, allowing them to perform effectively in novel situations.
4. **Robust Foundation for Decoupled Planning:** By making the video generation phase more aware of robot embodiment, EVA strengthens the decoupled visual planning-then-action extraction paradigm. This potentially allows for more robust robot policies that can leverage large-scale visual data while ensuring physical grounding.

The potential impact of this work is substantial for advancing generalist robotics:
- **Scalable Robot Learning:** By aligning video world models with executable actions, the framework opens up avenues for leveraging vast amounts of internet-scale video data for robot learning. The ability to generate stable and feasible actions from visual rollouts could alleviate data scarcity, a major bottleneck in robot learning, particularly through zero-shot generation with synthetic scenes.
- **Safer and More Reliable Robot Deployments:** By penalizing kinematically infeasible motions and out-of-bound commands, EVA contributes to the development of safer and more reliable robot control systems, reducing the risk of erratic behaviors or mechanical stress in real-world applications.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research into richer dynamics-aware reward signals, potentially incorporating concepts like forces, friction, or torques to address precision contact-rich manipulation. Furthermore, the framework encourages exploration of faster sampling methods (e.g., distillation) to enable more responsive closed-loop control, moving towards more reactive and agile robot behaviors.

In conclusion, EVA represents a step towards creating more effective video world models that are not only visually intelligent but also physically astute, ultimately contributing to the realization of more capable and generalist robots.
