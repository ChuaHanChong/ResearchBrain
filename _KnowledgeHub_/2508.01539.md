# HALO: Human Preference Aligned Offline Reward Learning for Robot Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Maryland, College Park, developed HALO, an offline reward learning algorithm that translates human navigation preferences into a vision-based reward function. This method enables robots to navigate complex, real-world environments using only RGB cameras, improving success rates by at least 33.3% and reducing trajectory length by 12.9% compared to existing methods, while aligning with human-like social compliance.

## Problem

- Hand-engineered reward functions for reinforcement learning in robot navigation are complex, demand extensive domain expertise, and require meticulous, often iterative, real-world tuning.
- Many existing RL navigation systems rely on expensive depth sensors (LiDAR or stereo cameras), leading to increased cost, power consumption, and hardware complexity, which limits scalability.
- Emerging LLM/VLM-based navigation solutions face high computational costs, potential reliance on continuous internet connectivity, and challenges for real-time deployment on resource-constrained edge hardware.

## Method

- **HALO** (Human Preference ALigned Offline Reward Learning) is developed as an offline algorithm to learn a ==reward model== directly from human preferences for vision-based robot navigation.
- The approach uses the ==Plackett-Luce framework== to learn from human-generated preference rankings of discrete, dynamically feasible actions, based on egocentric visual input and binary navigation queries.
- A reward model architecture combines a frozen ==DINOv2== visual encoder with action-conditioned visual feature aggregation, utilizing predicted trajectory masks to focus attention on relevant image regions for reward prediction.

## Results

- **HALO**-based policies achieved at least a **33.3%** improvement in success rate, a **12.9%** reduction in normalized trajectory length, and a **26.6%** reduction in Fréchet distance to expert trajectories compared to state-of-the-art vision-based methods.
- The system demonstrated real-time performance (approximately **50 Hz**) and robustly navigated complex real-world scenarios on a Clearpath Husky robot, including outdoor environments with pedestrians and crosswalks, and indoor hallways with glass walls and low-light conditions.
- HALO-trained policies generalized effectively to unseen environments and hardware setups, successfully handling visually challenging situations like glass walls that confused LiDAR-based and hand-engineered reward methods, showing human-like social compliance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human intuition, captured through preference feedback on egocentric visual observations, can be effectively formalized into a robust, vision-based reward function for autonomous navigation.
> - The Plackett-Luce model provides a scalable and stable mechanism for learning context-aware reward functions from ranked actions, offering advantages over pairwise comparison methods for efficiency and stability.
> - The learned human-aligned reward model can seamlessly enhance both offline reinforcement learning policies (e.g., TD3-BC, IQL) and classical navigation planners (e.g., DWA), enabling superior performance without depth sensors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous visual navigation is a fundamental capability for mobile robots operating in complex, dynamic environments. Traditional robot navigation systems often rely on depth sensors such as LiDAR or stereo cameras to estimate environmental geometry and facilitate obstacle avoidance. While effective, these sensors contribute to increased cost, power consumption, and hardware complexity, which can limit the scalability of robot platforms. In contrast, RGB cameras offer a low-cost and widely deployable sensing modality. However, developing robust navigation systems solely based on RGB input remains challenging due to the inherent difficulties in inferring navigability and obstacle relevance directly from raw visual observations, especially under varying lighting conditions, ambiguous pathways, or unstructured terrains. The integration of human intuition to interpret visual scenes and guide navigation decisions is an area of ongoing research aimed at enhancing safety and navigation success.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated utility in developing adaptive navigation behaviors, particularly through online RL approaches that learn from direct environmental interaction, often in simulated environments. A significant limitation of these methods is the "sim-to-real" transfer gap, where policies trained in simulation frequently exhibit poor generalization in real-world settings. Offline RL has emerged as a strategy to mitigate this by enabling policy learning directly from pre-collected, real-world datasets, thereby circumventing the need for costly or unsafe online exploration. Despite these advancements, both online and offline RL approaches typically depend on hand-engineered reward functions, which demand substantial domain expertise for their formulation and meticulous tuning, often requiring iterative real-world testing. Furthermore, many RL-based navigation algorithms still incorporate LiDAR or depth camera data for obstacle distance estimation, which, while effective, introduces scalability challenges due to sensor costs and deployment constraints.

Recent research has also explored the application of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) to visual navigation challenges. These models aim to leverage natural language understanding to interpret goals, identify obstacles, or infer human intent from egocentric observations. While promising for capturing nuanced human intentions and adaptive navigation strategies, LLM/VLM-based solutions often incur significant computational costs, may require continuous internet connectivity due to reliance on cloud-based services, and are not readily deployable on resource-constrained edge hardware. These factors can limit their real-time performance and scalability, particularly in safety-critical scenarios.

This work, HALO, positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of hand-crafted reward functions in vision-based navigation and the practical constraints of LLM/VLM-based methods. It proposes a novel approach that leverages human preferences to learn reward models from egocentric visual inputs, aiming to imbue robots with human-like navigational intuition without relying on expensive depth sensors or high-compute language models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop HALO, a novel Offline Reward Learning algorithm that quantifies human intuition in navigation into a vision-based reward function for robot navigation. This overarching goal is motivated by several identified limitations and challenges in the field of autonomous robot navigation:

1. **Addressing the Challenges of Hand-Engineered Reward Functions:** A significant motivation stems from the difficulty and resource intensity associated with crafting effective reward functions for reinforcement learning. Traditional RL approaches for navigation often necessitate extensive domain expertise to mathematically formalize human intuition for tasks like obstacle avoidance or goal-following. Furthermore, these reward functions require meticulous tuning, which can involve multiple rounds of costly and resource-intensive real-world training and testing. HALO aims to alleviate this burden by learning reward functions directly from human preferences.

2. **Overcoming Sensor Limitations and Promoting Scalability:** Many existing RL-based navigation algorithms, and even hand-engineered reward functions, rely on LiDAR or depth cameras for accurate obstacle distance estimation. While effective, these sensors introduce significant costs, power requirements, and hardware complexity, thereby limiting their scalability across diverse robot platforms and deployment scenarios. A key motivation for HALO is to enable robust navigation using only low-cost, widely deployable RGB cameras, reducing hardware dependence and promoting broader applicability.

3. **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap and Enabling Real-World Learning:** While online RL often benefits from simulation for efficient training, the "sim-to-real" transfer gap remains a challenge, as policies trained in simulation frequently fail to generalize to real-world complexities. Offline RL offers a solution by learning directly from pre-collected real-world data, circumventing hazardous or expensive online interaction. HALO operates within this offline RL paradigm, further motivated by the need for robust policies learned directly from real-world observations.

4. **Integrating Human Intuition for Safer and More Socially Compliant Navigation:** A critical motivation is to imbue robots with a nuanced understanding of human navigation preferences, leading to safer and more socially compliant behaviors. Human intuition can interpret visual scenes to infer navigability and obstacle relevance in ways that are difficult to program explicitly. HALO seeks to leverage human feedback to capture this intuition, especially in ambiguous or unstructured visual environments where traditional obstacle detection might struggle.

5. **Mitigating the Practical Constraints of LLM/VLM-based Solutions:** While LLMs and VLMs offer promising avenues for interpreting goals and inferring human intent through natural language, their practical deployment in real-time, safety-critical navigation scenarios is often hampered by high computational resource demands, reliance on continuous internet connectivity for cloud-based services, and difficulty in deployment on edge hardware. HALO is motivated to provide a more computationally efficient and deployable alternative that can operate in real-time on edge devices without these extensive requirements.

In summary, HALO aims to develop an efficient, vision-based reward learning framework that leverages human preferences to create robust, generalizable, and socially compliant navigation policies, addressing the limitations of prior reward engineering and advanced language model approaches.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HALO (Human Preference ALigned Offline Reward Learning) is an offline algorithm designed to learn a reward model that aligns with human navigational preferences, utilizing the Plackett-Luce framework. The approach involves several key components:

#### 3.1. Dataset

The primary dataset used for offline training is the Socially Compliant Navigation Dataset (SCAND), which provides demonstrations from human-operated wheeled or legged robots in indoor and outdoor settings. From SCAND's 139 scenes, 25 scenes (approximately 107,000 frames) were manually annotated with human preference scores. To further enrich the dataset, 116 additional scenes (approximately 33,000 frames) were collected using a legged robot. Crucially, around 100 of these additional scenes contained short, truncated trajectories exhibiting collisions or unsafe behaviors. These "negative examples" were deliberately included to balance the predominantly successful demonstrations in SCAND, guiding the reward model towards safer behavior.

#### 3.2. Formulating the Reward Learning Problem

Robot navigation is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP). The state space `S` is defined by the robot's egocentric RGB camera observation (`I_RGB`), while the action space `A` consists of continuous control commands represented by linear and angular velocity pairs `(v, ω)`. The objective is to learn a goal-independent reward function `R(s_t, a_t)` that quantifies the quality of actions based solely on visual input, guided by human preference data. The policy `π(a_t|s_t)` is a stochastic distribution over continuous actions, conditioned on the current state.

#### 3.3. Preference Scores from Human Feedback

To capture human intuition, binary responses to five navigation queries are collected for each robot's egocentric view and a corresponding "expert" action `(v*, ω*)`:
1. "Can the robot turn left?"
2. "Can the robot turn right?"
3. "Can the robot decelerate?"
4. "Can the robot accelerate?"
5. "Is the robot in immediate danger or behaving suboptimally?"
Annotators could reuse responses for consecutive frames if the scene's meaningful aspects remained unchanged, reducing annotation effort. If "danger or suboptimal behavior" is flagged, a corrective reference action `(v*, ω*)` is assigned, typically involving reduced linear velocity and a sharp turn, overriding the recorded expert action to emphasize safer behaviors.

A discrete, dynamically feasible action set `A_local` is generated, centered around `(v*, ω*)`. Each action `(v, ω) ∈ A_local` is assigned a probability score based on its proximity to `(v*, ω*)` using a separable Boltzmann distribution. The temperature values (`τ_v(U)` and `τ_ω(U)`) in this distribution are adaptively selected based on user preferences `U` (the four directional queries). If a directional preference is indicated, the corresponding temperature is set to concentrate at least 95% of the probability mass on the preferred direction. Otherwise, a higher temperature flattens the distribution. The final probability score is scaled by a factor `λ`, which is positive if no danger is flagged and negative if danger is flagged, further weighted by the sum of directional preferences. These scores `Pref(v, ω | U)` are then used to rank the candidate actions, with the reference action always ranked highest, forming the supervision signal `σ` for reward model training.

#### 3.4. Reward Model Learning Based on Preference Data Using the Plackett-Luce Loss

The reward function is learned by minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the Plackett-Luce ranking probability (Eq. 1 in paper), ensuring the learned reward function aligns with the human-generated preference rankings. This Plackett-Luce loss offers a scalable and context-aware formulation for learning from ranked actions, capturing the relative importance of all candidates in a single ranking. This approach avoids the `(n_v × n_ω)^2` pairwise comparisons required by other methods, leading to a more stable loss landscape and efficient optimization. This is highlighted as a novel application of Plackett-Luce preference-based reward learning in autonomous navigation.

#### 3.5. Reward Model Architecture

The reward model takes the current RGB observation `I_RGB` and a candidate action `a_t = (v_t, ω_t)` as input, outputting a scalar reward `R(s_t, a_t)`.
- **Image Feature Extraction:** A frozen DINOv2 encoder (Vision Transformer) extracts `N_p` patch-level embeddings of dimension `d_e` from `I_RGB`. These are further refined by Transformer layers.
- **Action-Conditioned Visual Feature Aggregation:** For a given action `(v, ω)`, a binary trajectory mask is generated by projecting the robot's predicted path onto the image plane via a homography transform. This mask is processed by a lightweight CNN to produce a spatial relevance map. This map is then flattened and used to modulate the DINOv2 patch embeddings through element-wise multiplication, directing the model's attention to image regions relevant to the intended action.
- **Final Reward Prediction:** The aggregated feature representation is passed through a simple MLP head to predict the scalar reward value.

#### 3.6. Training and Offline Navigation Policy

Once trained, the HALO reward model can be utilized in two ways:
1. **Offline Policy Supervision:** To supervise a goal-conditioned offline policy, specifically using Behavior Cloning (TD3-BC) and Implicit Q-Learning (IQL).
2. **Classical Navigation Augmentation:** To augment classical navigation algorithms, such as the Dynamic Window Approach (DWA) or Model Predictive Control (MPC), by serving as an additional cost term.
Two regularization strategies are incorporated during training to improve reward learning stability and generalization: (i) a focal-style penalty applied to raw preference scores, and (ii) a diversity regularizer to discourage similar rewards for dissimilar actions. Detailed training methodologies and hyperparameters are provided in the appendix.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of HALO was demonstrated through real-world deployments on a Clearpath Husky robot across diverse navigation scenarios. The reward model was evaluated in two downstream applications: supervising offline learned policies (TD3-BC and IQL) and augmenting a Model Predictive Control (MPC) based planner (DWA).

#### 4.1. Implementation and Comparisons

The Clearpath Husky robot was equipped with a Realsense d435i camera and an onboard laptop (Intel i7, NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU). HALO's performance was compared against several baseline methods:
- **Hand-Engineered Reward (HER):** A reward model incorporating goal distance, obstacle distance (using LiDAR), and a smoothness term. This was used to train IQL and Behavioral Cloning (BC) policies.
- **Behavioral Cloning (BC):** Trained on the same data used for reward model training.
- **Classical Method:** Dynamic Window Approach (DWA).
- **Learning-based Vision-Action Method:** VANP.
Methods marked with a dagger (†) in the results table benefited from LiDAR data. HALO-based policies were tested as HALO + TD3-BC, HALO + IQL, and HALO + DWA.

#### 4.2. Evaluation Metrics

The methods were evaluated using three primary navigation metrics:
- **Success Rate (↑):** Percentage of episodes where the robot reached the goal without collisions.
- **Normalized Trajectory Length (↓):** Ratio of the executed trajectory length to the human expert trajectory length.
- **Fréchet Distance to Expert Trajectory (↓):** Measures the maximum distance between points on the robot's trajectory and the expert trajectory, capturing both spatial proximity and temporal alignment.

#### 4.3. Discussion and Key Results

HALO consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to baseline methods across diverse real-world scenarios, particularly in success rate, trajectory safety, and alignment with human-expert trajectories. The policies trained with HALO generalized effectively to unseen environments and hardware setups.

**Overall Quantitative Performance:**
- HALO achieved at least a **33.3% improvement in success rate** compared to state-of-the-art vision-based navigation methods.
- It showed a **12.9% reduction in normalized trajectory length**.
- A **26.6% reduction in Fréchet distance** compared to human expert trajectories was observed.
- The reward-model-based policies operated in real-time at approximately 50 Hz on the laptop.

**Scenario-Specific Observations:**

- **Scenario 1 (Outdoor, pedestrians, crosswalk):**
 - HALO + DWA (MPC planner with HALO reward) produced smoother, more socially compliant trajectories, leading to lower Fréchet distance and higher success rate, mirroring human-like behavior.
 - The standard DWA planner, relying on LiDAR, often chose direct paths ignoring social context.
 - VANP exhibited jerky motions despite pedestrian avoidance.
 - HALO + IQL also showed social compliance and comparable success rates to DWA, but without LiDAR.
 - HER-based policies (HER + TD3-BC, HER + IQL) struggled, often failing or getting stuck.

- **Scenario 2 (Low-light, pedestrians, pavement):**
 - HALO + DWA demonstrated socially aware navigation by maintaining a larger buffer around pedestrians.
 - HALO + IQL and HALO + BC achieved high success rates but were less socially compliant, often passing closer to pedestrians.
 - HER-based policies consistently failed to reach the goal.

- **Scenario 3 (Indoor hallway, glass walls, pedestrians):**
 - LiDAR-based DWA struggled significantly due to its inability to detect glass walls, leading to collisions.
 - HER-based IQL and BC policies failed to generalize to this unseen scenario.
 - HALO + IQL exhibited human-like behavior, successfully recognizing and navigating around both glass walls and pedestrians to reach the goal. HALO + BC showed relatively better generalization.

In summary, HALO's preference-driven reward learning enabled robots to navigate more safely and align more closely with human intuition, particularly in complex social and visually challenging environments where traditional methods and even other learning-based approaches faltered. This was achieved without relying on expensive depth sensors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HALO presents a significant contribution to the field of autonomous robot navigation by addressing several critical limitations of existing approaches and offering a novel framework for learning human-aligned behaviors. Its potential impact spans theoretical contributions, practical applications, and future research directions.

**1. Overcoming Limitations of Hand-Crafted Rewards:** The most direct impact of HALO is its ability to learn nuanced reward functions directly from human preferences, thereby circumventing the laborious, expertise-dependent, and often sub-optimal process of hand-engineering rewards. This approach can lead to more robust and generalized navigation policies, as human intuition inherently encodes complex safety, social, and efficiency considerations that are difficult to explicitly formalize mathematically.

**2. Enabling Cost-Effective and Scalable Vision-Based Navigation:** By relying solely on RGB camera input, HALO significantly reduces the hardware cost, power consumption, and complexity associated with traditional navigation systems that depend on LiDAR or depth sensors. This makes autonomous navigation more accessible for deployment on a wider range of robot platforms and in various environments, fostering broader adoption of robotic systems in diverse applications such as logistics, service robotics, and personal assistance.

**3. Enhancing Robot Safety and Social Compliance:** The preference alignment mechanism allows HALO to imbue robots with human-like navigational behaviors, including implicit obstacle avoidance and social compliance. This is particularly crucial for robots operating in human-populated environments where understanding and adhering to social norms (e.g., maintaining personal space, using crosswalks) are vital for acceptance and safe interaction. The demonstrated ability to navigate challenging scenarios like glass walls or crowded spaces highlights this impact.

**4. Versatility Across Navigation Frameworks:** The modular nature of HALO's reward model allows for its seamless integration into both modern learning-based control policies (offline RL like IQL and TD3-BC) and classical control algorithms (MPC/DWA). This versatility demonstrates HALO's broad applicability and offers a powerful tool for enhancing existing navigation stacks without requiring a complete overhaul. It suggests that human intuition captured by HALO can benefit a wide spectrum of robotic control paradigms.

**5. Improved Generalization in Real-World Scenarios:** The extensive real-world evaluation on a Clearpath Husky platform across diverse, unseen indoor and outdoor environments, and even different hardware setups, showcases HALO's strong generalization capabilities. This is a crucial step towards deploying truly robust autonomous systems that can adapt to the unpredictable nature of real-world operation, moving beyond the limitations of simulation-trained policies.

**6. Contribution to Reward Learning Methodology:** The novel application of the Plackett-Luce model for preference-based reward learning in autonomous navigation is a significant methodological contribution. This approach provides a structured, scalable, and globally consistent framework for aligning navigation policies with human preferences, offering advantages over pairwise comparison methods by efficiently handling ranked action sets and yielding a more stable loss landscape.

**7. Open-Sourcing Data and Code:** The commitment to releasing the annotated user preference dataset for suboptimal trajectories, alongside the code, will be valuable for the research community. This promotes reproducibility, encourages further research into human-robot interaction and preference learning, and allows others to build upon HALO's foundations.

In conclusion, HALO represents a substantial step towards more intuitive, safe, and deployable autonomous robot navigation. By effectively leveraging human preferences and vision-only sensing, it addresses practical constraints and enhances robot capabilities in complex, real-world settings, paving the way for more intelligent and socially aware robotic agents.
