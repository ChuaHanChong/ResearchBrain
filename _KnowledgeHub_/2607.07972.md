# In vivo feasibility study of humanoid robots in surgery

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC San Diego conducted the first in-vivo feasibility study of a general-purpose humanoid robot performing laparoscopic cholecystectomies in a porcine model, demonstrating its ability to execute core surgical tasks using standard manual instruments. The system achieved significant performance improvements over manual operation in dry lab settings, though it lagged behind specialized surgical robots in speed and precision.

## Problem

- Existing surgical robots are purpose-built and often require proprietary instruments, limiting versatility and increasing costs.
- General-purpose humanoid robots, despite their human-like form factor, had not been systematically evaluated for precision, stability, and safety in actual surgical procedures.
- The healthcare sector faces staffing shortages, demanding solutions for embodied clinical tasks that current robots do not fully address.

## Method

- A teleoperation framework was developed for a Unitree G1 humanoid robot to manipulate standard wristed laparoscopic instruments, incorporating a virtual ==Remote Center of Motion (RCM)== control.
- Benchtop experiments characterized kinematic workspace, system latency (156 ms follower-leader latency), and motion accuracy (1.30 mm RMS for lines, 10.40 mm RMS for circles).
- Dry-lab user studies with surgeons and novices evaluated performance against manual and da Vinci systems on O-ring transfer and FLS peg transfer tasks, followed by in-vivo laparoscopic cholecystectomies in a porcine model.

## Results

- In dry lab studies, the humanoid achieved significantly lower weighted errors in O-ring transfer tasks compared to manual operation, with intermediate completion times (**74.67 s** vs **64.22 s** for manual).
- For FLS peg transfer, the humanoid significantly outperformed manual operation (**560.1 s** vs **877.8 s** completion time, **85.39** FLS score vs **70.47** for manual).
- Two laparoscopic cholecystectomies were successfully completed robotically in vivo, demonstrating active console times of **56:15** and **31:59**, though requiring major pauses for recalibration (**8** in Case 1, **4** in Case 2).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - General-purpose humanoids can perform core laparoscopic surgical tasks, demonstrating a technical feasibility beyond theoretical concepts or dry-lab demonstrations.
> - Despite performance improvements over manual operation, current humanoid systems face limitations in speed, precision, and workflow efficiency compared to specialized surgical robots due to latency, control accuracy, and mechanical constraints.
> - Frequent recalibrations and repositioning were necessary during in-vivo procedures, highlighting the need for more robust RCM localization, dynamic stability, and improved range of motion for clinical deployment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The development of humanoid robots has progressed from theoretical concepts to potential real-world applications due to advancements in actuation, control systems, and machine learning. These robots, with their human-like morphology, are considered compatible with environments, tools, and workflows designed for humans, positioning them for deployment in various sectors.

Healthcare is identified as a particularly relevant domain, currently facing challenges such as staffing shortages and increasing demands for care. While existing robotic applications in hospitals primarily address logistical and administrative tasks, a significant portion of clinical work remains embodied, necessitating mobility, manipulation, and safe interaction within human-centric spaces.

Current robotic systems for surgery are predominantly purpose-built platforms, exemplified by Intuitive Surgical’s da Vinci Surgical System. These systems are highly refined for specific surgical procedures but often require additional space and coordination for equipment positioning and intraoperative maneuvering, as operating rooms are typically designed for manual surgery. Some research has explored using general-purpose manipulators with kinematic control strategies to maintain laparoscopic port constraints, but these systems still rely on robot-specific instruments.

This research distinguishes itself by investigating whether general-purpose humanoid robots can manipulate standard, human-designed surgical tools directly. This approach offers several potential advantages:
1. **Compatibility with Existing Infrastructure:** Humanoid form factors may integrate more seamlessly into existing operating rooms without extensive modifications.
2. **Use of Standard Instruments:** The ability to use manual surgical tools could reduce reliance on proprietary and specialized robotic instruments.
3. **Versatility and Cost-Effectiveness:** A multi-purpose humanoid platform could potentially perform a broader range of clinical tasks beyond surgery (e.g., patient interaction, bedside assistance, logistics), improving resource utilization and reducing capital, storage, and operational costs.

Previous studies, such as the "Surgie study," have demonstrated that general-purpose platforms can perform dexterous medical tasks under teleoperation in controlled conditions, suggesting a pathway towards surgical workflows. However, a significant gap remained in demonstrating the stability, precision, and safety of humanoids for actual surgical procedures in clinical environments. This work aims to bridge this gap by conducting a systematic evaluation of contemporary humanoid technology, including the first in-vivo study, to assess its feasibility for laparoscopic surgical tasks and identify the technical and safety challenges for clinical deployment.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research was to systematically evaluate the capabilities and limitations of contemporary humanoid robots for performing laparoscopic surgical tasks. This evaluation aimed to determine how closely current general-purpose humanoid systems align with the precision, control, and safety requirements of minimally invasive surgery.

The motivation for this study stemmed from several factors:
1. **Addressing Healthcare Workforce Challenges:** The increasing demand for healthcare services, coupled with persistent staffing shortages, places a significant strain on clinical systems. While automation has been introduced for logistical tasks, many critical clinical workflows, particularly in surgery, require embodied interaction and dexterous manipulation. Humanoid robots offer a potential solution to augment the skilled labor force in these areas.
2. **Exploring the Potential of Humanoid Form Factors in Surgery:** Traditional surgical robots are specialized, purpose-built systems. The human-like morphology of humanoids, however, suggests they could operate within existing surgical environments and utilize standard manual medical equipment, potentially offering practical advantages in terms of deployment flexibility, reduced space requirements, and adaptability within established operating room infrastructure.
3. **Evaluating the Feasibility of General-Purpose Robots for Precision Tasks:** It was unclear whether current general-purpose humanoid robots, designed for broader applications, could meet the high precision, stability, and safety demands inherent in surgical procedures. This study sought to provide an evidence-based assessment of their technical readiness.
4. **Developing a Humanoid-Based Teleoperation Framework:** A key objective was to develop a functional teleoperation framework that enables a humanoid robot to perform laparoscopic manipulation using manual wristed instruments. This framework aimed to map intuitive human hand motions to scaled instrument control, replicating capabilities found in conventional minimally invasive surgical (MIS) robots.
5. **Quantifying Performance and Identifying Gaps:** Through a multi-stage evaluation process—including benchtop characterization, dry-lab user studies with varying surgical experience levels, and in-vivo porcine studies—the researchers aimed to quantify technical feasibility, task performance, and clinical readiness. This comprehensive assessment was crucial for identifying the specific technical and safety challenges that need to be addressed before humanoids can be considered for broader clinical deployment.

In essence, the study was driven by the potential of humanoids to transform surgical assistance by leveraging their versatile form factor and the need for a rigorous, data-driven assessment of their current state for safety-critical medical interventions.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The researchers developed and evaluated a humanoid-based laparoscopic teleoperation framework through a multi-stage approach comprising benchtop experimentation, dry-lab user studies, and in-vivo porcine surgeries.

#### 3.1. Humanoid Laparoscopic Teleoperation System

The system utilized a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, a commercially available platform. The core of the system was a teleoperation setup consisting of:
- **Operating Console:** Equipped with two Master Tool Manipulator (MTM) modules for bimanual instrument control, a foot pedal for instrument clutching/unclutching, a control workstation, and a GOOVIS G3 Max stereo-HD headset for a low-latency endoscopic view from a dual-1920p stereo-laparoscopic camera.
- **Robot Actuation:** The humanoid manipulated commercially available non-robotic wristed instruments (LivsMed ArtiSential bipolar fenestrated forceps). A custom mount was developed to interface these instruments with the robot's hands, allowing the humanoid to actively control all wrist degrees of freedom of the end-effector. Jaw opening and closing were achieved via a servo-driven finger interface, mapping MTM input to a 0-45 degree gripper range.
- **Remote Center of Motion (RCM) Control:** Unlike conventional surgical robots with mechanically fixed RCMs, the humanoid system employed a virtual RCM. The instrument control was formulated as an inverse mapping of an extended kinematic chain, subject to an RCM constraint. The RCM position was calibrated using ArUco markers detected by the humanoid’s head camera, with a temporal filter applied to prevent disturbances from dynamic RCM positions.
- **Communication:** The Robot Operating System 2 (ROS2) network facilitated synchronized bimanual manipulation between the console and the humanoid.

#### 3.2. Benchtop Experimentation

Controlled experiments were conducted to assess baseline performance and identify technical limitations:
- **Kinematic & Workspace Analysis:** The reachable instrument workspace was analyzed as a function of RCM placement relative to the robot base. This involved sampling candidate robot hand positions and evaluating feasibility using an inverse-kinematics (IK) solver, subject to joint-limit and collision constraints. The reachable workspace was compared against specifications for conventional surgical robots (e.g., dVRK, modeled as a spherical sector with a 100° apex angle and 20 cm radius).
- **Motion Accuracy Evaluation:**
 - **System Latency:** Measured as follower-leader latency (operator hand to robot end-effector motion) using a high-frame-rate external camera, and control loop latency on the control PC.
 - **Command-Execution Tracking:** Quantified by commanding the tool tip to execute repeatable planar trajectories (100 mm straight lines, 80 mm diameter circles) while maintaining the RCM constraint. An OptiTrack motion-capture system tracked the executed trajectories using a reflective marker attached to the tool tip. Precision was reported as Root-Mean-Square (RMS) geometric residuals: orthogonal deviation for lines, and radial and out-of-plane deviation for circles, relative to fitted motion primitives (PCA-based).

#### 3.3. Dry Lab Evaluation of the Framework

User studies were performed to compare the humanoid system’s performance and robustness against existing surgical platforms and manual operation:
- **Participants:** Two groups were recruited: 18 participants (6 surgeons, 12 novices) for the O-ring transfer task and 13 medical professionals (8 junior surgeons, 5 senior surgeons) for the FLS peg transfer task.
- **Comparative Platforms:** The da Vinci Research Kit (dVRK) was used for the O-ring task, and the da Vinci Xi system for the FLS peg transfer, alongside manual laparoscopic operation using trainer boxes to enforce RCM constraints.
- **Tasks:**
 - **O-ring Transfer:** Standardized bi-manual transfer of two rubber O-rings between four pegs. Metrics included task completion time and weighted error scores (penalties for failed pick-up, stretching, dropping, collision, straw displacement).
 - **FLS Peg Transfer:** Classic Fundamentals of Laparoscopic Surgery (FLS) task involving bi-manual transfer of six pegs from one side of a board to the other and back. Metrics included completion time, operational errors (peg drops, unintentional collisions, failed peg pickups), and a balanced FLS score (min-max normalized time and error, rescaled to).
- **Procedure:** Participants underwent standardized familiarization for each platform, performed trials in randomized order, and completed a post-experiment Likert-scale questionnaire assessing mental/physical demand, motion accuracy, feedback quality, and overall performance.

#### 3.4. Live Porcine Surgery (In-vivo)

Two laparoscopic cholecystectomies were performed to evaluate the system in a physiological environment:
- **Setting:** Surgical simulation center with equipped operating rooms.
- **Ethical Approval:** UCSD IACUC #S08027.
- **Setup:** Abdominal access and port placement (four trocars) were performed by attending surgeons. The humanoid was positioned at the bedside, and its RCM was calibrated using ArUco markers. A harness was used as a safety precaution.
- **Workflow:** A single senior surgeon operated the humanoid from the console, while a human bedside assistant managed camera control, tissue retraction, exposure, and minor adjustments. A second humanoid was briefly used for camera holding and retraction in the first case. Standard laparoscopic cholecystectomy workflow was followed, with robot-compatible wristed instruments. Cystic duct clipping was performed laparoscopically due to unavailability of compatible robot clip appliers.
- **Pauses and Recalibration:** Procedures were paused for recalibration in cases of RCM drift, degraded visualization, or unexpected instrument behavior. These were classified as micro-pauses (<1 minute) for routine adjustments and major pauses (>3 minutes) for robot redeployments or recalibration.
- **Metrics:** Active console time, duration of segment-specific operative steps (e.g., tissue dissection, critical view of safety, gallbladder mobilization), number of robot deployments, and number of major/minor pauses.
- **User Experience:** A structured post-procedure survey (n=4 participants, attending surgeons and surgical trainees) evaluated system effectiveness, clinical readiness, ergonomics, and fidelity of motion on a Likert scale, specific to the roles performed.

### 4. Main findings and results

The study's findings are presented across benchtop, dry-lab, and in-vivo evaluations, detailing the humanoid system's technical capabilities, performance, and current limitations.

#### 4.1. Benchtop Experimentation

- **Kinematic & Workspace Analysis:**
 - The humanoid platform demonstrated a reachable surgical workspace that was approximately cone-shaped, with its volume and approach direction modulated by RCM placement.
 - Compared to the dVRK's modeled workspace (5985 cm³), the humanoid achieved a comparable workspace for a subset of RCM placements, suggesting that standard trocar placements might need adaptation for humanoid surgical deployments.
- **Motion Accuracy Evaluation:**
 - **System Latency:** The follower-leader latency was measured at approximately 156 ms, and the control loop latency was about 24 ms (with 11 ms for inverse kinematics computation per cycle). This follower-leader latency is generally higher than the desired <150 ms for conventional surgical robotic systems, indicating room for improvement in responsiveness.
 - **Command-Execution Tracking:**
 - Straight-line motions (100 mm) achieved a Root-Mean-Square (RMS) orthogonal deviation of 1.30 ± 0.03 mm.
 - Circular motions (80 mm diameter) exhibited a larger RMS radial deviation of 10.40 ± 1.32 mm, with low out-of-plane error (1.23 ± 0.14 mm RMS).
 - The millimeter-scale accuracy for line tracking was noted, but curved motions were limited by in-plane accuracy. This was attributed primarily to inaccuracies in the kinematic chain model for manually-wristed laparoscopic instruments (due to manually measured geometric parameters) and the RCM control's sensitivity to vision-based trocar localization errors.

#### 4.2. Dry Lab Evaluation of the Framework

- **User Study Task: O-ring Transfer (n=18: 6 surgeons, 12 novices):**
 - **Overall Performance:** Manual operation resulted in moderate completion time (64.22 ± 31.82 s) but the highest weighted error (7.03 ± 4.88). Both the humanoid (4.53 ± 3.14 weighted error) and dVRK (4.59 ± 2.53 weighted error) achieved lower weighted errors, with comparable accuracy. The dVRK provided the fastest completion time (43.01 ± 15.25 s), while the humanoid had the longest (74.67 ± 39.22 s).
 - **Expertise-based Differences:**
 - Novices: dVRK showed the fastest time and lowest error. The humanoid showed intermediate accuracy but required the longest time.
 - Surgeons: Generally more accurate and consistent. Surgeons achieved their lowest error on the humanoid platform while remaining fastest on the dVRK.
 - **Statistical Significance:** Humanoid and dVRK both showed significant reductions in weighted error compared to manual operation. The dVRK was significantly faster than both manual operation and the humanoid platform.
- **User Study Task: FLS Peg Transfer (n=13 medical professionals: 8 junior, 5 senior surgeons):**
 - **Performance Hierarchy:** A clear performance hierarchy was observed: da Vinci Xi (best: 118.2 ± 56.7 s, 1.33 ± 1.23 total errors, 97.67 ± 1.54 FLS score) > Humanoid (intermediate: 560.1 ± 595.5 s, 6.25 ± 6.36 total errors, 85.39 ± 16.25 FLS score) > Manual operation (lowest: 877.8 ± 774.3 s, 17.45 ± 16.80 total errors, 70.47 ± 27.53 FLS score).
 - **Expertise-based Differences:** Senior surgeons consistently achieved better overall performance across all three platforms.
 - **Statistical Significance:** The da Vinci Xi significantly outperformed both the humanoid and manual conditions across all metrics. The humanoid platform also showed significant improvements over manual operation in terms of time, error, and FLS score.
 - **Post-Participation Questionnaire (Senior Surgeons):** Subjective feedback aligned with quantitative metrics. Compared to the da Vinci Xi, the humanoid required higher mental demand (5.40 vs 2.00), physical demand (4.40 vs 1.60), and effort (5.60 vs 2.20), and resulted in higher frustration (4.20 vs 1.40). Overall performance was rated intermediate (6.00 vs 9.20 for da Vinci Xi).
 - **Limitations from Dry Lab:** Participants reported reduced responsiveness in control feedback (due to latency), a less intuitive control interface compared to da Vinci, and constrained reachability from the humanoid's compact arm span (approximately 450 mm vs. 1.6-1.8 m for adult humans).

#### 4.3. Live Porcine Surgery

- **Feasibility:** Both laparoscopic cholecystectomies were successfully completed robotically using the humanoid system without requiring conversion to conventional laparoscopy or open surgery. This demonstrated the system’s initial capacity for hepatocystic dissection and gallbladder mobilization under physiological conditions.
- **Efficiency:** Active console time was efficient, indicating effective robotic manipulation for core surgical tasks (Case 1: 56:15, Case 2: 31:59). Segment-specific operative steps were executed rapidly.
- **Complications:** The first case was completed without major intraoperative complications. The second case experienced minor biliary spillage and liver-bed bleeding, which were managed with suction and electrocautery.
- **Workflow Analysis:**
 - Micro-pauses (<1 minute) for routine instrument adjustments and camera cleaning by the bedside assistant were observed, similar to human-assisted laparoscopic surgery.
 - Major pauses (>3 minutes) for robot redeployment or recalibration were recorded (8 deployments in Case 1, 4 in Case 2), highlighting workflow interruptions and areas for improvement.
- **User Experience (n=4 participants from setup, bedside, console roles):**
 - Quantitative ratings indicated the system was moderately effective for core operative tasks and possessed some, but not full, clinical readiness.
 - **Strengths:** Compact physical footprint, intuitive console interface, and effective remote center pairing were highlighted. Smooth dissection and instrument control were noted when operating within system constraints.
 - **Limitations:** Range-of-motion and strength limitations required frequent repositioning and increased vigilance. Workflow interruptions due to system recalibration, targeting, and intermittent overheating were significant concerns. Users emphasized the need to address these pauses before clinical use in human patients.
 - **Suggestions for Improvement:** Enhance range of motion, improve instrument exchange efficiency, and ensure compatibility with standard laparoscopic instruments.

Overall, the findings consistently demonstrate that the humanoid platform can achieve performance superior to manual operation in many aspects but still lags behind established, purpose-built surgical robots like the da Vinci system in terms of speed, precision, and workflow efficiency, primarily due to factors like latency, control inaccuracies, and mechanical limitations.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This study represents a notable contribution to the field of surgical robotics by presenting the first in-vivo feasibility assessment of a general-purpose humanoid robot performing standard laparoscopic cholecystectomies in a porcine model. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

#### 5.1. Pioneering In-vivo Application and Benchmark Establishment

The successful completion of two live porcine cholecystectomies using a humanoid robotic system is a landmark achievement. It moves the discussion of humanoids in surgery from theoretical potential and dry-lab demonstrations to actual physiological conditions. This study establishes an early, quantitative benchmark for humanoid surgical performance in vivo, providing crucial data for future development and comparison.

#### 5.2. Validation of Technical Feasibility for Core Surgical Tasks

The research demonstrates the technical feasibility of teleoperating a humanoid robot to manipulate manual laparoscopic instruments with sufficient fidelity for core operative steps, such as hepatocystic dissection and gallbladder mobilization. The quantitative assessment of efficient active console times and precise instrument control indicates that humanoids can execute critical surgical tasks effectively.

#### 5.3. Identification of Key Technical and Workflow Gaps

While demonstrating promise, the study rigorously identifies current limitations, providing a clear roadmap for future engineering improvements. Key challenges include:
- **Latency and Control Accuracy:** The measured follower-leader latency (∼156ms) and inaccuracies in curved motion tracking highlight the need for more responsive and precise control systems.
- **Workspace and Kinematic Constraints:** The humanoid's compact arm span and sensitivity to RCM placement necessitate adapted trocar placements and improved range of motion.
- **Calibration and Robustness:** The observed RCM drift and the need for frequent recalibrations or repositioning (major pauses) point to requirements for more robust RCM localization and control under dynamic conditions.
- **Sterility:** The current method of maintaining sterility (gloves over robot arms) is not fully adequate for human surgery, necessitating new protocols for sterile integration without impairing sensor function or motion calibration.

#### 5.4. Potential for Increased Accessibility and Efficiency in Surgical Care

The research highlights several potential long-term benefits once these technical challenges are addressed:
- **Reduced Costs:** Humanoid platforms, potentially lower in acquisition cost and offering versatile task deployment, could make robotic-assisted surgery more accessible to a wider range of healthcare facilities, reducing reliance on expensive, proprietary purpose-built systems.
- **Optimized Operating Room Design:** A humanoid’s human-like form factor and reduced footprint could alleviate crowding in operating rooms, improve sightlines, and provide greater freedom for bedside assistants, potentially enhancing team situational awareness and communication.
- **Multi-purpose Utility:** The ability of humanoids to perform diverse tasks—from surgery to patient interaction and hospital logistics—could improve resource utilization and reduce capital and operational demands in resource-constrained healthcare environments.

#### 5.5. Importance of Clinician-Engineer Collaboration

The study underscores the value of close collaboration between clinicians and engineers for iterative testing and refinement in realistic surgical environments. This interdisciplinary approach is essential for translating early-stage robotic systems into clinically ready solutions.

In conclusion, this research marks a significant step towards understanding the practical utility of humanoid robots in surgery. By thoroughly evaluating current capabilities and delineating critical areas for development, it lays a foundation for future advancements that could expand the reach of robotic surgery, address healthcare resource constraints, and potentially redefine surgical assistance.
