# HoMeR: Learning In-the-Wild Mobile Manipulation via Hybrid Imitation and Whole-Body Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Stanford University and University of Cambridge researchers developed HOMER, a mobile manipulation framework that combines a kinematics-based whole-body controller with a hybrid imitation learning policy. This approach achieves an average success rate of 79.17% across diverse in-the-wild tasks, outperforming baselines by 29.17%, and shows enhanced generalization through VLM conditioning and visual augmentations.

## Problem

- Coordinating the many degrees of freedom in mobile manipulators for complex tasks presents a significant control challenge, often leading to suboptimal movements with decoupled base and arm control.
- Everyday manipulation tasks are multi-phase, requiring both long-range reaching and fine-grained interaction, which is difficult for policies relying solely on dense low-level actions or sparse keyposes.
- Robots struggle to generalize effectively to the high variability in object appearances, spatial arrangements, and clutter found in unstructured real-world environments.

## Method

- HOMER integrates a kinematics-based whole-body controller (WBC) to offload low-level joint coordination, allowing the imitation learning policy to operate in a simplified 6-DoF end-effector action space.
- A hybrid imitation learning agent is developed with two sub-policies: a keypose sub-policy for long-range absolute pose predictions and a dense sub-policy for fine-grained relative pose predictions, with learned mode switching.
- The HOMER-COND variant incorporates externally provided 3D keypoints from Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and uses visual augmentations during training to improve generalization to novel scenarios.

## Results

- HOMER achieved an average success rate of 79.17% across six diverse mobile manipulation tasks, outperforming the next best baseline by an average of 29.17% (e.g., 85% on 'Cabinet Opening' vs. 35% for the best baseline).
- The hybrid action space and whole-body control components individually contributed to improved performance, with the full HOMER system consistently demonstrating superior robustness in challenging maneuvers.
- HOMER-COND, when conditioned on VLM-derived keypoints and trained with augmentations, maintained high performance in generalization scenarios involving distractor objects and unseen object colors, demonstrating improved robustness in varied visual environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Using a kinematics-based whole-body controller simplifies the learning problem for mobile manipulators by handling complex joint-space coordination, enabling the policy to focus on task-level end-effector commands.
> - A hybrid action space, combining keypose predictions for macro-movements and dense relative deltas for micro-manipulation, allows for seamless execution of multi-phase tasks within a single learned policy.
> - Leveraging external salient points from Vision-Language Models and employing robust visual augmentations are effective strategies for enhancing the generalization capabilities of mobile manipulation policies to novel and cluttered environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is progressively moving from controlled laboratory environments towards more diverse and unstructured real-world settings, such as homes and offices. This transition necessitates robots capable of both navigation and complex manipulation. Mobile manipulators, which combine mobility with manipulation capabilities, are central to this goal. However, these platforms introduce significant control complexity due to their many degrees of freedom (DoF), requiring coordinated motion between a mobile base and a robotic arm, or between limbs and torso in legged systems.

Prior research has addressed this complexity through several avenues:
- **Whole-Body Controllers (WBCs):** These systems map high-level end-effector commands to coordinated whole-body movements. While successful in specific domains like quadrupeds (where balance and stability are critical) and humanoids (often using high-dimensional joint-space actions), general application for wheeled mobile manipulators often involves decoupled base and arm control, which complicates teleoperation and policy learning.
- **Visual Imitation Learning (IL):** This approach trains robots from human demonstrations using visual observations. Different action granularities have been explored:
 - **Dense policies:** Predict low-level, continuous actions (e.g., 6-DoF deltas or joint velocities) at every timestep. These are effective for reactive tasks but can struggle with long-horizon tasks and spatial generalization due to the cumulative nature of many small actions. Examples include Diffusion Policy and various Visual-Language-Action (VLA) models.
 - **Keypose-based policies:** Predict sparse, high-level 6-DoF absolute end-effector poses. These are often sample-efficient for reaching but may lack the precision required for fine-grained manipulation. Examples include PerAct and RVT/RVT-2.
 - **Hybrid policies:** Combine both keypose and dense actions to leverage the strengths of both, enabling both long-range movement and precise local manipulation. While promising in tabletop settings (e.g., Hydra, AWE, SPHINX), these approaches had not been extensively applied to mobile manipulation, which involves greater embodiment complexity, larger workspaces, and longer task horizons.

HOMER integrates a kinematics-based whole-body controller with a hybrid imitation learning policy. This combination specifically addresses the control challenges of mobile manipulation and the multi-phase nature of real-world tasks. Furthermore, HOMER's modular design allows for integration with vision-language models (VLMs), enabling generalization to novel object appearances and cluttered environments, a critical need for deployment in human-centric spaces. This work extends hybrid imitation learning into the mobile manipulation domain, aiming to bridge the gap between structured lab environments and unstructured real-world applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable mobile manipulators to effectively perform complex, multi-phase manipulation tasks in realistic, unstructured "in-the-wild" environments, such as homes. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and aims to address key challenges inherent in current mobile robotics:

1. **Addressing Control Complexity in Mobile Manipulation:** Mobile manipulators, featuring a combination of a mobile base and an articulated arm, possess a high number of degrees of freedom. Coordinating these DoFs to achieve specific end-effector goals while ensuring stable and efficient whole-body motion presents a significant control challenge. Traditional approaches often simplify this by decoupling base and arm control, which can lead to suboptimal movements, complex teleoperation, and difficulties in learning coordinated behaviors. The motivation is to manage this complexity by offloading low-level joint coordination to a dedicated controller, allowing policy learning to focus on task-level decisions.

2. **Facilitating Multi-Phase Task Execution:** Everyday manipulation tasks frequently involve distinct phases, such as long-range reaching to an object, followed by fine-grained, precise interaction (e.g., grasping, inserting, opening). Policies relying solely on dense, low-level actions often struggle with the efficiency and stability of long-range movements, requiring numerous consecutive actions. Conversely, purely keypose-based policies might lack the granularity for delicate manipulation. The objective is to develop an action representation that can inherently accommodate both macro (long-range) and micro (fine-grained) movements within a single, learned policy, thereby improving task performance and efficiency.

3. **Enhancing Generalization to Novel Scenarios:** Real-world environments are characterized by high variability in object appearances, spatial arrangements, and clutter. Robots deployed in these settings must be able to generalize beyond the specific instances seen during training. Prior imitation learning methods often struggle with this variability. A key motivation is to improve the policy's ability to adapt to unseen objects and layouts, moving beyond rote memorization of demonstrations. This includes exploring how internet-scale priors from vision-language models (VLMs) can be leveraged to guide manipulation in unfamiliar contexts.

4. **Improving Sample Efficiency in Learning:** Collecting large datasets for robot learning in real-world environments is labor-intensive and time-consuming. Therefore, developing methods that can learn effectively from a limited number of demonstrations is crucial for practical deployment. The research aims to demonstrate a framework that can achieve robust performance on diverse tasks with a relatively small dataset, thereby addressing a practical bottleneck in robot learning.

In summary, the motivation behind HOMER is to move beyond the limitations of static robot arms and tabletop settings, enabling mobile robots to execute practical tasks in complex, dynamic human environments by strategically combining robust whole-body control with a flexible, generalizable, and sample-efficient hybrid imitation learning approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HOMER (Hybrid whole-body policies for Mobile Robots) is an imitation learning framework designed for mobile manipulation that integrates a kinematics-based whole-body controller (WBC) with a hybrid action space policy. This design aims to manage control complexity and enable effective performance on multi-phase, in-the-wild tasks.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The system considers a mobile manipulator with a holonomic base and an N-DoF robotic arm. Instead of learning actions in the high-dimensional joint space, the Imitation Learning (IL) policy $\pi(o_t)$ predicts a 6-DoF end-effector target pose $x_{ee,t+1}$ and gripper state $g_{t+1}$. This higher-level end-effector command is then executed by the WBC, allowing the IL policy to focus on task-space reasoning.

#### 3.2. Whole-Body Controller (WBC)

HOMER employs a kinematics-based WBC that maps a desired 6-DoF end-effector pose $x_{ee}$ to joint position commands $q$ for the entire embodiment (mobile base and arm). The controller, implemented using MuJoCo and the mink IK library, handles low-level joint coordination, constraints, and redundancy resolution.

The WBC operates by iteratively finding joint velocities that minimize the pose error between the current end-effector pose and the desired target pose. This optimization considers:
- **Objective:** Minimize the pose error, maintain a neutral resting posture (a tucked arm posture) to resolve redundancy, and damp base motion. Weights are assigned to each term to balance their influence.
- **Constraints:** The optimization is subject to hardware-specific joint velocity and position limits. It also includes velocity-based collision avoidance between specified robot components (e.g., arm-base, arm-camera mounts), modeled as geometric primitives, to slow motion as clearance decreases.
The IK solver iteratively integrates the optimized joint velocities to produce the next joint position command, which is then executed on the hardware. All WBC hyperparameters are fixed and reused across tasks without specific tuning.

#### 3.3. Hybrid Imitation Learning Agent

The hybrid IL agent, built upon the WBC's end-effector control space, consists of two sub-policies: a keypose sub-policy for long-range movements and a dense sub-policy for fine-grained manipulation. Each sub-policy predicts both the next end-effector action and the next control mode ($m_{t+1} \in \{\text{keypose, dense, terminate}\}$), which dictates the choice of sub-policy for the subsequent action.

##### 3.3.1. Teleoperation and Data Collection

Demonstrations are collected using an iPhone-based interface that streams the phone's 6-DoF pose to the robot's end-effector. Gripper commands are issued via swipe gestures. The WBC translates these high-level commands into joint-space actions, and observations and actions are recorded at 10 Hz.

##### 3.3.2. Keypose Sub-policy ($\pi_{keypose}$)

- **Function:** Handles long-range movements like reaching, predicting an absolute 6-DoF end-effector pose.
- **Input:** A third-person point cloud $P_t$.
- **Output:** 6-DoF end-effector pose $x_{ee}$, gripper state $g_{t+1}$, and next control mode $m_{t+1}$.
- **Mechanism:** It predicts per-point saliency probabilities over the input point cloud and per-point 3D offsets to the ground-truth end-effector position. The point with the highest saliency defines a task-relevant 3D location. The policy combines this positional component with predicted orientation, gripper state, and control mode.
- **Training:** Supervised with action labels, mode labels, and salient point annotations obtained via a custom interface. This interface allows annotators to segment demonstrations into keypose and dense modes and specify salient points in the 3D point cloud. The policy uses a Transformer-based architecture and incorporates temporal and spatial augmentations.

##### 3.3.3. Conditioned Keypose Sub-policy (HOMER-COND)

- **Function:** Extends the keypose sub-policy to enhance generalization by optionally incorporating externally provided 3D keypoints, typically from Vision-Language Models (VLMs).
- **Mechanism:** When an external keypoint is provided, a distance-weighted saliency map is constructed and concatenated as an additional input channel.
- **Training:** A masked supervision strategy is employed: 50% of the time, the conditioned saliency map is masked, and the model learns to predict saliency and actions autonomously. In the other 50%, the conditioned saliency map is provided, and only action predictions are supervised. Visual augmentations (e.g., omitting RGB, adding distractor points) are applied to improve robustness.

##### 3.3.4. Dense Sub-policy ($\pi_{dense}$)

- **Function:** Designed for fine-grained manipulation near salient points (e.g., inserting, aligning, grasping).
- **Input:** Third-person and wrist-mounted RGB images, current end-effector state $x_{ee,t}$.
- **Output:** A 6D delta action $\Delta x_{ee,t}$ relative to the current end-effector pose, and the next control mode $m_{t+1}$.
- **Mechanism:** Implemented using Diffusion Policy, which predicts a horizon of 16 future actions, executing the first 8 before replanning.

##### 3.3.5. Execution

During execution, the agent automatically switches between the keypose and dense sub-policies based on the current predicted mode $m_t$. The initial mode is typically keypose. The predicted end-effector action $x_{ee,t+1}$ from the chosen sub-policy is then passed to the WBC for execution. This learned mode switching enables dynamic alternation between reaching and precise manipulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates HOMER's performance on a holonomic mobile manipulator (TidyBot++) equipped with a 7-DoF Kinova arm, in both simulation and real-world household environments. The experiments investigate the benefits of hybrid actions (Q1), whole-body control (Q2), and generalization capabilities (Q3). All methods were trained with 20 demonstrations per task.

#### 4.1. Q1 & Q2: Benefits of Hybrid Actions and Whole-Body Control

The evaluation involved six mobile manipulation tasks (three simulated, three real-world), including `Cube Pick`, `Dishwasher Open`, `Cabinet Opening`, `Pillow`, `TV Remote`, and `Sweep Trash`. These tasks were selected for their varying requirements in terms of spatial generalization, precision, and long-horizon reasoning.

**Baselines:**
- **Diffusion Policy (B+A):** A dense policy with decoupled base-arm control, predicting 10-DoF relative poses (3-DoF base, 6-DoF end-effector, 1-DoF gripper).
- **HOMER (B+A):** A hybrid policy with decoupled base-arm control, predicting either a 3-DoF base keypose, a 6-DoF arm keypose, or a 10-DoF relative pose.
- **Diffusion Policy (WBC):** A dense policy using whole-body control, predicting 7-DoF relative poses (6-DoF end-effector, 1-DoF gripper) executed via the WBC.
- **HOMER (OURS):** The full HOMER system, a hybrid IL agent predicting either a 6-DoF end-effector keypose or a 6-DoF relative end-effector pose, plus a 1-DoF gripper command, executed through the WBC.

**Results (Success Rate / 20 attempts):**
- **DP (B+A):** This baseline consistently exhibited the lowest success rates across tasks. It struggled with initial reaching and alignment, especially in tasks with significant randomization in robot or object poses (e.g., `Cube`, `Dishwasher`, `Cabinet Opening`, `Pillow`). The dense end-effector deltas often led to policies veering off course.
- **DP (WBC):** Showed marginal improvement over DP (B+A), indicating some benefit from the simplified end-effector action space provided by the WBC, particularly in the low-data regime. However, it still struggled without explicit keyposes for long-range movements.
- **HOMER (B+A):** This hybrid policy, even with decoupled base-arm control, was the strongest baseline. Its ability to use base and arm keyposes allowed it to achieve favorable pre-manipulation poses. However, it faced difficulties when smooth base-arm coordination was critical (e.g., `Cabinet`, `Dishwasher`, `Sweep Trash`) or when base misalignment limited arm reachability.
- **HOMER (OURS):** HOMER achieved the highest success rates across all tasks, with an overall average success rate of 79.17%, outperforming the next best baseline by an average of 29.17%. It demonstrated robust performance in challenging maneuvers, such as manipulating appliances larger than the robot itself (`Cabinet`, `Dishwasher`), executing smooth long-horizon motions (`Sweep Trash`), performing precise actions (`TV Remote`), and generalizing under randomization of both object and robot poses.

These results indicate that both hybrid action modes and the whole-body controller contribute significantly to improved performance. Hybrid actions effectively handle multi-step tasks by allowing the policy to switch between coarse and fine control, while the WBC simplifies the learning problem by managing complex joint-space coordination.

#### 4.2. Q3: Generalization to Novel Scenarios

To evaluate generalization, HOMER-COND, a variant of HOMER conditioned on external salient points from a VLM and trained with point cloud augmentations, was tested. The evaluation used simulated `Cube` tasks with variations in cube sizes, distractor objects, and different-colored cubes. MolMo 7B-D, a VLM, was used to detect task-relevant keypoints (e.g., "point to the green cube") which were then deprojected into 3D and provided as input to HOMER-COND.

- **HOMER (unconditioned) and HOMER-COND-NoAugs (conditioned but no augmentations):** Both performed adequately in simple settings but showed degraded performance when faced with distractors or novel object appearances.
- **HOMER-COND (conditioned with augmentations):** This variant maintained high performance across all generalization scenarios, including the presence of distractors and unseen object colors.

This highlights that both salient point conditioning from VLMs and the application of visual augmentations (such as removing color channels and injecting distractor points during training) contribute to enhanced robustness and generalization in cluttered or visually varied environments.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Results: Whole-Body Teleoperation in the Wild

Qualitative assessments of the WBC through teleoperated demonstrations in a real home revealed its utility for data collection. The WBC enabled smooth and reliable teleoperation for a diverse range of household tasks, including opening and closing cabinets, doors, blinds, and ovens; coordinated motions like wiping tables and watering plants; and precise maneuvers such as putting away shoes or moving a guitar. The optional collision avoidance feature between the arm, base, and camera mounts further contributed to safe operation. These qualitative observations underscore the WBC's practicality for scalable data collection in unstructured real-world settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HOMER framework represents a notable advancement in mobile manipulation, primarily due to its integrated approach combining whole-body control with hybrid imitation learning. This research offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotics:

**Significance of Contributions:**

1. **Sample-Efficient and Robust Mobile Manipulation:** HOMER demonstrates a method for achieving high success rates in complex mobile manipulation tasks using a limited number of demonstrations (20 per task). This sample efficiency, combined with robust performance across diverse simulated and real-world tasks, addresses a critical bottleneck in deploying robots in real-world environments where extensive data collection is impractical. The success rate of 79.17% across challenging tasks, outperforming baselines significantly, indicates a robust and effective learning strategy.
2. **Effective Management of Control Complexity:** By employing a kinematics-based whole-body controller, HOMER simplifies the learning problem for the imitation learning agent. The controller handles the low-level coordination of the mobile base and arm, allowing the policy to operate in a reduced 6-DoF end-effector action space. This abstraction offloads the intricacies of high-dimensional control from the learning agent, making the policy learning more tractable and stable.
3. **Hybrid Action Space for Multi-Phase Tasks:** The learned ability to switch between absolute keypose predictions for long-range movements and relative pose predictions for fine-grained manipulation is crucial. This hybrid action space enables the robot to perform both large-scale repositioning and precise interactions seamlessly within a single task, reflecting the multi-phase nature of everyday manipulation. This is a key enabler for tasks requiring both broad motion and delicate contact.
4. **Enhanced Generalization through VLM Conditioning:** The modular architecture of HOMER, particularly the HOMER-COND variant, demonstrates compatibility with vision-language models (VLMs). By conditioning the keypose policy on VLM-derived keypoints and utilizing visual augmentations (e.g., color removal, distractor injection), HOMER can generalize to novel object appearances, spatial layouts, and cluttered scenes. This ability to leverage internet-scale visual and semantic priors is vital for robots operating in highly variable human environments.
5. **Practical Teleoperation for Data Collection:** The developed whole-body controller facilitates intuitive iPhone-based teleoperation, which is essential for efficiently collecting high-quality demonstration data in complex, real-world settings. This practical aspect directly supports the sample-efficient learning paradigm and enables scalable data acquisition for future research.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Advancement Towards General-Purpose Mobile Robots:** HOMER's capabilities move mobile manipulation beyond controlled tabletop settings and into unstructured, human-centric spaces. This is a foundational step towards developing more versatile and assistive robots that can perform a wider array of tasks in homes, offices, and other everyday environments.
- **Scalability of Assistive Robotics:** By providing a sample-efficient and generalizable framework, HOMER contributes to the scalability of assistive robotics. Robots leveraging this approach could potentially be deployed more broadly to assist with household chores, elderly care, or logistical tasks, without requiring extensive, task-specific retraining.
- **New Directions in Robot Learning:** The work highlights the benefits of structuring action spaces and integrating control capabilities with learning. This could inspire further research into hybrid control architectures, intelligent mode switching, and more sophisticated ways of incorporating external knowledge (like VLM priors) into robot policies.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies clear limitations, such as the need for environmental collision avoidance, active perception, and integration with navigation systems for truly long-horizon tasks. HOMER provides a strong basis upon which these future research directions can build, paving the way for more autonomous and capable mobile manipulation systems. The simplified end-effector action space also holds potential for multi-task co-training across diverse mobile and non-mobile datasets.
