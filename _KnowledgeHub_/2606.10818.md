# IMPACT: Learning Internal-Model Predictive Control for Forceful Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> IMPACT introduces a robotic control framework that learns an internal model to predict and compensate for external forces, such as object weight, enabling robust manipulation across varying object dynamics without requiring specialized force sensors. It achieved high success rates in tasks involving out-of-distribution object masses by decoupling high-level task planning from low-level force compensation.

## Problem

- Learning-based approaches for forceful robotic manipulation, which implicitly generate forces through end-effector tracking errors, demonstrate limited generalization to objects with out-of-distribution physical properties.
- Explicit force control methods rely on specialized and costly hardware (e.g., wrist force sensors), increasing system complexity and expense.
- Current methods often require extensive data collection across a wide range of object properties or environmental conditions to achieve robustness.

## Method

- The framework learns an internal model online to estimate slowly varying external wrenches (e.g., payload weight) from robot joint torque residuals.
- A high-level diffusion policy is trained for kinematic trajectory planning, while a low-level ==internal-model-based predictive controller== handles online force compensation.
- The predicted wrench from the internal model is applied as a feedforward command within an impedance control loop, reducing steady-state tracking errors.

## Results

- In simulation, IMPACT maintained high task success rates (near **100%**) across an object mass range of 0-10 kg, significantly outperforming baselines that saw success rates decline sharply beyond their training distributions.
- On a real Franka FR3 robot, IMPACT achieved 21/25 success on a 5 kg dumbbell after being trained only on a **2.5** kg dumbbell, while baselines trained on **2.5** kg failed (0/25) or had limited success even with varied training data (5/25).
- The internal model's 'surprise gate' enabled rapid re-adaptation to new loads (e.g., switching from a **2.5** kg to a 5 kg dumbbell) within a few interactions, maintaining low pose tracking errors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling high-level kinematic planning from low-level dynamic force compensation, through an internal model, significantly enhances generalization to unseen object properties.
> - An online-learning internal model, equipped with a 'surprise gate' mechanism, allows for rapid adaptation to unexpected external loads while maintaining stability during known interactions.
> - Robust estimation of external wrenches from readily available joint torque measurements eliminates the need for additional, costly force/torque sensors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Real-world robotic manipulation frequently involves forceful interactions, such as handling objects of diverse masses, using tools, or engaging in contact-rich tasks like wiping. The current landscape of learning-based approaches for these tasks typically employs imitation learning policies. These policies commonly output desired end-effector poses, which are then tracked by low-level impedance controllers.

Within this paradigm, forceful interactions are managed in two primary ways:
1. **Implicit realization:** Forces are generated indirectly through steady-state tracking errors. This approach, however, exhibits limited generalization capabilities when object properties, such as weight, vary significantly. The policy must learn to intentionally overshoot or induce errors to generate the required forces, coupling task planning with object-dependent dynamics and requiring substantial training data.
2. **Explicit command:** Forces are directly specified using specialized hardware, such as wrist force/torque or tactile sensors. While effective, this method introduces increased system complexity and cost due due to the requirement for additional, accurate sensing equipment.

The paper identifies a gap in existing methods, noting that neither implicit nor explicit approaches fully address the need for robust and adaptive forceful manipulation in varied environments without either sacrificing generalization or increasing hardware complexity. Humans, in contrast, demonstrate superior adaptive motor control without relying on highly accurate wrist force sensors. Instead, human motor control mechanisms, particularly the cerebellum, are hypothesized to build internal models of the body and external objects, using these models to generate anticipatory feedforward forces to counteract predictable disturbances.

IMPACT aims to bridge this gap by introducing a framework that draws inspiration from human motor control. It proposes a departure from conventional learning-based methods by decoupling high-level task planning from low-level force compensation through an internal-model-based predictive control strategy. This work positions itself as an alternative to both implicit and explicit force control methods, offering a solution that seeks to achieve robust generalization and adaptability without the need for specialized force sensors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robotic manipulation framework, termed IMPACT (Internal-Model Predictive ConTrol), that enables robots to perform forceful interaction tasks robustly and adaptively under varying environmental dynamics. Specifically, the framework aims to facilitate tasks such as lifting objects of different weights, executing hybrid position-force control actions like calligraphy, and compensating for external disturbances, all while demonstrating enhanced generalization capabilities.

The motivation behind developing IMPACT stems from several limitations observed in existing robotic manipulation systems:

1. **Poor Generalization of Implicit Force Control:** Current learning-based methods that implicitly generate forces through end-effector tracking errors struggle to generalize to objects with out-of-distribution physical properties, such as significantly different weights. For instance, a policy trained to lift a light object may fail when presented with a heavy one because it relies on learned positional offsets to induce compensatory forces. This coupling of high-level task planning with low-level dynamics makes the learning problem more challenging and data-intensive.
2. **Hardware Dependency and Complexity of Explicit Force Control:** Approaches that explicitly command forces using wrist force/torque or tactile sensors require the integration of specialized and often costly hardware. This increases the overall system complexity, maintenance requirements, and expense, limiting their widespread applicability.
3. **Inefficiency in Data Collection:** Both implicit and explicit methods often necessitate extensive data collection across a wide range of object properties or environmental conditions to achieve a degree of robustness. This is time-consuming and can be impractical in many real-world scenarios.
4. **Inspiration from Human Motor Control:** The researchers are motivated by the adaptive capabilities of human motor control. Humans can manipulate objects of diverse weights effortlessly, a skill attributed to the brain's ability to form and utilize internal models that predict object dynamics and generate feedforward commands. This biological mechanism suggests a more effective paradigm for robotic force control than current robotic approaches.

By addressing these issues, IMPACT aims to provide a system that can autonomously learn and adapt to object dynamics online, generate appropriate feedforward forces for predictable disturbances, and maintain compliant behavior, leading to higher success rates, improved generalization, and potentially better safety and energy efficiency in forceful manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The IMPACT framework proposes a control architecture that decouples high-level task planning from low-level force compensation. The core of the methodology lies in learning an internal model that predicts and compensates for slowly varying external wrenches, such as those induced by payload weight or persistent contact forces.

**Overall Framework:**
The system is composed of two main layers:
1. **Task Planning (High-level):** A diffusion policy (πθ) is trained via imitation learning on demonstration data. This policy outputs a horizon of desired end-effector target poses (Xd t:t+Hp), conditioned on a history of observations. This part of the system focuses on the kinematic trajectory.
2. **Internal-Model-Based Predictive Control (Low-level):** This component operates online and is responsible for estimating and compensating for interaction forces. It utilizes an impedance controller for feedback regulation and an internal model for feedforward compensation.

**External Wrench Estimation:**
The method begins by estimating external wrenches (wext) acting on the end-effector. This is achieved by:
- Measuring joint torques (τmeas) from the robot's sensors.
- Predicting model-based joint torques (τmodel) using the manipulator equation (M(q)¨q + C(q, ˙q)˙q + g(q) + τfric(˙q)). The joint accelerations (¨q) are estimated from encoder measurements.
- Calculating the torque residual (τres = τmeas - τmodel), which represents the contribution of unmodeled external interactions.
- Solving a regularized least-squares problem to derive an estimate of the external wrench (ˆwext) from τres and the end-effector Jacobian (J): ˆwext = (JJ⊤ + λ2I)−1 Jτres. The regularization term (λ) is used to stabilize the estimation.
- To isolate persistent effects from noise and high-frequency transients, the raw wrench estimate (ˆwext) is passed through a first-order low-pass filter to produce a slowly varying estimate (wslow). This wslow serves as the supervision signal for training the internal model.

**Internal Model Learning:**
The internal model (Mφ) is a predictor parameterized by φ that learns to anticipate wslow from the robot's recent state history.
- **Input:** A short history of joint states and end-effector measurements (zt-L:t).
- **Online Training:** The model (wpred = Mφ(zt-L:t)) is trained online using gradient descent.
- **Robustness Mechanism (Surprise Gate):** To prevent adaptation to noisy or transient events, the internal model is updated only when its prediction error (difference between wpred and wslow) exceeds a predefined threshold. When the error is below the threshold, learning is suspended, and the model's output is deemed reliable for prediction. This mechanism enables rapid adaptation to unexpected loads while maintaining stability during known interactions.

**Feedforward Compensation:**
During task execution, the learned internal model enables predictive force compensation:
- The predicted wrench from the internal model (wpred) is directly applied as a feedforward command (wff = wpred).
- This feedforward wrench is then mapped to joint torques (τff = J(q)⊤wff) and integrated into the low-level impedance control loop.
- The total joint torque command sent to the robot is τ = τimp + τff + τnull, where τimp is from the impedance feedback and τnull handles secondary objectives (e.g., joint limits).
- This anticipatory compensation minimizes steady-state tracking errors and reduces the demand on the impedance controller, which would otherwise need to generate larger deflections to compensate for external loads.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Real-world experiments:** Conducted on a 7-DOF Franka FR3 manipulator, utilizing two RGB-D cameras and a Nintendo Switch Joy-Con for teleoperation.
- **Simulation experiments:** Performed in the MuJoCo physics engine with a simulated Franka FR3 and virtual cameras, allowing for controlled variation of object properties like mass.
- **Baseline methods:**
 - **Vanilla DP:** A standard Diffusion Policy trained on demonstrations with a single nominal object mass. Forces are implicitly generated by the impedance controller.
 - **Augmented DP:** A Diffusion Policy trained with demonstrations collected across a broad range of object masses through domain randomization. Forces are also implicitly generated.

The methodology emphasizes combining robust online learning of environmental dynamics with feedforward control, inspired by biological systems, to enhance generalization and performance in forceful robotic manipulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research systematically evaluated the IMPACT framework against baseline methods in both simulated and real-world forceful manipulation tasks. The main findings are detailed as follows:

**Controlled Experiments in Simulation (Figure 6):**
- **Task:** Manipulation of objects with varying masses (0-10 kg) in a MuJoCo environment.
- **Vanilla DP (trained on 0.2 kg):** Exhibited a sharp decline in task success rate as the object mass increased beyond its training distribution. This indicates a strong tendency to overfit to the nominal dynamics and poor generalization to unseen object weights.
- **Augmented DP (trained on 0.1-8.0 kg):** Showed improved robustness compared to Vanilla DP due to training on a wider range of masses. However, its success rate still degraded significantly when faced with object masses at the higher end of or beyond its training distribution (e.g., > 8 kg). This suggests that increasing data diversity alone has limits in addressing out-of-distribution generalization for forceful interactions.
- **IMPACT (trained on 0.2 kg):** Maintained high task success rates across the entire evaluated mass range (0-10 kg), demonstrating superior performance. Notably, IMPACT outperformed Augmented DP despite being trained solely on a nominal object mass, without explicit exposure to varied object weights during policy training. This indicates that the explicit modeling and feedforward compensation of payload dynamics in IMPACT lead to more effective generalization than relying solely on broader data distribution.

**Real-World Experiments on Franka FR3 Robot (Table 1):**
- **Task:** Pick-and-place dumbbells of varying masses (2.5 kg and 5 kg). Policies were trained using demonstrations with a 2.5 kg dumbbell, or with a combination of 2.5 kg and 5 kg dumbbells for Augmented DP.
- **Vanilla DP (trained on 2.5 kg):** Achieved a high success rate on the 2.5 kg dumbbell (18/25) but completely failed on the 5 kg dumbbell (0/25).
- **Augmented DP (trained on 2.5 kg + 5.0 kg):** Showed limited success on both 2.5 kg (9/25) and 5 kg (5/25) dumbbells, indicating that even with varied training data, implicit force control struggled with robustness.
- **IMPACT (trained on 2.5 kg):** Demonstrated high success rates on both the 2.5 kg dumbbell (22/25) and the significantly heavier 5 kg dumbbell (21/25). These results confirm IMPACT's generalization capabilities in real-world scenarios, adapting effectively to a heavier, previously unencountered payload.

**Analysis and Ablations of IMPACT (Figures 7, 8, 9, 10):**
- **Adaptation Mechanism:** A detailed analysis of IMPACT's internal model behavior during a pick-and-place task protocol (2.5 kg, then 5 kg dumbbell) revealed its adaptive capabilities.
 - **Initial Learning (Phase A, 2.5 kg):** When first encountering the 2.5 kg dumbbell, the "surprise gate" mechanism activated, allowing the internal model to rapidly learn and converge to an accurate estimate of the external wrench (Figure 7a). This led to effective feedforward compensation and minimal pose tracking error (Figure 10a).
 - **Known Load (Phase B, repeated 2.5 kg):** With the 2.5 kg load now learned, the surprise gate remained inactive, and the internal model continued to provide accurate feedforward compensation, maintaining low pose error (Figure 7b, Figure 10b).
 - **Re-adaptation (Phase C, 5 kg):** Upon switching to the 5 kg dumbbell, the internal model's prediction for a 2.5 kg load became inaccurate. This discrepancy reactivated the surprise gate, triggering rapid re-adaptation of the internal model to the new 5 kg dynamics (Figure 7b, Figure 8). This enabled the controller to quickly adjust and continue maintaining low pose error (Figure 10b).
- **Pose Tracking Performance:** Comparison with a baseline impedance controller without feedforward compensation (Figure 9) showed that the baseline exhibited significant steady-state errors across all phases due to its inability to compensate for the external load. In contrast, IMPACT's feedforward compensation effectively canceled external loads, keeping pose error within the noise floor (Figure 10).

In summary, the main findings establish IMPACT as a framework that significantly improves robustness and generalization for forceful robotic manipulation tasks. Its bio-inspired internal model, coupled with a surprise-gate-driven online learning mechanism, enables rapid adaptation to varying object dynamics without extensive prior data, outperforming data-intensive and implicitly force-controlled baselines.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The IMPACT framework presents a notable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in tasks requiring forceful interactions with the environment. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** A primary impact of IMPACT is its demonstrated ability to generalize robustly to unseen object dynamics, specifically varying masses, without requiring extensive training data for each new object property. This addresses a critical limitation of previous learning-based approaches, which often struggle with out-of-distribution scenarios. Robots equipped with IMPACT could perform tasks more reliably in dynamic and unstructured environments where object properties are not always known beforehand.

2. **Reduced Hardware Dependency and System Complexity:** By estimating interaction forces from joint torque measurements and learning an internal model, IMPACT mitigates the need for specialized and often costly wrist force/torque sensors. This can lead to more economical, simpler, and potentially more durable robotic systems, broadening the accessibility and applicability of advanced manipulation capabilities.

3. **Biologically Inspired and Adaptive Control:** The framework draws inspiration from human motor control, particularly the role of the cerebellum in building and utilizing internal models for predictive control. This bio-inspired approach could pave the way for more intrinsically adaptive and intelligent robotic systems that mimic the robustness and flexibility observed in biological motor control. The "surprise gate" mechanism, allowing rapid re-adaptation to unexpected changes, is a key element of this adaptability.

4. **Decoupling of High-Level Planning and Low-Level Control:** IMPACT successfully decouples the high-level visuomotor policy, which plans kinematic trajectories, from the low-level force compensation mechanism. This modularity simplifies the learning problem for the policy, allowing it to focus on task execution without being burdened by explicit force generation for varying loads. Such decoupling could lead to more efficient policy learning and potentially facilitate transfer learning across different robot platforms or tasks.

5. **Improved Safety and Energy Efficiency:** By accurately predicting and compensating for interaction forces in a feedforward manner, IMPACT can reduce steady-state tracking errors and minimize excessive force generation by the impedance controller. This can contribute to safer interactions between the robot and its environment or human collaborators, as well as potentially reducing energy consumption by avoiding unnecessary compensatory movements.

6. **Broader Applicability to Forceful Tasks:** The framework has been demonstrated across a range of forceful manipulation tasks, including heavy object pick-and-place, calligraphy (a hybrid position-force task), and disturbance compensation. This versatility suggests its potential application in various industrial, service, and even assistive robotics scenarios where precise and adaptive force control is essential.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges current limitations, noting that IMPACT is primarily effective for dynamic properties directly related to forceful interactions, such as object gravity, contact stiffness, and friction. It is not directly applicable to non-rigid-body objects (e.g., deformable materials, fluids, granular materials) which require modeling more complex dynamic properties. Future work is proposed to extend the internal model to handle these more complex contact conditions and integrate richer sensory modalities, which would further broaden the framework's generalizability and impact.

Overall, IMPACT represents a significant step towards enabling robots to perform complex forceful manipulation tasks with greater autonomy, adaptability, and robustness in diverse and dynamic real-world environments.
