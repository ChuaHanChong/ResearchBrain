# Socially Consistent Multi-Robot Navigation Using Decoupled Planning and Trajectory Coordination

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for multi-robot navigation in human-centric environments integrates macroscopic social norms into global path planning, using a decoupled trajectory coordination approach. The system demonstrated enhanced social compliance and predictability, achieving approaching 100% success rates in collision avoidance for up to 10 robots with approximately one-second planning times, and was successfully deployed on hardware.

## Problem

- Existing human-aware navigation research often focuses on local, reactive obstacle avoidance, lacking long-horizon, globally consistent, and socially compliant behaviors.
- Traditional multi-robot path planning (MAPF) methods often neglect social conventions, leading to unpredictable and undesirable robot behaviors in human-shared spaces.
- Coordinating multiple robots to avoid collisions is a computationally challenging combinatorial problem, especially without considering social norms which can exacerbate conflicts.

## Method

- A partially decentralized, two-stage framework decouples global socially compliant path planning from ==conflict-free trajectory coordination==.
- A modified ==A* search algorithm== generates individual robot paths that incorporate social costs (e.g., "keep-right" conventions) and leverages a collaboratively learned ==social graph== for fleet-wide consistency and efficiency.
- Conflict-free trajectories are computed using a ==Mixed-Integer Convex Program (MICP)== that optimizes event waypoint arrival times, specifically designed for structured encounters simplified by social paths.

## Results

- The social planner produced paths that were socially compliant (e.g., **2.64m** average distance to right wall vs. **8.31m** for A*) and the social graph reduced planning time to less than vanilla A* after sufficient learning.
- The MICP trajectory coordinator achieved approaching **100%** success rates in collision avoidance for up to **10** robots with approximately one-second runtime, outperforming CBS and Priority Planning.
- Hardware experiments demonstrated robots naturally staying to the right in hallways and safely negotiating intersections, with distributed computation protocols showing an average speedup of at least **50%** for three robots.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly embedding macroscopic social norms into global path planning simplifies the complex multi-robot coordination problem by structuring and reducing potential conflict points.
> - A collaboratively learned "social graph" provides fleet-wide path consistency, enhances predictability for humans, and significantly reduces subsequent path planning computation time.
> - Decoupling geometric path planning from temporal trajectory coordination allows for efficient and scalable conflict resolution, even for larger robot fleets, by leveraging the structured nature of social paths.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of autonomous mobile robots into human-centric environments, such as restaurants, warehouses, and airports, necessitates navigation capabilities that extend beyond mere safety and efficiency to include predictability and alignment with social conventions. This work addresses a critical gap in existing mobile robot navigation research, which often prioritizes short-term, reactive obstacle avoidance over long-horizon, globally consistent, and socially compliant behaviors.

A significant body of prior work in human-aware navigation primarily focuses on local planners. These local planners are responsible for immediate, short-term adjustments within a robot's sensor range, often employing techniques like social force models, cost maps derived from sensor data, or timed elastic band (TEB) algorithms to respect human personal space and anticipate movements. While crucial for immediate safety, these local approaches frequently lack mechanisms to enforce map-level social norms, such as consistently keeping to the right or left side of a hallway. Consequently, paths generated by such systems, while locally safe, can appear erratic or inefficient over longer distances, potentially undermining human comfort and predictability expectations. The absence of a socially aware global planner can also overburden local planners, forcing frequent reactive maneuvers.

In the realm of multi-robot path planning (MAPF), existing solutions broadly categorize into centralized and decentralized approaches. Centralized methods, such as Conflict-Based Search (CBS) or priority motion planning, aim for globally optimal or near-optimal solutions but often face high computational complexity as the number of robots and conflicts increases. Decentralized methods, like Optimal Reciprocal Collision Avoidance (ORCA), focus on local interactions for scalability and immediate collision prevention. A common limitation across most MAPF approaches is their neglect of social conventions, which can lead to undesirable and unpredictable robot behaviors in shared human spaces.

Furthermore, learning-based approaches, including reinforcement learning (RL) and inverse reinforcement learning (IRL), have been employed to learn social cost functions or control policies from human demonstrations. While these methods can produce sophisticated behaviors, their "black-box" nature can hinder predictability and guarantees regarding robot actions. Moreover, learning-based techniques often concentrate on ego-centric local interactions rather than enforcing global, fleet-wide consistency, which is a core objective for predictable human-robot co-existence.

This research paper aims to bridge the gap between social navigation and multi-robot coordination by introducing a framework that explicitly integrates macroscopic social norms into global path planning. It seeks to ensure fleet-wide consistency and predictability, which, in turn, simplifies the complex multi-robot trajectory coordination problem. Unlike traditional MAPF methods that ignore social context or single-robot social navigation techniques that lack multi-robot coordination, this work proposes a decoupled approach that embeds social intelligence at the global planning stage and leverages the resulting structured paths for efficient, scalable multi-robot coordination.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable the successful integration and widespread adoption of autonomous mobile robots in human-centric environments by developing navigation systems that are not only safe and efficient but also predictable and aligned with human social conventions. The authors identify predictability and social consistency as critical precursors for fostering human comfort and acceptance of robotic presence.

The motivation for this work stems from several observed limitations in current robotic navigation paradigms:
- **Human Acceptance:** Human acceptance of mobile robots is highly sensitive to the quality and nature of robot motion. Aggressive, unpredictable, or socially awkward movements can lead to apprehension, distrust, or outright rejection of robots in shared spaces. Thus, developing motion planning algorithms that prioritize human comfort and understanding is essential.
- **Limitations of Local Planning:** While existing human-aware navigation research significantly contributes to local, reactive adjustments for obstacle avoidance, it often fails to induce socially preferred behaviors that are consistent and intuitive over long navigational horizons. Social norms, such as maintaining a side of a hallway, are map-level decisions that cannot be effectively enforced by local, short-term planners alone. This deficiency can lead to globally erratic or inefficient paths and places an undue burden on local planners to constantly react to unforeseen social conflicts.
- **Complexity of Multi-Robot Coordination:** Coordinating multiple mobile robots to avoid collisions is a computationally challenging combinatorial problem. Existing multi-robot coordination techniques typically overlook social conventions, which can exacerbate conflicts in human-shared environments.
- **Opportunity for Simplification:** The authors propose that rather than complicating the problem, social conventions can simplify multi-robot coordination. By guiding robots to follow structured, socially compliant paths, the number and nature of potential conflict points can be significantly reduced, making the trajectory coordination problem more tractable.
- **Enhanced Predictability:** By encouraging robots to adhere to macroscopic human behavior norms and share established routes, the framework aims to generate robot movements that humans can anticipate. This predictability is expected to reduce uncertainty for humans interacting with robots, thereby improving comfort and fostering safer coexistence.

Based on these motivations, the paper outlines three key objectives:
1. **Develop a global path planning framework** that explicitly incorporates macroscopic human social norms. This framework is designed to generate paths that are efficient while conforming to social expectations, moving beyond simply finding the shortest geometric route.
2. **Establish a mechanism for achieving fleet-wide path consistency and predictability** through inter-robot communication. This involves robots collaboratively building and adhering to a shared set of common paths, referred to as a "social graph," to ensure consistent behavior across the entire fleet and improve planning efficiency.
3. **Formulate the multi-robot coordination problem as a mixed-integer convex program (MICP).** This formulation aims to provide an efficient and scalable method for generating coordinated, conflict-free trajectories for multiple robots, leveraging the structured nature of the socially constrained paths.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework for socially consistent multi-robot navigation employs a partially decentralized, two-stage architecture that decouples global path planning from trajectory coordination. This approach assumes a known, static 2D environment represented as an occupancy grid, transformed into C-space by inflating obstacles with the robot's radius. Dynamic obstacles are handled by temporary overlays and replanning, distinguishing them from permanent environmental features.

#### 3.1. Global Path Generation

The first stage focuses on generating socially compliant geometric paths for individual robots while ensuring fleet-wide consistency.

- **Socially Compliant Path Planning for a Single Robot:**
 - A modified A\* search algorithm is used, operating on the C-space grid. A\* is selected for its flexibility in cost function definition, optimality guarantees, and deterministic nature.
 - The standard A\* cost function, `f(x) = g(x) + h(x)`, is adapted. `h(x)` is the Manhattan distance heuristic.
 - The edge cost `c(x', x)` is redefined to balance path length and social norm adherence: `c(x', x) = ||x - x'||_2 + φ(x', x)`. The `φ(x', x)` term represents the social cost.
 - The social cost `φ` translates abstract social rules into numerical penalties, specifically designed to reflect conventions observed in the United States:
 - `C_right(x)`: Enforces a "keep-right" behavior by penalizing distance to the nearest obstacle on the right.
 - `C_const(x', x)`: Promotes smooth, parallel motion by penalizing changes in distance to the right wall between consecutive steps.
 - `C_buf(x)`: Active in wide-open areas, encourages leaving a buffer for oncoming traffic by penalizing proximity to the left wall beyond a desired buffer width.
 - These social cost components are weighted (`w_right`, `w_const`, `w_buf`) and can be tuned for different environments. Weights are set to zero near start/goal positions to allow necessary maneuvers.

- **A Learned Social Graph for Fleet-Wide Path Consistency:**
 - To address the computational expense of constantly re-calculating the social cost and to enforce consistent path choices across the fleet, a collaboratively learned, directed "social graph" `H(V_H, E_H)` is introduced. This graph represents established, socially compliant routes.
 - The social graph is built and refined through a three-step dynamic process:
 1. **Path Aggregation:** Each robot broadcasts its successfully planned paths. Every robot maintains eight directional heatmaps (one for each cardinal/intercardinal direction). Path segments increment corresponding cells in these heatmaps. Directional heatmaps differentiate between traffic moving in opposite directions.
 2. **Subgraph Extraction:** Periodically, directed edges are added to `H` if the corresponding heatmap cell value exceeds a predefined threshold. Edge weights are set to Euclidean distances.
 3. **Graph Pruning:** Weakly connected components of the social graph below a minimum node size are discarded to filter out noise from transient or exploratory paths, ensuring `H` contains only significant trajectories.
 - This social graph then biases future path planning by modifying the A\* edge cost function: `c'(x', x) = ||x - x'||_2` if `(x', x)` is an edge in `E_H`, and `c'(x', x) = c(x', x) + ε` otherwise. `ε` is a small positive penalty. This encourages adherence to established social routes while allowing for exploration and adaptation to new paths if sufficiently advantageous.

#### 3.2. Decentralized Conflict-Free Trajectory Coordination

The second stage computes conflict-free trajectories for a fleet of `R` robots along their planned social paths. This is formulated as a Mixed-Integer Convex Program (MICP).

- **Problem Setup: Event Waypoints and Encounter Windows:**
 - Instead of optimizing arrival times at every path waypoint, the MICP optimizes over a sparse set of "event waypoints" `W_r`, which include start/goal points and the entry/exit points of "encounter windows" `E(r, s)`. Encounter windows are segments where two robot paths are within collision distance.
 - Robots are assumed to travel at constant velocity between consecutive event waypoints.
 - The social path planner's structured output reduces potential collision points, classifying encounter windows into three types:
 - **Intersection:** Robots cross at a point; one must fully clear before the other enters.
 - **Opposite Direction:** Robots traverse the same corridor in opposing directions; one must fully clear before the other enters.
 - **Same Direction:** Robots travel in the same direction along the same corridor; a consistent leader-follower ordering is enforced.

- **MICP Constraints:**
 - **Kinematic Constraints:** Ensure robots do not exceed their maximum speed `V_max^r` between event waypoints: `t_(k+1)^r - t_k^r >= l_k^r / V_max^r`, where `l_k^r` is the arc length. Start times `t_1^r = 0`.
 - **Collision Avoidance Constraints:** A time safety margin `δ` is enforced.
 - For intersection and opposite direction encounters, a binary variable `z_rs ∈ {0,1}` and a large constant `M` (Big-M method) are used to enforce a logical disjunction: either robot `r` exits before robot `s` enters, or vice-versa.
 - For same direction encounters, the same Big-M method and `z_rs` are used to impose a consistent leader-follower ordering throughout the entire encounter window at all relevant event waypoints.
 - Special cases exist for conflicts at start/goal waypoints, where ordering is fixed, reducing the need for binary variables.
 - `M` is dynamically computed as an upper bound on the total mission horizon to maintain numerical stability.

- **MICP Formulation:**
 - The objective is to minimize the sum of completion times (SOC) across all mobile robots (`∑_r t_K_r^r`).
 - The problem is a mixed-integer linear program (MILP), with linear objective and constraints, and binary `z_rs` variables. The number of binary variables scales with the number of non-boundary encounter windows, not path length or resolution.
 - This formulation supports sequential (new robot plans around existing trajectories), simultaneous (all robots plan together), and unified (planning robots around existing fixed trajectories) scenarios by appropriately setting decision variables as free or constant.
 - Full trajectories are recovered by linear interpolation of arc length between optimized event waypoint arrival times.

#### 3.3. Robustness and Scalability

- **Dynamic Obstacles:**
 - When a robot detects a temporary obstacle, it creates a local occupancy grid overlay.
 - The robot replans, deviating from the social graph if necessary, incurring the social cost `φ` and penalty `ε`.
 - These detour paths are tagged "temporary" and aggregated into a separate heatmap, preventing them from immediately altering the main social graph.
 - Upon obstacle removal, the robot reverts to the original social graph. If an obstacle becomes permanent, temporary paths that consistently bypass it can be promoted to the main social graph, updating `H`.

- **Distributed Computation for Scalable Trajectory Optimization:**
 - To address the `O(R^2)` complexity of pairwise collision checks for constraint generation in large fleets, a distributed computing strategy is employed. It assumes communication latency is negligible compared to collision checking computation.
 - **Sequential Planning:** The planning robot broadcasts its waypoints; other robots compute and transmit conflict constraints with their own trajectories back to the planning robot.
 - **Simultaneous Planning:** All `R` robots broadcast their paths. Each robot then uses a deterministic circular ordering scheme to check for collisions with approximately `R/2` other robots. The discovered encounter windows are broadcast, allowing each robot to locally build and solve the identical, full MICP.
 - **Unified Planning:** Combines elements of sequential and simultaneous protocols, distributing collision checks among planning robots and having already-planned robots generate constraints. This parallelization reduces the total computation time for MICP formulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted a series of simulation and hardware experiments to validate the framework's performance and characteristics.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

- **Social Planner Performance and Compliance:**
 - Comparison of the social planner against vanilla A\* (shortest path) and RRT\* (sampling-based) in three environments (two artificial, one real-world map).
 - **Path Length:** The social planner produced paths that were, on average, longer than those from A\* (e.g., 74.0m vs. 64.3m in Environment 1), which is an expected consequence of balancing efficiency with social adherence. RRT\* also yielded longer paths.
 - **Social Compliance:** The social planner demonstrated improved social compliance, maintaining a significantly smaller and more consistent average distance to the right-hand wall (e.g., 2.64m vs. 8.31m for A\* in Environment 1). RRT\* exhibited the least compliance.
 - **Computation Time:** The social planner incurred a higher computational cost (e.g., 20.7s vs. 0.348s for A\* in Environment 1) due to its more complex cost function.
 - Qualitative results (Figure 6) illustrated the social planner's adherence to "keep-right" conventions, while A\* prioritized shortest paths, often using the center or left of hallways, and RRT\* showed variable behavior.

- **Social Graph Analysis:**
 - **Formation and Convergence:** As more paths were aggregated, the social graph demonstrated the emergence of distinct, highway-like lanes, particularly along walls (Figure 7a, 7b). This indicates successful capture of social conventions and the creation of predictable routes.
 - **Dynamic Obstacles:** The social graph successfully handled temporary environmental changes. New paths rerouted around obstacles were labeled "temporary" and were not integrated into the primary social graph, preserving long-term consistency. If obstacles persist, these temporary detours can be promoted (Figure 7c).
 - **Computational Benefit:** The social graph significantly reduced social path planning time. With sufficient paths, planning time using the biased cost function with the social graph decreased to less than the vanilla A\* baseline in some environments (Figure 8). This efficiency gain is attributed to the constrained search space provided by the social graph.

- **Multi-Robot Trajectory Coordination:**
 - **Collision Avoidance:** An example with four robots at an intersection demonstrated the trajectory controller's ability to compute collision-free arrival times while optimizing total mission time (Figure 9). Faster robots allowed slower robots to proceed, and consistent ordering was maintained in same-direction encounters. Social paths concentrated potential conflicts at predictable locations.
 - **Comparison to Baseline Approaches:**
 - The MICP approach was compared against Conflict-Based Search (CBS) and Priority Planning (PP) methods for varying numbers of robots.
 - **Success Rate:** The MICP approach exhibited a higher success rate (approaching 100%) compared to CBS and PP, especially for larger fleets (Figure 10a).
 - **Solver Runtime:** The MICP solver demonstrated low and scalable runtime (approximately one second even for 10 robots), significantly outperforming CBS and PP, whose runtimes increased more rapidly (Figure 10b). This efficiency is attributed to the reduced number of binary variables due to fewer conflicts along social paths.
 - **Makespan:** The MICP solution produced competitive makespans, even though social paths may not be geometrically shortest, due to efficient pipelining facilitated by structured social routes (Figure 10c).
 - **Overlapping Path Segments:** The social planner substantially reduced the number of conflicting (non-same-direction) overlapping path segments compared to A\* and RRT\*, simplifying the trajectory coordination problem (Figure 11).
 - **Scalability of Trajectory Optimization:**
 - **Constraint Generation Time:** In simultaneous planning, centralized constraint generation showed quadratic growth (`O(R^2)`) with the number of robots, while the distributed approach (parallelizing collision checks) exhibited linear growth (Figure 12). This demonstrated significant computational savings for large fleets by leveraging the fleet's collective computing power.

#### 4.2. Hardware Experiments

Experiments were conducted on custom differential drive mobile robots in an academic office building environment (Stanford Y2E2 building map).

- **Head-On Traffic and Intersection Crossings:**
 - **Head-on Hallway Traffic:** With the social planner, robots naturally stayed to the right side of the hallway, avoiding collisions without explicit trajectory coordination, leading to minimal navigation times. In contrast, A\* paths often led to head-on conflicts requiring significant delays (Figure 14).
 - **Intersection Negotiation:** The framework successfully managed intersection scenarios for two and three robots. The MICP formulation dictated that only one robot occupy the intersection at a time, with others waiting to provide a clear path (Figure 15). These demonstrations confirmed the effectiveness of social path planning and trajectory coordination on physical hardware.
- **Communication Latency and Distributed Computing:**
 - Communication latencies (via Wi-Fi based DDS) were consistently less than 100ms, which was significantly shorter than the typical collision constraint generation time (>200ms for a single pair of robots).
 - The distributed constraint generation approach yielded an average speedup of at least 50% for three robots, with the speedup increasing as the number of robots grew (Table 3), underscoring its practical benefit in reducing overall computation time.
- **Local Planner Considerations:** The authors acknowledge that a human-aware local planner is necessary to complement the global framework, addressing localization noise, actuation errors, and dynamic objects in real-world deployments. The framework supports replanning (sequential, unified, or simultaneous) for significant deviations or new obstacles, allowing robots to reintegrate with the existing collision-free trajectories.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for multi-robot navigation that aims to enhance the safety, efficiency, and social acceptability of autonomous mobile robots in human-centric environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Improved Human-Robot Coexistence:** By explicitly embedding macroscopic social norms into global path planning and enforcing fleet-wide consistency, the framework generates robot motions that are predictable and aligned with human expectations. This predictability is critical for increasing human comfort, trust, and ultimately, the widespread acceptance and seamless integration of robots into shared spaces.
- **Enhanced Navigation Predictability and Consistency:** The introduction of a learned social graph, built collaboratively by the robot fleet, ensures that all robots adhere to a shared set of established, socially compliant routes. This mechanism promotes predictable behavior across the entire fleet over long horizons, addressing a limitation of prior work focused solely on local reactive planning.
- **Scalable Multi-Robot Coordination:** The work demonstrates that enforcing social consistency at the path planning stage simplifies the inherently complex multi-robot trajectory coordination problem. By concentrating potential conflicts at fewer, well-defined locations (like intersections rather than arbitrary path overlaps), the Mixed-Integer Convex Program (MICP) formulation becomes more tractable and scalable. This is particularly evident in the lower solver runtime and higher success rate compared to traditional multi-robot pathfinding algorithms like CBS and Priority Planning.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The social graph not only ensures path consistency but also significantly reduces the computational overhead of social path planning by biasing the A\* search towards established routes. Furthermore, the proposed distributed computation protocols for constraint generation in the MICP effectively parallelize collision checks, reducing the total problem formulation and solution time for large robot fleets, thereby improving scalability.
- **Robustness to Dynamic Environments:** The framework incorporates strategies to handle temporary dynamic obstacles by generating temporary detour paths without permanently altering the learned social graph. This allows for both immediate reactive responses and long-term adaptation to permanent environmental changes, maintaining the integrity of learned social conventions.
- **Flexible Deployment Scenarios:** The MICP formulation is flexible enough to support various real-world deployment scenarios, including sequential dispatch of new robots, simultaneous planning for an entire fleet, or unified planning where new robots must navigate around existing fixed trajectories.

In conclusion, this research offers a practical and principled approach to designing multi-robot systems that operate in a manner considerate of human social conventions. By decoupling global, socially aware path planning from trajectory coordination, the authors have developed a system that generates scalable, predictable, and socially compliant fleet-wide behavior. This work contributes to the development of autonomous systems that not only share spaces with humans but also respect and reinforce human social conventions, paving the way for safer and more harmonious human-robot interactions.
