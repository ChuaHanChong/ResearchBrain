# Beyond Robustness: Learning Unknown Dynamic Load Adaptation for Quadruped Locomotion on Rough Terrain

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Zhejiang University developed an RL-control framework that enables quadruped robots to adapt to unknown dynamic loads on rough terrain by inferring load characteristics solely from proprioceptive sensors. The method demonstrated improved locomotion stability and load stabilization in simulations, performing comparably to a model with direct load information, and achieved successful zero-shot transfer to a physical robot.

## Problem

- Quadruped robots face challenges in maintaining stability and maneuverability when carrying unknown dynamic loads that induce sudden shifts in the robot's center of mass.
- Existing methods either oversimplify dynamic loads as general disturbances or rely on restrictive assumptions about load properties, primarily focusing on static loads.
- There is a need to capture complex load dynamics and the mutual interaction between robot and load using only internal sensors, especially for operation on rough terrains.

## Method

![[2507.07825-method.png]]
*Overview of the proposed framework for dynamic load adaptation on the quadruped robot.*

- A **LoadAdapt** reinforcement learning control framework was developed, integrating a novel ==load characteristics modeling== approach to represent dynamic loads using properties like relative position, velocity, mass, and friction coefficient.
- The framework employs a ==teacher-student architecture== with concurrent training, utilizing a ==privileged encoder== for full state information, a ==proprioceptive encoder==, and a dedicated ==Load Characteristics Estimator== to infer load dynamics from proprioceptive observations.
- A tailored ==reward function== encourages stable locomotion and actively minimizes the linear velocity of the dynamic load relative to the robot base, promoting load stabilization.

## Results

- In simulation experiments, the proposed method significantly reduced linear velocity tracking error and base roll deviation across various terrains compared to robustness-based baselines, performing close to an Oracle model with direct load information.
- The robot achieved faster settling times for its orientation and effectively stabilized dynamic loads during sudden load appearance in stationary tests, outperforming comparative methods.
- Successful zero-shot transfer to a physical Unitree Go2 quadruped robot was demonstrated, showing the ability to traverse uneven terrain and adapt to lead balls of various masses (e.g., **4 kg**, **6 kg**) using only proprioceptive information.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Traditional robustness-based control methods are insufficient for effectively handling heavy, unknown, and dynamically shifting loads, often leading to instability or failure.
> - Explicitly modeling dynamic load characteristics (mass, position, velocity, friction) within an RL framework enables the robot to capture and adapt to complex load behaviors.
> - Quadruped robots can accurately infer unknown dynamic load characteristics and adapt their locomotion solely from proprioceptive sensing, eliminating the need for external load sensors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Quadruped robots are subjects of extensive research due to their potential for operation in complex and unstructured environments, such as those involving slopes, stairs, and gravel. A critical requirement in many practical applications for these robots is the ability to carry loads while maintaining maneuverability and balance. Specifically, interactions with dynamic loads, which can roll or slide, pose a significant challenge. These dynamic loads can induce sudden shifts in the robot's center of mass (CoM), leading to variations in robot state, which in turn affect the load state. The constantly changing nature of these loads and the mutual interaction between the load and the robot necessitate advanced control strategies for dynamic load adaptation to maintain stability during locomotion.

Prior research on load handling in quadruped robots has generally adopted two main approaches. The first treats the effects of the load as a disturbance, relying on the inherent robustness of the controller, often enhanced by techniques like domain randomization. This perspective simplistically views the load without modeling its underlying dynamics. The second approach involves making restrictive assumptions about the load properties and designing controllers based on these assumptions. A common limitation across many of these methods is their focus on static loads, neglecting the complex dynamics of moving loads and the intricate mutual effects between the load and robot dynamics. Furthermore, much of the existing work does not simultaneously consider the challenges posed by dynamic loads and rough terrain.

This research addresses these gaps by proposing a new perspective that integrates the capture of load dynamics with the robot-load mutual effects. It aims to develop a general policy for highly dynamic load handling, specifically focusing on unknown dynamic loads carried on a crate mounted on the robot base, using only proprioceptive information. By leveraging recent advancements in Reinforcement Learning (RL), the work seeks to enable robots to infer load dynamics and adapt to them, distinguishing itself from robustness-based methods that do not explicitly account for load dynamics. The inclusion of rough terrain scenarios in both training and evaluation further positions this work to advance the practical applicability of quadruped robots in challenging real-world tasks involving dynamic payload transport.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an RL-control framework that enables a quadruped robot to effectively manage sustained and varying external forces resulting from unknown dynamic loads. The framework aims to stabilize the load on the robot while the robot locomotes across rough terrains, relying exclusively on proprioceptive sensing (from an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) and joint encoders) without any external sensing information about the load.

The motivation for this work stems from the practical demands of real-world applications where quadruped robots may need to transport dynamic objects. For instance, a delivery robot might carry a heavy, spherical object that shifts its position with changes in the robot's base pose. Such scenarios highlight three major challenges in quadruped locomotion control that this paper specifically addresses:

1. **Modeling or Representing Load Dynamics:** The first challenge is to devise a generic method to model or represent the dynamics of a diverse range of unknown loads. Existing methods either oversimplify loads as disturbances or impose restrictive assumptions, which are inadequate for dynamic and unknown payloads.
2. **Capturing Load Dynamics Without External Sensing:** The second objective is to enable the robot to infer or capture the dynamics of the carried load solely through its internal sensors (proprioception). This is crucial for real-world deployment where external load sensors might be impractical, unreliable, or unavailable.
3. **Managing Robot-Load Mutual Effects and Stabilizing the Load:** The third challenge involves empowering the robot to interact with the dynamic load, handling the reciprocal influences between robot movement and load state, and actively stabilizing the load to prevent loss or instability.

The authors are motivated to move beyond mere robustness (which treats loads as general disturbances) towards an adaptive approach that understands and responds to the underlying dynamics of the load. By focusing on a crate-based dynamic load scenario, the research aims to address a configuration relevant to practical applications where animals already carry shifting loads, reflecting both complexity and utility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on an RL-control framework designed for dynamic load adaptation. This framework integrates a novel load modeling technique with advanced RL training architectures to enable a quadruped robot to infer unknown load dynamics and stabilize the load during locomotion on rough terrain, using only proprioceptive observations.

**A. General Load Modeling:**
The core of the approach is the "load characteristics modeling." This method represents a dynamic load using a set of characteristics: its position relative to the robot, its velocity relative to the robot (both in the robot's frame), its mass, and its friction coefficient. The rationale is that load mass significantly alters the total system mass, while load position influences the system's Center of Mass (CoM) shift. Load velocity and friction coefficient govern the rate and manner in which these influences on system dynamics change. This comprehensive yet concise representation aims to generalize how dynamic loads are incorporated into the learning framework.

**B. Proposed Training Framework:**
The training framework employs a teacher-student architecture, concurrent training techniques, and an asymmetric actor-critic design. It operates in two phases:

1. **Teacher-student Training Phase:**
 - **Observation Inputs:**
 - `o_t`: Proprioceptive observations (angular velocity, gravity vector, velocity command, joint positions, joint velocities, previous action). A history `o_t-H:t` (with H=15) is used for time-series context.
 - `s_t`: Full robot states, including `o_t`, linear velocity, terrain height samples, joint torques, joint accelerations, and feet contact forces.
 - `p_t`: Dynamics randomization parameters (e.g., PD control gains, motor strength, leg mass/CoM, base mass/CoM).
 - `l_t`: Ground truth load characteristics (position, velocity, mass, friction coefficient) obtained directly from the simulator.
 - **Network Components:**
 - **Privileged Encoder (E_p_theta):** Compresses `s_t` and `p_t` into a latent vector `z_t`. This `z_t` is L2-normalized onto a unit hypersphere.
 - **Actor Network (pi_theta):** Receives the latent vector `z_t` (or `z_s_t` in the student phase), proprioception `o_t`, and estimated load characteristics `hat_l_t` as input to output actions `a_t`.
 - **Critic Network (V_phi):** Receives `s_t`, `z_t`, ground truth load characteristics `l_t`, and dynamics randomization `p_t` as input to estimate the value `hat_V_t`.
 - **Proprioceptive Encoder (E_s_theta):** Trained to reconstruct `z_t` as `z_s_t` from `o_t-H:t` using a reconstruction loss, `L_rec(theta) = ||z_s_t - z_t||^2_2`. This teaches the student to implicitly infer privileged information from proprioception.
 - **Load Characteristics Estimator (E_l_theta):** Crucially, this estimator is trained *concurrently* with the main RL policy. It estimates load characteristics `hat_l_t` from `o_t-H:t` using a load estimation loss, `L_est(theta) = w_l * (hat_l_t - l_t)^2_2`. This enables the robot to infer unknown load dynamics without direct sensing.
 - **Training Algorithm:** The privileged encoder, actor, and critic are trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. The proprioceptive encoder and load estimator are updated through supervised learning with their respective losses.

2. **Student Reinforcing Phase:**
 Following the teacher-student phase, the student policy (comprising `E_s_theta` and `pi_theta`) undergoes further fine-tuning using PPO in the same simulation environment. This phase aims to mitigate performance degradation caused by the minor discrepancies between the reconstructed latent state `z_s_t` and the privileged `z_t`, thereby reinforcing the student policy with direct reward signals.

**C. Reward Design:**
The reward function includes standard locomotion-related terms derived from previous work (e.g., linear/angular velocity tracking, penalties for joint acceleration/power/torque, base height deviation, action rate, collisions, joint limits, and rewards for feet air time and contact forces). A specific reward term, `Load lin. velocity` (`1/(1+v_load)`), is introduced to encourage the robot to minimize the linear velocity of the load (`v_load`) relative to its base, thus actively stabilizing the dynamic payload. An emergent behavior observed was the robot naturally maintaining a flat base to stabilize the load, without an explicit orientation reward.

**D. Details of Training Setting:**
- **Network Architecture:** All network modules are Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) with Exponential Linear Unit (ELU) activation functions.
- **Simulated Environment:** IssacGym simulator is used for parallel training with 8192 agents. Episodes last 20 seconds (1000 timesteps at 50 Hz control frequency). Episode termination occurs on robot fall, timeout, or load fall.
- **Robot Model:** Unitree Go2 quadruped with specified PD controller parameters.
- **Terrain Curriculum:** Training utilizes various terrain types, including stairs, discrete obstacles, rough terrains, and smooth slopes, with specified maximum obstacle heights.
- **Dynamic Load Setup:** A 0.6 m × 0.8 m plate is attached to the robot base, carrying a dynamic cube load whose initial position, velocity, mass (ranging from 0.001 to 8 kg), size, and friction coefficient are randomized.
- **Sim-to-Real Gap Mitigation:** Extensive domain randomization is applied to robot parameters (link/base mass/CoM, friction, PD gains, motor strength, action delay) and load parameters. Noise is added to proprioceptive observations, and the robot is subjected to random pushes to enhance robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed method against three comparative policies: the Non-load-privileged Walk Model (NLW), the Load-privileged Walk Model (LW), and an Oracle model. All comparative models utilize a two-stage teacher-student pipeline and an asymmetric actor-critic architecture with identical training configurations where applicable.

- **NLW:** A robustness-based baseline, where load characteristics are excluded from privileged observations, and no load-related rewards are used.
- **LW:** Load characteristics are available as privileged information to the critic and privileged encoder, with load-related rewards.
- **Oracle:** The actor network has direct access to ground truth load characteristics, incorporating load-related rewards. This represents an upper bound on performance with perfect load information.
- **Ours:** The proposed method, where the load estimator provides estimated load characteristics to the actor, using only proprioceptive sensing during deployment.

**A. Evaluation with Simulation:**

1. **Dynamic Experiment:**
 Experiments were conducted across plane, stair, rough terrain, and slope environments with a 7 kg load and 0.01 friction coefficient while the robot was commanded to move at 1 m/s.
 - **Linear Velocity Tracking Error (Fig. 6a):** The proposed method demonstrated significantly lower velocity tracking error and reduced variance across all four terrains compared to NLW and LW, performing comparably to the Oracle model. LW exhibited a notable failure on rough terrain, where the robot fell.
 - **Base Roll Deviation (Fig. 6b):** The proposed method maintained a substantially more stable robot base orientation than NLW and LW, achieving performance consistent with the Oracle model.
 - **Load Velocity in Robot Base Frame (Fig. 6c):** The proposed model displayed lower load velocity and reduced variance relative to the robot base, closely matching the Oracle model, indicating superior dynamic load stabilization.

2. **Stationary Experiment:**
 This experiment assessed the robot's ability to adapt to a sudden appearance of a dynamic load (7 kg box, 0.02 friction, 0.2 m/s initial velocity) while standing still.
 - **Robot Adaptation of Orientation (Roll angle variation, Fig. 7a):** The proposed method achieved the shortest settling time for the robot's roll orientation upon load appearance. In contrast, NLW and LW showed divergent or highly unstable roll orientations.
 - **Load Velocity Relative to the Robot (Fig. 7b):** The proposed method effectively stabilized the dynamic load after an initial adaptation period, exhibiting improved adaptability compared to NLW and LW.
 - **Load Trajectory (Fig. 8):** The load trajectory under the proposed method was observed to be much denser, indicating enhanced stabilization performance compared to the other methods.

Based on these simulation results, the following conclusions were drawn:
- **Q1 (Robustness-based training sufficiency):** The observed robot falls (LW on rough terrain), rapid orientation variations, and high load velocities in NLW and LW indicate that robustness-based methods are insufficient for tasks involving heavy, dynamic, and unknown loads.
- **Q2 (Contribution of load characteristics modeling):** The performance of the Oracle model, which had direct access to load characteristics, strongly supports the effectiveness of the proposed load characteristics model in enabling the robot to capture load dynamics.
- **Q3 (Accuracy of load characteristics inference from proprioception):** The proposed method, relying solely on proprioceptive sensing, achieved performance levels close to the Oracle model across various metrics (velocity tracking, orientation adaptation, and load stabilization) in both dynamic complex terrain and stationary scenarios.

**B. Physical Validation:**
The research included sim-to-real deployment on a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot. The robot successfully traversed uneven and soft terrain while carrying a 4 kg dynamic lead ball (Fig. 1 and Fig. 5). Additional real-world stationary experiments involved 2 kg, 4 kg, and 6 kg lead balls falling onto the robot. These physical experiments demonstrated the zero-shot sim-to-real transferability of the proposed method, confirming its ability to enable the robot to adapt to unknown dynamic loads using only proprioception during both complex terrain traversal and static balancing.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that significantly advances the capabilities of quadruped robots in handling unknown dynamic loads on rough terrain, an area with notable practical relevance and existing limitations.

The **significance** of this work lies in several key contributions:
1. **Addressing a Critical Gap:** It moves beyond conventional robustness-based or static load adaptation strategies by explicitly modeling and inferring unknown dynamic load characteristics, thereby enabling genuine adaptation rather than mere disturbance rejection.
2. **Novel Load Modeling:** The "load characteristics modeling" approach offers a general and effective way to represent dynamic loads within an RL framework, making complex load dynamics learnable without requiring direct measurement.
3. **Enhanced Autonomy and Perception:** By demonstrating that robots can infer load characteristics and adapt to them using only proprioceptive information, the work eliminates the need for specialized external load sensors, enhancing robot autonomy and reducing system complexity.
4. **Improved Locomotion Performance:** The developed framework enables quadruped robots to maintain stable locomotion, command velocity tracking, and significantly stabilize dynamic payloads even on challenging terrains, outperforming methods that do not explicitly account for load dynamics.
5. **Proven Sim-to-Real Transferability:** The successful zero-shot transfer of the trained policy from simulation to a physical robot validates the robustness and practical applicability of the approach.

The **potential impact** of this research is substantial, particularly for applications requiring quadruped robots to operate in unstructured environments while transporting variable or unstable cargo. This includes:
- **Logistics and Delivery:** Robots could reliably transport packages that might shift or roll, such as in last-mile delivery services over varied urban or natural landscapes.
- **Hazardous Environments:** Deployment in disaster relief, search and rescue, or exploration tasks where robots need to carry equipment or samples that might be dynamic or whose properties are unknown.
- **Industrial Applications:** Facilitating automated material handling in factories or construction sites where loads might be irregularly shaped or subject to movement during transport.

Furthermore, the methodological integration of concurrent training and teacher-student architectures with specific domain randomization strategies for dynamic loads offers a blueprint for addressing similar complex perception-control challenges in robotics. The self-reflection on future work, such as investigating the representational benefits of load characteristics over direct wrench dynamics and improving performance on sloped terrain, indicates a continued commitment to advancing the field.
