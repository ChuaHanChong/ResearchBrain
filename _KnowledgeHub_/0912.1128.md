# How to Explain Individual Classification Decisions

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Technische Universität Berlin developed a general method to explain individual classification decisions made by diverse "black box" machine learning models. The approach generates local explanation vectors that identify which input features are most influential for a specific prediction, successfully confirming existing domain knowledge and revealing novel context-dependent insights in applications like chemical mutagenicity prediction.

## Problem

- Modern machine learning classifiers often operate as "black boxes," providing accurate predictions but lacking explanations for individual decisions.
- Current interpretation methods primarily offer global feature importance, failing to capture instance-specific influences and local feature interactions.
- A general framework for instance-based explanations, applicable to a wide range of nonlinear classifiers beyond decision trees, was missing.

## Method

- A framework defining "**local explanation vectors**" as the ==local gradient== of a classifier's probability function with respect to its input features.
- For inherently probabilistic classifiers, these vectors are calculated analytically from the model's differentiable components.
- For non-probabilistic classifiers, a ==Parzen window estimator== is used to create a mimicking probabilistic classifier whose gradients can then be computed.

## Results

- Local explanation vectors effectively visualized instance-specific feature contributions on synthetic data and real datasets like Iris and USPS digits.
- The method precisely differentiated the reasons behind classifications, for instance, explaining why both Iris setosa and Iris virginica are "not Iris versicolor" through distinct petal size contributions.
- For Ames mutagenicity, the approach confirmed known toxicophores/detoxicophores and notably discovered that epoxides in steroid compounds showed near-zero mutagenicity influence, consistent with specialized chemical literature.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Local gradients of class probability functions provide a direct, quantitative measure of instance-specific feature influence on classification decisions.
> - Explaining opaque classifiers is achievable by mimicking their decision boundaries with probabilistic models from which local gradients can be derived.
> - Instance-based explanations uncover localized feature interactions and context-dependent behaviors that global feature importance methods often miss.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Modern machine learning has developed powerful classification tools that often act as "black boxes," providing accurate predictions for unseen data without explaining the reasoning behind individual decisions. This lack of interpretability is a significant challenge in many practical applications where understanding *why* a particular classification was made is as crucial as the prediction itself.

Existing methods for model interpretation typically offer a global or ensemble view of feature importance. For instance, feature selection techniques (e.g., Automatic Relevance Determination) identify features generally salient for good generalization across the entire dataset. Similarly, input pruning in neural networks assesses the overall utility of input dimensions. While these methods provide a coarse impression of feature usefulness, they do not offer instance-specific explanations. Decision trees are acknowledged as the primary classification method currently capable of providing individual explanations by tracing the path a data point takes through the tree.

This paper addresses the gap in providing *instance-based explanations* for *any* classification method, moving beyond global feature saliency to offer insights into local decision-making. It aims to generalize the concept of individual explanations, which has previously been limited to specific model types like decision trees, to a broader range of nonlinear "black box" classifiers.

The proposed methodology is conceptually related to sensitivity analysis, a technique common in various scientific and engineering disciplines. Sensitivity analysis evaluates the effect of changes in input variables or data points on a model's output or estimated parameters. For example, in statistics, outlier sensitivity and leverage analysis assess the impact of individual data points. In the AI community, particularly for expert systems based on Bayesian belief networks, sensitivity analysis has been used to identify influential variables for inference. Related work by Robnik-Šikonja and Kononenko (2008) and Štrumbelj and Kononenko (2008) explores explaining individual instance classifications by measuring prediction differences when subsets of features are omitted. Similarly, Féraud and Clérot (2002) and Lemaire and Feraud (2007) examined the importance of individual input variables for neural network outputs.

This work distinguishes itself from these related approaches by:
1. Considering continuous features without requiring specific structural assumptions among them, unlike some frameworks that start with binary features or necessitate discretization.
2. Allowing for changes in *any* direction in the input space (i.e., weighted combinations of variables), in contrast to methods that primarily consider single-feature perturbations or the omission of feature sets. This allows for a more comprehensive understanding of feature interactions at a local level.

By proposing a general framework for generating local explanation vectors, this research positions itself as a foundational step towards making a broader class of complex machine learning models more transparent and interpretable on a per-instance basis.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a general procedure that can explain individual classification decisions made by *any* classification method, particularly nonlinear "black box" models. This initiative is driven by a recognized need in practical data analysis: while modern machine learning algorithms excel at predicting labels for unseen data, they typically do not provide insight into *why* a particular label was assigned to a specific instance or which input features were most influential for that decision.

The detailed objectives and motivations include:

1. **Addressing the "Black Box" Problem:** Modern nonlinear classifiers, despite their high predictive accuracy, are often opaque. This opacity limits their utility in domains where transparency and trust are paramount, such as medical diagnosis, financial risk assessment, and drug discovery. The paper aims to shed light into these black boxes by providing concrete, instance-specific explanations.

2. **Providing Instance-Based Explanations:** Current explanation methods predominantly offer a global or ensemble view of feature importance, indicating which features are generally relevant for a model's overall performance. However, features influential for one instance might be different for another. The paper's core motivation is to move beyond this global perspective and provide local explanation vectors that highlight features relevant for the prediction at a specific data point, thereby capturing local peculiarities and feature interactions that might be obscured in a global view due to cancellation effects.

3. **Generalizability Across Classifiers:** The paper seeks a framework applicable to *any* classification method. Recognizing that only decision trees inherently provide individual explanations, a key objective is to extend this capability to other powerful models like Support Vector Machines (SVMs) and Gaussian Process Classifiers (GPCs), which typically do not offer such insights directly.

4. **Enabling Deeper Understanding of Model Behavior:** By identifying features that most influence a prediction for a single instance, the method aims to foster a better understanding of how the classifier operates in different regions of the data space. This includes understanding the impact of increasing or decreasing specific feature values on the predicted class label.

5. **Facilitating Domain-Specific Insights and Discovery:** In application-oriented fields, human experts often need to interpret model predictions to make informed decisions or gain new scientific knowledge. For example, in drug discovery, understanding which molecular substructures contribute to a compound's toxicity can guide chemical modifications. The motivation includes developing a tool that can not only confirm existing domain knowledge but also uncover novel, context-dependent relationships.

In essence, the motivation stems from the practical necessity of augmenting powerful predictive models with interpretable mechanisms, thereby enhancing their trustworthiness, utility, and potential for scientific discovery across various complex domains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on defining and calculating "local explanation vectors" which quantify the influence of individual features on a classifier's decision for a specific data instance. The approach is bifurcated: one for classifiers that directly provide class probabilities and another for those that do not.

#### 3.1. Core Concept: Local Explanation Vectors as Gradients

The fundamental idea is to use the local gradient of a classifier's probability function with respect to its input features.

- **For Multi-class Bayes Classification (Definition 1):**
 For a data point $x_0$ and its predicted Bayes class $g^*(x_0)$, the explanation vector $\zeta(x_0)$ is defined as the derivative of the conditional probability $P(Y \neq g^*(x_0) | X=x)$ with respect to $x$ at $x=x_0$. This $d$-dimensional vector (where $d$ is the number of features) indicates how small changes in each feature of $x_0$ would alter the probability of $x_0$ belonging to a class *other than* its predicted class. Large absolute values in $\zeta(x_0)$ highlight influential features, with the sign indicating the direction of influence.

- **For Binary Classification (Definition 2):**
 For a probability function $p(x) = P(Y=1 | X=x)$ (the probability of belonging to the positive class), the explanation vector $\eta_p(x_0)$ for a test point $x_0$ is simply the local gradient $\nabla p(x)|_{x=x_0}$. Each entry in $\eta_p(x_0)$ shows whether increasing that feature would increase or decrease the probability of the positive class, and its absolute value quantifies the magnitude of this influence. The vector $\eta_p(x_0)$ points in the direction of the steepest ascent towards higher probabilities for the positive class.

#### 3.2. Application to Probabilistic Classifiers (e.g., Gaussian Process Classification - GPC)

For classifiers that inherently provide a probability function, such as GPC, the explanation vectors can be calculated analytically if the kernel function is differentiable.
- **GPC Model:** A GPC model estimates a latent function $f(x) = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i k(x, x_i)$ and its variance $var_f(x)$. The probability for the positive class $p(x_0)$ is then derived using a likelihood term (e.g., probit likelihood with the complementary error function).
- **Analytical Gradient:** The paper provides a detailed derivation for $\nabla p(x)|_{x=x_0}$ for GPC with a probit likelihood and an RBF kernel $k(x_0, x_1) = \exp(-w(x_0 - x_1)^2)$. This involves taking derivatives of the error function and the kernel function with respect to each feature dimension. This approach ensures that the explanation directly reflects the internal workings of the GPC model.

#### 3.3. Estimating Explanation Vectors for Non-Probabilistic Classifiers (e.g., Support Vector Machines - SVM)

For classifiers like SVMs that output a decision score rather than a direct probability, an estimation framework is proposed.
- **Mimicking the Classifier:** The core idea is to approximate the behavior of the target classifier $g$ (which lacks direct probability interpretation) with another classifier $\hat{g}$ that *does* provide probability estimates and for which gradients can be computed. This $\hat{g}$ is chosen to mimic the Bayes classifier's form.
- **Parzen Window Estimator:** The paper utilizes a Parzen window estimator to construct $\hat{g}$.
 - Weighted class densities $\hat{p}_\sigma(x, y=c)$ are estimated for each class $c$ using Gaussian kernels centered at training points $x_i$ labeled by the target classifier $g$.
 - These densities are then used to estimate the conditional probability $\hat{p}_\sigma(y=c|x)$.
 - The mimicking classifier $\hat{g}_\sigma(x)$ is defined as the class with the minimum estimated probability of not belonging to that class.
- **Hyperparameter Selection:** The kernel width $\sigma$ for the Parzen windows is a crucial hyperparameter. It is selected by minimizing the misclassification error between the target classifier $g$ and the mimicking classifier $\hat{g}_\sigma$ on a test set. This ensures that $\hat{g}_\sigma$ accurately approximates the decisions of $g$.
- **Definition 3:** The explanation vector $\hat{\zeta}(z)$ for an instance $z$ is then calculated as the derivative of $\hat{p}(y \neq g(z)|x)$ with respect to $x$ at $x=z$. The detailed derivation involves the quotient rule for derivatives of $\hat{p}_\sigma(y=c|x)$.
- **Advantages of Mimicking:** This approach allows for generating an arbitrary amount of labeled data from the classifier $g$ (by querying $g$ on unlabeled points) to robustly train the mimicking classifier $\hat{g}$, especially in high-dimensional settings.

#### 3.4. Interpretation and Visualization

The $d$-dimensional explanation vectors can be directly interpreted. A positive value for a feature dimension indicates that increasing that feature's value would increase the probability of the current class (for positive class gradient) or decrease the probability of the predicted class (for multi-class definition). The magnitude reflects importance. For visualization, these vectors can be displayed as "feature maps" (e.g., pixel-wise gradients for images) or used to generate "artificial" instances by moving along the gradient direction, illustrating how changes in features affect classification.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper demonstrates the utility of local explanation vectors across several datasets and classification models, from synthetic examples to real-world applications.

#### 4.1. Explaining Simple Object Classification with GPC

Using a 2D synthetic dataset with a triangular class boundary, the authors illustrate that GPC can model the class probabilities. The local explanation vectors, derived analytically, clearly show how feature importance varies across the input space. At the corners and hypotenuse of the triangle, both features contribute to the classification, while along the straight edges, one feature dominates. This visually confirms the instance-specific nature of the explanations, showing how the classifier's reliance on features changes locally.

#### 4.2. Explaining Iris Flower Classification by k-Nearest Neighbors

A k-NN classifier was applied to the classic Iris dataset, distinguishing Iris versicolor (class 0) from Iris setosa and Iris virginica (class 1). The k-NN classifier's decisions were mimicked using a Parzen window estimator.
- The most influential features for distinguishing Iris species were identified as petal length and petal width, which together approximate petal area.
- Crucially, the explanation vectors revealed *different reasons* for why Iris setosa and Iris virginica were classified as "not Iris versicolor." Specifically:
 - Iris setosa was different due to its *smaller* petal area.
 - Iris virginica was different due to its *larger* petal area.
This finding highlights the method's ability to provide nuanced, instance-specific insights that aggregate global feature importance methods would miss.

#### 4.3. Explaining USPS Digit Classification by Support Vector Machine

An SVM was trained to classify 'twos' versus 'eights' from the USPS digit dataset. The SVM's decisions were explained by mimicking its output with a Parzen window classifier.
- The explanation vectors for individual digits were visualized as images, showing "edit instructions" (pixels to add or remove) that would transform a digit from one class to the other.
- For a 'two' classified as such, the explanation vector highlighted missing lines or parts that, if added (e.g., closing a loop), would make it resemble an 'eight'. Conversely, for an 'eight', the vectors showed parts to remove to make it a 'two'.
- This demonstrated that even for high-dimensional image data, the method provides interpretable insights into the specific pixel configurations crucial for a given classification decision.

#### 4.4. Explaining Mutagenicity Classification by Gaussian Processes

In a challenging real-world application, the method was used to explain GPC predictions of Ames mutagenicity for 6512 chemical compounds, represented by 142 molecular substructure counts.
- **Validation with Domain Knowledge:**
 - Known *toxicophores* (substructures known to cause mutagenicity, e.g., aromatic nitro, epoxide) consistently exhibited positive local gradients, indicating that their presence increased the predicted probability of mutagenicity.
 - Known *detoxicophores* (substructures known to reduce mutagenicity, e.g., sulfonamide, sulfonic acid) consistently showed negative local gradients, indicating their presence decreased the predicted probability of mutagenicity.
 - Ranking features by their mean local gradients successfully placed most known toxicophores near the top and detoxicophores near the bottom of the list, confirming agreement with established chemical domain knowledge.
- **Discovery of Local Peculiarities:** The method uncovered a specific context where global trends did not apply.
 - For *steroid compounds*, the presence of an *epoxide* substructure (a known toxicophore globally) was found to exhibit near-zero or even slightly negative local gradients. This suggests that for steroids, epoxides do not increase mutagenicity, aligning with established pharmaceutical literature concerning the "immunity" of steroids to this toxicophore.
 - A similar, though less pronounced, effect was observed for aliphatic nitrosamine in steroids.
This demonstrates the method's capability not only to validate existing knowledge but also to potentially discover novel, context-dependent scientific insights.

In summary, the results consistently show that local explanation vectors provide valuable, instance-specific insights into classifier decisions, revealing underlying feature importance and interactions that are crucial for understanding complex models and making informed decisions in scientific and practical domains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of interpretable machine learning by addressing the "black box" nature of many powerful nonlinear classifiers. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhancing Trust and Transparency in AI Systems:** By providing instance-specific explanations, the method increases the transparency of machine learning models. This is particularly crucial in sensitive domains like healthcare, finance, and legal applications, where understanding the rationale behind a decision is essential for user trust, regulatory compliance, and accountability.

2. **General Applicability to Diverse Classifiers:** The framework is designed to work with virtually *any* classification method, either directly (for probabilistic models with differentiable outputs) or indirectly through the approximation framework (for non-probabilistic or opaque models). This broad applicability means that practitioners are not limited to inherently interpretable models like decision trees when detailed explanations are required.

3. **Uncovering Localized and Nuanced Insights:** Unlike global feature importance methods, the proposed approach reveals which features are most influential for *individual* predictions and how their influence changes across the feature space. This capacity to identify local peculiarities (e.g., the differential impact of petal size for different Iris species, or the "immunity" of steroids to certain toxicophores) can lead to a deeper, more nuanced understanding of the data and the underlying processes.

4. **Facilitating Scientific Discovery and Hypothesis Generation:** The application to Ames mutagenicity prediction demonstrated the method's ability to not only confirm existing domain knowledge (e.g., identifying known toxicophores and detoxicophores) but also to uncover previously undiscussed or subtle phenomena (e.g., the specific behavior of epoxides in steroid compounds). This capability can serve as a powerful tool for scientists to generate new hypotheses and guide further experimental investigation in fields like chemistry, biology, and materials science.

5. **Guiding Practical Decision-Making:** In applied settings, explanations are critical for human experts to make informed decisions. For example, in drug discovery, understanding which substructures contribute to a compound's predicted toxicity can guide medicinal chemists in modifying lead compounds to improve safety profiles. In image analysis, knowing which pixel regions led to a classification can assist in refining image features or understanding perceptual biases.

6. **Potential for Broader Integration:** The authors envision the method becoming a standard tool in practitioners' toolboxes, particularly in computational biology (e.g., understanding signal detectors) and psychophysics (e.g., analyzing decision-making experiments). Its extension to other prediction problems, such as regression, is identified as a promising future direction, which would further expand its impact.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper also discusses limitations, acknowledging that the explanation vector can be a zero vector in regions where the probability function is maximal, limiting information about influential directions. Potential remedies involve examining higher-order derivatives or employing larger Parzen window widths. The sensitivity of local gradients to outliers and the inheritance of stationarity assumptions from the underlying model are also noted. Addressing these issues, for example, through smoothing methods or integrating non-stationary data handling, represents avenues for future research.

Despite these considerations, the framework provides a robust and general mechanism for extracting meaningful, instance-level explanations from complex classifiers, significantly advancing the field of interpretable machine learning and promising broad impact across scientific and industrial applications.
