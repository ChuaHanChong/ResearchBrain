# A Finite Sample Analysis of Distributional TD Learning with Linear Function Approximation

> [!summary] Summary
> Peng et al. provide the first non-asymptotic statistical analysis for distributional Temporal Difference (TD) learning with linear function approximation, demonstrating that learning the entire return distribution is statistically as efficient as learning its expectation. Their proposed Linear-Categorical TD (Linear-CTD) algorithm achieves an O(T⁻¹ᐟ²) L2 convergence rate and matches the sample complexity of classical Linear-TD.

## Problem

- Classical policy evaluation estimates only the expected return, which is insufficient for risk-sensitive decision-making and understanding outcome uncertainty.
- While distributional TD learning (DRL) emerged to estimate full return distributions, its non-asymptotic statistical properties with function approximation were largely unexplored.
- It was unknown whether the statistical efficiency observed in tabular DRL (learning distributions being as efficient as expectations) would persist when using function approximation for scalability.

## Method

- An improved Linear-Categorical TD (**Linear-CTD**) algorithm is proposed, which uses linear function approximation and categorical parameterization to represent return distributions.
- Linear-CTD is derived by applying linear stochastic approximation to solve the projected distributional Bellman equation, acting as a preconditioned semi-gradient descent that mitigates issues with categorical support scaling.
- The analysis utilizes a fine-grained examination of the linear-categorical Bellman equation and exponential stability arguments for random matrices to establish non-asymptotic convergence bounds.

## Results

- The work establishes the first non-asymptotic statistical guarantees for distributional TD learning under linear function approximation.
- Linear-CTD achieves an O(T⁻¹ᐟ²) L2 convergence rate and its sample complexity matches state-of-the-art bounds for classical Linear-TD learning.
- Empirical evaluations confirm Linear-CTD's stable convergence across different categorical supports and its improved performance over vanilla stochastic semi-gradient descent (SSGD), which struggles with larger support numbers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning the full return distribution with linear function approximation does not incur additional statistical cost compared to learning only its expectation.
> - The preconditioning mechanism within Linear-CTD is essential for robust convergence rates that are independent of the number of categorical supports (K).
> - Linear-CTD generalizes classical Linear-TD by preserving the mean estimation property, ensuring consistent expected value estimates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of Reinforcement Learning (RL), specifically focusing on policy evaluation, and more particularly, distributional policy evaluation. Traditional policy evaluation in RL primarily aims to estimate the expected return, or value function, for a given policy in a Markov Decision Process (MDP). This classical approach has been extensively studied, yielding comprehensive theoretical understandings, including finite-sample analyses and convergence rates for algorithms like Temporal Difference (TD) learning, both in tabular settings and with function approximation.

Distributional reinforcement learning emerged as an extension of classical RL, seeking to estimate the entire distribution of returns rather than just their expectation. This approach is motivated by its ability to capture uncertainty and risk, which is crucial for applications requiring risk-sensitive decision-making or a more complete understanding of possible outcomes. The seminal work by Bellemare et al. introduced distributional TD learning, extending the concept of TD learning to estimate return distributions.

Prior theoretical analyses of distributional TD learning predominantly focused on the tabular setting. In this context, works such as Rowland et al. and Peng et al. demonstrated that learning the return distribution (e.g., in terms of 1-Wasserstein distance) is statistically as efficient as learning its expectation. However, a significant gap remained in the theoretical understanding of distributional TD learning when combined with function approximation. Function approximation is indispensable for handling large or continuous state spaces encountered in many practical applications of RL. While some algorithms for linear distributional TD learning with various parameterizations exist (e.g., Bellemare et al., Lyle et al., Bellemare et al.), their non-asymptotic statistical properties, particularly finite-sample rates, have remained largely unexplored. Bellemare et al. provided an asymptotic convergence result, but the precise non-asymptotic bounds were an open question.

This paper addresses this gap by providing the first non-asymptotic statistical rates for distributional TD learning with linear function approximation. It aims to determine if the observation of similar statistical efficiency between distributional and classical TD learning in the tabular case holds when function approximation is employed, thus bridging the theoretical understanding between these two regimes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct a finite-sample statistical analysis of distributional Temporal Difference (TD) learning when linear function approximation is utilized. This involves deriving sharp non-asymptotic convergence rates for such algorithms.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several key areas:

1. **Capturing Uncertainty and Risk in RL:** Classical policy evaluation focuses solely on the expected return (value function), which may not be sufficient for decision-making in environments where uncertainty or risk is a critical factor. Distributional policy evaluation, by estimating the full return distribution, provides a more comprehensive representation of future outcomes. This information is vital for applications in areas such as finance, healthcare, or autonomous systems, where understanding the range of possible returns and their associated probabilities is crucial for making robust or risk-aware decisions. The paper cites various works highlighting the importance of uncertainty-aware and risk-sensitive tasks.

2. **Addressing the Scalability Challenge with Function Approximation:** In practical reinforcement learning scenarios, the state space is often too large or continuous to be represented explicitly in a tabular manner. Function approximation techniques, such as linear function approximation or neural networks, are necessary to generalize learning across states. While classical TD learning with function approximation has been extensively studied, the theoretical guarantees for its distributional counterpart, particularly non-asymptotic bounds, have been underdeveloped.

3. **Extending Prior Tabular Findings:** Previous research in the tabular setting demonstrated that learning the full return distribution is statistically as "easy" as learning its expectation. A key motivation for this work is to investigate whether this remarkable statistical efficiency property persists when function approximation, specifically linear function approximation, is introduced. Answering this question is fundamental to understanding the computational and statistical trade-offs involved in using distributional methods in more complex, real-world settings.

4. **Developing Statistically Efficient Algorithms:** The paper aims to propose and analyze an improved linear-categorical TD learning algorithm (Linear-CTD). This involves addressing the practical challenges of representing and manipulating infinite-dimensional distributions through finite-dimensional parameterizations (categorical) while integrating linear function approximation. A specific motivation here is to develop an algorithm that avoids performance degradation due to the choice of parameterization basis, such as the number of categorical supports (K), which can be a challenge for naive implementations.

In essence, the research is motivated by the need for robust theoretical foundations for distributional reinforcement learning algorithms that can operate in realistic, large-scale environments, and to determine the statistical cost, if any, of learning richer information about returns.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this paper involves developing and theoretically analyzing a specific distributional TD learning algorithm with linear function approximation. The approach can be broken down into problem formulation, algorithm design, and rigorous theoretical analysis.

**Problem Formulation:**
The objective is distributional policy evaluation within a discounted Markov Decision Process (MDP) for a given policy π. The goal is to estimate η_π(s), the return distribution for each state *s*. This distribution satisfies the distributional Bellman equation: η_π(s) = E[b_(r,γ)#η_π(s')] (where b_(r,γ)(x) = r + γx, and # denotes pushforward measure).

**Function Approximation and Parameterization:**
To make the problem tractable in large state spaces, two key approximations are used:
1. **Linear Function Approximation:** The state-dependent components are approximated using a linear combination of *d*-dimensional feature vectors, ϕ(s) ∈ ℝ^d.
2. **Categorical Parametrization:** The infinite-dimensional return distributions are represented by a finite set of *K+1* fixed, equally-spaced support points {x_0..., x_K} ∈ [0, (1-γ)^-1]. The distribution at each state *s* is then defined by a vector of "probabilities" p(s) = (p_0(s)..., p_(K-1)(s))^T, which may be negative to allow for a signed measure space P_sign. The Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) at each support point x_k for a state *s* is linearly parameterized: F_k(s; Θ) = ϕ(s)^T θ(k) + (k+1)/(K+1), where Θ = (θ(0)..., θ(K-1)) is a *d x K* matrix parameter. The full parameter vector is θ = vec(Θ) ∈ ℝ^dK.

**Algorithm Design: Linear-Categorical TD Learning (Linear-CTD):**
1. **Projected Bellman Equation:** The combined linear-categorical parametrization defines an affine subspace P_(φ,K)^sign. The unique target parameter Θ* is the fixed point of the projected distributional Bellman operator Π^(π)_(φ,K) T_π, which is shown to be a √γ-contraction. This fixed-point equation is formulated as a linear system for Θ (Equation 12 in the paper).
2. **Stochastic Approximation:** Linear-CTD (Equation 13) is derived by applying linear stochastic approximation (LSA) to solve this linear system. At each time step *t*, using observed data (s_t, a_t, r_t, s_(t+1)), the parameter Θ_t is updated based on the Bellman error.
3. **Preconditioning:** The authors highlight that Linear-CTD can be interpreted as a preconditioned version of a vanilla stochastic semi-gradient descent (SSGD) algorithm using a probability mass function (PMF) representation. This preconditioning effectively removes a matrix C^T C (where C relates PMF and CDF representations) from the update rule. This matrix C^T C has a condition number that scales with K^2. By implicitly inverting this matrix, Linear-CTD achieves convergence rates that are independent of *K*, which the vanilla SSGD cannot.
4. **Tail Averaging:** Polyak-Ruppert tail averaging (average of iterates from T/2 to T) is applied to the sequence of parameters {θ_t} to obtain the final estimator θ_T_bar. This technique is known to improve convergence rates and stability in LSA algorithms.

**Theoretical Analysis Techniques:**
The analysis aims to derive finite-sample bounds for the estimation error W_(1,μ_π)(η_θ^*, η_θ_T_bar), building upon the approximation error bound for W_(1,μ_π)(η_π, η_θ^*).

1. **Error Decomposition:** The total error is decomposed into approximation error (W_(1,μ_π)(η_π, η_θ^*)) and estimation error (W_(1,μ_π)(η_θ^*, η_θ_T_bar)). An approximation error bound is provided (Proposition 3.2), showing dependence on K and the quality of linear function approximation.
2. **Vectorization of Update Rule:** The matrix-based update rule for Θ_t is vectorized into a dK-dimensional vector update rule for θ_t = vec(Θ_t). This transforms the problem into a standard LSA framework: θ_t = θ_(t-1) - α(A_t θ_(t-1) - b_t).
3. **Norm Equivalence:** Lemma 5.1 establishes a connection between the W_1,μ_π distance of the distributional parameters and the Euclidean norm of the expected LSA error term (||A_bar (θ - θ^*)||). This allows the application of standard LSA analysis techniques.
4. **Exponential Stability Arguments:** The core analytical tool is the exponential stability argument for products of random matrices, as developed by Samsonov et al. for linear stochastic approximation. This involves:
 - **Bounding Matrix and Noise Terms:** Deriving upper bounds for the spectral norm of A_t (C_A) and the norm of the noise term b_t (C_e), as well as the covariance of the noise (tr(Σ_e)). These bounds are crucial for characterizing the stochasticity of the update.
 - **Spectral Radius Analysis:** A key step (Lemma 5.4 and its use in Equation 29) involves demonstrating that the spectral radius of E[Y ⊗ (Σ_φ^-1/2 φ(s)φ(s')^T Σ_φ^-1/2)] is bounded by √γ, where Y is related to the categorical Bellman operator. This ensures contraction properties and forms the basis for exponential stability.
 - **Analysis of Bellman Equation:** A fine-grained analysis of the linear-categorical Bellman equation is conducted to derive tight bounds for the matrix C G̃(r) C^-1 and the constant term b_t. This includes showing non-negativity of entries and specific bounds on the L1 and L∞ norms (Lemma H.2) to support the √γ contraction.
5. **Convergence Analysis:** Using these bounds and exponential stability, the paper derives L_p convergence rates (Theorem E.1) and specifically L_2 convergence (Theorem 4.1) for the averaged estimator θ_T_bar, both in a generative model setting. These L_p bounds are then used to derive high-probability convergence guarantees (Theorem 4.2). The analysis is extended to the Markovian setting.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key findings, both algorithmic and theoretical, alongside empirical validations.

**Algorithmic Contributions:**
1. **Improved Linear-CTD Algorithm:** The authors propose an improved version of the linear-categorical TD learning algorithm, termed Linear-CTD. This algorithm is derived by directly solving the linear-categorical projected Bellman equation using linear stochastic approximation.
2. **Preconditioned Approach:** Linear-CTD is characterized as a preconditioned version of vanilla stochastic semi-gradient descent (SSGD) with a probability mass function (PMF) representation. This preconditioning step is shown to be critical for achieving statistical efficiency that is independent of the number of categorical supports, K.
3. **Mean-Preserving Property:** A notable property of Linear-CTD is its mean-preserving nature. If initialized consistently with Linear-TD, Linear-CTD maintains the same expected value estimate as Linear-TD throughout the learning process. This positions Linear-CTD as a direct generalization of Linear-TD.

**Theoretical Contributions (Non-Asymptotic Statistical Guarantees):**
1. **First Non-Asymptotic Guarantees:** The paper establishes the first non-asymptotic (finite-sample) statistical guarantees for distributional TD learning in the setting of linear function approximation. This addresses a theoretical gap in the field.
2. **L2 Convergence Rates (Theorem 4.1):** For a generative model setting, with Polyak-Ruppert tail averaging and a constant step size, the algorithm achieves an L2 convergence rate where the dominant term is of order O(T^-1/2). The bounds show explicit dependencies on the discount factor γ, the minimum eigenvalue of the feature covariance matrix λ_min, and other problem parameters.
3. **Sample Complexity Matching (Corollary 4.1):**
 - With an optimal instance-dependent constant step size (α ~ (1-γ)λ_min), the sample complexity to achieve an ε-accurate estimator (in μ_π-weighted 1-Wasserstein distance) is determined to be O((ε^-2 + λ_min^-1) (1-γ)^-2 λ_min^-1 * (||θ^*||^2_V1 + 1)).
 - Crucially, these sample complexity bounds are shown to match the state-of-the-art bounds for classic Linear-TD learning algorithms, which only estimate the expected return.
4. **High-Probability Convergence (Theorem 4.2):** The L2 convergence results are extended to provide high-probability convergence bounds, showing that the error decays at a similar rate with additional logarithmic factors related to confidence probability. The results are also extended to the Markovian setting (Remark 4).
5. **Statistical Efficiency Implication:** The central theoretical finding is that, with linear function approximation, learning the full distribution of the return from streaming data is statistically no more difficult than learning its expectation (value function). This confirms and extends observations previously made in the tabular setting to the function approximation regime.
6. **Key Technical Insights:** The derivations rely on a fine-grained analysis of the linear-categorical Bellman equation and the application of exponential stability arguments for products of random matrices. Specific technical contributions include Lemma 5.4, which provides a tight bound for the spectral norm of expectations of Kronecker products, and bounds for the components of the LSA noise term.

**Empirical Validations (Appendix F):**
Numerical experiments on a 3-state MDP validate the theoretical findings:
1. **Linear-CTD Convergence:** The proposed Linear-CTD algorithm exhibits stable convergence for varying numbers of categorical supports (K), using a fixed constant step size (Figure 1).
2. **Comparison with Vanilla SSGD (PMF):**
 - The baseline SSGD algorithm with PMF representation is shown to diverge for larger K values when a fixed step size is used (Figure 2).
 - Empirically, the maximum stable step size for the vanilla algorithm is observed to scale approximately with K^-2 (Figure 4), aligning with theoretical predictions (Lemma E.3). In contrast, Linear-CTD's maximum stable step size does not significantly decrease with increasing K (Table 2).
 - Linear-CTD consistently outperforms the vanilla algorithm in terms of sample complexity (Table 1 vs. Table 2).
 - An analysis comparing theoretical RHS bounds with empirical LHS values (Figure 5) indicates that while the theoretical bounds for the vanilla algorithm predict a significant increase in error with K, the empirical gap is less pronounced, suggesting potential for tighter analysis in specific cases.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the theoretical understanding of distributional reinforcement learning (DRL) by providing finite-sample statistical guarantees for algorithms employing linear function approximation. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Filling a Theoretical Gap:** The paper addresses a notable gap in DRL theory by establishing the first non-asymptotic sample complexity bounds for distributional TD learning with linear function approximation. This is crucial for understanding the performance of such algorithms in practical, finite-data settings, which is particularly relevant given the increasing adoption of DRL with deep function approximators.

2. **Fundamental Statistical Equivalence:** The primary finding—that learning the full return distribution is statistically no more difficult than learning its expectation under linear function approximation—is a significant theoretical insight. It suggests that the increased representational power of DRL, which can capture crucial information about risk and uncertainty, does not necessarily come at a higher statistical cost in terms of sample complexity, at least for this class of function approximators. This strengthens the theoretical justification for using DRL in applications where understanding the return distribution is vital.

3. **Guiding Algorithmic Design:** The analysis of Linear-CTD as a preconditioned version of a vanilla SSGD algorithm offers valuable guidance for future algorithm design. It highlights the importance of appropriate parametrization and optimization techniques (like preconditioning) to mitigate potential computational or statistical inefficiencies arising from the higher-dimensional representations in DRL. The proposed Linear-CTD is a well-founded algorithm with strong theoretical backing.

4. **Foundation for Advanced DRL:** By establishing a solid theoretical foundation for linear DRL, this work provides a stepping stone for analyzing more complex DRL algorithms that utilize non-linear function approximation (e.g., neural networks). The insights gained into contraction properties, noise characteristics, and the role of linear algebra in the Bellman equations can inform future research into deep DRL.

5. **Implications for Risk-Sensitive RL:** For domains where risk assessment and uncertainty quantification are paramount (e.g., finance, robotics, healthcare), this work provides theoretical assurance that DRL approaches are statistically competitive with traditional value-based methods. This can lead to the development of more robust, transparent, and interpretable RL systems that are better equipped to handle real-world complexities.

**Potential Future Work:**
The authors themselves identify several avenues for future research, including:
- Applying variance-reduction techniques or polynomial-decaying step sizes to potentially break the "sample size barrier" (dependence on λ_min^-1).
- Analyzing softmax-based linear-categorical algorithms, which are closer to the practical C51 algorithm but introduce non-linearities that make theoretical analysis more challenging.
- Extending the finite-sample analysis to more complex and widely used non-linear function approximators, such as deep neural networks.
- Further investigating the discrepancy between theoretical bounds and empirical performance for certain algorithm variants (e.g., the PMF representation), which could lead to refined analytical tools.

Overall, this paper significantly advances the theoretical understanding of distributional reinforcement learning, providing both rigorous non-asymptotic guarantees and practical insights into algorithm design for scenarios involving function approximation.
