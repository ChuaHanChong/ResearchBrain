# The Alignment Auditor: A Bayesian Framework for Verifying and Refining LLM Objectives

> [!summary] Summary
> The Alignment Auditor framework, developed by researchers at Imperial College London, establishes a Bayesian method for verifying and refining the implicit objectives of Large Language Models by quantifying and systematically reducing uncertainty in inferred reward functions. It offers uncertainty-aware diagnostics and demonstrates that these refined objectives can successfully guide LLM fine-tuning, achieving alignment efficacy comparable to ground-truth rewards.

## Problem

- Large Language Models implicitly optimize opaque objectives, making it difficult to assure their safety and alignment with desired behaviors.
- Traditional Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) methods provide single, deterministic reward estimates that suffer from non-identifiability, leading to potentially unreliable conclusions.
- A lack of uncertainty quantification around inferred objectives prevents auditors from assessing the fragility or trustworthiness of these estimates.

## Method

- The framework re-conceptualizes LLM objective inference as a three-stage ==auditing protocol==, modeling LLM behavior as a contextual bandit.
- It uses Bayesian Inverse Reinforcement Learning (BIRL) to infer a full posterior distribution over plausible reward functions, explicitly quantifying non-identifiability.
- The framework systematically reduces non-identifiability through sequential Bayesian updates and provides actionable, uncertainty-aware diagnostics based on epistemic uncertainty.

## Results

- Inferred reward characteristics and calibration improved with the scale of the base LLM, consistently distinguishing toxic from non-toxic completions.
- Sequential Bayesian updates monotonically decreased the log-determinant of the posterior covariance, providing direct evidence of reduced non-identifiability and improved inference accuracy.
- Policies fine-tuned with refined, low-uncertainty inferred rewards achieved toxicity reductions comparable to ground-truth RLHF, while uncertainty diagnostics reliably identified ambiguous or out-of-distribution inputs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Quantifying the non-identifiability of reward functions with Bayesian posteriors allows for systematic reduction of ambiguity through sequential evidence collection and Bayesian updates.
> - Epistemic uncertainty can be leveraged to diagnose spurious shortcuts and identify out-of-distribution (OOD) prompts, enhancing the transparency and robustness of LLM alignment.
> - Refined, low-uncertainty reward functions are functionally useful for guiding LLM alignment, producing desired behaviors (e.g., toxicity reduction) comparable to those achieved with ground-truth rewards.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The escalating deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) in critical domains has accentuated the need for robust alignment and safety mechanisms. A persistent issue in this field is the inherent opacity of the objectives LLMs implicitly optimize, despite extensive pretraining, fine-tuning, and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) processes (Bender et al., 2021; Bommasani et al., 2021; Weidinger et al., 2022). This opacity complicates the diagnosis and anticipation of undesirable behaviors such as reward hacking, shortcut exploitation, and preference inconsistencies (Casper et al., 2023; Kenton et al., 2021). Understanding the internalized objectives of LLMs is recognized as fundamental for developing trustworthy and accountable AI systems.

Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) is a computational framework that infers reward functions from observed behavior (Ng & Russell, 2000; Abbeel & Ng, 2004). Prior applications of IRL to LLMs have demonstrated its potential in uncovering implicit training goals and diagnosing issues like preference inconsistencies or reward misspecification (Joselowitz et al., 2025; Sun & van der Schaar, 2025). However, existing IRL methods typically yield a single, point estimate of the reward function. This approach often overlooks the fundamental challenge of non-identifiability, where multiple distinct reward functions can equally account for the same observed behaviors (Ng & Russell, 2000; Hadfield-Menell et al., 2017). Without a quantification of uncertainty around these inferred objectives, auditors lack the means to assess the fragility or trustworthiness of the estimates, potentially leading to reliance on spurious explanations.

Bayesian IRL addresses the non-identifiability problem by inferring a distribution over plausible reward functions (Ramachandran & Amir, 2007; Choi & Kim, 2011; Levine ets al., 2011). While this approach provides a more principled way to handle ambiguity, its application to LLMs has been limited, mainly confined to posterior inference without a comprehensive framework for auditing. Recent extensions in Bayesian reward modeling aim to mitigate over-optimization in RLHF (Yang et al., 2024) or reduce epistemic uncertainty in preference collection via active learning (Melo et al., 2024), but these efforts are typically integrated within the RLHF optimization loop rather than serving as an external auditing mechanism. Cai et al. (2025) propose a Bayesian IRL approach for LLM alignment focusing on inference efficiency.

The current work distinguishes itself by reframing reward inference from a simple estimation task into a comprehensive, multi-stage auditing process. It integrates Bayesian IRL with sequential uncertainty reduction and policy-level validation, a scope that extends beyond previous contributions. Furthermore, while uncertainty quantification in LLMs is an active area of research (Tonolini et al., 2024; Balabanov & Linander, 2024; Ross et al., 2025; Kruse et al., 2025; Dey et al., 2025), most existing methods focus on quantifying uncertainty over output predictions or prompt parameters. This paper addresses a gap by specifically recovering and validating posterior distributions over the underlying reward functions that implicitly drive LLM behavior, providing objective-level verification rather than surface-level calibration.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenge of the opaque objectives implicitly optimized by Large Language Models (LLMs), which complicates the assurance of their safety and alignment with desired behaviors. Existing methods for inferring these objectives, such as traditional Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL), typically provide single, deterministic estimates of reward functions. These estimates often suffer from fundamental non-identifiability, meaning multiple reward functions can explain the same observed LLM behavior, leading to potentially overconfident and unreliable conclusions about the model's true goals. Without a robust understanding of these objectives, it is difficult for auditors, safety teams, and regulators to anticipate failures, diagnose issues like reward hacking, or ensure LLMs operate as intended in critical applications.

The key objectives of "The Alignment Auditor" framework are therefore detailed as follows:

1. **Quantify and Systematically Reduce Non-Identifiability:** The framework aims to move beyond single-point reward estimates by inferring a full posterior distribution over plausible reward functions. This makes the inherent ambiguity (non-identifiability) explicit. A central objective is to demonstrate that this ambiguity can be systematically reduced through sequential rounds of evidence collection and Bayesian updates, evidenced by posterior contraction. This capability is designed to strengthen confidence in the inferred objectives as more data is observed.

2. **Provide Actionable, Uncertainty-Aware Diagnostics:** A crucial objective is to equip auditors with diagnostic tools that leverage the quantified uncertainty in the reward estimates. This includes:
- **Exposing Spurious Shortcuts:** Identifying instances where the LLM might be optimizing for superficial or unintended features (shortcuts) rather than the true underlying objective. The framework aims to detect such behavior by observing how the reward model's uncertainty changes in response to specific probes.
- **Identifying Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Prompts:** Flagging prompts or contexts where the inferred objective is less trustworthy due to high uncertainty. This helps users understand the limitations of the inferred reward model and where its guidance might be unreliable.

3. **Validate Policy-Level Utility:** The framework seeks to establish that the refined, low-uncertainty reward functions are not merely descriptive but are functionally useful for guiding LLM alignment. This involves demonstrating that an LLM fine-tuned with the inferred reward function via standard RLHF techniques can achieve training dynamics and ultimately exhibit desired behaviors (e.g., toxicity reduction) that are comparable to those achieved when using an oracle, ground-truth reward function. This objective aims to provide practical utility and confidence in the deployability of the inferred objectives for future alignment efforts.

Overall, the motivation is to transform reward inference from a simple estimation task into a comprehensive verification process, offering a practical toolkit for evaluating and ensuring what LLMs are truly optimizing for, thereby fostering more trustworthy and accountable artificial intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The "Alignment Auditor" framework re-conceptualizes LLM objective inference as a three-stage auditing protocol. It models LLM behavior as a contextual bandit, simplifying the interaction to a single-step Markov Decision Process (MDP) where states are prompts (p), actions are text completions (o), and rewards quantify completion desirability. The reward function $R_{\theta}(o)$ is parameterized linearly as $\theta^{\top} \phi(o)$, where $\phi(o)$ is a feature vector derived from the LLM's own embedding space. The work utilizes a RoBERTa toxicity classifier as the ground-truth reward, $R_{\star}(o) = -f_{\theta}(o)$, where lower toxicity scores yield higher rewards. Expert-aligned policies ($\pi_E$) are generated via KL-regularized PPO using this ground-truth reward, contrasting with baseline policies ($\pi_B$) to form paired demonstrations $(o_i^+, o_i^-)$.

The three stages of the auditing framework are:

1. **Stage 1: Quantifying Ambiguity with Bayesian Inverse Reinforcement Learning (BIRL)**
- **Objective:** To explicitly quantify the non-identifiability of reward functions by inferring a posterior distribution over reward weights ($\theta$).
- **Data:** A dataset of paired completions $D = \{(o_i^+, o_i^-)\}_{i=1}^N$, where $o_i^+$ is an expert completion and $o_i^-$ is a baseline completion for the same prompt. A feature margin $\Delta\phi = \phi(o^+) - \phi(o^-)$ is computed for each pair.
- **Bayesian Formulation:**
 - **Prior:** A zero-mean isotropic Gaussian prior $p(\theta) = \mathcal{N}(\theta|0, \sigma_0^2 I)$ is placed over the reward weights, reflecting initial unbiased beliefs.
 - **Likelihood:** The expert's preference for $o^+$ over $o^-$ is modeled using the Bradley-Terry model, $P(o^+ \succ o^-|\theta) = \sigma(\alpha \theta^{\top}\Delta\phi)$, where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid and $\alpha$ is a temperature parameter. The full data likelihood $p(D|\theta)$ is the product of individual preference probabilities.
 - **Posterior:** Bayes' theorem is used to derive the posterior $p(\theta|D) \propto p(D|\theta)p(\theta)$. The spread (variance) of this posterior directly indicates the degree of non-identifiability.
- **Variational Approximation:** Due to the analytical intractability of the posterior, variational inference (VI) is employed. A tractable mean-field Gaussian family $q_{\lambda}(\theta) = \mathcal{N}(\theta|\mu, \text{diag}(\sigma^2))$ with parameters $\lambda = \{\mu, \sigma\}$ is optimized to minimize the KL divergence to the true posterior by maximizing the Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO) (Equation 5), utilizing the reparameterization trick and mini-batches.

2. **Stage 2: Auditing Trustworthiness with Uncertainty-Aware Diagnostics**
- **Objective:** To systematically reduce non-identifiability and diagnose the robustness of the inferred reward model.
- **Systematic Reduction of Non-Identifiability (Sequential Bayesian Updates):** The training data $D$ is split into $K$ disjoint rounds ($D_1, \dots, D_K$). In each round $k$, the posterior distribution $q_{k-1}(\theta)$ from the previous round is used as the prior for inferring the current posterior $q_k(\theta)$ using data $D_k$. The primary metric for tracking reduction in ambiguity is the log-determinant of the posterior covariance matrix, $\log \det(\Sigma_k)$, which is expected to decrease monotonically across rounds.
- **Actionable Uncertainty Diagnostics:**
 - **Uncertainty Decomposition:** Predictive uncertainty for any completion $o$ is decomposed into aleatoric (inherent data noise) and epistemic (model uncertainty) components. High epistemic uncertainty, quantified by Mutual Information $I(\theta, y|o, D)$ (Equation 6), indicates contexts where the reward model is less confident, suggesting genuinely ambiguous or out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs.
 - **Diagnostic Probing:** This signal is used to probe the model. For example, injecting spurious features into prompts is expected to increase epistemic uncertainty for a robust model, whereas a model that learned a shortcut might exhibit spurious overconfidence.

3. **Stage 3: Policy-Level Validation of the Inferred Reward**
- **Objective:** To validate the practical utility of the inferred reward function as a signal for LLM alignment.
- **Approach:** The mean of the final, contracted posterior distribution from Stage 2, $\hat{R}(o) = \mu_K^{\top}\phi(o)$, is utilized as the reward signal within a standard RLHF pipeline (using PPO) to fine-tune the initial baseline LLM ($\pi_B$).
- **Evaluation:** The performance and training dynamics of this fine-tuned policy are compared against a ground-truth reference: a policy fine-tuned using the original oracle reward $R_{\star}(o)$. Key metrics include:
 - **Reward Mean Curves:** Tracking the average reward received during training.
 - **KL Divergence:** Monitoring the KL divergence between the training policy and the baseline to ensure controlled learning.
 - **Downstream Toxicity Reduction:** Assessing the percentage of toxic outputs on a held-out set of high-risk prompts.
- Successful validation occurs if the policy trained with the inferred reward substantially replicates the behavior and alignment efficacy of the policy trained with the ground-truth reward.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides empirical evidence supporting the efficacy of the Alignment Auditor framework across its three stages.

**Inferred Reward Characteristics and Scaling Effects (Stage 1):**
The framework successfully recovers a reward function that discerns toxic from non-toxic completions. For instance, analysis of Llama-3.2-1B demonstrated that both pairwise preference predictions and single-text toxicity scores were well-calibrated, with distinct separation boundaries between reward distributions for toxic and non-toxic texts (Figure 2). The performance of the reward inference, including pairwise accuracy, single-text accuracy, AUROC, and F1-score, consistently improved with the scale of the base LLM (e.g., from Pythia-70M to Llama-3.2-1B). This indicates that larger LLMs produce more linearly separable features that facilitate a more faithful recovery of the expert's underlying preference signal. Calibration, as measured by Expected Calibration Error (ECE), also improved with model scale, with pairwise calibration generally outperforming single-text calibration (Figure 3).

**Systematic Reduction of Non-Identifiability (Stage 2):**
The sequential Bayesian update mechanism proved effective in reducing ambiguity. Across five rounds of sequential updates for Llama-1B, a monotonic decrease was observed in the log-determinant of the posterior covariance ("Posterior Tightness") (Figure 4a), providing direct evidence of posterior contraction and a reduction in non-identifiability. Concurrently, epistemic uncertainty, quantified by Mutual Information, decreased over the rounds (Figure 4b). This posterior tightening led to tangible performance improvements, with AUROC and pairwise accuracy increasing, and calibration errors (Brier, ECE) decreasing (Figure 4c, d). The approach also showed mitigation of reward hacking tendencies that were present in single-round inference.

**Uncertainty Diagnostics and OOD Detection (Stage 2):**
The framework's uncertainty-aware diagnostics demonstrated the ability to identify problematic inputs. Prompts injected with spurious features ("marked" prompts) were associated with higher local uncertainty (Figure 5 left). A strong positive correlation (r=0.989) was observed between the inferred reward variance (representing epistemic uncertainty) and the Mahalanobis distance from the training data distribution (Figure 5 middle). This indicates that the reward model is sensitive to inputs that are genuinely ambiguous or fall outside its training distribution, reliably flagging situations where its inferred objective might be less trustworthy.

**Policy-Level Validation and Downstream Alignment (Stage 3):**
The most critical finding is the functional utility of the inferred rewards for LLM alignment. When the mean of the final, contracted posterior reward (from later sequential rounds, e.g., Rounds 2-5) was used to fine-tune a baseline LLM via RLHF, the resulting training dynamics closely mirrored those of a policy trained with the ground-truth oracle reward. This was evident in the reward mean curves, standard deviation, and objective KL divergence trajectories (Figure 6). Crucially, the policy fine-tuned with the inferred reward achieved downstream toxicity reductions on a held-out set of prompts that were comparable to those obtained with the ground-truth RLHF (Figure 5 right). In contrast, using an under-identified reward from only the first sequential round led to reward hacking during PPO, characterized by unstable training dynamics and inferior toxicity reduction, underscoring the importance of posterior contraction before deployment. Qualitative results (Appendix Tables 1-5) further illustrated this, showing that early-round inferred rewards produced degenerate, incoherent outputs indicative of hacking, while later rounds yielded fluent, on-topic, and safer completions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Alignment Auditor framework introduces a principled and comprehensive approach to verifying and refining LLM objectives, extending beyond traditional reward function estimation. This work makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact:

Firstly, it addresses the fundamental challenge of **non-identifiability** in Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) for LLMs. By adopting a Bayesian approach, the framework quantifies the inherent ambiguity in inferred reward functions and, crucially, demonstrates a systematic method for reducing this ambiguity through sequential Bayesian updates. This capability enhances the trustworthiness of inferred objectives, moving the field from speculative estimation to verifiable characterization of LLM goals.

Secondly, the framework provides **actionable, uncertainty-aware diagnostics**. The ability to decompose predictive uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic components allows auditors to identify genuinely ambiguous contexts or out-of-distribution (OOD) prompts where the inferred objective may be unreliable. Moreover, the diagnostic probes can expose scenarios where an LLM might be optimizing for unintended shortcuts rather than the desired behavior, offering critical insights for safety teams and developers. This level of transparency and diagnostic capability is a step towards more robust and interpretable LLM alignment.

Thirdly, the work demonstrates **policy-level utility** of the inferred rewards. The validation that a policy trained with the framework's refined objective can achieve alignment efficacy comparable to one trained with a ground-truth oracle reward is a strong indicator of its practical value. This confirms that the inferred objectives are not merely descriptive but can serve as functional signals to guide future LLM fine-tuning, directly impacting the development of safer and more aligned models. The observation that under-identified rewards can lead to reward hacking further emphasizes the importance of the systematic ambiguity reduction process.

The "Alignment Auditor" offers a generalizable blueprint for **auditors, safety teams, and regulators**. By providing a structured toolkit to verify what LLMs are truly trying to achieve, it contributes to fostering greater accountability in AI development. Beyond detoxification, the framework has the potential to generalize to other critical alignment dimensions such as helpfulness, factuality, and bias, thereby strengthening alignment guarantees across a broader spectrum of LLM applications.

In summary, this research advances the state of the art in LLM alignment by providing a robust methodology to infer, verify, and utilize LLM objectives. Its emphasis on uncertainty quantification and practical validation positions it as a valuable tool for enhancing the trustworthiness and safety of large language models in increasingly critical deployment scenarios.
