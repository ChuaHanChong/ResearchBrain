# GuidedAttention: Interpretable and Correctable Visual Attention for OOD-Robust Robot Manipulation via Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at AIST developed GuidedAttention, a visuomotor imitation learning framework that incorporates interpretable and correctable visual attention via explicit keypoint predictions. This framework consistently improved robot manipulation performance, particularly under out-of-distribution conditions, by allowing one-time human correction of task-relevant attention keypoints.

## Problem

- End-to-end visuomotor policies lack interpretability, making it difficult to understand robot decision-making and diagnose failures, especially regarding visual attention.
- Current policies offer limited mechanisms for human intervention or correction when the robot's visual focus is incorrect, requiring autonomous learning of all cues.
- Imitation learning policies exhibit poor robustness and performance degradation in out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, such as novel object positions or appearances.

## Method

- Introduces **GuidedAttention** (**DP-GA**), a framework that predicts task-relevant visual attention keypoints using a ==DETR-style== encoder and conditions a ==diffusion policy== on these features.
- Implements a ==keypoint override mechanism== allowing users to optionally correct a subset of predicted keypoints once at rollout initiation, which are then inverse-mapped to features for the policy.
- Leverages an off-the-shelf tracking module (==Co-Tracker==) to automatically propagate initial human-corrected keypoints throughout the execution sequence, minimizing interaction cost.

## Results

- GuidedAttention (autonomous) showed up to **15** percentage points (pp) improvement over baselines in in-distribution and OOD conditions in simulation, demonstrating the benefit of structured attention.
- With optional human attention correction, OOD performance substantially increased by **15-30** pp in simulation and **20-40** pp in real-world tasks, achieving **20-30** pp higher success rates than baselines.
- The method demonstrated robustness to moderate noise in tracking and user annotations, and consistently outperformed marker overlay prompting (MOP) by over **20** pp in comparative tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly integrating interpretable and correctable visual attention provides an intuitive interface for human-in-the-loop guidance, enhancing transparency and fault diagnosis in robot manipulation.
> - A one-time human correction of visual attention, coupled with automatic tracking, effectively mitigates performance drops under various out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions without continuous supervision.
> - Careful training techniques, including randomized feature routing and feature-space alignment loss, are crucial for robustly integrating both autonomously predicted and human-corrected attention features.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of robot manipulation, specifically focusing on visuomotor imitation learning with an emphasis on interpretability, correctability, and out-of-distribution (OOD) robustness.

End-to-end visuomotor policies, which learn robot behaviors directly from high-dimensional sensory inputs, have shown promise for dexterous and flexible manipulation. However, a limitation of these policies is their lack of transparency regarding internal decision-making, particularly concerning visual attention. This opaqueness hinders human understanding of failure modes and the ability to provide targeted corrections.

**Relationship to Visual Attention in Imitation Learning:**
Previous work in visual imitation learning has explored extracting task-relevant visual information to improve policy performance. This includes representing observations through visual keypoints for structured control, and methods for obtaining these cues via dynamics prediction, object proposal priors, or human demonstration analysis. Additionally, studies have investigated integrating such representations into policies, either as primary visual inputs, augmented with semantic information, or selectively filtered for robustness. GuidedAttention distinguishes itself by explicitly treating visual attention as an *interpretable and correctable* intermediate representation, offering an intuitive interface for human guidance that previous approaches did not prioritize.

**Relationship to Human Guidance for Visuomotor Policies:**
Human guidance for visuomotor policies has been investigated at various levels of abstraction. Some methods involve high-level language or vision-language models for task goals, while others use more direct spatial prompting such as bounding boxes, sketches, trajectory annotations, semantic annotations, or sparse point prompts. GuidedAttention adopts a different paradigm by introducing an explicit intermediate attention representation that resides *between perception and action generation*. Instead of directly instructing the policy, users interact with the policy through its predicted attention keypoints, which can be inspected and optionally corrected prior to action generation.

**Relationship to OOD Robustness in Imitation Learning:**
Imitation learning policies are known to perform well within their training data distribution but often suffer significant performance degradation under OOD conditions, such as changes in object positions (positional OOD) or environmental appearance (appearance OOD). Existing approaches to address OOD issues include expanding the training distribution by generating additional data or learning representations that are inherently robust to distribution shifts. GuidedAttention complements these efforts by providing a mechanism for *human-guided correction of visual attention* when autonomous prediction becomes unreliable. This human-in-the-loop approach allows the policy to regain task-relevant visual focus under OOD conditions, addressing a gap in existing robustness strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from a recognized limitation of current end-to-end visuomotor policies in robot manipulation: their lack of interpretability and correctability concerning visual attention. This deficiency impacts the transparency and robustness of robotic systems, especially in environments that deviate from training data.

The key motivations driving this work are:

- **Enhancing Interpretability:** End-to-end learning policies often operate as "black boxes," making it challenging for human operators to understand the robot's reasoning or diagnose the root causes of failures. Specifically, it is difficult to determine what visual cues the policy is attending to. Introducing an explicit, interpretable representation of visual attention allows users to observe the policy's visual focus, thereby improving transparency.

- **Enabling Correctability and Human-in-the-Loop Interaction:** When a robot policy fails, a human supervisor could often provide a simple, crucial hint if an appropriate interface existed. For instance, if the policy misidentifies a task-relevant object in a novel environment, a human could easily point out the correct object. Current end-to-end systems generally lack an intuitive mechanism for such corrective feedback, necessitating that the policy autonomously learn all necessary visual cues. This research aims to provide an explicit interface for human intervention.

- **Improving Robustness to Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Conditions:** Imitation learning policies are prone to performance degradation when deployed in environments that differ significantly from their training distribution (e.g., novel object positions, altered appearances, or unseen backgrounds). This "distribution shift" can cause the policy to lose its focus on critical task-relevant entities. The ability to guide or correct the policy's visual attention is posited as a method to recover performance in such challenging OOD scenarios without requiring continuous human supervision.

Based on these motivations, the **key objective** of this research is to propose and evaluate GuidedAttention, a visuomotor imitation learning framework that integrates an explicit, interpretable, and correctable visual attention representation into end-to-end policies.

Specific objectives include:
- To develop a mechanism for predicting task-relevant attention keypoints from camera images.
- To design a diffusion-based action policy that conditions its actions on these predicted keypoints.
- To establish a user interface that allows human operators to inspect and *optionally* correct a subset of these keypoints *once* at the initiation of a robot rollout.
- To implement an automatic tracking module that propagates these (potentially corrected) attention keypoints throughout the execution sequence.
- To empirically demonstrate that GuidedAttention consistently improves robot manipulation performance, with a particular focus on its effectiveness under various positional and appearance OOD conditions in both simulated and real-world environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, termed GuidedAttention, integrates an explicit visual attention representation into a visuomotor imitation learning pipeline. This is instantiated as Diffusion Policy with GuidedAttention (DP-GA).

**Overall Policy Architecture:**
DP-GA takes multi-view RGB observations and proprioceptive joint states as input. It deviates from conventional visuomotor policies by introducing an intermediate step: a visual attention encoder (AttnEnc) processes camera images to predict task-relevant attention keypoints and extract corresponding attention features. These attention features, concatenated with proprioceptive features, then condition a CNN-based denoising diffusion policy (built on a U-Net backbone) for action generation. This conditioning on interpretable visual attention features, rather than global visual features, forms the basis for human interaction.

**Visual Attention Encoder (AttnEnc):**
The AttnEnc module is responsible for predicting the explicit intermediate representation of visual attention. It uses a DETR-style architecture, comprising a Transformer encoder and decoder. RGB images are initially processed by a ResNet-18 backbone to obtain patch features, which are then fed into the Transformer encoder with positional embeddings. The decoder, using learnable queries for a predefined number of keypoints ($N_k$), generates attention features. A linear layer (coordinate head) maps each attention feature $f_i$ to a 2D coordinate $p_i$ as $p_i = h_θ(f_i) = Af_i + b$. The AttnEnc outputs a set of attention features $\{f_i\}_{i=1}^{N_k}$ for each image, which are subsequently used to condition the diffusion policy.

**Training of AttnEnc:**
The training process optimizes both action prediction (standard diffusion policy loss) and keypoint prediction. The keypoint loss ($L_{kp}$) is defined as the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted keypoint coordinates $p_i$ and human-annotated ground-truth coordinates $p_{gt,i}$. This loss encourages the policy to learn to focus on spatially relevant locations critical for manipulation tasks.

**Keypoint Override Mechanism:**
During policy rollout, the predicted attention keypoints are presented to the user as an interpretable interface. If the autonomously predicted attention is deemed unreliable (e.g., due to OOD conditions), the user can optionally correct a subset of the keypoint coordinates at the initial rollout frame. These corrected coordinates ($p_{gt,i}$) are converted into attention features via the inverse mapping $h_θ^{-1}(p_i) = A^+(p_i - b)$, where $A^+$ is the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. These inverse-mapped features then replace the corresponding autonomously predicted features in the final conditioning set for the diffusion model.

Three techniques are employed to ensure the consistency and reliability of this override mechanism:
1. **Forward–inverse weight sharing:** The inverse mapping reuses the same parameters ($A, b$) as the forward mapping, promoting a consistent relationship between features and coordinates.
2. **Feature-space alignment loss ($L_{feat}$):** To address the underdetermined nature of the inverse mapping when the feature dimension $d_f > 2$, an MSE loss is introduced between forward-predicted features $f_i$ and inverse-restored features $h_θ^{-1}(p_{gt,i})$. This encourages alignment between features derived from both paths.
3. **Randomized feature routing:** During training, for each keypoint, the conditioning feature for the diffusion process is randomly selected from either the prediction path (from AttnEnc) or the override path (inverse-mapped from ground-truth). This regularization prevents the diffusion model from relying exclusively on one pathway and stabilizes training when initial keypoint predictions may be inaccurate.

**Keypoint Annotation and Tracking:**
- **Training Data Generation:** Ground-truth keypoints for supervision are generated with minimal human effort. A human annotates keypoint locations *only in the initial frame* of each demonstration. An off-the-shelf tracking module (Co-Tracker) then automatically propagates these annotations through the subsequent frames of the demonstration. Keypoints designated as static remain fixed throughout.
- **Rollout Time Human Interaction:** At test time, the policy can execute autonomously by predicting keypoints and actions in a feedforward manner. Alternatively, users can optionally correct predicted keypoints at the *initial rollout frame* by clicking on camera images. The tracking module then continuously propagates these user-corrected keypoints throughout the execution, providing updated conditioning inputs to the diffusion process. This allows for human-controllable attention with reduced interaction cost.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted in both simulation (MuJoCo) and real-world environments using a Universal Robots UR5e manipulator. Tasks involved cable manipulation, ring placement, particle scooping (simulation), cup insertion, chain pick-and-place, and towel folding (real-world). Each task utilized two attention keypoints (one dynamic, one static). Datasets comprised teleoperated demonstrations (30 in simulation, 32 in real-world). Evaluation included In-Distribution (ID), Positional OOD, and Appearance OOD conditions. Comparison baselines included standard Diffusion Policy (DP) and Action-Chunking Transformer (ACT).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that GuidedAttention consistently improves robot manipulation performance, particularly under out-of-distribution (OOD) conditions, in both simulation and real-world settings.

**Simulation Experiments:**
- **Autonomous Performance (without human intervention):**
 - DP-GA (default variant) generally outperformed the DP baseline across most conditions. For example, it showed approximately a 15 percentage point (pp) improvement under ID conditions and around 10 pp improvement in most OOD conditions, with a slight underperformance in appearance OOD. This indicates that leveraging spatially structured attention cues is beneficial.
 - ACT performed comparably to DP in ID settings but experienced a substantial performance drop in OOD settings, highlighting the generalization capabilities of diffusion models.
- **Performance with Attention Correction (human-guided):**
 - When users optionally corrected attention keypoints once at the initial rollout frame, both DP-GA variants exhibited substantial performance improvements under distribution shift.
 - OOD performance increased by approximately 15–30 pp compared to their fully autonomous execution.
 - Consequently, DP-GA with human correction achieved 20–30 pp higher success rates than the DP baseline in both positional and appearance OOD settings. This indicates that initial human guidance, followed by automatic tracking, effectively mitigates failures in OOD conditions.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - Disabling randomized feature routing during training resulted in a significant performance drop (nearly 60 pp) when attention correction was enabled. This highlights the importance of training the policy to integrate override-based features.
 - Removing attention supervision (keypoint loss and feature-alignment loss) led to substantial performance degradation (around 30 pp without correction, and 65 pp with correction). This suggests that aligning attention features with task-relevant structures is crucial for robustness.
- **Additional Analyses:**
 - The method demonstrated robustness to moderate noise in both tracking and user annotation, with performance degrading gradually while still maintaining advantages over baselines.
 - Compared to marker overlay prompting (MOP), GuidedAttention consistently outperformed MOP variants by over 20 pp in both ID and OOD conditions on the Ring task, indicating that simply overlaying markers is less effective than explicit attention prediction and supervision.
 - The framework showed effective use of multiple cameras and flexibility in partially overriding keypoints and camera views, emphasizing the importance of selecting task-relevant viewpoints rather than merely increasing camera count.

**Real-World Experiments:**
- **ID Conditions:** Both DP-GA variants outperformed baselines, showing 15–30 pp improvement over ACT and 5–15 pp over DP, even without OOD conditions. This confirmed the benefits of task-aligned attention cues in real environments.
- **Positional OOD Conditions:** DP-GA (autonomous) achieved slightly higher success rates than baselines. With optional human attention correction, performance further boosted by 20–40 pp, while baselines experienced significant degradation.
- **Appearance OOD (Towel Fold):** Under severe visual distractors, human attention correction in DP-GA led to a substantial recovery (approximately 80 pp improvement over its autonomous execution and 40 pp over the DP baseline). This highlights the effectiveness of human guidance in highly challenging visual conditions.
- **Qualitative Evaluation:** Visualizations confirmed that DP-GA autonomously maintained focus on task-relevant structures in OOD conditions (e.g., Chain Pick-and-Place). Furthermore, initial human correction, followed by tracking, enabled successful task completion in cases where autonomous attention failed due to severe visual clutter (e.g., Towel Fold with appearance OOD).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents GuidedAttention, a framework that addresses critical limitations in end-to-end visuomotor policies, offering several significant contributions and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Interpretability and Human-in-the-Loop Interaction:** By introducing an explicit and interpretable visual attention representation, GuidedAttention allows human operators to understand the policy's visual focus. This transparency enables a more intuitive human-in-the-loop interaction paradigm where users can optionally correct the policy's attention with minimal effort (a one-time correction at rollout initialization), rather than continuously supervising or re-training the entire system. This bridges the gap between opaque end-to-end learning and human guidance.

- **Improved Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Robustness:** The work demonstrates that this human-correctable attention significantly improves robot manipulation performance, particularly under challenging positional and appearance OOD conditions, in both simulation and real-world scenarios. This enhanced robustness is crucial for deploying robots in diverse and unstructured environments where perfectly matching training data is impractical. The ability to recover from perception failures through targeted human input makes the system more reliable and generalizable.

- **Reduced Annotation and Supervision Burden:** The framework minimizes the annotation effort during training by requiring keypoint annotations only in the initial frame of demonstrations, with subsequent frames tracked automatically. During deployment, human intervention is optional and limited to a single correction per rollout, which is then propagated. This efficiency makes the approach practical for real-world applications where extensive data annotation or continuous human oversight is prohibitive.

- **Foundation for More Trustworthy and Deployable Robotic Systems:** By providing a mechanism for humans to understand and correct robot behavior, GuidedAttention contributes to the development of more trustworthy robotic systems. Operators can diagnose issues and intervene effectively, fostering confidence in autonomous capabilities while retaining a safety net for unexpected situations. This moves beyond purely black-box learning towards systems that can effectively collaborate with human users.

- **Guidance for Future Research:** This work encourages future research in several related areas, including the development of richer or adaptive attention representations for more complex tasks, explicit handling of depth and occlusion for keypoints, and improvements in the robustness of underlying tracking modules. It also highlights the broader importance of developing human-interpretable representations and human-in-the-loop interaction mechanisms for other end-to-end robot learning applications.

In summary, GuidedAttention represents a step towards more robust, interpretable, and user-friendly robot manipulation systems, leveraging human cognitive abilities to complement autonomous learning in challenging and dynamic real-world environments.
