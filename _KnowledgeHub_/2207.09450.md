# Human-to-Robot Imitation in the Wild

> [!summary] Summary
> A robot learning algorithm enables robots to acquire complex manipulation skills by observing unstructured human videos, demonstrating successful application across 20 diverse real-world tasks with efficient iterative refinement. The system effectively addresses the human-robot embodiment mismatch by learning from environmental changes rather than direct action imitation.

## Problem

- Robot manipulation successes are largely confined to simulations or highly controlled laboratory settings due to data inefficiency and safety concerns in real-world deployment.
- Traditional robot learning methods (Reinforcement Learning, Imitation Learning) are either data-hungry, unsafe for real-world exploration, or require expensive, time-consuming expert demonstrations.
- A significant embodiment mismatch, lack of explicit action/reward signals, and sample inefficiency hinder direct robot learning from ubiquitous, unstructured human video data.

## Method

- **WHIRL** (In-the-Wild Human Imitating Robot Learning) follows a ==Watch, Repeat, Improve== paradigm, starting by extracting human priors from third-person video demonstrations.
- Extracted human hand positions, orientations, and interaction events are converted into robot-executable ==action priors== (waypoints, gripper commands).
- A ==residual policy==, learned using ==Conditional Variational Auto-Encoders (CVAEs)==, is iteratively refined through real-world interaction using a ==sampling-based optimization (CEM-like)== guided by an ==agent-agnostic video alignment objective==.

## Results

- WHIRL was successfully applied to **20 diverse manipulation tasks** in unstructured real-world settings, with training requiring only a few hours per task.
- For core tasks, success rates consistently increased over **3 training iterations**, improving from approximately **43%** to **83%** for the drawer task and **40%** to **92%** for the door task.
- The method significantly outperformed state-of-the-art offline Reinforcement Learning baselines and Behavior Cloning, demonstrating robust generalization to new task instances, different scenes, and related tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human videos, despite embodiment mismatch, can provide valuable initial priors for robot skill learning in unstructured environments.
> - An agent-agnostic video alignment objective, utilizing inpainting and action recognition embeddings, effectively bridges the human-robot embodiment gap by focusing on environmental changes rather than specific movements.
> - An iterative "Watch, Repeat, Improve" paradigm combining residual learning, conditional variational auto-encoders (CVAEs), and a sampling-based optimization enables safe and sample-efficient real-world policy refinement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation has seen significant advancements in recent years, enabling robots to perform complex tasks like grasping, pushing, and even solving Rubik's cubes. However, as the authors highlight, a major bottleneck has been the **confinement of most successful experiments to simulated environments or highly controlled laboratory tabletop settings**. This limitation stems primarily from two challenges: **data inefficiency** and **safety concerns** in real-world deployment.

Traditional approaches to robot learning often fall into two main categories:
1. **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** While powerful in simulation, RL is notoriously data-hungry and can be unsafe in the real world due to extensive trial-and-error exploration. Defining structured reward functions for real-world tasks is also a significant challenge.
2. **Imitation Learning (IL):** IL allows robots to learn from expert demonstrations. However, common IL methods typically require numerous kinesthetic (human physically guiding the robot) or teleoperated demonstrations, which are expensive, time-consuming, and difficult to generalize to new settings or different robots. One-shot imitation methods exist but often require large pre-training datasets or many interactions.

Simultaneously, the field of **computer vision** has made enormous strides in processing passive, unstructured human video data. Techniques for human detection, pose estimation, action recognition, and video understanding are now highly sophisticated. This abundance of "in-the-wild" human video data (e.g., from YouTube) represents a vast, untapped resource for robot learning.

This paper addresses the critical gap between these two areas: how to **leverage the rich, readily available human video data to teach robots complex manipulation skills in unstructured, real-world environments**. Previous attempts at visual imitation learning have faced significant challenges:
- **Embodiment mismatch:** Humans and robots have different morphologies, making direct imitation difficult.
- **Lack of action information:** Human videos only provide visual observations, not underlying actions.
- **Absence of task information/rewards:** Beyond pixels, there's no inherent reward signal.
- **Sample inefficiency:** Many end-to-end learning approaches still require vast amounts of data.
- **Domain adaptation issues:** Models trained on human data often don't directly transfer to robots.

"Human-to-Robot Imitation in the Wild" (WHIRL) positions itself as a crucial step towards bridging this gap. It proposes an efficient **one-shot robot learning algorithm** that directly learns from unstructured human videos and can generalize to new tasks. By combining advanced computer vision techniques to extract meaningful "priors" from human demonstrations with an efficient, interaction-based policy refinement scheme, WHIRL aims to make real-world robot manipulation scalable and safe, moving it decisively out of the lab. This work stands out by demonstrating capabilities on 20 diverse tasks across various "in the wild" settings, a scale unprecedented for such direct human-to-robot visual imitation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **enable robots to learn complex manipulation skills in unconstrained, "in-the-wild" environments by observing human demonstrations**. This ambitious goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Overcome Data Bottleneck:** Current robot learning methods are severely limited by the cost and difficulty of collecting large-scale, high-quality robot demonstration data (e.g., kinesthetic teaching or teleoperation). The vast amount of publicly available, unstructured human video data on the internet (e.g., YouTube) represents a potentially unlimited, cheap, and diverse source of task demonstrations. The paper aims to unlock this resource.
2. **Move Beyond Lab Settings:** The success of robot manipulation has largely been confined to controlled lab environments or simulations. The authors are motivated to enable robots to operate effectively in diverse, uncalibrated, and dynamic real-world settings like homes, offices, or kitchens, which are characterized by varying lighting, backgrounds, and object instances. This requires robust generalization capabilities.
3. **Enhance Safety and Efficiency:** Traditional RL methods, while powerful, are often too sample-inefficient and potentially unsafe for real-world robots due to extensive exploration. Similarly, naive imitation of human actions can be dangerous if not properly adapted for robot morphology and capabilities. The paper seeks to develop a learning framework that is both sample-efficient (requiring minimal robot interaction) and inherently safe, by grounding robot actions in reasonable human priors.
4. **Bridge the Embodiment Gap:** A fundamental challenge in human-to-robot imitation is the significant difference in physical structure and capabilities (e.g., hand size, degrees of freedom) between humans and robots. A core objective is to develop a method that can abstract the "intent" of the human demonstration, rather than directly mimicking actions, allowing the robot to achieve the task goal despite morphological differences.
5. **Achieve One-Shot Learning with Generalization:** The ideal scenario is for a robot to learn a new task from a single human demonstration ("one-shot"). This paper aims not only for one-shot learning but also for policies that can generalize effectively to new instances of the same task, different scenes, and even other related tasks, without requiring extensive re-training.

In essence, the paper seeks to develop a practical, scalable, and robust solution for teaching robots new skills by watching human videos, thereby democratizing robot learning and expanding its applicability to everyday scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **WHIRL (In-the-Wild Human Imitating Robot Learning)**, is structured around a "Watch, Repeat, Improve" paradigm. It combines computer vision techniques for extracting human priors with an efficient, sampling-based policy optimization in the real world.

**A. "Watch": Extracting and Converting Human Priors**
The first crucial step is to obtain a rough understanding of the human's intent and action from a third-person video demonstration ($V_k$). This "prior" serves as an initial guideline for the robot.
1. **Extracting Hand and Interaction Information:** The system processes each frame of the human video using advanced computer vision models:
 - **Hand Position and Orientation:** It uses the 100DOH (100 Days of Hands) detection model and the MANO (Minimal Hand) parameterization to estimate 3D hand positions ($h_t$) and wrist orientations ($\theta_{hand}$).
 - **Interaction Detection:** The 100DOH model is also employed to detect discrete contact events ($c_t$) between the human hand and objects (e.g., no contact, contact with portable/fixed object).
 - **Smoothing:** A Savitzky-Golay filter is applied to smooth noisy contact detections, identifying precise interaction start ($t_{interaction}$) and end ($t_{end}$) times.
 - **Waypoint Extraction:** From smoothed detections, key interaction waypoints are sampled: initial contact ($h_{interaction}$), intermediate points ($h_{mid}$), and final release ($h_{end}$). Binary gripper open/close commands ($o_{1:T}$) are also inferred.
 - The extracted prior is a set of interaction waypoints ($h$), wrist rotation ($\theta_{hand}$), and gripper commands ($o_{1:T}$).
2. **Converting to Robot Priors:** The extracted human-centric priors are then converted into the robot's action space. Using depth images from an external camera and a mapping function ($f_{map}$), the human waypoints are projected to the robot's coordinate frame ($w_{interaction}, w_{mid}, w_{end}$). Robot-specific heuristics convert the wrist orientation and gripper commands into robot-executable parameters ($\theta_{YPR}, g_{1:T}$). This combined set of robot actions forms the initial robot prior ($\Psi_k$).

**B. "Repeat" & "Improve": Policy Learning via Interaction**
Direct execution of the robot prior is insufficient due to embodiment mismatch, detection errors, and calibration inaccuracies. Thus, WHIRL iteratively refines its policy through real-world interaction, prioritizing safety, efficiency, and broad exploration.
1. **Policy Structure (Residual Learning with CVAEs):**
 - WHIRL learns a residual policy, $\pi(\Psi_k, V_k) = \Delta\Psi_k$, which outputs a small deviation from the initial prior. This residual learning approach ensures safety by keeping actions close to the reasonable human prior and enables efficient exploration around it.
 - **Conditional Variational Auto-Encoders (CVAEs)** are used for both the task and exploration policies. CVAEs are well-suited for capturing multi-modal distributions (multiple ways to perform a task) and conditioning on the input human video embedding $\phi(V_k)$.
2. **Two Types of Policies:**
 - **Task Policy ($\pi$):** Aims to achieve task success by minimizing the difference between the robot's trajectory and the human demonstration.
 - **Exploration Policy ($\pi_{exp}$):** Designed to maximize the "change" the robot causes in the environment. This encourages exploration beyond the immediate vicinity of the prior, helping to avoid local minima. The "change" is quantified as the maximum pairwise L2 distance between frame embeddings of the robot's recorded video ($|| \Phi_f(R_{k,i}) - \Phi_f(R_{k,j}) ||_2$).
3. **Agent-Agnostic Video Alignment (Objective Function):** This is a core innovation for evaluating robot performance against human demonstrations without requiring strict correspondences.
 - **Inpainting:** To overcome embodiment mismatch, both human and robot videos are processed using **Copy-Paste Networks** to mask out and inpaint (fill in) the agent (human or robot). This creates an "agent-agnostic" view of the environment's state changes.
 - **Action Recognition Embedding:** The inpainted videos are then passed through a pre-trained action recognition model (e.g., SlowFast 3D ResNets, Multi-Moments) to obtain a high-level video embedding, $\Phi$.
 - **Objective:** The objective function for policy optimization is the L2 distance between the embedded human video and the embedded robot video: $|| \Phi(V) - \Phi(R_k) ||_2$. This function implicitly captures task success by comparing the resulting environmental state changes.
4. **Sampling-based Optimization (CEM-like):**
 - WHIRL employs a zeroth-order optimization procedure similar to the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) for sample efficiency.
 - **Iteration 1:** Initialize by sampling $\Delta\Psi_k$ from a Gaussian distribution around the prior.
 - **Execution:** Execute $M$ sampled actions ($\Psi_k + \Delta\Psi_{k,m}$) in the real world and record robot videos ($R_{k,m}$).
 - **Evaluation:** Compute the agent-agnostic cost for each robot video.
 - **Elite Selection:** Select the top-performing "elite" samples (e.g., 10 out of 30) based on the task policy cost, and samples maximizing "change" for the exploration policy.
 - **Policy Refinement:** Fit the CVAEs for both task ($\pi$) and exploration ($\pi_{exp}$) policies to these elite samples.
 - **Repeat:** This process is repeated for a few iterations until convergence. During subsequent iterations, samples are drawn from the refined policies instead of just a Gaussian.

**Hardware and Setup:** The experiments use the **Stretch Robot** (a mobile base with a 6 DoF arm and gripper) and an **Intel Realsense D415 depth camera**. This mobile setup is crucial for enabling "in the wild" deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents a comprehensive evaluation of WHIRL across various real-world tasks and scenarios, demonstrating its effectiveness and robustness.

1. **Scalability to Diverse "In the Wild" Tasks:**
 - WHIRL was successfully applied to **20 different manipulation tasks** in diverse, unstructured real-world settings (e.g., opening/closing drawers, doors, dishwashers, fridges; placing objects on shelves; folding shirts; cleaning whiteboards; turning on taps).
 - The training process was efficient, requiring only a few hours per task, even in varied locations and settings. This is a significant achievement in moving robot learning out of controlled lab environments.

2. **Effective Iterative Improvement:**
 - For core tasks like opening drawers, doors, and dishwashers, WHIRL consistently showed a **clear increase in success rates over 3 training iterations**. For instance, the drawer task's success rate improved from ~43% (initial prior) to 83%, and the door task from ~40% to 92%.
 - This demonstrates that the iterative policy refinement using real-world interactions is highly effective in correcting inaccuracies from the initial human prior and adapting to the robot's specific embodiment and environment.

3. **Outperformance of State-of-the-Art Baselines:**
 - WHIRL **strongly outperforms several offline Reinforcement Learning (RL) baselines** (e.g., CQL, CQL-ours, CQL-CycleGAN, CQL-TCN) and **Behavior Cloning (BC)**.
 - Offline RL methods struggled significantly with the limited datasets typical for real-world scenarios, highlighting their data inefficiency and difficulty in learning without online interaction.
 - Behavior Cloning, even when using WHIRL's agent-agnostic objective, performed worse than WHIRL, underscoring the importance of WHIRL's iterative refinement and exploration. The superior performance of CQL-ours and BC over other offline RL methods indicated that WHIRL's agent-agnostic objective function itself is very effective in distinguishing between good and bad trajectories.

4. **Generalization Capabilities:**
 - **Generalization to New Instances:** Policies trained with WHIRL showed improved success rates on *held-out instances* of the same task (e.g., a different drawer or door), demonstrating the ability to generalize within the same task category.
 - **Generalization to New Scenes:** When tested on tasks in *entirely new scenes* with different camera angles, geometry, and calibrations, WHIRL still showed improvement in success rates, though typically lower than on training scenes. This indicates robustness to visual changes in novel environments.
 - **Task-Level Generalization:** Policies trained on one task (e.g., drawer opening) showed some degree of performance improvement when directly applied to a *different but related task* (e.g., door opening). This suggests that WHIRL learns more generalizable motor skills or task structures.
 - **Multi-task Generalization:** A single policy trained across multiple tasks (drawer, door, dishwasher) demonstrated overall improvement across iterations, albeit with slightly lower success rates compared to individual task policies. This shows potential for learning more versatile agents.

5. **Ablation Studies Confirm Key Components' Importance:**
 - **Agent-Agnostic Objective is Crucial:** Without the novel agent-agnostic video alignment objective function (which uses inpainting and action recognition embeddings), there was almost no gain in success rate. This confirms that this objective is vital for correctly evaluating and selecting successful robot trajectories by focusing on environmental changes rather than agent-specific movements.
 - **Exploration Policy Boosts Efficiency:** While WHIRL could learn without the explicit "maximize change" exploration policy, learning was slower and success rates were lower. This highlights the exploration policy's role in efficiently searching the action space and avoiding local minima.
 - **Objective Function Discriminates Success:** The agent-agnostic cost function successfully differentiated between "failure," "partial success," and "success" trajectories, showing a clear reduction in cost as task success increased. This validates its utility as a reliable reward signal.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

"Human-to-Robot Imitation in the Wild" represents a significant advancement in robot learning, with the potential for broad impact across several dimensions:

1. **Democratizing Robot Skill Acquisition:** By enabling robots to learn from readily available, unstructured human videos, WHIRL significantly lowers the barrier to entry for teaching robots new skills. This moves beyond expensive and specialized data collection methods like kinesthetic teaching or teleoperation, making robot skill acquisition more accessible and scalable. This could profoundly accelerate the development and deployment of robots in homes and workplaces.

2. **Scalability to Real-World Complexity:** The demonstration of WHIRL's effectiveness across 20 diverse manipulation tasks in various "in-the-wild" settings is a major breakthrough. It signifies a crucial step away from lab-confined robot learning, proving that robots can acquire practical skills in dynamic, uncalibrated, and cluttered environments that closely resemble real-world scenarios. This is critical for developing truly useful general-purpose robots.

3. **Leveraging Ubiquitous Data Sources:** The ability to learn from passive human videos—a virtually limitless data source found on platforms like YouTube—unlocks unprecedented potential for robot training. This paradigm shift could allow robots to learn from the collective human experience, continuously expanding their repertoire of skills without explicit expert supervision or extensive real-world interaction data.

4. **Methodological Innovations:** The paper's core methodological contributions are highly impactful:
 - The **agent-agnostic video alignment objective** is a novel and effective solution to the embodiment mismatch problem, allowing for robust comparison between human and robot actions based on task outcome rather than exact motion. This approach has implications for other cross-embodiment learning problems.
 - The **efficient sampling-based policy optimization** combined with residual learning and a tailored exploration strategy addresses the critical need for sample efficiency and safety in real-world robot learning, making online interaction feasible.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** WHIRL lays a strong foundation for future work in several areas. The authors explicitly state the ambition to learn policies directly from passive offline video datasets (e.g., YouTube) without *any* online interactions or human demonstrations, which would be the ultimate goal of "learning by watching." Further research could focus on improving the robustness of human prior extraction, enhancing generalization to even more visually divergent scenes, and integrating multi-modal sensing for richer environmental understanding.

In conclusion, WHIRL represents a pivotal step towards building more autonomous, adaptable, and general-purpose robots that can learn and operate effectively in the complex, unstructured environments of the real world by leveraging the vast wealth of human observational data.
