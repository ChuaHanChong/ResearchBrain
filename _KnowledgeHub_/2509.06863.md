# floq: Training Critics via Flow-Matching for Scaling Compute in Value-Based RL

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and the University of Warsaw introduce "floq," a novel Q-function parameterization that utilizes flow-matching to iteratively predict Q-values, incorporating dense step-wise supervision. This approach achieved nearly 1.8x improved performance over state-of-the-art FQL on challenging offline RL benchmarks and enabled more effective capacity scaling compared to monolithic critics or ResNets under matched compute budgets.

## Problem

- Traditional value-based RL Q-functions are monolithic, struggling to leverage deep networks effectively and suffering from generalization issues, especially in offline RL.
- Existing iterative Q-function approaches (e.g., ResNets) lack dense, step-wise supervision, limiting their ability to fully exploit iterative computation benefits.
- There is a need for architectural designs that can scale Q-function capacity in a controlled and effective manner, and enable test-time scaling for value function estimation.

## Method

- **floq** parameterizes the Q-function as a time-dependent, state-action-conditioned ==velocity field== that transforms a scalar noise variable into a Q-value through numerical integration.
- It adapts a linear flow-matching objective for TD-learning, providing dense supervision to the velocity field at every integration step to learn the path from noise to target Q-value.
- Three key design choices—a wide initial noise sampling range, categorical HL-Gauss embedding for interpolant inputs, and Fourier-basis embedding for time—were crucial to stabilize training and prevent flow collapse.

## Results

- floq consistently outperformed prior state-of-the-art offline RL methods, achieving nearly **1.8x** improved performance compared to FQL on challenging "hard" OGBench environments.
- Increasing the number of flow integration steps (K) generally improved floq's performance, demonstrating effective capacity scaling without increasing the number of network parameters.
- Agents trained with floq provided stronger initial policies for online fine-tuning, adapted more rapidly during online interaction, and converged to higher final success rates across tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reframing Q-value estimation as an iterative flow-matching process with dense step-wise supervision unlocks greater expressivity and scalability for critics.
> - The sequential integration process of floq, where each step builds upon the previous, is more effective for scaling critic capacity than simply increasing network depth via ResNets or parallelizing with monolithic ensembles under matched compute.
> - Specific design choices for handling the initial noise, interpolant inputs, and time embedding are critical for the stability and performance of flow-matching critics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning (RL), specifically addressing challenges in Q-value estimation, which is central to value-based RL methods. Modern large-scale machine learning, exemplified by Transformers and diffusion models, has demonstrated significant success through iterative computation and dense supervision during intermediate steps. In these domains, complex outputs are generated by composing sequences of simpler operations, with feedback provided at each stage (e.g., next token prediction in language models, denoising steps in diffusion models).

In contrast, traditional temporal difference (TD) learning methods for Q-functions typically represent value functions monolithically, without iterative computation. While Q-functions are known to be complex and difficult to fit accurately, standard TD learning often struggles to fully leverage the capacity of deep networks, leading to generalization issues. These challenges are amplified in offline RL settings, where learning must occur from static datasets. Previous attempts to introduce iterative computation into Q-functions, such as using ResNets, have shown modest gains but typically lack the dense, step-wise supervision present in other successful iterative models. Moreover, these ResNet-based approaches often require specific normalization and regularization strategies for stable training.

Concurrently, there has been a growing emphasis in offline RL on developing more expressive generative policy classes, such as diffusion models and flow-based policies, to overcome bottlenecks in policy learning. However, less attention has been paid to enhancing the expressivity and scaling of the Q-function itself. Similarly, research into "test-time scaling" in RL has largely focused on planning with learned world models, where increased computation at deployment improves performance. This paper introduces a novel approach to apply iterative computation and test-time scaling directly to the value function estimation.

This work specifically addresses the gap in scaling Q-functions by drawing inspiration from flow-matching techniques, typically used in generative modeling. It proposes a method that not only introduces iterative computation to Q-function estimation but also incorporates dense, step-wise supervision, aiming to achieve performance and scaling benefits analogous to those observed in other advanced machine learning models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the accuracy and scalability of Q-value estimation in reinforcement learning by adopting principles of iterative computation and dense supervision, inspired by modern generative models.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several key observations and challenges:

- **Limitations of Monolithic Q-functions:** Standard Q-functions, represented by single, monolithic neural networks, struggle to effectively learn the complex functions required for accurate value estimation. This often leads to poor generalization, particularly when using deep networks, and is exacerbated in the offline RL setting where agents learn solely from fixed datasets.
- **Success of Iterative Computation in Other ML Fields:** Large-scale machine learning models like Transformers and diffusion models achieve high performance and generalization by iteratively refining outputs and receiving dense supervision at intermediate steps. The authors hypothesize that applying this paradigm to Q-function learning could yield similar benefits.
- **Inadequacy of Existing Iterative RL Approaches:** While ResNets introduce a form of iterative computation to Q-functions, they lack dense, step-wise supervision for each residual block. The entire supervision signal typically comes from the final prediction, which may limit their ability to fully exploit the benefits of iterative computation.
- **Need for Scalable Q-function Capacity:** There is a need for architectural designs that can scale Q-function capacity in a more controlled and effective manner than simply increasing network depth or width, or using ensembles, especially given the computational demands of complex RL tasks.
- **Enabling Test-Time Scaling for Value Functions:** Current test-time scaling efforts in RL primarily focus on planning with world models. The authors are motivated to explore whether increased computation at inference time can also directly enhance the quality of value function estimates, analogous to how LLMs use more compute for better reasoning.

To address these motivations, the paper introduces "floq" (flow-matching Q-functions), aiming to:
1. **Parameterize the Q-function** as a velocity field that transforms a noise variable into a Q-value through numerical integration, thereby intrinsically incorporating iterative computation.
2. **Train this velocity field** using a flow-matching objective adapted for TD-learning, which provides dense supervision at every integration step.
3. **Demonstrate that this approach** allows for more fine-grained control and scaling of Q-function capacity by adjusting the number of integration steps.
4. **Evaluate floq's performance** against state-of-the-art monolithic Q-function architectures on challenging offline RL benchmarks and online fine-tuning tasks, showing improved performance and better scaling properties.
5. **Identify key design choices** that stabilize training and enable effective capacity scaling with this novel architecture.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, `floq` (flow-matching Q-functions), reframes Q-value prediction as a process of transforming a scalar noise variable into a Q-value through numerical integration of a learned velocity field. This design facilitates iterative computation and enables dense supervision during training.

#### 3.1. floq Parameterization

- **Velocity Field:** Unlike standard Q-networks that directly map state-action pairs $(s,a)$ to a scalar Q-value, `floq` parameterizes a time-dependent, state-action-conditioned velocity field $v_\theta(t, z | s, a)$. This field operates over a one-dimensional latent input $z \in \mathbb{R}$.
- **Flow Integration:** At time $t=0$, an initial noise variable $z(0)$ is sampled uniformly from an interval $[l, u]$. The velocity field $v_\theta$ is then numerically integrated over $K$ steps (e.g., using the Euler method) to transform $z(0)$ into a final Q-value sample, $Q(s, a, z):= \psi_\theta(1, z | s, a)$. The goal is for $\psi_\theta(1, z|s,a)$ to approximate a Dirac-Delta distribution centered at the true Q-value $Q_\pi(s,a)$ at $t=1$.
- **Iterative Computation:** Each integration step involves a call to the velocity field, where the input to the current step depends on the output of the previous step. This recursive dependence differentiates `floq` from simple ensembles and enables dynamic adjustment of Q-function complexity by varying $K$.

#### 3.2. Training Loss for floq

- **Bellman Bootstrapping:** `floq` adapts the linear flow-matching objective from generative modeling to incorporate Bellman bootstrapping, similar to TD-learning.
- **Target Network:** A target velocity field $\tilde{v}_\theta$ (a stale moving average of $v_\theta$) is used to compute target Q-values, consistent with practices in value-based RL.
- **TD-Target Computation:** For a given transition $(s, a, r, s')$, an action $a'$ is sampled from the current policy $\pi(\cdot|s')$. Multiple initial noise samples $z'_{j}$ are drawn, and the target flow $\tilde{v}_\theta$ is integrated to obtain Q-value samples $\psi_{\tilde\theta}(1, z'_j | s', a')$. These samples are averaged to estimate the target Q-value $Q_{\tilde\theta}(s', a')$. The bootstrapped TD-target is then $y(s, a) = r(s, a) + \gamma \cdot \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \psi_{\tilde\theta}(1, z'_j | s', a')$.
- **Flow-Matching Loss:** The velocity field $v_\theta$ is trained to minimize the squared error between its prediction $v_\theta(t, z(t) | s, a)$ and the slope of the linear interpolating path between the initial noise $z$ and the target Q-value $y(s, a)$. The interpolant is defined as $z(t) = (1-t) \cdot z + t \cdot y(s, a)$ for $t \sim \text{Unif}$. This forms the core loss: $\mathcal{L}_{\text{floq}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{z,t} \left[ \left\| v_\theta(t, z(t) | s, a) - (y(s, a) - z) \right\|^2_2 \right]$.

#### 3.3. Preventing Flow Collapse: Key Design Choices

Initial experiments revealed that a naive implementation of `floq` could collapse to a monolithic Q-network. Three crucial design choices were introduced to address this and enable `floq` to effectively utilize iterative computation:
1. **Distribution of the Initial Noise Sample $z(0)$:**
 - **Rationale:** The choice of the interval $[l, u]$ from which $z(0)$ is sampled significantly impacts the curvature of the flow trajectories. If the interval is too narrow or disjoint from target Q-values, the velocity field may not learn meaningful dependence on $z(t)$ and $t$, leading to collapse.
 - **Approach:** `floq` sets $u = Q_{\max}$ (0 for most tasks) and chooses $l \geq Q_{\min}$ to maximize the interval width $u-l$, subject to stable learning. This ensures overlap with target Q-values and encourages curved trajectories where the velocity field must use $z(t)$ and $t$ to predict customized velocities.
2. **Representing Interpolant Inputs $z(t)$ to the Velocity Network:**
 - **Rationale:** The scalar interpolant $z(t)$ changes magnitude throughout training (non-stationarity), which can destabilize the velocity network.
 - **Approach:** A categorical input representation of $z(t)$, inspired by HL-Gauss encoding, is used. This involves adding Gaussian noise to $z(t)$, then converting its probability density function into a categorical distribution over $N$ bins spanning the expected Q-value range. A larger standard deviation $\sigma$ is used (e.g., 16.0) to encourage broader bin coverage and reduce sensitivity to non-stationarity.
3. **Fourier-Basis Embedding of Time $t$:**
 - **Rationale:** To allow the velocity network $v_\theta(t, z(t))$ to meaningfully condition its predictions on the integration time $t$.
 - **Approach:** A 64-dimensional Fourier-basis embedding of $t$ is provided as input to the velocity network. This enables the network to produce distinct behaviors across different integration steps.

#### 3.4. Policy Training and Distillation

- `floq` is implemented on top of FQL (Flow Q-learning), which uses a flow-matching policy.
- To address the computational cost of policy gradient extraction from an iterative flow critic, a separate, one-step `distilled critic` $Q^{\text{distill}}_\psi(s, a)$ is trained to approximate the mean predictions of the `floq` critic $Q^{\text{flow}}_\theta(s, a, z)$. Policy updates are then performed on this distilled critic.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of `floq` was conducted across a suite of 50 challenging offline RL tasks from the OGBench benchmark, covering both locomotion and manipulation environments, as well as online fine-tuning scenarios.

#### 4.1. Main Offline RL Results

- **Superior Performance:** `floq` consistently outperformed prior state-of-the-art methods, including FQL (which uses a flow-matching policy but a monolithic Q-network), ReBRAC, DSRL, and SORL. On average, `floq` improved performance by nearly 1.8x compared to FQL on the more challenging "hard" environments (where FQL scored below 50% success rate).
- **Statistical Significance:** Rigorous statistical evaluation using metrics like median, interquartile mean (IQM), mean scores, optimality gap, and $P(X > Y)$ statistics confirmed that the performance gains of `floq` were significant, with no confidence interval overlap in comparisons with FQL.
- **Comparison to Distributional RL:** `floq` also demonstrated superior performance compared to IQN, a representative distributional RL approach.

#### 4.2. Main Online Fine-Tuning Results

- **Stronger Initialization and Adaptation:** When pre-trained offline and then fine-tuned online, `floq` agents provided a substantially stronger initial policy compared to FQL. They also adapted more rapidly during online interaction and converged to higher final success rates across challenging tasks.

#### 4.3. Understanding Scaling Properties and Behavior of floq

- **Effect of Integration Steps (K):** Increasing the number of flow integration steps (K) generally improved `floq`'s performance, particularly on harder tasks. `floq` often outperformed FQL even with as few as 4 steps. However, diminishing returns or slight performance degradation were observed at very high K (e.g., K=16 on antmaze-giant), suggesting a potential for overfitting or integration errors with excessive steps.
- **Comparison to Monolithic Ensembles:** `floq` (e.g., with 8 integration steps) significantly outperformed an ensemble of 8 monolithic critics, even when both approaches consumed the same total forward-pass compute (8x forward passes of a 4-layer MLP). This indicates that `floq`'s benefits stem from its sequential integration process and not merely parallel computation.
- **Comparison to ResNet Critics:** `floq` (using a 4-layer MLP as its velocity network) surpassed the performance of optimized ResNet critics (with 32 effective layers), even under a matched inference compute budget. This suggests that the dense, step-wise supervision inherent in `floq`'s flow-matching objective is more impactful than simply adding depth through residual connections without such intermediate supervision.
- **Importance of Dense Supervision (Training at All t):** A variant of `floq` trained only at $t=0$ (i.e., with single-step integration but still utilizing auxiliary tasks from noise sampling) outperformed FQL. However, the full `floq` (supervising the velocity field at all $t \in$ and using multiple integration steps) consistently achieved the best performance. This confirms that dense supervision across all intermediate integration steps is crucial for unlocking the full potential of `floq`.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies for floq

- **Interpolant $z(t)$ Embedding:** HL-Gauss embeddings for $z(t)$ were found to be critical for `floq`'s performance, significantly outperforming scalar or normalized scalar embeddings. Larger $\sigma$ values (for broader bin coverage) consistently led to stronger performance, indicating the importance of robustly handling the non-stationarity of $z(t)$ inputs.
- **Time $t$ Embedding:** Replacing the 64-dimensional Fourier-basis embedding of time $t$ with a simple scalar embedding resulted in a substantial performance drop across tasks. This highlights the necessity of rich, high-dimensional time embeddings for the velocity network to meaningfully condition its predictions on time.
- **Initial Noise Range $[l, u]$:** The width of the initial noise sampling interval $[l, u]$ significantly influenced performance. Optimal performance was observed at intermediate variance values, where the learned flow exhibited sufficient curvature to leverage iterative computation, but not so much as to make numerical integration overly challenging. The flow curvature was shown to increase with the noise range.
- **Policy Extraction Integration Steps:** `floq`'s performance was relatively robust to reducing the number of integration steps used specifically for policy updates, provided that the target value computation utilized a sufficient number of steps. This suggests that once accurate target Q-values are learned, the policy can be effectively distilled even with a reduced number of integration steps for its own updates.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The `floq` research presents a notable advancement in the domain of value-based reinforcement learning by introducing a novel architectural paradigm for Q-function estimation. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Novel Q-function Parameterization:** `floq` innovates by parameterizing the Q-function as a velocity field within a flow-matching generative model. This design inherently incorporates iterative computation into value estimation, a departure from traditional monolithic Q-networks. This new perspective opens avenues for designing more expressive and scalable critics.
- **Enhanced Performance and Generalization:** By leveraging iterative computation with dense, step-wise supervision, `floq` demonstrates consistent and substantial performance improvements over existing state-of-the-art offline RL algorithms on challenging benchmarks and in online fine-tuning scenarios. This suggests that the proposed approach can lead to more accurate Q-value estimates and, consequently, better learned policies.
- **Scalable Capacity and Compute Efficiency:** `floq` introduces a new axis for scaling Q-function capacity by allowing dynamic adjustment of the number of integration steps ($K$). The findings indicate that this method of scaling computation (sequential processing within a single network) is more effective for critic networks than simply increasing network depth via ResNets or parallelizing with ensembles, especially under matched compute budgets. It achieves these gains without increasing the number of network parameters, only the inference-time computation.
- **Enabling Test-Time Scaling for Value Functions:** This work is among the first to demonstrate that increasing inference-time compute for value functions can directly improve their quality, akin to "test-time scaling" observed in large language models. This capability provides a practical mechanism to trade off compute for performance at deployment.
- **Robustness through Design Principles:** The research provides specific design choices (wide initial noise distribution, categorical interpolant embedding, Fourier-basis time embedding) that are crucial for the stability and effectiveness of flow-matching critics. These prescriptive guidelines are valuable for practitioners and future research.
- **Broad Applicability:** The `floq` architecture is agnostic to the specific offline RL algorithm or policy used (as demonstrated by its integration with FQL). This modularity suggests that `floq` could be adopted with various existing and future RL frameworks, enhancing their critic capabilities.
- **Opens New Theoretical and Empirical Research Avenues:**
 - **Theoretical Quantification:** Future work can formally quantify the properties of iterative computation in `floq`, exploring how "error correction" or "backtracking" mechanisms might emerge in the continuous value space, similar to reasoning capabilities in LLMs.
 - **Representation Learning:** Investigating the feature learning mechanisms induced by `floq` compared to standard regression- or classification-based TD learning could provide insights into its representational benefits.
 - **Cascaded Critics and Adaptive Compute:** The inherent "cascaded" nature of `floq` (representing a family of critics with varying capacities within a single network) offers opportunities for adaptive network sizing, cross-validation of model complexity, and improved policy extraction efficiency.
 - **Combined Scaling Strategies:** Exploring the synergistic effects of combining `floq`'s sequential scaling with other methods like parallel ensembling or horizon reduction techniques could lead to even more powerful and efficient RL systems.

In conclusion, `floq` represents a significant step towards developing more powerful and scalable value functions in reinforcement learning, drawing parallels from successful iterative computation paradigms in other advanced machine learning domains.
