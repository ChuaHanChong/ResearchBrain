# Difference-Aware Retrieval Policies for Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Difference-Aware Retrieval Policies (DARP) introduces a semi-parametric imitation learning approach that improves policy robustness and generalization by implicitly applying manifold regularization. This is achieved by leveraging difference vectors between query states and their k-nearest neighbors from expert demonstrations, resulting in substantial performance gains across continuous control and robotic manipulation tasks, with success rates often improving by 15-35 percentage points over standard behavior cloning.

## Problem

- Standard Behavior Cloning (BC) policies exhibit brittleness and instability in long-horizon tasks due to covariate shift, where small errors accumulate and drive the agent into out-of-distribution states.
- BC policies do not explicitly manage policy variance or enforce smoothness, leading to arbitrary and unreliable actions in unseen or low-density regions of the state space.
- Many existing methods that address BC's limitations often require additional resources such as simulators, interactive expert feedback, or large quantities of sub-optimal data, moving beyond the pure BC regime.

## Method

- The research proposes Difference-Aware Retrieval Policies (**DARP**), a semi-parametric, ==retrieval-based== policy architecture that implicitly applies manifold regularization to enforce smoothness.
- DARP's policy network conditions its predictions on the *difference vectors* between a query state and its k-nearest neighbors from the expert dataset, along with the neighbor's state and action, thereby grounding predictions in local expert data.
- It utilizes permutation-invariant aggregation functions to combine these neighbor-derived candidate actions, allowing for robust prediction of unimodal or multimodal action distributions while being trained with a standard behavior cloning objective.

## Results

- DARP consistently outperformed standard behavior cloning (BC) across diverse tasks, achieving up to 5515.41 score in MuJoCo tasks (vs. 2658.40 for BC), improving robotic manipulation success rates by 15-25 percentage points (e.g., **85%** vs. **54%** on RoboCasa Drawer), and doubling BC's success rate on a real-world furniture assembly task (**92%** vs. **44%**).
- The method effectively scales to high-dimensional visual inputs (R3M embeddings), demonstrating average improvements of ~**35%** over BC in Robosuite tasks, and can model multimodal action distributions, achieving a **70%** success rate in the Push-T environment compared to BC's **48%**.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the inclusion of difference vectors and permutation-invariant aggregation are critical components for DARP's performance, and the method exhibits significant robustness to accumulated errors, enabling recovery from states where BC policies typically fail.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Manifold regularization, which improves policy smoothness and reduces variance, can be achieved implicitly through a specific policy architecture that aggregates information from k-nearest neighbors, eliminating the need for explicit regularization terms or hyperparameter tuning.
> - Reparameterizing the imitation learning problem by conditioning on relative differences (delta vectors) between a query state and its expert neighbors is crucial for robust generalization, especially in out-of-distribution states.
> - The use of permutation-invariant aggregation for combining neighbor-specific action proposals is essential for effectively leveraging local data, modeling complex action distributions, and preventing the blurring of distinct behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation Learning (IL), particularly via behavior cloning (BC), is a method for teaching robots complex behaviors from expert demonstrations. Despite its simplicity, BC often exhibits brittleness in practice, especially in long-horizon tasks. A primary cause of this fragility is covariate shift, where small errors accumulate during policy execution, driving the agent into states not adequately represented in the training data. In such out-of-distribution (OOD) regions, BC policies can become unstable and produce unreliable actions.

Various approaches have been developed to address compounding errors in imitation learning. These often involve going beyond the standard BC assumptions, requiring additional resources such as simulators, interactive expert feedback, large quantities of sub-optimal data, or task-specific knowledge. Examples include methods that actively query the expert during deployment (DAGGER, DAgger), use model-based planning, or generate synthetic corrective labels.

A separate line of research focuses on enforcing smoothness in learned policies, ensuring that nearby states map to similar actions. This can be achieved through data augmentation, global priors, temporal penalties, or explicit graph regularizers. However, these methods sometimes present their own drawbacks, such as lack of consistency guarantees for augmentation, potential blurring of distinct behaviors with global priors, limited scope of temporal penalties, or the need for hyperparameter tuning in graph regularizers.

The field also distinguishes between "global" and "local" learning methods. Global parametric models attempt to compress the entire demonstration dataset into a single function, which can be brittle under distribution shift. Local methods, conversely, adapt predictions based on neighborhoods of similar states, using non-parametric or semi-parametric operations on the training data. While local methods offer robustness by grounding predictions in observed data, their effectiveness is often dependent on the distance metric used, they can blur distinct actions, and their generalization may be limited by a sole reliance on absolute state similarities.

This work positions itself within the "pure BC regime," aiming to enhance policy stability and generalization using only expert state-action pairs, without additional supervision, feedback, or data collection. It seeks to bridge the gap between global and local learning by combining the robustness offered by local data-dependent methods with the stability provided by regularized global policy learning, thereby addressing the variance and generalization issues inherent in standard behavior cloning while adhering to its core assumptions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the generalization capabilities and robustness of imitation learning policies, specifically those trained via behavior cloning (BC), when faced with out-of-distribution (OOD) states during deployment. The core motivation stems from the inherent limitations of standard BC.

**Detailed Objectives and Motivation:**

1. **Mitigate Covariate Shift and Compounding Errors:** Standard BC policies learn a mapping from states to actions based on a fixed dataset of expert demonstrations. During closed-loop deployment, small prediction errors can accumulate, causing the agent to drift into states that were not part of the training distribution. BC policies often perform arbitrarily in these OOD regions, leading to unstable and unreliable behavior and ultimately task failure. The research aims to develop a method that can perform reliably even when encountering states slightly outside the expert data manifold.

2. **Reduce Policy Variance and Enhance Smoothness:** From a statistical perspective, BC primarily minimizes the supervised risk on expert states, which controls bias on the training distribution. However, it does not explicitly manage policy variance, particularly in low-density regions of the state space. This lack of control allows for arbitrary policy fluctuations in unseen states. The work is motivated by the idea that enforcing smoothness, where nearby states yield similar predicted actions, can reduce variance and improve rollout stability.

3. **Operate within the Pure Behavior Cloning Regime:** A key motivation is to achieve these improvements without relaxing the fundamental assumptions of standard behavior cloning. This means the proposed solution should not require additional data collection, online expert interaction, access to simulators, sub-optimal data, or task-specific knowledge. The goal is to enhance the core BC paradigm using *only* the provided expert state-action pairs.

4. **Combine Strengths of Local and Global Learning:** The authors observe that "global" parametric models can be brittle under distribution shift, while "local" non-parametric methods, though robust to distribution shift due to their data grounding, may struggle with generalization if they simply average actions or rely on naive distance metrics. The objective is to devise an approach that integrates the data-grounded robustness of local methods with the stability and generalization capabilities of a structured, regularized global policy.

5. **Reparameterize the Imitation Learning Problem:** The paper proposes to reframe the imitation learning problem not as learning a direct state-to-action mapping, but rather in terms of the local neighborhood structure of the expert data. Specifically, the motivation is to leverage the relative differences between query states and their nearest neighbors to inform action predictions, thereby implicitly capturing local dynamics and expert intent in a context-aware manner. This reparameterization is intended to make predictions more robust and locally consistent.

6. **Develop a Parameter-Free Smoothing Mechanism:** While explicit regularization terms can enforce smoothness, they often introduce additional hyperparameters (e.g., λ for the regularization weight) that require careful tuning. A motivation for this work is to achieve the benefits of such smoothing implicitly through architectural design, thereby removing the need for manual hyperparameter selection for regularization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on **Difference-Aware Retrieval Policies for Imitation Learning (DARP)**, a semi-parametric retrieval-based approach that reparameterizes imitation learning by emphasizing local neighborhood structure and relative state differences. The approach is built upon theoretical insights into manifold regularization and its implicit realization through architectural design.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Framework: Manifold Regularization and Implicit Smoothing

The paper first establishes a theoretical foundation by introducing two concepts:

- **Manifold Regularized Imitation Learning (MRIL):** This approach extends the standard behavior cloning objective (L_BC) with an additional smoothness penalty (L_S). The penalty encourages nearby states in the expert dataset to be mapped to similar actions, thereby enforcing local consistency. Mathematically, L_MRIL(f) = L_BC(f) + λ * L_S(f), where L_S involves a sum of squared differences between policy predictions for a state and its kernel-weighted k-nearest neighbors. This smoothness term is shown to correspond to a graph Laplacian penalty, which is known to enforce Tikhonov regularization on the learned policy.
 - **Theoretical Guarantees (Theorem 1):** MRIL is proven to reduce estimator variance, provide a uniform bound on the local Lipschitz constant of the learned policy (ensuring smoothness), and lead to sublinear error accumulation during closed-loop rollouts, in contrast to the linear accumulation seen in vanilla BC. These properties collectively suggest improved generalization and stability.
 - **Limitations:** MRIL requires tuning the regularization hyperparameter λ and modifies the optimization objective, which could potentially complicate the training process.

- **Implicit Manifold Regularization (iMRIL):** To address MRIL's limitations, the paper proposes achieving the same manifold regularization effect implicitly through a modified policy architecture, without altering the standard BC objective or introducing additional hyperparameters.
 - **Architectural Idea:** Instead of learning a standard feedforward policy `f(s)` and applying a smoothness penalty, iMRIL embeds neighborhood aggregation directly into the policy's parameterization. The action predictor `f̂(s)` is defined as an aggregation (e.g., average) of predictions from a base network `fθ` applied to the k-nearest neighbor states `{s*_i}`. The model `fθ` is then trained with a standard BC objective.
 - **Theoretical Equivalence (Theorem 2):** iMRIL is formally proven to be equivalent to a parameter-free form of Laplacian regularization. The neighbor aggregation step acts as a fixed spectral filter (specifically, `φ(μ) = 1 - μ`) on the k-NN graph Laplacian, which inherently preserves low-frequency modes (smooth variations) and suppresses high-frequency modes (noisy fluctuations). This implicitly provides a smoothing effect akin to MRIL with an effective λ ≈ 1, but without explicit tuning.

#### 3.2. Practical Instantiation: Difference-Aware Retrieval Policies (DARP)

DARP is the practical realization of the iMRIL framework, incorporating specific design choices for policy parameterization and aggregation:

1. **Difference-Based Parameterization of `fθ`:** The core innovation in DARP is to condition the base network `fθ` not just on neighbor states, but on a tuple of `(neighbor state s*_i, neighbor action a*_i, and the difference vector ∆s_i = s*_i - s_q)` between the neighbor state and the query state `s_q`. This `difference-aware` context allows `fθ` to learn how expert actions vary under local perturbations, adaptively proposing actions for each neighbor based on its *relative* position to the query state.
 - The candidate action for each neighbor `i` is computed as `a'_i = fθ(s*_i, a*_i, ∆s_i)`.

2. **Permutation-Invariant Aggregation `gψ`:** Initially, DARP can use a simple average of the candidate actions: `ˆa_q = (1/k) * Σ a'_i`. However, the methodology extends this to more expressive, permutation-invariant aggregation functions `gψ` (e.g., Set Transformer or DeepSets). This generalization allows DARP to model richer action distributions (e.g., Gaussian Mixture Models or diffusion models) rather than being restricted to unimodal L2-regression.
 - For multimodal distributions, `gψ` predicts the parameters `α` of an action distribution `p(a_q; α)`.
 - The overall action prediction for a query state `s_q` is then `ˆa_q = gψ({fθ(s*_i, a*_i, ∆s_i = s*_i - s_q)}i∈Nk(s_q))`.

3. **Training Objective:** DARP is trained using a standard imitation learning objective. For unimodal actions, this is an L2 regression loss: `arg min_θ E_(s*_q,a*_q)∼D* [||f_DARP(s*_q) - a*_q||^2]`. For multimodal action distributions, it's a maximum likelihood objective: `arg max_θ E_(s*_q,a*_q)∼D* [log p(a*_q; α_θ(s*_q))]`.

4. **Retrieval Mechanism:** For any given query state `s_q`, DARP first retrieves its `k`-nearest neighbors `N_k(s_q)` from the expert demonstration dataset `D*`. The distance function `d(s_q, s*_i)` typically uses Euclidean distance in a pre-trained embedding space. For sequential data, a modified distance metric can incorporate historical states with exponential decay to prioritize recent context.

#### 3.3. Implementation Details

- **Policy Backbones:** `fθ` can be implemented with standard feedforward or convolutional neural networks. The aggregation function `gψ` can be a simple average or a more complex set-compliant neural model.
- **Evaluation Environments:** Experiments cover continuous control (MuJoCo), robotic manipulation (Robosuite, RoboCasa) with both low-dimensional states and high-dimensional visual features (R3M embeddings), and specialized discontinuous environments.
- **Baselines:** Comparisons are made against R&P (nearest neighbor action), LWR (locally weighted regression), standard BC, REGENT (transformer-based in-context learning), and MRIL (explicitly smoothed DARP).

This comprehensive methodology allows DARP to enforce implicit smoothness and ground predictions in local expert data, providing a principled way to improve BC performance without requiring additional data or supervision.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of DARP addresses three primary questions, demonstrating consistent performance improvements and insights into its architectural components.

#### 4.1. Consistent Outperformance of Standard Behavior Cloning (Q1)

- **Low-Dimensional State Representations:** DARP consistently outperforms standard behavior cloning (BC) across a range of tasks with low-dimensional state features.
 - In MuJoCo continuous control tasks (Hopper, Ant, Walker, HalfCheetah), DARP achieves significant score improvements, ranging from **3545.57 to 5515.41** compared to BC's **1063.23 to 2658.40** (Table 1).
 - In robotic manipulation tasks from Robosuite (Stack, Threading, Square Peg) and RoboCasa (Drawer, Door, Stove), DARP shows success rate gains of **15-25% points** over BC. For instance, on the Robosuite Stack task, DARP achieves 72% success vs. BC's 47%, and on the RoboCasa Drawer task, DARP reaches 85% vs. BC's 54% (Table 2).
 - In a real-world furniture assembly task, DARP's success rate of 92% more than doubles BC's 44% (Table 2).
- **Comparison with Other Baselines:** Purely non-parametric methods (R&P, LWR) generally perform poorly. While Manifold Regularized Imitation Learning (MRIL), the explicitly smoothed version of DARP, typically outperforms vanilla BC, DARP's architecture consistently achieves the highest scores among all tested approaches.

#### 4.2. Handling Complex State Representations and Action Distributions (Q2)

- **High-Dimensional Visual Inputs:** DARP effectively scales to high-dimensional state representations. When using R3M image embeddings for Robosuite tasks, DARP demonstrates an average improvement of **~35%** over standard BC (e.g., 75% vs. 44% on Stack, 76% vs. 38% on Threading, and 52% vs. 17% on Peg insertion) (Table 3). This improvement is notably higher than that observed with low-dimensional states, suggesting DARP's suitability for complex visual input.
- **Multi-modal Action Distributions:** DARP can model complex multimodal action distributions. On the Push-T environment, which requires such distributions, DARP with a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) head (using a Set Transformer aggregator) achieves a 22% improvement over BC (70% vs. 48% success rate) (Table 4).
- **Discontinuous Environments:** Even in a deliberately challenging "Long Maze" environment with strong discontinuities (where spatially close states may require opposing actions), DARP achieves a **57%** success rate, significantly outperforming BC's **25%** (Table 5). This indicates its robustness to contexts where direct spatial proximity does not imply action similarity.
- **Combination with Diffusion Policy:** DARP can be combined with Diffusion Policy, a state-of-the-art generative model for IL, yielding further performance gains. For example, DARP combined with Diffusion Policy achieves 82% success on the Robosuite Stack task, compared to 52% for Diffusion Policy alone and 34% for BC (Table 8).
- **Computational Efficiency:** While Diffusion Policy can be more powerful, DARP with an MLP backbone is significantly faster: approximately **10x** faster in training and **36x** faster during inference compared to Diffusion Policy (Table 9). This makes DARP (MLP) suitable for real-time applications, achieving a control frequency over 230 Hz.

#### 4.3. Contribution of Architectural Components (Q3) and Robustness Analyses

- **Ablation Study:** A systematic ablation study on the Robosuite Stack environment reveals the critical components of DARP (Figure 6):
 - **Difference Vectors (`s*_i - s_q`):** Including these difference vectors in `fθ` is crucial for DARP's performance, indicating that relative positional information is highly impactful.
 - **Permutation-Invariant Aggregation `gψ`:** The permutation-invariant nature of the aggregation function is essential; permutation-dependent alternatives significantly degrade performance.
 - **Neighbor Actions (`a*_i`):** Including neighbor actions has a modest but positive impact.
 - **Meaningful Distance Metrics:** Random neighbor selection results in poor performance, confirming that using a meaningful distance metric for retrieval is fundamental.
- **Divergence Analysis:** Analysis of rollouts where BC fails but DARP succeeds (referred to as "step of divergence," SoD) indicates that at the SoD, the query state is often out-of-distribution (low state likelihood), but the "delta likelihood" (likelihood of difference vectors to neighbors) remains high. This supports the hypothesis that DARP's reparameterization enables robust predictions in slightly OOD states (Figure 7).
- **Recovery from BC Errors:** DARP exhibits robustness to accumulated error. Even when a BC agent is rolled out into states where it is failing (and thus drifting far from the expert manifold), a DARP agent forked from those states is able to recover and achieve high scores (Figure 12). This demonstrates DARP's capacity to handle OOD states.
- **Comparison with Smoothness Baselines:** DARP exhibits competitive or superior performance to other methods designed to induce smoothness, such as CCIL, achieving higher or equal relative gains over BC across MuJoCo tasks (Table 6).
- **Distance Metric Sensitivity:** DARP maintains performance when using alternative distance metrics like cosine similarity in high-dimensional embeddings (e.g., R3M features), showing only a minor reduction in success rate compared to Euclidean distance (Table 7).
- **Human Demonstrations:** DARP's performance gains over BC are maintained when trained on human expert demonstrations, not just those collected by optimal RL policies (Table 10).
- **Hyperparameter Selection:** Selecting retrieval hyperparameters (e.g., number of neighbors `k`, lookback, decay) by minimizing validation loss is an effective strategy to find performant settings (Table 11).

In summary, DARP consistently enhances behavior cloning performance, generalizes to diverse and complex scenarios, and derives its efficacy from its difference-aware parameterization and permutation-invariant aggregation, validated by both ablations and robustness analyses.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Difference-Aware Retrieval Policies for Imitation Learning (DARP) introduces a significant contribution to the field of imitation learning, with several potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Robustness to Covariate Shift:** DARP provides a method to mitigate the longstanding problem of covariate shift and compounding errors in behavior cloning. By reparameterizing the problem in terms of local neighborhood structure and difference vectors, it enables policies to make more informed and stable predictions even when encountering states slightly outside the expert data distribution. This directly addresses a core limitation that has hindered the deployment of BC in real-world, long-horizon tasks.

2. **Theoretical Justification for Implicit Smoothing:** The work offers a theoretical framework, demonstrating that embedding neighborhood aggregation directly into the policy architecture implicitly achieves a form of Laplacian smoothing. This theoretical grounding (Theorem 2) explains *why* DARP improves stability and reduces variance without requiring explicit regularization terms or additional hyperparameters, thereby simplifying policy design and training.

3. **Broad Applicability and Scalability:** DARP has been demonstrated to be effective across diverse domains, including continuous control (MuJoCo), robotic manipulation (Robosuite, RoboCasa), and real-world tasks. It is shown to handle various state representations, from low-dimensional features to high-dimensional visual embeddings, and different action distribution modeling requirements (unimodal, multimodal, and even in conjunction with diffusion policies). This broad applicability suggests DARP is a general and scalable architectural paradigm for imitation learning.

4. **No Additional Data or Supervision Requirements:** A key strength of DARP is that it achieves performance improvements while adhering to the standard behavior cloning paradigm – it requires no additional data collection, online expert interaction, access to simulators, or task-specific knowledge beyond the initial expert demonstrations. This makes it a practical solution for scenarios where obtaining such additional resources is costly or impossible.

5. **Improved Generalization for Robotics:** The consistent performance gains in robotic manipulation tasks, including those with visual inputs and real-world setups, indicate that DARP can lead to more reliable and generalizable robot behaviors. This could accelerate the deployment of learned policies in industrial, service, and assistive robotics, where robustness to unexpected situations is crucial.

6. **Insights into Retrieval-Augmented Learning:** The detailed ablation studies provide specific insights into the critical architectural components of DARP, highlighting the importance of difference vectors and permutation-invariant aggregation. These findings contribute to the broader understanding of effective design principles for retrieval-augmented algorithms in sequential decision-making problems.

7. **Foundation for Future Research:** DARP's success in implicitly regularizing policies and leveraging local data structures opens avenues for future research. This includes exploring other forms of implicit regularization, novel aggregation functions, and advanced retrieval mechanisms, potentially leading to even more robust and data-efficient imitation learning methods. The combination with diffusion policies also points to hybrid approaches that leverage the strengths of both retrieval-based and generative models.
