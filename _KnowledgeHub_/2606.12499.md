# Action-Effect Memory Pretraining for Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou) and Shenzhen University developed Action-Effect Memory (AEM), a pretraining framework that learns compact, fixed-size temporal representations from long-horizon robot interaction histories. This approach boosted average success rates by up to 20.7% on simulated RoboTwin2.0 tasks and 38.4% on real-world manipulation tasks, outperforming raw history stacking while reducing inference latency.

## Problem

- Existing pretraining methods for robot manipulation primarily focus on single-frame visual encoding, neglecting the crucial temporal information inherent in sequential manipulation tasks.
- Directly stacking raw past observations or features to incorporate history leads to increased computational cost, memory consumption, and inference latency, and offloads temporal abstraction entirely to the downstream policy.
- Effective memory for robot manipulation requires a compact, dense, and structured representation that explicitly incorporates action-driven state changes and captures action-effect dependencies over time.

## Method

- Introduces the Action-Effect Memory (**AEM**) framework, which pretrains a ==Mamba encoder== using a masked action-effect reconstruction objective on interleaved vision-action sequences.
- Utilizes a final visual token bottleneck within the Mamba encoder to compress the entire visible interaction history into a single, compact, fixed-size vector representation.
- Integrates this pretrained AEM history vector as an additional input to downstream visuomotor policies (e.g., Diffusion Policy, ManiFlow) to provide efficient and structured temporal context.

## Results

- Improved average success across eleven RoboTwin2.0 tasks (DP: **29.8%**→**50.5%**, +**20.7%**) and on non-Markovian RMBench tasks (e.g. Swap Blocks: DP+AEM **24%** vs. DP **16%**), while maintaining robustness in randomized, cluttered scenes.
- Outperformed direct DINOv2 feature stacking, achieving higher success rates (e.g., **0.76%** vs. **0.52%** on Place Shoe) with lower FLOPs (41.7 G vs. 57.8 G) and reduced inference latency (**730.01 ms** vs. **824.21 ms**).
- Transferred effectively to real-world tasks on a Franka Emika arm, boosting average success from **43.3%** to **81.7%** under standard settings and maintaining higher success in cluttered scenes (**68.3%** vs. **15%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning action-conditioned temporal representations through masked autoencoding of interleaved vision-action sequences effectively captures dynamic action-effect dependencies for robot manipulation.
> - A compact, fixed-size bottleneck, specifically derived from the final vision token of a selective state space model like Mamba, can efficiently summarize long-horizon interaction history while preserving critical temporal structure.
> - Explicitly pretraining a dense temporal memory allows downstream policies to leverage historical context without incurring the computational and memory costs of raw history stacking, enhancing performance and efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Representation pretraining has become a method for enhancing the sample efficiency and generalization capabilities of robot manipulation systems. Previous research in this area has primarily concentrated on single-frame visual encoding, employing techniques such as masked reconstruction, contrastive learning, and geometric consistency constraints to improve spatial representations of current observations. These methods have demonstrated utility in static scene understanding and have contributed to the adoption of visual pretraining in robotics.

However, the authors identify a limitation in these existing approaches: they largely frame pretraining as a single-timestep observation encoding problem, neglecting the temporal information inherent in manipulation tasks. Robot manipulation is a sequential process, often involving partial observability, dynamic contact changes, and delayed environmental responses, which can render a single current observation insufficient for fully characterizing the state. While stacking past observations or features is a straightforward way to incorporate history, this approach can lead to increased computational cost, memory consumption, and inference latency, and it delegates temporal abstraction entirely to the downstream policy.

The paper aims to address this gap by developing a pretraining framework that explicitly focuses on learning compact, action-conditioned temporal representations. This places the work in the intersection of representation learning, sequence modeling (specifically Mamba models), and robot manipulation, diverging from prevalent single-frame or raw history stacking approaches by proposing a structured, compact memory representation that captures action-effect dependencies over time. Related work includes various forms of visual pretraining, memory modeling for robot manipulation (e.g., history compression, external memory banks, or built-in temporal states), and the use of models like Mamba for sequential data. This research seeks to provide a transferable temporal representation learned during pretraining, rather than solely at the policy stage, distinguishing it from methods that primarily focus on how memory is used by the policy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a pretraining framework that enables robot manipulation policies to effectively utilize long-horizon temporal information in a compact and efficient manner. The authors propose to achieve this by learning an "Action-Effect Memory" (AEM) that captures the dynamic relationship between actions and their resulting environmental effects.

The motivation for this objective stems from several identified limitations in current robot manipulation research and the perceived requirements for an effective memory representation:

- **Insufficiency of Single-Frame Observations:** Current pretraining methods for robot manipulation primarily focus on encoding single-frame visual observations. However, real-world manipulation tasks frequently involve partial observability, occlusions, complex contact dynamics, and delayed environmental responses. In such scenarios, the current visual observation alone is often insufficient for accurately determining the robot's state or the task's progress. Historical information becomes crucial for disambiguation and robust control.
- **Limitations of Raw History Stacking:** A direct approach to incorporating historical information is to stack raw past observations or features. While sometimes effective, this method leads to a linear increase in computational cost, memory usage, and inference latency with longer history lengths. Furthermore, it offloads the complex task of temporal abstraction entirely to the downstream policy, which may not be optimal. The authors question what *kind* of memory representation is truly beneficial for robot manipulation, beyond just raw data.
- **Need for Vision-Action Interaction:** Manipulation is an action-driven process where the robot's actions actively change the environment. Therefore, an effective memory representation should not only be built from visual observations but also incorporate the robot's actions. This allows the memory to encode how actions cause state transitions, fostering a deeper understanding of cause-effect relationships.
- **Requirement for Compactness and Density:** To be practical for downstream control, the memory representation should be compact and of fixed size, regardless of the history horizon. This design avoids the scaling issues of raw stacking and minimizes the interface cost when integrating with existing policies.
- **Capture of Interaction Structure:** The memory must capture the temporal structure of interactions, particularly the dependencies between actions and subsequent state changes. Simply compressing information without structuring these dependencies may lead to a representation that is not informative or usable for effective robot control.

To address these motivations, the AEM framework is proposed to pretrain a compact, dense, and structured history representation from long-horizon vision-action interaction histories. This representation is intended to serve as a reusable temporal context for downstream policies, alleviating the burden of temporal abstraction from policy learning and providing critical historical cues for complex, partially observable tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed AEM (Action-Effect Memory) framework is designed to learn a compact, fixed-size temporal representation from long-horizon vision-action interaction histories. The methodology can be broken down into pretraining and downstream policy integration.

**A. AEM Pretraining**

1. **Vision-Action Sequence Construction:**
 - Visual features (v_t) and robot actions (a_t) are first projected into a shared latent space. The paper uses DINOv2 CLS features (768-D) for vision and 14-D robot actions, both projected to a 512-D latent space.
 - These projected features are then interleaved in temporal order to form a single sequence: `[z_v1, z_a1..., z_vL, z_aL]`.
 - Learnable type embeddings (distinguishing vision from action tokens) and relative-time embeddings are added to this sequence to provide additional context.

2. **Masked Action-Effect Pretraining for Compact History:**
 - **Aligned Masking:** During pretraining, a temporal window of variable length (e.g., 32 steps) is sampled from full trajectories. Instead of masking individual tokens, AEM applies aligned masking at the time-step level. If a time-step *t* is masked, both its visual token (z_vt) and action token (z_at) are masked together. This strategy aims to encourage the model to infer action-state dependencies. A high mask ratio (e.g., 70%) is used to facilitate learning longer-range dependencies.
 - **Mamba Encoder and Final-Vision-Token Bottleneck:** Only the *visible* tokens are fed into a Mamba encoder. Mamba, a selective state space model, is utilized for its efficiency in sequence modeling and its ability to maintain internal states that summarize preceding context. A key design choice is to *keep the final visual token visible* and use its Mamba-encoded output as the compact history representation (m). This design creates a single-vector bottleneck, forcing the model to compress the entire visible interaction history into a fixed-size representation without needing a dedicated memory token. The Mamba encoder comprises 6 layers with a state dimension of 256.
 - **Decoder and Reconstruction Objective:** To reconstruct the masked tokens, a full-length decoder input is constructed, incorporating learnable mask tokens along with position, time, and type embeddings. The compact final-token representation (m) extracted from the encoder acts as a global context, injected into every decoder position. A lightweight 2-layer Transformer decoder then predicts both the masked visual features (ˆv_t) and masked actions (ˆa_t).
 - **Loss Function:** The pretraining objective involves a reconstruction loss applied only to the masked positions. This loss combines Mean Squared Error (MSE) and cosine distance: `ℓ(ˆy, y) = ||ˆy - y||^2_2 + α * (1 - cos(ˆy, y))`. The overall AEM loss is a weighted sum of visual and action reconstruction losses: `L_AEM = λ_v * Σ_t∈M ℓ(ˆv_t, v_t) + λ_a * Σ_t∈M ℓ(ˆa_t, a_t)`. Hyperparameters `λ_v` and `λ_a` balance these terms. The model is trained with AdamW, learning rate 1.0 × 10^-5, weight decay 0.05, batch size 128, EMA 0.99, for 200 epochs.

3. **Final-Vision-Token History Extraction:** After pretraining, the AEM encoder processes the full interleaved vision-action history *without masking*. The Mamba-encoded output at the final vision-token position (m = h_vL) is directly used as the compact and dense summary of the long-horizon interaction history. This single vector serves as the temporal context for efficient control.

**B. Downstream Policy Integration**

For downstream robot manipulation tasks, the pretrained AEM encoder is integrated into existing visuomotor policies. Instead of the standard `a_t = π(v_t, p_t)` (where p_t are other policy conditions like language instructions), the action prediction becomes `a_t = π(v_t, m, p_t)`. Here, `m` is the compact history representation extracted by the pretrained AEM encoder from the entire history up to the current timestep. This means the policy receives temporal context as a single pretrained history vector, rather than raw stacked observations.

The framework was evaluated by integrating AEM with two different policy families: Diffusion Policy (DP) and ManiFlow Policy. All downstream policies use 224x224 images and a batch size of 128. Training for policies utilized AdamW, learning rate 1.0 × 10^-4, and cosine scheduling for 1000 epochs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents extensive evaluation of AEM across various simulation and real-world robot manipulation tasks, comparing AEM-enhanced policies against their baselines without explicit temporal memory or with direct history stacking.

**A. Simulation Results (RoboTwin2.0 and RMBench)**

1. **Improvement on Standard Manipulation Tasks (RoboTwin2.0):**
 - AEM consistently improved manipulation performance across eleven diverse RoboTwin2.0 tasks (e.g., Handover Block, Place Object Cabinet, Stack Blocks Two).
 - Diffusion Policy (DP) average success rate improved from 29.8% to 50.5% (+20.7%).
 - ManiFlow average success rate improved from 9.8% to 29.1% (+19.3%).
 - These gains were observed across two distinct policy families, suggesting that the learned history representation provides complementary temporal context independent of the specific policy architecture.

2. **Effectiveness in Randomized Scenes:**
 - On selected tasks (Place Shoe, Handover Block) evaluated under randomized scenes (with distractors, pose perturbations, lighting, and texture variations), DP+AEM maintained higher success rates compared to baseline DP.
 - For example, on Handover Block, DP+AEM achieved 24% success rate while DP only achieved 8%. On Place Shoe, DP+AEM achieved 20% compared to DP's 4%. This indicates the compact history helps policies better interpret task phases and recover from visual ambiguities.

3. **Benefits for Non-Markovian Tasks (RMBench):**
 - AEM demonstrated larger performance improvements on RMBench's non-Markovian tasks (Put Back Block, Swap Blocks) where current observations are insufficient.
 - For Swap Blocks, DP+AEM achieved 24% vs. DP's 16%. For Put Back Block, DP+AEM achieved 20% vs. DP's 0%. This supports the hypothesis that masked action-effect pretraining condenses task-relevant temporal information.

4. **Efficiency and Performance vs. Direct History Stacking:**
 - Ablation studies on the Place Shoe task compared DP+AEM with direct DINOv2 feature concatenation and stacking of 1, 8, or 16 frames.
 - Direct DINOv2 concatenation (1 frame) improved success from 0.40% (DP) to 0.52%, but DP+AEM achieved 0.76%.
 - Stacking more DINOv2 frames (8 or 16 frames) did not significantly improve success over 1-frame concatenation (0.52% for 8/16 frames) while increasing total FLOPs and inference latency.
 - DP+AEM (16-step memory) achieved a higher success rate (0.76% clean, 0.20% random) with lower total FLOPs (41.7 G) and latency (730.01 ms) compared to DINOv2 stacking (e.g., 16 frames: 57.8 G FLOPs, 824.21 ms latency, 0.40% clean, 0.12% random). This suggests that learned compact history is more effective and efficient than raw feature stacking.

5. **Preservation of Temporal Information by Final Token:**
 - Visualization of decoded masked visual features from the final-token bottleneck showed a strong diagonal cosine similarity matrix. This indicates that the compact final token preserves structured, position-specific temporal information over the history window.

**B. Ablation Studies**

1. **Memory Fusion and Pretraining Strategy (Table III):**
 - Concatenating AEM memory with the current visual feature outperformed feature addition (summing features).
 - Pretraining AEM with masked action-effect reconstruction (using both observation and action) yielded higher success rates than training the memory encoder jointly with the policy (without AEM pretraining) or pretraining with only observation reconstruction.
 - Controlling the policy with only memory (removing current observation) or only current observation (without action reconstruction) resulted in lower performance than the full AEM approach, indicating AEM serves as a critical temporal complement to current perception.

2. **Visual Encoder Choice (Table IV):**
 - DINOv2 CLS features (0.96%/0.76% on Handover Block/Place Object Basket) performed best compared to DINOv2 pooled, DINOv3 (pooled/CLS), or CLIP CLS features. CLS tokens generally outperformed pooled features, suggesting they provide a more stable compression target.

3. **Mask Ratio (Fig. 8a):**
 - Higher mask ratios (up to 0.7) during pretraining generally led to better downstream success rates, suggesting that harder reconstruction tasks encourage the final token to encode more robust and longer-range temporal dependencies.

4. **Pretraining and Inference History Lengths (Fig. 8b):**
 - A 32-frame pretraining window with a 16-step inference horizon demonstrated optimal performance, balancing temporal compression with a compact, effective history signal for policy conditioning.

**C. Real-World Experiment (Franka Emika Arm)**

1. **Improved Real-World Task Success (Table V):**
 - AEM transferred to physical manipulation on a Franka Emika arm across three tabletop tasks (Place Corn to Plate, Rotate Mouse Forward, Put Block into Drawer).
 - Average success rate under standard settings improved from 43.3% (DP) to 81.7% (DP+AEM).
 - Gains were most notable on tasks requiring short-term interaction history, such as reorientation and drawer closure.

2. **Robustness to Task-Irrelevant Distractors:**
 - In cluttered scenes with distractors, both methods degraded, but DP+AEM consistently maintained higher success rates than DP on all three tasks (average 68.3% vs. 15%). This suggests AEM provides robust temporal cues even when visual observations are perturbed by noise.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, Action-Effect Memory (AEM), for robot manipulation that addresses a perceived limitation in existing representation pretraining methods by focusing on temporal interaction structures rather than solely single-timestep visual encoding. The significance and potential impact of this work include:

1. **Advancement in Temporal Representation Learning:** AEM introduces a novel paradigm for temporal representation pretraining in robot manipulation. By compressing long-horizon vision-action interaction history into a single, compact final-token representation, it offers a transferable and fixed-size temporal context for downstream policies. This contributes to moving beyond simple observation stacking towards more structured and abstract temporal memory.

2. **Enhanced Robot Manipulation Performance:** The consistent performance improvements observed across various simulated benchmarks (RoboTwin2.0, RMBench) and real-world tasks, when integrated with different policy families (Diffusion Policy, ManiFlow), indicate that AEM's learned compact memory effectively enhances robot manipulation capabilities. This is particularly relevant for tasks involving partial observability, randomized scenes, and non-Markovian dynamics, where historical context is critical.

3. **Improved Efficiency and Resource Utilization:** Compared to direct frame stacking of visual features, AEM achieves better performance while reducing inference latency and computational cost. This addresses practical constraints in deploying robot learning systems by providing a dense temporal signal without incurring the scaling penalties of raw data. The fixed-size nature of the final-token bottleneck ensures computational efficiency regardless of the history length.

4. **Modeling Action-Effect Dependencies:** By interleaving visual and action features and employing a masked autoencoding objective over these sequences, AEM is designed to learn action-conditioned state evolution. This "action-effect memory" is intended to capture how actions drive subsequent state changes, which is a fundamental aspect of intelligent manipulation.

5. **Generalizability Across Policy Architectures:** The framework's ability to improve both Diffusion Policy and ManiFlow suggests that the learned temporal representations are general and can complement diverse policy backbones without requiring extensive modifications to their core action generation mechanisms. This implies AEM could be widely applicable to various existing and future robot control policies.

6. **Potential for Broader Applications:** While evaluated in robot manipulation, the concept of a compact, action-conditioned temporal memory learned through masked modeling of interleaved vision-action sequences could have implications for other embodied AI domains that require understanding sequential interactions and partial observability.

A noted limitation is the current lack of large-scale pretraining validation. Future work is proposed to scale AEM to broader robot datasets and further refine the bottleneck mechanism to better distill task progress and action-consequence cues, potentially leading to even more robust and generalizable robot learning systems.
