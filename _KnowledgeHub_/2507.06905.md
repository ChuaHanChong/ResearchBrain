# ULC: A Unified and Fine-Grained Controller for Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ULC is a Unified Loco-Manipulation Controller that enables humanoid robots to perform precise loco-manipulation tasks across an extensive operational workspace using a single, integrated policy. It demonstrates superior tracking accuracy for locomotion, 3-DoF torso orientation, and dual-arm manipulation, while also proving robust against external loads and communication delays on a Unitree G1 robot.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid controllers often rely on hierarchical or decoupled architectures, limiting whole-body coordination and integrated performance in loco-manipulation tasks.
- Designing a comprehensive command space that balances expressiveness (e.g., full 3-DoF torso, dual-arm control) with stability and feasibility remains a challenge, often leading to constrained movements.
- Challenges in training data sources (motion capture limitations vs. insufficient procedural command coverage) and ensuring generalization and robustness to real-world conditions like external loads and communication delays.

## Method

- **ULC** employs a single, ==unified policy== trained with massively parallel reinforcement learning, integrating locomotion, 3-DoF torso, and dual-arm control to achieve full whole-body coordination.
- It utilizes a sequential skill acquisition strategy with an ==adaptive curriculum==, progressively mastering base velocity, base height, and then torso/arm tracking to handle the high-dimensional command space efficiently.
- Deployment-realistic mechanisms are integrated, including quintic polynomial interpolation for smooth command trajectories, a stochastic delay mechanism for robustness to communication latency, and load generalization via random mass distribution and explicit Center of Mass (CoM) tracking.

## Results

- ULC achieves the widest operational workspace, supporting a root height range of **0.30**-**0.75 m** and full 3-DoF torso rotation (Yaw: [-2.62, 2.62] rad, Pitch: [-**0.52**, 1.57] rad, Roll: [-**0.52**, **0.52**] rad), exceeding baselines.
- It consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods in tracking accuracy across all metrics (locomotion, torso orientation, dual-arm control), demonstrating superior performance in scenarios involving whole command space, edge commands, and **2kg** wrist loads.
- The controller shows enhanced robustness to stochastic command delays and external loads, maintaining low tracking errors (e.g., arm tracking error EaE_aEa of **0.06**±**0.01 rad** under command mutation) and significantly outperforming traditional PD control in real-world loaded manipulation tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A unified policy can achieve superior whole-body coordination and performance in complex loco-manipulation tasks, outperforming decoupled or hierarchical designs, especially when simultaneous movements are required.
> - Progressive, adaptive curriculum learning effectively addresses the challenges of exploring and mastering high-dimensional command spaces in a stable manner, allowing for the composition of complex skills.
> - Integrating deployment-realistic factors such as stochastic command delays and varying external loads directly into the training process significantly enhances the robustness and sim-to-real transfer capabilities of the robot controller.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are designed to operate in human-centric environments, requiring the integration of mobility (locomotion) with upper-body capabilities (manipulation). This combination, termed loco-manipulation, presents significant challenges in control design. Existing research in humanoid control often employs hierarchical architectures that decouple control into isolated upper-body (manipulation) and lower-body (locomotion) policies. While this decomposition can reduce training complexity, it inherently limits coordination between subsystems, potentially leading to suboptimal whole-body performance and contradicting the integrated control observed in humans.

Advances in locomotion and manipulation have often been achieved by coupling high-level decision-making (e.g., via Imitation Learning or Vision-Language-Action models) with Loco-Manipulation Controllers (LMCs). An effective LMC must translate motion commands into joint-level actions with minimal tracking error while maintaining dynamic stability. The design of LMCs involves trade-offs concerning the command space (e.g., joint positions, Cartesian poses, root velocity), control architecture (whole-body vs. decoupled), and training data sources (motion capture vs. procedurally sampled commands). Whole-body controllers offer strong coordination but are harder to train, while decoupled designs simplify learning but can weaken coordination. Motion capture data provides realistic motion but can be noisy or kinematically infeasible, whereas procedurally sampled commands improve coverage but are often limited by stability constraints.

This work addresses the limitations of decoupled and motion-capture-dependent approaches by proposing a unified control framework. It builds upon previous efforts to achieve whole-body tracking and skill acquisition but seeks to improve command space coverage, coordination, and robustness under varying conditions, including external loads and command variations, which often challenge existing controllers. The paper aims to demonstrate that a single unified policy can achieve a combination of tracking accuracy, large workspace, and robustness without sacrificing performance, thereby offering an alternative to traditional hierarchical or specialized approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a Unified Loco-Manipulation Controller (ULC) that enables humanoid robots to perform complex loco-manipulation tasks with high precision, extensive workspace coverage, and robustness. The motivation stems from several limitations observed in current humanoid control paradigms:

- **Limited Coordination in Decoupled Architectures:** Most existing loco-manipulation controllers adopt hierarchical or decoupled architectures, separating control of the upper and lower body. While this simplifies training, it often compromises the integrated, whole-body coordination necessary for tasks requiring simultaneous locomotion and manipulation, which contradicts the natural, unified control exhibited by humans. The objective is to demonstrate that a single, unified policy can overcome these coordination limitations.
- **Trade-offs in Command Space Design:** The choice of command space (e.g., joint positions, Cartesian poses, root velocity, root height) directly impacts the feasibility, potential conflicts among objectives, and the robot's ability to fully exploit its kinematic and dynamic range. Existing methods often struggle to balance expressiveness with feasibility or face limitations in specific degrees of freedom, particularly for torso rotation. ULC aims for a feasibility-aware command space that allows for simultaneous tracking of root velocity, root height, torso orientation (yaw, pitch, roll), and dual-arm joint positions.
- **Challenges in Training Data and Generalization:** Training data sources, such as motion capture, can introduce biases or kinematic infeasibilities, while procedurally sampled commands may lack sufficient coverage, especially for complex leg motions. ULC seeks to overcome these by training with massively parallel reinforcement learning on procedurally sampled commands, aiming to improve coverage of the feasible command space while preserving whole-body coordination.
- **Need for Precision and Robustness:** An effective controller must translate motion commands with minimal tracking error and maintain dynamic stability under various conditions, including external disturbances and varying payloads. Existing methods may achieve moderate performance but can degrade significantly under extreme commands or external loads. The objective is to develop a controller that exhibits high tracking accuracy, adapts to external loads, and maintains robustness against command variations (e.g., communication delays).
- **Deployment-Realistic Training and Sim-to-Real Transfer:** To ensure practical deployability, training methodologies must account for real-world phenomena like communication delays and dynamic changes. ULC aims to integrate deployment-realistic command generation, including stochastic delay modeling, and explicit balance optimization to improve sim-to-real transfer and robustness to external disturbances.

In summary, the motivation is to advance humanoid loco-manipulation beyond decoupled or constrained approaches by developing a unified, fine-grained controller that can handle diverse, complex whole-body tasks with high performance and reliability in realistic scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Unified Loco-Manipulation Controller (ULC) framework leverages massively parallel reinforcement learning to train a single unified policy. It systematically addresses challenges in high-dimensional exploration and skill coordination through four key technical innovations: sequential skill acquisition, command interpolation with stochastic delay modeling, load generalization via dynamic mass distribution and center of mass (CoM) tracking, and residual action modeling.

**Problem Formulation:**
The task is formulated as a goal-conditioned Markov Decision Process (MDP). The policy $\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t, g_t)$ maps state-command observations to a Gaussian distribution over actions. The observation $o^{(t)}_{prop}$ includes joint positions/velocities, base angular velocity, gravity projection, previous actions, and current commands. A factorized command space $g = [g_{loco}, g_{torso}, g_{arms}]^T$ is designed, allowing independent control of locomotion (linear/angular velocity, pelvis height), torso orientation (ZXY Euler angles), and arm joint targets. Actions are target joint positions executed via PD control. Arm actions use residual modeling: $q_{final_{arms}} = a_{arms} + q_{desired_{arms}}$.

**Sequential Skill Acquisition and Adaptive Curriculum Learning:**
ULC addresses high-dimensional command space exploration using a sequential skill acquisition strategy with an adaptive command curriculum, progressing through three ordered skills:
1. **Base velocity tracking (T1):** Focuses on linear ($v_{xy}$) and angular ($\omega_z$) velocities.
2. **Base height tracking (T2):** Introduces pelvis height ($h_{pelvis}$) control.
3. **Torso and arm tracking (T3):** Incorporates torso orientation ($g_{torso}$) and arm joint targets ($g_{arms}$).

A curriculum parameter $\alpha_i(t) \in$ controls difficulty progression. Advancement is based on a reward-based gating mechanism: $\alpha_i(t+1) = \min\{1, \alpha_i(t) + \Delta\alpha\}$ if $C_i(t)$ is true, where $\Delta\alpha = 0.05$.
- **Height Curriculum Advancement ($C_2$):** Requires satisfactory average rewards for height ($R_{avg_{height}} \ge \tau_{height}=0.85$), velocity ($R_{avg_{vel}} \ge \tau_{vel}=0.8$), and hip deviation ($R_{avg_{hip}} \ge \tau_{hip}=0.2$). This ensures fundamental locomotion mastery before height variations.
- **Upper Body Curriculum Advancement ($C_3$):** Requires satisfactory average rewards for upper body joint tracking ($R_{avg_{upper}} \ge \tau_{upper}=0.8$) and torso orientation tracking ($R_{avg_{torso}} \ge \tau_{torso}=0.8$), while maintaining all previously acquired skills (C2 conditions) and having reached near-full height curriculum ($\alpha_2 \ge 0.98$). This multi-criteria gating prevents catastrophic forgetting and maintains stable performance across learned capabilities.

**Stochastic Delay Mechanism and Command Interpolation:**
To ensure stable arm movements and enhance robustness to real-world communication delays:
- **Quintic Polynomial Interpolation:** Upper body commands are smoothly interpolated between randomly sampled target positions over a fixed interval ($T_{interval} = 1.0$ s). The quintic smoothing factor $s(t) = 10t^3 - 15t^4 + 6t^5$ ensures $C^2$ continuity with zero velocity and acceleration at endpoints, providing physically plausible trajectories.
- **Stochastic Delay Mechanism:** This mechanism introduces deployment realism by accumulating and probabilistically releasing incremental command changes ($\Delta q^{(t)}$). A random delay mask $d^{(t)}_j \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p_{delay})$ ($p_{delay}=0.5$) determines whether an incremental command is applied or buffered. The effective command $\Delta q^{(t)}_{effective}$ incorporates current non-delayed increments and previously accumulated, now released, commands.

**Load Generalization and Balance Control:**
- **Random Load Distribution:** Random masses are applied to the robot's wrists during environment reset, simulating diverse payload conditions and modifying the total wrist mass distribution.
- **Center of Mass Tracking:** A reward function $r_{CoM} = \exp\left( - \frac{\|p_{xy_{CoM}} - p_{xy_{feet}}\|^2}{\sigma^2_{CoM}} \right)$ encourages maintaining the horizontal CoM projection close to the midpoint between ankle positions ($p_{xy_{feet}}$), providing explicit policy gradients for stability under varying loads.

**Residual Action Modeling for Arm Control:**
Residual action modeling for arm joints enhances precise tracking while maintaining training stability. The final control command $q_{final_{[J_{upper}]}}$ combines the policy output $q_{processed_{[J_{upper}]}}$ with the desired arm joint positions $q_{desired_{[J_{upper}]}}$ generated through interpolation and delay mechanisms. The residual term serves as a feedforward component, allowing the policy to focus on learning corrective adjustments.

**Experimental Setup:**
ULC is validated on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot with a three-degrees-of-freedom waist. Comparisons are made against state-of-the-art methods (HOMIE, HOMIE-3-DoF-Waist, FALCON, AMO, R2S2) using identical simulator settings, evaluation protocols, and low-level assumptions. Metrics include various tracking errors for linear/angular velocity, root height, torso orientation (yaw, pitch, roll), and arm joint positions. Evaluations are conducted across 1024 parallel environments in Isaaclab for 50,000 steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate ULC's performance in terms of reachable workspace, tracking accuracy across various scenarios, and the contributions of its individual components, along with real-world validation.

**Comparison of Reachable Workspace (Table IV):**
- **Baselines:** Methods like HOMIE and HOMIE-3-DoF-Waist use PD control for the waist, leading to decoupled torso and leg control, and in some cases, restricted torso pitch/roll. FALCON and R2S2 exhibit limited yaw range or reliance on skill libraries/motion capture data, constraining torso rotation or dual-arm control. AMO achieves asymmetric pitch control but is constrained in roll.
- **ULC:** Achieves the maximum root height range (0.30-0.75 m) and supports complete 3-DoF torso rotation tracking across all axes (Yaw: [-2.62, 2.62] rad, Pitch: [-0.52, 1.57] rad, Roll: [-0.52, 0.52] rad). It employs procedurally sampled dual-arm control without motion capture constraints, indicating a broader and more flexible operational workspace.

**Comparison of Tracking Accuracy (Table III):**
ULC was compared against baselines across four scenarios: whole command space, edge command space, wrist loaded (2kg), and command mutation. Errors are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

- **Locomotion Control ($E_v$, $E_{\omega}$, $E_h$):**
 - AMO demonstrates strong linear and angular velocity tracking due to its hierarchical design. ULC achieves competitive performance, outperforming most other methods in root height tracking ($E_h$: ULC 0.02±0.00 m vs. AMO 0.03±0.01 m in whole command space).
- **Torso Orientation Control ($E_y$, $E_p$, $E_r$):**
 - ULC excels in torso yaw tracking ($E_y$: ULC 0.05±0.01 rad vs. AMO 0.07±0.01 rad) and demonstrates superior pitch ($E_p$: ULC 0.06±0.01 rad vs. AMO 0.11±0.02 rad) and roll ($E_r$: ULC 0.05±0.01 rad vs. AMO 0.12±0.02 rad) control in the whole command space. HOMIE-3-DoF-Waist's PD-based torso control results in degraded accuracy.
- **Dual-Arm Tracking ($E_a$):**
 - HOMIE and AMO, relying on PD controllers, show moderate performance. FALCON's upper-body RL policy improves tracking but is constrained by motion capture data. ULC consistently outperforms all methods in dual-arm tracking ($E_a$: ULC 0.04±0.01 rad in whole command space), attributed to its residual action modeling and sequential skill acquisition.
- **Robustness Under Extreme Conditions (Edge Command Space):**
 - In extreme torso rotation scenarios, HOMIE-3-DoF-Waist shows severe performance degradation due to inadequate coordination. ULC maintains robust performance ($E_v$: 0.11±0.01, $E_{\omega}$: 0.13±0.02, $E_h$: 0.03±0.01, $E_y$: 0.06±0.01, $E_p$: 0.08±0.01, $E_r$: 0.07±0.01, $E_a$: 0.05±0.01), demonstrating the benefit of its unified architecture.
- **External Load Adaptation (2kg Wrist Load):**
 - Under 2kg wrist loads, AMO maintains locomotion advantage but its PD-based arm control degrades. FALCON shows partial robustness. ULC achieves superior load adaptation across all metrics ($E_a$: ULC 0.05±0.01 rad), indicating effective force adaptation.
- **Command Mutation Robustness (Stochastic Delays):**
 - ULC demonstrates superior robustness to stochastic command delays, while other methods, including AMO and HOMIE variants, exhibit significant performance deterioration. ULC maintains low errors ($E_a$: ULC 0.06±0.01 rad) in this challenging scenario.

**Ablation on Policy Training (Table V):**
Ablation studies, conducted under a 2kg wrist load, confirmed the necessity of each ULC component:
- **Without Sequential Skill Acquisition:** Significantly degraded torso orientation control (e.g., $E_p$: +100%, $E_r$: +100%), highlighting the importance of progressive skill composition.
- **Without Residual Action Model:** The largest degradation in arm tracking ($E_a$: +140%), validating its role in fine-grained upper body control.
- **Without Load Randomization:** Noted deterioration in locomotion and arm tracking under payload.
- **Without Center of Mass Tracking:** The most severe balance degradation (e.g., $E_v$: +10%, $E_{\omega}$: +38%, $E_h$: +100%), demonstrating its essential role in dynamic stability.
The full ULC model consistently achieved the lowest errors across all metrics.

**Real-World Results (Figures 3, 4, 5):**
Teleoperation tasks (doll pick-and-place, bread in refrigerator) on the Unitree G1 robot demonstrated ULC's capabilities:
- **Coordinated Loco-Manipulation:** ULC exhibited stable locomotion during height transitions, dynamic balance during coordinated arm movements (e.g., hand switching for doll transfer), and precise height/torso adjustments for squatting and manipulation.
- **Precision in Force-Interactive Tasks:** Consistently low dual-arm tracking errors were observed throughout complex multi-step sequences, even in tasks requiring interaction with objects and environments (e.g., opening a refrigerator door).
- **Loaded Comparison (Figure 5):** A controlled experiment showed ULC consistently achieved lower joint angle deviations than traditional PD control across various external wrist loads (0.5 kg, 1.0 kg, 1.5 kg). ULC maintained high accuracy even at 1.5 kg, while PD control struggled due to inadequate gravity compensation, validating ULC's superior force adaptation and robustness in real-world manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ULC framework presents a significant advancement in humanoid loco-manipulation control by demonstrating the feasibility and performance benefits of a single, unified policy. Its ability to simultaneously achieve whole-body coordination, cover a large operational workspace, and maintain high-precision tracking under challenging conditions addresses several long-standing limitations of previous approaches.

The primary impact lies in overcoming the inherent coordination limitations of hierarchical or decoupled control architectures. By integrating all degrees of freedom in a single controller and leveraging principled procedural command sampling, ULC enables more natural, human-like whole-body movements. This unified control paradigm is crucial for complex, interactive tasks in dynamic environments where precise and coordinated movements of both the lower and upper body are required.

Specific areas of significance include:
- **Enhanced Versatility:** ULC's comprehensive command space and unified control enable humanoid robots to execute a wider range of tasks, from precise object manipulation and interaction with the environment (e.g., opening doors, cleaning) to coordinated locomotion over varied terrains, all while maintaining balance and stability.
- **Improved Robustness:** The integration of load generalization (random mass distribution, CoM tracking) and deployment-realistic training (stochastic delay mechanism) significantly enhances the robot's ability to adapt to external disturbances, varying payloads, and communication imperfections, bridging the sim-to-real gap more effectively.
- **High Precision under Load:** The real-world experiments demonstrate ULC's superior force adaptation compared to traditional PD control, maintaining high tracking accuracy even with significant external loads. This is critical for practical applications where robots must handle objects of different weights or apply forces accurately.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** ULC provides a robust foundation for integrating higher-level decision-making systems, such as vision-language-action models, with a capable low-level controller. This could accelerate the development of truly autonomous and general-purpose humanoid robots capable of operating in unstructured human environments.

While the current work simplifies locomotion commands compared to motion-capture-driven approaches, ULC's contributions represent a step towards more capable and reliable humanoid systems. Its potential impact extends to various domains requiring dexterous and mobile manipulation, including logistics, domestic assistance, industrial automation, and exploration in hazardous environments. Future work aims to enhance locomotion expressiveness, further expanding the range of real-world tasks that can be performed.
