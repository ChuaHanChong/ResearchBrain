# REBOOT: From Failure to Recovery - A Dataset and Benchmark for Precision Assembly

> [!summary] Summary
> REBOOT is a real-robot precision-assembly benchmark built around failure as supervision: 2,160 teleoperated episodes across 18 NIST-board connector install/remove tasks, half expert and half recovery-from-failure, each decomposed into five kinematic phases with failure-mode, symmetry and clearance metadata. Per-phase benchmarking of ACT, Diffusion Policy and pi-0-FAST exposes architecture-specific failure points that binary success evaluation hides.

## Problem

- Manipulation datasets consist almost exclusively of successful demonstrations, so policies get no supervision for retracting, re-aligning and recovering after mid-task deviation.
- Real-world benchmarks collapse performance into binary success, giving no resolution to localize where along a long-horizon trajectory a policy began to diverge.
- Existing failure datasets organize failures by semantic category rather than kinematic phase or violated tolerance, treating sub-millimetre and multi-millimetre misalignment as identical events.

## Method

- Shared **five-phase decomposition** (Align(pick), Engage(pick), Transport, Align(place), Engage(place)) with manually annotated frame boundaries on every trajectory, enabling ==per-phase progress scoring== and cross-task failure comparison.
- **Failure-calibrated recovery collection**: train ==ACT==, ==Diffusion Policy== and ==pi-0-FAST== per task, log the phase and mode where rollouts deviate, then teleoperate 60 recovery episodes matching that empirical distribution.
- Structured mechanical metadata plus grounded per-phase language annotations: ==rotational symmetry class==, ==clearance-based precision tier==, matched install-remove pairs, four failure modes, and four synchronized RGB-D views at 30 Hz.

## Results

- Align(place) and Engage(place) account for **54%** of installation-task rollout failures versus **8%** for Transport, concentrating failures in the two contact-rich placement phases.
- Assembly is far harder than disassembly: mean rollout success is **16%** across nine install variants against **56%** across the nine matched remove variants.
- Engage-phase failure rises with keying: continuous-symmetry objects fail **14%**, C1 **19%**, C2 **22%**; ACT and Diffusion Policy match overall yet diverge per phase, pi-0-FAST leads.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Binary success hides architecture-specific weaknesses; phase-resolved progress metrics reveal where policies break and make otherwise equivalent models distinguishable.
> - Allocate corrective data where policies actually fail, measured from their own rollouts, rather than uniformly or from synthesized failures that overproduce easy-to-simulate modes.
> - Mechanical structure predicts failure: tight clearance and low rotational symmetry convert small alignment errors into irrecoverable jamming, so tolerance metadata is diagnostic signal.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A precision assembly policy can reach the mating point but still miss by less than a connector's clearance, jam, or release too early. The authors illustrate this with a USB-A plug. With 0.62 mm engagement clearance, an offset insertion can catch the port rim and jam. The missing behavior is to retract, realign, and re-approach. This is the paper's illustrative scenario, not a measured trial. Success-only demonstrations do not show what to do after such a deviation. A binary success score also cannot tell whether the policy failed to grasp, dropped the part in transit, or stalled while seating it.

REBOOT addresses this by treating *where and how* a robot fails, together with a human operator's correction, as data. It is a real-robot precision-assembly dataset and benchmark of 2,160 demonstrations across 18 tasks: 1,080 expert demonstrations and 1,080 recovery episodes. The recovery episodes reproduce failures observed in autonomous policy rollouts and continue through a teleoperated correction to task completion. The key measured finding is that installation failures concentrate near alignment and seating.

### 2. Turning mistakes into data

The collection proceeds in four steps:

1. The authors train ACT, Diffusion Policy and π₀-FAST on expert demonstrations.
1. They run these policies autonomously on each task.
1. They record the phase and failure mode where each rollout first deviates.
1. An operator reproduces the observed failure and demonstrates a correction through to task completion.

Recovery episodes start at object initialization, so they include the approach as well as the induced failure and correction. The distribution of recovery examples is calibrated to observed policy failures rather than split uniformly across phases.

Every episode is divided into five shared stages:

- align to pick
- grasp
- transport
- align at the goal
- seat or release

Stage boundaries are annotated, which gives a shared vocabulary for comparing where progress stops. The tasks cover nine object types, each with install and remove variants. Clearance spans the interference-fit RCA (0 mm) to HAN 10E (2.73 mm). Observations include four synchronized RGB-D views and robot state, and each phase has grounded natural-language outcome descriptions.

### 3. What the stage count reveals

For each autonomous rollout, the authors record the last successfully completed stage. A rollout counts as a success only when all five stages are completed. The analysis pools ACT, Diffusion Policy and π₀-FAST, with 15 rollouts per task per policy family and 810 rollouts in total.

Across all nine installation tasks, Align(place) and Engage(place) together account for 54% of rollout failures. Transport accounts for 8%. These are shares of failures. The authors interpret the concentration as reflecting precision alignment under contact.

A second comparison uses the matched install/remove design, which holds object identity and fixture geometry constant. Mean full-task rollout success is 16% across installation variants and 56% across removal variants. This gap is consistent with insertion being more precision-demanding than extraction, but it does not isolate insertion as the sole cause.

### 4. Scope

REBOOT's established contribution is a dataset and measurement framework for studying failures in the precision-assembly regime. It turns "the robot failed" into a question of which stage failed and what correction an operator demonstrated.

The paper does not test policies trained on the recovery data; the effectiveness of ways to train on the recovery segments is explicitly left for future work. The evidence also comes from a lab-controlled suite with fixed cameras and lighting. The authors identify limited visual diversity, and transfer to cluttered, occluded, variable real-world settings, as open questions.
