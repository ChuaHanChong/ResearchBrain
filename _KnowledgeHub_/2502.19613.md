# Self-rewarding correction for mathematical reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Self-rewarding reasoning LLMs generate step-by-step math solutions, judge their own correctness with a [VERIFY] token, and revise until the self-evaluation says correct, with no external reward model. Two stages: sequential rejection sampling builds long chain-of-thought trajectories for self-rewarding instruction fine-tuning, then rule-based RL (PPO or multi-turn DPO) sharpens it. Qwen2.5-Math-7B reaches 80.2% MATH500 final accuracy.

## Problem

- Successful self-correction methods lean on external ground-truth or learned reward models, forcing multi-model inference that raises compute cost and deployment complexity.
- Intrinsic self-correction by prompting alone reliably degrades accuracy; models revise answers regardless of correctness because nothing tells them when to stop.
- Rejection-sampling fine-tuning like STaR/RAFT teaches revision but not stopping, and vanilla rule-based RL does not consistently produce self-correction behavior.

## Method

- **Self-rewarding IFT**: ==sequential rejection sampling== draws initial answers, self-evaluations, and corrections separately, then stitches them into long chain-of-thought trajectories carrying ==[VERIFY]== correct/wrong tokens.
- ==Data filtering== keeps three trajectory types: wrong-then-corrected, correct-but-misjudged-then-preserved, and correct-judged-correct, balancing classes so models learn both revision and early termination.
- ==KL-regularized rule-based RL== over the multi-turn MDP, using only final-answer correctness as oracle reward, optimized by ==PPO== or trajectory-level ==multi-turn DPO==.

## Results

- On MATH500 with Qwen2.5-Math-7B, self-rewarding IFT plus PPO hits **80.2%** final accuracy versus **79.4%** single-turn PPO and **51.4%** intrinsic self-correction.
- Correct-to-incorrect flips drop to **0.4%** on MATH500 versus **15.4%** for intrinsic self-correction and **3.8%** for STaR/RAFT+; PPO beats DPO **43.4%** versus **40.1%** on OlympiadBench.
- Under equal budget, self-rewarding correction reaches **42.8%** on MATH with **26.4** average samples, beating independent sampling's **40.4%** at **64** samples.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unifying generator and verifier in one model gives adaptive compute: the model stops early on confident answers, so correction costs far less than doubling samples.
> - Knowing when not to revise matters more than revision skill; low correct-to-incorrect flips drive most of the gain over prior self-correction training.
> - Self-generated reward data goes out-of-distribution as the policy shifts during fine-tuning, and RL tends to trade correction ability for stronger first attempts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Here's a detailed 1-2 page report analyzing this research paper:

Title: Self-rewarding correction for mathematical reasoning

Authors & Institutions:
- Wei Xiong, Hanning Zhang, Chenlu Ye (University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign)
- Lichang Chen (University of Maryland, College Park)
- Nan Jiang, Tong Zhang (University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign)

### 2. Research Context

This work addresses a key challenge in large language models (LLMs) - enabling them to autonomously detect and correct errors in their mathematical reasoning without relying on external feedback systems. While prior work has shown LLMs can improve responses using external reward models, this requires running multiple models and increases computational costs. The authors propose a novel framework that unifies generation and evaluation capabilities within a single model.

### 3. Key Objectives

1. Develop a self-rewarding reasoning framework where LLMs can simultaneously generate solutions and evaluate their correctness
2. Enable autonomous error detection and correction without external reward models
3. Demonstrate computational advantages compared to multi-model approaches

### 4. Methodology

The authors propose a two-stage algorithmic framework:

Stage 1 - Self-rewarding instruction fine-tuning:
- Use sequential rejection sampling to generate training data with both self-rewarding and self-correction patterns
- Fine-tune models on this curated data to learn error detection and correction capabilities

Stage 2 - Reinforcement learning optimization:
- Further enhance models' evaluation and refinement abilities through RL with rule-based signals
- Use techniques like DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) and PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization)

### 5. Main Findings

1. The self-rewarding framework significantly outperforms intrinsic self-correction capabilities:
- Higher accuracy in detecting errors
- Better ability to preserve correct initial responses
- More efficient use of computational resources

2. Models demonstrate strong performance compared to systems using external reward models:
- Comparable accuracy levels
- Reduced computational overhead
- More streamlined deployment

3. The two-stage training approach is effective:
- Sequential rejection sampling creates high-quality training data
- RL further enhances evaluation and correction abilities

### 6. Significance & Impact

This work makes several important contributions:

1. Theoretical: Introduces a novel framework unifying generation and evaluation in a single model

2. Practical:
- Reduces computational costs for deployment
- Eliminates need for separate reward models
- Enables more efficient scaling of inference compute

3. Methodological:
- Demonstrates effectiveness of sequential rejection sampling for training data generation
- Shows benefits of combining instruction fine-tuning with RL

### 7. Future Directions

The authors identify several promising areas for future work:
- Addressing distribution shifts during training
- Exploring larger model architectures
- Investigating alternative RL approaches for multi-turn interactions
- Extending to other reasoning tasks beyond mathematics

This research represents an important advance in making LLMs more autonomous and computationally efficient in mathematical reasoning tasks. The framework could potentially be extended to other domains requiring complex reasoning and self-correction capabilities.
