# Adversarial Attacks on Robotic Vision Language Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> University of Pennsylvania, Carnegie Mellon University, and Gray Swan AI researchers demonstrate that VLA models controlling physical robots are highly vulnerable to adversarial text prompts, with Greedy Coordinate Gradient attacks achieving over 90% success rates in forcing OpenVLA-controlled robotic arms to execute arbitrary targeted actions across four fine-tuned variants (Libero-Goal: 96.5%, Libero-Object: 93.8%, Libero-Spatial: 97.5%), while attacks persist across multiple rollout steps with 28x improvement over nominal baselines and transfer from simulation to real-world environments despite reduced effectiveness (61.2% to 38.0%), revealing that fine-tuned VLAs are significantly more susceptible than base models and that standard LLM defenses like perplexity filtering prove either ineffective or impractical for robotic control, fundamentally redefining AI safety from preventing harmful text generation to preventing malicious control authority over physical actions.

## Problem

- VLA models that directly control robot actuators inherit vulnerabilities from their LLM backbones but lack targeted safety research
- Traditional LLM jailbreaking focuses on eliciting harmful text rather than controlling physical actions
- No systematic study exists of adversarial attacks on low-level robotic control systems
- Current AI safety frameworks inadequately address embodied AI systems with real-world physical consequences

## Method

- Adapt ==Greedy Coordinate Gradient== (**GCG**) attacks from LLM jailbreaking to VLA control by optimizing ==adversarial text suffixes==
- Reframe threat model from semantic harm to "==control authority==" — ability to force arbitrary physical actions
- Develop three attack variants: ==single-step targeting==, ==persistence across visual changes==, and ==cross-architecture transfer==
- Test attacks on multiple **OpenVLA** variants controlling 7-DOF robotic arms with ==256-bin discretized action spaces==

## Results

- Single-step attacks succeed in 30–110 optimization steps (3–10 minutes) with **77–97%** success rates across models
- Persistence attacks maintain targeted actions for **28×** more steps than nominal baselines
- Sim-to-real transfer maintains substantial attack effectiveness (**61.2%** → **38.0%**) despite environmental differences
- Token budget directly correlates with attack success — halving tokens reduces success by factor of two
- Current LLM-based defenses achieve **0%** attack success but corrupt legitimate robot functionality

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLA action spaces are highly "reachable" with adversarial prompting achieving 90%+ success rates
> - Fine-tuned VLAs show 40-60% increased vulnerability compared to base models due to concentrated action distributions
> - Attacks persist across changing visual inputs and transfer from simulation to real-world environments
> - Standard LLM defenses (perplexity filtering, smoothing) prove ineffective or impractical for VLA outputs
> - Cross-architecture transfer shows limited success, suggesting attack specificity to model architecture

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical, evolving frontier in artificial intelligence: the safety and security of robotic foundation models (RFMs), particularly Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. It bridges the gap between two major research areas:

- **Robotics Foundation Models (RFMs) and Vision-Language-Action (VLAs):** The field of robotics has been significantly transformed by the advent of RFMs, which leverage large-scale pre-training and multimodal inputs (vision and language) to achieve impressive capabilities in areas like robot manipulation, autonomous driving, and surgery. The paper specifically distinguishes between high-level planners (LLMs/VLLMs that output abstract commands via an API) and low-level actuators, which are VLAs that directly output continuous control actions. Prominent VLA architectures like Google's RT-1, RT-2, RT-X, OpenVLA, and Physical Intelligence's π0 are highlighted as examples of this paradigm shift. This work specifically targets the latter—the low-level actuators—which represent a more direct form of control over a robot's physical actions.

- **Adversarial Machine Learning and AI Safety:** The AI safety community has extensively studied threats to large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLLMs), particularly prompt injection and "jailbreaking" attacks. These attacks aim to elicit objectionable or unsafe content from models that have been aligned to refuse such requests. This paper explicitly draws inspiration from this line of work, especially the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack, which is known for its effectiveness in generating universal and transferable adversarial suffixes for LLMs. However, the existing literature primarily focuses on textual or visual output safety in chatbots or virtual agents.

This paper's unique contribution is its explicit focus on **adversarial attacks on *low-level VLA actuators***. While previous work by some of the same authors explored jailbreaking *high-level LLM-controlled robotic planners*, this study extends the threat model to direct physical control. It redefines "safety" from semantic refusal of harmful content (e.g., "tell me how to build a bomb") to **control authority**—the ability to compel a robot to execute *any* targeted action, regardless of its context or perceived harm. This reframing highlights a new, more profound risk: malicious actors potentially gaining complete control over a robot's physical movements, with direct implications for real-world safety. The paper argues that traditional LLM alignment techniques are less meaningful for low-level VLA outputs because safety often depends on the interaction between the action and the environment, not just the action itself. By initiating this study, the authors are proactively identifying vulnerabilities in systems that are rapidly approaching widespread commercial deployment, emphasizing the urgency of developing robust defense mechanisms specifically tailored for embodied AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation for this research stems from the rapid advancement and impending widespread deployment of AI-enabled robots, particularly those controlled by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Given that VLA architectures often rely on frontier large language models (LLMs) as their backbone, and LLMs are notoriously susceptible to adversarial misuse like "jailbreaking," the authors pose a critical question: **to what extent do VLAs inherit these vulnerabilities, and what are the implications for physical safety?**

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Initiate the study of adversarial attacks on VLA-controlled robots:** Recognizing the gap in existing AI safety literature, the authors aim to be the first to systematically investigate how VLAs can be manipulated.
2. **Adapt and apply LLM jailbreaking attacks to obtain complete control authority over VLAs:** The central algorithmic contribution is to demonstrate that techniques like Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG), previously used for text-based models, can be effectively modified and applied to force VLAs into executing arbitrary actions.
3. **Redefine the threat model for VLAs from "semantic harm" to "control authority":** Unlike chatbot jailbreaks that aim to elicit harmful *text*, the authors seek to demonstrate the ability to continuously drive a VLA-controlled robot to *any* targeted action, irrespective of its sensory inputs or original training objective. This emphasizes the critical difference between linguistic safety and physical safety.
4. **Characterize the distinct attack landscape for VLAs:** The research aims to explore properties of VLA attacks, such as their persistence over time, generalizability across environments, and transferability between different VLA architectures, noting how these differ from LLM jailbreaking.
5. **Underline the necessity for new forms of defenses:** By exposing these vulnerabilities, the authors aim to stimulate research into VLA-specific safety mechanisms that go beyond current LLM alignment techniques.

In essence, the authors are driven by a proactive safety agenda: understanding potential misuse and risks *before* highly capable AI-enabled robots become ubiquitous collaborators in society.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adapt and apply techniques from the LLM jailbreaking literature, specifically the Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG) attack, to the domain of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

**A. Threat Model Formulation:**
The paper reframes the concept of "safety" for VLAs from semantic harm (e.g., generating dangerous instructions) to **control authority**. This means the adversary's goal is to gain the ability to continuously force a VLA-controlled robot to execute *any* targeted action, regardless of its intended function or environmental context. The attack surface considered is the **textual prompt**, where an adversary can either append tokens to a nominal instruction or replace the prompt entirely. Visual attacks are noted as future work.

**B. Algorithmic Adaptation (GCG for VLAs):**
The core of the attack is an adapted GCG optimization process. Given a textual instruction (x1:n) and an image embedding (z), a VLA predicts a sequence of low-level control actions (xn+1:n+d). The GCG algorithm iteratively modifies a fixed-length suffix (S) of the input prompt to minimize a loss function. For VLAs, this loss function (ℓ) is defined as the negative log-probability of generating the *targeted action* (xn+1:n+d), conditioned on the combined text and image inputs. This is analogous to GCG's objective of eliciting a target string from an LLM.

**C. Attack Variants:**
The authors explore three primary types of attacks:

1. **Single-step Attacks:**
 - **Objective:** To elicit a single, fixed, targeted action.
 - **Method:** Adapts GCG to minimize the loss for a given textual prompt and the image embedding from the *first* rollout step.
 - **Evaluation:** Tested on four fine-tuned OpenVLA variants (Libero-Goal, Libero-Object, Libero-Spatial, Libero-10) using a sparse gridding of the action space (7 dimensions, 256 discrete bins per dimension), focusing on "one-hot" target vectors (1792 distinct targets per model). Metrics include overall success rate, per-dimension success rate, and computational efficiency (optimization steps, time).

2. **Persistence Attacks:**
 - **Objective:** To cause a targeted action to persist across multiple VLA inference steps, even as the robot's visual inputs (and thus image embeddings) change.
 - **Method:** Modifies the single-step objective to aggregate the loss over `r` distinct image embeddings (collected either from a "burn-in" period of random actions or the initial steps of the rollout). This encourages the attack string to be invariant to visual changes.
 - **Evaluation:** Measures the number of rollout steps during which the targeted action is successfully elicited, distinguishing between "seen" (during optimization) and "unseen" images. Compares against a nominal (non-attacked) baseline.

3. **Transfer Attacks:**
 - **Objective:** To determine if attack strings optimized on one VLA architecture (source model) can successfully elicit targeted actions on *different* VLA architectures (target models), or if "universal" attacks can be optimized across multiple models.
 - **Method:**
 - **Architecture Transfer:** Optimizes attack strings on the OpenVLA base model and attempts to transfer them to TraceVLA, CogACT, and OpenPi0 (models with different architectural characteristics like dual image inputs, diffusion-based action modules, or "action experts"). Evaluated using L2 distance between elicited and target actions, as exact matches were rare.
 - **Ensemble Transfer:** Optimizes a "universal" suffix across a subset of OpenVLA fine-tunes (n=2) and tests its transferability to the remaining models.

**D. Implementation Details:**
- **VLA Architecture:** Primarily uses OpenVLA, an open-source VLA, fine-tuned on subsets of the LIBERO dataset. The models control a 7-degree-of-freedom robotic arm with a gripper. Each action dimension is discretized into 256 bins.
- **Prompting:** Adversarial tokens are either appended as a suffix to a nominal instruction (e.g., "What action should the robot take to [INSTRUCTION]? [ADVERSARIAL_SUFFIX]") or used as the entire instruction.
- **Normalization:** Targeted actions are normalized using the specific VLA's training dataset statistics, ensuring the targets are within the model's expected action space.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Maximum of 500 optimization steps for GCG, with termination upon exact match.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings that underscore the vulnerability of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to adversarial attacks:

1. **High Success Rates for Single-Step Targeted Action Elicitation (Reachability):**
 - The adapted GCG attack is highly effective. For OpenVLA variants fine-tuned on Libero-Goal, Libero-Object, and Libero-Spatial datasets, the overall attack success rate (ASR) for eliciting one-hot targeted actions reached **well over 90%** (96.5%, 93.8%, 97.5% respectively). Even the Libero-10 model, which was slightly more robust, achieved a respectable 77.4% ASR.
 - This demonstrates that the action space of commonly used VLAs is highly "reachable" – adversarial prompting can compel the robot to perform nearly any specific action.
 - **Efficiency:** Compared to LLM jailbreaking, VLA attacks are significantly faster. Successful matches were found in 30-110 optimization steps (3-10 minutes on H100 GPUs), whereas LLM jailbreaks often require all 500 steps and over an hour.

2. **Impact of Attacker's Token Budget:**
 - Increasing the number of adversarial tokens an attacker can manipulate (`|I|`) generally leads to higher success rates and fewer optimization steps needed for a successful attack. Halving the token budget (from 20 to 10) can reduce the success rate by at least a factor of two. This implies that a larger budget grants the attacker more reliable control.

3. **Environmental Agnosticism (Sim-to-Real Transfer of Attacks):**
 - Attacks optimized in a simulated environment (SIMPLER) successfully transferred to a real-world environment (HYDRA), albeit with some reduction in overall success rate (61.2% in HYDRA vs. 38.0% in SIMPLER for the base model). This crucial finding indicates that the vulnerabilities are not confined to simulation and can manifest in realistic, open-world robotic deployments.

4. **Attack Persistence over Longer Horizons:**
 - Adversarial attacks on VLAs tend to persist across multiple rollout steps, even as the model observes new visual inputs.
 - For the Libero-Spatial model, attacks showed nearly a **28x improvement** in the number of persistence steps compared to the nominal (non-attacked) baseline.
 - Increasing the number of images used during optimization (`r`) generally improves generalization to unseen images later in the rollout, demonstrating the robustness of the crafted prompts to visual changes.

5. **Limited Architectural Transferability:**
 - Direct transfer of attack strings optimized on one VLA (OpenVLA base) to other architecturally distinct VLAs (TraceVLA, CogACT, OpenPi0) showed little to no success in eliciting exact matches. In fact, optimized instructions performed no better than randomly chosen instructions in terms of L2 distance to the target action. This suggests that attack strings are highly specific to the source VLA's architecture.
 - **Ensemble transfer** (optimizing a universal suffix for two models and testing on others) showed a non-trivial effect on control authority, but it was uncorrelated with whether the initial optimization on the source models was successful. The paper suggests this is largely due to the target action's proximity to the models' default action distributions, rather than true transferability.

6. **Effectiveness of Defenses (and their limitations):**
 - **LLM-Only Perplexity Filter:** This defense, which rejects queries based on high perplexity (unusualness) of the text prompt, effectively achieved 0% ASR. However, it is deemed impractical due to the difficulty of setting a robust perplexity threshold in real-world scenarios.
 - **Multimodal Perplexity Filter:** Ineffective, as the image inputs often dominate the loss calculation, masking the textual anomaly.
 - **Smoothing Defense:** Achieved 0% ASR but also corrupted non-attacked tasks, rendering it impractical for real-world use.
 - These results highlight the inadequacy of current LLM-based defenses for the unique output format and safety requirements of VLAs.

7. **Increased Susceptibility of Fine-Tuned VLAs (Appendix Finding):**
 - A critical finding from the appendix is that OpenVLA models fine-tuned on downstream tasks are **significantly more susceptible** to adversarial attacks (40-60% increase in attack effectiveness) compared to the base OpenVLA model. This suggests that task-specific fine-tuning, while improving performance, may inadvertently concentrate the action space in a way that makes it easier for an adversary to reach targeted actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a pivotal moment in AI safety and robotics, initiating the critical study of adversarial attacks on low-level Vision-Language-Action (VLA) actuators. Its findings carry significant implications for the design, deployment, and regulation of future AI-enabled robotic systems.

**A. Novel Threat Model and Urgency:**
The paper's most significant contribution is the redefinition of "safety" in VLA-integrated systems from abstract semantic harm to **direct control authority over physical actions**. By demonstrating that adversaries can compel robots to execute *any* arbitrary action, regardless of context or perceived harm, it exposes a fundamental vulnerability with immediate and tangible physical risks. This shifts the safety discourse from preventing "bad words" to preventing "bad actions" in the real world. The authors emphasize the urgency of this study, arguing that understanding these risks *before* widespread deployment of highly capable AI-enabled robots is paramount.

**B. Fundamental Vulnerability of VLAs:**
The high success rates of single-step attacks and their persistence across visual changes reveal that VLAs, due to their LLM backbones, inherit significant vulnerabilities. The finding that fine-tuned VLAs are even *more* susceptible to these attacks further amplifies this concern, as fine-tuning is a common practice to enhance task performance. This suggests a potential trade-off between capability and robustness that needs to be addressed.

**C. Call for New Defense Paradigms:**
The ineffectiveness or impracticality of current LLM-based defense mechanisms (like perplexity filtering and smoothing) against VLA attacks is a crucial takeaway. This highlights the unique challenges posed by VLAs' physical outputs and multimodal inputs. The paper explicitly calls for:
- **VLA-specific refusal mechanisms:** Similar to how LLMs refuse to generate harmful content, VLAs need to "refuse" to execute subverted actions when an attack is detected.
- **Integration of classical control theory:** Leveraging tools from classical control, such as control barrier functions or formal methods, could provide robust safety guarantees that are absent in current VLA designs. This represents a crucial area for interdisciplinary research.

**D. Implications for Real-World Deployment:**
The demonstration that attacks optimized in simulation can transfer to real-world environments is deeply concerning. As AI-enabled robots become more prevalent in industries like manufacturing, logistics, healthcare (e.g., robot-assisted surgery), and even in homes, the potential for malicious actors to gain control and cause physical damage, injury, or disruption becomes a non-trivial societal risk. This research provides a crucial foundational understanding of how such misuse might occur.

**E. Future Research Directions:**
The paper also outlines its limitations, which serve as clear directions for future work:
- **Black-box attacks:** The current GCG approach is white-box (requires model access). Developing black-box attack methods would make these threats more realistic in practice.
- **Diffusion-based models:** Extending attack frameworks to diffusion-based VLAs (like CogACT and π0, which showed resistance in initial transfer tests) is necessary for comprehensive risk assessment.
- **Vision-based attacks:** Exploring adversarial attacks on the visual input stream of VLAs (beyond textual prompts) would complete the threat landscape.

In conclusion, this paper does more than just identify a vulnerability; it fundamentally redefines how we should think about AI safety in the context of embodied systems. It serves as an urgent call to action for the AI and robotics communities to develop robust, VLA-specific safety and alignment mechanisms to prevent potential misuse as these powerful technologies transition from research labs to real-world deployment.
