# GMT: General Motion Tracking for Humanoid Whole-Body Control

> [!summary] Summary
> The GMT framework introduces a method for training a single, unified policy that enables humanoid robots to track a wide range of human motions with high fidelity in the real world. This approach, developed by researchers from UC San Diego and Simon Fraser University, achieves state-of-the-art tracking performance in simulation and demonstrates robust reproduction of diverse skills on a Unitree G1 robot.

## Problem

- Endowing general-purpose humanoid robots with a broad repertoire of human-like movements for diverse real-world tasks remains a grand challenge.
- Existing learning-based approaches struggle with unbalanced motion data distribution, limited model expressiveness for diverse skills, partial observability, and the sim-to-real gap on physical humanoids.
- Prior methods often relied on task-specific controllers, multiple specialist policies, or achieved generality with compromised motion fidelity, lacking a unified high-fidelity solution.

## Method

- A two-stage teacher-student training framework employs a soft Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) policy architecture to enhance expressiveness and generalization across diverse motion types.
- ==An Adaptive Sampling== strategy utilizes random clipping and tracking performance-based probabilities to address unbalanced motion data, prioritizing difficult motions during training.
- Advanced motion inputs, including stacked future motion frames processed by a convolutional encoder, and explicit sim-to-real transfer techniques like domain randomization and action delay, are integrated for robust real-world deployment.

## Results

- GMT achieved state-of-the-art tracking performance in simulation, outperforming the re-implemented ExBody2 baseline across all metrics, with an E_mpkpe of **42.07** vs. **50.28** and E_yaw vel of **0.2238** vs. **0.3402** on the AMASS-Test set.
- The unified policy was successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, demonstrating stable and high-fidelity reproduction of a wide array of diverse real-world skills, including stylized walking, high kicking, and dancing.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the Motion Mixture-of-Experts architecture and Adaptive Sampling were critical for performance, with policies lacking these components showing degraded tracking and instability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A single, unified policy can effectively track a broad spectrum of human whole-body motions on a physical humanoid robot when equipped with an expressive architecture and intelligent data sampling.
> - Explicitly addressing data imbalance in large motion datasets through adaptive sampling significantly improves the learning of challenging and dynamic skills.
> - A Mixture-of-Experts architecture allows a single policy to implicitly specialize across different regions of the motion manifold, outperforming monolithic networks in fidelity and generalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of general-purpose humanoid robots capable of performing diverse tasks in everyday environments is a grand challenge in robotics. A key approach to achieving this generality is to endow robots with a broad repertoire of human-like movements. Historically, controllers for humanoid robots were labor-intensive, model-based designs, often task-specific and struggling with the high degrees of freedom and complex dynamics.

The field has seen a significant shift towards **learning-based approaches**, particularly reinforcement learning (RL) and motion imitation, which leverage large datasets of human motion. In computer graphics, methods like DeepMimic have successfully enabled simulated characters to reproduce highly diverse and agile motions. However, transferring these successes to **real-world humanoid robots** introduces substantial challenges:
- **Partial Observability:** Real robots lack full state information (e.g., global velocities, root positions) accessible in simulation.
- **Hardware Limitations:** Real robots have torque limits, joint ranges, and robustness constraints that make certain human motions (e.g., back-flips) infeasible.
- **Unbalanced Data Distribution:** Large motion capture (mocap) datasets like AMASS are often heavily skewed towards simple motions (walking, in-place activities), making it hard to learn complex, dynamic skills.
- **Model Expressiveness:** Simple neural network architectures struggle to capture the vast diversity and temporal dependencies within large motion datasets.

Previous works in humanoid motion imitation have attempted to address some of these issues. For example, teacher-student frameworks handle partial observability, while some methods fine-tune separate specialist policies for different skills. Others have tried to use larger models like transformers for expressiveness. However, a unified, general motion tracking controller capable of tracking diverse motions (including both upper and lower body skills, and variations in locomotion, crouching, kicking, etc.) with state-of-the-art fidelity on a *real robot* remained an open problem.

GMT directly addresses this gap. It builds upon the successful paradigm of learning from human motion data but introduces novel architectural and training strategies to overcome the specific challenges of unbalanced data and model expressiveness in the context of real-world humanoid deployment. By developing a *single unified policy* that can track a broad range of whole-body motions, GMT distinguishes itself from approaches that rely on multiple specialist policies or achieve generality at the cost of motion naturalness. It moves closer to the vision of truly general-purpose humanoid control by making a single policy robust and versatile enough for deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a **general and scalable motion-tracking framework (GMT)** that trains a single, unified policy. This policy aims to enable humanoid robots to track a diverse range of human motions in the real world with high fidelity and stability.

The motivations behind this objective are multifaceted:
- **Enabling General-Purpose Humanoids:** The ultimate goal for humanoid robots is to perform a wide array of tasks in dynamic, unstructured environments. A robust, general whole-body controller that can leverage a vast corpus of human motor skills is a crucial prerequisite for achieving this.
- **Overcoming Manual Design Complexity:** Manually designing controllers for high-DoF humanoid robots is exceedingly difficult and time-consuming. Learning from human motion data offers a data-driven alternative.
- **Addressing Limitations of Prior Learning Methods:** As discussed in the broader landscape, existing learning-based approaches for real humanoids face significant hurdles related to data distribution, model capacity, partial observability, and hardware constraints. Prior methods either achieved generality with compromised fidelity or required multiple specialized policies.
- **Leveraging Human Motion Data:** Human motion provides an ideal, rich resource for equipping robots with natural and agile skills, given that humanoids are designed to resemble the human body.
- **Facilitating High-Level Planning:** A robust low-level motion tracking controller would allow higher-level planners to autonomously select and sequence skills, moving towards fully autonomous and adaptable humanoid robots.

GMT's core motivation is to provide a single, effective, and deployable solution that tackles the combined challenges of data diversity, model capacity, and real-world transfer for comprehensive whole-body motion imitation on physical humanoid platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GMT is a general and effective framework designed to train a single, unified, high-quality motion tracking policy for real-world humanoid robots from large motion capture datasets. It follows a two-stage teacher-student training framework, similar to prior works:
1. **Teacher Policy Training:** A privileged teacher policy, observing full state information (proprioception and privileged information), is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
2. **Student Policy Training:** A deployable student policy, observing only proprioceptive history and motion targets (partial observability), is then trained through imitation learning using DAgger, minimizing the L2 loss between its output and the teacher's output.

The core innovations of GMT are:

- **Adaptive Sampling:** This strategy addresses the highly unbalanced distribution of motions in large datasets (e.g., AMASS, where common motions like walking dominate). It comprises:
 - **Random Clipping:** Long motions are clipped into shorter (max 10s) sub-clips with random offsets, and periodically re-clipped. This prevents over-sampling of easy segments within long motions.
 - **Tracking Performance-based Probabilities:** During training, the completion level `c_i` for each motion is logged, and episodes terminate if tracking error `E_i` is exceeded. `c_i` decays upon successful completion, and `E_i` is dynamically adjusted. A sampling level `s_i` is then defined, which gives higher sampling probability to motions where the robot is struggling more (i.e., `c_i` is low or `E_max_key_body_error` is high). This ensures the policy focuses on difficult motions.

- **Motion Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Architecture:** To enhance the expressiveness and generalization capabilities of the policy network, GMT incorporates a soft MoE module during teacher policy training.
 - The network consists of multiple **expert networks** and a **gating network**.
 - Expert networks take robot state observations and motion targets as input, outputting actions.
 - The gating network takes the same input and outputs a probability distribution over the experts.
 - The final action is a weighted combination of actions from individual experts, allowing specialized experts to handle different regions of the motion manifold.

- **Dataset Curation:** A two-stage process is used to curate a clean dataset from AMASS and LAFAN1:
 - **Rule-based filtering:** Eliminates clearly infeasible motions (e.g., extreme root angles, abnormal heights).
 - **Performance-based filtering:** A preliminary policy is trained, and motions with low completion rates are further filtered out, leading to a curated dataset of 8925 clips (33.12 hours).

- **Advanced Motion Inputs:** Instead of using only the immediate next motion frame, GMT stacks multiple consecutive future frames (approximately 2 seconds, [g_t..., g_t+100]) to capture long-term trends. These stacked frames are compressed by a convolutional encoder into a latent vector, which is then combined with the immediate next frame (g_t) and fed into the policy network. This provides both global motion context and precise immediate targets.

- **Sim-to-Real Transfer Techniques:** To bridge the reality gap, domain randomization (varying terrain height, gravity, friction, robot mass/CoM, motor strength, push velocities) and action delay are applied. Additionally, the effect of the reduction drive's moment of inertia is explicitly modeled in simulation by configuring the armature parameter.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of GMT was rigorously evaluated in both simulation and real-world settings.

**Simulation Experiments:**
- **Performance Metrics:** Policies were evaluated using E_mpkpe (mean per keybody position error), E_mpjpe (mean per joint position error), E_vel (linear velocity error), and E_yaw vel (yaw velocity error).
- **State-of-the-Art Performance:** GMT consistently outperformed the re-implemented ExBody2 baseline across all metrics on both the AMASS-test set and the LAFAN1 dataset (Table 2). For instance, on AMASS-Test, GMT achieved an E_mpkpe of 42.07 vs. ExBody2's 50.28, and E_yaw vel of 0.2238 vs. 0.3402, demonstrating superior local and global tracking.
- **Ablation Studies (Table 2(a) and Fig. 5):**
 - **Motion MoE:** Policies without MoE ("GMT w.o. MoE") showed degraded performance across all metrics, especially on challenging motions (higher top percentile errors). Qualitative analysis (Fig. 4) showed clear expert specialization for different motion phases (standing, kicking, walking), validating MoE's role in capturing motion diversity.
 - **Adaptive Sampling:** Policies without Adaptive Sampling ("GMT w.o. A.S.") also performed worse, particularly on difficult motions. Qualitative visualization (Fig. 6(a)) demonstrated that without Adaptive Sampling, the robot struggled with balance and exhibited jittery movements on challenging segments, making deployment infeasible. Joint torque plots (Fig. 6(b)) further illustrated the instability.
 - **Combined Ablation:** Removing both MoE and Adaptive Sampling ("GMT w.o. A.S. & MoE") led to the poorest performance, underscoring the synergistic importance of these two components.
- **Motion Inputs Ablation (Table 2(b)):** The study showed that incorporating a window of future motion frames significantly improved tracking accuracy. Specifically, `GMT-L2-M` (2 seconds of future frames + immediate next frame) achieved the best performance. Relying solely on the immediate next frame (`GMT-M`) or only a long window without the immediate frame (`GMT-L2`) resulted in sub-optimal tracking, highlighting the necessity of both global trend and immediate target information.

**Real-World Deployment:**
- **Hardware:** The policy was successfully deployed on the Unitree G1, a medium-sized humanoid robot with 23 DoFs.
- **Diverse Skill Reproduction:** GMT demonstrated the ability to reproduce a wide array of human motions with high fidelity and stability, including stylized walking, high kicking, dancing, spinning, crouch walking, soccer kicking, kungfu, and other dynamic skills (Figure 1). This confirms the effectiveness and robustness of the unified policy in a physical environment.

**Applications - Tracking MDM-Generated Motions:**
- GMT was tested with motions generated by the motion diffusion model (MDM) using text prompts in sim-to-sim settings (MuJoCo). The results (Fig. 7) showed that GMT could effectively track these novel, AI-generated motions, demonstrating its generalizability beyond human-captured datasets and its potential for integration with downstream motion generation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GMT framework represents a substantial step forward in the field of humanoid robot control, particularly in its ability to achieve truly general and high-fidelity motion tracking with a single unified policy.

**Key Significance:**
- **Unified General Control:** GMT successfully tackles the long-standing challenge of training a single policy to track a wide range of diverse whole-body human motions on a real humanoid robot. This eliminates the need for separate, task-specific controllers or ensembles of specialist policies, simplifying deployment and increasing versatility.
- **Robust Real-World Performance:** The successful deployment on the Unitree G1 robot for complex and dynamic skills validates GMT's robustness and capability to bridge the sim-to-real gap effectively. This brings general-purpose humanoid robots closer to reality.
- **Novel Methodological Contributions:** The Adaptive Sampling strategy provides an elegant solution to the pervasive problem of unbalanced data distribution in large motion datasets, ensuring that challenging motions are adequately learned. The Motion Mixture-of-Experts architecture effectively enhances model expressiveness, allowing a single network to specialize across different motion types. The sophisticated motion input design, combining immediate and future frames, is also a critical innovation.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This general controller can serve as a foundational component for developing more advanced whole-body algorithms on humanoid robots. It paves the way for integrating high-level planners that can autonomously sequence complex behaviors, enabling humanoids to perform more intelligent and adaptive tasks.
- **Synergy with Motion Generation:** The demonstration of tracking MDM-generated motions highlights GMT's potential to unlock the capabilities of AI-driven motion generation models, allowing robots to embody novel, text-prompted behaviors.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Humanoid Development:** By providing a robust, general motion tracking base, GMT could significantly accelerate the development and deployment of humanoid robots in various applications, from logistics and healthcare to hazardous environments and personal assistance.
- **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction:** More natural and human-like movements could lead to more intuitive and safer human-robot interaction.
- **Broader Application of Motion Data:** GMT increases the utility of large motion capture datasets and opens avenues for robots to learn from diverse sources of human movement data, including video.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Lack of Contact-Rich Skills:** The current framework does not support skills involving complex contacts like getting up from a fall or rolling, primarily due to simulation complexity and hardware constraints. This is an important area for future research.
- **Challenging Terrains:** The policy is currently trained without terrain observations and is not designed for locomotion on complex terrains like slopes or stairs. Extending the framework to handle challenging terrains is a clear next step.

Despite these limitations, GMT represents a significant advancement in humanoid robot control, offering a general, unified, and highly performant solution for imitating diverse human motions in the real world.
