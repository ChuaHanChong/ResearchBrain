# MARCH: Model-Assisted Reinforcement Learning for the Perceptive Control of Humanoids over Sparse Footholds

> [!summary] Summary
> MARCH introduces a model-assisted reinforcement learning framework for perceptive humanoid control, enabling robust bipedal locomotion over sparse footholds with lateral constraints through vision-based policies. The method achieves improved sample efficiency and smoother movements in simulation, successfully transferring to a Unitree G1 robot for real-world navigation of stepping stones.

## Problem

- Model-based bipedal locomotion methods are often brittle in real-world scenarios due to reliance on precise system dynamics and complete environmental knowledge, especially with high-dimensional visual input.
- Model-free reinforcement learning methods struggle to discover the precise, constrained motions required for safety-critical tasks like navigating sparse footholds, often demanding extensive computational resources and complex training curricula.
- Previous model-assisted RL (CLF-RL) approaches had not adequately addressed the challenge of simultaneous forward and lateral foothold constraints, which are common in complex stepping-stone tasks.

## Method

- The **MARCH** framework generates safe ==reference trajectories== using simplified dynamics (single integrator for foot, HLIP for CoM) to define desirable robot motions.
- A privileged teacher policy is trained with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), employing Control Lyapunov Function (CLF)-inspired rewards to guide learning towards safe reference tracking and convergence.
- A vision-based student policy is distilled from the teacher, utilizing a CNN for depth image processing, a transformer for multi-modal fusion, and a Mixture Density Network (MDN) output to enable perceptive control on hardware.

## Results

- Model-assisted training achieved comparable performance to model-free baselines with approximately **50%** fewer teacher training episodes (10,000 vs. 20,000), demonstrating improved sample efficiency.
- The learned policy exhibited at least **12%** less mean absolute joint torque and **39%** less mean absolute angular jerk compared to the model-free baseline, indicating smoother and more efficient locomotion.
- The distilled student policy successfully enabled a Unitree G1 humanoid robot to navigate a sequence of physical stepping stones with both forward and lateral constraints using only onboard depth camera and proprioception.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating simplified dynamic models to generate safe reference trajectories and using CLF-inspired reward shaping significantly improves RL sample efficiency and locomotion quality for safety-critical tasks.
> - A specialized student policy architecture featuring a CNN for depth perception, a transformer for multi-modal fusion, and a Mixture Density Network (MDN) output enables robust, vision-based control capable of disambiguating action choices in complex terrains.
> - Domain randomization during simulation training, combined with a model-assisted approach, facilitates effective sim-to-real transfer for complex bipedal locomotion policies on humanoid hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of bipedal robotics aims to enable robots to navigate diverse and challenging environments similar to human capabilities. A significant challenge within this domain is perceptive bipedal locomotion over sparse terrain, particularly when such terrain imposes lateral constraints. Existing methodologies for achieving safe locomotion generally fall into two categories: model-based and model-free approaches.

Traditional model-based methods, such as those relying on Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) or Model Predictive Control (MPC), offer theoretical safety guarantees and precision. However, these methods typically assume precise knowledge of system dynamics and environmental maps, which can lead to brittleness and difficulty in generalizing to real-world scenarios with uncertainties or when relying on high-dimensional visual input. While some model-based methods have been deployed on hardware, they often required data-driven refinements that compromised theoretical guarantees.

Conversely, model-free learning-based methods, specifically Reinforcement Learning (RL), have demonstrated robustness in locomotion over nominal terrains and, more recently, for quadrupedal robots in complex environments. For humanoids, however, the increased instability of bipedal systems makes applying model-free methods to safety-critical scenarios, such as sparse footholds with lateral constraints, more challenging. Purely model-free approaches for such tasks often demand extensive computational resources, large amounts of data, and complex training curricula to discover the precise, constrained motions required. Recent works have begun to achieve this, but at the cost of high compute and structured curricula.

A developing middle ground between these two paradigms involves model-assisted or physics-guided RL. This approach integrates system models to guide the learning process, often by shaping reward functions. Control Lyapunov Function (CLF)-based RL (CLF-RL) is one such method that uses an approximate model within a CLF reward function to improve learning efficiency and performance. Prior CLF-RL work has been demonstrated for safe locomotion with forward foothold constraints, but its application to scenarios involving lateral foothold constraints remained an open problem. This research extends the CLF-RL framework to address this specific gap, incorporating visual perception and novel architectural components to enable humanoid locomotion over sparse footholds with both forward and lateral constraints, while aiming to improve sample efficiency and locomotion quality.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient control policy that enables humanoid robots to perform perceptive bipedal locomotion over sparse terrain, specifically focusing on environments that impose significant lateral foothold constraints. This task is identified as safety-critical, where small errors in foot placement or balance can lead to catastrophic robot failure.

The motivation for this work arises from several limitations observed in current bipedal locomotion strategies:
1. **Brittleness of Model-Based Methods**: While model-based control offers precision and theoretical safety guarantees, its reliance on accurate system dynamics and environment models often renders it fragile in the face of real-world uncertainties, unmodeled dynamics, and the complexities of high-dimensional sensor data (e.g., visual input for identifying sparse footholds). When models are imperfect or environment maps are unknown, these methods may fail to generalize effectively.
2. **Difficulty of Model-Free Methods with Precision and Constraints**: Model-free RL methods, while robust to uncertainties and capable of learning complex behaviors, typically struggle to discover the precise, constrained motions necessary for safety-critical tasks like navigating sparse footholds. The reward landscape for such problems is often poorly conditioned, requiring extensive exploration, high sample efficiency, and elaborate training curricula, which translates to high computational costs and long training times.
3. **Lack of Solutions for Lateral Foothold Constraints**: Previous model-assisted RL works, particularly those utilizing CLF-RL, have shown promise in improving sample efficiency and performance for forward foothold constraints. However, the problem of simultaneous forward and lateral foothold constraints, which is common in stepping stone tasks, has not been adequately addressed by these methods. Lateral stability is critical and more challenging for bipedal robots.
4. **Need for Vision-Based Control**: For humanoids to operate effectively in unknown or dynamic environments, policies must directly incorporate visual information to identify and react to terrain features like sparse footholds. Existing methods often rely on pre-mapped environments or simplified perception, limiting their practical deployment.

To address these motivations, the research aims to combine the strengths of both model-based precision and model-free robustness through a model-assisted reinforcement learning framework. The specific objectives include:
- Developing a framework that leverages simplified dynamic models to generate safe reference trajectories.
- Utilizing these reference trajectories to guide reinforcement learning through CLF-inspired reward shaping, thereby improving sample efficiency.
- Designing a neural network architecture capable of processing visual and proprioceptive data to enable perceptive control over sparse terrain with lateral constraints.
- Demonstrating the transferability of the learned policy from simulation to a physical humanoid robot, specifically on a Unitree G1 navigating stepping stones.
- Achieving smoother and more energy-efficient locomotion compared to purely model-free alternatives.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named MARCH (Model-Assisted Reinforcement Learning for the Perceptive Control of Humanoids over Sparse Footholds), follows a three-step process: (1) generation of a safe reference trajectory, (2) training of a privileged teacher policy guided by a Control Lyapunov Function (CLF) reward, and (3) distillation of the teacher policy into a vision-based student policy for hardware deployment.

#### 3.1. Reference Trajectory Generation

This initial step involves creating a safe, model-based reference trajectory that defines desirable robot motions. The reference includes swing foot position/velocity, Center of Mass (CoM) position/velocity, pelvis orientation/derivatives, swing foot orientation/derivatives, and upper body joint positions/derivatives.
- **Swing Foot Position Reference (p_f, v_f):** A finite, discrete-time optimal control problem is solved using a simplified discrete-time single integrator model of the stance foot location. This planner, with a short horizon (N=4 steps), determines safe two-dimensional foot placements and step timings to track a desired velocity. It accounts for stepping-stone constraints, step feasibility (L_step feas.), and step length bounds (L_step bound), while penalizing steps near edges. A solution is approximated via random shooting over discretized time and safe foot placements. Bezier curves are then used to construct desired 3D swing foot trajectories.
- **CoM Reference (p_com, v_com):** A Hybrid Linear Inverted Pendulum (HLIP) model is utilized to generate the CoM reference trajectory. This model simplifies bipedal dynamics, assuming point feet, massless legs, no ankle torque, and a constant CoM z-height. It characterizes both continuous CoM flow and discrete step-to-step motion. The CoM trajectory is interpolated between pre-impact states using the continuous HLIP dynamics.
- **Pelvis and Swing Foot Orientation References:** Heuristic oscillations at the step frequency are incorporated for pelvis roll and pitch, while yaw tracks a commanded rate. The swing foot orientation reference maintains parallelism with the ground and aligns its yaw with the pelvis for flat-foot stepping.
- **Upper Body Reference:** This maintains nominal relative positions and orientations with respect to the CoM.

#### 3.2. Reward Shaping and Training the Teacher Policy

A privileged teacher policy, π_T,θ, is trained in simulation using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- **Teacher Architecture and Observations:** The teacher's actor and critic are Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs). The teacher has access to a 5-step history of proprioception data and privileged access to the full reference trajectory (6), providing indirect ground-truth safe foot placement information.
- **Reward Shaping (CLF-RL):** To guide the policy towards safe reference tracking and convergence, the reward function is augmented with two CLF-inspired terms, r_clf and r_dclf, as in the CLF-RL paradigm.
 - A Lyapunov-like function V(η) = (1/2)ηᵀPη is defined, where η represents the error between simulated values and reference values. P is a positive definite matrix.
 - `r_clf = exp(-min(V(η), V_max) / V_max)` rewards small error values (accurate tracking).
 - `r_dclf = -max(dV/dt(η, a) + αV(η) / V_dmax, 0)` rewards decreasing error values (convergence).
 - These are added to standard locomotion rewards, which include holonomic stance-foot rewards (penalizing displacement/velocity of the stance foot to satisfy HLIP assumptions), torque minimization, joint limits, and action smoothness.
- **Training Setup:** The teacher is trained on procedurally generated terrain featuring stepping stones with variable dimensions, gaps, heights, and random side-by-side stone pairs to induce left/right foot placement decisions. A curriculum increases difficulty. Domain randomization is applied to bridge the sim-to-real gap, including friction, mass, encoder bias, CoM offset, motor limits, joint damping/friction, push perturbations, control delay, and depth camera parameters. Termination conditions include timeout, fall over, pelvis/foot too low, and MPC infeasibility.

#### 3.3. Distillation of the Student Policy

After the teacher policy is trained, a student policy, π_S,ϕ, is distilled by minimizing the negative log likelihood loss (5) to match the teacher's actions.
- **Student Observations:** The student policy receives a 5-step history of proprioception data, desired velocity commands, and depth images from a body-mounted camera (32x24 pixels). It does not have access to privileged reference trajectories or ground-truth height maps.
- **Student Architecture:** The student policy employs a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) to process depth images, a transformer to fuse CNN embeddings with proprioception history, and a Mixture Density Network (MDN) as the output layer. The MDN generates a bimodal action distribution, allowing the policy to select between different stepping modes or footholds. Depth observations are corrupted with noise, pixel dropout, and delay to approximate real sensor values.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation validation trials and physical hardware deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot.

#### 4.1. Simulations

- **Ablation Study:** An ablation study was conducted to evaluate the individual contributions of the transformer network and the Mixture Density Network (MDN) within the student policy architecture.
 - Comparison: The proposed architecture was compared against two variants: one where the transformer was replaced with a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) and another where the MDN was replaced with an MLP configured for deterministic regression.
 - Results: The study, illustrated in Figure 3, indicated that both the transformer and the MDN components contributed to improved student policy performance. Policies incorporating these elements demonstrated a greater ability to generate reward during distillation, suggesting their utility in processing complex observations and disambiguating action choices.

- **Baseline Comparison:** The model-assisted framework was compared against a model-free PPO baseline, which utilized a similar teacher-student distillation framework but lacked the CLF-inspired rewards for reference trajectory tracking.
 - Sample Efficiency: While both the model-informed and model-free methods converged to a comparable mean distance traveled over the stepping stone terrain, the model-informed approach achieved this with approximately 50% fewer teacher training episodes (10,000 episodes for model-informed vs. 20,000 for model-free). This demonstrates improved sample efficiency for the proposed method.
 - Locomotion Quality: Post-distillation, the model-assisted method resulted in significantly smoother locomotion. Specifically, it exhibited at least 12% less mean absolute joint torque and at least 39% less mean absolute angular jerk compared to the model-free baseline after 500 distillation iterations. This indicates a more efficient and less aggressive control strategy.
 - Curriculum Complexity: The model-free baseline reportedly required a more structured curriculum, commencing on flat ground before progressing to stepping stones, whereas the model-assisted method required a less complex curriculum.

#### 4.2. Hardware Experiments

- **Experimental Setup:** The hardware trials involved a Unitree G1 humanoid robot navigating a sequence of four stepping stones arranged in a pseudo-random, alternating left-and-right configuration. The stones presented both forward and lateral foot placement challenges.
- **Deployment and Performance:** The distilled student policy was executed entirely on the robot's onboard Jetson Orin Nx computer, using an Intel Realsense D435i depth camera for visual feedback. The full vision-proprioception pipeline, including the CNN and transformer, operated at approximately 50 Hz, matching the simulation control loop frequency.
- **Results:** The robot successfully traversed the sparse footholds, demonstrating that the model-assisted, vision-based control policy could be effectively transferred from simulation to physical hardware. This confirms the ability of the framework to enable safety-critical locomotion over sparse terrain with lateral constraints in a real-world setting. However, the paper notes that physical deployment was less repeatable than simulation, attributed to camera latency and accuracy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a model-assisted reinforcement learning framework that addresses several key challenges in perceptive bipedal locomotion, particularly over safety-critical sparse terrain with lateral constraints. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood through its contributions to methodological advancements, performance improvements, and hardware applicability.

**Methodological Advancements:**
The core contribution is the integration of simplified dynamics models (discrete-time single integrator for foot planning, HLIP for CoM) to generate safe reference trajectories that actively guide the learning process. By incorporating these model-based insights into a reinforcement learning framework via Control Lyapunov Function (CLF)-inspired rewards, the approach effectively combines the precision of model-based methods with the robustness of model-free learning. The use of a teacher-student distillation framework, coupled with a specialized student policy architecture (CNN-Transformer-MDN), further enables robust vision-based control from partial observations. The introduction of an MDN output for the student policy is a notable innovation, allowing the policy to disambiguate and choose between different safe stepping modes, which is crucial for complex terrains.

**Performance Improvements:**
The empirical results demonstrate tangible improvements over purely model-free baselines.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency:** The model-assisted approach required significantly fewer training episodes (50% less teacher training) to achieve comparable performance in terms of distance covered. This reduction in data requirements is critical for the practical deployment of RL in robotics, where real-world data collection is expensive and time-consuming.
- **Smoother Locomotion:** The learned policies exhibited notably lower joint torques (at least 12% less) and angular jerk (at least 39% less) compared to model-free alternatives. Smoother locomotion is indicative of more stable, energy-efficient, and less mechanically stressful movements, which enhances the longevity and operational range of robotic platforms. This is particularly important for humanoids due to their inherent instability and complex joint structures.
- **Reduced Curriculum Complexity:** The ability to achieve robust performance with a less structured training curriculum suggests that the model guidance effectively simplifies the learning problem, reducing the need for extensive manual reward shaping and environment design.

**Hardware Applicability and Generalization:**
The successful deployment of the distilled student policy on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot, navigating physically challenging stepping stones with lateral constraints, validates the sim-to-real transfer capabilities of the framework. This demonstration on real hardware, utilizing only onboard sensors (depth camera and proprioception), is a crucial step towards autonomous humanoid operation in unstructured environments. It shows that the learned policy can generalize from a randomized simulation environment to a physical system, a common hurdle in robot learning.

**Potential Impact:**
This research contributes to making humanoid robots more capable and reliable in safety-critical, perceptually challenging environments. The demonstrated ability to navigate sparse footholds with lateral constraints through vision-based control opens avenues for humanoids to operate in disaster relief, construction sites, and exploration scenarios that require traversing uneven and discontinuous terrain. The principles of model-assisted RL, specifically the CLF-RL paradigm and the architectural choices for perceptive student policies, could be generalized to other complex robotic locomotion tasks and potentially other domains requiring precise, constrained actions under uncertainty. Future work, as noted by the authors, may focus on expanding the sensory capabilities (active camera direction), incorporating more dynamic behaviors (jumping), and further improving robustness against real-world sensor inaccuracies and latency, which could extend the robot's operational envelope even further.
