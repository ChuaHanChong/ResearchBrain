# Recursive Criticality of AI Self-Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> This theory paper models AI self-improvement as a delayed dynamical system where capability growth depends on baseline research productivity, recursive gain, operational closure, and frontier hardening. It derives a recursive reproduction number, the ratio of realized recursive gain to frontier hardness, whose crossing of unity marks a local stability transition into self-amplifying improvement. Extending to coupled research actors, the network's criticality is the spectral radius of a reproduction matrix, so an ecosystem can amplify while every actor stays subcritical.

## Problem

- Prior economic and growth models place AI inside research production but do not supply a stability criterion separating self-amplifying feedback from merely rapid, externally driven acceleration.
- Existing research-agent benchmarks measure components of the loop, yet none identify how a capability improvement causally raises the productivity of subsequent AI development.
- Earlier treatments leave open how deployment delay, progressive hardening of research opportunities, and coupling between competing organizations shape whether feedback remains stable.

## Method

- A ==delayed capability ODE== where growth equals baseline productivity times a frontier-productivity factor times an exponential feedback potential evaluated at lagged capability, separating gain, operational closure, delay, and hardening.
- A ==recursive reproduction number== defined as closure times gain divided by frontier hardness; linearizing around a reference trajectory yields a characteristic equation whose root sign proves ==criticality at unity==.
- A finite-frontier form with hardening exponent and a ==logistic operational-closure function==, plus a multi-actor linearization whose ==row-normalized reproduction matrix== gives network criticality via its ==spectral radius==.

## Results

- Calibrated to a **24-year** baseline, recursive gains of **0.5**, **3**, **6**, and **15** advance AGI crossing from **24 years** to **21.4**, **12.9**, **8.4**, and **4.5 years** respectively.
- Strong feedback compresses the AGI-to-ASI interval from **72 years** at baseline to **0.45 years**, while the reproduction number still falls back below unity as the frontier hardens.
- In network scenarios, individually subcritical open-ecosystem actors reach spectral radius **1.55** and the shortest **2.8-year** AGI-to-ASI gap, beating higher-effort global competition at **4.45 years**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fast benchmark progress is neither necessary nor sufficient evidence of recursive amplification; diagnosis requires estimating recursive gain and frontier hardening across successive development cycles.
> - Scaling compute, spend, or headcount can speed progress without shifting the critical threshold, since baseline throughput scales damping and feedback terms alike.
> - Aggregate safety monitoring of individual labs is insufficient: cross-organization diffusion of improvements can push a research ecosystem supercritical with no single actor above threshold.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

AI systems are already being used for coding, experiments, analysis, paper replication, and other work inside the process that builds future AI systems. The authors argue that the relevant feedback loop belongs to the whole AI-enabled R&D system, not necessarily to a lone, autonomous machine. That system may include human researchers, labs, infrastructure, and deployment processes.

The difficulty is that speed alone is not diagnostic in this model. The authors therefore do not judge self-improvement by how sharply a capability curve rises. They compare the research boost that an improvement produces in successor development with the growing difficulty of making further progress. In one high-feedback scenario, the interval between the paper's illustrative AGI and ASI thresholds shrinks from 72 years in the no-RSI baseline to 0.45 years. This is a model result, not an observed forecast.

Simulated trajectories for smooth scaling, weak supercriticality, transient takeoff, rapid AGI-to-ASI, and the no-RSI baseline: (a) capability $x(t)$ with AGI and ASI thresholds, and (b) the reproduction number with the $\mathcal{R}_{\text{AI}}=1$ boundary. Stronger-feedback trajectories cross the capability thresholds earlier. Thresholds and scenarios are illustrative; these are simulated, not measured, trajectories.

### 2. What the threshold measures

The loop runs in sequence. Existing capability can increase subsequent AI-R&D productivity. Only the fraction that propagates through the development pipeline becomes realized gain. That gain returns after an end-to-end delay in a validated successor. Meanwhile, frontier hardening makes further direct improvement less productive.

The surviving fraction, called operational closure, is a property of the process rather than of model autonomy. Human participation can coexist with high closure, and autonomy can coexist with low closure if outputs fail to propagate.

Schematic of the proposed mechanism: capability $x(t)$ feeds AI–R&D productivity through gain $a$ and closure $\chi$, and baseline research $r(t)$ also contributes. A delayed return $\tau$ carries a validated successor back to capability, while frontier hardening $\sigma$ reduces further capability gains.

The **recursive reproduction number** compares these terms:

$$\mathcal{R}_{\text{AI}} = \frac{g}{\sigma} = \frac{\chi a}{\sigma}$$

Here $a$ is potential recursive gain, $g=\chi a$ is realized gain, and $\sigma$ is the decline in direct-improvement productivity as capability advances. Above one, small perturbations amplify locally; below one, they damp. In the minimal model, baseline research throughput can change the pace without changing whether the system is subcritical or supercritical. A high baseline research rate can therefore drive fast but subcritical progress. A newly supercritical system may not yet show conspicuous acceleration.

### 3. What the scenarios show

The AGI-to-ASI interval is the difference between the first crossing of $x_{\text{AGI}}=0.50$ and the first crossing of $x_{\text{ASI}}=0.80$ on an illustrative capability coordinate. In the no-RSI baseline, AGI comes at 24.00 years and ASI at 96.00 years, an interval of 72.00 years. In the $a=15$ rapid-transition scenario, AGI comes at 4.50 years and ASI at 4.95 years, an interval of 0.45 years. These are years within the scenario, not predicted calendar dates. Only recursive gain varies across the primary set.

Subcritical feedback can still advance the trajectory. A temporary supercritical episode can also leave a persistent lead after frontier hardening pushes the system back below the boundary. Recursive gain, closure steepness, and frontier hardness are not directly estimated in the paper, so the numbers are conditional outputs of the stated parameterization.

### 4. Criticality can be collective

In the model, every actor can be individually subcritical while the network is supercritical if cross-actor transfer is strong enough. This stability proposition is for the zero-delay system. In the open-ecosystem illustration, the actor-level values are 0.65, 0.55, and 0.45, while the network value is 1.55. That scenario also assumes an effort multiplier of 0.8 and a delay of 0.50 years, and all strategic parameters are illustrative.

The paper's contribution is a local stability criterion, not a claim of indefinite acceleration or unbounded capability. To detect the boundary, the model says one must estimate recursive gain and frontier hardening rather than read a benchmark trajectory alone. The model identifies these quantities for measurement; it does not establish their current real-world values.
