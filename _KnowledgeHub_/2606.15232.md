# Rethinking Implicit Spatial Representation in Visuomotor Policy Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A study from Nanyang Technological University empirically validates implicit spatial representations for robotic manipulation, identifies "spatial information collapse" in conventional visual encoders, and introduces PRISM. This novel encoder preserves multiscale spatial information, leading to up to 168% relative improvement on a high-precision ToolHang task and better generalization on multi-task benchmarks.

## Problem

- Traditional visual encoders in visuomotor policies, optimized for semantic recognition, often lose fine-grained spatial information due to aggressive spatial downsampling.
- Robotic manipulation tasks require precise spatial cues, such as object pose and contact structures, which are diminished by this "spatial information collapse" in current encoder designs.
- The specific role and effectiveness of implicit spatial representations, like Spatial Softmax Pooling (SSPool), in visuomotor policy learning for robotics were not thoroughly understood.

## Method

- Systematically investigated and compared implicit spatial representations (**SSPool**) against implicit semantic representations (AvgPool, MaxPool) through controlled pooling ablations.
- Diagnosed "==spatial information collapse==" as a critical bottleneck for geometry-sensitive tasks by testing performance on a low-resolution, high-precision task (ToolHang).
- Proposed PRISM, a visual encoder that extracts multiscale implicit spatial representations from different stages and fuses them using a Top-Down Cross-Attention mechanism.

## Results

- SSPool achieved an average **90.0%** success rate on short-horizon Robomimic tasks, outperforming AvgPool (**81.1%**) and MaxPool (**71.9%**) with significantly lower dimensionality.
- PRISM improved performance on the high-precision ToolHang task by up to **168.0%** relative, increasing average success rates from **5.0%** to **13.4%** across different policy backbones.
- The proposed method demonstrated improved generalization on multi-task benchmarks, boosting success rates on LIBERO-10 from **22.0%** to **53.5%** and on LIBERO-Spatial from **37.0%** to **52.5%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicit spatial representations from SSPool provide more effective and stable visual cues for robotic manipulation, yielding cleaner and more localized saliency responses than semantic pooling methods.
> - Aggressive spatial compression in standard visual encoders leads to "spatial information collapse," significantly weakening fine-grained spatial cues essential for precise, geometry-sensitive manipulation tasks.
> - Preserving and intelligently aggregating multiscale implicit spatial representations through a top-down attention mechanism effectively mitigates spatial information collapse, enhancing precision and robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has increasingly adopted generative model-based imitation learning (IL) as a paradigm for acquiring complex skills from human demonstrations. These policies often rely on denoising Gaussian noise into action distributions, conditioned on visual features extracted from observations. The effectiveness of such policies is critically dependent on the quality and nature of these visual representations.

Traditional visual encoders, such as ResNet backbones, have been widely used in these policies. These encoders primarily derive their design principles from semantic recognition tasks like image classification, where the emphasis is on identifying *what* visual patterns are present. This often involves aggressive spatial downsampling and global aggregation, which can lead to a loss of fine-grained spatial information. However, robotic manipulation inherently requires precise spatial cues, including object pose, contact structures, and robot-object alignment, to execute actions accurately.

Spatial softmax pooling (SSPool) has been incorporated into some visuomotor policies as an architectural component to introduce spatial awareness. However, its specific role as an *implicit spatial representation* mechanism, its effectiveness, and its underlying function have remained underexplored. Prior work has often treated SSPool as a modular addition rather than systematically investigating its fundamental properties in the context of visuomotor control.

This research addresses this gap by systematically investigating the properties of implicit spatial representations, particularly those generated by SSPool, in the context of robotic manipulation. It questions whether these representations offer stable and effective visual features. Furthermore, the work identifies a bottleneck in existing encoder designs, termed "spatial information collapse," where repeated downsampling diminishes the fine-grained spatial data before it can be effectively utilized by the policy. By proposing PRISM, a visual encoder that preserves multiscale implicit spatial information, this research aims to enhance the spatial reasoning capabilities of visuomotor policies, thereby contributing to the development of more robust and precise robotic manipulation systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to re-evaluate and enhance the understanding and utility of implicit spatial representations in visuomotor policy learning for robotic manipulation. This overarching objective is driven by several detailed motivations and specific research questions:

1. **Understanding Implicit Spatial Representations:** The central question motivating this work is: "Do implicit spatial representations provide effective and stable visual features for robotic manipulation?" While spatial softmax pooling (SSPool) has been used, its mechanisms and effectiveness have not been sufficiently elucidated. The authors aim to empirically demonstrate whether such coordinate-aware representations offer advantages over conventional feature-value representations (e.g., from average or max pooling) that primarily capture semantic content. This involves systematic studies to compare different pooling methods.

2. **Identifying Limitations in Current Visual Encoders:** The researchers are motivated to investigate potential bottlenecks within current visual encoder designs commonly adopted in robotic manipulation. They hypothesize that standard encoders, designed for semantic recognition, with their repeated spatial downsampling and global aggregation, may inadvertently lead to "spatial information collapse." This collapse could weaken the fine-grained spatial structure essential for tasks requiring precise pose estimation, object alignment, and contact reasoning, especially when input observations are of low resolution. The motivation here is to diagnose if the limitations of SSPool are inherent to the representation itself or arise from how it is currently integrated into encoder architectures.

3. **Developing an Improved Visual Encoder:** Motivated by the findings from the above investigations, the third objective is to propose and develop a novel visual encoder architecture, named PRISM (Preserving Multiscale Implicit Spatial Representation). The goal of PRISM is to mitigate the identified spatial information collapse by preserving multiscale implicit spatial representations throughout the encoding process. This aims to provide the action-generation module with richer and more detailed spatial cues, particularly for geometry-sensitive manipulation tasks.

In summary, the key motivations are to gain a deeper empirical understanding of spatial representations, diagnose architectural shortcomings in current visual encoders for robotic manipulation, and subsequently propose an improved encoder design that leverages multiscale spatial information to enhance policy performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research comprises a systematic investigation of pooling strategies in visual encoders, the identification of an encoder-level bottleneck, and the proposal and evaluation of a novel visual encoder architecture, PRISM.

#### 3.1. Investigating Implicit Spatial vs. Implicit Semantic Representations

The research begins by categorizing visual representations based on how they process feature maps $F \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$.
- **Implicit Semantic Representation:** This category includes conventional global aggregation methods like Average Pooling (AvgPool) and Max Pooling (MaxPool), which extract scalar values per channel. These representations primarily reflect the presence or strength of visual features, emphasizing *what* visual patterns are present, without explicitly preserving relative spatial locations.
- **Implicit Spatial Representation:** This category refers to the output of Spatial Softmax Pooling (SSPool). For each channel, SSPool normalizes activations over spatial locations and computes an expected coordinate $(x_c, y_c)$ by combining feature activations with a coordinate grid. This process yields coordinate-aware descriptors that preserve *where* feature patterns occur, providing cues relevant to pose and alignment.

To systematically compare these, the authors conducted controlled pooling ablations. They used a ResNet18 encoder and varied only the pooling strategy applied to the final feature maps (AvgPool, MaxPool, NoPool, SSPool). This was done under standard and truncated ResNet18 backbones to observe the effect of spatial compression. Policies were trained with Diffusion Policy (DP), Flow Matching (FM), and MeanFlow (MF) backbones on various Robomimic tasks.

#### 3.2. Identifying Spatial Information Collapse

To investigate the limitations of aggressive spatial compression, the researchers used the low-resolution, high-precision ToolHang task as a stress test. They compared the performance of different pooling strategies on this task and specifically evaluated the impact of using less-compressed feature maps (via a truncated ResNet) and multiscale SSPool. This allowed them to diagnose if the limitation was inherent to implicit spatial representations or due to their application after significant spatial downsampling.

#### 3.3. Proposed Methodology: PRISM

Motivated by the finding that aggressive compression weakens fine-grained spatial cues, PRISM (Preserving Multiscale Implicit Spatial Representation) was proposed. PRISM addresses this by:

- **Multiscale Implicit Spatial Softmax (MS-SS):** Instead of applying SSPool only to the final feature map, PRISM extracts implicit spatial representations from multiple residual stages of the visual encoder. For each feature map $x_l$ from stage $l$, a residual refinement block $F_l^{ref}(\cdot)$ is applied to produce a refined feature map $\hat{x}_l$, followed by SSPool$_l(\cdot)$ to extract the implicit spatial representation $s_l$. This generates a set of multiscale spatial representations $\{s_l\}_{l=1}^L$.
- **Top-Down Cross-Attention (TDCA) Fusion:** To aggregate these multiscale cues efficiently, PRISM employs a lightweight TDCA module. The deepest spatial representation $s_L$ (which contains broader task-level context) is used as the query. The earlier-stage representations $\{s_l\}_{l=1}^{L-1}$ (which retain finer spatial details) are concatenated to form $s_{1:L-1}$ and serve as keys and values. The fusion is formulated as $z = \text{softmax} \left( \frac{Q(s_L)K(s_{1:L-1})^\top}{\sqrt{d}} \right) V(s_{1:L-1})$, where $Q(\cdot)$, $K(\cdot)$, and $V(\cdot)$ are learnable projections. The resulting attended feature $z$ is then projected to a fixed-dimensional vector, which serves as the compact visual condition for the downstream policy. This top-down mechanism allows higher-level context to selectively retrieve relevant finer-scale cues.

#### 3.4. Experimental Evaluation

The evaluation of PRISM and the various pooling strategies was conducted across multiple scenarios:
- **Robomimic Tasks:** Short-horizon tasks (Lift, Can, Square) and the high-precision, low-resolution ToolHang task.
- **Policy Backbones:** Diffusion Policy (DP), Flow Matching (FM), and MeanFlow (MF).
- **Input Resolutions and Camera Views:** Experiments included 84x84 and 240x240 observations, and single-view versus multi-view setups.
- **Component Ablations:** Studies were conducted to assess the individual contributions of MS-SS and TDCA within PRISM.
- **Multi-task and Real-world Scenarios:** PRISM was evaluated on the LIBERO-10 and LIBERO-Spatial benchmarks for generalization, and on real-world robotic tasks (StackCube, KeyPick) for physical deployment validation.
- **Control Measures:** All encoder comparisons maintained consistent policy architectures, training schedules, and evaluation protocols to isolate the impact of visual representation choices.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research addressed five specific questions, yielding the following main findings:

**RQ1. What evidence supports implicit spatial representations for robotic manipulation?**
- **Saliency Analysis:** Saliency maps for tasks like Lift, Can, and Square revealed that SSPool produced cleaner and more localized responses on task-relevant regions compared to AvgPool, MaxPool, and NoPool, which generated more diffuse or inconsistent attention patterns. This qualitatively suggested that SSPool helps the encoder preserve spatial cues.
- **Quantitative Performance (Table 1):** On three short-horizon Robomimic tasks with a standard ResNet18 backbone, SSPool achieved an average success rate of 90.0%, outperforming AvgPool (81.1%), MaxPool (71.9%), and NoPool (70.7%). This was achieved with a significantly smaller representation dimensionality (64 dimensions for SSPool vs. 512 for AvgPool/MaxPool and 4608 for NoPool).
- **Stability with Truncated ResNet18 (Table 1):** When using a truncated ResNet18 (less spatial compression), SSPool's performance remained stable (90.0% to 89.8%), while AvgPool and MaxPool showed performance drops. This suggested SSPool provides a stable spatial representation that benefits from richer feature maps.
- **Channel-Shuffle Ablation (Table 2):** Randomly shuffling coordinate channels in SSPool led to a substantial performance drop (e.g., from 89.7% to 34.0% for DP on Lift) but still resulted in non-zero success rates. This indicated that SSPool provides both coordinate-level spatial cues and a channel-structured implicit spatial representation important for policy learning.

**RQ2. Does spatial compression limit geometry-sensitive manipulation?**
- **ToolHang Task (Table 3):** For the low-resolution (84x84), high-precision ToolHang task, standard SSPool applied to the final ResNet feature map achieved a lower success rate (4.7%) compared to AvgPool (7.3%), MaxPool (8.0%), and NoPool (8.7%). This contrasted with the findings on simpler tasks.
- **Mitigation by Less Compression (Table 3):** When SSPool was applied to a less-compressed feature map (Truncated ResNet), the success rate improved by 55% (from 4.7% to 7.3%). Further, a multiscale SSPool (MS-SSPool) approach significantly improved performance by 140% (from 4.7% to 11.3%), achieving the best result in this setting. These results supported the hypothesis that aggressive encoder compression degrades fine-grained implicit spatial information crucial for challenging manipulation.

**RQ3. How does PRISM improve low-resolution manipulation performance?**
- **ToolHang Improvements (Table 4):** PRISM consistently improved performance on the geometry-sensitive ToolHang task. Under Flow Matching, the success rate increased from 4.7% to 14.0%, and under Diffusion Policy, it improved from 5.3% to 12.7%. The average relative improvement for ToolHang across both policies was 168.0% (from 5.0% to 13.4%).
- **Performance across Resolutions (Table 5):** PRISM showed improvements across various input resolutions and camera views for ToolHang. Notably, for single-view 240x240 inputs, PRISM significantly boosted success rates for DP, FM, and MF from averages of 22%, 18%, and 16% to 42%, 30%, and 32% respectively.
- **Saliency Maps (Fig. 4):** Qualitative analysis showed PRISM producing cleaner and more localized saliency maps on task-relevant regions throughout different stages of ToolHang (grasping, alignment, insertion, hanging), indicating improved focus.

**RQ4. How does each component of PRISM contribute to performance?**
- **Component Ablation (Fig. 5):** On the ToolHang task, Multiscale Spatial Softmax (MS-SS) alone substantially improved over baseline final-stage SSPool. The addition of Top-Down Cross-Attention (TDCA) further enhanced both final and peak success rates, demonstrating the benefit of guided aggregation of multiscale cues. The full PRISM model achieved performance comparable to a 240x240 input with only 84x84 observations.
- **Multiscale for Other Poolings (Fig. 6):** Applying multiscale feature enhancement to other pooling operators showed that MS-SSPool achieved the highest success rate with the lowest parameter overhead. While multiscale AvgPool also benefited, it required more additional parameters. MaxPool showed negligible gains, and NoPool degraded performance, suggesting that multiscale features are most effective when paired with pooling methods that preserve implicit spatial structure.

**RQ5. Can PRISM be deployed in multi-task and real-world scenarios?**
- **Multi-task LIBERO (Table 6):** PRISM improved performance on the LIBERO-10 benchmark from 22.0% to 53.5% and on LIBERO-Spatial from 37.0% to 52.5%, indicating generalization to multi-task visuomotor learning.
- **Real-world (Table 7):** In real-world experiments on StackCube and KeyPick tasks, PRISM achieved success rates of 70.0% and 80.0% respectively, maintaining performance comparable to the baseline. While not showing clear gains on these relatively simpler tasks, it demonstrated successful physical deployment without degradation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work provides empirical evidence supporting the utility of implicit spatial representations in robotic manipulation, challenging the default adoption of visual encoders primarily designed for semantic recognition. The systematic studies and saliency analyses demonstrate that coordinate-aware spatial representations can offer more effective and stable visual conditioning signals for visuomotor policies compared to compact feature-value representations, often with fewer dimensions. This represents a foundational insight for designing visual perception systems in robotics.

The identification of "spatial information collapse" as a critical encoder-level bottleneck in geometry-sensitive manipulation tasks, particularly with low-resolution observations, highlights a significant architectural limitation in commonly used vision backbones. This diagnosis suggests that the issue is not inherent to spatial representations themselves but rather how they are typically extracted from overly compressed feature maps.

The proposed PRISM encoder directly addresses this bottleneck by introducing multiscale implicit spatial softmax extraction and top-down cross-attention fusion. Its consistent performance improvements across various tasks, policy backbones, and input resolutions, particularly on high-precision tasks like ToolHang, indicate its practical value. The ability of PRISM to achieve performance comparable to higher-resolution inputs using only low-resolution observations suggests a potential for efficiency gains in robotic systems.

Beyond the specific encoder architecture, the broader significance of this work lies in its contribution of empirical evidence for implicit spatial representations as a fundamental and effective design principle for visuomotor policy learning. This suggests a shift in focus for future visual encoder development, advocating for a balanced approach that integrates strong semantic understanding with preserved spatial structure, particularly for tasks involving contact-rich and precision-sensitive manipulation. While PRISM introduces some computational overhead and its real-world benefits were not demonstrably superior on simpler tasks, it lays groundwork for more robust and spatially aware robotic control systems.
