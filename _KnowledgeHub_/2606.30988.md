# Multisensory Continual Learning: Adapting Pretrained Visuomotor Policies to Force

> [!summary] Summary
> Stanford University researchers developed the Multi-Sensory World Model (MuSe), enabling pretrained robot policies to integrate new sensor modalities like force-torque (F/T) for novel contact-rich tasks while preserving or enhancing existing skills. MuSe demonstrated improved success rates on new contact-rich tasks, such as 11.5/15 on vase wiping, and enhanced performance on original pretraining tasks, reaching 12.5/15 on whiteboard wiping, compared to prior methods.

## Problem

- Vision-only robot policies struggle to fully capture the physical interaction state necessary for contact-rich tasks.
- Integrating new sensor modalities (e.g., force-torque, tactile) into pretrained policies is challenging due to smaller, less diverse multisensory datasets and the risk of catastrophic forgetting.
- Existing robot learning approaches often treat multisensory learning and continual learning as separate, lacking a framework for adapting to *new* modalities for *new* tasks while preserving existing skills.

## Method

- **MuSe** employs a ==multi-stage fusion== architecture (==early and late fusion==) to combine new modality features (e.g., F/T) with existing visual, proprioceptive, and action representations without corrupting pretrained knowledge.
- A ==multisensory future prediction== objective trains the model to forecast future actions, original observations, and new modality values, promoting a unified, physically grounded latent space.
- ==Experience replay== is utilized during finetuning, sampling from both new multisensory and original pretraining datasets, to mitigate ==catastrophic forgetting== and preserve performance on prior tasks.

## Results

- MuSe achieved the lowest F/T prediction error (**8.424** L2 error) on pretraining tasks where F/T was recorded but never used for supervision, indicating strong cross-modal generalization.
- On new contact-rich tasks, MuSe demonstrated improved success rates, achieving **11.5/15** on vase wiping and **13/15** on peg insertion, outperforming baselines that lacked F/T input or pretraining.
- Multimodal finetuning with MuSe enhanced performance on original pretraining tasks (e.g., **12.5/15** on whiteboard wiping, **10/15** on diverse peg insertion), preventing catastrophic forgetting and surpassing the performance of the original vision-only policy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective cross-modal generalization for new sensor modalities requires both deep multi-stage fusion and a comprehensive multisensory future prediction objective that includes original observation modalities (e.g., video).
> - Experience replay is crucial for preventing catastrophic forgetting when continually adapting pretrained policies with new sensor inputs, allowing the model to retain proficiency on original tasks.
> - Integrating a new physical modality like force-torque can provide backward transfer benefits, improving performance on existing vision-only tasks by grounding the policy in a more physically aware understanding of interaction dynamics.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning has made significant advancements through large-scale vision-action pretraining, which enables policies to acquire broad manipulation capabilities from diverse visual data. However, a limitation of vision-only approaches is their inability to fully capture the physical interaction state required for contact-rich tasks. These tasks often depend on signals such as contact forces, tactile feedback, or audio cues, which are only indirectly inferable from images. While incorporating additional modalities like force-torque (F/T), tactile, and audio sensing can provide direct feedback on these hidden interaction dynamics, there are practical challenges associated with their use.

Multisensory datasets are typically smaller and less diverse than vision-only datasets, and the sensors themselves are often hardware- and task-specific. Consequently, it is generally impractical to include every possible sensor during the initial large-scale pretraining phase. This necessitates that robot policies adapt to new sensor modalities post-pretraining, efficiently integrating limited data from new sensors while leveraging existing visual knowledge.

Existing research has largely addressed multisensory learning and continual learning as separate paradigms. Multisensory learning approaches usually assume access to all relevant modalities from the outset of training. Conversely, continual learning typically focuses on acquiring new tasks under a fixed set of sensory inputs, aiming to mitigate catastrophic forgetting of previously learned skills. This paper addresses the intersection of these two areas, specifically focusing on the challenge of augmenting an existing, pretrained policy with *new* sensory modalities for *new* tasks, while simultaneously preserving performance on the *original* tasks and utilizing the original sensor suite. This "multi-sensory continual learning" setting reflects a practical deployment scenario where a robot system must incrementally expand its sensory capabilities without requiring complete re-training or sacrificing established skills.

The work builds upon prior developments in multisensory robot learning, which have demonstrated the utility of modalities such as touch and force for understanding contact state and interaction dynamics. It also relates to continual learning in robotics, where techniques like experience replay and regularization are employed to maintain performance when adapting policies to new data. Furthermore, it touches upon multisensory adaptation methods that extend generalist robot policies, but distinguishes itself by specifically targeting contact-rich manipulation and investigating how a newly introduced modality can offer backward transfer benefits to original, vision-only tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for multi-sensory continual learning that allows a pretrained robot policy to incorporate new sensory modalities for novel tasks, while ensuring that its performance on the original tasks is preserved or enhanced. The authors formalize this challenge and articulate three specific goals for a successful method:
1. **Forward transfer:** The method should improve performance on new tasks by effectively utilizing the newly introduced modality, relative to training a policy from scratch.
2. **Backward transfer:** The method should retain or improve the performance on pretraining tasks even after learning new tasks that incorporate new sensory data. This aims to prevent catastrophic forgetting and potentially enhance existing capabilities.
3. **Cross-modal generalization:** The method should enable the policy to understand and utilize the newly introduced modality on tasks where it was never explicitly observed during finetuning.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from several technical challenges inherent in this setting. A straightforward approach of finetuning a policy on new tasks with new sensor data often leads to catastrophic forgetting, where the policy's proficiency on its original pretraining tasks degrades. Moreover, successfully incorporating a new modality requires the model to interpret new input dimensions that were absent during pretraining. The policy must learn to integrate this new information effectively without either over-relying on it (e.g., in situations where the new sensor is uninformative or absent) or disregarding it. This necessitates an algorithm capable of preserving the vast prior knowledge acquired during pretraining while seamlessly integrating new input modalities through a robust and effective cross-modal representation.

To address these challenges, the authors propose the Multi-Sensory World Model (MuSe), designed to enable efficient integration of new sensory modalities into pretrained policies. The core motivations for MuSe's specific components are:
- **Multi-stage fusion:** To combine modalities expressively without corrupting the pretrained representation. This ensures that the new modality can interact deeply with existing vision, proprioception, language, and action representations without overwriting critical visual-action knowledge or causing excessive reliance on contact signals when inappropriate.
- **Multisensory future prediction (world modeling):** To encourage the model to integrate new sensory information into a unified, physically grounded latent space. This objective promotes a more generalized representation that extends beyond the finetuning data and aims to improve backward transfer.
- **Experience Replay:** To directly counteract catastrophic forgetting. By revisiting pretraining data during finetuning, the model can retain performance on original tasks while simultaneously acquiring new contact-rich behaviors using the added modality.

The paper instantiates MuSe through a case study focused on augmenting a pretrained vision-action model with force-torque (F/T) input for unseen manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Multi-Sensory World Model (MuSe) is a general framework for multisensory continual learning. The methodology is structured around integrating a new sensor modality, such as force-torque (F/T), into a pretrained policy.

#### 3.1. Problem Statement

The research considers a pretrained robot model initially trained on trajectories with `n` observation modalities (e.g., RGB images, proprioception) and robot actions. The goal is to adapt this model to incorporate an additional sensor modality, denoted `o_{n+1,t}` (e.g., F/T). During finetuning, the model has access to a smaller multisensory dataset (`D_new`) from new tasks, which includes the new modality, and to the original pretraining dataset (`D_pre`), which may not contain `o_{n+1}`. The objective is to learn a policy that can condition on `o_{n+1}` when available, predict its future values when supervision is provided, and importantly, preserve the capabilities learned from the original pretraining distribution. This distinguishes the approach from standard multimodal learning (where all modalities are present from the start) and standard continual learning (where the sensor suite is fixed).

#### 3.2. Multi-stage Fusion

MuSe employs a multi-stage fusion architecture to combine new modalities without degrading the pretrained representation. This involves both early and late fusion mechanisms:
- **Early Fusion:** A modality-specific encoder and projection layer are added for the newly introduced modality (`o_{n+1,t}`). The features from this new modality are mapped into the shared embedding space of the pretrained observation and action tokens. These resulting tokens are then concatenated with the existing tokens along the sequence dimension and processed jointly by the transformer backbone. This allows for deep cross-modal attention throughout the model's layers, enabling rich interaction between all modalities.
- **Late Fusion:** As a complementary pathway, features from the new modality are also injected into intermediate transformer layers. This is achieved through lightweight cross-attention adapters, which strengthen modality-specific conditioning throughout the backbone without requiring full re-computation of the early fused tokens.
This combined approach aims to leverage the benefits of both strategies: early fusion for global token-level interaction and late fusion for targeted, modality-specific conditioning.

#### 3.3. Multisensory Future Prediction

To encourage the model to integrate new sensory information into a unified, physically grounded latent space, MuSe is trained with a flexible multisensory future prediction objective. Beyond predicting future actions, the model is trained to predict future observations from both the original modalities (e.g., video frames) and the newly introduced modality (e.g., F/T). The overall objective function is defined as `L = λ_a L_act + λ_o L_obs + λ_n+1 L_new`, where `L_act` supervises action prediction, `L_obs` supervises prediction of original observation modalities, and `L_new` supervises prediction of the new modality. Each loss term is applied only when the corresponding target is available in the dataset, allowing the same objective to be used across datasets with varying modality coverage. This joint prediction task is intended to foster a more integrated representation that generalizes beyond the specific finetuning data and improves backward transfer.

#### 3.4. Experience Replay

To counteract catastrophic forgetting of skills learned during pretraining, MuSe incorporates experience replay during the finetuning phase. Training batches are sampled from a mixture of the new multisensory dataset (`D_new`) and the original pretraining dataset (`D_pre`).
- For samples from `D_new`, the model receives the expanded multisensory input (e.g., vision + proprioception + F/T) and is supervised with all relevant prediction losses (`L_act`, `L_obs`, `L_new`).
- For samples from `D_pre`, where the new modality is absent, the missing input is replaced with a learned modality-mask token. The `L_new` loss term is masked out, while supervision for actions (`L_act`) and original observation prediction (`L_obs`) is retained. This strategy enables the same multisensory prediction objective to be applied across both datasets using per-modality availability masks.

#### 3.5. Implementation (Case Study with Force-Torque Sensing)

The authors instantiate MuSe by augmenting the Unified Video-Action (UVA) model with force-torque (F/T) sensing. UVA is a model that jointly processes video and action sequences. For F/T integration:
- An F/T encoder (causal convolutional) processes F/T histories from both gripper fingers. The resulting tokens are prepended to the pretrained token sequence for early fusion.
- F/T features are also injected into intermediate transformer layers via cross-attention adapters for late fusion.
- An F/T diffusion head is added, mirroring the UVA action head design.
At each decision step, the policy conditions on past image frames, past actions, and synchronized F/T histories. It predicts future actions, future image frames, and future F/T samples. The predicted future wrench trajectory is used for adaptive compliance control: it sets a virtual target for a 6D task-space admittance controller, allowing the robot to adjust its stiffness based on anticipated contact.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MuSe focuses on three key areas: cross-modal generalization, forward transfer, and backward transfer, using real-world robot manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. Experiment Setup

The experiments utilized a UMI-FT setup equipped with CoinFT sensors on each gripper finger. The UVA backbone was pretrained on 1,271 vision-only episodes from 21 diverse tasks. Subsequently, this model was extended with F/T inputs and finetuned on 434 multisensory episodes from five contact-rich tasks, where F/T supervision was available. Crucially, F/T signals were recorded but withheld during the initial vision-only pretraining.

#### 4.2. Cross-Modal Generalization

This section evaluated the model's ability to predict F/T signals on pretraining tasks where F/T was recorded but never used for supervision, assessing the transferability of the learned contact representation.
- **Performance:** MuSe achieved the lowest F/T prediction error (L2 error of 8.424).
- **Ablations:** Weakening the fusion mechanism (e.g., late-only fusion, linear, or additive fusion) consistently increased F/T prediction error. Removing the video prediction auxiliary loss also increased error (9.90). The most significant degradation (12.376) occurred when experience replay was removed, indicating its importance for retaining F/T prediction capability across the broader pretraining distribution.
- **Conclusion:** These results suggest that both early and late fusion, multisensory future prediction (including video), and continuous exposure to the pretraining distribution via experience replay are critical for learning transferable F/T representations.

#### 4.3. Forward Transfer

This evaluation assessed MuSe's ability to acquire force-aware behaviors on new, multimodal contact-rich tasks from a small number of demonstrations. MuSe was compared against "No F/T" (pretrained, finetuned without F/T input/loss) and "No Pretrain" (multisensory architecture trained from scratch on finetuning data).
- **Contact-rich tasks (Vase Wiping, Peg Insertion):** MuSe demonstrated improved success rates on contact-rich tasks. On vase wiping, MuSe achieved 11.5/15 success compared to 5/15 for "No F/T" and 8/15 for "No Pretrain." For peg insertion, MuSe achieved 13/15 success compared to 9/15 and 6/15, respectively. Failures of "No F/T" were often related to contact regulation (e.g., insufficient/excessive force, inability to recover from jams).
- **Less contact-critical task (Pick and Place):** On pick and place tasks (where F/T is less central), MuSe and "No F/T" (both utilizing pretraining) achieved 7.5/10 success, outperforming "No Pretrain" (5/10).
- **Conclusion:** MuSe effectively leverages F/T to improve performance on contact-rich tasks, while pretraining contributes to generalization on task variations. F/T finetuning did not degrade performance on tasks where force feedback was not the primary bottleneck.

#### 4.4. Backward Transfer

This evaluated whether multimodal finetuning preserved original vision-only skills and transferred the newly learned F/T modality back to pretraining tasks. MuSe was compared against "No ER" (MuSe without experience replay) and "Pretraining Only" (the original vision-only UVA policy).
- **Results:** MuSe achieved higher success rates across all evaluated pretraining tasks: whiteboard wiping (12.5/15), diverse peg insertion (10/15), and diverse pick and place (6.5/10). "Pretraining Only" showed lower performance (8.5/15, 8/15, 5.5/10, respectively), indicating a lack of force-aware execution. "No ER" exhibited severe catastrophic forgetting, with significantly degraded performance (0.5/15 on whiteboard wiping, 2/15 on peg insertion).
- **Qualitative Observations:** "Pretraining Only" policies often attempted correct behaviors but failed in contact regulation. "No ER" policies frequently forgot original task structures or overfit to finetuning-specific strategies. MuSe, in contrast, could observe increasing F/T during jams and adapt its motion.
- **Conclusion:** Experience replay is crucial for preventing catastrophic forgetting and preserving visual skills. Furthermore, the integration of F/T input and output can improve, rather than merely preserve, performance on original pretraining tasks.

#### 4.5. Supplementary Ablations (Appendix)

- **Adaptive Compliance:** An ablation showed that MuSe's use of predicted F/T for adaptive compliance control significantly contributed to performance on contact-rich tasks, beyond just conditioning on F/T history. Disabling adaptive compliance reduced success rates (e.g., vase wiping from 11.5/15 to 8/15).
- **Diffusion Policy Baseline:** Comparison with a Diffusion Policy (DP) baseline, which omits future visual prediction and late fusion adapters, revealed that MuSe's multisensory future prediction (including future images) led to substantially lower F/T prediction error on pretraining tasks (8.42 vs 18.27 for DP). DP showed strong performance on finetuning tasks but degraded on contact-rich backward transfer. This suggests that future image prediction helps ground force prediction in visual dynamics, promoting stronger cross-modal generalization and backward transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic manipulation: the need for adaptable policies that can integrate new sensor modalities without requiring complete re-training or sacrificing previously acquired skills. The proposed Multi-Sensory World Model (MuSe) provides a framework that allows pretrained robot policies to efficiently incorporate new sensory information, specifically force-torque (F/T) sensing in this case study.

The findings have several significant implications:
- **Enhanced Robot Adaptability:** MuSe demonstrates that robot policies can be effectively expanded with new sensors *after* the initial pretraining phase. This reduces the practical barriers associated with requiring large-scale multisensory datasets from the outset, enabling more flexible and cost-efficient deployment of robot systems.
- **Improved Performance and Generalization:** The research shows that integrating F/T sensing, even from limited contact-rich data, can not only improve performance on new tasks requiring force feedback (forward transfer) but also *enhance* performance on the original vision-only tasks (backward transfer). This suggests that a more physically grounded understanding, facilitated by the new modality, can generalize to and benefit a broader range of manipulation behaviors.
- **Key Design Principles for Continual Learning:** The identification of multi-stage fusion, multisensory future prediction, and experience replay as critical components provides valuable architectural and training insights for future work in multisensory continual learning. These principles contribute to mitigating catastrophic forgetting and fostering robust cross-modal representation learning.
- **Foundation for Future Multi-Sensory Systems:** By demonstrating successful multi-sensory continual learning with F/T, this work establishes a foundation for exploring the integration of other modalities (e.g., tactile, audio, depth) or combinations thereof, paving the way for more sophisticated and perceptually rich robot capabilities.

The primary limitation noted by the authors is the current focus on a single sensor-expansion case (F/T) and a limited set of tabletop manipulation tasks. Future work would need to validate the generality of these principles across different modalities, more diverse robots, longer-horizon tasks, and interactions with deformable objects. Additionally, reducing the reliance on direct access to original pretraining data for experience replay represents another important avenue for enhancing practical deployability. Overall, this research contributes to advancing the field towards more adaptable, general-purpose robot policies capable of continuous learning and skill expansion in dynamic, real-world environments.
