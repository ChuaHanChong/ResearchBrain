# Whatever Remains Must Be True: Filtering Drives Reasoning in LLMs, Shaping Diversity

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from NAVER Labs Europe and an independent researcher introduce Distributional Matching with Verifiable Rewards (DMVR) and a novel α-DPG method to fine-tune Large Language Models. This framework enables explicit management of the trade-off between solution precision (pass@1) and output diversity (pass@k) in reasoning tasks, achieving superior coverage (e.g., best pass@256 scores with α=0.25α=0.25) while maintaining competitive precision on formal theorem proving benchmarks.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) fine-tuned for reasoning tasks using Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) often suffer from reduced output diversity, a phenomenon known as 'mode collapse'.
- RLVR's implicit objective, which is tied to minimizing Reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence, is 'mode-seeking', causing models to concentrate on a narrow set of high-probability solutions and potentially neglect other valid options.
- In formal theorem proving, this lack of diversity hinders the ability to solve complex theorems that may require exploring infrequent or less obvious proof strategies.

## Method

- The **DMVR** framework explicitly defines a desirable ==target distribution== that includes only correct answers (as verified by an external function) while preserving the relative probabilities of these correct answers from the base, untuned model.
- The paper proposes α-DPG, a novel approach utilizing Distributional Policy Gradient algorithms with α-divergences, to approximate this target distribution and provide continuous control over the trade-off between diversity (Forward KL, α→0α \to 0α→0) and precision (Reverse KL, α→1α \to 1α→1).
- The partition function for the target distribution is estimated online by averaging verifier outcomes of sampled responses, avoiding additional computational overhead compared to baselines.

## Results

- α-DPG models successfully spanned a significant portion of the Pareto frontier for pass@1 (precision) versus pass@256 (coverage) on the Lean theorem-proving benchmark, demonstrating controllable balance.
- With α=**0.25**α=0.25α=**0.25**, α-DPG achieved the highest coverage among all tested methods (superior pass@256 scores for k≥4k ≥ 4k≥4) while also improving precision over the base SFT model.
- High-alpha\\alphaalpha α-DPG (e.g., α=**0.999**α=0.999α=**0.999**) and traditional RL methods like GRPO exhibited a 'polarizing effect' on problem difficulty, improving many medium problems but rendering some hard problems unsolvable, whereas low-alpha\\alphaalpha α-DPG better maintained the solvability of harder problems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The observed diversity loss in RLVR is a consequence of its implicit objective being equivalent to minimizing the 'mode-seeking' Reverse KL divergence to a target distribution that favors correct answers.
> - Fine-tuning LLMs with verifiable rewards primarily reweights and amplifies existing solution pathways present in the base model rather than discovering fundamentally novel ones, as suggested by perplexity analysis.
> - The α-divergence provides a principled and unified framework, allowing continuous interpolation between 'mass-covering' (diversity-promoting) and 'mode-seeking' (precision-emphasizing) objectives, thereby enabling explicit control over the precision-diversity trade-off.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in the fine-tuning of Large Language Models (LLMs) for reasoning tasks, particularly those involving verifiable rewards. Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has become a standard approach for enhancing LLM performance on such tasks. However, a growing body of evidence indicates that models trained using RLVR methods often exhibit a significant reduction in output diversity, sometimes referred to as "mode collapse". This phenomenon was previously observed in the context of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).

The paper posits that this loss of diversity in RL-based training stems from the implicit objective of optimizing the Reverse Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence to a target distribution that favors correct answers. Reverse KL divergence is characterized as "mode-seeking" or "zero-forcing," meaning it tends to concentrate probability mass on a narrow set of high-probability solutions within the target distribution while potentially neglecting other valid solutions. This perspective builds on prior work by Korbak et al. that established a connection between RLVR objectives and Reverse KL minimization.

In contrast, Forward KL divergence is "mass-covering," encouraging the model to cover all modes of the target distribution, which typically enhances diversity but may reduce precision. The broader research landscape includes approaches such as Distributional Matching (DM), which aims to align LLMs with desired distributions by minimizing various f-divergences. This paper fits into this framework by introducing $\alpha$-divergences, a family of f-divergences that allows for continuous interpolation between Forward and Reverse KL, thereby offering a controllable trade-off between diversity and precision.

The specific application domain for this research is formal theorem proving, utilizing proof assistants like Lean. In this context, diversity of proof attempts is critical because difficult theorems may only be solvable through infrequent or less obvious derivations. Prior RL-based approaches in Lean (e.g., GRPO) have also been observed to suffer from diversity reduction. This work contributes to the advancement of formal reasoning methods for LLMs by attempting to improve both the precision of individual solutions and the diversity of solution candidates.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for fine-tuning Large Language Models that addresses the observed trade-off between solution precision and output diversity in reasoning tasks, particularly when using verifiable rewards. The authors aim to enable controlled management of this trade-off, leading to models that achieve both high precision (pass@1) and high coverage (pass@k for large k).

This overarching objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Diagnosing Diversity Loss in RLVR:** The authors are motivated to clarify why current RL-based training methods, such as RLVR, lead to reduced output diversity. They argue that this issue is inherent to the implicit objective of RL, which often optimizes the Reverse KL divergence. Reverse KL is "mode-seeking," meaning it prioritizes placing high probability on a small set of the most rewarding solutions, thereby neglecting other valid but less probable solutions in the target distribution. This diagnostic aims to reframe the understanding of RL's impact on LLM capabilities, suggesting it reweights existing behaviors rather than creating fundamentally new ones.

2. **Explicit Definition of a Desirable Target Distribution:** To counter the implicit, mode-seeking nature of RLVR, the research is motivated to explicitly define an "ideal" target distribution. This target distribution is conceived as one that only includes correct answers (as verified by an external function) while preserving the relative probabilities of these correct answers from the base, untuned model. This approach is rooted in the principle of ensuring correctness while simultaneously retaining the inherent diversity of solutions present in the original model.

3. **Controllable Interpolation between Precision and Diversity:** Given the identified tension between mode-seeking (precision-oriented) and mass-covering (diversity-oriented) divergences, a key motivation is to introduce a mechanism that allows for a smooth, explicit control over this trade-off. The authors propose leveraging $\alpha$-divergences for this purpose. This family of divergences unifies existing methods (Reverse KL for RLVR, Forward KL for KL-DPG/RS-FT) and provides a continuous parameter ($\alpha$) to tune the balance between concentrating on highly probable correct solutions and exploring a broader range of correct solutions.

4. **Application to Formal Theorem Proving:** The research is specifically motivated by the challenges in formal theorem proving with LLMs. In this domain, success often requires exploring a diverse set of proof strategies, as some complex theorems may only be solvable through rare or unconventional derivations. Therefore, a method that can enhance both the precision of generated proofs and the diversity of proof attempts is highly valuable for advancing automated theorem proving.

In summary, the work aims to offer a principled framework, Distributional Matching with Verifiable Rewards (DMVR), that provides a clearer understanding of RL's effects on LLM outputs and offers a novel, controllable method for fine-tuning LLMs to achieve a desired balance of precision and diversity in reasoning tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on addressing the limitations of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) by introducing Distributional Matching with Verifiable Rewards (DMVR).

**Critique of RLVR's Implicit Objective:**
The paper begins by framing RLVR as optimizing a pseudo-reward function $R_\theta(y,x) = v(y,x) - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y|x)}{\pi_{base}(y|x)}$, where $v(y,x)$ is a binary verifier and $\beta$ is a KL penalty coefficient. Drawing on previous work, the authors formally demonstrate (Lemma 1) that maximizing this objective is equivalent to minimizing the Reverse KL divergence $D_{KL}(\pi_\theta || p_{x,\beta})$ to a smoothed target distribution $p_{x,\beta}(y) \propto \pi_{base}(y|x) \exp(v(y,x)/\beta)$. They further show (Lemma 2) that as $\beta \to 0^+$, this smoothed target $p_{x,\beta}$ converges to an ideal target $p_x(y) \propto \pi_{base}(y|x)v(y,x)$. The core argument is that Reverse KL is "mode-seeking," leading to policies that concentrate mass on a few high-reward regions, thereby reducing diversity.

**Proposed Target Distribution (DMVR Framework):**
The DMVR framework explicitly defines the desired target distribution $p_x(y) \propto \pi_{base}(y|x)v(y,x)$. This distribution is characterized by two properties: it filters out all incorrect responses (since $v(y,x)=0$ implies $p_x(y)=0$) and it maintains the relative probabilities of correct responses from the base model, making it the closest distribution to $\pi_{base}$ (in terms of $D_{KL}(\cdot||\pi_{base})$) that satisfies the correctness constraint. This ensures correctness while preserving the diversity of solutions inherent in the base model.

**Approximation Strategy: $\alpha$-DPG (Distributional Policy Gradient with $\alpha$-divergences):**
To approximate this target distribution $p_x$ with an autoregressive policy $\pi_\theta$, the authors utilize Distributional Policy Gradient (DPG) algorithms, specifically $f$-DPG. This generalizes prior KL-DPG methods by minimizing an $f$-divergence $D_f(\pi_\theta, p_x)$.
The key innovation is the use of the $\alpha$-divergence family within $f$-DPG, leading to "$\alpha$-DPG." The $\alpha$-divergence smoothly interpolates between:
- **Forward KL ($\alpha \to 0$):** This is "mass-covering," encouraging the model to assign sufficient probability to all regions where the target distribution has support, thus promoting diversity. This setting recovers the original KL-DPG algorithm and, if samples are drawn from $\pi_{base}$, Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (RS-FT).
- **Reverse KL ($\alpha \to 1$):** This is "mode-seeking," penalizing placing probability where the target assigns little, thereby emphasizing precision. For $\alpha$ close to 1, $\alpha$-DPG recovers the REINFORCE learning rule, linking it to RLVR.

The $\alpha$-DPG pseudo-reward is given by $R_\theta(y,x) = \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \left( \left(\frac{p_x(y)}{\pi_\theta(y|x)}\right)^{1-\alpha} - 1 \right)$. To enhance stability, particularly for low $\alpha$ values, the parenthetical factor is clipped to a maximum value $M=10$. The gradient is computed using the $f$-DPG formula (Eq. 7), and a leave-one-out per-context average of pseudo-rewards is used as a baseline for variance reduction.

The partition function $Z_x = \sum_{y \in Y} \pi_{base}(y|x)v(y,x)$ for the target distribution $p_x$ is estimated online by averaging verifier outcomes of sampled responses for each problem, thus avoiding additional computational cost compared to baselines.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Task:** Formal mathematical theorem proving in Lean. This task requires both correctness and diversity of proof strategies.
- **Base Model:** DeepSeek-Prover-V1.5-SFT, a 7B parameter LLM pre-trained on mathematical and formal language data.
- **Dataset:** 10,000 solvable Lean problems from the Lean Workbook dataset for training, and 200 unseen problems for testing.
- **Reward Function & Verifier:** A binary reward (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect) is assigned based on automatic verification by the Lean proof assistant (Lean4:v4.9.0, Mathlib4).
- **Baselines:** A range of critic-free RL methods are compared, including GRPO (unbiased instantiation Dr. GRPO), GRPO variants with diversity-preserving regularization (High-KL, Rw-Ulkly, Pass@k training), GPG, ReMax, RLOO, and the Base SFT model.
- **Training Details:** All models were trained on a single node with 4x A100 GPUs and 28 CPUs for parallel proof assessment. The reward function operated asynchronously. The veRL framework was used. Advantage normalization was disabled, and KL divergence penalty $\beta$ was set to 0 for RL-based baselines. $\alpha$-DPG training used float16 and enforced a minimum partition function value of $Z_x \ge 1e^{-4}$. Responses were limited to 1024 tokens, with 512 generated sequences per step over 200 iterations.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** pass@k is used, specifically pass@1 (precision) and pass@256 (coverage). Responses were generated using temperature $T=1$ and nucleus sampling ($p=0.99$).
- **Analysis:** Beyond pass@k, analyses included:
 - **Problem Difficulty Transition Matrices:** Categorizing problems into easy (≥80% correct samples), medium (20-80% correct), and hard (<20% correct) to observe how training changes solvability.
 - **Diversity Analysis:** Measuring Shannon and Gini-Simpson indices for tactic and premise choices within 256 generated proof sequences per problem.
 - **Perplexity Analysis:** Computing self-perplexity and perplexity under the base model for generated solutions to assess novelty and deviation from the base distribution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation on the Lean theorem-proving benchmark yielded several findings regarding the trade-off between precision and coverage, the effects on problem difficulty, and the relationship with output diversity.

**Coverage vs. Precision Analysis:**
- The models' performance was mapped onto a Pareto frontier, illustrating the trade-off between pass@1 (precision) and pass@256 (coverage).
- The base SFT model demonstrated broad coverage but low precision, indicating its ability to address diverse problems, albeit with a low single-sample success rate.
- Traditional RL-based methods like GRPO, RLOO, and GPG generally achieved high precision at the cost of reduced coverage.
- GRPO variants with regularization (e.g., GRPO with High-KL penalty, GRPO-Rw-Ulkly, Pass@k training) improved precision while better preserving coverage compared to unregularized GRPO.
- $\alpha$-DPG models, by varying the $\alpha$ parameter, demonstrated the ability to span a significant portion of this Pareto frontier.
 - Lower $\alpha$ values (e.g., $\alpha=0.25$) achieved the highest coverage among all tested methods, while still improving precision over the base SFT model.
 - Higher $\alpha$ values (e.g., $\alpha \ge 0.995$) yielded precision comparable to or exceeding RL-based methods, typically with superior coverage.
- Overall, $\alpha$-DPG models were found to lie on or near the Pareto frontier, indicating an efficient and controllable balance between precision and coverage.

**Pass@k Curves:**
- Analysis of pass@k curves over 256 samples showed that no single model universally dominated across all $k$ values, reinforcing the presence of a Pareto trade-off.
- $\alpha$-DPG ($\alpha=0.999$) generally outperformed GRPO and other pure RL-based techniques, with ReMax being a notable exception that showed better coverage despite slightly lower precision.
- $\alpha$-DPG ($\alpha=0.25$) demonstrated superior performance over the base model and other diversity-preserving baselines (Pass@k training, GRPO with KL regularization) for $k \ge 4$, achieving the best pass@256 scores.
- Models with $\alpha \ge 0.75$ tended to be surpassed by the base model for larger $k$ values, suggesting their diversity benefits were less pronounced in these settings.

**Problem Difficulty Analysis:**
- Problems were categorized into easy, medium, and hard based on solution success rate.
- GRPO and high-$\alpha$ $\alpha$-DPG ($\alpha=0.999$) exhibited a "polarizing effect": they significantly improved the solvability of many medium-difficulty problems (making them easy) but also led to several hard problems becoming unsolvable.
- In contrast, low-$\alpha$ $\alpha$-DPG ($\alpha=0.25$) and GRPO (High-KL) were more conservative. They improved the sample efficiency on fewer problems but were more effective at maintaining the solvability of harder problems, with fewer problems transitioning to "unsolved."

**Diversity Analysis:**
- Diversity metrics, specifically Shannon and Gini-Simpson indices, were computed for tactic and premise choices in generated proofs.
- A correlation was observed: higher diversity in tactics and premises generally correlated with improved pass@256 performance (coverage).
- Conversely, higher diversity was found to be anticorrelated with pass@1 performance (precision), indicating the inherent trade-off.

**Perplexity Analysis:**
- Perplexity was computed for generated solutions under both the generating model and the base SFT model.
- The analysis indicated that sequences generated by all models, including those trained with RL, remained highly probable under the base model. This suggests that the fine-tuning process primarily reweights existing solution pathways rather than discovering fundamentally novel ones.
- In one illustrative example, GRPO generated 16 identical sequences, which were also highly probable under the base model, demonstrating a collapse in diversity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers several points of significance and potential impact across the fields of Large Language Models, Reinforcement Learning, and formal reasoning.

**Conceptual Clarification and Diagnostic Insight:**
The paper introduces the Distributional Matching with Verifiable Rewards (DMVR) framework, which explicitly defines a verifier-based target distribution. This framework provides a new perspective on Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), identifying its implicit objective as minimizing the Reverse KL divergence. This clarifies that RLVR's mode-seeking nature is the root cause of observed diversity loss. The work challenges the notion that RL solely creates novel capabilities, instead suggesting that RLVR primarily reweights and amplifies behaviors already present in the base model. This diagnostic insight is significant for understanding the limitations of current LLM fine-tuning paradigms and for guiding future research toward more principled approaches.

**Methodological Advancement for Precision-Diversity Trade-off:**
By proposing $\alpha$-DPG (Distributional Policy Gradient with $\alpha$-divergences), the paper introduces a method that unifies several existing approaches, including RLVR (Reverse KL), KL-DPG, and Rejection Sampling Fine-Tuning (Forward KL). This unification is valuable as it provides a single framework that allows for continuous and explicit control over the precision-diversity trade-off through the $\alpha$ parameter. This methodological advancement offers a more flexible and principled way to optimize LLMs for tasks where both the correctness of individual outputs and the breadth of generated solutions are important.

**Empirical Validation in Formal Reasoning:**
The application and empirical validation on the Lean theorem-proving benchmark are significant. Achieving state-of-the-art performance along the coverage-precision Pareto frontier, especially in terms of coverage, demonstrates the practical utility of $\alpha$-DPG. In formal reasoning tasks, where exploring diverse proof paths is often crucial for solving complex or rare theorems, the ability to control and enhance diversity without drastically sacrificing precision represents a notable step forward for automated theorem proving.

**Implications for LLM Tuning and Research:**
The findings have broader implications for the development and application of LLMs:
- **Beyond Mode Collapse:** The work provides a concrete strategy to mitigate "mode collapse," a known issue in RL-tuned LLMs, offering a pathway to building more robust and versatile models.
- **Controllable Behavior:** The explicit control over precision and diversity provides practitioners with a tool to tailor LLMs to specific task requirements, for instance, prioritizing exploration in discovery tasks or convergence in critical applications.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The DMVR framework and $\alpha$-DPG open avenues for future research, such as investigating curriculum learning strategies for $\alpha$ to better understand the interplay between training dynamics and optimal solutions, and generalizing the approach to other complex reasoning tasks beyond formal theorem proving (e.g., code generation, scientific problem-solving).

**Ethical Considerations and Transparency:**
The paper explicitly highlights the ethical implications of the chosen target distribution. By making the target distribution explicit, the framework promotes transparency and accountability in LLM alignment. While the trade-off between strict adherence to a verifier's feedback and diversity carries low risk in theorem proving, the authors note that in broader "alignment" contexts (e.g., aligning models with specific policies), such trade-offs require careful consideration. This emphasis on transparency in defining model goals is a significant contribution to responsible AI development.
