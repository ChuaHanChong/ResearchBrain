# DIGIT: A Novel Design for a Low-Cost Compact High-Resolution Tactile Sensor with Application to In-Hand Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> DIGIT is a low-cost, compact vision-based tactile sensor: a camera images deformations of a replaceable reflective elastomer, with custom illumination electronics and a tool-less press-fit housing costing about 15 USD per unit at 1000-piece scale. Mounted on all fingers of an Allegro hand, it supports learned keypoint dynamics plus model-predictive control that rolls a glass marble to target in-hand positions.

## Problem

- Prior vision-based tactile sensors like GelSight and GelSlim are too bulky to mount on multi-fingered hands, blocking high-resolution touch sensing during in-hand manipulation.
- Existing elastomer gels wear out fast under abrasion, and largely manual manufacturing yields high sensor-to-sensor variability, forcing recalibration or retraining after replacement.
- Prior tactile model-predictive control was shown on a single sensor with 3-DOF commands; image-space video prediction is too slow for multi-finger, high-DOF planning.

## Method

- **DIGIT** hardware: three-piece ==press-fit plastic housing==, custom PCB with an OVM7692 60 fps camera and three manually dimmable RGB LEDs, swappable elastomers (reflective, marked, transparent).
- Three-stage gel process: airbrushed silicone white pigment cured with a chemical kicker as ==image-transfer layer==, Solaris base layer, then Sil-Poxy bonding to an acrylic window.
- **Struct-NN** control stack: ==keypoint autoencoder== (tiny ==ResNet-18==, Gaussian-blob decoding) compresses two tactile images into a 14-D state, an ==MLP dynamics model== drives ==CEM-based model-predictive control== in that state space.

## Results

- Abrasion testing: DIGIT gel degrades **0.3 percent** transmittance after **15 passes**, versus **805 percent** for the Yuan et al. gel and **918 percent** for the GelSight Inc. gel.
- Struct-NN needs **1.4 s per MPC step** versus **69 s** for CDNA with **1.2 M** versus **4 M** parameters, despite slightly higher per-pixel RMSE on BAIR pushing and marble videos.
- On real marble manipulation over **50 trials**, learned-dynamics MPC steadily reduces goal distance while a hand-tuned P controller diverges; about **25 percent** of trials still drop the marble.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Planning in a compact learned keypoint state, not in pixel space, is what makes model-predictive control tractable when sensor count and action dimensionality both grow.
> - Sensor durability is a design variable, not a fixed cost: a more translucent but abrasion-resistant transfer layer preserved tactile performance while extending usable lifetime enormously.
> - Hand-tuned linear controllers fail on deformable curved contact surfaces because contact dynamics are nonlinear in joint commands, so fixed gains are only locally optimal.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has long sought to achieve human-level dexterity in manipulation tasks, but this goal remains largely unfulfilled. A primary bottleneck identified by the authors is the difficulty in precisely sensing contact forces. While proprioception and vision provide useful information, touch sensing offers a direct, noiseless, and crucial modality for understanding and controlling interactions with objects and the environment.

Historically, various types of tactile sensors have been developed, employing different technologies such as piezoresistive, capacitive, and optical methods. However, widespread adoption of high-resolution tactile sensing in general-purpose robotic manipulation has been hampered by a lack of sensors that simultaneously meet several critical criteria: high resolution, high sensitivity, reliability, ease of use, compactness, and low cost.

This paper positions DIGIT within the specific and increasingly popular sub-field of **vision-based tactile sensors**. These sensors, including predecessors like TacTip, FingerVision, and notably GelSight, operate by imaging the deformation of a soft elastomer surface upon contact. They excel in providing high spatial resolution and sensitivity, often at a lower manufacturing cost compared to other types. However, existing vision-based sensors have had significant drawbacks:
- **Bulkiness:** Their size often limits their integration into multi-fingered robotic hands, especially for in-hand manipulation tasks.
- **Durability:** The soft elastomer surfaces are prone to rapid wear and tear.
- **Manufacturability & Consistency:** Production often involves complex, largely manual processes, leading to high variability between sensors and difficulties in replacement or scaling.

**DIGIT directly addresses these limitations.** It aims to improve upon the state-of-the-art vision-based sensors by miniaturizing the form factor to enable mounting on multi-fingered hands, enhancing the durability of the contact surface, simplifying and streamlining the manufacturing process for repeatability and cost-effectiveness, and offering modularity for easy component replacement. By doing so, DIGIT seeks to bridge the gap between high-resolution tactile sensing capabilities and the practical requirements for widespread deployment in complex robotic manipulation scenarios.

Furthermore, the paper highlights the growing importance of **deep learning and model-based control** in integrating high-dimensional tactile information. Previous work often struggled to extract meaningful features from such data for control algorithms. This research builds upon recent advancements, particularly `tactile-MPC` (Model Predictive Control using tactile feedback), by extending it to a more complex multi-finger setting. This signifies a move towards data-driven, learning-based approaches that can leverage the rich information provided by high-resolution sensors like DIGIT for intricate manipulation tasks previously unattainable. The open-sourcing of the design is a significant contribution to the community, aiming to accelerate research in an area where hardware accessibility has been a major barrier.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is two-fold:

1. **To design and introduce DIGIT, a novel, low-cost, compact, and high-resolution tactile sensor.** The motivation behind this design is to overcome the aforementioned practical limitations of existing tactile sensors, particularly vision-based ones, that have hindered their widespread adoption in robotic manipulation. Specifically, the authors aimed for a sensor that is:
 - **Compact:** Small enough to be mounted on multiple fingers of a robotic hand, enabling true in-hand manipulation.
 - **Cost-effective:** Inexpensive to produce, facilitating widespread availability and use.
 - **High-resolution & Sensitive:** Capable of providing rich, detailed tactile measurements, characteristic of vision-based sensors.
 - **Reliable & Robust:** Possessing enhanced mechanical durability, especially for its elastomer, to ensure a longer lifespan.
 - **Easy to Manufacture & Use:** Featuring a streamlined, repeatable manufacturing process and a modular design for simple assembly, repair, and "plug-and-play" functionality.

2. **To demonstrate the capabilities of the DIGIT sensor by applying it to a challenging in-hand manipulation task using deep neural network model-based controllers.** This objective serves to validate DIGIT's practical utility and showcase how its improved design enables more complex robotic dexterity. The specific task chosen – manipulating glass marbles in-hand with a multi-finger robotic hand – highlights the need for precise contact force control and the ability to handle delicate object interactions.

Ultimately, the overarching motivation is to **stimulate future research in tactile sensing and manipulation within the robotics community** by providing an affordable, robust, and user-friendly tactile sensing platform. By open-sourcing the design, the authors seek to remove the complexities typically associated with tactile sensor hardware, thereby lowering the barrier to entry for other researchers and accelerating progress towards human-level manipulation skills in robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research methodology encompasses both the hardware design and fabrication of DIGIT, and the development and application of a learning-based control strategy for an in-hand manipulation task.

#### 3.1. DIGIT Sensor Design and Manufacturing

- **Mechanical Design:** DIGIT features a compact (20x27x18mm, 20g) multi-body, three-piece plastic enclosure. This modular design, emphasizing "press-fit" connections and a single screw for elastomer replacement, facilitates easy assembly, component swapping, and allows for rapid prototyping via 3D printing and potential large-scale production via injection molding. It is designed to accommodate various types of elastomers (reflective, with markers, transparent) for different applications.
- **Electronic Design:** Instead of relying on off-the-shelf camera solutions, the authors custom-designed the electronics. A compact (7 cm²) PCB integrates an Omnivision OVM7692 60fps color CMOS camera, three RGB LEDs for illumination (with manual intensity control), and a SuperSpeed USB 3.0 hub to allow multiple DIGITs to connect to a single host USB port.
- **Elastomer Design:** A novel three-stage manufacturing process for the elastomer gel was developed to enhance durability and consistency. It involves airbrushing a silicone-based white pigment onto a mold to form a controlled-thickness image transfer layer, followed by applying a base layer of Smooth-On Solaris silicone (a durable silicone typically used for coating photo-voltaic cells). This silicone is then glued to an acrylic window using an optically clear adhesive, forming a robust, interchangeable acrylic-gel unit.
- **Mechanical Robustness Testing:** The DIGIT elastomer's durability was rigorously tested against gels from existing GelSight designs and a commercial GelSight Inc. product. An industry-standard linear abrasion device was used, performing cycles of linear sweeps with a calibrated weight and abrasive tip. Degradation was quantified by measuring the percentage increase in light transmittance through the gel, indicating wear of the opaque image transfer layer.

#### 3.2. Learning In-Hand Manipulation

- **Task Setup:** An Allegro robotic hand, equipped with DIGIT sensors on its thumb and middle finger, was mounted on a Sawyer robotic arm. The task involved precisely picking up a glass marble from a stand and manipulating its position using a pincer grip between the two DIGIT-equipped fingers.
- **Self-supervised Data Collection:** To train the dynamics model, 4800 trials of autonomous data collection were performed. In each trial, after picking up the marble, the robot randomly moved its fingers for approximately 10 seconds (issuing 20 angular displacement commands to 8 joint servos). The system automatically reset by dropping the marble into a bowl. Videos from both DIGITs, joint angular positions, and action commands were recorded.
- **Tactile Predictive Model:** The core of the learning approach involved a two-stage process:
 1. **Keypoint Autoencoder:** A self-supervised autoencoder with a "structural bottleneck" was trained to extract a compact, low-dimensional (14-D) state representation from the high-resolution DIGIT images. This representation, `s = [k_l, k_r, j]`, consists of keypoints (2D position `(x,y)` and intensity `i` representing depth) from the left (`k_l`) and right (`k_r`) DIGITs, and the 8 joint angles (`j`) of the fingers. A tiny ResNet-18 backbone was used, trained with L2 image reconstruction error and auxiliary losses to encourage sparse, non-redundant keypoints. The authors found that a single active keypoint reliably represented the marble's position and depth.
 2. **Neural Network Dynamics Model:** A simple Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) was trained on this compact state representation to predict the next state `s'` given the current state `s` and action `a` (`s' = f(s, a)`). The training data was augmented with zero-action tuples and perturbations to RGB values/gamma correction to enhance robustness.
- **Model-based Control (MPC with CEM):** For real-time control, Model Predictive Control (MPC) with the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM) was employed. To address the computational complexity of high-dimensional tactile data in a multi-finger setup, a critical optimization was made: planning was performed directly in the compact 14-D state space. The computationally expensive keypoint encoder was called only once per MPC step (to map initial observations), and the lightweight MLP dynamics model was recursively applied for future predictions. The cost function for planning was the sum of Euclidean distances in the `(x, y, i)` keypoint coordinates between predicted and target marble positions, penalizing both positional errors and excessive force/dropping.
- **Baseline Comparison:** The learned MPC approach was compared against a hand-tuned linear Proportional (P) controller operating in the keypoint space to assess the necessity and performance advantages of learning complex, non-linear dynamics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded significant findings regarding both the DIGIT sensor itself and its application to in-hand manipulation.

#### 4.1. DIGIT Sensor Performance

- **Cost-Effectiveness:** DIGIT achieves a remarkable estimated manufacturing cost of approximately **$15 per sensor** when produced in batches of 1000, making it substantially more affordable than comparable vision-based sensors like GelSight.
- **Compactness:** With dimensions of 20x27x18mm and a weight of 20g, DIGIT is significantly more compact than previous GelSight (35x60x35mm) and GelSlim (50x20x50mm) sensors, enabling its mounting on multi-fingered robotic hands (e.g., Allegro hand).
- **High Resolution and Sensitivity:** Despite its compactness, DIGIT retains the ability to capture high-resolution tactile images, clearly resolving sub-millimeter structures and textures of contacted objects (demonstrated in Fig. 3). The image resolution (640x480) and frame rate (60 FPS) are competitive or superior to prior work.
- **Modularity and Manufacturability:** The multi-body, press-fit design facilitates easy and repeatable assembly, and allows for rapid replacement of components like the elastomer or housing. This addresses a major pain point of previous designs.
- **Enhanced Robustness:** The novel elastomer manufacturing process significantly improves durability. In abrasion tests, DIGIT's gel showed minimal degradation (0.3% increase in light transmittance after 15 abrasion passes), whereas previous GelSight gels exhibited massive degradation (764-918% increase), becoming visibly damaged and unusable. This indicates a greatly extended lifespan and reliability for DIGIT.

#### 4.2. In-Hand Marble Manipulation Results

- **Video Predictive Model (Struct-NN):** The custom-designed Struct-NN model, which operates on the compact 14-D keypoint representation, proved to be highly efficient.
 - **Speed:** It achieved a crucial **1.4 seconds per MPC step**, making real-time control feasible for the multi-finger task. This is a dramatic improvement over alternative visual predictive models like CDNA, which would take 69 seconds per MPC step, rendering them impractical for this application.
 - **Compactness:** Struct-NN also has a smaller model size (1.2M parameters) compared to CDNA (4M parameters).
 - **Accuracy:** While Struct-NN showed slightly higher RMSE on image reconstruction compared to CDNA on certain benchmarks, it produced qualitatively good predictions and was sufficiently accurate for the control task.
- **Manipulation Performance:**
 - **Successful Manipulation:** The MPC controller, leveraging the learned dynamics from DIGIT's tactile inputs, successfully manipulated glass marbles to desired target positions. The Euclidean distance error to the goal position consistently decreased over time, demonstrating effective control (Fig. 8 top).
 - **Superiority over Baseline:** The learned dynamics model significantly outperformed a hand-tuned linear Proportional (P) controller. The P controller's performance diverged, highlighting the necessity of learning the complex, non-linear dynamics involved in fine manipulation with deformable surfaces.
 - **Challenges:** Despite the successes, approximately 25% of trials resulted in the marble being dropped before reaching the goal (Fig. 8 bottom). The authors attribute this to planning inaccuracies and actuation noise in the robotic hand, suggesting areas for future improvement such as better low-level controllers or more training data.

These results collectively validate that DIGIT provides the high-resolution, robust, and cost-effective tactile sensing necessary for enabling complex, fine-grained multi-fingered in-hand manipulation. Furthermore, the demonstrated efficacy of the Struct-NN and MPC approach proves a viable pathway to scale tactile control to such challenging tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DIGIT sensor and the accompanying research hold substantial significance and promise for a wide array of robotic applications.

**1. Accelerating Tactile Sensing Research and Adoption:** DIGIT directly tackles the long-standing practical barriers to widespread adoption of high-resolution tactile sensors: size, cost, reliability, and ease of manufacturing. By offering a compact, inexpensive, and robust solution, DIGIT can make advanced tactile sensing accessible to a much broader research community and potentially to real-world robotic systems. The open-sourcing of its design is a particularly impactful contribution, effectively democratizing access to state-of-the-art tactile hardware and enabling rapid iteration and innovation by researchers globally, without the prohibitive costs and development time typically associated with custom sensor fabrication.

**2. Enabling Advanced Robotic Dexterity:** The ability to mount DIGITs on multi-fingered hands opens up entirely new possibilities for in-hand manipulation research. Previously, high-resolution tactile feedback was often restricted to single-finger setups due to sensor bulk. DIGIT allows robots to perceive and control delicate interactions across multiple contact points simultaneously, a crucial capability for achieving human-level dexterity in tasks like reorienting objects, assembling small components, or handling fragile items. The successful demonstration of marble manipulation is a testament to this, showcasing the sensor's capacity for fine motor control and complex contact dynamics.

**3. Advancing Learning-Based Control for High-Dimensional Sensing:** The research demonstrates a practical and efficient approach to integrating high-dimensional tactile data into learning-based control algorithms. The use of a compact keypoint representation combined with a lightweight dynamics model (Struct-NN) significantly reduces computational overhead, making real-time Model Predictive Control feasible even for multi-finger systems. This methodology can serve as a template for future work in robot learning, particularly for tasks requiring precise, adaptive control based on rich sensory feedback.

**4. Bridging the Gap to Real-World Applications:** The low cost and enhanced robustness of DIGIT make it a more viable candidate for deployment in practical, non-laboratory settings. Robots equipped with such sensors could perform a wider range of tasks in manufacturing, logistics, healthcare, or service industries where delicate handling and precise interaction with objects are paramount. This moves tactile sensing from an academic curiosity to a practical tool for industrial and commercial robotics.

**5. Foundation for Future Innovations:** The modular design of DIGIT allows for easy modification and integration of different elastomer types or other components, fostering further research into task-specific tactile properties. The paper also points to future work on further miniaturization and omni-directional sensing fields, suggesting that DIGIT is not just a sensor but a foundational platform for continuous innovation in tactile robotics.

In conclusion, "DIGIT" represents a significant step forward in the design and application of tactile sensors. By addressing key practical limitations and making its design open-source, this work has the potential to profoundly impact the trajectory of research in robotic manipulation, bringing robots closer to achieving the delicate and dexterous capabilities seen in humans.
