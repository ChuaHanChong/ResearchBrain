# The Importance of Being a Band: Finite-Sample Exact Distribution-Free Prediction Sets for Functional Data

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed a nonparametric method using Conformal Prediction to construct finite-sample valid prediction bands for functional data, without relying on strong distributional assumptions or dimension reduction. Their approach introduces a data-driven modulation function, ¯s_I₁(t), which robustly adapts the prediction band's width to local data variability and outlier presence, resulting in more efficient and interpretable prediction sets.

## Problem

- Quantifying uncertainty for functional data often relies on computationally intensive parametric bootstrapping or dimensionality reduction, introducing approximations or high computational cost.
- Existing prediction set methods for functional data frequently depend on strong, untestable distributional assumptions or asymptotic arguments, limiting their finite-sample validity.
- Generating easily interpretable prediction sets for functional data, specifically in the form of intuitive "bands" that provide simultaneous coverage across the entire domain, has been a challenge.

## Method

- A nonparametric approach is developed using the ==Split Conformal Prediction== framework, which provides finite-sample coverage guarantees by dividing functional data into training and calibration sets.
- A ==nonconformity measure== based on the ==L∞ (supremum) metric== is introduced, directly yielding prediction sets in the form of closed-form, simultaneous prediction bands.
- A data-driven ==modulation function ¯s_I₁(t)== is proposed to adapt the band's width to local data variability and robustly handle outliers, enhancing prediction set efficiency without sacrificing validity.

## Results

- Proposed Conformal Prediction methods consistently achieved empirical conditional coverage within **99% confidence intervals** of the nominal **90%** across almost all simulation scenarios and sample sizes, demonstrating finite-sample validity.
- In simulations with varying functional data variability, the modulated Conformal Prediction bands, particularly those using ¯s_I₁(t), produced **smaller average band widths**, indicating enhanced efficiency compared to constant-width bands.
- The ¯s_I₁(t) modulation function exhibited superior robustness to outliers and heterogeneous data in simulations and the Berkeley Growth Study, yielding the **smallest average band widths** and more informative prediction sets in complex scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conformal Prediction, when combined with a supremum-based nonconformity measure, can directly generate simultaneous prediction bands for functional data with rigorous finite-sample coverage guarantees.
> - Modulating the prediction band's width based on local data variability significantly improves efficiency, and a robust data-driven modulation function can achieve this while maintaining theoretical validity.
> - The proposed ¯s_I₁(t) modulation function effectively filters out the influence of extreme training functions, leading to prediction bands that are more robust to outliers and better adapted to heterogeneous functional data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Functional Data Analysis (FDA) is an area of statistical inquiry dedicated to developing methods for data sets composed of functions. Within FDA, the quantification of uncertainty in prediction and forecasting, specifically through the construction of prediction sets, has been identified as an area requiring further development.

Existing approaches to creating prediction sets for functional data generally fall into two categories. The first involves parametric bootstrapping techniques (e.g., Degras 2011, Cao et al. 2012), which are often computationally intensive. The second category employs dimensionality reduction techniques, where infinite-dimensional functional data are projected onto a finite-dimensional basis (e.g., Hyndman & Shahid Ullah 2007, Antoniadis et al. 2016). Both categories typically rely on assumptions that may be difficult to verify (e.g., Gaussianity), or on asymptotic arguments for validity, and the dimension reduction approaches introduce approximations.

This research positions itself within the framework of Conformal Prediction (CP), a nonparametric method that originated in the Machine Learning community (Vovk et al. 2005, Shafer & Vovk 2008). CP is known for its ability to generate distribution-free prediction intervals that offer finite-sample validity or exactness under minimal assumptions (primarily exchangeability or i.i.d. data). While CP has been applied to functional data previously, such applications often involved dimension reduction techniques, such as using a truncated basis approach (Lei et al. 2015).

The present work aims to address the limitations of existing methods by proposing a novel nonparametric approach within the Conformal Prediction framework. Its contribution to the broader landscape is the development of a procedure that yields finite-sample valid or exact prediction sets for functional data, without relying on strong distributional assumptions, dimension reduction, or asymptotic arguments. A particular focus is placed on ensuring that these prediction sets take the form of "bands," a feature highlighted as crucial for visualization and interpretation in functional contexts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a new nonparametric approach for generating prediction sets for functional data. This overarching goal is motivated by several specific challenges and desired properties:

1. **Validity and Exactness in Finite Samples:** A core motivation is to construct prediction sets that reliably achieve a desired coverage level. Specifically, the aim is to ensure that a new, unobserved function will be contained within the prediction set with a nominal confidence level (1-α). This includes achieving "valid" coverage (where the true coverage is no less than 1-α) or "exact" coverage (where the true coverage precisely equals 1-α), even with finite sample sizes. This contrasts with many existing methods that primarily guarantee coverage asymptotically.

2. **Distribution-Free Nature:** The authors seek to develop a method that requires minimal distributional assumptions about the underlying data-generating process. This is intended to increase the robustness and applicability of the method across diverse functional data types, avoiding the restrictive assumptions (e.g., Gaussianity) often made by other techniques.

3. **Efficiency:** Beyond merely guaranteeing coverage, a significant objective is to produce prediction sets that are as small or narrow as possible. Smaller prediction sets are considered more informative and desirable from an application perspective, as they concentrate on regions where the probability mass is concentrated.

4. **Prediction Band Shape:** A key motivation, emphasized by the paper's title, is the generation of prediction sets that specifically take the form of "bands." A prediction band is defined as a region where, for every point `t` in the domain `T`, the function `y(t)` falls within a specified interval `B_n(t)`. The authors argue that this shape is essential for the easy visualization and interpretation of prediction sets in the functional context, unlike more general or amorphous regions in function space. An illustrative example (Figure 1 in the paper) demonstrates how non-band shapes can complicate interpretation by creating conditional relationships between points on the functional domain. Bands, by contrast, coincide with their envelope, simplifying interpretation.

5. **Computational Tractability:** The method should be fast and scalable to handle the increasing volume and complexity of functional data encountered in modern applications. This implies avoiding computationally intensive procedures.

6. **Avoidance of Dimension Reduction:** The authors aim to develop a method that does not rely on dimension reduction techniques. While dimension reduction can simplify problems involving high-dimensional or infinite-dimensional data, it introduces approximations that can impact the accuracy and theoretical guarantees of prediction sets. The goal is to work directly in the functional space.

7. **Flexibility in Nonconformity Measure:** The framework should allow for the selection of different nonconformity measures, offering flexibility to adapt the prediction sets to the specific characteristics of the problem at hand while maintaining validity. This includes the ability to modulate the width of the prediction band across the functional domain.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to fill a gap in the FDA literature by providing a theoretically sound, computationally efficient, and practically interpretable method for functional data prediction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodological core of this work is built upon the Conformal Prediction (CP) framework, specifically utilizing the computationally efficient Semi-Off-Line Inductive Conformal (or Split Conformal) approach. This method partitions the observed functional data into two distinct sets to ensure robust finite-sample guarantees.

#### 3.1. Conformal Prediction Framework

Given a set of `n` independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random functions `Y_1..., Y_n` and a new, independent function `Y_{n+1}`, the objective is to construct a prediction set `C_{n,1-α}` for `Y_{n+1}` that satisfies `P(Y_{n+1} ∈ C_{n,1-α}) ≥ 1-α` (validity) or `P(Y_{n+1} ∈ C_{n,1-α}) = 1-α` (exactness).

The Split Conformal procedure involves the following steps:
1. **Data Split:** The `n` observed functions `y_1..., y_n` are randomly divided into a training set `I_1` of size `m` and a calibration set `I_2` of size `l`, where `n = m + l`.
2. **Nonconformity Measure:** A measurable function `A({y_h: h ∈ I_1}, y)` is defined. This "nonconformity measure" quantifies how much a function `y` deviates from the patterns observed in the training set `I_1`.
3. **Nonconformity Scores:** Scores are computed for each function in the calibration set (`R_j = A(I_1, y_j)` for `j ∈ I_2`) and for a candidate new function (`R_{n+1} = A(I_1, y)`).
4. **P-value (Credibility Score):** For any candidate function `y`, a p-value `δ_y = |{j ∈ I_2 ∪ {n+1}: R_j ≥ R_{n+1}} | / (l + 1)` is calculated. This represents the proportion of calibration and candidate scores that are as "nonconforming" as `y`.
5. **Prediction Set Construction:** The prediction set is defined as `C_{n,1-α}:= {y ∈ Y(T): δ_y > α}`.
 The paper formally proves (Theorem 1) that under i.i.d. data and continuous joint distribution of nonconformity scores, `P(Y_{n+1} ∈ C_{n,1-α}) = 1 - b(l+1)αc / (l+1)`, guaranteeing validity and providing a precise coverage level.
 For exact coverage, a "smoothed" version of Split Conformal Prediction is introduced, incorporating a random variable uniformly distributed in ``, which is formally proven to achieve `P(Y_{n+1} ∈ C_{n,1-α,τ_{n+1}}) = 1-α`.

#### 3.2. Supremum Metric as a Nonconformity Measure

To address the objective of obtaining prediction bands without dimension reduction, the authors propose a specific nonconformity measure based on the `L∞` (supremum) metric:
`A({y_h: h ∈ I_1}, y) = sup_{t∈T} |y(t) - g_{I_1}(t)|`.
Here, `g_{I_1}(t)` is a point predictor function (e.g., the sample functional mean) estimated from the training set `I_1`.

This choice of nonconformity measure leads to prediction sets of the form:
`C_{n,1-α}:= {y ∈ Y(T): y(t) ∈ [g_{I_1}(t) - k, g_{I_1}(t) + k] ∀t ∈ T}`,
where `k` is determined as the `b(l+1)(1-α)c`-th smallest value among the nonconformity scores `R_j` from the calibration set.
This methodology inherently produces prediction sets that are *bands*, are in *closed form*, are *fast and scalable* (the most intensive part being the computation of `g_{I_1}`), and guarantee *simultaneous coverage* over the entire domain `T`.

#### 3.3. Improving Efficiency: The Modulation Function

Recognizing that constant-width bands (resulting from the initial `L∞` measure) might not be efficient in scenarios with varying functional data variability across the domain, the authors introduce a family of nonconformity measures using a "modulation function" `s_{I_1}(t)`:
`A({y_h: h ∈ I_1}, y) = sup_{t∈T} |(y(t) - g_{I_1}(t)) / s_{I_1}(t)|`.
The function `s_{I_1}(t) > 0 ∀t ∈ T` is also derived from the training set `I_1` and acts to rescale the deviations based on local variability. This modified nonconformity measure yields prediction bands of the form:
`C^s_{n,1-α}:= {y ∈ Y(T): y(t) ∈ [g_{I_1}(t) - k_s * s_{I_1}(t), g_{I_1}(t) + k_s * s_{I_1}(t)] ∀t ∈ T]`,
where `k_s` is the `b(l+1)(1-α)c`-th smallest nonconformity score calculated using the modulated differences. This approach allows the band width to adapt to local data characteristics while maintaining the CP validity guarantees.

The selection of an optimal `s_{I_1}(t)` is guided by the criterion of maximizing efficiency (minimizing the size of the prediction band), defined as `Q(s_{I_1}) = ∫_T 2 * k_s * s_{I_1}(t) dt = 2 * k_s`. The authors propose a specific data-driven modulation function, `¯s_{I_1}(t)` (termed a "t-function" for its dependence on the training set):
`¯s_{I_1}(t):= max_{j∈H_1} |y_j(t) - g_{I_1}(t)| / ∫_T max_{j∈H_1} |y_j(t) - g_{I_1}(t)| dt`,
where `H_1` is a subset of `I_1` containing functions whose supremum nonconformity score (`sup_t |y_j(t) - g_{I_1}(t)|`) is below a certain quantile `γ`. This design ensures that the modulation is primarily influenced by the "central" (less extreme) functions in the training set.

The theoretical justification for `¯s_{I_1}(t)` is provided by three theorems:
- **Theorem 2:** Establishes asymptotic convergence of `¯s_{I_1}(t)` (and its calibration counterpart `¯s^c_{I_1}(t)`) to a common limit function as sample size increases, ensuring that the resulting prediction bands also converge.
- **Theorem 3:** Proves that `Q(s_0) ≥ Q(¯s^c_{I_1})`, meaning that the modulated prediction bands using `¯s_{I_1}(t)` are asymptotically no wider than those obtained with no modulation (`s_0(t) = 1/|T|`).
- **Theorem 4:** Provides conditions under which `¯s_{I_1}(t)` can lead to strictly smaller prediction bands compared to other modulation functions.

#### 3.4. Simulation Study and Application

The methodology is evaluated through a two-stage simulation study comparing the proposed Conformal approach (with `s_0`, `s^σ_{I_1}` - normalized standard deviation, and `¯s_{I_1}`) against four alternative methods: Naive, Band Depth (BD), Modified Band Depth (MBD), and a Bootstrap method. Three scenarios are considered: constant variability, varying variability, and varying variability with outliers. Sample sizes `n = 18, 198, 1998` are used. The study first assesses empirical conditional coverage, then compares the efficiency (size) of methods that achieve proper coverage.

Finally, the approach is applied to the Berkeley Growth Study dataset, analyzing growth velocity curves for girls and boys, which are characterized by complex features such as heteroscedasticity, phase misalignment, and outliers.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields both theoretical and empirical results, demonstrating the properties and performance of the proposed Conformal Prediction bands for functional data.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

- **Coverage Guarantees:** Theorem 1 formally proves that split conformal prediction sets provide a precise coverage probability of `1 - b(l+1)αc / (l+1)`, ensuring validity (coverage no less than `1-α`) for finite samples under i.i.d. data and continuous scores. The paper also provides a formal proof for the exactness (`1-α` coverage) of smoothed split conformal prediction sets when randomization is introduced.
- **Prediction Band Shape:** The initial nonconformity measure based on the supremum metric (`sup_t |y(t) - g_{I_1}(t)|`) is demonstrated to directly yield prediction sets that are *bands* of constant width, which are simultaneous (guaranteeing coverage over the entire domain `T`).
- **Modulation Function Properties:** The proposed data-driven modulation function, `¯s_{I_1}(t)`, is shown to possess desirable asymptotic and efficiency properties:
 - **Asymptotic Equivalence (Theorem 2):** `¯s_{I_1}(t)` and its calibration counterpart `¯s^c_{I_1}(t)` converge to the same function as the sample size increases, implying that the prediction bands generated by them also converge.
 - **Asymptotic Efficiency (Theorem 3):** Prediction bands produced using `¯s_{I_1}(t)` are asymptotically no less efficient (i.e., not wider) than those obtained without any modulation (`s_0(t)`). This demonstrates an improvement in efficiency where local variability exists.
 - **Relative Efficiency (Theorem 4):** Under specified conditions, `¯s^c_{I_1}(t)` is shown to produce prediction bands that are strictly smaller than those generated by a class of other modulation functions.

#### 4.2. Simulation Study Results

The simulation study comprised a comparison across three scenarios (constant variability, varying variability, varying variability with outliers) and three sample sizes (`n=18, 198, 1998`), focusing on coverage and efficiency.

- **Coverage Performance:**
 - The proposed Conformal Prediction methods (using `s_0`, `s^σ_{I_1}`, or `¯s_{I_1}`) consistently demonstrated empirical conditional coverage that included the nominal `1-α = 0.90` within their 99% confidence intervals across almost all scenarios and sample sizes (53 out of 54 cases). This empirical evidence strongly supports the finite-sample validity and approximate exactness claimed theoretically.
 - In contrast, alternative methods (Naive, Band Depth, Modified Band Depth, Bootstrap) generally failed to achieve the desired coverage, often exhibiting significant undercoverage, particularly with smaller sample sizes. This outcome validates the robustness of the Conformal approach.

- **Efficiency Performance (among Conformal methods):**
 - **Small Sample Sizes (n=18):** For the smallest sample size, `s_0` (no modulation) tended to produce smaller prediction bands. This suggests that complex, data-driven modulation functions might be unstable or less effective when training data are extremely limited.
 - **Constant Variability (Scenario 1):** As expected in a scenario with uniform variance, `s_0` performed comparably to, or slightly better than, the modulated approaches (`s^σ_{I_1}` and `¯s_{I_1}`), as modulation offered no practical advantage. Differences diminished as sample size increased.
 - **Varying Variability (Scenario 2):** In this scenario, `s^σ_{I_1}` and `¯s_{I_1}` outperformed `s_0` by producing smaller average band widths. This indicated that modulation effectively adapted the band to regions of lower variability, leading to more informative prediction sets. `s^σ_{I_1}` generally performed slightly better than `¯s_{I_1}` in this scenario.
 - **Varying Variability with Outliers (Scenario 3):** `¯s_{I_1}` demonstrated superior performance, yielding the smallest average band widths. While `s^σ_{I_1}` adapted to varying variability, it showed sensitivity to outliers, causing the band to unnecessarily widen in outlier-affected regions. `¯s_{I_1}`, by construction, filters out the most extreme functions from the training set, allowing it to modulate data based on the non-extreme components and maintain a more appropriate band shape despite outliers.

#### 4.3. Application Results (Berkeley Growth Study)

The application to the Berkeley Growth Study data (growth velocity curves for girls and boys) showcased the method's utility in a realistic, complex functional data context marked by heteroscedasticity, phase misalignment, and outliers.

- The approach successfully generated interpretable prediction bands for growth velocities, clearly differentiating between girls' and boys' growth patterns.
- For girls' curves, which exhibited lower variance heterogeneity, the choice of modulation function had a less dramatic impact, with `¯s_{I_1}` providing slightly narrower bands due to outlier handling.
- For boys' curves, characterized by stronger heteroscedasticity (e.g., a localized high peak around 13 years), the benefits of modulation were more pronounced. `s_0` yielded unnecessarily wide bands in some regions. `¯s_{I_1}` produced the smallest average band width compared to `s_0` and `s^σ_{I_1}` for both sexes, reaffirming its robustness in complex, outlier-prone scenarios.
- A practical advantage observed was the ability to truncate the prediction bands at zero (as growth velocity cannot be negative) without compromising the coverage guarantees, a direct benefit of the nonparametric Conformal approach.
- Comparison with a pointwise Conformal approach revealed that the proposed simultaneous bands, while generally wider, offer a stronger guarantee of global coverage over the entire functional domain, emphasizing the function as a whole rather than a collection of independent points.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of Functional Data Analysis, particularly for the problem of uncertainty quantification in functional prediction. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

1. **Robust and Reliable Uncertainty Quantification:** The primary impact is providing a theoretically sound method for constructing prediction sets for functional data that offers finite-sample validity or exactness. This addresses a significant gap where many existing FDA methods rely on strong, untestable distributional assumptions, dimension reduction approximations, or asymptotic guarantees, which limit their practical reliability. The explicit coverage probability statements provided by Conformal Prediction offer a high level of confidence in the derived prediction sets.

2. **Enhanced Interpretability through Prediction Bands:** The deliberate focus on generating "prediction bands" as the shape of the prediction set is crucial for the functional context. Unlike more general prediction regions, bands are intuitively visualizable and easily interpretable across the functional domain, enabling scientists, business people, and policymakers to draw clear conclusions about the probable range of a future function. This improves the practical utility of functional forecasting.

3. **Computational Efficiency and Scalability:** The closed-form nature of the prediction sets and the computational efficiency of the Split Conformal framework ensure that the method is fast and scalable. This is particularly important given the increasing size and complexity of modern functional datasets, making the approach viable for real-world, high-throughput applications that traditional computationally intensive methods might struggle with.

4. **Improved Efficiency with Data-Driven Modulation:** The introduction and validation of the `¯s_{I_1}` modulation function represent a significant step towards optimizing prediction band efficiency. By adapting the band width to the local variability of the data and robustly handling outliers, `¯s_{I_1}` yields narrower and more informative prediction sets. This is particularly impactful in heterogeneous functional data, where a constant-width band would be inefficient or misleading.

5. **Broad Applicability and Flexibility:** The nonparametric, distribution-free nature of the method allows it to be applied across a wide range of functional data types and scenarios, including those with complex features like heteroscedasticity, phase misalignment, and outliers, as demonstrated by the Berkeley Growth Study. The flexibility to choose different modulation functions further enhances its adaptability. The ability to incorporate domain knowledge, such as positivity constraints, without affecting coverage is a practical advantage for many applications.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a promising foundation for future methodological developments. The authors explicitly suggest extensions to functional regression and classification problems within the Conformal framework. Furthermore, they highlight the importance of future investigations into the impact of the data splitting process (choice of `m` and `l`) on efficiency and the potential for developing multi-band prediction sets for more complex data structures, such as functional mixtures.

In summary, this research offers a robust, interpretable, and computationally efficient framework for functional data prediction, addressing key limitations of existing methods and opening avenues for further statistical innovation in this growing field.
