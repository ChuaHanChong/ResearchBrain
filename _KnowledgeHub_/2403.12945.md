# DROID: A Large-Scale In-The-Wild Robot Manipulation Dataset

> [!summary] Summary
> The DROID project introduces a large-scale robot manipulation dataset collected "in-the-wild" across 16 institutions, featuring unprecedented diversity in scenes, tasks, and objects. Policies co-trained with DROID show an average 20% increase in success rate and enhanced robustness to out-of-distribution variations, outperforming existing dataset aggregations.

## Problem

- Existing robot manipulation datasets lack diversity in scenes, tasks, and objects, limiting the generalization capabilities of learned policies.
- Data collection for physical robots is complex due to logistical, safety, and hardware constraints, leading to a scarcity of high-quality, real-world data.
- Robot learning algorithms struggle to generalize beyond controlled laboratory environments, hindering real-world deployment.

## Method

- Over 80 authors from 16 institutions collaboratively collected data using 18 standardized robot platforms across diverse real-world environments.
- A rigorous ==in-the-wild data collection protocol== encouraged scene augmentations, random task selection, and systematic introduction of variations to maximize diversity.
- Post-processing included ==crowdsourced natural language annotations== and automatic camera calibration refinement using deep learning methods to enhance data quality and geometric accuracy.

## Results

- DROID consists of **76,000** successful trajectories (**350 hours**) with an order of magnitude more unique scenes (**564**) and tasks (**86**) than prior datasets.
- Policies co-trained with DROID achieved a **20%** average increase in success rate and significantly improved robustness on out-of-distribution tasks compared to in-domain baselines.
- DROID co-training outperformed co-training with the Open X-Embodiment (OXE) dataset, demonstrating that DROID's unique 'in-the-wild' diversity is more impactful than combining existing less diverse datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unprecedented scene diversity (564 unique scenes across 52 buildings) is a crucial factor for improving robot policy robustness and generalization to novel environments.
> - A distributed, multi-institutional data collection effort using standardized hardware effectively overcomes traditional bottlenecks in acquiring large-scale, 'in-the-wild' robot data.
> - High-quality natural language annotations and refined camera calibration are essential components for maximizing the utility of large-scale robot manipulation datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of DROID is a direct response to a critical bottleneck in robot manipulation research: the scarcity of large, diverse, and high-quality "in-the-wild" datasets. This challenge has historically limited the ability of robot learning algorithms to generalize beyond controlled laboratory environments, a prerequisite for deploying robots in everyday settings.

In the broader machine learning landscape, fields like computer vision (e.g., ImageNet, LAION) and natural language processing (e.g., Common Crawl, The Pile) have witnessed revolutionary progress directly attributable to the availability of massive, diverse datasets. These datasets enable the training of large foundation models that exhibit remarkable generalization capabilities. The robotics community has sought to replicate this success, but data collection for physical robots is inherently more complex due to logistical, safety, and hardware constraints. Unlike images or text, robot manipulation data requires synchronized observations (vision, depth, proprioception) and actions, which cannot be easily "scraped" from the internet.

Prior work in robot learning has produced various datasets (e.g., MIME, RoboTurk, RoboNet, BridgeData, RT-1, RH20T), which have pushed the boundaries of scale and skill acquisition. However, the authors argue that most of these datasets suffer from limited diversity in terms of scenes, tasks, and interaction objects. Many are collected in a small number of lab environments, leading to policies that struggle with out-of-distribution generalization. Even efforts like the Open X-Embodiment (OXE) dataset, which aggregates existing datasets across multiple robot embodiments, still primarily consist of individual datasets with limited scene diversity (around 300 scenes in total at the time of DROID's creation).

DROID distinguishes itself by prioritizing *unprecedented diversity* across multiple axes, particularly "in-the-wild" scene realism. It aims to bridge the gap between lab-confined robotics research and real-world deployment by providing data collected in a wide array of realistic, uncontrolled environments. This aligns DROID with efforts in autonomous driving (e.g., nuScenes, KITTI) and navigation (e.g., Waymo Open Dataset), which have long leveraged large-scale, real-world data to train robust systems. By focusing on generalizable policies that "just work," DROID positions itself as a foundational resource that could catalyze progress akin to that seen in other data-driven AI domains. It directly contributes to the goal of developing generalist robot agents capable of performing diverse tasks in arbitrary environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objective of the DROID project is to **create a large-scale, diverse, and high-quality robot manipulation dataset** collected "in-the-wild." This objective is driven by a clear motivation: to overcome the limitations of existing datasets and enable the development of more capable, robust, and generalizable robot manipulation policies.

The authors explicitly state the following key motivations:
- **Enable Generalization:** A primary goal is to train policies that can generalize, meaning they can effectively perform desired manipulation tasks under new lighting conditions, in novel environments, or with previously unseen objects. This is crucial for real-world deployment where conditions are dynamic and unpredictable.
- **Address Data Scarcity and Homogeneity:** Current general robot manipulation policies are often trained on data collected in limited, controlled lab settings. This lack of diversity prevents them from adapting to the variations found in everyday environments. DROID seeks to provide the necessary breadth of data to overcome this.
- **Push the Envelope on Policy Generalization:** Building on the success of large, diverse datasets in computer vision and natural language processing, the authors hypothesize that a similar large-scale, diverse dataset is essential for advancing robot manipulation capabilities, including positive transfer to new objects, instructions, scenes, and embodiments.
- **Facilitate "Download and Just Work" Models:** The ultimate dream for many roboticists is the ability to download pre-trained robot models that function reliably in new, unseen robot setups. DROID is envisioned as a stepping stone towards this goal by fostering the development of truly generalizable policies.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The project aims to open-source the full dataset, policy learning code, and a detailed guide for reproducing the robot setup, fostering community engagement, reproducibility, and accelerating research in robot learning.

In essence, DROID's motivation stems from the recognition that data diversity is paramount for achieving robust and adaptable robot intelligence, allowing robots to move beyond constrained lab environments and integrate into diverse real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DROID project employed a meticulous and highly coordinated methodology to achieve its ambitious goal of collecting a diverse, high-quality dataset. The approach can be broken down into several key components:

**a. Distributed Data Collection and Standardization:**
- **Multi-Institutional Collaboration:** DROID was collected by 50 data collectors across 18 research labs in 13 institutions spanning North America, Asia, and Europe over 12 months. This geographical and institutional diversity was fundamental to achieving "in-the-wild" scene variations.
- **Standardized Hardware Platform:** To ensure consistency and reproducibility across diverse collection sites, a uniform "DROID robot platform" was designed and replicated 18 times. This platform includes:
 - **Franka Emika Panda 7 DoF robot arm:** Chosen for its widespread adoption, reliability, and relative affordability.
 - **Robotiq 2F-85 gripper:** A common and versatile gripper.
 - **Three synchronized stereo cameras:** Two external Zed 2 cameras on adjustable tripods (for flexible viewpoints) and one wrist-mounted Zed Mini camera (for egocentric views).
 - **Oculus Quest 2 headset with controllers:** Used for intuitive 6D teleoperation of the arm and continuous gripper control.
 - **Portable, height-adjustable standing desk with wheels:** Essential for easily moving the robot between different scenes and buildings.
 - **Polymetis controller:** A popular open-source robot control framework, ensuring consistent robot control.
 - **Simplified power:** A single power cable for the entire setup to enhance portability.

**b. Data Collection Protocol:**
- **Focus on Diversity:** The protocol was designed to encourage the collection of diverse data by allowing collectors to creatively choose scenes and tasks, while systematically introducing variations.
- **New Scene Setup:** Each session began with moving the robot to a new scene, with collectors encouraged to select scenes featuring multiple interesting tasks, numerous interaction objects, and natural clutter.
- **Camera Calibration:** After setting up in a new scene, extrinsic camera calibration was performed using a checkerboard and the OpenCV algorithm.
- **Guided Task Selection:** A data collection GUI prompted collectors with randomly sampled tasks from a list of scene-appropriate options entered by the collector. This ensured high coverage of diverse tasks and prevented bias towards easier or more familiar ones.
- **Scene Augmentations:** The GUI periodically prompted collectors to perform "scene augmentations," such as nudging the mobile base, moving and recalibrating third-person cameras, changing room lighting, and adding/removing items within the scene. This further boosted scene and environmental diversity.
- **Data Recording:** Each trajectory recorded: three RGB camera streams, depth information, camera calibration, robot joint positions and velocities, end-effector pose and velocity, gripper position and velocity, a data collector ID, and metadata (including success labels).
- **Session Length:** Collectors typically gathered up to 100 trajectories (approx. 20 minutes) per scene before moving to a new one, maximizing scene changes.

**c. Post-processing:**
- **Natural Language Annotations:** Natural language commands were added to each episode using crowdsourcing via the tasq.ai platform, with up to three independent annotations per episode for diversity.
- **Automatic Camera Calibration Refinement:** Recognizing potential inaccuracies in manual calibration, an automatic post-hoc calibration process was implemented. This utilized state-of-the-art deep learning methods (e.g., SAM for robot mask generation, CtRNet-X for camera-to-robot base, DUSt3R for camera-to-camera) to refine and provide quality metrics for three sets of calibration matrices, enhancing geometric accuracy crucial for 3D perception and action grounding.

This comprehensive methodology, combining distributed effort, standardized hardware, a structured yet flexible collection protocol, and robust post-processing, enabled the creation of DROID's unprecedented scale and diversity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The DROID paper presents two main categories of findings: a detailed analysis of the dataset's characteristics and empirical results demonstrating its utility for training generalizable robot manipulation policies.

**a. Dataset Characteristics (DROID's Scale and Diversity):**
- **Size and Interaction Hours:** DROID comprises 76,000 successful demonstration trajectories, accumulating 350 hours of robot interaction data. This is a significant scale for a robot manipulation dataset collected "in-the-wild."
- **Unprecedented Scene Diversity:** DROID features an order of magnitude more unique scenes than any existing robot manipulation dataset, with **564 unique scenes across 52 buildings**. These scenes span 9 distinct types (e.g., kitchen, office, bedroom, bathroom, laboratory), reflecting realistic real-world environments with natural object arrangements and clutter.
- **High Task Diversity:** The dataset covers **86 distinct tasks or verbs**, showcasing a wide range of behaviors with a long-tailed distribution, meaning many different tasks are represented beyond a few common ones. This contrasts with many prior datasets that focus on a limited set of skills.
- **Extensive Object Diversity:** DROID includes interactions with a broad array of everyday objects across numerous categories (e.g., containers, textiles, furniture, appliances, food), facilitating generalization to new objects. The joint distribution of verbs and objects demonstrates that most objects are interacted with in diverse ways.
- **Viewpoint Diversity:** Unlike datasets with fixed camera setups, DROID captures data from **1,417 unique camera viewpoints** due to the adjustable nature of its external cameras and the movement of the robot. Each episode also includes intrinsic and extrinsic stereo camera calibration.
- **Interaction Location Diversity:** The dataset covers a significantly wider range of 3D interaction locations relative to the robot's base, moving beyond typical tabletop interactions to incorporate variations in table heights and workspace areas. This is attributed to the robot being moved freely between collection sessions.
- **Data Richness:** Each episode provides three synchronized RGB camera streams, depth information, camera calibration, natural language instructions (crowdsourced and de-duplicated), and robot state (joint positions/velocities, end-effector pose/velocity, gripper state).

**b. Empirical Evaluation (Policy Performance and Robustness):**
The paper evaluates the effectiveness of DROID by co-training state-of-the-art diffusion policies with in-domain data and comparing them against baselines.
- **Experimental Setup:** Policies were trained and evaluated on 6 diverse tasks (e.g., "Close Waffle Maker," "Place Chips on Plate," "Cook Lentils") across 4 real-world locations (lab, office, household). Each task included in-distribution and challenging out-of-distribution (OOD) variants (e.g., distractors, novel objects, camera shifts) to test robustness.
- **Performance Boost:** Policies co-trained with DROID demonstrated a **20% average increase in success rate** over policies trained solely on in-domain demonstrations.
- **Improved Robustness and Generalizability:** DROID co-trained policies showed significantly higher robustness to OOD variations compared to baselines. For example, they consistently performed better when facing distractor objects or novel instances of manipulated items. In complex multi-step tasks like "Cook Lentils," DROID-trained policies were the only ones able to consistently complete all stages in OOD settings.
- **Superior to Existing Aggregations:** DROID co-training also **outperformed co-training with the Open X-Embodiment (OXE) dataset**, which aggregates many existing large robot manipulation datasets. This suggests that DROID's unique "in-the-wild" diversity is more impactful than simply combining existing, less diverse datasets.
- **Validation of Scene Diversity's Importance:** A controlled experiment showed that co-training with a subset of DROID emphasizing diverse scenes yielded better OOD performance than co-training with a same-sized subset focused on only 20 scenes. This directly validates the hypothesis that **scene diversity is a crucial factor** contributing to improved policy robustness and generalization.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Policies trained with DROID exhibited smoother and more precise motions, particularly in challenging OOD scenarios, indicating better task understanding and execution.

In summary, the main findings confirm that DROID is not only a quantitatively massive and diverse dataset but also empirically effective in significantly boosting the performance, robustness, and generalization capabilities of robot manipulation policies, particularly in real-world, varied environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DROID dataset represents a significant step forward for data-driven robot learning, with profound implications for future research and development in robot manipulation.

**a. Catalyzing General-Purpose Robot Manipulation:**
The most immediate and significant impact of DROID is its potential to serve as a catalyst for the development of **general-purpose robot manipulation policies**. By providing an unprecedented breadth of data from diverse, real-world scenes, tasks, objects, and viewpoints, DROID offers the raw material needed to train models that can generalize effectively across varied conditions. This moves the field closer to the long-sought goal of robots that are truly versatile and adaptable, rather than task-specific or environment-specific machines.

**b. Enabling Robustness and Zero-Shot Generalization:**
The empirical results clearly demonstrate that training with DROID leads to policies with higher performance and significantly improved robustness to out-of-distribution variations. This is critical for deploying robots in unpredictable real-world environments where novel objects, lighting, and scene layouts are commonplace. Furthermore, DROID could accelerate research into **zero-shot generalization**, allowing robots to perform tasks in entirely new scenes without requiring any in-domain demonstrations, a major unsolved challenge in robot learning.

**c. Advancing Fundamental Research Questions:**
DROID opens up numerous avenues for fundamental research:
- **Data Combination Strategies:** How can DROID be optimally combined with other large-scale robot datasets (like OXE) to maximize learning and generalization?
- **Visual Representation Learning:** Can the diverse interaction data in DROID be leveraged to learn more robust and transferable visual representations for robotic control, similar to the impact of ImageNet on computer vision?
- **Optimal Data Utilization:** What are the best strategies for training on such diverse data? When is it beneficial to use the entire dataset versus curated subsets for specific tasks or generalization targets?
- **Embodiment Transfer:** The standardized hardware platform for DROID can also be a testbed for cross-embodiment learning experiments.

**d. Promoting Reproducibility and Community Growth:**
The open-sourcing of the full DROID dataset (under CC-BY 4.0 license), policy learning code, and a detailed guide for reproducing the robot hardware and software setup is a major contribution to the robotics community. This transparency and accessibility will:
- **Enhance Reproducibility:** Allow other researchers to validate findings and build upon the work with a standardized and well-documented platform.
- **Lower Barriers to Entry:** Provide high-quality data and tools, making advanced robot learning more accessible.
- **Facilitate Dataset Expansion:** The standardized and easy-to-reproduce hardware platform, already deployed in 18 labs, provides a strong foundation for future, incremental additions to the DROID dataset, fostering a community-driven expansion of robot learning data.

Ultimately, DROID aims to move the robotics field closer to the vision of "robot models that can be downloaded and 'just work' when tested on a new robot setup." By providing an unparalleled resource of diverse, real-world robot interaction data, it lays a crucial groundwork for the next generation of intelligent, adaptable, and deployable robotic systems.
