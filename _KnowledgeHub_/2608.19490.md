# Fine-Tuning VLAs with Self-Demonstrated Generative Control for Multi-Task Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A self-supervised fine-tuning method for VLA policies (base: pi_0.5) that rolls out the frozen pretrained policy on familiar tasks on the target robot, then jointly trains on these self-generated rollouts plus a small set of expert teleoperation demos. On ALOHA and RoboTwin, this yields multi-task policies that retain instruction following and pretrained behaviors while learning new expert tasks with far less teleoperation.

## Problem

- Fine-tuning VLAs on in-domain expert data restores physical grasping on a new embodiment but catastrophically erodes prior instruction following and pretrained behavioral priors, e.g. forgetting place after only pick demos.
- Existing rehearsal-based continual learning methods for VLAs assume access to the original proprietary pretraining dataset or task list, which downstream users of a released foundation policy do not have.
- Prior anti-forgetting techniques (parameter-efficient tuning, adapter routing, weight merging) mitigate but do not fully solve forgetting, and mid-scale intermediate fine-tuning can overwrite pretrained representations entirely.

## Method

- ==Self-supervised rollout collection==: the frozen base VLA is executed on the target robot under pretraining-family prompts, and its own predicted action chunks and resulting observations are recorded as a rehearsal dataset with zero embodiment domain gap.
- ==Joint expert-plus-self-supervised training objective== combining cross-entropy on discretized FAST action tokens with ==conditional flow matching==, summed as a weighted loss over expert-supervised and self-supervised data mixed 1:1.
- New ==RoboTwin simulation benchmark== and evaluation protocol with mid-training and post-training stages, testing generalization to expert tasks, self-supervised tasks, novel objects, and novel skill compositions without stored mid-training data access.

## Results

- On real ALOHA pick-and-place, joint fine-tuning raises pick-and-place success from **0%** (expert-only) to **55%**, and instruction following from 50 to **55%** and 75 to **90%** across two test sets, without expert place demos.
- On contact-rich bimanual gear insertion, self-supervision improves success rate from **30%** to **90%** on trained gear colors and enables **30%** success on three unseen gear colors versus **0%** for expert-only baselines.
- In RoboTwin simulation, expert-only fine-tuning drops old-task success from **90.8%** to **16.6%**, while the joint method recovers it to **70.6%** and even improves new expert-task success from **93%** to **98%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A pretrained policy's own noisy, often-failing rollouts can serve as effective rehearsal targets, showing pretraining is useful not just for initialization but as a reusable behavioral prior generator.
> - Multi-task training across several prompts in a shared scene, rather than single-task fine-tuning, is necessary to prevent the model from collapsing onto visual shortcuts and ignoring language conditioning.
> - Expert supervision on all tasks can overwrite rather than adapt base policy priors, causing the model to default to trained motion chains even on held-out skills; self-demos preserve unrelated behaviors better.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represents a significant advancement in robotics, promising improved task generalization by enabling robots to interpret natural language instructions and visual inputs to perform complex actions. Prominent VLA models, such as `π_0.5`, exhibit robust semantic understanding, instruction following, and diverse task behaviors when trained on large, varied datasets.

However, a critical challenge arises when these pre-trained VLA models are deployed on new robotic platforms. Even minor differences in hardware configurations, such as gripper types, camera placements, or other embodiment characteristics, can lead to substantial performance degradation. Standard fine-tuning approaches, which involve training the VLA on in-domain expert demonstrations from the target robot, often suffer from catastrophic forgetting. This phenomenon causes the model to lose its original instruction following capabilities and the rich behavioral priors acquired during its extensive pre-training.

The broader research landscape in VLA post-training and continual learning has explored various strategies to mitigate catastrophic forgetting. These include visual representation alignment, parameter expansion and adapter routing, weight merging techniques, and on-policy reinforcement fine-tuning. Experience replay, which involves storing and rehearsing past rollouts, is recognized as a particularly effective method. However, existing experience replay methods typically assume access to either the original pre-training datasets or require intermediate fine-tuning on mid-scale benchmarks that can overwrite pre-trained representations.

This work addresses a practical scenario where a downstream user receives a pre-trained VLA (e.g., `π_0.5`) without access to its proprietary pre-training data or a comprehensive list of its original training tasks. It positions itself as a self-supervised instantiation of generative replay, drawing parallels to prior-preserved fine-tuning in text-to-image diffusion models like DreamBooth. However, instead of sampling the pre-trained model's prior offline, this research proposes generating replay data directly through online physical interaction on the target robot. This approach distinguishes itself by distilling semantic priors and task behaviors directly from the zero-shot base policy's online rollouts during post-training, aiming for seamless policy transfer to new user setups without relying on the original pre-training data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective method for fine-tuning pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for deployment on new robotic embodiments. This fine-tuning process must achieve two key outcomes simultaneously: (1) enable the VLA to learn new skills from limited expert demonstrations on the target robot, and (2) preserve the generalist instruction following capabilities and diverse behavioral priors distilled from its extensive pre-training, which are susceptible to catastrophic forgetting.

The motivation for this work stems from observed limitations when deploying state-of-the-art VLAs, specifically `π_0.5`, on novel robotic hardware:

1. **Embodiment Gaps and Zero-Shot Performance Degradation:** When `π_0.5` was deployed on the ALOHA robotic platform without any fine-tuning, it exhibited strong instruction following and semantic understanding. For instance, the robot would correctly identify and approach the object specified in an instruction, such as "pick up the purple cube." However, it consistently failed to execute fine-grained control actions, particularly grasping, leading to a 0% success rate for physical tasks. This indicated a substantial "embodiment gap" between the pre-training environment(s) and the target ALOHA robot, likely due to minor discrepancies in gripper mechanisms, camera calibration, or other physical configurations.

2. **Catastrophic Forgetting During Expert-Supervised Fine-Tuning:** To address the grasping failures, the natural next step was to fine-tune `π_0.5` using "expert-supervised" teleoperated demonstrations collected directly on the ALOHA robot for specific tasks, such as "pick-up objects." While this fine-tuning successfully improved low-level control and grasping for the expert-demonstrated task, it led to a severe degradation of the model's broader capabilities. The fine-tuned policy would lose its generalist instruction following, sometimes picking up the wrong object (e.g., a green cube instead of a purple one). More critically, it would catastrophically forget multi-stage behaviors, such as the "place" motion in a "pick-and-place" task, because the expert demonstrations only covered the "pick-up" phase. This highlighted the trade-off between acquiring new skills and retaining existing knowledge, a common challenge in continual learning.

The key observation motivating the proposed solution is that even when the zero-shot base policy fails to fully complete a task on the new robot, its action predictions and online rollout trajectories remain semantically correlated with the given instructions. For example, while `π_0.5` could not grasp, it consistently moved toward the correct object and exhibited initial task-relevant movements. This suggests that the latent semantic understanding and high-level behavioral priors of the pre-trained model persist despite low-level control failures.

Leveraging this insight, the research aims to exploit these semi-competent but semantically rich rollouts. By generating "self-supervised" demonstrations from the frozen base policy on a range of tasks similar to its pre-training, the model can effectively "rehearse" its prior knowledge on the target hardware. This self-generated data, collected in the target environment, inherently addresses the domain gap without requiring access to the original pre-training datasets.

The specific objectives of the proposed fine-tuning scheme are to yield strong multi-task policies that, on the target robot:
- Inherit prior tasks distilled from the zero-shot model.
- Enable generalist instruction following.
- Learn new skills from expert data with improved sample efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology focuses on fine-tuning a pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model, specifically `π_0.5`, to perform various tasks on a new robot while addressing embodiment gaps and mitigating catastrophic forgetting. The approach combines expert-supervised imitation learning with a novel self-supervised imitation learning technique.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries

The base model used is `π_0.5`, a pre-trained continuous action chunking VLA policy. At each decision-making time step *t*, the policy receives a multi-modal observation *o_t* = (*I_t*, *p*, *q_t*), consisting of camera images (*I_t*), a natural language task prompt (*p*), and the robot's proprioceptive state (*q_t*). It outputs a continuous action chunk *a_t* = *a_t:t+H*, representing a sequence of *H* actions with dimension *D*.
The `π_0.5` architecture comprises a VLM backbone (using SigLIP vision encoder and PaliGemma text tokens) and a specialized action expert. The action expert is trained using a flow matching objective, predicting the flow velocity for continuous noise action tokens. During training, the VLM backbone is also conditioned on discrete action tokens (FAST) derived from ground truth actions via discrete cosine transform quantization. The authors re-implemented FAST conditioning for training due to its observed benefit in generalization but omitted the sub-task generation head. All fine-tuning experiments default to updating both the VLM and action expert parameters.

#### 3.2. Expert-Supervised Imitation Learning

This component involves fine-tuning the VLA on a dataset of expert demonstrations collected on the target robot.
- **Dataset (D_ES):** Consists of observation-action pairs {(o_t, a_t)} drawn from expert-supervised (ES) trajectories collected via teleoperation or a motion planner on the target embodiment. Each trajectory is associated with a task prompt *p_ES*.
- **Objective:** The model is optimized using a dual training objective combining cross-entropy on the discretized FAST tokens and conditional flow matching on the continuous actions. The per-sample loss is defined as:
 ℓ(*o_t*, *a*; θ) = E_τ,ω[L_CE(*a_FAST_1:M*, *f_ℓ_θ*(*o_t*, *a_FAST_<m*))] + α *||(*ω* - *a*) - *f_a_θ*(*a_τ,ω*, *o_t*)||²*
 where L_CE is the cross-entropy loss over FAST action token positions, *f_a_θ* is the action expert’s predicted velocity, and *α* is a trade-off coefficient.
- **Expert-supervised loss (L_ES):** The expectation of ℓ over *D_ES*.

#### 3.3. Self-Supervised Imitation Learning

This is the core novel component designed to mitigate forgetting and preserve generalist priors.
- **Data Generation:** The frozen base VLA checkpoint (`π_0.5_base`) is rolled out online on the target robot. It is prompted with a set of self-supervised task instructions (*p_SS*) chosen from the base model’s pre-training family of tasks.
- **Self-Demonstrations (D_SS):** At each decision step *t* along a rollout, a teacher action chunk is sampled from the base policy’s conditional flow distribution: *â_t* ~ `π_0.5_base`(*·* | *I_t*, *p_SS*, *q_t*). These actions are executed in the environment, and the resulting observation-action pairs are recorded. This aggregation forms the rehearsal dataset *D_SS*.
- **Domain Gap Bridging:** Crucially, since these self-demonstrations are collected by executing the base policy on the *target hardware*, in *target scenes*, and under *target prompts*, they inherently lack any embodiment or domain gap relative to deployment.
- **Self-supervised loss (L_SS):** The expectation of the per-sample loss ℓ over *D_SS*, with the base policy’s sampled predictions *â_t* serving as target actions.
- **Joint Optimization:** The final model is optimized jointly on both objectives: *L*(θ) = *L_ES*(θ) + λ *L_SS*(θ), where *λ* controls the weight of the self-supervision term.
- **Data Collection and Mixture:** Trajectories with different task instructions are collected from the same initial scene to encourage text-conditioned behavior. A 1:1 mixture ratio of expert-supervised and self-supervised data, with uniform sampling, is found to perform best. Undesired behaviors from self-demos are filtered out before distillation.

#### 3.4. Proposed Benchmarks

Experiments are conducted on both real and simulated environments:
- **Real Robot:** Stationary ALOHA-1 platform (bimanual).
- **Simulation:** ALOHA-Agilex embodiment in RoboTwin 2.0.
Evaluation is performed across four test distributions:
- **T_ES:** Tasks from the expert-supervised training distribution.
- **T_SS:** Tasks from the self-supervised training distribution (i.e., base policy's pre-training family).
- **T_NO:** Tasks with similar skills to T_ES but involving novel objects not seen during training.
- **T_NC:** New tasks composed of skills from T_ES (e.g., stacking three blocks instead of two), primarily in RoboTwin.
In simulation, an initial mid-training stage establishes a "RoboTwin base policy" as `π_0.5` shows 0% zero-shot success in simulation. This isolates task-incremental learning from hardware gaps.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

The ALOHA robot has 7 DoFs per arm. `π_0.5` predicts action chunks with *H* = 50 and *D* = 32. Half of the actions (*H*/2 = 25) are executed open-loop before querying the VLA for the next observation. Real experiments are at 30-50 Hz control frequency. Hyperparameters like iterations (10,000-18,000), batch size (64-128), and α (8.0-10.0) are tuned per benchmark. The `π_0.5` checkpoint from the openpi codebase is used as the zero-shot policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents detailed experimental results across real-world ALOHA robot tasks and a simulated RoboTwin benchmark, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed self-supervised fine-tuning method.

#### 4.1. Bimanual ALOHA Robot Experiments (Table 2 & 3)

- **Zero-shot `π_0.5` Performance:** On the ALOHA robot, `π_0.5` zero-shot exhibited strong instruction following (80% IF on `T_ES` picking, 70% on `T_NO` picking, 65-70% on `T_SS` pick-and-place) but consistently failed to grasp objects (0% SR across all tasks). This confirmed the existence of an embodiment gap requiring fine-tuning. For `T_SS` tasks, it showed pre-training behaviors like moving towards containers after an attempted pick but still failed to complete the task.

- **Impact of Expert-Only Fine-tuning (Multi-Task ES):** Fine-tuning with 30 expert demonstrations for "pick up red/green cube" tasks (Multi-Task ES) improved grasping on these specific tasks (65% SR). However, it led to catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained behaviors. For instance, on `T_SS` pick-and-place tasks, it showed 0% post-grasp IF and 0% SR, indicating it implicitly learned to stop after picking because expert demos ended there. Instruction following also degraded on `T_NO` tasks (from 70% zero-shot to 50% IF). Single-task fine-tuning showed even worse instruction following.

- **Effectiveness of Multi-Task ES+SS (Ours):** The proposed method, combining expert-supervised data with self-supervised generative rollouts (Multi-Task ES+SS), yielded substantial improvements:
 - **Improved Expert Task Performance:** Achieved 90% SR on the expert-demonstrated "pick up red/green cube" tasks and 55% SR on picking novel objects (`T_NO`), demonstrating effective embodiment gap closure and generalization.
 - **Distillation of Pre-trained Behaviors:** Crucially, it recovered the "place" behavior for pick-and-place tasks from the base policy's self-demonstrations, reaching 90% post-grasp IF and 55% SR on `T_SS` pick-and-place tasks, compared to 0% for Multi-Task ES. It also achieved 40% partial SR on the challenging laundry task.
 - **Enhanced Instruction Following:** Attained the best IF on `T_ES` and `T_SS` test sets, surpassing zero-shot performance in 5 out of 6 ALOHA test sets.
 - **Contact-Rich Bimanual Task (Caterpillar Gear Insertion - Table 3):** For this complex, out-of-distribution task, Multi-Task ES+SS dramatically improved SR on expert-demonstrated gear colors (`T_ES`) from 30% (Multi-Task ES) to 90%. It also achieved 30% SR on novel gear colors (`T_NO`), where baselines had 0% SR, indicating improved sample efficiency for new, complex skills.

#### 4.2. RoboTwin Simulation Experiments (Table 4 & 5)

- **Mid-Training Necessity:** `π_0.5` yielded 0% success in RoboTwin zero-shot, necessitating an initial "mid-training" phase on 10 diverse tasks to establish a "RoboTwin Base Policy" (90.8% average SR). This base policy was then used for post-training experiments, mimicking the real-world scenario where pre-training data is inaccessible.

- **Catastrophic Forgetting with Naive Multi-Task ES:** Similar to the real robot, fine-tuning only on two new expert stacking tasks (`T_ES`) caused catastrophic forgetting of the 10 mid-training tasks (`T_SS`), dropping average SR from 90.8% to 16.6%. While `T_ES` performance was good (93.0%), this highlights the memory retention challenge.

- **Mitigation of Forgetting with Multi-Task ES+SS (Ours):** Using self-supervision (Multi-Task ES+SS) by collecting rollouts from the RoboTwin base policy on `T_SS` tasks:
 - **Reduced Forgetting:** Recovered 54% of the forgotten `T_SS` performance, reaching 70.6% average SR, demonstrating effectiveness across diverse skills (hammering, opening microwaves, pick-and-place).
 - **Improved New Task Acquisition:** Also achieved peak plasticity on the new expert stacking tasks (`T_ES`) with 98% SR, surpassing the 93% of expert-only fine-tuning. This indicates self-supervision not only prevents forgetting but can also enhance learning of new tasks.

- **Oracle Comparisons:** The proposed method (70.6% on `T_SS`, 98% on `T_ES`) performed comparably to or better than several "oracle" baselines that assumed access to the stored mid-training expert data. For example, it outperformed "Rehearsal + LoRA, frame-wise (Oracle)" (84.6% on `T_SS`, 77% on `T_ES`) and was competitive with "Rehearsal, frame-wise (Oracle)" (85.6% on `T_SS`, 97% on `T_ES`), despite using only 10 self-generated episodes per task without stored original data.

#### 4.3. Discussion on Held-Out Skills (Table 7)

- When evaluated on a held-out skill family ("Push objects") not present in either expert or self-supervised fine-tuning data:
 - Multi-Task ES+SS preserved the "push" prior effectively, resulting in 70% push motion compared to only 10% for the full expert supervision baseline (MT ES+ES) and 35% for `π_0.5` zero-shot.
 - The MT ES+ES baseline, having been trained on all pick-and-place tasks, often defaulted to picking up the object even when instructed to push, suggesting it overwrites base policy priors with its trained action chains. This indicates that self-demos primarily distill *relevant* priors without overwriting unrelated ones.

In summary, the main findings indicate that self-supervised generative control effectively mitigates catastrophic forgetting of pre-trained skills and instruction following during fine-tuning on new robots. It also improves performance and sample efficiency for learning new tasks and helps retain unrelated, held-out skills, making it a viable approach for practical VLA deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing a critical practical challenge: adapting powerful pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to new robotic hardware without sacrificing their valuable generalist capabilities. The proposed self-supervised generative control method offers several key implications and potential impacts:

1. **Practical Deployment of Generalist Robots:** The work demonstrates a viable pathway for deploying pre-trained VLAs on diverse target robots, even when faced with embodiment gaps or minor hardware differences. This is crucial for making generalist robotic policies accessible and useful beyond their initial training environments, enabling wider adoption in various real-world applications.

2. **Mitigation of Catastrophic Forgetting:** Catastrophic forgetting has been a major impediment in continual learning for embodied AI. This research effectively demonstrates how self-generated data, derived from the base policy's own (often unsuccessful) rollouts on the target robot, can serve as a potent form of rehearsal to preserve instruction following, semantic understanding, and behavioral priors without requiring access to the original, often proprietary, pre-training datasets.

3. **Enhanced Sample Efficiency for New Skills:** The method not only preserves existing knowledge but also improves the sample efficiency of learning new, complex, and contact-rich tasks from limited expert demonstrations. This is particularly valuable in robotics, where collecting large amounts of high-quality expert data is costly and time-consuming.

4. **Leveraging Latent VLA Priors:** The core insight that even failing zero-shot VLA rollouts contain valuable semantic and behavioral priors, which can be distilled, highlights the latent potential of pre-trained models. This suggests that VLA pre-training is not merely for initialization but also for generating rich prior knowledge that can be selectively transferred and adapted.

5. **Robustness to Hardware Differences and Domain Gaps:** By generating rehearsal data directly on the target robot, the method inherently eliminates the domain gap between pre-training and deployment. This leads to policies that are more robust to variations in camera placement, gripper types, and scene configurations.

6. **Extensibility and Future Directions:** The data-based approach is potentially extensible to other robotic foundation models. It also opens several avenues for future research, including:
 - Investigating the specific mechanisms by which self-supervision helps (e.g., states traversed vs. action targets).
 - Automating the selection of self-supervised prompts and filtering of undesired behaviors in generated rollouts.
 - Exploring the integration of this approach with reinforcement learning for continuous self-improvement and robust physical grounding.
 - Developing "data flywheel" systems where progressively improving policies generate better self-demonstrations.

7. **Addressing Data Scarcity:** In scenarios where acquiring new expert demonstrations for every task is impractical or where original pre-training data is unavailable, this method provides a pragmatic solution for adapting and expanding a robot's capabilities. The surprising effectiveness of using noisy or failed self-demos further underscores its potential in data-constrained environments.

The main limitation acknowledged by the authors, such as manual curation of `P_SS` prompts and manual filtering of self-demos, presents opportunities for further automation to scale the approach. Despite these, the work represents a significant step towards making pre-trained VLAs more adaptable, reliable, and practical for the broader robotics community, reducing the dependence on resource-intensive, from-scratch pre-training.
