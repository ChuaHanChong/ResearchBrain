# Monocular Vision Based Control Framework for Grasping

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at TU Wien and DLR developed a unified monocular vision-based control framework that enables a standard robotic gripper to grasp both soft and rigid objects. The system integrates advanced vision models for detection, segmentation, tracking, and depth estimation with a language-based stiffness estimator (StiffNET) to adapt gripper control strategies in real-time, successfully performing pick-and-place operations for diverse items like lettuce, mozzarella, and plastic bottles.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation of diverse objects (e.g., deformable, rigid) is challenging, often requiring specialized sensors, explicit mechanics models, or custom gripper designs.
- Current solutions struggle with the high degrees of freedom, variable material properties, and lack of ground-truth data for deformable objects.
- Specialized sensors (e.g., tactile) and grippers limit versatility and increase system cost and complexity.

## Method

- A unified monocular vision-based framework that uses only RGB camera input and a standard position-controlled gripper, inspired by human adaptability.
- Integrates open-vocabulary object detection (==YOLOv8x-worldv2==), segmentation (==SAM2==), point tracking (==TAPIR==), and monocular depth estimation (==Depth Anything==) to derive visual feedback.
- Employs a language-based stiffness estimation network (**StiffNET**) to predict object compliance from semantic descriptions, guiding the selection between deformable and rigid grasping modes.
- Utilizes adaptive gripper width control based on visual cues: ==dissimilarity== of tracked points for deformable objects and ==scaling factor== for rigid objects.

## Results

- StiffNET provided stiffness predictions numerically close to actual values for various objects, demonstrating its ability to infer physical properties from semantic descriptions.
- Successfully performed pick-and-place operations for highly deformable objects (e.g., lettuce, mozzarella, croissants) by dynamically adjusting gripper width based on tracked point dissimilarity.
- Achieved stable grasping of rigid objects (e.g., hard plastic bottles) by proportionally controlling gripper width based on the scaling factor of tracked points.
- The system operated in real-time, achieving **30 frames per second** on an Nvidia RTX A4000 GPU with **four** grid points for tracking.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining state-of-the-art monocular vision and language models can create a sensor-efficient and generalizable robotic grasping system without relying on specialized hardware or explicit physical models.
> - Visual proxies like changes in object shape (dissimilarity of tracked points) and object-camera distance (scaling factor) can effectively inform real-time adaptive gripper control for diverse object properties.
> - A language-based prior on object stiffness (StiffNET) provides valuable pre-contact information, enabling the system to select appropriate grasping strategies for both soft and rigid items.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The manipulation of diverse objects, particularly deformable items, is a significant challenge in robotics, with implications for industries such as food processing and home automation. Existing robotic grasping approaches often address different categories of objects (e.g., soft vs. rigid) separately. Many rely on specialized sensing modalities like tactile sensors, explicit mechanics-based object models, or custom gripper designs.

Traditional force-based methods are frequently inadequate for deformable objects due to their high degrees of freedom and variable material properties, which are not captured by such methods. Vision-based tactile sensors offer improved precision but face limitations with objects softer than the sensor's elastomer, degradation over time, and substantial cost. Mechanics-based models, often utilizing Finite Element Analysis (FEA), can be effective but require accurate material properties, which are challenging to obtain for real-world, time-varying, and nonlinear deformable objects like food products. Specialized compliant end-effectors, such as soft grippers, excel at handling delicate items but typically lack the force generation capabilities required for rigid objects, thus limiting their versatility.

Prior work has also explored leveraging large language models (LLMs) to infer object interaction properties from semantic descriptions, but these often assume force-controllable grippers with additional sensing. Table I in the paper provides a comparative overview, highlighting that representative prior approaches derive robustness from tactile instrumentation, explicit mechanics models, or specialized end-effector designs.

This work positions itself within this landscape by seeking to overcome the limitations of these specialized approaches. It proposes a unified monocular vision-based framework that aims to handle both soft and rigid objects using only RGB input and a standard position-controlled gripper. The framework draws inspiration from human adaptability, where individuals use the same hands for diverse objects by integrating visual and semantic knowledge. By minimizing sensory requirements and avoiding explicit physical models or specialized hardware, this research aims to offer a more sensor-efficient, practical, and generalizable solution for robotic manipulation in unstructured environments. It integrates advancements in computer vision, such as open-vocabulary object detection, segmentation, point tracking, and monocular depth estimation, to enable adaptive grasping across a wide range of everyday items.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified monocular vision-based grasping framework capable of handling both soft/deformable and rigid objects within a single control pipeline. This framework aims to operate using only RGB camera input and a standard, position-controlled gripper, thereby minimizing the need for specialized sensors, object-specific models, or customized hardware.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and limitations observed in existing robotic grasping systems:

- **Diverse Object Properties:** Real-world environments, particularly in food handling and household settings, present objects with a wide spectrum of mechanical properties, from highly compliant (e.g., lettuce, mozzarella) to rigid (e.g., plastic bottles). Most current robotic grasping solutions are specialized for one category or rely on complex, category-specific approaches.
- **Limitations of Specialized Sensors:**
 - **Tactile Sensors:** While beneficial for precision, vision-based tactile sensors often struggle with objects softer than their elastomers, experience performance degradation over time due to wear, and incur substantial costs for initial purchase and maintenance. The authors aim to eliminate the dependency on such sensors.
 - **Explicit Mechanics Models:** Approaches based on Finite Element Analysis (FEA) or other mechanics models require accurate material properties. These properties are often difficult to obtain in real-world scenarios, especially for deformable objects which exhibit time-varying and nonlinear behaviors (e.g., food products changing with age or moisture content). The framework seeks to avoid explicit physical modeling.
- **Limitations of Specialized Grippers:** Soft, compliant, or variable-stiffness grippers are effective for delicate objects but typically lack the force necessary to manipulate rigid items, thus limiting their generalizability. The goal is to achieve versatility with a standard, position-controlled gripper.
- **Inspiration from Human Manipulation:** Humans adeptly handle both deformable and rigid objects using the same hands by seamlessly integrating visual and, when available, tactile feedback. Furthermore, humans leverage semantic knowledge (e.g., "soft" vs. "stiff") to anticipate object properties. This human adaptability motivates the development of a robot framework that similarly relies on visual feedback and semantic priors to simplify sensory requirements and enhance applicability.
- **Leveraging Computer Vision Advancements:** Recent breakthroughs in computer vision, such as open-vocabulary object detection (YOLOv8x-worldv2), transformer-based image segmentation (SAM2), real-time point tracking (TAPIR, CoTracker), and monocular depth estimation (Depth Anything), offer the potential to recover object motion and geometry from visual observations alone. The authors aim to integrate these advanced computer vision techniques to provide the necessary visual feedback for adaptive grasping.

In summary, the core motivation is to achieve sensor-efficient, practical, and generalizable robotic grasping by developing a unified framework that combines state-of-the-art monocular vision with semantic priors from language, thereby enabling a standard gripper to adapt its strategy for both deformable and rigid objects without relying on costly specialized sensors, complex physical models, or custom end-effectors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates several computer vision and control components to achieve unified grasping. An overview of the system is depicted in Figure 1.

**A. Visual Perception Pipeline:**
1. **Object Detection & Segmentation:**
 - An input RGB image frame (I) and a textual prompt (p) describing the object of interest are fed into the YOLOv8x-worldv2 model for real-time open-vocabulary object detection.
 - The detection output provides bounding box coordinates, from which a center coordinate (o\_c) is derived. This coordinate, with a user-defined constant (c), helps select segments near the bounding box edge.
 - The coordinate o\_c is then passed to Meta SAM2 (Segment Anything Model) for image segmentation, generating a binary mask (M) of the object of interest.
2. **Boundary-Aware Point Assignment:**
 - Contours are extracted from the binary mask M, and the largest contour (C) is selected.
 - All points (P) within the mask are identified.
 - For each point p ∈ P, its distance d(p) to the nearest contour point is calculated.
 - A weight w(p) inversely proportional to d(p) is assigned (w(p) = 1 / (1 + d(p))), emphasizing points closer to the contour.
 - A weighted k-means clustering algorithm is applied to partition P into k clusters, where k is a user-defined number of desired grid points. The cluster centers (G) serve as query points for tracking. The objective function minimizes weighted within-cluster variance, and cluster centers are updated using weighted means.
 - After clustering, the framework verifies that all grid points g\_i ∈ G lie within the mask M. If not, they are discarded. If the number of valid points is less than k, new points are added by finding the farthest pair of existing points and adding their midpoint (or the closest valid point within P). This process ensures distributed points with a denser concentration near the object's boundary, improving tracking robustness, particularly for low-texture objects.
3. **Point Tracking and Depth Estimation:**
 - The generated grid points (G) serve as input query points for the TAPIR (Tracking Any Point with per-frame Initialization and Temporal Refinement) model, which tracks these points in 2D across video frames (G\_t).
 - The Depth Anything model is then used for monocular depth estimation, converting the 2D tracked points into 3D tracking points in space. Both TAPIR and Depth Anything operate in real-time.
 - Depth can also be inferred using a scaling factor, assuming the object remains in the same reference plane, or enhanced by Depth Anything's relative depth estimates for 3D orientation.

**B. Language-Based Stiffness Estimation (StiffNET):**
This component provides a prior on object compliance before contact.
1. **Text Representation:** An object's text description (x) is mapped to a normalized semantic embedding z(x) using a frozen CLIP text encoder.
2. **Stiffness Network:** A neural network f\_θ(z(x)) + b\_θ predicts the log-stiffness (ℓ̂(x)). Predicted stiffness k̂(x) is obtained by exponentiating ℓ̂(x). Predicting log-stiffness addresses numerical conditioning for values spanning multiple orders of magnitude.
3. **Training Data:** StiffNET is trained using two sources:
 - **Pairwise Hardness Comparisons (D\_P):** A dataset of object pairs (x\_p^(1), x\_p^(2)) with an indicator (y\_p ∈ {+1, -1}) of which is harder, and an annotation confidence (c\_p). This dataset is generated by prompting a Large Language Model (LLM, specifically Gemma 3) to compare everyday objects and provide confidence scores. This provides relative ordering supervision.
 - **Sparse Ground-Truth Stiffness Measurements (D\_G):** A dataset of objects (x\_q) with measured stiffness values (k\_q^* > 0). This provides absolute scale supervision.
4. **Auxiliary Margin Network:** An auxiliary neural network h\_ϕ is introduced during training to predict a pair-specific margin score (m̂\_p) for the pairwise comparisons. This allows the ranking margin to adapt to the semantic content of the pair and known stiffness differences.
5. **Loss Functions:**
 - **Ranking Loss (L\_rank):** A hinge loss is applied in log-space to enforce correct relative ordering based on pairwise comparisons, pushing harder objects upward and softer objects downward on the learned log-stiffness axis.
 - **Regression Loss (L\_reg):** A Huber loss is used to pull the predicted log-stiffness towards the measured ground-truth values, determining the absolute scale.
6. **Joint Training:** The stiffness network f\_θ is trained jointly by both L\_rank and L\_reg (L = λ\_rank L\_rank + λ\_reg L\_reg), ensuring it learns both relative order and absolute scale. The auxiliary margin network h\_ϕ is updated only through L\_rank.

**C. Grasp Control:**
StiffNET's estimated stiffness (k̂) is compared against a threshold (k\_th) to select the appropriate grasping mode:
- **Deformable/Compliant Mode (k̂ < k\_th):**
 - **Procrustes Analysis:** Dissimilarity (D\_t) between initial grid points (G\_0) and current 3D tracked points (G\_t) is computed. G\_0 and G\_t are centered and normalized. Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) is used to find optimal rotation (R) and scaling (s). D\_t quantifies the dissimilarity, serving as a visual proxy for deformation and, implicitly, applied grasp force. A sensitivity gain (Γ) is applied.
 - **Scaling Factor (s\_f\_t):** The ratio of the pairwise distance sum of current tracked points to initial tracked points provides s\_f\_t, which captures changes in object-camera distance.
 - **Gripper Width Control:** The commanded gripper width (W\_t+1) is adjusted based on the difference between a reference dissimilarity (D\_ref\_t) and the current dissimilarity (D\_t). D\_ref\_t is dynamically set as D\_min + Ω(1 - s\_f\_t), where D\_min is a minimum dissimilarity (potentially user-defined or derived from initial trials), and Ω is a scaling factor gain. If the object begins to slip, s\_f\_t decreases, increasing D\_ref\_t, which causes the gripper to close until slipping stops and a stable grasp is achieved.
- **Rigid Mode (k̂ ≥ k\_th):**
 - The gripper width (W\_t) is directly controlled by the scaling factor (s\_f\_t) using a linear mapping: W\_t = (s\_f\_t - s\_f\_min) \* (W\_max - W\_min) / (1 - s\_f\_min) + W\_min. W\_max and W\_min are the gripper's maximum and minimum widths, and s\_f\_min is a user-defined minimum scaling factor. This allows the gripper width to adapt until the scaling factor stabilizes, indicating a secure grasp.

The entire system operates in real-time, integrating these components to enable adaptive and stable grasping across diverse object types.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of the proposed framework was conducted through a series of real-world grasping experiments using a Franka Emika Research 3 robotic arm equipped with a Franka Hand and 3D-printed PLA finger extensions with foam cushions. A RAZER Kiyo-X generic webcam provided visual input. The models were deployed on an Nvidia RTX A4000 GPU, achieving 30 frames per second with four grid points (k=4).

**A. StiffNET Evaluation:**
- A preliminary comparison of Young's modulus estimates (GPa) from GPT-5.3 and StiffNET against true values was presented in Table II.
- StiffNET demonstrated an ability to produce stiffness predictions that are numerically close to actual values for objects like Tofu, Wooden Block, Steel, and PVC. For objects without reported true values (Cucumber, Walnut, Scissors, Power Bank, Plastic Bottle), StiffNET provided plausible estimates, generally within the expected range, showing a capability to infer physical properties from semantic descriptions.

**B. Deformable Object Grasping (Lettuce):**
- The system was prompted with "green vegetables" for object detection.
- Control parameters were set as k\_th = 0.1, D\_min = 10, Ω = 500, λ = 7 × 10^-6, Γ = 10^4, and c = 5. A safety factor of 7.5 was applied to D\_min for robust dynamic cases.
- During the robot's upward motion, the scaling factor (s\_f\_t) decreased, indicating the object was moving away from the camera. This reduction in s\_f\_t led to an increase in the reference dissimilarity (D\_ref\_t).
- The gripper controller, in response, decreased the commanded finger width (W\_t+1) to match the increasing D\_ref\_t.
- This adaptive adjustment continued until the lettuce was grasped, at which point the scaling factor converged, and the gripper width stabilized.
- Figure 4 illustrates this process, showing the decrease in scaling factor and subsequent adjustment of finger width, leading to stabilization after grasping.

**C. Multi-Object Deformable Grasping:**
- The framework successfully performed pick-and-place operations for a variety of challenging deformable objects, including fresh mozzarella cheese, croissants, and paper towels (Figure 3).
- For each object, the system initially adjusted to a desired dissimilarity (which could be pre-determined by an initial upward movement trial where dissimilarity stabilizes), and then maintained this desired state through adaptive gripper width control.
- The system demonstrated that the finger width adapts accordingly to ensure desirable tracking performance and stable grasps, even for objects with non-homogeneous (lettuce, croissants) or slippery (mozzarella) properties.
- The results indicated that while measured dissimilarity could vary across trials and objects due to property variations, the estimated stiffness was primarily used for control-mode selection rather than direct quantitative feedback.

**D. Rigid Object Grasping (Hard Plastic Bottle):**
- For rigid object manipulation, specific parameters were used: s\_f\_min = 0.85, W\_max = 0.08 m, and W\_min = 0.01 m.
- The experiment started with the gripper fully open. As the robot moved upward, the distance between the camera and the bottle's tracking points increased, causing the scaling factor (s\_f\_t) to decrease.
- The rigid object control strategy, using Equation 29, commanded a narrower finger width proportionally to the decreasing scaling factor.
- Once the bottle was securely grasped, the relative distance between the bottle and the camera became fixed, leading to the stabilization of both the scaling factor and the commanded finger width.
- Figure 5 visually demonstrates the robot's upward motion, the corresponding reduction in the scaling factor, and the subsequent stabilization of both scaling factor and finger width after a successful grasp.

In summary, the experiments demonstrated that the proposed unified monocular vision-based framework, combining semantic priors from StiffNET with visual feedback from point tracking and depth estimation, successfully achieves stable pick-and-place operations for both highly deformable (lettuce, mozzarella, croissant, paper towel) and rigid (plastic bottle) objects using a standard position-controlled gripper. The system adapts its gripper width based on visual cues (dissimilarity for deformable, scaling factor for rigid) to maintain secure grasps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The presented monocular vision-based control framework represents a significant advancement in robotic manipulation by addressing the challenge of grasping diverse objects with varying mechanical properties using a unified, sensor-efficient approach. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Unified Grasping for Diverse Objects:** The framework provides a single control pipeline that effectively handles both highly compliant/deformable and rigid objects. This unification contrasts with many existing methods that require separate strategies, specialized hardware, or complex models for each object type, thereby increasing the versatility of robotic systems.
- **Sensor-Efficient Design:** By relying solely on RGB camera input and a standard position-controlled gripper, the system eliminates the need for expensive, specialized sensors like vision-based tactile sensors or force/torque sensors. This reduces system complexity, cost, and maintenance requirements, making robotic grasping more accessible and practical for a wider range of applications.
- **Reduced Dependency on Physical Models:** The approach avoids explicit mechanics-based object modeling (e.g., FEA), which often requires precise, difficult-to-obtain, and time-varying material properties. Instead, it leverages semantic priors from language (StiffNET) for pre-contact strategy selection and real-time visual feedback for adaptive control, simplifying deployment in unstructured environments where object properties are unknown or change dynamically.
- **Leveraging AI Advancements:** The integration of state-of-the-art computer vision models (YOLOv8x-worldv2, SAM2, TAPIR, Depth Anything) and large language models (for StiffNET training) showcases how recent advancements in AI can be effectively combined to create robust robotic capabilities. This demonstrates a pathway for future robotic systems to intelligently interpret and interact with their environment based on high-level semantic information and low-level visual data.
- **Practical Applications in Food Handling and Household Automation:** The successful demonstration of grasping challenging objects like lettuce, mozzarella cheese, croissants, and hard plastic bottles highlights the framework's direct applicability in domains requiring delicate yet versatile manipulation. This includes automated food processing, kitchen assistance, and general household tasks, where robots need to handle a variety of items without causing damage.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a robust baseline for vision-based adaptive grasping. It opens avenues for future research into integrating more sophisticated visual cues, learning adaptive grasping parameters (e.g., D\_min, Ω, λ) online, and extending the framework to dynamic manipulation tasks or environments with greater uncertainty. The human-inspired approach of combining semantic priors with visual feedback could also inform the development of more intuitive and adaptable robotic intelligence.

In conclusion, this research presents a practical, cost-effective, and generalizable solution for robotic grasping that significantly enhances the capabilities of robots in unstructured, everyday environments by enabling them to handle a wide spectrum of objects with a minimalist sensor setup.
