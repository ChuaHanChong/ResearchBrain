# LASeR: Learning to Adaptively Select Reward Models with Multi-Armed Bandits

> [!summary] Summary
> LASeR (Learning to Adaptively Select Reward models) introduces a framework that uses Multi-Armed Bandits to dynamically select the most suitable reward model for each batch during iterative LLM training. This adaptive approach improved LLM alignment, achieving higher accuracy on reasoning tasks, better instruction-following capabilities, and up to 3x faster training compared to single-RM and ensemble baselines.

## Problem

- Single, fixed Reward Models (RMs) often lack generalization across diverse tasks, are prone to 'reward hacking,' and can provide unreliable feedback during LLM alignment.
- Ensemble-based RM approaches are computationally prohibitive due to the need to run multiple large models simultaneously and can suffer from conflicting preference signals.
- Manually selecting optimal RMs or designing effective aggregation strategies for ensembles is a labor-intensive and inefficient process.

## Method

- ==LASeR== frames the problem of selecting an RM from a pool of candidates as a contextual ==Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB)== problem, primarily utilizing the ==LinUCB algorithm==.
- It dynamically selects a single RM for each mini-batch during iterative LLM fine-tuning (e.g., DPO) and uses its feedback to update the LLM.
- The MAB's reward signal is defined as the ==negative normalized training loss== of the LLM from that batch, enabling it to learn which RMs provide the most informative guidance for different contexts.

## Results

- LASeR achieved an absolute average accuracy of **76.32%** on reasoning benchmarks (StrategyQA, GSM8K, MMLU) with Llama-3-8B, outperforming the 'Best RM' baseline by **1.45%** and the 'RM Score Ensemble' by **2.67%**.
- It demonstrated superior AlpacaEval win rates on instruction-following tasks, significantly beating 'Random RM Selection' (**78.33%**) and 'RM Score Ensemble' (**72.69%**) baselines.
- The method provided a **2x** to **3x** speedup in wall-clock training time compared to ensemble and sequential baselines while delivering higher performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically selecting a single, context-appropriate RM per batch mitigates the computational overhead and conflicting signals inherent in traditional ensemble methods.
> - The MAB effectively balances exploration of different RMs with exploitation of those performing well, adapting its selection strategy as the LLM policy evolves.
> - Using the downstream LLM's training loss as the MAB's reward provides a robust and direct signal for assessing the quality and utility of a selected RM in a given context.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper "LASeR" addresses a critical challenge in the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Model (LLM) alignment: effectively leveraging reward models (RMs) to guide LLMs toward desired behaviors, particularly those aligned with human preferences. This area is dominated by techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), where RMs serve as crucial proxies for human judgment.

The broader research landscape for LLM alignment is characterized by a drive to improve model performance, generalization, and safety, often through iterative learning processes. Early approaches relied on static, human-annotated preference datasets and single, fixed RMs. However, the community quickly recognized the limitations of this paradigm:
1. **Generalization:** A single RM, often specialized to a narrow task or domain, struggles to generalize to the vast and heterogeneous array of real-world queries and preferences (e.g., an RM for creative writing may not excel at evaluating math solutions).
2. **Reliability:** RM leaderboards and preference data themselves can be noisy, ambiguous, or contain biases, leading to unreliable rankings and potential misalignment.
3. **Reward Hacking:** Over-optimization on a single, potentially imperfect RM can lead to "reward hacking," where the LLM learns to exploit flaws in the RM rather than truly fulfilling the underlying objective, resulting in performance degradation.

To mitigate these issues, researchers explored using ensembles of multiple RMs. While ensembles can offer more robust preference signals, they introduce their own set of significant challenges:
1. **Computational Cost:** RMs are often LLMs themselves, making it computationally expensive to load and run multiple large models simultaneously for every generation and scoring step.
2. **Conflicting Signals:** Aggregating scores from multiple RMs can be problematic if RMs have conflicting preferences, especially for a given instance where some RMs might be ill-suited. This can degrade the quality of the synthetic preference data used for training.
3. **Manual Selection:** Manually selecting and combining RMs for optimal performance across diverse tasks is a labor-intensive, often heuristic, process.

LASeR directly tackles these shortcomings by proposing an **adaptive, instance-level RM selection strategy within an iterative LLM training framework**. It distinguishes itself from prior work by:
- **Moving beyond static RM selection:** Unlike using a "best" fixed RM or uniform sampling.
- **Addressing ensemble limitations:** By selecting *one* RM per instance/batch, it avoids the computational overhead of simultaneously running multiple RMs and mitigates the issue of conflicting signals.
- **Novel application of MABs:** While Multi-Armed Bandits (MABs) have been used in machine learning for exploration-exploitation trade-offs (e.g., hyperparameter optimization, sample selection), LASeR uniquely applies MABs to dynamically select *which reward model to use* during the iterative fine-tuning of an LLM. This is a crucial distinction, as the "arms" (RMs) are not static but interact with and influence the learning policy of the LLM itself, creating a dynamic reward landscape.

In essence, LASeR fits into the landscape by offering a more intelligent, efficient, and robust way to harness the power of diverse reward signals for LLM alignment, bridging the gap between the simplicity of single RMs and the complexity/inefficiency of naive RM ensembles.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind LASeR stems from the inherent limitations of current LLM alignment strategies that rely on Reward Models. The authors identify several critical problems:

**Motivations related to single, fixed Reward Models:**
1. **Lack of Generalization:** A single RM, typically trained on specific datasets or for particular objectives, often fails to generalize effectively to heterogeneous tasks or domains. For instance, an RM optimized for creative writing might be unsuitable for evaluating mathematical reasoning, leading to misaligned LLM outputs across different applications.
2. **Unreliable Rankings:** Existing RM leaderboards, which often rely on human-annotated preferences, can be prone to noise, ambiguity, and biases in the preference data itself. This makes selecting a single "best" RM a precarious endeavor, as its perceived quality might not translate reliably to new tasks or contexts.
3. **Reward Hacking:** Over-optimization on a solitary RM, particularly if it has imperfections, can lead to "reward hacking." The LLM might learn to produce responses that score highly according to the RM but do not genuinely reflect human preferences or task objectives, ultimately degrading downstream performance.

**Motivations related to ensemble-based Reward Model approaches:**
1. **Prohibitive Computational Cost:** Many RMs are themselves large language models. Simultaneously loading and managing multiple such RMs during iterative LLM training incurs a significantly high computational cost, making these methods infeasible as models continue to grow in size.
2. **Conflicting Signals:** Aggregating scores from multiple RMs, especially through simple averaging, can lead to conflicting preference signals. If some RMs are poorly suited for a specific task instance, their feedback can introduce noise or even contradictory guidance, degrading the quality of the generated preference data and hindering effective LLM updates.
3. **Labor-Intensive Manual Tuning:** Manually selecting a subset of RMs or designing complex aggregation strategies requires extensive human effort and experimentation across a combinatorially large space of possibilities, which is unsustainable and inefficient.

**The overarching objective of LASeR is to address these challenges by introducing an adaptive and efficient mechanism for Reward Model selection.** Specifically, the paper aims to:
- **Dynamically Select the Most Suitable RM:** For each specific instance or batch of queries, LASeR seeks to identify and utilize the RM from a given pool that is best-suited to provide informative feedback, thereby overcoming generalization issues.
- **Boost Iterative LLM Training:** By providing a more refined and context-aware reward signal, LASeR intends to improve the overall performance and alignment of LLMs during iterative fine-tuning processes.
- **Ensure Efficiency:** The method aims to achieve these improvements without incurring the high computational costs associated with simultaneously running multiple large RMs.
- **Mitigate Conflicting Signals:** By selecting only one RM at a time, LASeR inherently avoids the direct aggregation of potentially conflicting signals from multiple RMs.
- **Reduce User Burden:** The goal is for users to simply provide a pool of RMs without needing prior knowledge of which RM is best for a given task, as LASeR handles the selection automatically.

By framing this adaptive selection as a multi-armed bandit problem, the authors aim to leverage established exploration-exploitation trade-offs to efficiently learn and adapt RM choices, thereby fulfilling these objectives.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LASeR (Learning to Adaptively Select Rewards) introduces a novel methodology that integrates a Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) framework into an iterative LLM training pipeline. The core idea is to dynamically select the most appropriate Reward Model (RM) for each instance or batch of queries, optimizing both the LLM's performance and training efficiency.

The methodology can be broken down into three interconnected stages within an iterative training loop:

#### 3.1. Training LLMs Using a Reward Function (Single-RM Paradigm)

Before detailing the MAB integration, LASeR first establishes a standard single-RM iterative training pipeline, which forms the basis for how the *selected* RM is used:
- **Iterative LLM Training:** The LLM, denoted as $\pi^m$ at iteration $m$, is fine-tuned over $M$ iterations, starting from an initial pretrained model $\pi^0$.
- **Response Generation:** For each input query $x_i$ from a training dataset $D$, the current LLM $\pi^m$ generates a set of $n$ responses $y_i = \{y_i^1, \dots, y_i^n\}$.
- **Reward Function Application:** A chosen reward function $R^\star(\cdot)$ (which, in LASeR, will be the dynamically selected RM) scores each generated response $y_i^j$ based on its alignment with task objectives or instructions.
- **Preference Pair Generation:** By comparing the scores assigned by $R^\star$, preference pairs $(y_i^w, y_i^l)$ are formed, where $y_i^w$ is preferred over $y_i^l$ (i.e., $R^\star(y_i^w|x_i) > R^\star(y_i^l|x_i)$). This creates a preference dataset $D_{pref}$.
- **LLM Training Loss:** The LLM $\pi^m$ is then updated using the generated preference dataset $D_{pref}$. The Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) loss is primarily used, defined as $L_m^{DPO}(\pi^m) = -E_{D_{pref}}[\log \sigma(\beta(r_i^w - r_i^l))]$, where $r_i^w$ and $r_i^l$ are log-ratios of probabilities for preferred and dispreferred responses. For reasoning tasks, an additional Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL) loss on preferred responses ($L_m^{NLL}$) is added as a regularizer.

#### 3.2. Bandit Algorithms for Adaptive RM Selection

This is the core innovation of LASeR, where the MAB dynamically chooses $R^\star$ for each batch:
- **Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) Formulation:** The problem of selecting an RM from a pool of $K$ candidate RMs ($\mathcal{R} = \{R_1, \dots, R_K\}$) is framed as a contextual MAB problem. Each RM $R_k$ is an "arm."
- **Contextual Information:** For each batch of prompts $x_{m,t}$ at training step $t$ of iteration $m$, a "context" $c(t)$ is derived. This context is the mean sentence embedding of the prompts in the batch, obtained by passing the prompts through the current policy LLM $\pi^m$ and taking the last token embedding.
- **LinUCB Algorithm:** LASeR primarily employs the LinUCB algorithm, a contextual bandit known for its efficiency and effective balance between exploration and exploitation. LinUCB models the expected MAB reward as a linear function of the context features and uses an upper confidence bound (UCB) strategy to select arms, ensuring both utilization of well-performing RMs and continued exploration of less-tested ones. The selected RM $R^\star_t$ for step $t$ is chosen based on:
 $j = \text{arg max}_{k \in [1,K]} [c(t)^\top \hat{\theta}_k + \alpha \sqrt{c(t)^\top A_k^{-1} c(t)}]$
 where $\hat{\theta}_k$ are learned weights, $A_k$ is a covariance matrix, and $\alpha$ controls exploration.
- **MAB Reward Design:** A critical aspect is how the MAB reward is defined. Unlike traditional MABs with direct rewards, LASeR defines the MAB reward as the **negative normalized training loss** (e.g., $-L_m^{DPO}$) achieved by the LLM after being fine-tuned using preference pairs generated by the *selected RM* for that batch. A lower LLM training loss (i.e., a higher negative loss, which implies a higher MAB reward) signifies that the chosen RM provided more informative and consistent rankings, enabling the LLM to better distinguish between preferred and dispreferred responses. This reward signal dynamically updates the MAB's parameters ($A_k$, $b_k$).

#### 3.3. LLM and Bandit Training in LASeR (Iterative Loop)

The entire process operates iteratively:
1. At each training step (mini-batch) $t$ within an iteration $m$:
 - The MAB, using LinUCB and the context $c(t)$, selects an RM $R^\star_t$.
 - The current LLM $\pi^m$ generates responses for the batch $x_{m,t}$.
 - The selected RM $R^\star_t$ scores these responses, and preference pairs are created.
 - The LLM $\pi^m$ is fine-tuned using these preference pairs, resulting in a loss $L_m(t)$.
 - The MAB's parameters are updated using the normalized negative training loss ($-L_m(t)$) as its reward signal.
2. This loop repeats for a total of $M$ iterations, with the LLM progressively improving and the MAB continually refining its RM selection policy.

#### 3.4. LASeR with Best-of-n Sampling (Inference-Time Application)

For scenarios where LLM fine-tuning is computationally prohibitive (e.g., long-context tasks), LASeR can be adapted for inference-time reranking:
- Instead of fine-tuning the LLM, the MAB learns its parameters by using the negative NLL loss on training data as its reward.
- The trained MAB then selects an RM for a given test query.
- Multiple responses are generated by a base LLM, and the selected RM reranks them, choosing the "best" response (best-of-n sampling). This avoids LLM training costs while still leveraging adaptive RM selection.

This comprehensive methodology allows LASeR to dynamically adapt the reward signal to the context, improve training efficiency by using only one RM at a time, and mitigate the issues of generalization and conflicting preferences that plague traditional single-RM or ensemble approaches.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

LASeR demonstrates robust and consistent improvements across a variety of tasks, LLM sizes, and evaluation metrics, validating its adaptive RM selection strategy.

#### 4.1. Performance on Reasoning Tasks

- **Superior Accuracy:** On reasoning benchmarks (StrategyQA, GSM8K, MMLU) with Llama-3-8B, LASeR achieved the highest absolute average accuracy of 76.32% across the three datasets.
- **Outperforms Single RM Baselines:** It improved over the "Best RM" (Zephyr-7B-Alpha, the top-performing RM on RewardBench) by an average of 1.45% accuracy and surpassed the "Avg. RM" baseline by 2.7%.
- **Outperforms Multi-RM Ensemble Baselines:** Crucially, LASeR outperformed the "RM Score Ensemble" baseline by 2.67% average accuracy and the more sophisticated "RM Agreement Ensemble" by 0.91%. This highlights LASeR's ability to navigate conflicting signals more effectively than direct aggregation methods.
- **Consistency:** The results show LASeR's consistent gains, while second-place baselines often exhibit inconsistent performance across datasets.

#### 4.2. Performance on Instruction-Following Tasks

- **High AlpacaEval Win Rates:** On the WildChat dataset (open-ended instruction-following), LASeR-trained Llama-3-8B models consistently beat baselines as measured by length-controlled AlpacaEval win rates (using GPT-4o as a judge).
- **Adaptive Selection Advantage:** LASER achieved significantly higher win rates against single-RM baselines like "Random RM Selection" (78.33%) and "Sequential RM Selection" (71.45%).
- **Ensemble Baseline Superiority:** It also outperformed ensemble methods, achieving a 72.69% win rate against the "RM Score Ensemble" and 67.88% against the "RM Online Ensemble," suggesting its selective approach is better at handling diverse, real-world user queries.

#### 4.3. Performance on Long-Context Understanding

- **Effective Reranking:** For long-context tasks from LongBench (single-document QA, multi-document QA, summarization, few-shot learning), where fine-tuning is costly, LASeR was applied to learn MAB parameters and then used for best-of-n sampling (reranking responses at inference time).
- **Consistent Gains:** LASER consistently outperformed baselines across these tasks. For example, it improved single-document QA by 2.96 F1 points and few-shot learning by 2.97 F1 points over the "RM Score Ensemble" baseline.

#### 4.4. Efficiency and Robustness

- **Training Efficiency:** LASeR demonstrated superior training efficiency in terms of wall-clock time. On GSM8K, it was 3x faster than "Sequential RM Selection" and 2x faster than "RM Score Ensemble," while achieving higher accuracy. This is attributed to selecting only one RM per mini-batch, reducing GPU memory and computational overhead.
- **Mitigation of Conflicting Signals:** Analysis of RM preference agreement revealed significant disagreements between different RMs (F1 scores as low as 0.43 on MMLU and 0.48 on WildChat). LASeR's selection strategy inherently mitigates the negative impact of such conflicts, explaining its superior performance over ensemble methods that aggregate conflicting signals.
- **Adaptive RM Utilization:** LASeR's MAB dynamically adjusted RM selection based on query type. For example, on WildChat, it favored Olmo and Eurus RMs for creative writing but Qwen RM for math-related queries, aligning with the RMs' known strengths. Similar adaptive patterns were observed on LongBench.
- **Generalization and Robustness (from Appendix):**
 - **Statistical Significance:** LASER consistently outperformed baselines over multiple random seeds, with low standard deviations, indicating statistical stability.
 - **MAB Reward Convergence:** MAB rewards stabilized over training iterations, with more effective RMs retaining higher MAB rewards, confirming the learning process.
 - **Cold-Start Training:** A MAB policy trained on one dataset could be successfully reused for cold-start training on another, demonstrating transferability.
 - **Robustness to Noise:** LASeR maintained robust performance even when significant Gaussian noise was added to RM scores, outperforming baselines which saw larger accuracy drops. It also showed robustness to irrelevant evaluation metrics.
 - **Generalization to LLMs and RMs:** Performance gains were maintained when using different base LLMs (e.g., Mistral-7B, Qwen2.5-32B) and varying the number and diversity of RMs in the pool.
 - **Loss Function Agnostic:** LASeR's benefits were observed across different underlying LLM training loss functions (NLL, DPO, KTO).
 - **Inclusion of Correctness Rewards:** LASeR could effectively integrate correctness-based RMs, adaptively leveraging them for tasks where they are applicable (e.g., GSM8K) while downweighting them for unsuitable tasks (e.g., WildChat).

In summary, LASeR's adaptive selection mechanism consistently yields better LLM alignment, improved efficiency, and greater robustness compared to both single-RM and ensemble-based baselines across a broad spectrum of tasks and models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LASeR framework presents a significant advancement in the field of Large Language Model (LLM) alignment, offering a practical and theoretically grounded solution to several persistent challenges. Its potential impact spans both research and practical applications:

#### 5.1. Enhanced LLM Alignment and Generalization

By adaptively selecting the most suitable Reward Model (RM) for a given query or batch, LASeR allows LLMs to receive more precise and context-aware feedback during training. This directly addresses the critical issues of RM generalization (a single RM failing across diverse tasks) and reward hacking (over-optimization on a flawed RM). The empirical results demonstrate that LASeR leads to consistently higher accuracy on reasoning tasks, superior instruction-following capabilities, and improved performance in long-context understanding. This means LLMs trained with LASeR are likely to be better aligned with diverse human preferences and more capable across a wider range of real-world scenarios.

#### 5.2. Significant Computational Efficiency Gains

One of LASeR's most impactful contributions is its ability to achieve superior performance while simultaneously reducing computational costs. By selecting and utilizing only one RM per mini-batch, it circumvents the prohibitive memory and compute overhead associated with ensemble methods that require loading and evaluating multiple large, LLM-based RMs simultaneously. The demonstrated 2x to 3x speedup compared to ensemble and sequential baselines makes advanced LLM alignment techniques more accessible, especially for researchers and practitioners with limited computational resources, and enables training with larger base models and RM pools.

#### 5.3. Robustness and Mitigation of Conflicting Signals

The research highlights the prevalent issue of conflicting preferences among different RMs. LASeR's adaptive selection inherently mitigates this problem by avoiding the direct aggregation of disparate signals. Instead, it learns to pick the RM most likely to provide informative and consistent feedback for the current context. This robustness to noisy RMs and underperforming evaluation metrics, as shown in the ablation studies, makes LASeR a more reliable solution for real-world applications where the quality and consistency of available RMs can vary.

#### 5.4. Reduced User Burden and Automated RM Management

LASeR simplifies the complex process of RM selection and management. Users no longer need to manually identify the "best" RM for a specific task or meticulously design ensemble strategies. Instead, they can provide a pool of candidate RMs, and LASeR's Multi-Armed Bandit (MAB) automatically learns and adapts which RM to use, eliminating extensive experimentation and tuning. This lowers the barrier to entry for effective LLM alignment.

#### 5.5. Foundation for Future Research and Versatility

The MAB-based framework of LASeR is highly versatile and opens several avenues for future research:
- **Diverse Feedback Integration:** It provides a generalized framework for integrating any type of reward signal, including different RMs, domain-specific evaluation metrics (e.g., ROSCOE metrics), or even correctness-based signals, allowing for richer and more nuanced LLM training.
- **RM Analysis Tool:** The observed adaptive RM utilization patterns can serve as a diagnostic tool, providing insights into which RMs are most effective for different types of queries and domains, even those not explicitly categorized by humans.
- **Non-Stationary Environments:** The MAB's ability to adapt to changing reward landscapes (as the LLM itself evolves) is crucial for ongoing alignment and fine-tuning.
- **Beyond LLM Alignment:** The core idea of adaptively selecting feedback mechanisms could potentially be extended to other machine learning problems involving iterative optimization with multiple, potentially conflicting, reward signals.

#### 5.6. Limitations and Ethical Considerations

While powerful, LASeR's effectiveness still relies on the *availability* of a reasonable pool of RMs; if all RMs are weak or biased, the system's performance will suffer. Furthermore, while it handles conflicting *signals*, it does not explicitly address underlying safety, fairness, or ethical biases present *within* the RMs themselves. The authors acknowledge that LLMs (and thus LASeR-trained LLMs) can reflect societal biases from their training data. Future work could explore incorporating RMs specifically designed for safety and fairness as arms in the MAB to reinforce desired ethical behaviors.

In conclusion, LASeR represents a significant step towards more intelligent, efficient, and robust LLM alignment. By leveraging Multi-Armed Bandits for adaptive RM selection, it offers a compelling solution that improves model performance, reduces computational overhead, and simplifies the alignment process, making it a valuable tool for advancing the capabilities and widespread deployment of large language models.
