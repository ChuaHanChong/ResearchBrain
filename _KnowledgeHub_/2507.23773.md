# SimuRA: A World-Model-Driven Simulative Reasoning Architecture for General Goal-Oriented Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> The SIMURA architecture integrates explicit world-model-driven simulative reasoning into a goal-oriented agent, leveraging large language models for internal cognitive functions. It demonstrates improved task completion rates and reduced errors in complex web browsing tasks compared to autoregressive baselines, utilizing natural language as a discrete, concept-based latent space for planning.

## Problem

- Current AI agents, often based on foundation models, exhibit limited generality and suffer from a "one-task-one-agent" paradigm, hindering scalability and adaptability.
- Black-box autoregressive reasoning in agents leads to myopic decisions, error accumulation, and a lack of explicit mental simulation for evaluating future action consequences.
- Traditional world models often rely on noisy continuous embeddings, which can limit robustness and generalizability across diverse environments.

## Method

- SIMURA is designed with three core modules: a ==Policy== to propose simulated actions, a ==World Model== to predict outcomes, and a ==Critic== to evaluate goal progress, all orchestrated by large language models.
- It employs ==natural language as a discrete, hierarchical representation== for world states and simulated actions, enabling concept-based latent space reasoning.
- The architecture implements ==hierarchical planning==, where high-level "simulated actions" are translated into low-level "concrete actions" by a separate Actor module, facilitating knowledge transfer and efficient multi-step abstract plans.

## Results

- SIMURA with world-model planning achieved a **32.2%** success rate on the complex FlightQA dataset, outperforming its autoregressive counterpart by **124%** (**14.4%** success rate).
- On the FanOutQA dataset for multi-hop, multi-website tasks, world-model planning improved fact-level accuracy by **47.5%** (**29.8%** vs. **20.2%**) compared to autoregressive planning.
- Across general web automation tasks in the WebArena dataset, the full SIMURA architecture achieved a **23.0%** success rate, representing a **91.7%** improvement over the BrowsingAgent baseline and a **21.1%** improvement over its own autoregressive version.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit world-model-driven simulation offers a measurable performance advantage over purely autoregressive planning for complex, goal-oriented tasks, preventing myopic decisions and error propagation.
> - Representing internal world states and simulated actions using natural language provides a robust, concept-based latent space that abstracts away noisy observations and facilitates more coherent planning.
> - Decomposing agent intelligence into distinct modules for proposing, simulating, and evaluating actions, all powered by foundation models, enables more structured and adaptable reasoning than end-to-end approaches.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of intelligent agents capable of long-term planning and goal pursuit has been a longstanding objective in Artificial Intelligence. Contemporary agentic systems have demonstrated capabilities in domains such as web and computer automation, software development, and scientific research. These systems often operate by interacting with structured environments, tools, and predefined interaction logic.

However, a structural limitation identified in many current agentic systems is their reliance on reactive decision-making. Whether implemented as scaffolded Large Language Model (LLM) pipelines, prompt-based workflows, or end-to-end trained policies, these systems typically select the next action based on the current observation using a fixed procedure. While some exhibit adaptive computation, such as chain-of-thought reasoning, this typically involves generating longer internal reasoning sequences to better fit observations rather than explicitly modeling and comparing future outcomes. This reactive paradigm can limit generalizability, necessitating re-engineering for new tasks or environments and making systems vulnerable to local myopia and error accumulation over extended interactions.

In contrast, human cognition often employs "System II" thinking, which involves deliberative planning through mentally simulating the consequences of candidate actions within an internal model of the world. This capacity, referred to as simulative reasoning, supports flexible, goal-directed behavior across diverse contexts. The current work positions itself as an effort to bridge this gap by integrating simulative reasoning into agentic systems.

This approach builds on the established field of model-based planning, which has a history of success in sequential decision-making tasks such as classic games (e.g., Go, chess, Atari) and continuous control. More recently, with the advent of powerful foundation models, model-based planning has extended to complex problems like mathematical reasoning and web browsing. However, previous work in this area often used holistic continuous embeddings for world states, which can be susceptible to noise, or focused on narrow domains. This paper aims to introduce simulative reasoning as a general-purpose planning pattern, addressing these limitations through concept-based representations and a hierarchical planning architecture.

The paper also addresses limitations in existing benchmarks for agentic systems, such as WebArena, WebVoyager, and MiniWoB++. These benchmarks are often conducted in simulated environments, use outdated questions, or lack robust methods for measuring task completion in realistic open-web scenarios. To address the need for controlled evaluation under varying complexity, the authors introduce a new dataset, FlightQA, for constrained navigation tasks in live web environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to empirically verify the hypothesis that augmenting reactive policies (often referred to as System I) with simulative reasoning through a world model (analogous to System II) provides a generalizable improvement to agentic planning. The authors contend that current reactive agentic systems, despite their successes, fundamentally lack explicit modeling of future outcomes and a mechanism for grounded counterfactual evaluation. This absence leads to limitations in generalizability, requiring task-specific re-engineering rather than transferring a shared reasoning capacity.

The motivation for this work stems from the observation that human intelligence excels at planning by mentally simulating the consequences of potential actions within an internal model of the world. This deliberative capacity, or simulative reasoning, allows for flexible, goal-directed behavior across diverse contexts. The authors argue that this general-purpose planning mechanism, which operates by simulating state transitions rather than pattern-matching domain-specific responses, can transfer across tasks and environments.

To investigate this, the research sets out to:
1. **Introduce SiRA (Simulative Reasoning Architecture):** This goal-oriented architecture instantiates the principle of simulative reasoning. While implemented using LLMs as a substrate, its design is intended to be model-agnostic, allowing any system capable of encoding observations, predicting state transitions, and evaluating outcomes to serve as its underlying world model.
2. **Employ Natural-Language Belief States:** SiRA leverages compact natural-language summaries for belief states, aiming to provide discrete, concept-based representations for robust reasoning, countering the noise and variability often found in continuous embeddings.
3. **Adopt a Hierarchical Architecture:** The design isolates perception, simulative planning, and action selection. This aims to enhance adaptability across environments and disentangle high-level planning from low-level execution details.
4. **Evaluate Across Diverse Task Categories:** To test the generalizability of simulative reasoning, SiRA is evaluated across three qualitatively distinct task categories: constrained navigation, multi-hop information aggregation, and general instruction following. These tasks are instantiated in a web-browser environment chosen for its realistic complexity, partial observability, and long-horizon interaction.
5. **Develop a Controlled Evaluation Dataset:** For constrained navigation, the FlightQA dataset is introduced to enable systematic evaluation of agent reasoning under varying constraint complexity, allowing for a form of counterfactual analysis.

Ultimately, the core motivation is to demonstrate that grounding decisions in predicted future states through explicit simulation offers a measurable and repeatable advantage over approaches that rely solely on pattern-matched responses, thereby contributing to the development of more general-purpose and robust intelligent agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces SiRA (Simulative Reasoning Architecture), a goal-oriented framework designed to instantiate simulative reasoning for agentic planning. SiRA's architecture is modular and model-agnostic, although the current implementation utilizes Large Language Models (LLMs) for its components.

#### 3.1. SiRA Architecture and Decision Process

SiRA operates as a multi-level optimization problem comprising three core stages: Perception, Planning via Simulation, and Acting.

1. **Perception (Encoder `h`):** At each timestep `t`, the agent receives an observation `o_t` (e.g., HTML-based accessibility tree and/or screenshots in the web environment). An LLM-based encoder `h` processes this observation to infer a natural-language belief state `̂s_t`. This belief state is a compact summary of the webpage elements, including interactive components, their values, and any relevant details, dialogs, or error messages.

2. **Planning via Simulation (Policy `~π`, World Model `f`, Critic `v`):** This is the central component where simulative reasoning occurs.
 - **Proposing Actions:** Based on the current belief state `̂s_t` and a selective memory of past summaries and simulated actions, the planner uses a policy `~π` to propose `M` high-level *simulated actions* `a'_t`. These simulated actions are abstract, natural-language descriptions of intentions (e.g., "Go to the home page of Google Flights," "Click on the 'Search' button") rather than concrete API calls. These are then clustered into distinct actions.
 - **Simulating Consequences:** For each proposed simulated action `a'_t`, an LLM-based *world model* `f` predicts the next belief state `̂s_{t+1}`. This process of proposing and predicting continues for a set planning horizon `T'`. The world model essentially functions as a generative model of possible future world states, enabling "thought experiments."
 - **Evaluating Goal Progress (Critic `v`):** Upon reaching the planning horizon `T'`, a *critic* `v` evaluates the terminal belief state `̂s_{T'}` in terms of goal progress. This evaluation is performed by an LLM prompted to generate categorical answers (e.g., "success," "failure," "on the right track") which are converted into numerical scores. The system iterates this process `N` times to capture fine-grained differences.
 - **Optimizing Policy:** The planner uses a tree search algorithm (Depth-First Search (DFS) in this implementation) over the sampled simulated actions and predicted states. It selects the sequence of simulated actions `a'*{t:T'-1}` that yields the highest expected success (value function `V`).

3. **Acting (Actor `α`):** Once the best simulated action `a'*{t}` for the current step is chosen by the planner, an LLM-based *actor* `α` translates this high-level intention into a concrete, environment-specific action `a_t` (e.g., `click('a12')`, `fill('b34', 'value')`). The actor's prompt also includes the raw observation `o_t` to ensure proper action grounding. This concrete action `a_t` is then executed in the environment, leading to a new observation `o_{t+1}`, and the cycle repeats.

#### 3.2. Key Design Decisions

- **Concept-Based Mixed Representations:** Instead of holistic continuous embeddings, SiRA represents the world state `̂s_t` using a discrete natural language summary generated by the encoder `h`. This "concept-based factorization" allows the world model and critic to operate on a structured latent space where individual state components can be predicted and evaluated independently. This approach aims to reduce hallucination and enhance planning robustness.
- **Hierarchical Planning via Simulated Actions:** To promote generalizability and efficiency, SiRA separates the planning over abstract "simulated actions" `a'_t` from the execution of "concrete actions" `a_t`. Simulated actions, represented in natural language, capture broader intentions and can represent multiple low-level execution steps, thereby shortening the rollout steps for the world model, improving efficiency, and reducing error accumulation across multi-step predictions. This separation aims to allow knowledge transfer across different action spaces and environments.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

SiRA was evaluated across three qualitatively distinct task categories in a web-browser environment (BrowserGym), chosen for its complexity, partial observability, and long-horizon interaction:

1. **Constrained Navigation:** Tasks involve navigating live travel websites to find flights satisfying multiple compositional constraints (e.g., one-way, specific origin/destination, dates, times).
 - **Dataset:** **FlightQA**, a new dataset generated by LLMs to systematically vary the number of constraints (3 to 8) for controlled evaluation of compositional robustness.
 - **Evaluation:** LLM-based assessment of groundedness (response supported by interaction history) and relevance (response satisfies user constraints).
2. **Multi-Hop Information Aggregation:** Tasks require gathering and synthesizing information from multiple sources over long interactions (e.g., finding availabilities of top restaurants).
 - **Dataset:** **FanOutQA** dataset (first 100 examples from dev set).
 - **Evaluation:** Accuracy and strict accuracy against ground truth.
3. **General Instruction Following:** Broad tasks requiring diverse natural-language instructions (e.g., summarizing customer reviews, content management, form submission).
 - **Dataset:** **WebArena** benchmark (random subset of 100 examples).
 - **Evaluation:** Task success rate based on WebArena's specific response format.

#### 3.4. Baselines

- **Reactive Policy (System I):** SiRA with the planning module replaced by direct action selection from the policy without simulation, committing to the first sampled action. This isolates the effect of world-model-based simulation.
- **Unstructured Chain-of-Thought:** SiRA with strong reasoning LLMs (o1, o3-mini) as the planner, generating unstructured chain-of-thought without explicit simulation of future states.
- **OpenHands BrowsingAgent:** A representative open-web reactive agent that generates chain-of-thought before selecting an action.

Experiments were conducted in late 2024 and early 2025 using then-available LLMs (gpt-4o, o1, o3-mini) and browser tooling.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across three distinct task categories in a web-browser environment yielded consistent results, indicating the performance characteristics of simulative reasoning (System II) compared to reactive policies (System I) and other baselines.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Trend

Simulative reasoning (System II), as implemented in SiRA, uniformly outperformed both the matched reactive policy baseline (System I) and the external OpenHands BrowsingAgent across all three task categories. The persistent advantage suggests that the benefit is attributable to generalizable counterfactual evaluation rather than task-specific tuning.

#### 4.2. Constrained Navigation (FlightQA Dataset)

- **Comparison with OpenHands BrowsingAgent:** SiRA with simulative reasoning demonstrated a substantial improvement in accuracy, achieving a 32.2% correct task completion rate compared to 0% for OpenHands BrowsingAgent. BrowsingAgent exhibited a high rate of action errors (93.3%) and did not return any responses, indicating a complete failure in this complex task.
- **Comparison with Reactive Policy (System I):** Within the SiRA architecture, simulative reasoning (System II) achieved a 124% higher task completion rate (32.2% vs. 14.4%) compared to the reactive policy (System I). This difference was statistically significant (p < 0.01). The reactive policy also showed a higher incidence of repetitive actions (44.4%) compared to simulative reasoning (18.9%).
- **Unstructured Chain-of-Thought:** Using strong reasoning LLMs (o1/o3-mini) to generate unstructured chain-of-thought within the planner resulted in near-zero success rates (1.1%/3.3%), indicating that increasing internal computation without explicit future state simulation does not adequately address planning challenges in this domain.
- **Compositional Robustness:** An analysis of performance as a function of the number of constraints (from 3 to 8) showed that simulative reasoning consistently maintained an advantage over the reactive policy. This sustained gap supports the claim of generalizable counterfactual evaluation.

#### 4.3. Multi-Hop Information Aggregation (FanOutQA Dataset)

- **Comparison with OpenHands BrowsingAgent:** SiRA with simulative reasoning achieved an accuracy of 29.8% (4.0% strict accuracy) compared to 17.0% (4.0% strict accuracy) for OpenHands BrowsingAgent. The SiRA architecture, even in its reactive form, significantly reduced action errors (from 43% in BrowsingAgent to 10% for reactive SiRA and 1% for simulative SiRA).
- **Comparison with Reactive Policy (System I):** Simulative reasoning (System II) increased fact-level accuracy by 47.5% and the response rate by 48.6% (29.8% accuracy vs. 20.2% accuracy) compared to the reactive policy (System I). This difference was statistically significant (p = 0.011). Browser crashes remained a notable failure mode (24% for both SiRA variants).

#### 4.4. General Instruction Following (WebArena Benchmark)

- **Comparison with OpenHands BrowsingAgent:** SiRA with simulative reasoning achieved a 23.0% success rate, representing a 91.7% improvement over OpenHands BrowsingAgent (12.0%).
- **Comparison with Reactive Policy (System I):** Simulative reasoning (System II) improved the success rate by 21.1% (23.0% vs. 19.0%) compared to the reactive policy (System I).

In summary, across all evaluated categories, simulative reasoning consistently demonstrated a measurable advantage in task completion rates, handling of complex constraints, and reduction of errors, suggesting its role as a fundamental improvement for agentic planning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents empirical evidence supporting the utility of simulative reasoning through a world model as a general-purpose planning mechanism for agentic systems. The findings contribute to the field by demonstrating a systematic advantage of deliberative, model-based planning over purely reactive decision-making in diverse and complex environments.

The **significance** of this work can be delineated as follows:
- **Validation of Simulative Reasoning:** The consistent outperformance of SiRA's simulative reasoning module across three qualitatively distinct task categories (constrained navigation, multi-hop information aggregation, and general instruction following) provides empirical support for the hypothesis that grounding decisions in predicted future states, rather than pattern-matched responses, leads to more robust and generalizable agent behavior. This contrasts with the limitations observed in purely reactive systems, including those leveraging unstructured chain-of-thought, which often fail to effectively plan without explicit simulation.
- **Development of a Generalizable Planning Mechanism:** The research suggests that the benefits of simulative reasoning stem from a generalizable property of the reasoning pattern—grounded counterfactual evaluation—rather than task-specific tuning. This indicates a potential path toward more adaptable AI agents that can transfer planning capabilities across novel tasks and environments without extensive re-engineering.
- **Architectural Contributions:** SiRA's modular and model-agnostic architecture, which separates perception, planning, and acting, provides a flexible framework for future agent development. The use of natural-language belief states and hierarchical planning with simulated actions addresses challenges of noisy sensory inputs and efficient multi-step reasoning, contributing to more robust and coherent agent behavior.

The **potential impact** of this work extends to several areas:
- **Enhanced Agent Capabilities:** The demonstrated improvements in task completion rates and robustness in complex web environments could lead to more effective autonomous agents for a wide range of real-world applications, including advanced web automation, intelligent assistants, and autonomous research tools.
- **Foundation for Future AI Research:** SiRA's architecture and the principles it embodies offer a foundation for further research into advanced agentic systems. This includes exploring more sophisticated world models, optimizing the computational cost of simulation, and integrating multimodal perception for a richer understanding of environments.
- **Advancements in AI Learning:** The concept of world models as simulators opens avenues for improving continual reinforcement learning. By combining learning within a world-model sandbox with periodic grounding on real data, agents could achieve greater sample efficiency and robustness, particularly in environments where real-world interaction is resource-intensive or risky.
- **Safety and Alignment Considerations:** The explicit nature of world-model reasoning, by making future predictions and evaluations transparent, could potentially contribute to building AI agents that are more interpretable, controllable, and aligned with human values, fostering discussions within the community on these critical aspects.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The release of the SiRA-based web-browsing agent as an open-source research artifact supports reproducibility and facilitates further innovation in the field.

In conclusion, this research moves beyond reactive AI paradigms by demonstrating the significant advantages of simulative reasoning, positioning it as a core component for developing general-purpose, intelligent agents capable of complex, goal-directed behavior across diverse domains.
