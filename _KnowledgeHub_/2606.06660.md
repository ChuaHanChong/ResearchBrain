# AEGIS: A Backup Reflex for Physical AI

> [!summary] Summary
> AEGIS is a runtime escalation layer for robot manipulation. A two-layer probe reads a frozen SmolVLA action expert's layer-15 activations; a conformal threshold, early-harm gate and per-episode budget cap turn the per-step score into a trigger that hands control at chunk boundaries to a stronger frozen policy. It recovers 10.1% of weak-policy failures on LIBERO-Spatial while the strong policy runs on 38% of steps.

## Problem

- Detect-only runtime monitors predict impending failure from internal signals but only halt or defer to a human, and report no recovery metric at all.
- Recover-in-policy methods resample, roll back or re-prompt the same policy that is already failing, so no stronger separate executor ever takes over.
- Accurate prediction does not imply effective prevention, yet prior escalation gains are never isolated from the extra compute the intervention itself spends.

## Method

- ==Frozen-VLA hidden-state probe==: a 720-256-1 MLP mean-pools layer-15 action-expert activations per chunk and is trained on eventual-failure labels using early-window steps only.
- Gate stack: ==split-conformal threshold== at false-trigger level 0.10, ==early-harm guard== at 0.20T, per-episode budget cap, ==chunk-boundary handoff==, three-chunk hold, ==hysteretic de-escalation==.
- Four-arm ==common-random-number factorial== adding budget-matched-blind and random-trigger control arms, pre-registered with a conditional recovered-task-rate estimand, difficulty strata and explicit kill criteria.

## Results

- On LIBERO-Spatial with **700** paired episodes (**646** weak-policy failures), AEGIS recovers **10.1%** versus **4.6%** blind and **5.1%** random escalation, Holm-adjusted p values **8.5e-6** and **1.0e-4**.
- Early-window AUROC reaches **0.764** (95% CI **0.70** to **0.84**) and peaks exactly at the **30%** gate readout; the chunk-overlap surprise proxy reaches only **0.63**.
- Recover-to-disrupt ratio is **6.5** (**65** recoveries, **10** disruptions) against **1.8** blind and **3.3** random, at **38%** strong-policy duty cycle and roughly **44%** of always-strong compute.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Timing, not compute spend, is the lever: controls with identical strong-policy budget and temporal profile recover about half as much.
> - Probe placement decides everything: a vision-encoder hook gave chance-level AUROC because the cached feature never varied across live rollout steps.
> - Escalation also derails trajectories that would have succeeded, so report recover-to-disrupt accounting rather than arguing from predictive AUROC to deployment utility.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Long-horizon robot manipulation tasks frequently exhibit gradual failures, where initial errors compound over time, eventually leading to unrecoverable states. The paper identifies these failures as often being detectable in the policy’s internal activations well before they become catastrophic. This observation highlights a "runtime authorization gap" – the absence of a system that can make real-time decisions about a policy's continued control based on evidence of impending failure.

Current research efforts addressing this problem typically fall into two categories:
- **Detect-only failure prediction:** Methods such as SAFE, FIPER, FAIL-Detect, ReconVLA, and Sentinel focus on identifying impending failures using signals from policy internals or consistency checks. While these approaches can achieve high predictive accuracy (e.g., AUROC 72–93% on LIBERO), they primarily function by raising alarms, halting execution, or deferring to human operators. They do not implement automated recovery mechanisms or report recovery metrics.
- **Recover-within-the-same-policy:** Approaches like HELM, Pre-VLA, LiLo-VLA, FailSafe, and FPC-VLA aim to mitigate failures by resampling, replanning, or re-prompting the *same* failing policy. These methods engage in recovery actions but do not involve switching to a fundamentally different or more capable policy.

AEGIS (Activation-probe Early-warning, Gated Inference Switching) positions itself by addressing the unoccupied axis within this landscape. It proposes and evaluates a system that combines early failure prediction from a policy's frozen internal activations with a threshold-triggered escalation mechanism. Crucially, this escalation involves handing control to a *stronger, separate policy*, a distinct approach from merely retrying within the same policy or only detecting failures. The paper argues that this mechanism mirrors how a human supervisor might intervene by calling for a more expert intervention rather than repeatedly instructing a struggling agent.

The work is also contextualized within the evolving deployment strategies for scaled physical AI, where a hierarchy of policies (a cheap, default policy and a more expensive, frontier policy) will be dynamically scheduled based on latency and compute constraints. AEGIS directly addresses the question of *when* the cheaper policy should yield control to a stronger one, making it relevant for efficient and reliable robot deployment. The authors note that AEGIS is orthogonal to their companion memory work, AURA-Mem, which focuses on gating memory writes inward, whereas AEGIS gates compute and policy outward to enhance task success.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a mechanism that enables robot policies to proactively recover from impending failures in long-horizon manipulation tasks. The central hypothesis is that a robot policy can leverage its own internal activations as an early-warning signal to call a stronger policy, thereby mitigating failures more effectively and efficiently than existing methods.

The motivation for AEGIS stems from several detailed considerations:

- **Addressing Gradual Failure Modes:** Long-horizon robotic tasks are characterized by failure modes that often develop incrementally, with minor errors accumulating to destabilize the system. This gradual degradation provides a window of opportunity for intervention if impending failure can be identified sufficiently early. The paper posits that this "warning arrives long before the crash," manifest in the policy's internal state.
- **The "Runtime Authorization Gap":** Current robot control systems lack an explicit layer that, at runtime, assesses the policy's performance and decides whether it is authorized to continue or if an alternative, more robust course of action is required. AEGIS is designed to fill this gap by introducing a self-monitoring and policy-switching capability.
- **Limitations of Existing Approaches:**
 - **Detect-only methods** provide valuable predictive signals but offer no automated recovery, leaving the robot vulnerable to eventual failure.
 - **Recover-within-the-same-policy methods** attempt recovery but often rely on the same policy that is already struggling, which may not be sufficient for complex or critical deviations. The motivation here is that for genuinely challenging situations, a *fundamentally more capable* policy is required, akin to a human calling for expert assistance.
- **Computational Efficiency and Scalability:** Deploying large, highly capable robot policies can be computationally expensive. Running a "frontier policy" constantly might be prohibitively costly due to memory and compute demands. AEGIS is motivated by the need for a mechanism to selectively invoke a stronger policy *only when needed*, thereby optimizing resource allocation while maximizing task success. The aim is to achieve higher recovery rates without the uniform, high computational overhead of continuously running the most powerful policy.
- **Causal Validation of Intervention Effectiveness:** A key motivation is to ensure that any observed performance gains are genuinely attributable to the *selective timing and placement* of the stronger policy, rather than merely the additional computational budget or the signal acting as a proxy for task difficulty. This is explicitly driven by the "intervention paradox", which highlights that accurate failure prediction does not automatically translate to effective prevention if interventions disrupt otherwise successful trajectories. Therefore, the methodology includes robust causal controls to isolate the impact of selectivity.

In summary, the core objective is to create a practical, efficient, and causally validated system for enhancing robot autonomy by intelligently switching control to a stronger policy at critical moments of impending failure, thereby improving long-horizon task success while managing computational resources.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AEGIS is designed as a four-part system that operates at runtime. The methodology emphasizes a causally-controlled evaluation to distinguish the effect of selective intervention from general compute expenditure.

#### 3.1. AEGIS System Components

1. **Cheap Per-Step Signal from Frozen VLA Internals:**
 - **Primary Signal (Probe):** A two-layer multilayer perceptron ($g_\theta$) is trained to read specific frozen internal activations of the weak policy ($\pi_w$).
 - **Activation Source:** A forward hook is placed on the output projection of the self-attention block at layer 15 of the action expert within SmolVLA (a 450M VLA). This captures a 720-dimensional per-token representation, which is then mean-pooled over a 10-step action chunk to form a fixed-dimensional feature vector ($h_t$).
 - **Training:** The probe is trained using a binary cross-entropy objective with per-class weights, predicting the *eventual trajectory outcome* (success/failure, where failure = 1). Training is restricted to early steps ($t \le 0.30T$) to prevent leakage from later trajectory information.
 - **Leakage Control:** Trajectory-level train/validation/test splits (70/15/15) ensure that all steps from a given rollout reside entirely within one split, preventing temporal correlations from contaminating evaluation. All probe fitting and AUROC scoring are done offline.
 - **Precondition:** The early-window (t ≤ 0.30 T) AUROC of the probe is targeted to be $\ge 0.75$.
 - **Complementary Signals (Exploratory):** Three additional signals are computed from the same logged rollout:
 - `Chunk-overlap delta` ($\Delta_{chunk,t}$): L2 disagreement between overlapping regions of consecutive action chunks.
 - `STAC variance` ($Var_{STAC,t}$): Temporal-consistency variance of the action chunk.
 - `Flow-matching sampling disagreement` ($MAD_{K,t}$): Mean absolute deviation across K=4 action chunks from $\pi_w$ using distinct noise seeds. These are used for context, with the primary probe being the default trigger score ($s_t$).

2. **Conformal Trigger Threshold ($\delta_t$):**
 - The continuous probe score ($s_t$) is converted into a binary escalation decision ($esc_{raw,t}$) using a split-conformal calibration technique.
 - **Calibration:** A held-out calibration set of early-window steps from *successful* trajectories under the weak policy is used. $\delta_t$ is set as an empirical quantile of these nonconformity scores to control the per-step false-trigger rate at $\alpha=0.10$. $\delta_t$ is allowed to vary with trajectory fraction.
 - **Per-stratum Calibration:** To account for varying task difficulty, thresholds are calibrated per difficulty stratum where calibration data is sufficient; otherwise, a shared threshold is used.

3. **Early-Harm Gate and Per-Episode Budget Cap:**
 - **Early-Harm Gate:** Suppresses any escalation before $t_{min} = \max(0.20T, 2)$. This is motivated by the "intervention paradox," where premature intervention can disrupt trajectories that would have succeeded, and by similar warm-up horizons in prior work.
 - **Budget Cap:** A maximum of $K_{max} = \lceil 0.05T \rceil$ escalations are permitted per episode, prioritized by signal strength.
 - The final escalation decision ($esc_t$) incorporates these constraints.

4. **Escalation Handoff:**
 - **Switching Mechanism:** When $esc_t=1$, control transfers from $\pi_w$ to the stronger policy ($\pi_s$) at the next action-chunk boundary (H=10 steps) to maintain action granularity.
 - **Hold and Hysteresis:** Once $\pi_s$ is engaged, it is held for a minimum of $k=3$ chunks. De-escalation back to $\pi_w$ occurs only after the probe score consistently stays below a lower de-escalation threshold for the hold window, preventing rapid switching.
 - **Deployment Architecture:** Both policies ($\pi_w$ and $\pi_s$) are kept warm within a single process and container. This design minimizes latency and mitigates security vulnerabilities associated with networked policy servers.
 - **Policy Pair:** The weak policy is SmolVLA (450M parameters), and the stronger policy is pi05_libero_finetuned (4.14B parameters). The cost is approximated as $C(AEGIS) \approx C_w + C_{probe} + \rho C_s$, where $\rho$ is the strong-policy duty cycle.

#### 3.2. Experimental Design (Pre-Registered Factorial)

- **Environment:** Experiments were conducted on the LIBERO-Spatial benchmark (10 tasks), chosen because the weak policy's success rate (around 33%) provided sufficient headroom for recovery.
- **Paired Design:** A four-arm factorial design was employed using common random numbers (CRN). Each arm received the identical (task, seed, initial state) tuple, allowing for paired statistical comparisons.
- **Experimental Arms:**
 - **A. Weak-only:** The weak policy ($\pi_w$) runs for the entire trajectory (baseline).
 - **B. Targeted (AEGIS):** The proposed method, with signal-gated escalation.
 - **C. Budget-matched-blind:** Escalates the same number of steps as B, with the same temporal distribution (step-index-fraction CDF), but chooses steps randomly without using the signal. This control isolates the effect of selectivity from raw compute expenditure.
 - **D. Random-trigger placebo:** Escalates at the same per-step rate as B but on uniformly random steps. This isolates the informational content of the signal.
- **Reference Arms:**
 - **Always-strong:** The stronger policy ($\pi_s$) runs for the entire trajectory (recovery ceiling).
 - **HELM-style rollback:** A re-implementation of a "recover-within-the-same-policy" baseline, budget-matched to AEGIS's extra compute.
 - **GR00T N1.x:** An additional stronger policy from a different family (NVIDIA's GR00T) to test generalization.
- **Primary Estimand:** The conditional Recovered-Task-Rate (RTR) = Pr[task succeeds under arm B | weak-only arm A fails on that (task, seed)]. This metric focuses on the subset of trajectories where recovery is possible.
- **Statistical Analysis:** Three primary contrasts (B>A, B>C, B>D) were tested using one-sided exact paired McNemar tests (for B vs. C/D) or a binomial sign test (for B vs. A), with Holm–Bonferroni adjustment for family-wise error rate ($\alpha=0.05$). Paired bootstrap confidence intervals (10,000 resamples) were also computed.
- **Difficulty Stratification:** Tasks were categorized into easy, medium, and hard terciles based on weak-only success rates from a pilot run. This was to test if the gains hold within comparable difficulty levels, addressing the "difficulty-proxy artifact."
- **Power and Sample Size:** The study was powered to detect a 10 percentage-point RTR gap with 80% power, requiring 250–400 discordant/failing pairs. This translated to a pre-committed minimum of 700 episodes per headline arm.
- **Pre-registered Kill Criteria:** Specific conditions were outlined under which the claims would be falsified (e.g., probe AUROC at chance, RTR gain not surviving stratification, escalation fraction exceeding 50%, or prior art occupying the novelty axis).

#### 3.3. Deviations from Pre-Registration

- **Action-Chunk Horizon (H):** Changed from 50 to 10 to match the native granularity of the executed checkpoints.
- **Benchmark Suite:** LIBERO-Spatial was selected for the main factorial after a pilot sweep indicated it provided the appropriate difficulty and recovery headroom, instead of the initially considered LIBERO-Long or chained-LIBERO.
- **Probe Source:** An initial bug hooking a frozen vision-encoder layer was corrected, moving the probe to the live action-expert layer 15 output projection, which provides a dynamically varying signal.
- **Two-Stage Evidence:** A small Phase-D pilot (56 keys/arm) was conducted to establish initial directionality and authorize the larger confirmatory factorial (700 episodes/arm).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The core findings are derived from the confirmatory factorial run on LIBERO-Spatial, involving 700 common-random-number episodes per arm, with 646 trajectories failing under the weak-only policy ($n_{A-fail}=646$).

- **AEGIS Significantly Improves Recovery:**
 - **Versus Weak-only (A):** AEGIS (Arm B) increased the conditional Recovered-Task-Rate (RTR) from 0.000 to 0.101. This difference was statistically significant (Holm-adjusted p = $5.2 \times 10^{-11}$).
 - **Versus Budget-Matched-Blind (C):** AEGIS achieved an RTR of 0.101, significantly outperforming the budget-matched-blind control (Arm C), which yielded an RTR of 0.046. The difference was +0.054 (Holm-adjusted p = $8.5 \times 10^{-6}$). This indicates that the gains are attributable to the selectivity of the signal, not merely the additional computational resources.
 - **Versus Random-Trigger Placebo (D):** AEGIS (RTR 0.101) also significantly surpassed the random-trigger placebo (Arm D), which had an RTR of 0.051. The difference was +0.050 (Holm-adjusted p = $1.0 \times 10^{-4}$). This demonstrates that the probe's signal carries meaningful predictive information.
 - All paired-bootstrap intervals for these primary contrasts excluded zero, further supporting the statistical significance.

- **Efficiency and Resource Management:**
 - The stronger policy in AEGIS was active on only 38% of steps (duty cycle) in the confirmatory run. This suggests that AEGIS roughly doubles the recovery rate compared to blind or random escalation while operating at a matched strong-policy duty cycle.
 - This contrasts with the "always-strong" policy, which achieves a higher RTR of 0.319 but at approximately 4.6 times the compute cost.

- **Selectivity in Harm Accounting:**
 - AEGIS recovered 65 of the 646 weak-policy failures while disrupting only 10 of the 54 trajectories that the weak policy alone would have succeeded on. This yielded a recover-to-disrupt ratio of 6.5.
 - In comparison, budget-matched-blind escalation recovered 30 failures but disrupted 17 successes (ratio 1.8), while the random trigger recovered 33 failures and disrupted 10 successes (ratio 3.3). These results highlight AEGIS's ability to maximize beneficial interventions while minimizing detrimental ones.

- **Robustness to Difficulty Stratification:**
 - The selectivity advantage of AEGIS (B > D) was maintained across all three difficulty terciles (EASY, MEDIUM, HARD).
 - The advantage of AEGIS over budget-matched-blind (B > C) held in the EASY and MEDIUM bands. In the HARD band, the B−C interval just touched zero, indicating that even the stronger policy had limited recoverable margin for the most challenging tasks. This generally confirms the robustness of the causal claim across varying task difficulties.

- **Per-Task Performance:** The advantage of AEGIS over the controls was consistently observed across individual LIBERO-Spatial tasks.

- **Early-Window Probe Performance:**
 - The hidden-state probe achieved an early-window (t ≤ 0.30T) AUROC of 0.764 (95% CI [0.70, 0.84]), successfully clearing the pre-registered 0.75 precondition. This discrimination performance was observed to peak precisely within the early window where the gate is designed to read.

- **Robustness to Simulator Non-Determinism:**
 - A resampling analysis, involving 2,000 redraws of outcomes for cells with non-deterministic simulator behavior, confirmed that the directional result (AEGIS outperforming controls) never reversed. All three primary RTR gaps remained strictly positive, indicating the robustness of the selectivity advantage despite potential variations in simulation outcomes.

- **Comparison to Other Baselines:**
 - HELM-style rollback and the GR00T N1.7 cross-family generalization arm both achieved higher conditional RTRs (0.155) than AEGIS. However, these methods either operate within the same policy or deploy a strong policy from the first flagged step, implying a different computational cost profile. AEGIS's objective is to achieve high recovery at a lower, targeted computational overhead.

- **Kill Criteria:** All pre-registered kill criteria (K1-K4) were averted, indicating that the study's core assumptions and claims remained valid based on the experimental results.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "AEGIS: A Backup Reflex for Physical AI" holds several implications for the development and deployment of robust autonomous robotic systems:

- **Validation of Targeted Intervention:** The core significance of AEGIS lies in its causal demonstration that *when* a stronger policy is invoked, and not merely *that* it is invoked, is critical for efficient failure recovery. By outperforming budget-matched-blind and random-trigger controls, AEGIS provides empirical evidence that intelligently timing interventions based on internal policy signals is a more effective strategy than indiscriminate application of greater computational resources. This is a critical finding for designing deployable, resource-aware AI.

- **Bridging the "Runtime Authorization Gap":** AEGIS introduces a functional layer of "runtime metacognition" for robot policies. This capability allows a deployed policy to self-monitor for signs of impending failure and autonomously authorize an escalation to a more capable executor. This addresses a recognized limitation in current robotic systems, where policies often lack the ability to dynamically adapt their operational mode in response to internal state-based predictions of performance degradation.

- **Enhanced Reliability and Efficiency in Robot Deployment:** For practical applications, continuously running the most advanced (and often most computationally expensive) policies is often not feasible. AEGIS offers a principled method to achieve a balance between reliability and efficiency. By enabling a cheaper policy to drive most of the time and selectively activating a stronger policy only when needed (with a 38% duty cycle), AEGIS can significantly reduce overall computational overhead while substantially improving task success rates (e.g., doubling recovery over matched-budget controls). This is particularly relevant for scaling physical AI to real-world scenarios with limited resources.

- **Implications for Future AI Architectures:** The framework introduced by AEGIS supports the development of hierarchical policy architectures, where different policies with varying capabilities and costs can be strategically orchestrated. This paradigm is likely to become increasingly important as robot capabilities grow, demanding more sophisticated resource management and adaptive control strategies.

- **Robustness Against Intervention Paradox:** The paper's explicit design with causal controls (motivated by the "intervention paradox") contributes to building trust in AI systems. By demonstrating that interventions are indeed beneficial and do not inadvertently disrupt successful trajectories, AEGIS advances the understanding of how to safely and effectively integrate predictive signals into control loops.

- **Foundation for Further Research:** AEGIS establishes a validated baseline for dynamic policy switching. This work opens avenues for future research, including investigating richer failure prediction signals (e.g., incorporating world models), developing strategies for selecting among multiple stronger policies, and exploring real-world transferability of these concepts to diverse hardware platforms and complex long-horizon tasks. The public release of code and data also fosters reproducibility and collaborative advancement in this field.
