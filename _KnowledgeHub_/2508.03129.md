# Safety-Aware Imitation Learning via MPC-Guided Disturbance Injection

> [!summary] Summary
> MPC-SafeGIL is a design-time imitation learning framework that injects worst-case adversarial disturbances into expert demonstrations during data collection, steering the expert into safety-critical states so the cloned policy learns recovery behaviors. Sampling-based MPPI approximates the optimal disturbance, scaling to high-dimensional black-box dynamics. Across quadruped navigation, F1Tenth racing and real quadrotor flight it cuts collisions while raising success rates.

## Problem

- Deployment-time safety filters are hard to construct online without full state, costly in high dimensions, and capped by the underlying policy's own safety awareness.
- Design-time imitation methods reduce covariate shift and learning error but never treat safety as a first-class objective during data collection or optimization.
- The prior safety-guided approach uses Hamilton-Jacobi reachability, which suffers the curse of dimensionality beyond 5-6 states and demands analytical dynamics.

## Method

- **MPC-SafeGIL**'s ==safety-guided data collection== adds an ==optimal adversarial disturbance== to the expert action at each step, storing the undisturbed expert label while the disturbed action drives the system.
- A ==control-affine reduction== merges control and disturbance into one input with tightened bound, converting the two-player max-min ==reachability game== into a single-player max problem.
- ==MPPI== solves that problem: sample N control sequences over horizon H in parallel, keep the top-K by ==minimum-signed-distance cost==, and take a temperature-weighted average whose sign sets the ==bang-bang disturbance==.

## Results

- On the 3D Dubins car, the MPPI disturbance field matches the HJ reachability solution with **0.19** mean squared error over 100,000 sampled states.
- Quadruped navigation with 80 demonstrations drops collisions to about **25 percent**, beating behavior cloning, GAIL, DART and Gaussian or uniform noise on both collision and success rate.
- On an unseen F1Tenth track it travels **68.15 m** versus **25.55 m** for behavior cloning; the real quadrotor succeeds in **9 of 12** runs against **3 of 12**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured adversarial perturbation beats random or covariance-estimated noise: what matters is deliberately visiting safety-critical states, not merely widening the state distribution.
> - Injecting safety at data-collection time can outperform a test-time safety filter alone, since a filter cannot rescue a policy already committed to a dangerous state.
> - Disturbance magnitude has a sweet spot: too weak yields no risky exposure, too strong pushes the expert into unrecoverable states and degrades the demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation Learning (IL) has emerged as a promising methodology for enabling robots to acquire complex behaviors from expert demonstrations across various domains, including manipulation, locomotion, and navigation. A fundamental challenge in deploying IL-based policies, particularly in safety-critical applications, stems from the potential for learning errors, external disturbances, or covariate shift to lead to safety violations. This issue has driven research into methods for enhancing the safety of learned policies.

Current approaches to address safety in imitation learning can be broadly categorized into deployment-time (or test-time) and design-time (or training-time) strategies. Deployment-time solutions, such as safety filters (e.g., based on Control Barrier Functions, Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability, or predictive rollouts), aim to verify policy safety during execution and intervene when safety constraints are at risk. While these methods offer theoretical guarantees, they face limitations, including difficulties in online construction and updating, particularly in high-dimensional or vision-based systems, and their inherent dependency on the quality of the underlying learned policy.

Design-time methods aim to reduce learning errors and mitigate covariate shift by integrating safety considerations into the policy learning process. These include off-policy approaches that augment datasets with corrective actions, on-policy methods that iteratively refine policies with expert feedback, and adversarial imitation learning techniques that use discriminators to improve policy fidelity. However, many existing design-time approaches do not explicitly model safety as a primary objective during data collection or policy optimization. This can result in policies that perform inadequately in rare but high-risk scenarios not sufficiently represented in the training data.

A notable prior work, SafeGIL, addresses this gap by injecting optimal adversarial disturbances into expert demonstrations to expose the expert to safety-critical states. This allows the learned policy to observe and imitate recovery behaviors. However, SafeGIL relies on HJ reachability analysis, which is computationally intensive and suffers from the curse of dimensionality, limiting its applicability to systems with low state dimensions (typically 5-6). Furthermore, it requires an analytical model of the robot's dynamics, making it unsuitable for black-box or general robotic systems where such models are unavailable or complex.

This paper introduces MPC-SafeGIL, positioned as a design-time framework that aims to overcome the scalability and model dependency limitations of previous safety-guided imitation learning approaches. By leveraging sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC), it seeks to provide a scalable method for synthesizing adversarial disturbances in high-dimensional and black-box dynamical systems, thereby enhancing the safety and robustness of imitation policies. The work contributes to the advancement of safe robot learning by integrating proactive safety considerations directly into the data collection phase, offering a complementary approach to existing online safety mechanisms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a design-time framework that enhances the safety of imitation learning policies by enabling them to learn robust recovery behaviors from intentionally generated safety-critical scenarios. This framework, termed MPC-SafeGIL, aims to address the limitations of existing safety-aware imitation learning techniques.

The motivation for this work arises from several critical challenges in the deployment of imitation learning:

1. **Safety Vulnerabilities in Learned Policies:** Policies learned through imitation learning can exhibit errors, particularly when faced with external disturbances or states outside the expert's demonstrated distribution (covariate shift). These errors can lead to safety violations in real-world applications, which are often safety-critical. This limits the trustworthiness and deployability of IL-based systems.
2. **Limitations of Deployment-Time Safety Mechanisms:** While safety filters offer a reactive layer of safety at test time, they face practical constraints. Constructing and updating these filters online can be computationally intensive, especially for high-dimensional state spaces or systems relying on high-dimensional sensor data like vision. Furthermore, the overall performance of such systems is bounded by the inherent safety and robustness of the underlying learned policy, as filters primarily prevent immediate hazards rather than fostering proactive safe behavior.
3. **Inadequate Safety Modeling in Existing Design-Time Approaches:** Many design-time methods, while improving imitation fidelity or robustness to general errors, do not explicitly incorporate safety as a primary objective during data collection or policy optimization. This can lead to a lack of exposure to rare but critical unsafe scenarios during training, resulting in policies that are unprepared for such events during deployment.
4. **Scalability Issues of Prior Safety-Guided Methods:** Previous research, such as SafeGIL, has demonstrated the effectiveness of injecting adversarial disturbances to guide expert demonstrations towards safety-critical states. However, these methods often rely on techniques like Hamilton-Jacobi reachability analysis, which are known to suffer from the curse of dimensionality. This significantly restricts their application to low-dimensional systems and necessitates analytical models of robot dynamics, precluding their use with high-dimensional or black-box systems common in modern robotics.

Motivated by these challenges, the authors aim to develop a scalable and practical solution that can operate in high-dimensional and black-box dynamical systems. The core idea is to leverage sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPC) to approximate worst-case adversarial disturbances. This approach is intended to proactively steer expert demonstrations into a broader range of safety-critical states, allowing the imitation policy to learn robust and effective recovery behaviors, thereby mitigating safety risks and improving task performance without requiring analytical dynamic models or interactive expert involvement during the learning phase.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed MPC-SafeGIL framework is a design-time approach that integrates safety considerations directly into the data collection process for imitation learning. The central idea involves injecting adversarial disturbances during expert demonstrations to expose the expert to safety-critical scenarios, thereby enabling the imitation policy to learn robust recovery behaviors.

**A. Injection of Adversarial Disturbance:**
The method abstracts potential test-time errors as adversarial disturbances, denoted as $d(x)$, which push the system towards unsafe regions. The objective is to compute an optimal adversarial disturbance $d^*(x)$ at each state $x$ and inject it into the expert policy $\pi^*(x)$ to form a guided expert policy $\pi_G(x) = \pi^*(x) + d^*(x)$. This guided expert then generates demonstrations that contain experiences of near-collision states and subsequent recovery actions, which are subsequently used to train the imitation policy $\pi_\theta(x)$.

**B. Computation of Adversarial Disturbance:**
The adversarial disturbance computation is inspired by Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis. Safety is defined by a failure set $L = \{x: l(x) \le 0\}$, where $l(x)$ is a cost function (e.g., signed distance to an obstacle). The goal is to find a disturbance that minimizes the minimal "distance" to the failure set over a time horizon $T$.

1. **Disturbance Computation via Robust Optimal Control:** The problem is initially formulated as a two-player zero-sum dynamic game. The expert controller $u(\cdot)$ aims to maximize the minimum distance to the failure set, while the adversarial disturbance $d(\cdot)$ attempts to minimize this distance, driving the system into $L$. The dynamics are described as $x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t + d_t)$, with symmetric input bounds for both control $u$ and disturbance $d$. This results in a max-min optimization problem: $\max_{u(\cdot)} \min_{d(\cdot)} J(x_t, u(\cdot), d(\cdot))$, where $J$ is the minimum value of $l(x_k)$ over the trajectory.

2. **Reduction to Single-Player Optimal Control:** Solving the two-player game is computationally challenging, especially in high-dimensional systems. To address this, the problem is reformulated as a single-player optimal control problem by assuming control-affine dynamics ($x_{k+1} = f_1(x_k) + f_2(x_k)u_k$ is a common form). Under this assumption, the control $u$ and disturbance $d$ can be combined into a single input $w = u+d$, with an adjusted bound $|w| \le \bar{u} - \bar{d}$. This transforms the original max-min problem into a single maximization problem: $\max_{w(\cdot)} J(x_t, w(\cdot))$. The authors provide a formal proof for this reduction in the appendix. This simplification is stated to significantly reduce computational burden while retaining the ability to capture adversarial effects, even serving as a useful approximation for non-affine dynamics through linearization.

3. **MPPI Solution for Disturbance:** The single-player optimal control problem is solved using Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI), a sampling-based Model Predictive Control algorithm.
 - **Sampling:** MPPI generates $N$ candidate control sequences (trajectories) of length $H$.
 - **Simulation & Cost Evaluation:** The system dynamics are simulated in parallel for each sequence, and the cost function $J$ (minimal $l(x_k)$) is evaluated for each resulting trajectory.
 - **Weighted Sum:** A weighted sum of the top $K$ control sequences (based on their costs and a temperature parameter $\lambda$) is used to compute the optimal control input $w^*(x)$.
 - **Disturbance Recovery:** The first control input of the optimal sequence, $w^*(x)$, is extracted. Based on the control-affine assumption and the bounds, the optimal adversarial disturbance $d^*(x)$ is then recovered. Specifically, $d^*(x)$ is set to either $+\bar{d}$ or $-\bar{d}$ depending on the sign of $w^*(x)$, pushing the system in the direction that minimizes $J(x)$.

**C. Data Collection and Policy Training:**
The computed $d^*(x)$ is injected into the expert's control input at each step, producing states that are closer to the safety boundary. The state-action pairs $(x_t, \pi^*(x_t))$ collected from these perturbed trajectories constitute the training dataset, which is then used to train the imitation policy $\pi_\theta(x)$ (e.g., via Behavior Cloning). The intention is that by observing the expert's recovery actions from these deliberately induced safety-critical states, the learned policy develops robust and safer behaviors.

The methodology emphasizes scalability due to MPPI's sampling-based nature, which is efficient for high-dimensional and black-box systems, and its parallelizability on GPUs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents an extensive set of experiments to validate MPC-SafeGIL, encompassing simulations and real-world hardware tests. The findings demonstrate improvements in both safety and task performance across diverse robotic platforms.

**A. Verification of Adversarial Disturbance:**
The first validation step compared the optimal adversarial disturbance computed by the MPPI-based method with that derived from HJ reachability analysis for a 3D Dubins car system. The disturbance fields generated by both methods showed visual consistency, with a low mean squared error (MSE) of 0.19 for the optimal disturbance in regions where the spatial gradient of the value function was non-zero. This suggests that the MPPI-based approach effectively captures the adversarial behavior identified by HJ reachability, while offering computational advantages for higher dimensions.

**B. Quadruped Navigation through Cluttered Environment:**
This case study involved a quadruped robot navigating obstacle-rich environments using LiDAR and proprioception in a high-dimensional, black-box simulation (Isaac Gym).
- **Safety and Performance:** MPC-SafeGIL consistently achieved lower collision rates and higher success rates compared to vanilla Behavior Cloning (BC) across varying numbers of demonstrations. For instance, with 80 demonstrations, MPC-SafeGIL reduced the collision rate to approximately 25%. This was attributed to MPC-SafeGIL guiding the expert closer to obstacles, enabling the policy to learn robust recovery maneuvers.
- **Comparison with GAIL:** When compared to Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL), MPC-SafeGIL showed higher success rates. GAIL, while sometimes achieving comparable collision rates with limited data, tended to produce overly conservative trajectories that failed to reach the goal. MPC-SafeGIL maintained both safety and goal-reaching objectives by injecting structured disturbances.
- **Comparison with Other Noise-Injection Schemes:** MPC-SafeGIL significantly outperformed baselines that employed random noise injection (Gaussian Noise BC, Uniform Noise BC) and DART (an online noise covariance estimation method). This highlights the advantage of *adversarial* and *structured* disturbance in exploring safety-critical states over general noise for improving both safety and task performance.
- **Effect of Disturbance Bound:** Experiments showed that increasing the adversarial disturbance bound (up to 70% of control bounds) generally led to lower collision rates, as stronger perturbations exposed the expert to a broader range of risky states, resulting in more informative demonstrations for robust policy learning.

**C. F1Tenth Racing Car:**
Evaluations on an F1Tenth racing car simulator, using high-dimensional LiDAR inputs to predict steering and velocity, assessed visuomotor policy learning and generalization.
- **Performance Comparison:** MPC-SafeGIL consistently achieved the longest distance traveled and the lowest collision rate compared to BC, Gaussian noise, uniform noise, and DAgger. DAgger exhibited worse performance than BC due to the scripted expert's unreliability when queried in arbitrary states, leading to inconsistent training data.
- **Integration with Safety Filters:** MPC-SafeGIL demonstrated synergistic benefits when integrated with a predictive safety filter at test time. MPC-SafeGIL combined with a filter significantly outperformed BC with a filter. Furthermore, MPC-SafeGIL *without* a filter achieved lower collision rates than BC *with* a filter, indicating its inherent safety-awareness acquired during training. This suggests that proactive safety integration during training is more effective than relying solely on reactive corrections at test time.
- **Generalization to Unseen Environments:** Policies trained with MPC-SafeGIL generalized more effectively to a novel, unseen track, achieving the highest average distance traveled (68.15m) compared to other methods. This was attributed to the diverse and safety-critical state exposure during training.

**D. Hardware Experiments:**
The method was tested on a Crazyflie 2.0 quadrotor, a resource-constrained platform, for navigation in cluttered environments using time-of-flight (ToF) sensors and an optical flow estimator.
- **Simulated vs. Real-world Safety:** In simulation, MPC-SafeGIL achieved a 73% safety rate, significantly higher than BC's 41%. Critically, in real-world deployment on a physical environment replicating the simulation, MPC-SafeGIL maintained consistent safety performance (9 out of 12 successful runs), whereas BC's safety rate dropped considerably (3 out of 12 successful runs), highlighting a sim-to-real gap for BC.
- **Analysis of Failure Modes:** The BC policy exhibited a recurring failure mode in the real world: retreating from an obstacle but then failing to reattempt forward progress, leading to indefinite backward motion. This was linked to the underrepresentation of states with significant negative velocity in the BC training dataset. In contrast, MPC-SafeGIL, due to its exposure to a wider distribution of safety-critical states, learned a robust recovery strategy: retreat, reorient, and safely navigate through openings.
- **Generalization to Complex Environments:** MPC-SafeGIL successfully navigated challenging real-world scenarios, including densely cluttered environments and dynamic scenes with a walking pedestrian, where the BC policy consistently failed.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that MPC-SafeGIL improves both the safety and task performance of imitation learning policies. It achieves this by generating more informative training data through carefully synthesized adversarial disturbances, leading to more robust policies that generalize better across different environments and transfer effectively from simulation to reality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of MPC-SafeGIL represents a significant contribution to the field of safe robot learning, particularly in addressing the limitations of deploying imitation learning policies in safety-critical applications. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Addresses Scalability for Safety-Aware IL:** MPC-SafeGIL directly tackles the critical challenge of scalability that has hindered prior safety-guided imitation learning methods, such as those relying on Hamilton-Jacobi reachability analysis. By leveraging sampling-based Model Predictive Control (MPPI), it provides a framework that can operate effectively in high-dimensional state spaces and with complex, black-box dynamical systems, which are pervasive in modern robotics. This expands the applicability of principled safety-aware training to a much broader range of robotic platforms and tasks.
2. **Enhances Robustness and Recovery Behaviors:** The core mechanism of injecting optimal adversarial disturbances during expert demonstrations enables the learned policies to observe and internalize robust recovery strategies from safety-critical situations. This proactive approach leads to policies that are inherently more resilient to unforeseen perturbations and errors during deployment, reducing collision rates and improving overall reliability.
3. **Improves Generalization Capabilities:** By intentionally exposing the expert to a wider and more meaningful distribution of safety-critical states, MPC-SafeGIL fosters better generalization. Policies trained with this method demonstrated superior performance and safety in novel, unseen environments and across the sim-to-real gap, indicating that the learned recovery behaviors are not merely rote memorization but adaptable strategies.
4. **Complements Existing Safety Mechanisms:** The research demonstrates that MPC-SafeGIL is not mutually exclusive with deployment-time safety mechanisms like safety filters. Instead, it can seamlessly integrate with them, leading to synergistic improvements in safety and task performance. Importantly, the study shows that policies trained with MPC-SafeGIL can achieve higher safety levels even *without* a reactive filter than traditionally trained policies *with* one, highlighting the value of safety integration at the training phase.
5. **Practical Applicability for Real-world Systems:** The use of MPPI, which is highly parallelizable on GPUs and does not require analytical dynamic models, makes MPC-SafeGIL a practical solution for complex robotic systems. Its successful validation on a quadruped, an F1Tenth race car, and a real-world Crazyflie quadrotor, including navigation in dynamic and cluttered environments, underscores its readiness for application in diverse and challenging real-world scenarios.
6. **Shifts Paradigm Towards Proactive Safety:** The work advocates for a shift from purely reactive safety interventions at test time to a more proactive integration of safety considerations during the policy training and data collection phases. This paradigm has the potential to yield more fundamentally safe and trustworthy autonomous systems, thereby accelerating the deployment of imitation learning in safety-critical domains such as autonomous driving, aerial navigation, and collaborative robotics.

In conclusion, MPC-SafeGIL offers a robust and scalable methodology for embedding safety awareness into imitation learning, addressing critical gaps in current approaches and paving the way for more reliable and deployable intelligent robotic systems.
