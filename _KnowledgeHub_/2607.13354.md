# A Hybrid Sampling-Based Trajectory Planner with Game-Theoretic Guidance for Autonomous Racing

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Technical University of Munich developed a hybrid trajectory planner that integrates game-theoretic reasoning into a sampling-based framework for autonomous racing. This approach enables proactive strategic maneuvers like blocking, reducing collisions from 14 to 6 and improving net position change from 0.54 to 0.61 compared to a reactive baseline, while maintaining online computational efficiency with 10-17 ms latency.

## Problem

- Existing autonomous racing planners typically treat opponent vehicles as dynamic obstacles, leading to reactive collision avoidance rather than proactive strategic interaction.
- Explicitly modeling multi-agent interactions using dynamic game solvers is often computationally prohibitive for online, high-frequency operation in high-dimensional state spaces.
- Pure learning-based methods, such as reinforcement learning, face challenges with extensive training requirements, safety concerns, and the sim-to-real gap for real-world deployment on racecars.

## Method

- A hybrid planning architecture was introduced, augmenting a ==sampling-based local planner== with an offline-learned, ==game-theoretic interaction module==.
- The game-theoretic module approximates ==non-cooperative dynamic games== using an ==α-potential function==, which is learned via neural networks to capture joint strategic incentives and ensure collision avoidance.
- During online operation, the module computes an ==Interaction Reference Path (IRP)== through ==gradient ascent== on the learned potential function; this IRP then acts as a dynamic strategic bias within the sampling planner's cost function.

## Results

- The hybrid planner demonstrated emergent defensive blocking behaviors in qualitative scenarios, enabling the autonomous vehicle to maintain its leading position against faster opponents, unlike a purely reactive baseline.
- In **200** randomized scenarios, the interaction-aware planner reduced collisions from **14 to 6** and improved the average net position change from **0.54 to 0.61** compared to the reactive baseline planner.
- The game-theoretic module maintained a low computational latency of **10-17 ms** for strategy optimization and IRP generation, demonstrating its suitability for online deployment in high-frequency autonomous racing systems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling high-level strategic decisions from trajectory generation, where a game-theoretic module dictates spatial intent and a sampling planner ensures kinematic feasibility, can enable competitive behaviors with online computational efficiency.
> - An offline-learned α-potential function can successfully translate complex multi-agent interactions into interpretable strategy parameters (e.g., velocity scaling, lateral deviation magnitude, blocking aggressiveness) that guide a local planner.
> - Integrating a dynamically generated geometric guideline (IRP) as a strategic cost term within a sampling-based planner's objective function allows for emergent proactive maneuvers, such as blocking, that go beyond reactive collision avoidance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous racing serves as a demanding environment for pushing the capabilities of vehicle control and perception to their physical limits. While significant progress has been made in handling vehicle dynamics, autonomous systems often exhibit limitations in competitive multi-agent scenarios, particularly when compared to human drivers' strategic decision-making. Existing autonomous racing planners typically fall into categories such as graph-based, optimization-based, or sampling-based methods.

Graph-based planners (e.g.,–) can determine trajectories but face challenges balancing grid resolution with computational feasibility at high speeds. Optimization-based frameworks, often formulated as Optimal Control Problems (OCPs) (e.g.,–), can incorporate non-linear dynamics and dynamic obstacles but frequently encounter computational intractability or convergence issues online due to the non-convex nature of adversarial racing. Sampling-based planners (e.g.,–) offer robustness and parallelizability by evaluating numerous dynamically feasible candidate trajectories against a cost function. However, a common limitation across these classical approaches is their tendency to treat opponent vehicles solely as dynamic obstacles, leading to reactive collision avoidance rather than proactive strategic interaction. This often results in conservative behavior that does not fully exploit competitive advantages such as strategic blocking or overtaking.

To address the lack of interactive behavior, game theory has been applied to model multi-agent interdependencies, particularly in urban traffic scenarios (e.g.,) and adversarial racing contexts (e.g.,–,). These approaches can model interactions as sequential leader-follower games or utilize methods like Iterated Best Response. However, directly solving full dynamic game equilibria in continuous, high-dimensional state spaces for high-frequency online operation is computationally expensive, often necessitating simplified kinematic models or slow update rates.

Pure learning-based methods, such as reinforcement learning (e.g.,–), can capture complex interactions and have achieved results in simulation, but their deployment on full-scale racecars is constrained by extensive training requirements, safety concerns, and the sim-to-real gap. Hybrid architectures aim to combine the strengths of learned models with deterministic frameworks. Kalaria et al. approximated non-cooperative dynamic games using an offline-learned α-potential function, maximizing it at runtime within a Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework. However, this approach directly enforces game-theoretic outputs as reference trajectory constraints, potentially limiting flexibility.

This work positions itself to address a research gap: the development of a robust, online-capable, sampling-based planner that incorporates game-theoretic reasoning to enable interactive and strategic maneuvers, moving beyond purely reactive collision avoidance, without incurring the high computational burden typically associated with full dynamic game solvers. The paper proposes a hybrid architecture that integrates game-theoretic strategic intent into a sampling-based trajectory planner, aiming to provide a balance between robust trajectory generation and competitive decision-making.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed performance gap between human and autonomous drivers in multi-vehicle racing scenarios. While autonomous systems excel at pushing physical limits, they frequently lack the strategic foresight and interactive capabilities of human drivers who proactively anticipate and influence opponents for positional advantage. Existing motion planning algorithms for autonomous racing face a trade-off: methods that explicitly model multi-agent interactions (e.g., dynamic game solvers, optimal control) often become computationally prohibitive online, while computationally robust sampling-based planners typically treat opponents as passive obstacles, leading to reactive and conservative behaviors.

The key objectives of this paper are:
- To introduce a hybrid motion planning framework that embeds game-theoretic strategies directly into a sampling-based trajectory planning architecture. This aims to decouple high-level strategic decisions from trajectory generation, allowing the game-theoretic module to dictate spatial intent while the sampling planner ensures kinematic feasibility and robust collision avoidance.
- To formulate an Interaction Reference Path (IRP) generated through online gradient ascent on an offline-learned α-potential function. This IRP is designed to serve as a dynamic geometric guide for competitive maneuvers, acting as a strategic bias within the cost function of the sampling planner.
- To demonstrate the framework's ability to generate emergent defensive behaviors, specifically blocking maneuvers, that are not reproducible by standard reactive planners.
- To quantitatively evaluate the trade-off between strategic positioning and lap time, and to demonstrate the online feasibility of the proposed game-theoretic module within typical autonomous racing planning frequencies.

In summary, the motivation is to enable autonomous racing agents to exhibit proactive, strategic behaviors akin to human drivers, such as blocking or effective overtaking, while maintaining the computational efficiency and robustness required for real-time operation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a hybrid trajectory planning framework that integrates a game-theoretic reasoning module into a sampling-based local planner. The overall architecture is depicted in Figure 2.

**A. Hybrid Planning Architecture:** The core of the trajectory generation is a sampling-based local planner, adapted from, operating in the Frenet reference frame (s, d). This planner uses an offline-optimized raceline as a base reference. It generates a pool of candidate trajectories by combining quartic polynomial longitudinal profiles (sampling target end velocities) and quintic polynomial lateral profiles (varying target lateral states). These candidates are filtered for feasibility against curvature, track bounds, and acceleration limits. Remaining feasible trajectories are evaluated using a baseline performance cost function, Jperf (τ), which is a weighted sum of penalties for deviation from target velocity, raceline curvature, lateral offsets from the global raceline, and proximity to opponents. To enable strategic behaviors, a game-theoretic module augments this reactive planner. This module computes an Interaction Reference Path (IRP), which is a geometric guideline encoding an approximate Nash-equilibrium strategy. The IRP then serves as an additional dynamic bias, Jstrat (τ), within the sampling planner’s cost function.

**B. Interaction Modeling via α-Potential Games:** The multi-agent interactions are modeled using an α-potential game formulation, building upon α-RACER. This framework approximates a non-cooperative dynamic game by employing a shared potential function Φ(·) that captures joint strategic incentives. The framework utilizes a two-stage learning process:
1. **Value Function Approximation:** Individual value functions Vi (x) are learned for each agent. These functions estimate the expected future progress over a fixed horizon given the current joint state x. A key difference from prior work is the introduction of an explicit collision handling mechanism during training data generation. Colliding vehicles incur severe velocity and progress penalties, embedding a high collision cost into the learned value functions to promote safe positional advantages. A neural network is used to learn Vi (x) by minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted progress and penalized ground truth displacement, using stochastic gradient descent (SGD).
2. **Potential Function Training:** A global potential function Φ(x, θ) is learned as another neural network. Its weights are warm-started by concatenating the pre-trained individual value functions. The training objective is to minimize the discrepancy between the gradient of the potential function and the individual value gradients, ensuring that maximizing Φ approximates a multi-agent Nash equilibrium. The formulation maps the joint state x to a reduced, 4-dimensional strategy vector θ, specifically designed to govern the ego vehicle's spatial intent, thereby decoupling it from dynamic tracking weights or rigid trajectory constraints.

**C. Online Inference and IRP Generation:** During online operation, the interaction module determines the optimal strategy parameters θ ∗ for the ego vehicle at each time step. Instead of solving a computationally expensive game, a gradient-ascent optimization is performed on the learned potential function: θ ∗ = arg maxθ Φ(xt, θ). This optimization uses backpropagation through the neural network Φ(·) for a fixed budget of 20 iterations to maintain online feasibility. The optimized strategy vector θ comprises four interpretable parameters:
- pvel: Scales the target velocity profile.
- pamp: Defines the maximum magnitude of lateral deviation.
- pdecay: Governs the spatial decay of the interaction effect.
- pblock: Modulates blocking aggressiveness based on relative velocity.

These parameters are then used to construct the Interaction Reference Path (IRP), a geometric reference for defensive blocking and active overtaking. The IRP is generated by laterally perturbing the optimal raceline Γ using lateral shift functions. For a blocking maneuver against an opponent i, the lateral shift profile dbl,i (s) is calculated based on the opponent’s lateral position di, relative velocity ∆vi, and spatial distance (s − si). The equation is given as: dbl,i (s) = Iblock · (di) 1 − e −pblock ∆vi e −pdecay (s−si)2 where Iblock is an indicator function. For overtaking, dot,i (s) is similarly calculated using pamp. The final IRP lateral profile dIRP (s) is a spatial superposition of the base raceline and the interaction-induced lateral shifts from all relevant opponents, clipped to track boundaries.

**D. Cost Function Integration:** The game-theoretic module is integrated by augmenting the sampling planner’s baseline performance cost Jperf (τ) with strategic cost terms derived from the optimized θ.
1. **Velocity Adjustment:** The pvel parameter scales the nominal target velocity vnom of the global raceline. The velocity tracking penalty c ∆v in Jperf (τ) is adjusted to penalize deviations from this scaled target: c ∆v (τ ) =Z (vτ (t) − pvel · vnom (t))2dt.
2. **Lateral Strategy Cost:** An additional strategy cost Jstrat (τ) is defined to penalize the lateral deviation between the candidate trajectory dτ (s) and the dynamically generated IRP dIRP (s): Jstrat (τ ) =Zdτ (s) − dIRP (s)ds. The total cost for trajectory selection is then a weighted sum: Jtotal (τ ) = Jperf (τ ) + wstrat · Jstrat (τ ), where wstrat is a tunable weight controlling the spatial bias towards the IRP. This design allows the game-theoretic module to guide the planner strategically, while the hard constraints and baseline penalizations of the sampling planner ensure dynamic feasibility and collision avoidance.

**E. Experimental Setup:** The framework was evaluated in a high-fidelity multi-agent simulation environment based on an autonomous racing software stack, using the Yas Marina Circuit. Opponent vehicles utilized the baseline sampling-based planner without game-theoretic capabilities. A dataset of 2247 simulated racing episodes was generated for training, involving three agents with randomized interaction parameters (Table I), and an explicit collision handling mechanism to penalize crashes. Evaluation included:
- **Qualitative Scenarios:** Two specific tests against a single opponent: "Straight Line Defense (T1)" and "Hairpin Defense (T5)," where the ego vehicle starts ahead and the opponent has a speed advantage or attempts an overtake. These scenarios compared the hybrid planner to the reactive baseline.
- **Quantitative Analysis:** A large-scale randomized scenario set comprising 200 episodes with two opponent vehicles, varying starting velocities, track locations, and grid positions for the ego vehicle. Metrics included the number of collisions and Net Position Change (∆P).
- **Interaction Weight Analysis:** A study varying wstrat logarithmically from 0 to 108 to assess its impact on collisions and ∆P.
- **Runtime Performance:** Measurement of the computational overhead of the game-theoretic module.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Training Performance:**
The training process for the interaction module demonstrated convergence. The Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss for the individual value functions decreased by 99.92% over 50,000 iterations, indicating that the network effectively learned to map vehicle states to expected future progress. The potential function training also showed a loss reduction of 99.82%, with the curve stabilizing around a consistent mean, which suggests that the potential function successfully captured the joint interaction dynamics of the multi-agent system.

**B. Qualitative Analysis: Emergent Blocking:**
In the "Straight Line Defense (T1)" scenario, the baseline reactive planner, by strictly following the optimal raceline, resulted in a collision with an approaching opponent due to speed differential. In contrast, the proposed hybrid planner generated an IRP with a calculated lateral deviation, guiding the ego vehicle to occupy the inside line. This action induced a deceleration in the opponent's reactive planner, preventing an overtake and allowing the ego vehicle to maintain its leading position.
Similarly, in the "Hairpin Defense (T5)" scenario, the baseline planner maintained the nominal raceline, which enabled the opponent to execute an inner overtake. The hybrid planner, however, shifted the IRP toward the inner track boundary early in the approach. By tracking this strategic bias, the ego vehicle constrained the opponent to a dynamically inferior outer trajectory, retaining its track position and increasing the longitudinal gap upon corner exit. These qualitative results indicate that the gradient-based optimization of interaction parameters successfully translates game-theoretic evaluations into dynamically feasible and competitively advantageous spatial trajectory biases.

**C. Quantitative Analysis:**
Across 200 randomized scenarios involving two opponents, the proposed hybrid interaction-aware planner demonstrated improved performance compared to the baseline reactive planner.
- **Collisions:** The baseline reactive planner recorded 14 collisions, while the hybrid planner reduced this to 6 incidents. This suggests that the strategic guidance does not compromise safety, and may contribute to safer interactions by anticipating opponent actions.
- **Net Position Change (∆P):** The baseline achieved an average net position change of ∆P = 0.54. The hybrid planner achieved an average ∆P = 0.61, indicating an improved ability for the ego vehicle to gain and defend positions effectively.

**D. Impact of the Interaction Weight (wstrat):** The parameter wstrat, which balances the strategic cost with baseline performance, significantly influenced the framework's performance.
- **Low Strategy Cost (wstrat = 102):** At this setting, trajectory selection was predominantly governed by the baseline performance cost. The IRP's lateral bias was insufficient to induce effective strategic shifts, leading to suboptimal competitive standing (∆P = 0.56) and 11 collisions.
- **Balanced Configuration (wstrat = 104):** This configuration yielded the highest net position change (∆P = 0.61) and a reduced collision count (6 collisions). This suggests that a balanced weighting allows for strategic lateral shifts while maintaining dynamic feasibility and collision avoidance through the baseline cost.
- **Over-Aggressive Tracking (wstrat ≥ 106):** When the strategy cost overshadowed baseline metrics, enforcing strict IRP tracking. This resulted in dynamically suboptimal trajectories, a sharp increase in collision rates (9 collisions for 106, 21 for 108), and a degradation of longitudinal progress (∆P = 0.48 for 106, 0.32 for 108). This indicates that the IRP, as a purely geometric projection, requires sufficient influence from the underlying kinematic and safety considerations of the sampling planner.

**E. Runtime Performance:**
The computational overhead of the game-theoretic module was analyzed for online feasibility. The strategy parameter optimization stabilized within 5 to 10 iterations. The total inference time for optimizing θ and generating the IRP consistently ranged between 10 ms and 17 ms across all experiments. This latency is considered suitable for online deployment within typical autonomous racing planning frequencies (10 Hz to 20 Hz).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant step toward enabling more sophisticated and competitive behavior in autonomous racing. By proposing a hybrid architecture that integrates game-theoretic reasoning into a sampling-based motion planner, the work addresses the long-standing challenge of balancing high-fidelity vehicle dynamics at the limits of handling with strategic decision-making in competitive multi-agent scenarios.

The primary significance lies in demonstrating that a standard sampling-based planner can be guided to exhibit complex strategic behaviors, such as blocking, without requiring the computational resources of full dynamic game solvers or the extensive training typical of pure reinforcement learning approaches. The introduction of the Interaction Reference Path (IRP) as a dynamic cost bias provides a novel mechanism for embedding strategic intent into the planning process in a computationally efficient manner.

The findings indicate that the approach can lead to:
- **Enhanced Competitive Performance:** The quantitative results show an improvement in net position change and a reduction in collisions, suggesting that the strategic guidance allows the autonomous vehicle to secure and defend its position more effectively.
- **Increased Safety through Strategic Anticipation:** By proactively influencing opponent trajectories, the system demonstrates an ability to avoid collisions more effectively than a purely reactive planner, as seen in the reduced collision count.
- **Practical Applicability:** The low computational latency of the game-theoretic module (10-17 ms) indicates its suitability for online deployment in high-frequency autonomous racing software stacks.

The potential impact of this work extends to the broader field of autonomous driving beyond racing, particularly in scenarios requiring interactive and strategic decision-making in mixed traffic environments. The framework offers a transferable methodology for integrating learned strategic models into robust, real-time motion planners.

Future work outlined by the authors includes validating the framework on a real-world autonomous race vehicle to assess its robustness against perception noise and model mismatches. This is crucial for bridging the sim-to-real gap. Additionally, investigating an adaptive, situation-aware scheduling mechanism for the strategy weight wstrat is proposed. Such a mechanism could dynamically adjust the balance between strategic aggressiveness and kinematic feasibility based on the complexity of local interactions, potentially leading to even more robust and adaptable strategic behaviors in dynamic racing environments.
