# Compute as Teacher: Turning Inference Compute Into Reference-Free Supervision

> [!summary] Summary
> A new method, Compute as Teacher (CaT), generates supervision signals for large language models in post-training scenarios lacking traditional ground truth or programmatic verifiers. It leverages inference compute to synthesize reference-free supervision, leading to relative performance increases of up to 33% on MATH-500 and 30% on HealthBench.

## Problem

- Post-training large language models (LLMs) often lack sufficient human-labeled reference data or external programmatic verifiers for specialized tasks.
- This data scarcity is particularly acute in "non-verifiable" domains like creative writing or open-ended dialogue, where objective ground truth is elusive.
- Existing alternatives like costly human annotation or LLM-as-a-judge methods suffer from inconsistencies and can introduce biases.

## Method

- ==Compute as Teacher (CaT)== synthesizes a high-quality reference by leveraging multiple parallel rollouts generated by the current policy for a given prompt.
- A ==frozen anchor policy== then integrates evidence from these rollouts to construct an improved reference response, without seeing the original prompt.
- This synthesized reference is converted into reward signals (binary for verifiable tasks, ==rubric-based== for non-verifiable tasks) to fine-tune the policy using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO).

## Results

- CaT-RL consistently improved model performance across tasks, achieving up to **33%** relative gain on MATH-500 and **30%** on HealthBench for Llama 3.1 8B, surpassing the initial policy and its own inference-time teacher signal.
- Self-proposed rubrics for non-verifiable tasks were highly effective, outperforming direct LLM-as-a-judge methods and demonstrating quality comparable to, or sometimes exceeding, human expert annotations.
- CaT exhibited a unique self-correction ability, producing correct answers that disagreed with all of the initial rollouts it was conditioned on in approximately **1%** of questions, demonstrating its generative power beyond simple selection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - CaT synthesizes entirely new reference answers by reconciling multiple rollouts, enabling it to correct errors even when individual model generations are flawed or disagree with the majority.
> - For non-verifiable tasks, the method uses self-proposed rubrics, decomposing complex judgments into atomic, verifiable criteria, which leads to more robust and less biased reward signals than direct LLM judging.
> - The approach fosters a virtuous learning cycle where an improving policy generates higher quality rollouts, leading to better synthesized supervision and further model enhancement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) for specialized tasks typically relies on two primary post-training paradigms: supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with human-labeled reference answers or reinforcement learning (RL) with rewards derived from programmatic checkers. However, both approaches present significant limitations that hinder the widespread application of LLMs, especially in diverse and complex domains.

SFT requires extensive, high-quality human annotations, which are often costly, time-consuming, difficult to scale, and can be prone to human bias or disagreement, particularly in nuanced qualitative domains. RL with programmatic checkers, while effective, is restricted to "verifiable" domains like mathematics or code generation, where correctness can be formally and deterministically computed. Many valuable real-world applications, such as healthcare guidance, freeform dialogue, and creative writing, fall into "non-verifiable" categories. In these domains, answers can be qualitative, multiple valid responses may exist, experts might disagree, and deterministic rule-checking is impractical or impossible.

Current alternative methods for non-verifiable domains, such as reliance on large-scale annotation pipelines, suffer from scalability issues. Another approach involves using LLMs themselves as judges to assign coarse scores to freeform outputs. However, this "LLM-as-a-judge" paradigm has known problems, including inconsistency, verbosity bias, and susceptibility to reward hacking.

This paper positions itself by addressing the fundamental challenge of acquiring learning signals when explicit ground truth or scalable human supervision is unavailable, particularly in non-verifiable settings. It seeks to bridge the gap between expensive human-labeled data and restrictive programmatic checkers by exploring a novel source of supervision: the inference compute generated by the model itself. By leveraging parallel rollouts, which sample the model's inherent uncertainty, the proposed framework aims to extract and consolidate information that can then be used to train and improve the model. This work extends existing concepts of self-training and knowledge distillation by reconciling multiple samples rather than trusting a single self-label and amortizes the benefits of test-time scaling methods into model weights, offering a more efficient and generalizable approach to post-training LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether inference compute can serve as a substitute for missing supervision in the post-training of large language models. The paper specifically asks: "Can inference compute substitute for missing supervision?"

This objective is motivated by several critical challenges in the current landscape of LLM development and deployment:

1. **Scarcity and Cost of Human Labels**: Post-training methods like supervised fine-tuning (SFT) are heavily reliant on human-annotated reference answers. Acquiring such labels is a laborious and expensive process, especially for specialized skills or complex domains. As LLMs are applied to an increasing diversity of tasks, the need for domain-specific expert annotations becomes a significant bottleneck, limiting the scalability and breadth of LLM applications.

2. **Limitations of Programmatic Verifiers**: Reinforcement learning (RL) often leverages programmatic checkers to provide verifiable rewards. However, these checkers are only applicable in narrow, "verifiable" domains like mathematics or code, where correct answers can be objectively and programmatically determined. Many valuable tasks, particularly those involving qualitative judgment, open-ended responses, or subjective criteria (e.g., healthcare advice, creative writing, nuanced dialogue), lack such formal correctness criteria.

3. **Challenges in Non-Verifiable Domains**: For "non-verifiable" domains, where answers are qualitative, multiple valid solutions may exist, and experts can reasonably disagree, existing methods face considerable difficulties. Practitioners often resort to:
 - **Annotation Pipelines**: These are difficult to scale and maintain.
 - **LLM-as-a-Judge Feedback**: While seemingly scalable, this approach suffers from well-documented issues such as inconsistency across prompts, bias towards verbose or stylistically fluent responses, and susceptibility to "reward hacking," where models optimize for the judge's perceived preferences rather than genuine quality.

The primary motivation is to overcome these supervision limitations by developing a framework that can generate effective learning signals internally, from the model's own inference process, without external human labels or domain-specific programmatic checkers. This would enable the application of reinforcement learning in domains previously deemed intractable due to the absence of reliable reward mechanisms, thereby broadening the utility and autonomy of LLM training. The paper aims to demonstrate that such a framework can not only enable training in challenging domains but also improve model performance while significantly reducing the computational cost associated with repeated inference-time aggregation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose **Compute as Teacher (CaT)**, a framework designed to convert inference compute into supervision for RL training. CaT operates through two main components: reference estimation and reward derivation.

#### 3.1. Reference Estimation

Given a prompt `q`, the current policy `πt` generates `G` parallel rollouts (`o1:G`). These rollouts are samples from the model's uncertainty distribution, with differences highlighting areas where the model is less certain. Reference estimation aggregates these `G` rollouts into a single "pseudo-reference" `s`, which then serves as the supervision target. The framework is designed to be agnostic to the specific aggregation method.

The paper explores several aggregation strategies:
- **Majority Vote**: `s` is the most common final answer among `o1:G`. This is applicable only in verifiable domains where answers can be directly compared.
- **Best-of-N**: `s` is selected as `arg max_oi f(oi)` for a scoring function `f`. This function could be based on perplexity, confidence heuristics, or LLM judge scores.
- **Synthesis (Primary Strategy)**: This is a generative strategy where a frozen anchor model (`π0`, typically the initial policy before training) conditions on the set of rollouts `o1:G` and generates a new response `s`. The anchor is prompted to reconcile information across the rollouts into a single, improved response. Synthesis is hypothesized to construct responses that combine correct fragments from multiple rollouts, potentially exceeding the quality of any individual rollout. The original prompt `q` is omitted from the anchor's input for simplicity, and a frozen anchor is used to decouple exploration (by `πt`) from estimation (by `π0`).

#### 3.2. Reward Derivation

Once a pseudo-reference `s` is estimated, rewards `R(oi; s)` are derived for each rollout `oi` to facilitate RL training. The method for reward derivation varies based on the domain type.

- **Verifiable Domains**: For domains where correctness can be programmatically checked (e.g., math problems), the reward `R_ver(o; s)` is binary: `1` if the answer extracted from rollout `o` matches the answer from the pseudo-reference `s`, and `0` otherwise. This approach is intentionally simple, as the complexity in verifiable domains is primarily in reference estimation.

- **Non-Verifiable Domains (Self-Proposed Rubrics)**: For qualitative, non-verifiable domains, direct programmatic checking is impossible, and coarse LLM-as-a-judge scoring is prone to inconsistencies and biases. The authors introduce "self-proposed rubrics" to address this.
 - **Rubric Generation**: The frozen anchor model (`π0`) generates a set of binary criteria, `R = {r1..., rn}`, from the pseudo-reference `s`. Each criterion `rj` is a specific, verifiable statement capturing an important property or characteristic of `s`. The prompt for rubric generation instructs the model to create at least five specific criteria.
 - **Rubric Scoring**: An independent judge model (`πJ`, typically a more capable LLM like GPT-4o) evaluates each rollout `oi` against every criterion `rj` in the generated rubric, determining whether `oi` satisfies `rj` with a "yes" or "no" answer. The final reward `R_rub(o; R)` for a rollout `o` is the fraction of criteria satisfied according to the judge: `(1/n) * Σ_j=1^n 1[πJ(o, rj) = "yes"]`.
 This decomposition into binary checks is intended to improve reliability, auditability, and reduce surface-form bias compared to direct LLM-as-a-judge scoring.

#### 3.3. Training

The CaT framework is instantiated with **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**, a memory-efficient variant of PPO. GRPO already samples `G` parallel rollouts for advantage estimation. CaT reuses these rollouts, adding minimal overhead: one synthesis call for `s`, and for non-verifiable domains, one rubric generation call and `n * G` parallel judge calls for scoring. The policy `πt` is updated using the group-normalized advantages derived from the calculated rewards.

#### 3.4. Inference-Time Use

After training, the policy `πt` produces a single response at inference time. This trained policy is expected to match or exceed the quality achieved by inference-time aggregation methods (which require `G` rollouts plus an aggregation step) while using significantly less test-time compute. This effectively amortizes the compute cost of aggregation into the model's weights.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

Experiments are conducted on:
- **HealthBench**: 5,000 freeform healthcare conversations (non-verifiable domain). Evaluation uses physician-designed rubrics and a GPT-4o judge.
- **MATH-500**: 500 competition math problems (verifiable domain). Evaluation is based on correct final answers.
Three instruction-tuned models (Gemma 3 4B, Qwen 3 4B, Llama 3.1 8B) are evaluated. `G=8` rollouts are used.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presented several key findings demonstrating the efficacy and characteristics of the Compute as Teacher (CaT) framework:

1. **Overall Performance Improvement**: CaT consistently improved performance over the initial, untrained policies across all models and both domain types. On HealthBench (non-verifiable), relative improvements reached up to +30%. On MATH-500 (verifiable), relative improvements were up to +33%.

2. **Compute Efficiency and Quality Amortization**: A significant finding is that models trained with CaT matched or exceeded the quality of inference-time synthesis, but with a 9x reduction in test-time compute on HealthBench. This indicates that CaT successfully amortizes the cost of multi-rollout inference-time aggregation into the model's weights, allowing the trained policy to achieve high performance with a single forward pass.

3. **Effectiveness of Self-Proposed Rubrics**:
 - **Competition with Expert Annotations**: In non-verifiable domains (HealthBench), self-proposed rubrics, generated by the model itself from pseudo-references, performed comparably to physician-annotated rubrics on two out of three models. This suggests that the model can generate effective evaluation criteria without human input.
 - **Superiority over Model-as-Judge**: Self-proposed rubrics consistently outperformed direct "model-as-judge" scoring on all three models, supporting the hypothesis that decomposed, binary criteria lead to more stable and reliable rewards compared to coarse-grained judgments.
 - **RL with Rubrics Outperforms SFT**: When comparing CaT (RL with rubric rewards) against supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on the same synthesized pseudo-references, CaT achieved superior performance across all models, often substantially (e.g., Llama 3.1 8B: 0.38 vs 0.28). This aligns with prior research indicating that RL can generalize better from limited data than SFT.

4. **Synthesis vs. Selection Baselines**: At inference time (without RL training), synthesis demonstrated strong performance as a reference estimation strategy:
 - It consistently outperformed all selection baselines (e.g., single sample, majority vote, best-of-N, min perplexity, mutual predictability) on HealthBench, the non-verifiable domain.
 - On MATH-500, synthesis remained competitive with or slightly exceeded the best selection methods, indicating its versatility across domain types.

5. **Mechanism of Synthesis**:
 - **Disagreement and Correction**: Analysis showed that synthesis disagreed with majority vote on 5-7% of questions and with Self-BoN on 7-10%. Crucially, when synthesis disagreed, it was correct in a substantial majority of cases (82-86% against majority vote, 70-82% against Self-BoN). This indicates that synthesis performs a beneficial reconciliation rather than random deviation.
 - **Correction Beyond Rollouts**: On approximately 1% of questions, synthesis produced a correct answer even when *all* individual rollouts were incorrect, demonstrating its ability to construct new, superior responses by integrating complementary correct fragments from disparate rollouts. This capability is impossible for pure selection methods.
 - **Reasoning Over Multiple Rollouts**: Further analysis confirmed that synthesis benefits from observing multiple rollouts rather than just acting as an additional independent sample. Its outputs showed similar overall length but fewer equations and lines compared to individual rollouts, suggesting it summarizes and reconciles existing reasoning rather than regenerating detailed derivations. Synthesis also displayed more "verification cues" and less step-by-step reasoning compared to raw policy rollouts.

6. **Learning Plateau**: The research observed that training gains plateau once rollouts converge and become less diverse. At this point, the synthesis-generated pseudo-reference offers diminishing signal over individual rollouts, limiting further improvement, which is consistent with known entropy collapse phenomena in RL fine-tuning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Compute as Teacher (CaT) framework presents several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of large language model development and application:

1. **Enabling Reference-Free Reinforcement Learning in Non-Verifiable Domains**: CaT directly addresses one of the most pressing challenges in LLM post-training: the absence of ground-truth labels or programmatic checkers in non-verifiable domains. By introducing "self-proposed rubrics" as a robust reward derivation mechanism, CaT makes RL feasible in complex, qualitative areas like healthcare guidance, creative writing, and open-ended dialogue. This expands the applicability of sophisticated RL training methods to a wider range of high-value, real-world tasks where human annotation is impractical or expert consensus is elusive.

2. **Amortization of Inference Compute**: The framework demonstrates that the computational cost of inference-time aggregation (e.g., generating multiple rollouts and then selecting or synthesizing the best response) can be amortized into the model's weights during training. This allows a trained model to achieve similar or superior performance using a single forward pass at test time, offering a 9x reduction in test-time compute. This is particularly impactful for deployment, making high-quality model responses more efficient and cost-effective for end-users, especially in latency-sensitive applications.

3. **Unified and Practical Training Framework**: CaT offers a unified "plug-and-play" framework that functions effectively across both verifiable and non-verifiable domains without requiring significant architectural modifications or domain-specific reward models. Its integration with existing RL algorithms like GRPO means it can reuse compute already spent on advantage estimation, making it a practical and drop-in solution for practitioners.

4. **Addressing the Human Annotation Bottleneck**: By leveraging the model's own inference capabilities to generate supervision, CaT significantly reduces or eliminates the dependency on expensive, time-consuming, and hard-to-scale human annotation pipelines. As human labels become an increasing bottleneck for developing specialized LLMs, especially in niche or rapidly evolving domains, CaT provides a scalable alternative for continuous improvement and adaptation.

5. **Improved Robustness and Interpretability of Rewards**: The use of self-proposed rubrics, which decompose fuzzy judgments into specific, binary criteria, contributes to more reliable and auditable reward signals. This approach mitigates common issues associated with direct LLM-as-a-judge scoring, such as inconsistency, verbosity bias, and reward hacking. The auditability of rubrics also aids in debugging and understanding why a model receives a certain reward, fostering more transparent model development.

6. **Advancement in Self-Supervised Learning**: CaT pushes the boundaries of self-generated supervision by demonstrating that models can not only learn from their own outputs but also reconcile disagreements and synthesize novel, improved responses from a distribution of possibilities. This highlights a meta-cognitive capability where the model acts as its own discerning teacher, potentially leading to more autonomous and continuous learning systems.

In essence, CaT offers a pragmatic and powerful paradigm shift in how LLMs can be post-trained, moving towards a future where high-quality learning signals can be extracted directly from the models' own computational processes, thereby accelerating their development and deployment across a broader spectrum of critical applications.
