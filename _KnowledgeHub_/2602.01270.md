# Mixture-of-World Models: Scaling Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning with Modular Latent Dynamics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduced Mixture-of-World Models (MoW), a modular architecture designed to scale multi-task reinforcement learning in visual environments by handling diverse observations and dynamics. The MoW agent achieved a 110.4% mean human-normalized score across 26 Atari games using 50% fewer parameters than an ensemble of single-task models, and set a new state-of-the-art for visual multi-task learning on Meta-World with a 74.5% average success rate.

## Problem

- Achieving sample efficiency in multi-task reinforcement learning (MTRL) within visual domains, where data collection is costly.
- The inability of monolithic world models to effectively capture heterogeneous visual observations and diverse underlying dynamics across multiple tasks.
- Lack of scalable world model architectures that maintain high-fidelity reconstruction and generalize well across diverse visual multi-task settings.

## Method

- A **Mixture-of-World Models** (MoW) architecture with a modular design, employing ==task-adaptive== Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) for perception and a hybrid Transformer-based dynamics model with expert routing for temporal modeling.
- A gradient-based task clustering strategy during a warmup phase to efficiently group tasks and optimize parameter sharing for VAE, predictor, and critic network modules.
- Integration of specialized training losses, including a harmonious loss to mitigate gradient conflicts, a balance loss to ensure even utilization of expert modules, and an auxiliary task prediction head to foster task-discriminative latent representations.

## Results

- Achieved a **110.4%** mean human-normalized score on 26 Atari games, matching the performance of a state-of-the-art ensemble (**114.2%**) with a **50%** reduction in model parameters (972.5 MB vs 1977.5 MB).
- Established a new state-of-the-art for visual MTRL on Meta-World with a **74.5%** average success rate across 50 tasks in 300k environment steps per task, demonstrating superior sample efficiency over model-free baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical contributions of modular VAEs, expert Transformers, the task prediction head, harmonious loss, balance loss, and gradient-based task clustering to MoW's overall performance and stability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modular architecture, comprising task-adaptive VAEs and expert Transformers, is effective for simultaneously capturing diverse visual inputs and dynamics across heterogeneous multi-task environments.
> - Gradient-based task clustering prior to training enables more meaningful parameter sharing and improved performance compared to random task partitioning for module assignment.
> - The combination of harmonious loss, expert balance loss, and an auxiliary task prediction head is critical for stabilizing training, preventing expert collapse, and promoting task-discriminative feature learning in complex modular MTRL systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated success in complex domains, but often in single-task environments where learned policies lack generalization capabilities. Multi-task Reinforcement Learning (MTRL) aims to address this by enabling agents to learn a unified policy across a diverse suite of tasks, leveraging shared experience and representations.

Traditional model-free MTRL methods, while extended from single-task counterparts (e.g., PPO, SAC), typically inherit their limitation of poor sample efficiency. This restricts their applicability in real-world scenarios where data collection is costly. In contrast, Model-Based RL (MBRL) has shown promise in improving sample efficiency in single-task settings. World models, a core component of MBRL, learn compact latent representations of environment dynamics, allowing for policy optimization through "latent imagination." These models often combine variational autoencoders (VAEs) for visual encoding with recurrent neural networks (RNNs) or Transformers for sequential dynamics modeling (e.g., Dreamer series, STORM).

The primary challenge in scaling world models to multi-task visual domains is the significant heterogeneity across tasks, both in observations and dynamics. A monolithic world model struggles to simultaneously capture disparate visual features and accurately predict diverse task-specific dynamics. Simply conditioning a shared model on task identity has been found insufficient without extensive expert data. Furthermore, for visual MBRL, maintaining high-fidelity reconstruction is critical for effective latent space policy learning, a concern that has not been extensively addressed in existing MTRL work.

Recent trends in MTRL have explored Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, widely successful in scaling large language models. While some model-free MTRL methods have incorporated MoE (e.g., MOORE, D2R), they primarily operate on low-dimensional state spaces and are still limited by the sample inefficiency inherent to model-free learning. Single-task world models like STORM and IRIS demonstrate strong performance on visual inputs, but their architectures are not directly designed to handle multi-task visual heterogeneity. While TD-MPC2 applies model-based principles to multi-task domains, it predominantly focuses on low-dimensional state inputs or relies on expert data, rather than challenging visual observations. This paper positions itself by addressing this specific gap: developing a world model architecture that can simultaneously handle diverse visual inputs and dynamics across multiple tasks while maintaining reconstruction fidelity and sample efficiency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The fundamental challenge that motivates this research is the difficulty in achieving sample efficiency in multi-task reinforcement learning (MTRL) within visual domains, where tasks exhibit considerable variation in both their visual observations and their underlying dynamics. Standard monolithic world model architectures, effective in single-task settings, typically lack the capacity to capture such diverse dynamics accurately, leading to degraded reconstruction and prediction quality. This limitation hinders their application in MTRL.

The key objectives of this work are:
1. **Develop a scalable architecture for multi-task visual world models:** The primary goal is to create a world model capable of operating across a diverse set of visual tasks without compromising performance or requiring an excessive amount of parameters.
2. **Enhance sample efficiency in visual MTRL:** By leveraging the principles of model-based RL, the research aims to enable agents to learn effective policies with fewer interactions with the real environment.
3. **Address visual and dynamic heterogeneity across tasks:** The architecture should explicitly account for the distinct visual features and transition dynamics present in different tasks, which often conflict when learned by a single, undifferentiated model.
4. **Maintain high-fidelity observation reconstruction:** Accurate reconstruction of visual observations is crucial for effective policy learning through latent imagination within a world model framework. The proposed solution must ensure this fidelity in a multi-task context.
5. **Improve parameter efficiency:** The goal is to achieve strong multi-task performance with a reduced parameter footprint compared to approaches that train separate models for each task or inefficiently scale shared models.

To achieve these objectives, the researchers were motivated to design a modular architecture, termed Mixture-of-World Models (MoW), that integrates several key components. Specifically, the motivation behind the components includes:
- **Modular VAEs:** To provide task-adaptive visual compression, recognizing that a single VAE might struggle to encode the diverse visual characteristics across heterogeneous tasks effectively.
- **Hybrid Transformer-based dynamics model:** To capture the varying temporal dynamics of different tasks. This hybrid design, combining task-conditioned experts with a shared backbone, aims to allow for specialization while retaining common knowledge.
- **Gradient-based task clustering:** To enable efficient parameter allocation by grouping tasks with similar learning characteristics, thus promoting beneficial sharing of VAEs and other components, and mitigating potential gradient conflicts.
- **Specialized losses (task prediction and expert balance):** To further guide the learning process by encouraging task-discriminative latent representations and ensuring balanced utilization of the different expert modules, preventing module collapse.

By addressing these motivations through a modular and specialized design, the authors aim to overcome the capacity and generalization limitations of prior world model approaches in complex multi-task, visual environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Mixture-of-World Models (MoW) architecture is designed to address multi-task heterogeneity in visual domains through a modular design, enabling parameter- and sample-efficient learning. The approach integrates two main modules: a perceptual module and a temporal module, augmented by specific training strategies.

#### 3.1. Architecture Overview

The MoW architecture consists of:
- **Perceptual Module:** Responsible for encoding high-dimensional visual observations into a compact latent space.
 - It employs a set of $N_m$ task-specific categorical Variational Autoencoders (VAEs). Each task $T_k$ is assigned a specific encoder-decoder pair ($q_{\phi,i_k}$, $p_{\phi,i_k}$) where $i_k$ is determined by a gradient-based clustering procedure.
 - A learnable task embedding $e_k$ (normalized to unit L2-norm) conditions both the encoding and decoding processes.
 - Observations $o_t^k$ are encoded into a stochastic representation $z_t^k$ sampled from a categorical posterior distribution.
- **Temporal Module:** Models the dynamics within the latent space. It takes sequences of stochastic representations and actions as input and predicts the next latent state, reward, task index, and a continuation flag.
 - **Input Representation:** The stochastic representation $z_t^k$ and action $a_t^k$ are concatenated with the task embedding $e_k$ and processed by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) $m_{\phi,j}$ to form a unified token $m_{1:t}^{j,k}$ for each expert $j$.
 - **Expert Selection (Task-Level Routing):** A router function, an MLP followed by a softmax and TopK operation, takes the task embedding $e_k$ as input. It produces a set of $n_k$ activated expert indices $J_k$ and their corresponding normalized weights $W_k$. This task-level routing ensures consistent expert activation within a given task, preventing fragmented learning of dynamics that might occur with token-level routing. The softmax temperature is annealed during training to balance stochasticity and determinism.
 - **Expert and Shared Transformers:** The tokens are processed by the activated expert Transformers $f_{\phi,j}$ for $j \in J_k$. The outputs of these experts are concatenated to form a token $l_{1:t}^k$. This concatenated token is then passed to a shared Transformer $F_{\phi}$ to produce the hidden state $h_t^k$. Expert Transformers specialize in task-specific dynamics, while the shared Transformer captures general knowledge.
 - **Prediction Heads:** The hidden state $h_t^k$ is used by task-specific prediction heads ($g_D, g_R, g_C$) to forecast the next latent state distribution ($\hat{Z}_{t+1}^k$), reward ($\hat{r}_t^k$), and continuation flag ($\hat{c}_t^k$). An auxiliary task predictor head ($g_T$) predicts the task index $\hat{k}$, encouraging task-discriminative features.

#### 3.2. World Model Learning

The MoW is trained end-to-end using a self-supervised learning paradigm that jointly leverages data from all tasks. The total loss for a task $T_k$, $L_k(\phi)$, combines several components:
- **Reconstruction Loss ($L_{rec}$):** Mean squared error between the reconstructed observation $\hat{o}_t^k$ and the original $o_t^k$.
- **Reward Prediction Loss ($L_{rew}$):** Symlog Cross-Entropy between predicted $\hat{r}_t^k$ and actual $r_t^k$.
- **Continuation Prediction Loss ($L_{con}$):** Binary Cross-Entropy for the continuation flag $\hat{c}_t^k$.
- **Task Prediction Loss ($L_{task}$):** Cross-Entropy between predicted task $\hat{k}$ and actual task $k$, explicitly guiding hidden states to be task-discriminative.
- **Dynamics Loss ($L_{dyn}$):** KL divergence between the predicted next latent state distribution $\hat{Z}_{t+1}^k$ and the stop-gradient version of the encoded next latent state $Z_{t+1}^k$.
- **Representation Loss ($L_{rep}$):** KL divergence between the encoded next latent state $Z_{t+1}^k$ and the stop-gradient version of the predicted next latent state $\hat{Z}_{t+1}^k$. These two KL losses (dynamics and representation) are clipped to a maximum of 1 nat.

To mitigate gradient conflicts and scale disparities among loss terms in MTRL, the MoW incorporates a **harmonious loss** ($L_H(\phi)$). This dynamically adjusts task weights based on individual task loss magnitudes, providing a rectified scale. An additional **balance loss** ($L_{bal}(\phi)$) is added to encourage the activation of all expert modules, preventing certain experts from being underutilized.

#### 3.3. Agent Learning

The agent learns entirely from imagined trajectories generated by the world model.
- A short context trajectory is sampled from the replay buffer to compute the initial posterior distribution $Z_t^k$.
- During inference, the latent representation $z_t^k$ is sampled from the prior distribution $\hat{Z}_t^k$.
- Key-value (KV) caching is used in the Transformer to accelerate autoregressive rollout during imagination.
- The agent's state $s_t^k$ is formed by concatenating $z_t^k$, $h_t^k$, and $e_k$.
- An actor-critic framework is employed: a shared actor network $\pi_{\theta}$ generates actions, and multiple critic networks $V_{\psi,i_k}^k$ (matching the number of VAE clusters) approximate the value function. The critic networks are trained using a maximum likelihood loss for $\lambda$-return estimates, with an exponential moving average for stabilization. The actor network is trained using a surrogate loss function, incorporating policy entropy.

#### 3.4. Warmuping MoW (Gradient-based Task Clustering)

Before main training, a warmup stage is performed:
1. Initial random interactions populate replay buffers for all tasks.
2. A single VAE-predictor stack is trained offline on this fixed dataset, initializing dynamics representation.
3. Upon warmup completion, gradient vectors associated with each task are extracted.
4. These task-specific gradient vectors are clustered (e.g., using k-means on averaged gradient vectors across layers) to determine which tasks share specific VAE, predictor, and critic network modules. This strategy aims to optimize parameter sharing based on learning correspondence. The parameters from the initially trained stack are then copied to the relevant clustered modules.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MoW was conducted on two distinct benchmarks: Atari 100k (discrete control, visual observations) and Meta-World (continuous control, visual observations), demonstrating improvements in sample efficiency and parameter scaling.

#### 4.1. Atari 100k Experiments

- **Performance:** A single MoW agent, trained once over 26 Atari games, achieved a mean human-normalized score of 110.4%. This performance is competitive with STORM, a state-of-the-art approach that employs an ensemble of 26 task-specific models, which achieved 114.2%.
- **Parameter Efficiency:** MoW demonstrated substantial parameter reduction, requiring approximately 972.5 MB for the single unified model, representing a 50% decrease compared to STORM's 1977.5 MB for its ensemble.
- **Imagination Fidelity:** Visual reconstructions from imagined trajectories generated by MoW showed improved fidelity and task discrimination compared to those produced by a multi-task vanilla Transformer (Figure 2, Appendix A.6), indicating better dynamics modeling.

#### 4.2. Meta-World Experiments

- **Performance:** On 50 robotic manipulation tasks, MoW achieved a 74.5% average success rate within 300k steps per task (15M total environment steps).
- **Sample Efficiency:** This result established a new state-of-the-art for visual MTRL on Meta-World, surpassing the performance of several model-free baselines (MTSAC, CARE, PaCo, MOORE) which operate on low-dimensional state inputs and require significantly more environment steps (20M to 100M). This highlights MoW's ability to achieve high performance from visual observations with notably fewer interactions.
- **Comparison to TD-MPC2:** While TD-MPC2 (adapted for visual input) achieved 25.3% success rate in the same environment, MoW demonstrated a higher success rate. TD-MPC2's MPPI control is designed for continuous action spaces, which poses challenges in adapting it effectively to discrete actions often encountered in other RL settings.

#### 4.3. Parameter Scalability

- MoW demonstrated effective parameter scalability. Increasing the number of expert modules (e.g., from 3 to 12 experts) led to substantial performance gains across the 26 Atari tasks (Figure 4).
- Increasing the number of expert Transformers yielded more pronounced improvements than increasing the number of VAE clusters, suggesting that specialized dynamics modeling is a more critical factor for performance scaling in this architecture.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies (Appendix A.5)

A series of ablation studies were conducted to evaluate the contribution of each architectural and training component:
- **Multi-task STORM:** An extension of the vanilla STORM framework to a multi-task setting (with task embeddings) failed to train effectively. Even with multiple VAEs, this baseline showed significantly higher dynamics modeling loss and impaired imagination, confirming the limitations of non-modular Transformer-based world models for diverse multi-task visual inputs.
- **Modular VAEs and Expert Transformers:** Performance degraded notably when the number of VAE-predictor clusters ($N_m$) or expert Transformers ($N_e$) was reduced, underscoring their importance in capturing task-specific visual and dynamic characteristics. Removing the expert Transformer led to a substantial performance drop, while removing the shared Transformer resulted in a modest decrease.
- **Task Prediction Head:** Removing the auxiliary task prediction loss led to a noticeable decline in performance, indicating its role in fostering task-discriminative latent representations.
- **Harmonious Loss:** Ablating the harmonious loss resulted in reduced RL performance and unstable training, confirming its importance in mitigating gradient conflicts and managing loss term scales.
- **Balance Loss:** The absence of the balance loss made training sensitive to initialization and could lead to expert collapse, affirming its role in ensuring balanced utilization of all expert modules.
- **Gradient-based Task Clustering:** Randomly partitioning tasks for VAE-predictor assignment, instead of using gradient-based clustering, led to reduced performance, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed warm-up strategy for meaningful parameter sharing.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Mixture-of-World Models (MoW) architecture represents a significant step towards developing scalable and parameter-efficient solutions for multi-task reinforcement learning (MTRL) in complex visual domains. The work addresses a fundamental challenge within the field: the inherent heterogeneity in observations and dynamics across diverse tasks, which has historically limited the efficacy of monolithic world models.

The primary significance of MoW lies in its demonstration that a modular design, encompassing task-adaptive VAEs and a hybrid Transformer-based dynamics model with expert routing, can effectively capture diverse task characteristics. This modularity not only enables robust learning across multiple tasks but also achieves sample efficiency competitive with, or superior to, single-task model-based agents and existing state-based multi-task methods, even when operating on high-dimensional visual inputs. The fact that a single MoW agent can match the performance of an ensemble of 26 task-specific models while requiring 50% fewer parameters on the Atari 100k benchmark highlights its parameter efficiency and scalability. Similarly, its state-of-the-art average success rate on Meta-World with significantly fewer environment steps underscores its sample efficiency in continuous control settings.

The introduction of specialized losses (task prediction and expert balance) and the gradient-based task clustering strategy are impactful methodological contributions. These components contribute to effective task discrimination in the latent space, ensure balanced utilization of expert modules, and optimize parameter sharing, respectively. Such strategies are crucial for stabilizing training and improving the overall performance of complex modular architectures in MTRL.

The potential impact of this work extends to several areas:
- **Generalist Agents:** MoW provides a scalable foundation for learning generalist policies capable of performing a wide range of tasks from raw pixel observations, moving closer to agents that can adapt to new environments or tasks with minimal retraining.
- **Robotics:** The strong performance on Meta-World suggests applicability in robotic manipulation, where learning complex skills across diverse objects and environments from visual input with limited real-world interaction is a critical challenge.
- **Game AI:** The results on Atari 100k indicate potential for developing more efficient and versatile AI agents for video games and simulated environments.
- **Resource Efficiency:** By reducing the number of parameters required for multi-task learning, MoW contributes to more resource-efficient AI development, making advanced RL techniques more accessible and sustainable.
- **Future Research:** The modular design of MoW, along with its specialized training components, offers a blueprint for future research in constructing more sophisticated and adaptable world models. It highlights the importance of carefully designed inductive biases for tackling the challenges of heterogeneity in broad domains. Further work could explore even larger task suites, more complex domain shifts, and the integration of pre-training and fine-tuning paradigms, as suggested by the authors.

In conclusion, MoW advances the state-of-the-art in multi-task visual reinforcement learning by offering a scalable, parameter-efficient, and effective framework that can handle the complexities of diverse task dynamics and observations.
