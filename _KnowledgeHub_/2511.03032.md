# Leveraging Discrete Function Decomposability for Scientific Design

> [!summary] Summary
> A new algorithm, DECOMPOSITION-AWARE DISTRIBUTIONAL OPTIMIZATION (DADO), enhances *in silico* design efficiency for high-dimensional discrete objects by explicitly leveraging the decomposable nature of objective functions. It consistently outperformed standard baselines on synthetic optimization tasks and protein property prediction benchmarks, demonstrating faster convergence to higher-performing designs.

## Problem

- Efficiently designing discrete objects (e.g., proteins, circuits) *in silico* is challenging due to the combinatorial explosion of high-dimensional search spaces.
- Current distributional optimization algorithms (like EDAs and RL) treat designs as indivisible, failing to leverage inherent decomposability often present in scientific objective functions.
- This lack of structural awareness leads to statistical inefficiency, requiring more samples and slower convergence for effective optimization.

## Method

- **DADO** represents objective function decomposability using a ==junction tree==, which factors the function into components over subsets of design variables.
- It employs a factorized generative model (search distribution) whose factors are independently updated using 'distributional value functions' computed via a message-passing scheme on the junction tree.
- These value functions act as localized, factor-specific weights for weighted maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) updates, coordinating learning across the interconnected factors of the search distribution.

## Results

- DADO consistently outperformed naive EDA, FDA, and PPO baselines on synthetic functions, demonstrating superior performance that scaled with increasing sequence length (L up to **200**) and alphabet size (D up to **100**).
- On protein property prediction tasks (AAV, Amyloid, Gcn4, TDP-43), DADO achieved significantly higher objective function values than all baselines, converging more effectively to high-performing designs.
- A case study on GB1 protein showed that stricter decomposition (smaller meta-variables) enhanced DADO's performance and improved predictive model robustness, especially when trained on suboptimal data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly leveraging objective function decomposability through a structured generative model and message passing enables a 'divide, collaborate, and conquer' strategy for significantly improved statistical efficiency in discrete design.
> - Distributional Q-functions, computed via expectation-based message passing on a junction tree, provide a tractable and unbiased way to guide factor-specific updates for the search distribution.
> - Decomposition structures for protein design can be practically derived from 3D contact graphs (e.g., using AlphaFold3), and enforcing such decomposition does not significantly compromise predictive model accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenge of *in silico* design of discrete objects (e.g., circuits, proteins, materials) to exhibit user-specified properties within the context of AI-driven science and engineering. The conventional approach involves training generative models over vast discrete design spaces to concentrate on desired properties. This is typically framed as distributional optimization, a category that includes Estimation of Distribution Algorithms (EDAs) and reinforcement learning policy optimization.

A fundamental difficulty in this landscape is the combinatorial nature and high dimensionality of discrete design spaces, which makes efficient optimization challenging. While many methods exist to handle inaccuracies in predictive models, the efficient navigation of large discrete design spaces remains an area requiring further development.

The authors identify a significant, unexploited opportunity: many property predictors in scientific applications are *decomposable*. This means their objective functions can be factorized over subsets of design variables, reflecting localized or modular interactions. For instance, the binding site of a protein might primarily determine its activity, with other regions playing a secondary, stabilizing role. Current distributional optimization algorithms generally do not leverage such inherent structure, thereby limiting their statistical efficiency.

This research introduces a novel distributional optimization algorithm, DECOMPOSITION-AWARE DISTRIBUTIONAL OPTIMIZATION (DADO), which explicitly incorporates function decomposability. This positions DADO as an advancement within the EDA and policy optimization communities by integrating structural knowledge (defined by a junction tree) that has historically been underutilized in these contexts for arbitrary graph structures. Previous work in EDAs like the Factorized Distribution Algorithm (FDA) also uses factorization but lacks a mechanism for coordinated message passing to guide the search distribution. Similarly, some policy optimization methods use message passing but are restricted to simpler graph topologies, such as Markov chains. In the Bayesian Optimization community, methods exist to infer and leverage function decomposition, sometimes using message passing, but these are typically applied to real-valued spaces and do not directly employ distributional optimization in the manner DADO does. DADO aims to bridge these gaps by enabling decomposition-aware distributional optimization for discrete spaces with general graph structures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and demonstrate a distributional optimization algorithm that can leverage the decomposable nature of objective functions in high-dimensional discrete design spaces, thereby improving optimization efficiency.

The detailed motivations include:

- **Addressing Combinatorial Explosion:** The direct enumeration or naive optimization of designs in discrete spaces of practical scientific interest (e.g., length-L protein sequences with D=20 amino acids) is computationally intractable due to the exponential growth of the search space with design variables. For example, a protein of length 50 has 20^50 possible sequences, an impractical number to search exhaustively.
- **Exploiting Inherent Decomposability:** Many real-world scientific and engineering problems feature objective functions `f(x)` that exhibit decomposability. This implies that the overall property can be expressed as a sum of component functions, each dependent on only a subset of the design variables. For example, in protein design, specific amino acid positions might form an active site, with their interactions primarily governing a particular function, while other positions contribute to overall stability. Recognizing and leveraging this modularity is crucial for efficient optimization.
- **Limitations of Current Methods:** Existing distributional optimization algorithms (like standard EDAs or reinforcement learning policy optimization methods) generally treat the design `x` as a single, indivisible entity. They do not possess a mechanism to recognize or exploit the factorized structure of `f(x)`. This results in statistical inefficiency, as the generative model (search distribution) must learn to navigate the entire joint design space without the benefit of localized updates.
- **Enhancing Statistical Efficiency:** By enabling a "divide, collaborate, and conquer" strategy, where optimization is broken down into smaller, interconnected subproblems, DADO aims to make more efficient use of sampled designs. Instead of weighting entire samples with `f(x)`, DADO uses localized "value functions" to guide updates for individual factors of the generative model, leading to faster learning and convergence to high-performing designs with a fixed sampling budget.
- **Extensibility of Distributional Optimization:** The choice of a distributional optimization framework is motivated by its inherent extensibility. This framework allows for integration with innovations from the EDA and policy optimization fields, such as combining pre-trained unconditional generative models with property predictors (e.g., using Bayes' rule for `p(x|y)`), and benefiting from ongoing advancements in generative modeling architectures.

In essence, the research is motivated by the hypothesis that by imbuing distributional optimization with awareness of function decomposability through a structured generative model and message-passing mechanism, it is possible to significantly overcome the combinatorial challenges of discrete design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology centers on DECOMPOSITION-AWARE DISTRIBUTIONAL OPTIMIZATION (DADO), an algorithm designed to leverage decomposability in the objective function `f(x)` for efficient discrete design optimization.

**A. Decomposability Representation:**
DADO requires the objective function `f(x)` to be expressed in a decomposed form. This decomposition is represented by an undirected **junction tree** `T = (N, E)`, where `N` is the set of nodes (meta-variables, i.e., subsets of the original design variables `x`) and `E` is the set of edges connecting these nodes. The function `f(x)` is then written as a sum of "node component functions" `f_i(x_i)` (acting on variables within node `i`) and "edge component functions" `f_{i,j}(x_i, x_j)` (acting on variables shared between connected nodes `i` and `j`):
`f(x) = Σ_{i∈N} f_i(x_i) + Σ_{(i,j)∈E} f_{i,j}(x_i, x_j)`.

**B. Junction Tree Construction for Proteins:**
For protein design problems, the authors outline a practical procedure to obtain the required junction tree:
1. **3D Structure Acquisition:** Obtain a 3D structure of the wild-type protein sequence (e.g., using AlphaFold3).
2. **Contact Graph Generation:** Construct a contact graph where nodes represent amino acid residues and edges connect residues whose atoms are within a specified distance threshold (e.g., 4.5 Å). This graph indicates direct physical interactions between residues.
3. **Junction Tree Formation:** Algorithmically convert this contact graph into a junction tree. This transformation ensures that the graph structure is amenable to message passing.
4. **Component Function Fitting:** A neural network-based predictive model is trained to enforce this decomposition. The neural network's architecture is structured to reflect the junction tree topology, with separate components for node and edge functions, which are then summed to yield `f(x)`.

**C. Factorized Search Distribution:**
To optimize `E_{p_θ(x)}[f(x)]`, DADO employs a factorized generative model `p_θ(x)`. The undirected junction tree `T` is first rooted (by selecting a root node `r` and directing all edges outwards) to form a directed tree `T'`. The search distribution `p_θ(x)` is then factorized according to `T'` as a directed acyclic graphical (DAG) model:
`p_θ(x) := p_θ(x_r) Π_{(p(i),i)∈E'} p_θ(x_i | x_{p(i)})`,
where `x_r` denotes the meta-variables of the root node, `x_i` denotes the meta-variables of a non-root node `i`, and `x_{p(i)}` represents the meta-variables of `i`'s parent. Each factor (`p_θ(x_r)` and `p_θ(x_i | x_{p(i)})`) is parameterized by a neural network (e.g., an MLP-based autoregressive model), with each factor having independent parameters.

**D. DADO Algorithm Steps (Iterative Process):**
DADO iterates through three main steps, adapting the standard EDA framework:
1. **Sample Generation:** `K` samples `{x^k}_{k=1}^K` are drawn from the current factorized search distribution `p_θ^n(x)`. This sampling process is sequential, starting from the root node `x_r` and proceeding down to the leaves by conditionally sampling each `x_i` given its parent `x_{p(i)}`.
2. **Distributional Value Function Computation (Message Passing):** This is the core novelty of DADO. Instead of computing a single score `f(x^k)` for each sample, DADO computes *distributional value functions* (Q-functions) for each node and edge, which serve as weights for the individual factors of the search distribution.
 - These Q-functions, `Q_θ^n_i(x_i, x_{p(i)})` (for non-root nodes `i`) and `Q_θ^n_r(x_r)` (for the root node), accumulate expected objective values from the sub-tree rooted at `i`.
 - Unlike classical message passing that uses `max` operations, DADO's value functions use `expectations` over `p_θ(x_i | x_{p(i)})`, making them tractable via Monte-Carlo approximation.
 - The computation proceeds in an "up-pass" from leaves to the root:
 - `V_θ_i(x_{p(i)}) := E_{p_θ(x_i|x_{p(i)})}[Q_θ_i(x_i, x_{p(i)})]`
 - `Q_θ_i(x_i, x_{p(i)}) := f_{p(i),i}(x_{p(i)}, x_i) + Q_θ_i(x_i)`
 - `Q_θ_i(x_i) := f_i(x_i) + Σ_{c∈children(i)} V_θ_c(x_i)`
3. **Factorized Search Distribution Update:** Each factor `p_θ_i(x_i | x_{p(i)})` (or `p_θ_r(x_r)`) is updated independently via weighted maximum likelihood estimation (MLE), using its corresponding Q-function as a weight. A monotonic shaping function `W(·)` (e.g., `exp(s/β)`) is typically applied to these weights.
 - `θ^{n+1}_r = argmax_{θ_r} Σ_{k=1}^K W(Q_θ^n_r(x_r^k)) log p_{θ_r}(x_r^k)`
 - `θ^{n+1}_i = argmax_{θ_i} Σ_{k=1}^K W(Q_θ^n_i(x_i^k, x_{p(i)}^k)) log p_{θ_i}(x_i^k | x_{p(i)}^k)`
 - These updates are coordinated through the Q-functions derived from message passing. This allows each lower-dimensional factor to be optimized efficiently based on information from the entire tree.

**E. Theoretical Underpinnings:**
The distributional value functions in DADO provide a tractable and unbiased approximation to classical value functions (which involve intractable maximization over variables). The optimization objective aims to maximize a lower bound on the expected value of `f(x)`. An alternative derivation for a maximum entropy objective also yields a similar message-passing structure, highlighting the generality of the framework.

**F. Implementation Details:**
The authors used MLP-based autoregressive neural networks for the search distribution factors. Optimization was performed using the AdamW optimizer. Variance reduction techniques, such as using mean-shifted Q-functions, were employed to stabilize training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented two sets of experiments to evaluate DADO: one on synthetic functions and another on protein property predictors derived from real protein data. Comparisons were made against a standard, decomposition-unaware Estimation of Distribution Algorithm (EDA), a Factorized Distribution Algorithm (FDA), and a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) variant.

**A. Performance on Fully Synthetic Functions:**
- **Varying Sequence Length (L):** Experiments with sequence lengths `L = {25, 50, 200}` (and an alphabet size `D = 20`) demonstrated DADO's superior performance. DADO consistently converged to designs with higher `f(x)` values than all three baselines. For `L=25`, baselines eventually approached DADO's performance, but for `L=50` and `L=200`, the performance gap between DADO and baselines (which converged to substantially lower `f(x)` values) was more pronounced. Supplemental experiments with `L=400` further supported this trend, indicating DADO's scaling advantages for larger design spaces.
- **Varying Alphabet Size (D):** Experiments with `L=100` and alphabet sizes `D = {20, 50, 100}` showed that DADO consistently found designs with higher `f(x)` than the naive EDA across all alphabet sizes. As `D` increased, optimization became more challenging for all methods, but DADO maintained a positive improvement trajectory, suggesting it had not converged to a local optimum as prematurely as the EDA.

**B. Performance on Protein Property Predictors:**
- **AAV, Amyloid, Gcn4, and TDP-43 Proteins:** For these four proteins, where AlphaFold3-derived junction trees exhibited relatively small node cardinalities (implying stronger decomposability), DADO consistently converged to designs with higher `f(x)` values than the naive EDA, FDA, and PPO baselines. Statistical analysis indicated significant performance differences, with DADO's confidence intervals often not overlapping with those of the most competitive baseline at the final iteration.
- **GB1, ynzC, and CreiLOV Proteins (Supplemental):** For proteins with larger junction tree node cardinalities (suggesting less overall decomposability or more complex local interactions), DADO still generally outperformed decomposition-unaware baselines. However, for GB1 and ynzC, FDA (which uses a factorized search distribution but lacks DADO's message-passing for factor-specific weighting) sometimes performed comparably or even slightly better, suggesting potential challenges for DADO's estimated distributional Q-functions in scenarios with very large, complex meta-variables.

**C. Investigation of Protein Predictive Model Decomposability and Robustness (GB1 Case Study):**
- **Impact of Contact Threshold (t) on Optimization:** A case study on GB1 investigated how varying the distance threshold `t` (used to define contacts for the junction tree) affected both predictive accuracy and optimization performance. It was found that decreasing `t` to 2.75 Å (which resulted in a stricter decomposition with smaller meta-variables) allowed DADO to significantly outperform the naive EDA. At this stricter decomposition, DADO converged more rapidly and reliably to high `f(x)` values. In contrast, at `t = 4.5 Å` (the default) and `t = 9 Å`, DADO's advantage over the EDA was less pronounced, and convergence was less stable. This indicates that while decomposition is beneficial, the "granularity" of the decomposition (i.e., size of meta-variables) influences DADO's effectiveness.
- **Robustness of Predictive Accuracy to Decomposition:**
 - **Varying `t`:** The holdout accuracy (Pearson correlation) of decomposed predictive models across a range of `t` values (0 Å to 15 Å) remained relatively consistent and comparable to a non-decomposed neural network. This suggests that enforcing a decomposition does not necessarily lead to a substantial loss in predictive power for these protein models.
 - **Random Graph Mutations:** Randomly adding or removing a moderate number of edges from the default `t = 4.5 Å` decomposition graph generally resulted in minor changes to holdout predictive accuracy. This indicates a degree of robustness of the predictive models to imperfections in the precise definition of the decomposition graph.
 - **Training with Suboptimal Data (Bottom 50%):** When models were trained on only the lowest-scoring 50% of the dataset, more strictly decomposed models (lower `t`) sometimes showed better holdout accuracy compared to less decomposed or non-decomposed models. This implies that simpler, more constrained models might exhibit better generalization properties under certain data scarcity or distribution shift scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a method for distributional optimization that can leverage arbitrary decomposability of objective functions, addressing a critical challenge in high-dimensional discrete design problems within AI-driven scientific discovery and engineering.

**A. Advancing Efficient Design in Complex Spaces:**
The primary significance lies in providing a mechanism to overcome the combinatorial explosion inherent in designing discrete objects like proteins, circuits, or materials. By explicitly accounting for functional decomposability, DADO enables more statistically efficient optimization, allowing the search for high-performing designs to proceed more rapidly and effectively than with decomposition-unaware methods. This capability is relevant wherever the objective function exhibits modular or local interactions, which is common in structured scientific domains.

**B. Practical Utility in Scientific Applications:**
The demonstration of DADO on real protein property prediction problems, using AlphaFold3-derived structural information to infer decomposition, highlights its practical applicability. The finding that accurate predictive models can be learned under varying degrees of decomposition, and that DADO benefits from stricter decompositions without sacrificing predictive power, suggests a viable pathway for its deployment in fields such as protein engineering, where *in silico* design is increasingly prevalent. The robustness observed against variations and mutations in the decomposition graph implies that perfect *a priori* knowledge of the underlying interaction network may not be strictly necessary for the method to be effective.

**C. Generalizability Across Domains:**
While the experimental focus is on protein design, the underlying principles of DADO are generalizable. Analogous decomposition strategies could be applied to other discrete design problems, such as circuit design (leveraging topology constraints) or materials science (optimizing component arrangements and properties). The method's core strength is its ability to adapt classical message-passing for exact optimization to the more flexible and data-driven distributional optimization paradigm.

**D. Foundation for Future Research:**
DADO's framework opens several avenues for future research:
- **Integration with Advanced EDAs:** Combining DADO with generalizations of the standard EDA, such as methods that account for uncertainty or inaccuracy in the property predictor `f(x)` (e.g., through Bayesian optimization or conditioning unconditional generative models), could enhance its robustness and applicability in real-world scenarios with limited data.
- **Dynamic Decomposition Discovery:** While the current work assumes a predefined or inferred decomposition, integrating methods for dynamically discovering functional decompositions from data during active learning could further automate and improve DADO's application.
- **Variance Reduction Techniques:** Further research into advanced variance-reduction techniques for DADO's distributional value functions could improve its performance, particularly in cases with very large or complex meta-variables where current estimates might be noisy.

In summary, DADO contributes a novel algorithm that leverages structural properties of scientific objective functions to enable more efficient and scalable *in silico* design, potentially accelerating discovery across various scientific and engineering disciplines.
