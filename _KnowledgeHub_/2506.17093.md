# Identifiability of Deep Polynomial Neural Networks

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Université de Lorraine established a localization theorem for deep Polynomial Neural Networks (PNNs), demonstrating that deep PNN identifiability can be determined by properties of its 2-layer subnetworks. Their work provides new linear bounds for activation degrees necessary for identifiability and extends the analysis to PNNs incorporating bias terms.

## Problem

- A lack of comprehensive theoretical understanding regarding the identifiability of deep Polynomial Neural Networks (PNNs) from their output.
- Existing identifiability results for PNNs were limited to shallow networks or required restrictive architectural conditions, such as very high activation degrees or uniform layer widths.
- Prior theoretical analyses of PNNs largely ignored the inclusion of bias terms, limiting their applicability to more realistic network architectures.

## Method

- Established an equivalence between 2-layer homogeneous PNNs (hPNNs) and partially symmetric ==Canonical Polyadic== Tensor Decompositions (CPD), enabling the application of tensor uniqueness theorems.
- Developed a "localization theorem" using an inductive proof, demonstrating that deep hPNNs are identifiable if all successive 2-layer blocks are identifiable.
- Utilized a homogenization procedure to extend identifiability analysis to PNNs with bias terms by transforming them into equivalent hPNNs with augmented dimensions.

## Results

- A "localization theorem" (Theorem **11**) was established, proving that an L-layer homogeneous PNN is finitely identifiable if every successive 2-layer block within it is finitely identifiable.
- Identifiability conditions were derived for specific architectures, showing that pyramidal hPNNs with non-increasing layer widths are generically identifiable with activation degrees of at least 2.
- New linear bounds for activation degrees were provided, indicating that homogeneous PNNs with layer width d_ell >= 2 are finitely identifiable if r_ell >= 2d_ell - 1, and biased PNNs if r_ell >= 2d_ell + 1.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The identifiability of deep Polynomial Neural Networks can be localized to the identifiability of their successive 2-layer subnetworks, simplifying the theoretical analysis of deep architectures.
> - A critical interplay exists between activation degrees and layer widths that dictates PNN identifiability, leading to improved linear bounds on activation degree requirements.
> - The theoretical framework successfully incorporates bias terms into the identifiability analysis, revealing that biased PNNs can achieve identifiability even with very narrow hidden layers (e.g., single neurons).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the theoretical understanding of neural networks, specifically Polynomial Neural Networks (PNNs), which are noted for their algebraic and geometric structure alongside competitive experimental performance. PNNs have found applications in diverse fields such as computer vision, image representation, physics, and finance. The study of PNNs is increasingly conducted through the lens of algebraic geometry, utilizing concepts like neuromanifolds and neurovarieties to analyze architectural properties such as expressivity, loss function landscapes, and training dynamics.

A significant aspect of PNNs is their intrinsic link to low-rank tensor decompositions, a mathematical framework fundamental to the study of latent variable models and their identifiability. For instance, single-output two-layer PNNs are known to be equivalent to symmetric tensors.

The concept of identifiability, which addresses whether the parameters and hidden representations of a neural network can be uniquely determined from its output (up to trivial ambiguities like neuron permutations), is a critical theoretical question in neural network research. It is essential for ensuring interpretability in representation learning, achieving provably disentangled representations, and understanding causal models. Identifiability also impacts how architectures affect inference processes and supports manipulations of pre-trained models.

Despite its importance, the identifiability of deep PNNs has remained largely unexplored. Existing work has shown finite identifiability for feedforward PNNs only under restrictive conditions, such as very high activation degrees or uniform layer widths. A standing conjecture proposed identifiability for PNNs with non-increasing layer widths and at least quadratic degrees, paralleling results for ReLU networks. However, a comprehensive general theory for deep PNNs has been absent. Furthermore, most prior theoretical studies on PNNs have overlooked architectures incorporating bias terms. This paper aims to fill these gaps by providing a foundational analysis of identifiability for deep PNNs, encompassing both homogeneous (bias-free) and biased architectures.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to present a comprehensive analysis of the identifiability of deep Polynomial Neural Networks (PNNs) that employ monomial activation functions. This goal is driven by several motivations:

Firstly, identifiability is paramount for ensuring the interpretability of neural network models. In representation learning, the ability to uniquely determine network parameters and hidden representations from output is crucial for understanding what the model has learned. This directly supports the development of provably disentangled representations and is foundational for the study of causal models, where establishing unique causal links is vital.

Secondly, understanding identifiability is motivated by the need to comprehend the architectural impact on the network's function. The architecture of a PNN (layer widths, activation degrees) significantly influences its expressivity, the landscape of its loss function, and the dynamics of its training process. Identifiability provides insights into how these architectural choices constrain or enable unique parameter recovery, which is relevant for manipulating or "stitching" pre-trained models and their internal representations.

Thirdly, the research seeks to address specific theoretical gaps in the understanding of PNNs. Previous studies on feedforward PNNs have shown limited identifiability results, often requiring very high activation degrees or uniform layer widths. A long-standing conjecture posited that identifiability might hold for PNNs with non-increasing layer widths and at least quadratic activation degrees. A key motivation is to provide a general theory of identifiability for deep PNNs that can either confirm or refine such conjectures.

Finally, a significant motivation is to extend the theoretical analysis to PNNs that include bias terms. Many prior theoretical investigations into PNNs have simplified the model by omitting biases, leaving a gap in the understanding of more realistic PNN architectures. This work aims to incorporate bias terms into the identifiability analysis, making the theoretical framework more comprehensive and applicable.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on establishing a connection between deep PNNs and low-rank tensor decompositions, leveraging tools from algebraic geometry and uniqueness theorems for tensors. The approach is primarily constructive and inductive.

**Homogeneous PNNs (hPNNs):**
1. **Connecting to Tensor Decompositions:** The core of the methodology for homogeneous PNNs (hPNNs, without biases) is the demonstrated equivalence between 2-layer hPNNs and partially symmetric Canonical Polyadic Tensor Decompositions (CPD) (Proposition 34). A 2-layer hPNN $W_2 \rho_r(W_1 x)$ is shown to correspond to a partially symmetric tensor $F = [[W_2, W_1^T, \dots, W_1^T]]$. This link allows the application of established uniqueness criteria from tensor theory.
2. **Kruskal-Type Uniqueness Theorems:** For 2-layer hPNNs, the identifiability is established by applying Kruskal-type uniqueness theorems for tensors. Proposition 35 provides a sufficient condition for the uniqueness of a 2-layer hPNN based on the Kruskal ranks of its weight matrices ($W_1$ and $W_2$) and the activation degree $r$. Specifically, it requires $r \ge \frac{2d_1 - \text{krank}\{W_2\}}{\text{krank}\{W_1^T\} - 1}$. For generic matrices, Kruskal ranks equal minimal dimensions, leading to Proposition 12 for generic identifiability.
3. **Localization Theorem for Deep hPNNs (Theorem 11):** The central methodological contribution is proving that an L-layer hPNN is finitely identifiable if every 2-layer block, formed by a pair of successive layers, is finitely identifiable. The proof proceeds by induction.
- **Base Case:** For L=2, the identifiability is directly derived from the tensor-based uniqueness criteria (Proposition 12).
- **Inductive Step:** Assuming identifiability holds for L-1 layers, the proof for L layers focuses on the Jacobian of the parameterization map. The key is to show that the Jacobian of the "last layer map" (which composes the output of the (L-1)-th layer with the final layer) has maximal rank. This is achieved by constructing a specific "certificate" polynomial vector $\hat{q}(x)$ (Proposition C.8) that simplifies the Jacobian structure, allowing its rank to be analyzed. The argument utilizes the orthogonality of polynomial terms derived from specific inputs, effectively "decoupling" different parts of the Jacobian matrix.
4. **Neuroalgebraic Geometry:** The methodology draws on concepts from neuroalgebraic geometry. Finite identifiability of an hPNN architecture is linked to its neurovariety (the Zariski closure of the image of the parameterization map) being "nondefective," meaning its dimension equals the effective number of parameters (Proposition 10). This equivalence allows the use of Jacobian rank analysis (Lemma A.1, Corollary A.5) as a criterion for finite identifiability.

**PNNs with Biases:**
1. **Homogenization Procedure:** To extend the analysis to PNNs with bias terms, the paper employs a homogenization technique (Definition 20, Proposition 23). A PNN with biases is transformed into an equivalent hPNN by augmenting the input and hidden layer dimensions by one and structuring the weight matrices. For instance, an affine map $W_i x + b_i$ is mapped to a homogeneous linear map defined by a block matrix $\begin{pmatrix} W_i & b_i \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$.
2. **Implication of Uniqueness:** Proposition 24 demonstrates that if the homogenized hPNN is unique (or finite-to-one), then the original PNN with biases is also unique (or finite-to-one). This is crucial because it allows the identifiability results established for hPNNs to be transferred to PNNs with biases.
3. **Extension of Localization Theorem:** The localization theorem is then extended to PNNs with biases (Proposition 27, Corollary 28). This involves applying the same inductive logic, but considering the augmented dimensions of the homogenized network. Although the weight matrices of the homogenized network are structured (not generic), the argument shows that the generic identifiability still holds for the original PNN (Lemma 26).

Overall, the methodology combines advanced algebraic tools from tensor decompositions and algebraic geometry with an inductive proof structure to systematically analyze the identifiability of complex deep polynomial neural network architectures.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several findings regarding the identifiability of deep Polynomial Neural Networks (PNNs), encompassing both homogeneous (hPNNs) and biased architectures.

1. **Localization of Identifiability (Theorem 11):** A significant finding is the "localization theorem," which establishes a connection between the identifiability of shallow and deep hPNNs. It states that an L-layer hPNN with architecture $((d_0, \dots, d_L), (r_1, \dots, r_{L-1}))$ is finitely identifiable if, for every successive 2-layer block, the architecture $hPNN_{(\tilde{d}_{\ell-1}, d_\ell, d_{\ell+1}), r_\ell}[\cdot]$ is finitely identifiable. Here, $\tilde{d}_{\ell-1} = \min\{d_0, \dots, d_{\ell-1}\}$, meaning the condition considers a potentially reduced input dimension for deeper layers. This result implies that the dimension of the hPNN's neurovariety reaches its expected value, a property referred to as non-defectivity (Proposition 10).

2. **Interplay of Activation Degrees and Layer Widths:** The findings highlight an intricate interplay between the chosen activation degrees and the widths of the layers in achieving identifiability. The proofs are constructive, leveraging connections to low-rank tensor decompositions and Kruskal-type uniqueness theorems for tensors.

3. **Identifiability of Specific Architectures:**
- **Pyramidal Networks (Corollary 16):** hPNNs with non-increasing layer widths ($d_0 \ge d_1 \ge \dots \ge d_{L-1} \ge 2$, with $d_L \ge 1$) are generically identifiable. This holds for activation degrees $r_1, \dots, r_{L-1} \ge 2$ if $d_L \ge 2$, or $r_1, \dots, r_{L-2} \ge 2, r_{L-1} \ge 3$ if $d_L=1$. This result is noted to settle a conjecture from prior work regarding the dimension of neurovarieties for such architectures.
- **Activation Thresholds (Corollary 17):** For a fixed architecture, the paper provides new bounds on the minimal activation degrees ($r_\ell$) required for identifiability, referred to as "activation thresholds." Specifically, for layers with width $d_\ell \ge 2$, an hPNN is finitely identifiable if $r_\ell \ge 2d_\ell - 1$. This is a linear bound, representing an improvement over previous quadratic bounds ($O(d_\ell^2)$) reported in earlier literature.
- **Bottleneck Networks (Corollary 19):** Bottleneck hPNNs (with $d_0 \ge \dots \ge d_b \le \dots \le d_L$ and $d_b \ge 2$) are finitely identifiable under certain conditions. The encoder part requires $r_1, \dots, r_b \ge 2$. For the decoder part, identifiability is achieved when the widths do not grow too rapidly compared to activation degrees, specifically if $d_\ell / r_\ell \le d_b - 1$ for $\ell \in \{b+1, \dots, L-1\}$.

4. **Identifiability of PNNs with Biases:** The work extends its analysis to general PNNs that include bias terms.
- **Homogenization Principle (Proposition 23, 24):** It is shown that any PNN with biases can be mapped to an equivalent hPNN (its "homogenization") with augmented layer widths. The uniqueness (or finite-to-one property) of this homogenized hPNN implies the same for the original PNN.
- **Generalized Identifiability Conditions (Proposition 27, Corollary 28, Remark 29):** The localization theorem is generalized to PNNs with biases. This requires the 2-layer subnetworks of the *homogenized* architecture (with dimensions $\tilde{d}_{\ell-1}+1, d_\ell+1, d_{\ell+1}$) to be finitely identifiable. The activation thresholds for biased PNNs become $r_\ell \ge 2d_\ell + 1$. A notable observation is that biased PNNs can be identifiable even with layers containing a single hidden neuron ($d_\ell = 1$).

5. **Distinction between Specific and Generic Uniqueness:** The paper clarifies that while the localization theorem provides sufficient conditions for *finite identifiability* (generic uniqueness), it does not extend to *specific uniqueness* (uniqueness for a particular choice of parameters) or *global identifiability* (generic uniqueness with only one equivalence class of parameters). A counterexample (Example 41) is provided to illustrate this distinction. A conjecture is proposed that global identifiability might hold under the conditions of Theorem 11, but this remains an open question.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the theoretical understanding of deep neural networks and offers potential impacts across various domains.

Firstly, by providing a comprehensive analysis of the identifiability of deep Polynomial Neural Networks (PNNs), the work addresses a fundamental theoretical gap. The "localization theorem" is a key result, establishing a surprising connection between the identifiability of shallow and deep PNNs. This insight simplifies the complex problem of deep network analysis by reducing it to properties of 2-layer subnetworks, paving the way for further theoretical advancements in deep learning.

Secondly, the findings have direct implications for **interpretability and representation learning**. Identifiability is a prerequisite for ensuring that learned representations are meaningful and unique. By providing conditions under which PNNs are identifiable, this research contributes to the development of provably interpretable AI systems and methods for achieving disentangled representations, which are critical for understanding and controlling complex models. This also impacts the study of **causal models**, where identifying unique underlying mechanisms is essential.

Thirdly, the work offers valuable **architectural design principles**. The revealed intricate interplay between activation degrees and layer widths, along with specific identifiability conditions for pyramidal and bottleneck architectures, can inform the principled design of PNNs. For instance, the linear bounds on activation thresholds are a practical guideline for choosing appropriate activation functions based on network depth and width. The finding that PNNs with biases can be identifiable even with very narrow layers (e.g., single hidden neurons) suggests new architectural possibilities.

Fourthly, this research advances the emerging field of **neuroalgebraic geometry**. By settling an open conjecture regarding the dimension of PNN neurovarieties and providing new conditions for them to reach their expected dimension, the paper deepens the understanding of the geometric properties of neural network function spaces. This connection to algebraic geometry provides powerful tools for analyzing network expressivity and learning dynamics.

Finally, the constructive nature of the proofs, rooted in tensor decompositions and Kruskal-type theorems, holds **potential for computational algorithms**. The established links to partially symmetric tensor decompositions, for which efficient algorithms exist, could serve as a foundation for developing new, theoretically-grounded computational methods for training or analyzing deep PNNs. This could potentially extend techniques like the method of moments, previously used for shallow networks, to deep architectures. The insights into network architecture can also aid in **neural network compression and pruning**, by identifying components that are not reducible without loss of function.

While the work primarily focuses on monomial activations and finite identifiability, it lays a crucial groundwork. The methodology could potentially be extended to more general polynomial activations, and the posed conjecture on global identifiability highlights an important direction for future research.
