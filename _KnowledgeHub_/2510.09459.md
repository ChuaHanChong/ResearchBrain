# Failure Prediction at Runtime for Generative Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> FIPER, a framework for generative robot policies, predicts runtime failures by combining aggregated out-of-distribution observation scores and action uncertainty scores. The method leverages conformal prediction for threshold calibration without requiring failure data, demonstrating superior accuracy and earlier detection across various robotic tasks.

## Problem

- Generative imitation learning (IL) policies are susceptible to unpredictable failures when encountering novel environments or accumulating action errors.
- Proactive failure prediction is crucial for safe deployment in human-centered and safety-critical robotic applications.
- Existing failure prediction methods often produce high false alarms, are reactive (detecting failures after they occur), or require costly and dangerous labeled failure data.

## Method

- **FIPER** proposes combining two proactive indicators: aggregated ==out-of-distribution (OOD)== observations and aggregated high ==action uncertainty==.
- It uses ==Random Network Distillation== in the policy's ==Observation Embedding space (RND-OE)== for OOD observations and ==Action-Chunk Entropy (ACE)== for action uncertainty.
- Failure thresholds are calibrated using ==conformal prediction== on a small set of successful policy rollouts, eliminating the need for failure data.

## Results

- FIPER achieved superior average Timestep-Wise Accuracy (**0.65**) and overall accuracy (**0.78**), with competitive Detection Time (**0.30**) across five diverse simulated and real-world environments.
- The logical 'AND' combination of RND-OE and ACE significantly improved accuracy, particularly the True Negative Rate, by preventing false alarms from isolated anomalies.
- Individual RND-OE and ACE scores demonstrated superior discrimination power in distinguishing between benign out-of-distribution situations and actual policy failures compared to baseline methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Actual policy failures are robustly identified by the simultaneous presence of persistent OOD observations and prolonged high action uncertainty.
> - Leveraging the policy's observation embeddings and quantifying the sharpness of action distributions (ACE) provides more relevant and robust signals for impending failure.
> - Conformal prediction allows for statistically bounded false positive rates for failure alarms, making the approach practical and safe without requiring failure data collection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a critical and evolving challenge in the field of robotics and machine learning: the robust and safe deployment of policies learned via imitation learning (IL) with generative models. Recent advancements in generative modeling, such as diffusion models and flow matching, have revolutionized IL, enabling robots to execute complex, long-horizon tasks by learning from high-dimensional, multimodal demonstration data. However, despite these breakthroughs, generative IL policies remain susceptible to unpredictable failures when encountering novel environments (distribution shifts) or accumulating small action errors (compounding errors). Such failures pose significant risks, especially in human-centered and safety-critical applications.

The broader research landscape for failure prediction in robotics can be categorized into several approaches. One category focuses on **out-of-distribution (OOD) detection**, aiming to identify observations or actions that deviate significantly from the training data. However, as the paper correctly points out, not all OOD situations lead to task failure; a policy might successfully generalize to certain novel scenarios. Pure OOD detectors, therefore, suffer from high false alarm rates. Another line of work employs **external monitoring**, often using vision-language models (VLMs), to track task progress and detect errors. While promising for interpretability, VLM-based methods are inherently reactive, identifying failures *after* they have manifested, rather than predicting them *before* they occur. Furthermore, many existing failure detection methods rely on access to labeled failure data, which is often difficult, expensive, or even dangerous to collect in real-world robotic systems.

FIPER distinguishes itself by addressing these fundamental limitations. It proposes a proactive, *runtime failure prediction* framework that operates *without requiring failure data*. By combining two distinct, yet complementary, indicators—OOD observations and high action uncertainty—FIPER aims to overcome the shortcomings of isolated approaches. It moves beyond simple OOD detection by seeking specific types of OOD situations coupled with policy uncertainty, and it offers *foresight* unlike reactive monitoring systems. Its general applicability to various generative IL policies (diffusion and flow matching) positions it as a valuable, task-agnostic tool for enhancing the safety and reliability of a wide array of modern robot learning systems. This work represents a significant step towards closing the gap between the impressive capabilities of advanced generative policies and their trustworthy deployment in practical, real-world settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind FIPER stems from the inherent fragility of even state-of-the-art generative imitation learning (IL) policies when deployed in complex, real-world environments. While these policies, powered by diffusion or flow matching models, have enabled robots to tackle challenging, long-horizon tasks, they are not infallible. Unexpected distribution shifts in observations (e.g., unseen lighting, object variations) or the accumulation of small action prediction errors can lead to erratic, unsafe, or ultimately failed behavior. In contexts where robots interact with humans or operate in safety-critical settings, predicting such failures *early and proactively* during runtime is paramount. Early warnings allow for timely human intervention, activation of safe fallback mechanisms, or dynamic requests for human demonstrations, thereby preventing hazardous situations or costly task restarts.

The authors identify two key challenges that prevent existing failure prediction methods from adequately addressing this need:
1. **Lack of Failure Data:** It is often impractical or dangerous to intentionally induce robot failures to collect labeled failure examples for training a classifier. The vast and diverse nature of potential failure modes further complicates comprehensive data collection.
2. **Limitations of Current Approaches:** Existing OOD detectors often trigger false alarms in benign novel situations that the policy can handle. Conversely, methods that monitor task progress (e.g., using VLMs) are reactive, identifying failures after they have already occurred, providing no predictive capability. Furthermore, many approaches focus solely on either observations or actions, neglecting the holistic interplay that leads to actual task failure.

Against this backdrop, the **key objectives** of this research are to:
1. **Develop a general framework (FIPER)** for runtime failure prediction specifically for generative IL policies.
2. **Eliminate the dependency on failure data**, making the framework practical and safe to implement.
3. **Identify and leverage robust indicators of impending failure** that go beyond simple OOD detection, specifically focusing on consecutive OOD observations and persistent high uncertainty in generated actions.
4. **Propose novel, robust scores** for these indicators: Random Network Distillation in the policy’s Observation Embedding space (RND-OE) for OOD observations, and Action-Chunk Entropy (ACE) for action uncertainty.
5. **Calibrate failure thresholds** using only a small set of successful policy rollouts via conformal prediction, ensuring a statistically bounded false positive rate.
6. **Combine these indicators logically** to better distinguish actual failures from benign novel situations.
7. **Demonstrate superior performance** in terms of accuracy, earliness of detection, and robustness across diverse simulated and real-world robotic environments and various failure modes, compared to existing baselines.
8. Ultimately, to contribute a foundational step towards **more interpretable and safer generative robot policies**.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FIPER's methodology is built on the core insight that actual policy failures are characterized by a dual pattern: persistent deviations in observations from expected successful execution, coupled with prolonged high uncertainty in the robot's generated actions. To operationalize this, FIPER employs two primary submodules, whose outputs are combined logically. Crucially, the entire framework is designed to operate without any failure data, relying solely on a small dataset of successful policy rollouts for training and calibration.

#### 3.1. Detecting Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Observations via Random Network Distillation (RND-OE)

To identify observation sequences indicative of impending failure, FIPER adapts Random Network Distillation (RND) to the policy's observation embedding space.
- **Mechanism:** RND involves two neural networks: a randomly initialized, frozen target network `g(·)` and a predictor network `f_θ(·)`. The predictor is trained to mimic the output of the target network using *successful in-distribution (ID) rollouts*.
- **Application to Policy Embeddings:** Instead of raw observations, RND-OE uses the *observation embeddings* (`h(O_t)`) produced by the policy's pre-trained encoder. This has two benefits: (1) it focuses on features relevant to the policy's decision-making, filtering out irrelevant visual noise, and (2) it allows for efficient training of the RND model even with limited successful data, leveraging the policy's existing feature extractor.
- **RND-OE Score (`s_RND(O_t)`):** The score is the L2 norm between the outputs of `f_θ(h(O_t))` and `g(h(O_t))`. Higher scores indicate greater deviation from the learned ID observation patterns.
- **Aggregation:** To account for compounding errors and short-lived, benign OOD situations, `s_RND(O_t)` is aggregated (summed) over a sliding time window of `w_O` policy timesteps, forming the observation-based failure prediction score `η_O(τ_:t)`.

#### 3.2. Detecting High Action Uncertainty via Action-Chunk Entropy (ACE)

While OOD observations signal a potentially problematic state, the ultimate consequence is determined by the policy's actions. FIPER measures uncertainty directly in the generated actions.
- **Challenge:** Generative IL policies often exhibit multimodal action distributions (e.g., multiple ways to grasp an object). Simple variance measures are insufficient as they might indicate high uncertainty even when the policy is deterministically selecting one of several valid modes.
- **Solution:** FIPER proposes Action-Chunk Entropy (ACE) to quantify the *sharpness* of the modes in the conditional action distribution `π(A|O_t)`.
- **Entropy Estimation (`s_ACE(A_t)`):** At each policy timestep `t`, a batch of `B` action chunks `A_t` is sampled from `π(·|O_t)`. The ACE score is computed as the sum of estimated entropies for each individual action prediction step (`t+i`) within the chunk. Entropy for each step is estimated using a computationally efficient dimension-wise binning approach, where the range of bins is determined offline from successful calibration data.
- **Aggregation:** Similar to RND-OE, `s_ACE(A_t)` is aggregated over a sliding window of `w_A` policy timesteps, yielding the action-based failure prediction score `η_A(τ_:t)`.

#### 3.3. Observation- AND Action-Based Failure Prediction with FIPER

- **Logical Conjunction:** FIPER combines the two scores (`η_O` and `η_A`) with a logical AND. A failure alarm is triggered *only if both* aggregated scores exceed their respective thresholds (`γ_O,t` and `γ_A,t`). This conjunctive approach is critical for robustness, as it prevents false alarms from isolated OOD observations or temporary, benign action uncertainties.
- **Conformal Prediction (CP) for Threshold Calibration:** The thresholds `γ_O,t` and `γ_A,t` are dynamically calibrated using conformal prediction on the same small set of successful ID rollouts. CP provides a statistical guarantee on the false positive rate (FPR), ensuring that the probability of incorrectly flagging a successful rollout as failed is bounded by a user-defined `δ`. The paper explores different CP-based threshold definitions: a one-sided CP band (time-varying), a CP constant threshold (single value across time), and a simpler time-varying threshold based on quantiles.

By integrating these components, FIPER constructs a lightweight, data-efficient, and robust framework capable of predicting policy failures at runtime without prior exposure to failure scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experimental evaluation of FIPER across five diverse environments (three simulation: SORTING, STACKING, PUSHT; two real-world: PRETZEL, PUSHCHAIR) and various failure modes yields several key findings:

1. **Discrimination Power of Proposed Scores (RND-OE and ACE):**
 - FIPER's individual scores, RND-OE and ACE, demonstrate a superior ability to differentiate between "Success OOD" (benign out-of-distribution situations the policy can handle) and "Fail ID" (in-distribution situations where the policy still fails) compared to baselines (PCA-kmeans, logpZO, RND-A, STAC).
 - Figure 4 visually confirms a clearer separation between these critical categories for RND-OE and ACE, indicating that they capture failure-relevant signals more effectively. This is crucial for avoiding excessive false alarms.

2. **Benefit of Combining Observation- and Action-Based Predictors:**
 - The logical conjunction (AND) of RND-OE and ACE is vital for robust failure prediction. Ablation studies (Table 2) show that the "AND" operator results in significantly higher Timestep-Wise Accuracy (TWA) and overall accuracy, primarily due to a much higher True Negative Rate (TNR).
 - This finding strongly supports FIPER's core hypothesis: actual policy failures are best predicted when *both* OOD observations and high action uncertainty are present simultaneously. The more conservative "AND" approach prevents false alarms from isolated anomalies, while still detecting a high proportion (91%) of true failures.

3. **Superior Accuracy and Earlier Detection Compared to Baselines:**
 - FIPER consistently outperforms state-of-the-art baselines across all evaluated environments (Table 1, Table 6). It achieves the highest average TWA (0.65) and accuracy (0.78), alongside a competitive Detection Time (DT) of 0.30.
 - FIPER's high average True Positive Rate (TPR) of 0.92 signifies its reliability in detecting diverse failure modes across different tasks. In contrast, several baselines struggle with either very low TPR (e.g., STAC in SORTING/STACKING/PUSHT) or very low TNR (e.g., PCA-kmeans, meaning many false alarms).
 - Specifically, ACE notably outperforms STAC, especially in tasks with high action multimodality, demonstrating the advantage of entropy-based uncertainty quantification over temporal consistency measures. RND-OE also shows better DT than other observation-based methods like logpZO.

4. **Effectiveness of Sliding Window Aggregation:**
 - The strategy of aggregating uncertainty scores over a *sliding window* (as used in FIPER) is shown to be superior to both cumulative score accumulation (used by STAC) and single-timestep detection (used by logpZO).
 - Cumulative scores (Figure 5) often lead to very late detection, as they tend to flag failures primarily due to the longer duration of failed rollouts, rather than predicting them early.
 - Single-timestep detection (Figure 6) leads to very low TNR (many false alarms), highlighting its lack of robustness to transient OOD situations.
 - The sliding window approach strikes an optimal balance, improving accuracy and significantly reducing false alarms while maintaining early detection capability.

In summary, FIPER successfully demonstrates that combining well-calibrated, aggregated scores for OOD observations and action uncertainty provides a robust, accurate, and proactive framework for failure prediction in generative robot policies, without the need for failure data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of FIPER represents a significant advancement in the field of safe and reliable robotics, particularly for systems leveraging modern generative imitation learning policies. Its impact can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Enhancing Robot Safety and Trustworthiness:** FIPER's ability to predict impending failures *at runtime and without failure data* is paramount for deploying robots in human-centered and safety-critical environments, such as collaborative industrial settings, healthcare, or personal assistance. By enabling early warnings, FIPER facilitates timely human intervention, activation of safe fallback procedures, or adaptive learning strategies (e.g., asking for human demonstrations), thereby preventing accidents, damage, or costly task failures. This directly contributes to building trust in autonomous robotic systems.

2. **Addressing a Core Limitation of Imitation Learning:** Generative IL policies, while powerful, inherently struggle with generalization beyond their training distribution and are susceptible to compounding errors. FIPER directly tackles this Achilles' heel by providing a robust mechanism to identify situations where the policy is likely to fail, making these advanced learning paradigms more viable for real-world deployment.

3. **Data Efficiency and Practicality:** The framework's independence from failure data is a critical advantage. Collecting comprehensive failure datasets is often impractical, costly, or even dangerous. By relying solely on a small set of successful rollouts for calibration, FIPER significantly lowers the barrier to entry for implementing effective failure prediction in new robotic applications.

4. **Generality and Broad Applicability:** FIPER's design is task-agnostic and demonstrated across diverse robot embodiments, observation spaces, and generative policy types (diffusion, flow matching). This generality suggests it can be readily integrated into a wide range of existing and future robot learning architectures, making it a broadly applicable tool for enhancing reliability.

5. **Increased Interpretability:** By separately monitoring OOD observations and action uncertainty, FIPER provides a more interpretable signal about the nature of an impending failure. This dual indicator can help diagnose whether the robot is facing an unfamiliar sensory input or is genuinely unsure about its next course of action, offering valuable insights for debugging, policy improvement, or human operators.

6. **Paving the Way for Adaptive and Proactive Robotics:** FIPER lays groundwork for more intelligent and adaptive robotic systems. Early failure prediction can inform online adaptation, policy retraining, active learning, or human-robot handovers, enabling robots to recover from challenging situations or learn new skills more efficiently and safely.

While the paper acknowledges limitations, such as the need to collect successful rollouts for calibration and potential computational demands for very high-dimensional action spaces, the fundamental contribution of a data-efficient, proactive, and robust failure prediction framework without relying on failure examples positions FIPER as an important step towards truly autonomous and dependable robotic deployment. It signifies progress not just in robot learning, but in the broader quest for safe AI.
