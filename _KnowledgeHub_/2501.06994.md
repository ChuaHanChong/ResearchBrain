# Motion Tracks: A Unified Representation for Human-Robot Transfer in Few-Shot Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Motion Track Policy (MT-pi) unifies human and robot demonstrations by predicting actions as short-horizon 2D keypoint trajectories in image space, bridging the embodiment gap so a policy trained mostly on human video plus few robot demos recovers 6DoF actions via two-view triangulation, hitting 86.5% average success across 4 real tasks.

## Problem

- Teleoperated robot demonstrations are labor-intensive and device-specific, so scaling imitation learning to hundreds or thousands of trajectories is impractical for many settings.
- Prior human-video approaches learn shared latent embeddings or reward functions whose output actions still depend on robot data, limiting generalization to motions absent from teleoperated demos.
- Explicit human-hand tracking methods assume tracked poses retarget directly to robot end-effectors, but morphological differences between hands and grippers break this direct replay.
- Pretrained visual representations from internet-scale human video prove brittle or hard to fine-tune for specific manipulation tasks due to pretraining data diversity.

## Method

- **Motion tracks**: a ==cross-embodiment action space== representing predicted 2D keypoint trajectories and grasp state for human hands or robot end-effectors, unifying action prediction across embodiments.
- **Keypoint Retargeting Network**: a small ==MLP== trained on noised human keypoints that maps robot gripper keypoints toward human-hand-like spacing, reducing embodiment-induced track divergence.
- **Motion Track Network** (**MT-pi**): a ==Diffusion Policy==-based head conditioned on ==ResNet== image embeddings and current keypoints, co-trained on human and robot data with ==KL-divergence== and ==domain-adaptation losses== to align visual embeddings.

## Results

- MT-pi achieves **86.5%** average success across 4 real-world tasks (Fork on Plate, Fold Cloth, Serve Egg, Put Away Socks), beating Diffusion Policy and ACT baselines by **40%** using only **25** robot demos plus human video.
- On Serve Egg, MT-pi matches DP/ACT success using **40%** less robot demonstration time by substituting roughly **10 minutes** of human video for teleoperated data.
- On drawer-closing, MT-pi trained with human plus robot data reaches **18/20** success closing in the human-video-only direction versus **0/20** for DP, ACT, and robot-only MT-pi.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Projecting actions onto the 2D image plane, rather than robot proprioceptive space, gives a representation naturally shared by human hands and robot end-effectors without paired demonstrations.
> - Explicitly representing human motions in the action space, not just implicitly through latent embeddings, lets policies generalize to behaviors never demonstrated on the robot.
> - Auxiliary embedding-alignment losses (KL-divergence, domain adaptation) help disentangle motion signal from visual appearance differences when co-training on mixed-embodiment data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical challenge in robotics: efficiently teaching robots to perform everyday tasks through Imitation Learning (IL). While IL is a powerful paradigm, its widespread adoption has been hindered by the substantial data requirements. Traditional IL often necessitates hundreds, or even tens of thousands, of human-teleoperated robot demonstrations, which are labor-intensive, time-consuming, and frequently demand specialized hardware and expertise.

The research landscape has explored various avenues to overcome this data bottleneck. One promising direction involves leveraging human videos, which are far more abundant and easier to collect. However, directly training IL policies from human videos is challenging due to two primary issues:
1. **Lack of Robot Action Labels:** Human videos provide visual information but no corresponding robot motor commands.
2. **Embodiment Gap:** The physical differences between human hands and robot end-effectors make it difficult to directly transfer observed human motions to a robot.

Prior works have attempted to bridge this gap in several ways:
- **Pretraining Visual Representations:** Approaches like those using Ego4D or Something-Something datasets pretrain visual encoders on large-scale human videos. However, these representations often prove brittle or difficult to fine-tune for specific robotic tasks due to the vast diversity of the pretraining data.
- **Shared State or Latent Embeddings:** Other methods collect task-specific human and robot data to learn shared state-space representations or latent embeddings. While these can effectively align input observations, they still rely on robot datasets to define the *output actions*, thereby limiting the policy's expressivity and generalization to motions not present in the robot data. For example, a robot trained only on demonstrations of closing a drawer to the right might fail to generalize to closing it to the left, even if such motions are present in human videos.
- **Explicit Human Hand Tracking:** Recent advancements in human hand tracking (e.g., HaMeR) allow for reliable extraction of human hand poses from videos. Some works attempt to directly retarget these human poses to robot end-effectors. However, the significant morphological differences (e.g., size and shape) between human hands and robot grippers mean that a direct replay often fails to produce the desired robot behavior.

"MOTION TRACKS" introduces a novel approach that differentiates itself from prior art by fundamentally rethinking the action representation itself. Instead of merely aligning input representations or implicitly extracting priors, this work proposes a **unified, cross-embodiment action space** based on 2D image-space trajectories of keypoints. This innovative action space directly enables co-training on both human and robot data, effectively addressing the limitations of prior methods by disentangling motion differences from visual differences and providing a common ground for learning. By building upon the Diffusion Policy framework, it integrates state-of-the-art policy architectures with its unique action representation and data utilization strategy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation behind "MOTION TRACKS" is to significantly reduce the data collection burden associated with training robot policies through Imitation Learning, thereby making robotic automation of everyday tasks more feasible and scalable. The authors identify the prohibitive cost and time investment of collecting teleoperated robot demonstrations as the primary bottleneck.

Against this backdrop, the paper sets out to achieve several key objectives:

1. **Develop a Sample-Efficient IL Policy:** The primary objective is to create an imitation learning policy (MT-π) that can be effectively trained with minimal robot data—specifically, just a few dozen teleoperated robot demonstrations—supplemented by easily-collectible human video data, on the order of minutes. This directly tackles the data bottleneck in robot learning.

2. **Bridge the Human-Robot Embodiment Gap through a Unified Action Space:** A core objective is to overcome the challenge of transferring skills from human videos to robots despite the physical differences between human hands and robot end-effectors. The paper proposes that representing actions as "motion tracks"—short-horizon 2D trajectories of keypoints in image space—can serve as a unified, cross-embodiment action space compatible with both human hands and robot grippers.

3. **Achieve High Performance on Real-World Tasks:** The goal is not just sample efficiency but also robust and reliable task execution. The policy should achieve high success rates across a variety of real-world manipulation tasks, demonstrating practical applicability.

4. **Enable Generalization to Novel Scenarios:** A critical objective is to enable the robot policy to generalize to task variations or specific motions that may only be present in the more diverse human video demonstrations, rather than being restricted by the (often limited) coverage of the robot demonstration dataset.

5. **Promote Reproducibility and Further Research:** By open-sourcing the code and resources, the authors aim to facilitate replication of their results and encourage the broader research community to build upon their contributions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the "MOTION TRACKS" (MT-π) approach lies in its novel, unified action space: **motion tracks**. These are defined as short-horizon 2D trajectories of keypoints in image space, along with a binary grasp indicator. This representation is agnostic to the specific embodiment (human hand or robot end-effector), making it suitable for co-training on both types of demonstrations.

The methodology can be broken down into three main stages: Data Preprocessing, Training Pipeline, and Action Inference.

#### 3.1. A. Data Preprocessing

1. **Robot Demonstrations:**
 - **Data Collection:** Requires at least one calibrated camera (with known camera-to-robot extrinsics) and access to robot proprioceptive states.
 - **Keypoint Extraction:** The 3D position of the robot's end-effector is projected into the 2D image plane using calibrated extrinsics to obtain `k=5` keypoints. These 5 points are strategically placed (two per gripper finger, one in the center) to represent gripper positioning for grasping.
 - **Grasp State:** The robot's gripper open/close state is directly recorded as a binary grasp variable.

2. **Human Demonstrations:**
 - **Data Collection:** Uses standard RGB cameras, simplifying collection as calibrated extrinsics are not required during this phase.
 - **Keypoint Extraction:** An off-the-shelf hand pose detector, HaMeR, extracts 21 keypoints for the human hand. A subset of `k=5` keypoints (wrist, two on the thumb, two on the index finger) is selected to roughly match the structure and positioning of the robot gripper keypoints, establishing an explicit correspondence.
 - **Grasp State Inference:** Since human videos lack explicit grasp labels, a heuristic approach is employed. Foundation models like GroundingDINO and SAM-v2 are used to obtain pixel-wise object masks. A grasp is inferred (`g=1`) if the proximity between keypoints on the thumb and any other fingertip and the object mask falls below a predefined threshold.

#### 3.2. B. Training Pipeline

The MT-π policy is co-trained on this hybrid dataset using a sophisticated architecture designed to handle embodiment differences and visual variations.

1. **Keypoint Retargeting Network:**
 - **Motivation:** Despite explicitly matched keypoint counts and general positions, inherent morphological differences (e.g., size, spacing) between human and robot end-effectors still exist. Directly using these points could lead the policy to over-index on these subtle differences.
 - **Mechanism:** A small Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) is trained to map "noisy" keypoints back to their original, canonical positions, using human demonstrations where noise is added to keypoints (except an anchor point). Once trained and frozen, this network effectively "normalizes" input keypoint spacing. For robot keypoints, it implicitly treats them as "noisy" versions of human-like spacing, mapping them to positions more closely resembling human hands. For human keypoints, it acts as an identity map on their original, noise-free positions. This helps create a more unified keypoint representation across embodiments.

2. **Motion Track Network:**
 - **Architecture:** The policy head is based on a Diffusion Policy objective, a state-of-the-art architecture for visuomotor policy learning.
 - **Input:** The network receives a concatenation of image embeddings (extracted from a pre-trained ResNet encoder) and the current 2D keypoint positions `s_t^(i)` in pixel space.
 - **Output:** It predicts future offsets for each of the 2D keypoints and the corresponding gripper state `g_t^(i)` over a short prediction horizon `H`. The output is a `(2k+1) x H` tensor, representing `k` (u,v) coordinates and one grasp state for `H` timesteps.
 - **Viewpoint Agnosticism:** Crucially, the model is trained on single-viewpoint images at a time and is agnostic to the specific viewpoint pose, making it compatible with diverse human video datasets often captured from a single RGB camera.

3. **Image Representation Alignment:**
 - **Motivation:** To prevent the policy from being distracted by visual discrepancies between human and robot images (e.g., human vs. robot skin/material, background objects), auxiliary losses are incorporated.
 - **Losses:**
 - `ℓ_KL`: A KL-Divergence loss minimizes the divergence between human and robot feature distributions in the embedding space.
 - `ℓ_DA`: A Domain Adaptation loss uses a discriminator to encourage the policy's image encoder to produce indistinguishable embeddings for human and robot inputs, forcing it to learn features relevant to the task rather than embodiment.
 - **Total Loss:** The overall training objective combines the Diffusion Policy loss with a weighted sum of `ℓ_KL` and `ℓ_DA`.

#### 3.3. C. Action Inference

At test time, the robot needs 6DoF end-effector actions. MT-π leverages multi-view geometry to convert its 2D motion track predictions into 3D robot commands.

1. **Input:** The robot's current gripper keypoints `s_t^(i)` are first passed through the frozen Keypoint Retargeting Network to obtain `s_t^(i)` (retargeted).
2. **Multi-View Prediction:** The Motion Track Network (`π_θ`) takes the retargeted keypoints and image embeddings from *two* cameras (with known extrinsics) and predicts 2D motion tracks and gripper states for both views.
3. **3D Reconstruction:** Stereo triangulation, using the known camera extrinsics, reconstructs the 3D positions of the predicted keypoints from their 2D correspondences in the two camera views.
4. **6DoF Action Recovery:** Given the current and predicted future 3D keypoints, a rigid transformation (rotation `R` and translation `t`) is computed to best align the 3D keypoints between consecutive timesteps. These relative transformations form the 6DoF end-effector delta actions (`a_t:t+H`) that are executed on the robot. The policy operates in a closed-loop fashion, using a short prediction horizon (`H=16`).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of MT-π focused on its ability to leverage human video, the efficiency of its action space, and its generalization capabilities. The policy was tested on four real-world manipulation tasks: Fork on Plate, Fold Cloth, Serve Egg, and Put Away Socks, and compared against two state-of-the-art image-based Imitation Learning algorithms, Diffusion Policy (DP) and Action Chunking Transformer (ACT).

1. **Superior Performance in Low Robot Data Regime:**
 - MT-π significantly outperformed both DP and ACT across all four real-world tasks. With just 25 teleoperated robot demonstrations and an additional 10 minutes of human video per task, MT-π achieved an average success rate of 86.5%.
 - This represents a substantial improvement of approximately 40% compared to DP and ACT, which were trained on the same 25 robot demonstrations (and additionally equipped with wrist camera observations, which MT-π did not use, making its performance even more striking).
 - This finding strongly supports the hypothesis that MT-π's ability to incorporate human video demonstrations leads to more robust and successful task executions, especially when robot data is scarce and might not capture the full diversity of required motions.

2. **Enhanced Sample Efficiency with Motion Tracks and Human Video:**
 - **Motion Tracks as an Efficient Representation:** When MT-π was trained *only* on robot data (without human videos), its success rates matched those of DP and ACT given the same amount of robot demonstrations. This indicates that the 2D pixel-space motion track representation itself is highly sample-efficient, even without cross-embodiment data.
 - **Value of Human Videos:** The performance heatmaps for the "Serve Egg" task showed that MT-π could achieve success rates comparable to baselines using 40% *less* robot demonstration minutes by leveraging only about 10 minutes of human video. The results also suggested that fixing a small amount of robot data, MT-π's performance can noticeably increase by merely scaling up human video demonstrations (on the order of minutes). This highlights the cost-effectiveness of integrating human video.

3. **Generalization to Motions Present Only in Human Video:**
 - One of the most compelling findings was MT-π's ability to generalize to novel motions that were *only* observed in human video demonstrations. In a drawer-closing task, where robot data showed only closing to the right, but human videos included both right and left closures, MT-π (trained with both human and robot data) successfully closed the drawer to the left in 18 out of 20 trials.
 - In contrast, DP, ACT, and even a variant of MT-π trained *only* on robot data showed 0% success in generalizing to the leftward closing motion. This demonstrates the power of the unified action space in enabling positive transfer of diverse human-observed behaviors to a robot, moving beyond the limitations imposed by solely robot-centric action spaces.

4. **Open-Source Contribution:** The authors have made their code and videos publicly available, fostering reproducibility and providing a valuable resource for the research community.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "MOTION TRACKS" paper presents a significant advancement in robot imitation learning, offering a novel and highly impactful solution to several long-standing challenges.

1. **Drastically Reduced Data Burden for Robot Learning:** The most immediate and profound impact is the significant reduction in the amount of robot-specific data required to train competent manipulation policies. By demonstrating that high success rates can be achieved with just minutes of human video and tens of robot demonstrations, MT-π makes robot learning far more accessible and scalable. This addresses a critical bottleneck that has historically limited the deployment of advanced robotic systems in diverse, real-world scenarios. It lowers the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners, accelerating the pace of innovation in robotic manipulation.

2. **Effective Bridging of the Human-Robot Embodiment Gap:** The introduction of "motion tracks" as a unified, cross-embodiment action space is a major conceptual contribution. Unlike previous attempts that focused on aligning input features or implicitly inferring priors, this work directly redefines the action space to be compatible across human and robot embodiments. This elegant solution allows for direct transfer of motion knowledge from human demonstrations without needing complex retargeting or suffering from morphological mismatches, paving the way for more natural and intuitive robot learning from human behavior.

3. **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities:** The demonstrated ability of MT-π to generalize to motions and scenarios seen *only* in human videos is a crucial step towards more adaptable and robust robot policies. This means robots can learn a broader range of skills from readily available human data, rather than being confined to the limited repertoire of teleoperated robot movements. This improved generalization is vital for deploying robots in dynamic and unstructured environments where every possible interaction cannot be explicitly demonstrated with a robot.

4. **Practical Applicability and Modularity:** The method's success on real-world table-top tasks (like "Fold Cloth" and "Serve Egg") underscores its practical utility. The modular design of MT-π, particularly its reliance on off-the-shelf hand pose detectors and foundation models for grasp inference, means it can readily incorporate future advancements in perception and visual understanding, making it a future-proof framework.

5. **Foundation for Future Research Directions:** The paper also clearly outlines current limitations, such as the sensitivity to noise in human video inputs, lack of explicit cross-view consistency, and reliance on heuristic grasp detection. These limitations themselves provide fertile ground for future research, including:
 - Developing methods for more robust handling of "in-the-wild" human videos (drastic viewpoint shifts, occlusions).
 - Integrating motion-centric approaches with object-centric representations from foundation models for more nuanced interaction.
 - Extending the framework to more complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks.
 - Implementing explicit architectural mechanisms to enforce cross-view consistency for 3D reconstruction.

In conclusion, "MOTION TRACKS" offers a compelling new paradigm for imitation learning, significantly advancing the field by making robot skill acquisition more data-efficient, generalizable, and scalable. Its impact is likely to be felt across academic research and practical robotic applications, accelerating the development of truly capable and adaptable robots.
