# Understanding Domain Randomization for Sim-to-real Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper builds a theory of sim-to-real transfer in which the simulator is a set of MDPs with tunable latent parameters, and domain randomization is an oracle returning the optimal history-dependent policy of the induced latent MDP. Under a bounded-diameter assumption it proves the sim-to-real gap is polylogarithmic in horizon for separated simulator classes and order square-root-H otherwise, matching a lower bound, using no real-world samples.

## Problem

- Domain randomization works empirically in robotics and driving, yet no theory explains why a policy trained purely in randomized simulation transfers without real-world fine-tuning.
- Earlier sim-to-real analyses measure model mismatch or prove sample-complexity savings, but all require real-world samples for adaptation, so none covers zero-shot randomized transfer.
- Latent-MDP and POMDP work studies finding optimal policies inside the latent model, leaving open how such a policy performs when deployed in one specific real environment.

## Method

- Sim-to-real formulation models the simulator as an MDP set with tunable latent parameters that contains the real MDP, scoring policies by the ==sim-to-real gap== versus the real world's optimal value.
- **Domain Randomization Oracle** casts training as returning the optimal history-dependent policy of a ==latent MDP== under a uniform distribution over the simulator set, formalizing randomization plus memory.
- A ==base-policy reduction== (two lemmas linking episodic value to average gain) turns gap bounding into algorithm design: ==MDP-elimination== under separation, ==optimistic exploration== with general function approximation otherwise.

## Results

- For a finite, delta-separated class of M MDPs with diameter D, the gap is **O(D·M³·log(MH)·log²(SMH/delta)/delta⁴)**, only polylogarithmic, order **log³H**, in the horizon.
- Without separation the bound becomes **O(D·sqrt(M³·H·log(MH)))**, and a lower bound of **Omega(sqrt(DMH))** proves square-root-H is unavoidable for any history-dependent policy.
- Infinite classes with L-Lipschitz smoothness give **O(D·sqrt(d_e·H·log(H·N))/nu + L·epsilon)**; the byproduct algorithm attains regret order **D·sqrt(d_e·T)** for average-reward MDPs with general function approximation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Memory is load-bearing: without history-dependent policies the agent cannot implicitly identify which environment it inhabits, and worst-case loss stays linear in horizon.
> - Randomization pays off only when the sampled class covers the true system smoothly; the probability mass near the real MDP enters the bound directly.
> - Reachability assumptions are not cosmetic: dropping bounded diameter, or smoothness in infinite classes, collapses the guarantee to trivial linear-in-horizon loss.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has achieved remarkable successes in domains with readily available and high-fidelity simulators, such as games like Atari, Go, and StarCraft. However, deploying RL agents directly in real-world environments—like robotics, autonomous driving, or healthcare—presents significant challenges. The primary obstacle is the prohibitive cost, time, and safety risks associated with collecting sufficient real-world interaction data. This limitation has spurred significant interest in **sim-to-real transfer**, a paradigm where an agent is trained in a simulated environment and then deployed in the real world.

The core challenge in sim-to-real transfer is the **"sim-to-real gap"** or **"reality gap"**: the inherent mismatch between the simulated environment and its real-world counterpart. This discrepancy can cause policies optimized in simulation to perform poorly when transferred, despite the simulator being a "rough approximation" of reality. Prior efforts to mitigate this gap have included engineering more accurate simulators and developing various domain adaptation techniques.

**Domain Randomization (DR)** has emerged as one of the most empirically successful and widely adopted methods for addressing the sim-to-real gap. Instead of meticulously trying to match the simulator to reality, DR takes a different approach: it intentionally randomizes various physical and visual parameters (e.g., friction coefficients, object appearances, lighting) within the simulator during training. The goal is to expose the RL agent to a diverse range of simulated environments, thereby compelling it to learn a robust policy that is less sensitive to specific simulation parameters and thus generalizes better to unseen real-world variations. DR has demonstrated impressive empirical successes in complex robotics tasks, such as dexterous manipulation and quadrotor control, often enabling **zero-shot transfer**, where the policy trained purely in simulation can be directly applied to the physical world without any real-world fine-tuning.

Despite its widespread practical success, the theoretical foundations of domain randomization have remained largely unexplored. Existing theoretical work on sim-to-real transfer (e.g., Jiang, 2018; Feng et al., 2019; Zhong et al., 2019) typically focuses on scenarios where some amount of real-world interaction is allowed for fine-tuning or adaptation. This paper explicitly distinguishes itself by focusing on the **zero-shot transfer capability** of domain randomization, which is one of its most appealing features in practical applications due to its cost-effectiveness.

Furthermore, this work draws connections to the theoretical framework of **Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs)**, specifically **Latent MDPs (LMDPs)** (Kwon et al., 2021). LMDPs model environments where the true underlying MDP is unknown but sampled from a known set. This paper posits that domain randomization effectively trains an optimal policy for an LMDP where the simulator implicitly defines the set of MDPs, and the randomization procedure defines a distribution over them (often uniform). While prior LMDP research focuses on learning optimal policies within the LMDP, this paper uniquely analyzes the *performance gap* when such an LMDP-optimal policy is transferred to a *specific* real-world MDP within the set.

Finally, the paper leverages and contributes to the field of **infinite-horizon average-reward MDPs**. This connection is crucial for analyzing the long-term performance and regret bounds of policies in their proposed theoretical framework. Notably, a byproduct of their analysis is the development of a novel, provably efficient algorithm for this setting, which is a significant standalone contribution to the broader RL theory literature.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to move beyond the empirical successes of domain randomization (DR) and provide a rigorous **theoretical understanding** of its effectiveness in sim-to-real transfer. Despite DR being a widely adopted and powerful technique in robotics and autonomous systems, the fundamental question of *why* this seemingly simple approach works so well has largely remained unanswered from a theoretical standpoint.

The authors are primarily motivated to quantify the **"sim-to-real gap"**—the performance degradation when a policy learned purely in simulation (using DR) is transferred to the real physical world without any additional real-world training samples. This is particularly challenging and important because real-world interactions are often costly, time-consuming, and potentially risky. A theoretical guarantee for zero-shot transfer would provide strong validation for DR and guide its future application.

More specifically, the paper aims to achieve the following key objectives:

1. **Formulate a Theoretical Framework:** Propose a novel mathematical model for sim-to-real transfer that adequately captures the essence of domain randomization. This involves modeling the simulator as a set of MDPs with tunable parameters and the real world as one specific MDP within this set.
2. **Quantify the Sim-to-Real Gap:** Derive sharp theoretical bounds on the difference between the value of the policy returned by domain randomization and the value of an optimal policy for the real world. This involves analyzing the gap under various conditions on the "simulator class" (the set of MDPs randomized over).
3. **Identify Critical Factors for Success:** Determine what conditions or properties of the simulator and the learning process are necessary for successful sim-to-real transfer with DR. In particular, the paper seeks to highlight the importance of "memory" (i.e., history-dependent policies) in domain randomization, which allows the agent to infer unknown environment parameters.
4. **Develop Novel Analysis Techniques:** Propose new proof techniques that bridge the gap between analyzing the sim-to-real problem and designing efficient learning algorithms for infinite-horizon MDPs, which could have broader implications for reinforcement learning theory.
5. **Contribute to General RL Theory:** As a byproduct, develop a new, provably efficient algorithm for learning in infinite-horizon average-reward MDPs with general function approximation, addressing an open problem in the field.

In essence, the motivation is to transform domain randomization from a "bag of tricks" into a principled approach by providing concrete performance guarantees and shedding light on its underlying mechanisms, thus fostering more effective and theoretically sound applications of sim-to-real transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a rigorous theoretical approach to analyze domain randomization (DR) for sim-to-real transfer, building a novel framework that bridges concepts from episodic RL, Partially Observable MDPs (POMDPs), and infinite-horizon average-reward MDPs.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Formulation

1. **Simulator as a Set of MDPs (U):** The core idea is to model the simulator not as a single MDP, but as a collection or "set" of MDPs, $U$. Each $M \in U$ represents a specific configuration of latent parameters (e.g., friction, damping) that influence the transition dynamics $P_M$. The reward function $R$ is assumed to be common across all MDPs in $U$ for simplicity, though the authors note this can be extended.
2. **Real World as an MDP within the Set ($M^*$):** A crucial assumption is that the real-world environment, $M^*$, is itself a member of this set $U$. This implies that the randomization space "covers" the real environment.
3. **Domain Randomization as a Latent MDP (LMDP) Oracle:** The training phase of DR is formulated as finding an optimal history-dependent policy, $\pi_{DR}^*$, for a Latent MDP $(U, \nu)$. Here, $U$ is the set of randomized MDPs, and $\nu$ is a uniform distribution over $U$. This means the agent optimizes for average performance across the randomized simulators, without explicit knowledge of which specific MDP it's currently interacting with. The choice of history-dependent policies (e.g., implemented with RNNs like LSTMs in practice) is explicitly motivated by their ability to "implicitly identify" the environment.
4. **Performance Metric: Sim-to-Real Gap:** The effectiveness of $\pi_{DR}^*$ is measured by the "sim-to-real gap," defined as $Gap(\pi) = V_{M^*,1}^*(s_1) - V_{M^*,1}^\pi(s_1)$. This measures how much worse the transferred policy $\pi$ performs in the real world $M^*$ compared to the optimal policy *for the real world itself*. A key aspect is that $\pi$ is learned *exclusively in simulation*, without any real-world samples for fine-tuning (zero-shot transfer).

#### 3.2. Key Assumptions

- **Episodic RL:** The problem is framed in an episodic setting with a finite horizon $H$.
- **Communicating MDPs (Assumption 1):** All MDPs in $U$ are assumed to be "communicating" with a bounded diameter $D$. This means an agent can reach any state from any other state in at most $D$ expected steps, which is crucial for exploration and ensures connectivity. A lower bound (Proposition 1) demonstrates that without this assumption, the sim-to-real gap can be $\Omega(H)$.

#### 3.3. Analysis Settings and Approaches

The paper analyzes the sim-to-real gap under three increasingly complex settings for the simulator class $U$:

1. **Finite Simulator Class with Separation Condition (Assumption 2):**
 - **Condition:** Any two distinct MDPs $M_1, M_2 \in U$ are $\delta$-separated, meaning there exists at least one state-action pair $(s,a)$ where their transition probabilities differ by at least $\delta$ in $L_1$ distance. This implies the MDPs are "distinguishable."
 - **Approach:** The proof involves constructing a *hypothetical "base policy"* $\hat{\pi}$ that explicitly attempts to identify the true $M^*$ from $U$. This $\hat{\pi}$ operates in two stages: (1) an "exploration" stage where it collects samples on informative state-action pairs to iteratively eliminate unlikely MDPs from the candidate set $D$, leveraging the $\delta$-separation; (2) once $M^*$ is identified, it switches to following the optimal policy for $M^*$. The bounds on $\hat{\pi}$'s performance are then used via a novel reduction lemma (Lemma 1) to bound the performance of $\pi_{DR}^*$.

2. **Finite Simulator Class Without Separation Condition:**
 - **Challenge:** Without the $\delta$-separation, explicitly identifying $M^*$ is no longer feasible, as MDPs can be arbitrarily close.
 - **Approach:** The authors introduce a crucial connection to **infinite-horizon average-reward MDPs**. Lemma 2 shows that the episodic value function $V_{M,1}^*(s_1)$ is approximately $H \cdot \rho_M^*$, where $\rho_M^*$ is the optimal average reward for MDP $M$. This allows bounding the episodic sim-to-real gap by the regret in an equivalent infinite-horizon setting.
 - **Base Policy Construction:** A new "optimistic exploration" algorithm (Algorithm 3) is proposed. This algorithm maintains a set of plausible MDPs and, at each episode, selects an optimistic MDP with the highest average reward. It interacts with this MDP until observed data indicates it's inconsistent with the true environment, then updates its belief set. This process is essentially a regret-minimization algorithm in the infinite-horizon setting.

3. **Infinite Simulator Class:**
 - **Challenge:** The set $U$ is continuous, reflecting scenarios like uniformly sampling physical parameters from an interval. This implies $\nu(M^*) = 0$.
 - **Assumptions:** Requires a "smoothness" condition (Assumption 3), stating that the value function of $\pi_{DR}^*$ is $L$-Lipschitz with respect to a distance measure $d(M_1, M_2)$ between MDPs, at least within an $\epsilon_0$-neighborhood of $M^*$.
 - **Complexity Measure:** The complexity of the infinite class $U$ is characterized using concepts from statistical learning theory: the $\epsilon$-eluder dimension ($d_e$) and $\epsilon$-covering number ($N(\mathcal{F}, \epsilon)$) of a function class $\mathcal{F}$ related to the MDP's transition dynamics and optimal bias functions (defined in Appendix A.2).
 - **Base Policy Construction:** Algorithm 4, a "General Optimistic Algorithm," is developed. This is an advanced model-based algorithm for infinite-horizon average-reward MDPs with general function approximation. It uses an "importance score" to decide when to update its internal model, aiming for a "low-switching" property to control overall regret.

#### 3.4. Proof Strategy

The overarching proof strategy involves:
1. **Reduction (Lemma 1):** Showing that if *any* history-dependent policy $\hat{\pi}$ can achieve a bounded maximum sim-to-real gap over all $M \in U$, then $\pi_{DR}^*$ (the LMDP optimal policy) also achieves a bounded gap. This shifts the burden to constructing a "good" $\hat{\pi}$.
2. **Construction of $\hat{\pi}$:** Designing concrete algorithms ($\hat{\pi}$) for each setting that demonstrate strong performance.
3. **Connection to Regret Minimization:** For settings without separation or with infinite classes, showing that the episodic sim-to-real gap of $\hat{\pi}$ is directly related to the regret achieved by an equivalent online learning algorithm in an infinite-horizon average-reward setting. This allows leveraging powerful tools and known results from online RL theory.

This layered methodological approach allows the authors to systematically tackle the problem under various realistic assumptions, progressively building toward more general and complex scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key theoretical findings that quantify the sim-to-real gap for domain randomization (DR) under various conditions, shedding light on its effectiveness and necessary components.

1. **Sim-to-Real Gap with Finite Simulator Class and Separation (Theorem 1):**
 - **Finding:** When the set of randomized MDPs $U$ is finite ($|U|=M$) and all MDPs are $\delta$-separated (Assumption 2), the sim-to-real gap of the policy $\pi_{DR}^*$ is remarkably small, specifically **$O(D M^3 \log(MH) \log^2(SMH/\delta) / \delta^4)$**.
 - **Implication:** This result shows that for long horizons $H$, the performance loss is only *poly-logarithmic* in $H$. This means DR can be highly effective in long-horizon tasks if the simulated environments are sufficiently distinct and the real environment is well-covered. The exponential dependence on $M$ and inverse polynomial on $\delta$ suggest that having too many *very similar* environments in the randomized set, or very subtle differences, can significantly increase the gap.

2. **Sim-to-Real Gap with Finite Simulator Class Without Separation (Theorem 2):**
 - **Finding:** When $U$ is finite but MDPs are not necessarily $\delta$-separated, the sim-to-real gap of $\pi_{DR}^*$ is **$O(D \sqrt{M^3 H \log(MH)})$**.
 - **Implication:** This demonstrates that even without strong separability, DR can achieve an $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H})$ sim-to-real gap. This is a crucial result, as $\sqrt{H}$ represents a sublinear loss per step ($\tilde{O}(H^{-1/2})$), which is generally considered very efficient in online learning. The authors explicitly highlight that this sublinear gap is due to the combined power of **randomization** (which provides a diverse training set) and **memory (history-dependent policies)**, which allows the agent to implicitly identify and adapt to the specific environment it is in. Without memory, the policy wouldn't be able to "tell the difference" and adapt.

3. **Lower Bound for Finite Class Without Separation (Theorem 3):**
 - **Finding:** Under the communicating MDPs assumption (Assumption 1), any history-dependent policy will suffer a sim-to-real gap of at least **$\Omega(\sqrt{DMH})$**.
 - **Implication:** This lower bound matches the $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H})$ upper bound from Theorem 2 (ignoring poly-log factors), proving that the derived bound is nearly optimal in terms of $H$, $D$, and $M$. This confirms that an $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H})$ gap is the best achievable in this general setting, validating the efficiency of domain randomization.

4. **Sim-to-Real Gap with Infinite Simulator Class (Theorem 4):**
 - **Finding:** For infinite simulator classes $U$, under Assumption 1 (communicating MDPs) and a "smoothness" condition (Assumption 3, where the value function is L-Lipschitz continuous around $M^*$), the sim-to-real gap of $\pi_{DR}^*$ is **$O(D \sqrt{d_e H \log(H \cdot N(\mathcal{F}, 1/H))} / \nu(C_{M^*, \epsilon}) + L\epsilon)$**.
 - **Implication:** This generalizes the $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H})$ result to continuous parameter spaces, which are common in practical DR. The complexity of the infinite class is captured by the $\epsilon$-eluder dimension ($d_e$) and $\epsilon$-covering number ($N$) of a related function class $\mathcal{F}$. The term $\nu(C_{M^*, \epsilon})$ represents the probability mass assigned by the uniform distribution $\nu$ to an $\epsilon$-neighborhood of $M^*$. This implies that for DR to work well, the real environment $M^*$ must not be in an "isolated" or "sparse" region of the randomized simulator space. The result highlights that proper design of the randomization distribution and parameters is crucial.

5. **Necessity of Assumptions and Negative Results (Proposition 1 & 2):**
 - **Proposition 1:** Confirms that without the "communicating MDPs" assumption (bounded diameter D), the sim-to-real gap can indeed be $\Omega(H)$, meaning DR would be ineffective.
 - **Proposition 2:** Shows that even if MDPs in $U$ have nice low-rank properties, the sim-to-real gap can still be $\Omega(H)$ for large simulator classes if the "smoothness condition" (Assumption 3) does not hold. This underscores the necessity of having a sufficiently "well-behaved" or "dense" randomization space around the real environment.

6. **Contribution to Infinite-Horizon Average-Reward MDPs (Algorithm 4):**
 - **Finding:** As a significant byproduct, the paper proposes Algorithm 4, which is claimed to be the *first provably efficient model-based algorithm for learning infinite-horizon average-reward MDPs with general function approximation*. It achieves a regret bound of $\tilde{O}(D \sqrt{d_e T})$, where $T$ is the total timesteps.
 - **Implication:** This is a notable theoretical contribution to general RL theory, independent of the sim-to-real context, addressing an open problem in efficient online exploration.

In summary, the main findings rigorously establish that domain randomization can achieve efficient zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with sublinear regret in $H$ under mild and realistic conditions, particularly emphasizing the critical role of history-dependent policies and the structure of the randomized simulator set.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper marks a significant step forward in the theoretical understanding of domain randomization (DR) for sim-to-real transfer, moving it from a largely empirical success story to a theoretically grounded method. Its findings have substantial significance and potential impact across several fronts:

1. **Bridging the Theory-Practice Gap:** The most immediate impact is providing the first comprehensive theoretical framework and provable guarantees for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer using DR. For a technique widely used in robotics and other real-world applications, having such guarantees is crucial. It enhances confidence in DR, allowing researchers and practitioners to understand *why* it works beyond intuitive empirical observations.

2. **Guiding Simulator Design and Randomization Strategies:** The derived bounds offer concrete insights into what makes DR effective.
 - The importance of the **"communicating MDPs"** assumption (bounded diameter $D$) suggests that simulators should ideally allow agents to navigate freely between states within a reasonable number of steps.
 - For **finite simulator classes**, the results highlight that while a "separation condition" (distinct MDPs) leads to very strong poly-logarithmic guarantees, even without it, the $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{H})$ bound is achievable. This suggests that creating many subtly different randomized environments is still beneficial.
 - For **infinite simulator classes**, the crucial role of the **"smoothness condition"** and the probability mass $\nu(C_{M^*, \epsilon})$ emphasizes that the randomization distribution should ideally be dense and "smooth" around the true real-world environment ($M^*$). This provides guidance for choosing the ranges and distributions of randomized parameters in practice.

3. **Validating the Role of Memory in Policies:** The theoretical results strongly emphasize that the success of DR, particularly in achieving sublinear regret, relies heavily on the use of **history-dependent policies (i.e., policies with "memory")**. In practice, this often translates to using recurrent neural networks (like LSTMs) in the agent's architecture. The paper rigorously explains that memory allows the agent to implicitly infer the underlying physical parameters of the environment during an episode and adapt its behavior accordingly, which is critical when the environment is partially observable (as it is for the agent in an LMDP setting). This theoretical validation strengthens the architectural choices commonly made in DR.

4. **Advancement in General Reinforcement Learning Theory:** The development of Algorithm 4, a provably efficient algorithm for infinite-horizon average-reward MDPs with general function approximation and $\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})$ regret, is a significant standalone contribution. This addresses an open problem in the field of online reinforcement learning theory, making it valuable beyond the direct context of sim-to-real transfer. It offers a new tool for designing efficient exploration strategies in complex, long-term RL problems where the underlying dynamics might be represented by rich function classes.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work opens several avenues for future research:
 - **Relaxing Assumptions:** Investigating DR under more relaxed assumptions, such as when $M^*$ is *not* perfectly covered by $U$, or when rewards are also randomized and complex.
 - **Integration with Real-World Fine-Tuning:** Extending the framework to analyze the benefits of DR when combined with limited real-world fine-tuning, which is a common practice.
 - **Practical Implications of Theoretical Insights:** Translating the theoretical insights on memory and simulator structure into concrete recommendations for real-world DR implementation, potentially leading to more effective and efficient sim-to-real pipelines.
 - **Beyond Eluder Dimension:** Exploring even richer function approximation classes or alternative complexity measures for infinite simulator spaces.

In conclusion, this paper provides a much-needed theoretical bedrock for domain randomization. By quantifying its effectiveness, highlighting its core mechanisms, and linking it to broader RL theory, it not only explains past successes but also lays down a robust foundation for the principled design and application of sim-to-real transfer in future intelligent systems.
