# Denoising Multi-Robot Trajectories

> [!summary] Summary
> D4ORM is a learning-free, GPU-parallel sampling-based optimizer that plans multi-robot trajectories by iteratively optimizing deformations of the team's joint control sequence, each iteration run as a diffusion-denoising pass guided by Monte Carlo score estimates. Extended into decoupled, coarse-to-fine receding-horizon and distributed variants, it beats MPPI, CEM, IPOPT and a learned diffusion planner, scaling to 100 robots and real quadrotor and ground-robot teams.

## Problem

- Numerical optimizers such as IPOPT struggle with the nonconvex collision constraints, multimodal solution landscapes and high dimensionality of multi-robot planning, failing outright at 16 coupled holonomic robots.
- Decoupled, receding-horizon planners scale better but restrict the solution space, so they miss long-term coordination and fall into deadlocks, livelocks and congestion.
- Sampling-based optimizers like MPPI lack informative search guidance in joint multi-robot spaces, while learned diffusion planners need platform-specific training and slow search-based conflict resolution.

## Method

- **D4ORM** iterative deformation optimization: each iteration runs a full ==diffusion-denoising== pass over a deformation to the current joint control sequence, unifying ==MPPI== (one step per iteration) and ==model-based diffusion== (one iteration).
- **D4ORM-D** ==failure-aware decoupling== first plans while ignoring inter-robot collisions, then repeatedly re-optimizes only the groups of colliding robots, easing ==Monte Carlo== gradient dilution as team size grows.
- **D4ORM-C2F** pairs offline coarse D4ORM-D guidance with short-horizon online D4ORM refinement for feedback control; **D-D4ORM** spreads sampling across robots, which exchange candidate solutions through a top-k reward update.

## Results

- In 2D holonomic tests D4ORM solves 16 robots in about **1.25 s**, roughly **2x** faster than MPPI, while IPOPT needs about **35 s** for 12 robots and fails at 16.
- D4ORM-D and D4ORM-C2F produce collision-free full-horizon plans for 64 robots within **2 s**, covering over 10^4 variables; D4ORM-D deconflicts 100 robots in about **30 s**.
- D4ORM-C2F replans online at **0.03 s** per control loop and stays robust under control noise where offline D4ORM-D fails; hardware runs cover ten quadrotors and six ground robots.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Starting each iteration's deformation from a fresh normal distribution avoids the distribution shift that stalls naively iterated diffusion, so repeated denoising keeps improving hard solutions.
> - Diffusion-style noise annealing supplies learning-free search guidance in very high-dimensional joint spaces, making GPU-parallel sampling a credible alternative to gradient solvers for coupled planning.
> - Monte Carlo gradients dilute as joint dimension grows, so even strong coupled samplers benefit from selectively decoupling only the conflicting subsets of a team.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A team of ten quadrotors must reach destinations in the same obstacle-filled space without colliding or violating their motion constraints. The plan has to coordinate the whole team, but the coupled planning problem becomes harder as more robots move together. In practical settings such as logistics and warehouse automation, teams must make progress with limited planning time.

The authors' approach tries many possible adjustments to the team's control plans in parallel. It then refines those adjustments repeatedly through denoising, instead of training a trajectory generator or relying on conventional numerical optimization. The paper reports that this method, D4ORM, can produce coordinated trajectories for real quadrotors without task-specific training.

Fig. 1(d): zero-shot deployment of deconflicted trajectories for ten multirotors in the presence of obstacles.

### 2. From noisy trials to useful changes

A *trajectory* is a time-ordered plan of robot states and controls. *Kinodynamic feasibility* means that the state sequence obeys the robot's own motion dynamics.

D4ORM builds on [[2407.01573|Pan et al.'s MBD]], a learning-free trajectory-optimization method that uses diffusion-style denoising. MBD was originally designed for single-robot planning. Each D4ORM step works as follows:

1. The planner samples candidate control sequences.
1. It rolls each sequence through the specified robot dynamics to obtain feasible candidate motions.
1. It scores the resulting team trajectories for goal progress and collision penalties.
1. It uses the reward-weighted batch of rollouts to estimate a Monte Carlo direction for refinement.

Many rollouts can be evaluated in parallel, including on a GPU. The method uses the dynamics and a reward function. It does not use a trained diffusion network or analytical gradients.

The distinctive step is what gets denoised. D4ORM does not generate a whole new team plan from scratch. It denoises an incremental change to the current candidate trajectory, called a *deformation*, applies the refined deformation, and repeats. Each iteration restarts the noise schedule, which gives the planner another opportunity to explore. In the authors' sense, the planner is "anytime": it can return a solution when stopped and can continue refining while time remains.

### 3. Two decisive comparisons

**Against iterated MBD.** One plausible alternative, i-MBD, reuses MBD by repeatedly optimizing the trajectory from its last result. The authors explain that this creates a distribution shift relative to MBD's assumed Gaussian initialization. Deformation-based D4ORM preserves the intended initialization at each attempt.

Across 50 runs, i-MBD did not reliably improve on more difficult deconfliction instances when given more iterations. D4ORM's success rate improved over iterations. This evidence favors the deformation-based design. Because the update rules differ, it does not prove that distribution shift is the only cause.

**Against MPPI.** The test used 16 robots modeled as 2D holonomic double integrators, with no obstacles. "Success" meant that the whole team had trajectories that obey robot dynamics, avoid collisions, and reach goals. D4ORM and MPPI used the same sampling setup, with 1,024 trajectory samples per step, over 50 runs.

D4ORM found a feasible solution in an average of 1.25 seconds and succeeded on every run under a 3-second deadline. The authors describe this runtime as a near 2× improvement over MPPI.

Reward is a separate measure from success. With sufficiently long runs, both methods can reach similar reward values.

Fig. 3: D4ORM (green), i-MBD (red), and MPPI (purple) across five tasks. The top row shows runtime versus number of robots (8–16). The middle row shows success rate versus planning time for 16 robots, and the bottom row shows reward versus total optimization steps. All methods use 1,024 samples per step; 50 runs. D4ORM generally reaches successful solutions sooner than MPPI, while i-MBD often plateaus at lower success in the harder cases.

### 4. Larger teams through variants

The paper also develops variants for larger teams. These results apply to the variants, not to vanilla D4ORM. In the 2D holonomic random-goal setting, D4ORM-D and D4ORM-C2F generated full-horizon, collision-free solutions for 64 robots within 2 seconds. D4ORM-D also resolved conflicts for 100 simulated robots in about 30 seconds.

### 5. Contribution and boundary

The paper establishes deformation-based denoising as a reusable, learning-free planning mechanism. The authors organize it in different ways for scaling, feedback, or distributed computation. The measured work spans simulated differential-drive and holonomic systems and a ten-quadrotor zero-shot deployment. It does not establish performance in maze-like spaces. Vanilla D4ORM remains a local collision-avoidance planner, and complex non-convex robot shapes can make collision checking a bottleneck.
