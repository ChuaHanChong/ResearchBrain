# Diffusion Trajectory-guided Policy for Long-horizon Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The Diffusion Trajectory-guided Policy (DTP) provides explicit 2D trajectory guidance to enhance robot imitation learning for long-horizon manipulation. This approach improves generalization and data efficiency, achieving a 33.9% increase in average sequence length on the CALVIN benchmark compared to GR-1 and doubling the success rate in some real-world tasks over the second-best method.

## Problem

- Compounding errors accumulate over extended action sequences in long-horizon robot tasks, leading to task failures.
- Limited availability of high-quality robot demonstration data hinders generalization, especially in out-of-distribution scenarios.
- A gap exists in effectively translating high-level vision-language instructions into precise robot actions, particularly without intuitive guidance.

## Method

- A two-stage **Diffusion Trajectory-guided Policy (DTP)** framework is introduced, combining a ==Diffusion Trajectory Model (DTM)== with a ==Vision-Language Action (VLA)== policy.
- The DTM learns to generate task-relevant ==2D-particle trajectories== of the end-effector from vision and language inputs using a ==causal diffusion decoding== structure.
- These generated 2D trajectories serve as explicit high-level guidance, conditioned into a ==causal Transformer-based VLA policy== alongside vision, language, and proprioception.

## Results

- DTP improved average sequence length by **33.9%** on the CALVIN benchmark (seen environments) compared to GR-1 and showed superior generalization in unseen environments (Task 5 success rate of **0.497**).
- It demonstrated enhanced data efficiency, outperforming baselines with only **10%** of training data, achieving an average sequence length of **2.55** versus GR-1's **2.00**.
- In real robot experiments, DTP achieved an aggregate success rate of **0.84** across individual tasks, **doubling** the success rate of the second-best method in complex tasks like OpenSideDrawer, and showed an average sequence length of **4.6** in long-horizon tasks compared to GR1's **2.0**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit 2D trajectory guidance, analogous to human intuition for high-level visualization, effectively bridges the gap between vision-language inputs and robot actions, mitigating compounding errors.
> - Generating coarse, task-relevant 2D trajectories using a diffusion model is more data-efficient and computationally less intensive than generating full videos or goal images.
> - The modular, two-stage DTP framework allows for integration as a plug-in with existing Transformer-based VLA models, enhancing their performance without extensive architectural modifications.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot imitation learning (IL) has demonstrated capabilities in acquiring diverse manipulation behaviors from expert demonstrations. However, the broader landscape of robotic manipulation faces challenges, particularly in long-horizon tasks, due to the scarcity of high-quality demonstration data and limitations in generalization. Expert demonstrations are often limited in coverage, failing to encompass all possible robot poses and environmental variations, which leads to compounding errors that accumulate over extended trajectories and cause task failures.

Recent advancements in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, such as RT-1, RT-2, RoboFlamingo, RT-X, and Diffusion Policy, have significantly pushed the boundaries of robot control by mapping multimodal inputs (vision, language) to robot actions, often leveraging large-scale internet datasets or extensive robot demonstration data. These models frequently utilize Transformer architectures to process complex instructions.

To mitigate compounding errors in long-horizon tasks, several approaches have emerged. Some methods, like Susie, generate goal images or future videos, often requiring pretraining on large datasets. Others, such as RT-Trajectory, use coarse trajectory sketches, while RT-H decomposes complex instructions into hierarchical commands. While these strategies aim to guide the policy and reduce error accumulation, they often rely on manually provided trajectories or goal images, which can limit flexibility in diverse environments or demand substantial computational resources during inference for video generation. Diffusion models have also been applied to generate future imagery or directly predict robot actions (e.g., Diffusion Policy, Octo).

This work positions itself within this landscape by addressing the challenge of compounding errors and data scarcity in long-horizon tasks through a novel diffusion-based paradigm. Unlike methods that generate full videos or goal images, this paper proposes generating *task-relevant 2D trajectories* from vision-language inputs. This approach draws an analogy to human intuition, where high-level visualizations (imagined trajectories) guide actions, aiming to provide a more intuitive and adaptable form of guidance for robot policies. The Diffusion Trajectory-guided Policy (DTP) aims to bridge the feature gap between vision-language inputs and action spaces by explicitly generating and leveraging these 2D trajectories to enhance data efficiency, generalization, and overall performance in long-horizon robot manipulation, particularly in out-of-distribution scenarios and with limited data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust policy that enables robots to effectively execute long-horizon manipulation tasks by accurately interpreting vision and language inputs. This overarching goal is driven by several motivations and aims to address specific challenges within the field of imitation learning (IL) for robotics:

1. **Mitigate Compounding Errors in Long-horizon Tasks:** A core challenge in imitation learning, especially for tasks involving extended sequences of actions, is the accumulation of small deviations from the expert trajectory. These compounding errors can cascade, leading to significant task failures. The paper is motivated by the need to develop a mechanism that provides continuous, high-level guidance to the robot policy, thereby reducing error propagation throughout a long task sequence.
2. **Improve Generalization with Limited and Scarce Data:** Robot data collection is inherently costly and time-consuming, resulting in limited demonstration datasets compared to other machine learning domains. This scarcity hinders the generalization capabilities of IL methods, particularly when encountering out-of-distribution scenarios or novel environments. A key motivation is to enhance the data efficiency of imitation learning, enabling policies to learn and generalize effectively from smaller, more constrained datasets.
3. **Bridge the Feature Gap between Vision-Language and Action Spaces:** The authors observe that humans intuitively use high-level visualizations, such as imagined trajectories, to guide their actions and adapt to environmental changes. They propose that instructing a robot with language should similarly allow it to "envision" a trajectory to guide its future actions based on current observations. This motivates the development of a paradigm that can generate task-relevant, high-level guidance (2D trajectories) directly from vision and language inputs, thereby creating a more direct and intuitive link to the robot's action space.
4. **Develop a Flexible and Adaptable Framework:** The researchers aim for a system that can be easily integrated with existing Transformer-based VLA models. This motivates a two-stage design where a trajectory generation module can operate independently to condition a subsequent policy learning stage, serving as a plug-in to enhance performance without requiring significant architectural overhaul of the base policy.
5. **Enhance Imitation Policy Training Efficiency:** By leveraging robot video data to pretrain a generative vision-language diffusion model for trajectory generation, the work seeks to make fuller use of available data, thereby improving the efficiency of the subsequent imitation policy training.

In summary, the key objective is to address the limitations of current imitation learning in long-horizon tasks, specifically compounding errors and poor generalization with limited data, by introducing a novel diffusion-based approach that generates and uses 2D trajectories as explicit guidance for the robot's manipulation policy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Diffusion Trajectory-guided Policy (DTP) framework is designed as a two-stage process to address long-horizon robot manipulation tasks. It leverages a diffusion model to generate 2D trajectories, which then guide the learning of the robot's manipulation policy.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The problem is defined as learning a language-conditioned policy $\pi_\theta$ that takes language instruction $l$, observation $o_t$, robot states $s_t$, and a diffusion trajectory $p_{t:T}$ to output a robot action $a_t$. The dataset consists of expert trajectories $\tau$, each comprising a language instruction and sequences of observations, robot states, and actions. The diffusion trajectory $p_{t:T}$ specifically predicts the future movement of the end-effector from time $t$ to task completion at time $T$.

**B. Framework Overview**
DTP operates in two distinct stages:

1. **Diffusion Trajectory Model (DTM) Learning Stage:** In this initial stage, the framework focuses on generating 2D-particle trajectories. This model takes language instructions ($l$) and initial visual observations ($o_t$) as input and outputs a sequence of future 2D-particle trajectories ($p_{t:T}$) that outline the essential motion trends for task completion, as observed from a static camera.
2. **Vision-Language Action Policy Learning Stage:** The generated or labeled 2D-particle trajectories from the first stage are then utilized to guide the learning of the robot's manipulation policy. The policy is based on a causal Transformer architecture, integrating vision, language, proprioception, and the learned diffusion trajectory.

The backbone network for DTP is a causal Transformer, which is adapted to handle diverse modalities. It integrates a frozen CLIP model as the language encoder and a frozen MAE model as the vision encoder for processing inputs. Vision tokens are further processed by a perceiver resampler to reduce their dimensionality. Robot states in world coordinates are also incorporated as input.

**C. Diffusion Trajectory Model (DTM)**

1. **Data Preparation:**
- **Inputs:** Observations ($o_t$) and language instruction ($l$).
- **Outputs (Ground Truth):** The future 2D-particle trajectory ($p_{t:T}$) of the end-effector gripper required to complete the task. This trajectory is obtained by mapping the 3D world coordinates ($x_w, y_w, z_w$) of the end-effector to 2D pixel-level positions ($x_c, y_c$) using the camera's intrinsic and extrinsic parameters in a static camera frame. This process aims for high precision and convenience in acquiring the ground truth sequence $p_{t:T}$.
- **Data Format:** The DTM is trained on datasets structured as $\{l, o_t, p_{t:T}\}$.

2. **Training Objective:**
- The DTM utilizes a causal diffusion decoding structure, inspired by Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs), to generate the 2D-particle trajectories.
- The generation process starts by sampling a Gaussian noise vector. A learned denoising network $\epsilon_\theta$ iteratively predicts and removes noise over K steps, transforming a noised trajectory ($p^k_{t:T}$) into a denoised one ($p^{k-1}_{t:T}$) conditioned on the observation ($o_t$) and language instruction ($l$).
- The training loss for DTM is calculated as the Mean Square Error (MSE) between the predicted noise and the actual Gaussian noise sampled for each step. This objective ensures that the generated diffusion 2D-particle trajectories are accurate by systematically reducing noise during the generation process.

**D. Diffusion Trajectory-guided Policy (DTP)**

1. **Policy Input:**
- The DTP is based on the GR-1 model, with the diffusion trajectory $p_{t:T}$ introduced as an additional input.
- For training the policy, the *labeled* 2D-particle trajectories from the dataset are used directly as the diffusion trajectory input, rather than the inference results from the DTM. This maintains precision during training and conserves computational resources.
- To manage the potential computational intensity due to a high number of tokens from the 2D-particle trajectories, a "diffusion trajectory resampler module" is designed (inspired by the perceiver resampler) to reduce the number of trajectory tokens.
- Other inputs include vision (static and wrist camera images processed by MAE and perceiver resampler), language (processed by CLIP), and proprioception states.

2. **Diffusion Trajectory-guided Policy Training:**
- The policy employs a causal Transformer architecture. During training, future particle trajectory tokens are generated *prior* to action tokens. This sequential generation ensures that the particle trajectory tokens effectively guide the formation of action tokens, optimizing action prediction in a contextually relevant manner.
- The framework also retains the video prediction output, maintaining consistency with the GR-1 baseline for comparative ablation studies.
- The optimal DTP objective function is a combination of three losses: $L_{DTP} = L_{trajectory} + L_{action} + L_{video}$. $L_{trajectory}$ supervises the diffusion trajectory resampler, $L_{action}$ supervises action token generation, and $L_{video}$ supervises video prediction.
- For ablation studies, the GR-1 baseline is considered in two configurations: one using only its structural components without pretraining, and another leveraging large-scale video pretraining (Ego4D dataset) to evaluate the effectiveness and compatibility of DTP.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted extensive experiments in both simulated and real-world environments to evaluate the Diffusion Trajectory-guided Policy (DTP).

**A. CALVIN Benchmark (Simulation)**

The CALVIN benchmark, comprising four distinct environments (A, B, C, D) and 34 long-horizon manipulation tasks, was used for evaluation. DTP was trained to predict relative xyz positions, Euler angles for arm movements, and binary gripper actions.

1. **Comparisons with State-of-the-Art Methods:**
- **Results in Seen Environments (D→D Setting):** DTP demonstrated superior performance over all baselines (HULC, GR-1, MT-ACT, HULC++) in long-horizon tasks. Specifically, it increased the Task 5 success rate from 0.400 (HULC++) to 0.509 and the average sequence length from 3.30 to 3.55. Compared to GR-1, DTP showed a 33.9% increase in average sequence length, indicating improved performance with increasing task complexity.
- **Results in Unseen Environments (ABC→D Setting):** DTP exhibited strong generalization capabilities. It achieved the highest Task 5 success rate of 0.497 and an average task completion length of 3.43, outperforming methods like RoboFlamingo, GR-1, and even 3D Diffuser Actor, despite not utilizing depth modality. This suggests DTP's effectiveness in guiding policy learning in novel environmental configurations.
- **Data Efficiency (10% ABCD→D Setting):** When trained with only 10% of the dataset, DTP still outperformed baselines. It achieved an average sequence length of 2.55, compared to GR-1's 2.00, demonstrating its ability to acquire skills more efficiently with limited demonstrations by leveraging diffusion-based trajectories.

**B. Ablation Studies**

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of diffusion trajectories to policy learning, using GR-1 as the base architecture, both with and without video pretraining.

1. **Diffusion Trajectory Policy from Scratch (No Pretraining):** Without any external pretraining, the inclusion of the diffusion trajectory method significantly enhanced performance. In the ABC→D setting, the average task completion length increased by 23.4%, and the success rate for Task 5, a key indicator of long-horizon success, rose by 56.6%.
2. **Diffusion Trajectory Policy with Video Pretraining:** When integrated with a GR-1 model pretrained on the Ego4D dataset, DTP consistently boosted baseline performance to state-of-the-art levels. Success rates increased across tasks, from a 4.2% improvement in the first task to a 23.9% increase in the fifth task in the ABC→D setting, validating DTP's effectiveness and compatibility.
3. **Diffusion Trajectory Model Scaling Law:** The research observed that increasing the training data for the Diffusion Trajectory Model (DTM) improved point accuracy, which in turn enhanced DTP's performance. This suggests that while robot demonstration data is expensive, DTM data, which requires only coarse trajectory sketches on RGB images, is easier to annotate and can effectively boost policy learning.

**C. Real Robot Experiment**

Experiments were conducted using a Franka Emika Panda robot with three Intel RealSense D435i cameras and a Robotiq gripper. A dataset of 1,512 demonstrations was collected.

1. **Individual Tasks:** DTP achieved an aggregate success rate of 0.84 across five individual tasks (Pick Bread, Pick Strawberry, Open Trash, Close Side Drawer, Open Side Drawer\*), outperforming ACT (0.52), BAKU (0.24), and GR1 (0.48). Notably, DTP demonstrated a success rate twice as high as the second-best method in the OpenSideDrawer task (starting from randomized initial arm poses) and superior guidance in the OpenTrash task, which requires precise alignment. An exception was the PickStrawberry task, where DTP underperformed ACT, attributed to DTP's 224x224 image resolution compared to ACT's 480x640, impacting performance on small target objects.
2. **Long-horizon Tasks:** DTP was evaluated on longer, rearranged sequences of subtasks (e.g., A-B-C-A-C) to test robustness against compounding errors and unseen contexts. DTP achieved an average sequence length of 4.6 compared to GR1's 2.0. The baseline often failed at later subtasks due to accumulated deviations, such as altered object states or novel robot poses, which were unseen during training. DTP demonstrated greater robustness and task completion capabilities in these challenging scenarios.

**D. Visualization of Diffusion Trajectory Model:**

Visualizations of the generated 2D-particle trajectories in both the CALVIN environment and real-world scenarios indicated that the predicted trajectories closely aligned with the ground truth. Even with minor deviations, the generated trajectories remained consistent with the robot arm paths required by the language instructions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Diffusion Trajectory-guided Policy (DTP) framework presents a notable contribution to the field of robot imitation learning, particularly for long-horizon manipulation tasks. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Addressing Core Challenges in Imitation Learning:** DTP directly tackles two fundamental limitations of current imitation learning methods: compounding errors over extended task horizons and the challenge of generalizing from limited, costly robot demonstration data. By providing explicit 2D trajectory guidance, the framework reduces the accumulation of small deviations, which often leads to cascading failures in complex tasks.
2. **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The method demonstrates improved generalization capabilities, particularly in unseen environments, and increased robustness to varied initial robot poses and altered object states in long-horizon tasks. This suggests a greater capacity for deploying robots in more diverse and unstructured real-world settings without requiring extensive re-training for every minor variation.
3. **Improved Data Efficiency:** The results indicate that DTP can acquire manipulation skills more effectively with significantly fewer demonstrations. This is particularly impactful in robotics, where data collection is resource-intensive. The ability to achieve strong performance with 10% of the training data reduces the barrier to entry for developing complex robotic behaviors.
4. **Flexible and Modular Design:** The two-stage framework, with the Diffusion Trajectory Model (DTM) acting as an independent helper module, promotes modularity. This design allows for DTM to be integrated as a "plug-in" with various existing Transformer-based vision-language-action policies, potentially enhancing their performance without requiring extensive architectural modifications. This flexibility broadens its applicability across different base models.
5. **Computational Accessibility:** The reported training times on consumer-grade GPUs (e.g., 8\*3090s) suggest that the method is computationally feasible and accessible to a wider range of researchers and practitioners, contrasting with some large-scale models that demand specialized infrastructure.
6. **Guidance via Intuitive Representation:** The use of 2D trajectories as guidance aligns with human cognitive processes for planning and executing actions. This intuitive representation, directly derived from vision and language, offers a high-level form of supervision that is more interpretable than raw action sequences or latent embeddings.
7. **Future Research Directions:** The paper identifies clear avenues for future work, such as extending the framework to other state-of-the-art policies and improving the acquisition of trajectory labels. The mention of using tools like Track-Anything or hardware like Aria glasses for generating trajectory labels for large-scale video pretraining highlights a path towards leveraging readily available visual data to further scale trajectory guidance.

In conclusion, DTP represents a step towards developing more generalized, robust, and data-efficient robot manipulation policies for complex, multi-step tasks. Its ability to provide explicit trajectory guidance addresses key limitations of current imitation learning paradigms, with direct implications for advancing autonomous robotic systems in both industrial and research contexts.
