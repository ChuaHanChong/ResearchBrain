# Magnecko: Design and Control of a Quadrupedal Magnetic Climbing Robot

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zürich developed Magnecko, a quadrupedal magnetic climbing robot with an insect-inspired design and custom actuators. It demonstrates stable locomotion on diverse ferrous surfaces, including ground, vertical walls, and ceilings, reaching speeds of up to 0.15 m/s and carrying payloads up to 7.5 kg. A model predictive control framework enabled speed improvements of 2 to 4.5 times over a baseline controller and facilitated transitions across 90° concave corners.

## Problem

- Industrial inspection and maintenance tasks on complex structures are often hazardous, labor-intensive, and challenging for human workers.
- Existing wheeled climbing robots are limited to smooth, unobstructed surfaces, while many legged climbing robots lack the dexterity for complex three-dimensional environments, such as navigating concave corners.
- Developing robotic platforms with robust, energy-efficient adhesion and comprehensive motion control for varying orientations (ground, walls, ceilings) remains a challenge.

## Method

- Designed **Magnecko**, a quadrupedal robot featuring an insect-inspired leg configuration with three active degrees of freedom per leg, chosen for its large range of motion and high manipulability for climbing.
- Integrated custom, torque-controllable actuators with high power density and electro-permanent magnets (EPMs) in each foot, providing strong, energy-efficient adhesion and maintaining attachment during power loss.
- Developed a hierarchical control framework incorporating a kinematic-aware ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) for whole-body motion optimization on various orientations and a specialized controller for seamless 90° concave corner transitions.

## Results

- Magnecko achieved stable crawling speeds of up to **0.15** m/s on the ground, **0.058** m/s on horizontal overhangs, and **0.033** m/s on vertical walls, with the MPC improving speeds by a factor of 2 to **4.5** compared to a baseline IK controller.
- The robot successfully performed transitions over 90° concave corners (e.g., ground-to-wall, wall-to-ceiling) in approximately 18 seconds per transition, demonstrating its enhanced maneuverability.
- It demonstrated a payload capacity of up to **7.5** kg (**65%** of its body weight) on all tested surfaces, with the custom actuators and EPMs operating within safe margins, showcasing its potential for practical applications.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The insect-inspired leg design significantly enhances manipulability and range of motion, proving effective for complex climbing maneuvers like 90° concave corner transitions, which are difficult for many other designs.
> - Integrating system kinematics directly into the MPC formulation enables more comprehensive whole-body motion optimization, leading to substantial improvements in locomotion speed and stability across different orientations.
> - Custom actuators operating well within their torque and speed limits, combined with robust EPMs, enable Magnecko to carry significant payloads (up to 65% of its body weight) while maintaining stable and controlled locomotion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of climbing robots addresses the demand for automated inspection and maintenance in industrial environments, such as ship hulls, storage tanks, and bridges. These tasks are frequently labor-intensive and hazardous for human workers. While wheeled climbing robots exist, their mobility is often restricted to continuous, unobstructed surfaces, limiting their utility in complex structures. Legged robots offer increased versatility for navigating diverse obstacles.

Existing legged climbing robots employ various adhesion methods tailored to their operational contexts. Suction cups and gecko-inspired adhesives are utilized for smooth surfaces, while spine grippers are employed for rough terrains like rocks. For ferrous structures common in industrial settings, magnetic adhesion is a prevalent choice, implemented through permanent magnets, electromagnets, or electro-permanent magnets (EPMs). The Magnecko system utilizes EPMs, a method also adopted by other magnetic quadrupeds such as MARVEL and Magneto. EPMs are noted for their energy efficiency, as they only consume power during magnetic state transitions, and their ability to maintain adhesion during power loss.

Prior magnetic quadrupedal robots have demonstrated climbing capabilities on ferrous surfaces. Magneto utilized an admittance controller for reliable magnet engagement but was limited to slow, static locomotion. MARVEL achieved dynamic locomotion at higher speeds through a model predictive controller (MPC) for optimizing contact forces and a separate PD controller for swing trajectories. MARVEL employed a mammal-inspired joint configuration, which is often suitable for high-speed locomotion.

The current work distinguishes itself by integrating kinematics into an MPC formulation to control whole-body motion, an extension compared to MARVEL's approach which primarily optimized contact forces. Furthermore, Magnecko adopts an insect-inspired joint configuration. This design choice aims to provide a higher range of motion (RoM) and manipulability, enabling complex maneuvers, in contrast to mammal-inspired designs often prioritized for speed. This design philosophy positions Magnecko as a research platform specifically for exploring legged climbing in complex three-dimensional environments, including the negotiation of concave corners.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce Magnecko as a quadrupedal climbing robot, designed specifically as a research platform for advancing legged climbing locomotion.

The motivation for developing Magnecko stems from several factors:
- **Addressing industrial inspection challenges:** Industrial structures require regular inspection for safety issues, a task that is often labor-intensive and hazardous for human workers. Climbing robots can mitigate these risks and improve efficiency.
- **Overcoming limitations of existing robots:** Current wheeled climbing robots struggle with complex, obstructed surfaces. Legged robots offer greater versatility but existing legged climbers often lack the dexterity for highly complex 3D environments.
- **Enhancing maneuverability in complex terrains:** A key objective was to design a platform capable of handling challenging environments, such as overcoming concave 90° corners. This led to the selection of an insect-style joint configuration, chosen for its inherent high manipulability and large range of motion, which is considered more efficient for climbing applications and allows the robot to lower its body closer to the surface for increased stability. This contrasts with mammalian configurations that often prioritize speed but may offer less versatility for complex climbing maneuvers.
- **Robust and energy-efficient adhesion:** The decision to use electro-permanent magnets (EPMs) was motivated by their fast, strong, and reliable adhesion on ferrous surfaces, their energy efficiency (as they only consume power during state changes), and their ability to maintain adhesion even during power loss, thereby reducing operational risk.
- **Advanced control for comprehensive motion:** The development of a model predictive controller (MPC) was a significant objective. This MPC was designed to optimize the motion of the entire body by explicitly including system kinematics in its formulation, allowing for integrated control of crawling on various orientations including ground, horizontal overhangs, and vertical walls.
- **Payload capacity for practical applications:** The platform was designed with custom actuators and EPMs powerful enough to support additional payloads. This enables the integration of various inspection modules, additional sensor packages, or other tools, expanding the robot's potential utility beyond basic locomotion.
- **Foundation for future research:** Magnecko is intended to serve as a versatile foundation for future research into dynamic climbing gaits and locomotion in increasingly complex three-dimensional environments, leveraging its torque-controllable actuation and substantial range of motion.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The development of Magnecko involved several interconnected components, encompassing mechanical design, actuator development, adhesion mechanism, electrical system, and a comprehensive control architecture.

**Robot Platform Design:**
Magnecko is a quadrupedal robot with three active degrees of freedom (DOF) per leg, totaling 12 actuated DOFs. Its structural components are primarily constructed from lightweight aluminum alloys, resulting in a total mass of 11.3 kg, including the battery. The robot has a compact form factor, with dimensions of approximately 22 cm (Hip Yaw), 18 cm (Hip Pitch), and 25 cm (Knee). The leg topology is based on an insect-inspired joint configuration, which offers a large range of motion (290° for Hip Yaw, 245° for Hip Pitch, 225° for Knee) and is intended to provide higher stability and manipulability for climbing tasks compared to more common mammalian configurations. This symmetrical design also eliminates a preferred locomotion direction.

**Actuator Design:**
Each leg is equipped with three identical, serially linked custom actuators. These actuators are integrated directly into the joints to mitigate backlash. They comprise a custom single-stage compound planetary gearbox with an 18:1 transmission ratio, driven by a high-torque-density Maxon DT 50 M motor. This transmission ratio aims to provide a balance between the robot's efficiency and the high speeds required for dynamic locomotion, while maintaining sufficient transparency for estimating joint torques from motor current measurements, thus providing proprioceptive feedback on foot reaction forces without external force sensors. Each actuator delivers a peak torque of 40 Nm and a maximum speed of 100 rpm.

**Magnetic Foot Design:**
Each foot incorporates an electro-permanent magnet (EPM) for adhesion to ferrous surfaces. The EPMs are based on the OpenGrab EPM v3, augmented with custom electronics featuring a new flyback converter and increased capacitance to boost discharge current and reduce switching times (0.18 s for turning on, 0.35 s for turning off). The pulsing pattern was also adapted to accelerate magnetization. The EPMs achieve a maximum adhesion force of 380 N on a flat S235JR steel surface. To enhance friction and lateral holding force, a rubber ring is pressed against the surface by preloaded springs, reducing the normal holding force to 280 N while providing a lateral holding force of 160 N. A passive 3 DOF ankle joint, composed of a universal joint and an angular contact ball bearing, connects the foot to the leg. This joint allows the foot to tilt up to ±45° and rotate up to ±90°, facilitating surface contact without restricting leg movement. Springs ensure the foot returns to a neutral position during leg motion.

**Electrical System:**
The robot operates on a 24V power supply, which can be provided by an onboard 6-cell lithium-polymer battery offering a 45-minute runtime, or via a tether. A power distribution board manages power delivery to actuators and includes DC/DC converters for additional voltage levels. Onboard computational power is provided by an Intel NUC11TNBv7 with an Intel Core i7-1185G7 processor. Sensor integration includes a Vectornav VN100 Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) and an Intel Realsense D435 front-facing depth camera. EzMotion MMP545400-75-E2-1 motor controllers, located both within the body and in the upper legs, are used to control the actuators and estimate joint torques from phase currents.

**Control Framework:**
The control framework is implemented in C++ using ROS2 and utilizes ros2_control for hardware abstraction, allowing seamless transitions between simulation and physical hardware. The control architecture operates as follows:
1. **Operator Commands:** An operator provides a target state and selects a predefined gait pattern.
2. **Contact Schedule:** Based on the gait pattern, a contact schedule is generated and continuously updated to correct for early or late touchdowns.
3. **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** An MPC, implemented using the ocs2 toolbox and based on Grandia et al., calculates an optimized motion plan at approximately 130 Hz. It solves a constrained optimization problem to minimize a cost term over a time horizon (T = 1.0 s, δt = 0.02 s). The state vector includes base position, linear and angular velocity, and joint positions, allowing the MPC to directly generate swing trajectories. Simplified single rigid body dynamics (SRBD) are used, with rotational dynamics formulated using singularity-free unit quaternions. The cost function penalizes deviations from target states and inputs. Constraints include zero velocity for stance end-effectors, normal swing trajectories, friction cone constraints for ground reaction forces (GRFs), a minimum normal force (F_min = 20 N) during magnet magnetization, and limits on joint positions, velocities, and torques.
4. **State Estimation:** Each MPC iteration is initialized with the estimated state from the open-source state estimator pronto, operating at 400 Hz.
5. **Actuator Control:** A PID controller tracks the MPC-generated target joint positions and velocities. Optimized GRFs are mapped to joint torques and applied as a feed-forward term.
6. **Magnet Switching:** Commands to switch the EPM state are sent with a fixed offset to the planned liftoff/touchdown times to account for the magnet switching duration.

**Transition Control:**
To enable locomotion over complex terrains, a specific controller was developed for transitioning between 90° concave corners. This controller utilizes point cloud data from the front-facing depth camera to detect upcoming surfaces by fitting a plane to a central patch of points. A heuristic motion plan is then generated using polynomial trajectories to pre-selected footholds, ensuring a defined distance between front and hind legs. A differential inverse kinematics (IK) controller tracks this motion plan.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted on flat steel surfaces to evaluate locomotion on the ground, vertical walls, and ceilings. The performance of the MPC was compared against a baseline differential inverse kinematics (IK) controller.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented the following main findings regarding the Magnecko robot's performance:

**Locomotion Capabilities:**
Magnecko successfully demonstrated stable crawling locomotion on various ferrous surfaces, including flat ground, vertical walls, and horizontal overhangs (ceilings). During locomotion, the control strategy ensured that a minimum of three feet maintained full adhesion at all times. This involved successive magnetization and demagnetization of the magnets, resulting in a full contact period of 0.6 seconds between steps when climbing. On the ground, a shorter contact period of 0.2 seconds was found to be sufficient.

**Speed Performance:**
The robot achieved maximum crawling speeds of 0.15 m/s on the ground, 0.058 m/s on a horizontal overhang, and 0.033 m/s on vertical walls. A comparison with a baseline differential inverse kinematics (IK) controller indicated that the implemented Model Predictive Controller (MPC) significantly enhanced speed, achieving improvements by a factor of 2 to 4.5 across different orientations. The lowest speed was recorded on the wall, attributed to foot orientation challenges during larger steps due to gravity.

**Adhesion System Performance:**
The electro-permanent magnets (EPMs) exhibited sufficient adhesion force margins. During ceiling climbing, the maximum observed normal pulling force was approximately 100 N, which is about 35% of the EPM's maximum normal holding force of 280 N. Experiments also indicated that the robot operated with a substantial margin against slipping, as tangential forces typically caused slipping before detachment due to tilting. However, a limitation was identified: the adhesion force decreased substantially on surfaces that were not perfectly flat or clean (e.g., due to cracks, dirt, or corrosion). The EPM switching durations (0.18 s for turn-on, 0.35 s for turn-off) were identified as a primary speed limitation.

**Actuator Performance and Payload Capacity:**
The custom-designed actuators operated well within their specified limits during all locomotion experiments. Maximum observed joint torques were around 16 Nm, significantly below the actuators' 40 Nm peak torque capacity. Similarly, actuator speeds reached up to 40 rpm, which is less than half of their 100 rpm maximum speed. The hip pitch actuators experienced the highest loads, averaging 5.1 Nm on the ceiling, yet remaining below continuous torque limits as indicated by stable motor winding temperatures below 40 °C.
Due to these performance margins, Magnecko demonstrated a substantial payload capacity. It successfully carried additional payloads of up to 7.5 kg (approximately 65% of its own weight) on the ground, walls, and ceiling, with the actuators still operating comfortably within their limits.

**Transition Capabilities:**
Magnecko successfully performed transitions between 90° concave corners. This included transitions from ground-to-wall, wall-to-ceiling, ceiling-to-wall, and wall-to-ground. The large range of motion provided by the insect-style joint configuration facilitated these maneuvers, with a typical transition requiring six steps and approximately 18 seconds to complete.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Magnecko robot represents a contribution to the field of legged climbing robotics, primarily by establishing a dedicated research platform with specific design attributes.

**Advancement of Legged Climbing Research:** Magnecko's design, particularly its insect-inspired joint configuration and high manipulability, provides a platform for investigating complex climbing maneuvers in three-dimensional environments. The successful demonstration of overcoming 90° concave corners indicates progress towards navigating geometries that challenge many existing climbing robots.

**Validation of Hardware Design Principles:** The research validates the effectiveness of the custom actuators, which provide a balance of torque, speed, and transparency for proprioceptive feedback. The performance margins observed in joint torques and velocities suggest that the hardware is capable of supporting more dynamic gaits and faster locomotion than currently demonstrated with crawling. The implementation of electro-permanent magnets is confirmed as a viable adhesion method for ferrous surfaces, offering energy efficiency and robust attachment.

**Advancements in Control for Complex Locomotion:** The model predictive control (MPC) framework, which integrates kinematics for whole-body motion planning, demonstrates improved performance in terms of speed and stability compared to a baseline inverse kinematics controller. This approach is relevant for developing robust and adaptable control strategies for robots operating in varied orientations and under external disturbances.

**Potential for Practical Applications:** The demonstrated ability of Magnecko to carry substantial payloads (up to 65% of its own weight) is significant for practical applications. This capacity enables the integration of various modules, such as inspection sensors (e.g., ultrasonic, thermal cameras), non-destructive testing equipment, or even light manipulation tools, extending the robot's utility beyond mere locomotion to active maintenance or detailed inspection tasks in industrial settings.

**Implications for Future Robotic Development:** The identified limitations and planned future work provide clear directions for the broader field. The need for improved adhesion robustness on imperfect surfaces and faster magnet switching times highlights areas for continued material and mechatronic research. Furthermore, the emphasis on integrating advanced perception capabilities for foothold selection and localization underscores the importance of sensor fusion and environmental intelligence for deploying climbing robots in unstructured, real-world environments. The platform is designed to facilitate future exploration of dynamic climbing gaits, which could lead to more agile and efficient climbing robots.
