# Diffusion Policies as an Expressive Policy Class for Offline Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Diffusion Q-learning (Diffusion-QL) introduces an approach for offline reinforcement learning by using conditional diffusion models to represent policies. This method addresses limitations in policy expressiveness and regularization, leading to state-of-the-art performance across various D4RL benchmarks including Gym-MuJoCo, AntMaze, Adroit, and Kitchen.

## Problem

- Existing offline reinforcement learning policies often use limited expressive policy classes (e.g., simple Gaussians) that fail to accurately capture complex, multi-modal behavior distributions in real-world datasets.
- Prior policy regularization techniques frequently involve a two-step explicit behavior cloning process, which can introduce compounding approximation errors.
- Learning from static datasets in offline RL is prone to "out-of-distribution" actions, leading to inaccurate Q-value estimates and suboptimal policies.

## Method

- **Diffusion-QL** represents the policy as a ==conditional diffusion model==, which is capable of capturing complex, multi-modal action distributions.
- Policy learning combines a ==behavior-cloning loss== (inherently from the diffusion model training) with a ==Q-learning objective==.
- ==Q-value gradients== are backpropagated directly through the diffusion model's generation process, guiding policy improvement while maintaining data distribution fidelity.

## Results

- In a 2D bandit task, BC-Diffusion accurately captures multi-modal behavior policies, unlike prior methods which exhibit mode-covering.
- Diffusion-QL achieves **state-of-the-art** performance on most **D4RL** benchmarks, including **Gym-MuJoCo**, **AntMaze**, **Adroit**, and **Kitchen** tasks.
- Ablation studies demonstrate that the diffusion model's expressiveness and its integration with Q-learning guidance are critical for the method's robust policy improvement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conditional diffusion models provide a highly expressive policy class capable of accurately modeling complex, multi-modal behavior distributions in offline datasets.
> - The dual-objective training of Diffusion-QL effectively combines implicit behavior cloning (via diffusion model training) with direct Q-value maximization for policy improvement.
> - Integrating strong generative modeling capabilities with direct reinforcement learning guidance leads to policies that can optimally exploit data while adhering to its distribution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Offline Reinforcement Learning (RL), also known as batch RL, is a critical paradigm in modern artificial intelligence. It aims to train optimal policies entirely from pre-collected static datasets, circumventing the need for costly, risky, or time-consuming online interactions with a real environment. This makes offline RL highly attractive for sensitive real-world applications such as autonomous driving, robotics, healthcare (e.g., patient treatment planning), and recommender systems, where exploration with an untrained or suboptimal policy is undesirable.

The inherent challenge in offline RL lies in the potential for "out-of-distribution (OOD)" actions. When a policy is trained solely on historical data, it may attempt to evaluate actions not present in the dataset, leading to inaccurate Q-value estimates (often overestimation) and consequently, poor or unsafe policies. Existing offline RL methods generally fall into four main categories:
1. **Policy Regularization:** Methods that constrain the learned policy to stay close to the data-generating behavior policy.
2. **Value Function Constraints:** Approaches that implicitly or explicitly constrain the learned Q-function to assign low values to OOD actions.
3. **Model-based Methods:** Techniques that learn a model of the environment dynamics from the offline data and perform pessimistic planning within this learned model.
4. **Sequence Prediction Methods:** Recent approaches that frame offline RL as a sequence modeling problem, often using large transformer models to predict trajectories or actions conditioned on desired returns.

This paper directly addresses the first category: policy regularization. However, the authors critically identify two significant limitations of prior policy-regularized methods. First, many existing approaches employ policy classes with *limited expressiveness*, typically simple Gaussian distributions with diagonal covariance matrices. This is problematic because real-world offline datasets are often collected by a mixture of diverse policies (e.g., different human demonstrators, various online agents), leading to complex, multi-modal, or skewed behavior policies. Fitting a simple Gaussian to such data often results in "mode-covering" behavior, where the policy places high density in regions of the action space that are actually low-density in the true data distribution. This misrepresentation can severely hinder effective policy improvement. Second, the *regularization methods themselves* (e.g., KL divergence, Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)) often require an intermediate step of explicit behavior cloning, where a separate model is trained to approximate the behavior policy. This two-step process can compound approximation errors, as the cloned behavior policy may not accurately capture the true data distribution due to the aforementioned expressiveness limitations, thus misguiding the subsequent policy improvement step.

By proposing a novel policy representation using diffusion models, this work positions itself as a significant advancement within the policy-regularization landscape. Diffusion models are a recent class of highly expressive deep generative models known for their ability to accurately capture complex, multi-modal data distributions. The paper differentiates its use of diffusion models from other concurrent works in RL, such as Diffuser, by focusing on modeling the action space conditioned on states in a model-free manner, rather than generating entire trajectories or relying on model-based planning. This distinct application of diffusion models directly tackles the expressiveness and regularization challenges, aiming to achieve more precise and effective policy learning in offline settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to overcome the inherent limitations of existing policy-regularized offline reinforcement learning algorithms, specifically concerning the expressiveness of their policy classes and the efficacy of their regularization techniques. The authors aim to develop a new offline RL algorithm, Diffusion Q-learning (Diffusion-QL), that leverages the power of diffusion models to achieve state-of-the-art performance on challenging benchmarks.

The core motivations driving this work are:

1. **Addressing Limited Policy Expressiveness:** Traditional offline RL methods often represent policies using simple unimodal distributions (e.g., diagonal Gaussians). However, real-world offline datasets are frequently aggregated from diverse sources (e.g., multiple human demonstrators, varied legacy policies), leading to complex, multi-modal, and non-Gaussian behavior distributions. These simple policy classes fail to accurately capture such rich data, leading to misrepresentations that negatively impact policy learning. The authors are motivated to find a policy class that can faithfully represent these intricate behavior patterns.

2. **Improving Policy Regularization Effectiveness:** Many existing policy regularization methods involve a two-step process: first, cloning the behavior policy, and then regularizing the learned policy towards this clone. This introduces an additional source of approximation error, as the cloned policy itself might be inaccurate due to expressiveness limitations. Furthermore, regularization techniques like KL divergence require explicit density estimation, and MMD needs multiple action samples, both of which can be computationally intensive or prone to errors. The motivation is to integrate behavior cloning and policy improvement more seamlessly and effectively, ideally using a method that naturally performs strong distribution matching.

3. **Leveraging Diffusion Models' Capabilities:** The recent success of diffusion models in generative tasks, particularly their ability to model complex, high-dimensional, and multi-modal data distributions, provides a strong motivation. The authors hypothesize that representing the policy as a conditional diffusion model could directly address the expressiveness issue. Moreover, the iterative refinement process of diffusion models, combined with their strong distribution-matching properties, offers a unique opportunity to inject Q-value guidance during policy generation, leading to more optimal actions while staying close to the data manifold.

In summary, the key objective is to propose and validate Diffusion-QL, an algorithm that utilizes a conditional diffusion model as an expressive policy class, integrating Q-learning guidance directly into its training process to overcome the "out-of-distribution" problem and learn high-performing policies from static datasets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Diffusion Q-learning (Diffusion-QL) introduces a novel approach to offline RL by representing the policy as a conditional diffusion model. The methodology centers on a dual-objective training process that combines precise behavior cloning with effective policy improvement.

**Policy Representation with a Conditional Diffusion Model:**
The core idea is to represent the RL policy, $\pi_\theta(a|s)$, as the reverse process of a conditional diffusion model, $p_\theta(a_{0:N}|s)$. Here, $a_0$ is the action ultimately used for RL evaluation, and $a_{1:N}$ are latent variables. The reverse diffusion chain, $p_\theta(a_{i-1}|a_i, s)$, is parameterized as a Gaussian distribution whose mean is constructed based on a noise prediction model, $\epsilon_\theta(a_i, s, i)$. This $\epsilon_\theta$ model, implemented as a 3-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with Mish activations and 256 hidden units, takes as input the current noisy action, the state, and a sinusoidal positional embedding of the diffusion timestep. The policy generates an action by first sampling noise $a_N \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and then iteratively denoising it through $N$ steps to obtain $a_0$. The authors use a specific noise schedule from Song et al. (2021) for the forward diffusion process.

**Dual-Objective Training for Policy Learning:**
The policy learning objective in Diffusion-QL is a linear combination of two critical terms:
1. **Behavior-Cloning (BC) Term ($L_d(\theta)$):** This term is the simplified objective for training the conditional diffusion model, $E_{i \sim U, \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I), (s,a) \sim D} [||\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_i}a + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_i}\epsilon, s, i)||^2]$. This loss inherently encourages the diffusion model to sample actions from the same distribution as the training dataset, effectively performing behavior cloning. Unlike traditional methods, this distribution matching is implicit and sampling-based, not requiring explicit density values or multiple action samples from the current policy, making it robust to the multi-modal nature of offline datasets. This term serves as a powerful and flexible policy regularization.
2. **Policy Improvement (Q-learning) Term ($L_q(\theta)$):** This term, $-\alpha \cdot E_{s \sim D, a_0 \sim \pi_\theta} [Q_\phi(s, a_0)]$, aims to maximize the action-value function. Crucially, the action $a_0$ is generated by the diffusion policy (Equation 1 in the paper), allowing the gradient of the Q-value function to backpropagate through the entire reverse diffusion chain. This directly guides the policy towards higher-value actions. The scaling factor $\alpha$ is normalized by the expected absolute Q-value to balance the two loss terms, making the training more stable across different datasets.

The total policy objective is $L(\theta) = L_d(\theta) + L_q(\theta)$.

**Q-value Function Learning:**
The action-value (Q) function is learned using a conventional double Q-learning approach, similar to DDPG/TD3-style algorithms. Two Q-networks ($Q_{\phi_1}, Q_{\phi_2}$) and their corresponding target networks ($Q_{\phi'_1}, Q_{\phi'_2}$) are maintained. The Q-networks are trained by minimizing the Bellman error using standard techniques, where the target Q-value is computed by sampling actions from a target policy network ($\pi_{\theta'}$).

**Algorithm Flow (Algorithm 1):**
The training alternates between updating the Q-networks and updating the policy network.
- **Q-network Update:** A mini-batch is sampled from the offline dataset. For each transition $(s_t, a_t, r_t, s_{t+1})$, a next action $a_{0,t+1}$ is sampled from the target policy $\pi_{\theta'}(a_{t+1}|s_{t+1})$. The Q-networks are then updated to minimize the squared difference between their predicted Q-values and the Bellman target.
- **Policy Network Update:** For each state $s_t$ in the mini-batch, an action $a_{0,t}$ is sampled from the current policy $\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)$. The policy network $\pi_\theta$ is then updated by minimizing the combined loss $L(\theta)$.
- **Target Network Updates:** Soft updates are applied to the target Q-networks and target policy network.

**Computational Considerations:**
While the iterative nature of diffusion model sampling can be a computational bottleneck, the authors found that a relatively small number of diffusion steps ($N=5$) yields good performance while keeping training time comparable to existing methods like CQL. The number of diffusion steps $N$ serves as a trade-off between policy expressiveness and computational cost.

In essence, Diffusion-QL proposes a method where the policy's capacity to model complex action distributions is greatly enhanced by using a diffusion model, and this expressive policy is simultaneously refined through direct Q-value optimization, moving towards optimality while respecting the data distribution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents compelling findings through a simple 2D bandit example and extensive evaluation on the D4RL benchmark, demonstrating the superiority of Diffusion-QL.

**A. 2D Bandit Example (Policy Regularization Illustration):**
The authors construct a 2D bandit task with a multimodal behavior policy, consisting of an equal mixture of four Gaussian distributions. This setup effectively highlights the limitations of prior methods and the strengths of Diffusion-QL.

- **Behavior Cloning (First Row, Figure 1):**
 - **Prior Methods (BC-MLE, BC-CVAE, BC-MMD):** These methods struggle significantly. BC-MLE (a simple Gaussian policy) exhibits severe mode-covering, placing high density in low-density regions between the true modes. BC-CVAE, despite being an implicit model, also shows mode-covering and sometimes fails to capture all modes. BC-MMD, using a Tanh-Gaussian policy, aligns densities near boundaries but lacks the expressiveness for the true distribution. This clearly shows their inadequacy in accurately cloning complex behavior policies.
 - **BC-Diffusion:** In stark contrast, the diffusion model successfully captures *all four density modes* of the true behavior policy, demonstrating its superior expressiveness and distribution-matching capabilities.
- **Policy Improvement (Second Row, Figure 1):**
 - When a reward function is introduced (with one mode being optimal), Diffusion-QL correctly identifies and converges its policy to the *optimal bottom corner* (highest reward mode).
 - Prior methods, due to their inaccurate behavior cloning and policy regularization, are constrained to suboptimal or incorrect exploration regions. TD3+BC fails to converge to the optimal mode, BCQ concentrates actions on sub-optimal diagonal corners, and BEAR-MMD shows random policy placement. This illustrates how poor behavior cloning can fundamentally misguide policy improvement.
- **Effect of Diffusion Steps (N) (Figure 2):**
 - Increasing N (number of diffusion steps) for BC-Diffusion leads to a more accurate recovery of the underlying data distribution, confirming N's role in expressiveness.
 - For Diffusion-QL, a moderately small N (e.g., N=5) is sufficient for good performance due to the combined loss. Larger N provides stronger regularization, keeping the policy within valid data regions for exploration.

**B. D4RL Benchmark Performance:**
Diffusion-QL is evaluated against various state-of-the-art baselines across four diverse D4RL domains: Gym-MuJoCo locomotion, AntMaze (sparse rewards, challenging exploration), Adroit (human demonstrations, narrow data distribution), and Kitchen (long-term value optimization).

- **Overall Superiority (Table 1):** Diffusion-QL achieves state-of-the-art performance on the *majority* of D4RL tasks.
- **Gym Domain:** Outperforms existing methods, particularly on 'medium' and 'medium-replay' tasks, which contain exploratory and less concentrated behavior policies. The expressiveness of the diffusion model allows it to better capture these complex behavior policies, leading to more effective policy improvement.
- **AntMaze Domain:** Shows clear superiority, especially on the harder 'medium' and 'large' tasks which involve sparse rewards and require stitching sub-optimal trajectories. This highlights the stability and effectiveness of Diffusion-QL's Q-learning guidance in challenging exploration settings where BC-based methods typically fail.
- **Adroit and Kitchen Domains:** Achieves strong results, demonstrating that its expressive policy and precise regularization are beneficial where strong constraints (Adroit's narrow human data) or long-term planning (Kitchen) are crucial.
- **Hyperparameter N:** Empirical study on D4RL confirms N=5 as a good balance between performance and computational cost, making the method practical.
- **Offline Model Selection (Appendix D):** The authors show that using the $L_d$ loss as a lagging indicator allows for effective offline model selection, picking models with good performance without online interaction.

**C. Ablation Study (Table 2):**
The ablation study on Gym tasks quantitatively validates the contribution of Diffusion-QL's two main components: the expressive diffusion policy and the Q-learning guidance.
- **BC-Diffusion vs. BC-CVAE:** BC-Diffusion significantly outperforms BC-CVAE in pure behavior cloning, confirming the superior expressive power of diffusion models for complex data distributions.
- **BCQ-Diffusion vs. BCQ-CVAE:** Even when integrated into the BCQ framework (which uses an explicit deviation limit), BCQ-Diffusion performs better, showing the benefit of the diffusion policy's superior behavior cloning.
- **CVAE-QL vs. Diffusion-QL:** CVAE-QL (CVAE-based policy with free Q-learning guidance) fails to perform well, whereas Diffusion-QL excels. This critical finding indicates that merely having a generative model is not enough; the strong expressiveness of the diffusion model *coupled with* its inherent regularization (from $L_d$) and additive Q-learning guidance ($L_q$) is essential for robust policy improvement.

**D. Gaussian Mixture Policy (Appendix H):**
A Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) policy (TD3+BC-GM) was tested as an alternative expressive policy. While it could capture modes in the toy bandit, it failed to converge to the optimal mode in Q-learning and performed poorly on D4RL tasks. This suggests that GMMs are hard to fit well in high dimensions and may struggle with capturing dependencies between action dimensions, further reinforcing the advantages of diffusion models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Diffusion-QL represents a significant advancement in the field of offline reinforcement learning, addressing several long-standing challenges and opening new avenues for research.

**Significance:**

1. **Overcoming Policy Expressiveness Limitations:** The most salient contribution is demonstrating that representing policies as conditional diffusion models effectively resolves the limited expressiveness issue prevalent in prior offline RL methods. This enables the policy to accurately model complex, multi-modal, and non-Gaussian behavior distributions found in real-world datasets, which is crucial for safety and performance in offline settings.
2. **Precise and Integrated Policy Regularization:** Diffusion-QL elegantly integrates behavior cloning and policy improvement within a single, unified objective function. The diffusion model's inherent ability to perform strong distribution matching serves as a precise and flexible policy regularization mechanism, avoiding the compounded errors of multi-step regularization approaches. This directly leads to policies that can optimally exploit the observed data without drifting into unreliable out-of-distribution regions.
3. **State-of-the-Art Performance:** The consistent outperformance of Diffusion-QL across a diverse range of challenging D4RL benchmarks (Gym, AntMaze, Adroit, Kitchen) validates its practical efficacy and robustness. This indicates its potential for broad applicability in various offline RL scenarios, including those with sparse rewards, narrow data distributions, or long-horizon tasks.
4. **Novel Application of Diffusion Models in RL:** This work pioneers the direct application of diffusion models as policy classes for action generation in a model-free offline RL context. While other works explore diffusion for trajectory generation, Diffusion-QL focuses on generating single actions, highlighting the versatility of diffusion models beyond their typical generative image/audio applications.

**Potential Impact:**

1. **Enabling Real-World Offline RL:** By offering a more robust and effective solution for learning from static data, Diffusion-QL could accelerate the deployment of RL in high-stakes real-world applications where online interaction is impractical or dangerous (e.g., healthcare, autonomous systems, complex robotics).
2. **Bridging Generative Models and RL:** This research strengthens the connection between deep generative models and reinforcement learning. It suggests that advanced generative architectures like diffusion models can play a pivotal role not just in data synthesis but also in shaping highly expressive and robust policies.
3. **Inspiration for Future Research:** The success of Diffusion-QL encourages further exploration of other advanced generative models (e.g., score-based models, normalizing flows) as policy representations in RL. It also prompts deeper investigation into the interplay between distribution matching and policy optimization.

**Limitations and Future Work (as identified by the authors):**

1. **Inference Speed:** The primary limitation is the computational cost at inference time. Generating an action from a diffusion policy requires iteratively computing the $\epsilon_\theta$ network $N$ times (where N is the number of diffusion steps). While the authors achieved good performance with a small $N=5$, this can still be 4-5 times slower than traditional one-step feedforward policies, potentially hindering deployment in real-time scenarios requiring fast responses.
2. **Future Directions for Speed Improvement:** The authors suggest leveraging recent advancements in accelerating diffusion model sampling (e.g., DPM-Solver, progressive distillation, Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models).
3. **Policy Distillation:** A promising future direction is to distill the complex, iterative diffusion policy into a simpler, faster feedforward policy after training. This would retain the high performance learned by the diffusion model while enabling quick inference for deployment.

Overall, Diffusion-QL marks a significant step forward in offline RL, demonstrating the power of integrating highly expressive generative models with reinforcement learning principles to achieve superior performance and reliability in data-driven decision-making.
