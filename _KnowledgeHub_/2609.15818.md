# Atria Dawn: The Dawn of Agentic Superintelligence

> [!summary] Summary
> Atria Dawn Preview is a 744-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts agentic language model for research and engineering, trained by a Verifiable Experience Pipeline that grounds every task in a live execution environment and keeps only trajectories confirmed by external checks such as tests, metrics, or file state. It leads on five of 16 agentic benchmarks, and its own development doubles as a human-AI collaboration study.

## Problem

- Benchmark scores measure task completion but cannot answer who identifies worthwhile problems, selects among proposed methods, or decides what to pursue next in real projects.
- Prior automated-research systems show agents can propose and revise solutions, yet the division of responsibility between humans and agents in actual model development remains unmeasured.
- Gains on task performance do not strengthen the capabilities that drive research, so a model can improve at trained tasks without improving at developing its successor.

## Method

- **Verifiable Experience Pipeline**: every training task runs in a real environment, with outcomes checked by executable tests, experiment metrics, file or application state, geometric checks, or source support.
- Trajectory curation drops incomplete, contradictory, duplicate, or behaviorally invalid rollouts, keeping only links between task, trajectory, artifacts, and verification evidence; failure analysis then seeds new tasks and environment refinements.
- Development case study instrument: 769 task records from 56 participants plus agent logs, coding each decision by who proposed versus who selected goals, methods, and acceptance criteria.

## Results

- Across 16 benchmarks it leads on five, including AutomationBench **53.8**, BFCL v4 **77.0**, DeepSearchQA **96.0**, BrowseComp **92.5**, and CyberGym **86.5**, but trails on SWE-bench Pro **59.6**.
- Participants rated **151** of **455** completed AI-assisted tasks, or **33.2%**, infeasible without AI; daily median agent actions per human prompt rose from **11.0** to **28.5** over four weeks.
- AI proposed **64.6%** of method or parameter options while humans made **85.5%** of final choices; **76.0%** of **588** recorded difficulties advanced through human context or diagnosis, not takeover.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Grounding every training trajectory in an executable environment and retaining only externally verified outcomes is a transferable recipe across tests, metrics, file state, geometry, and sources.
> - Delegation volume is not autonomy: a rising agent-action-per-prompt ratio means each human judgment propagates through more agent work, not that humans decide less.
> - Agents generate options while humans still choose; self-improvement stalls because agents keep lessons per-session whereas researchers carry diagnoses across tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of large language models, tool-using agents, autonomous software engineering, AI-assisted scientific discovery, and research on recursive self-improvement. Earlier language-model systems primarily generated text or code in response to isolated prompts. More recent agentic systems interact with external environments, execute programs, search the web, operate files and applications, run experiments, and revise their actions based on feedback.

The paper describes this development as a progression through three stages. In the first, AI systems are research objects developed and evaluated entirely by humans. In the second, they function as task-level runners that complete clearly specified subtasks within human-designed workflows. In the third, they become project-level coworkers capable of planning and iterating over bounded research objectives while humans retain responsibility for goals and major decisions.

This framing connects Atria Dawn to work on autonomous research systems such as AlphaEvolve, AI co-scientists, automated algorithm discovery, software-engineering agents, and environments for training long-horizon agents. It also addresses a central issue in recursive self-improvement: performing more experiments is not equivalent to identifying valuable research directions or improving the process by which future improvements are discovered.

The paper makes two related contributions. First, it presents **Atria Dawn Preview**, a large agentic language model and its performance across 16 benchmarks. Second, it studies the development of Atria Dawn itself as an empirical case of human–AI collaboration. This second component distinguishes the paper from evaluations that report only aggregate benchmark scores.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Model-development objective

The primary technical objective is to develop an agentic foundation model for scientific research, engineering, digital work, and cybersecurity. The model is intended to perform sustained workflows rather than isolated question answering. Such workflows may include searching for information, preparing data, writing and executing code, inspecting logs, diagnosing failures, generating reports, and validating outputs in an external environment.

Atria Dawn is built on a **744-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts foundation model**. The paper does not present a full architecture specification or detailed optimization recipe, but it emphasizes a training process intended to connect model behavior to verifiable results.

#### 2.2. Human–AI collaboration objective

The second objective is to examine how responsibilities change when AI agents participate in the development of later AI systems. The authors ask several questions:

- Which party proposes research methods and goals?
- Who makes final decisions?
- Who diagnoses failures and determines revisions?
- How much work becomes feasible because of AI assistance?
- Does increasing agent activity correspond to increasing autonomy?

These questions are motivated by the observation that conventional task-level evaluations do not reveal how research projects are actually conducted. A model may generate code or execute an experiment successfully while humans continue to determine what should be studied, which hypotheses are credible, and whether a result justifies further investment.

#### 2.3. Recursive self-improvement

The paper treats sustained recursive self-improvement as a more demanding capability than autonomous task execution. A system would need to generate diverse research directions, estimate their value before results are available, design informative experiments, interpret uncertain evidence, and convert experience from previous iterations into durable improvements in its own research ability.

The authors argue that current systems demonstrate parts of this process but do not yet establish a self-sustaining improvement loop.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Verifiable Experience Pipeline

Atria Dawn is trained using a **Verifiable Experience Pipeline**. Each training task is connected to an executable environment in which the agent observes state, calls tools, produces intermediate artifacts, and receives feedback. The final outcome is checked using external signals rather than relying solely on the model’s textual explanation.

Examples of verification signals include executable tests, experiment metrics, file or application state, geometric consistency, source evidence, and human-defined criteria. The pipeline retains associations among the original task, the agent trajectory, generated artifacts, and verification evidence.

The authors describe several stages:

1. **Rollout:** The agent interacts with an environment, selects tools, inspects outputs, and revises actions.
2. **Verification:** The result is checked through domain-specific tests or measurements.
3. **Trajectory curation:** Incomplete, contradictory, duplicate, or behaviorally invalid examples are removed.
4. **Failure analysis:** Recurring problems, such as poor tool selection, incomplete verification, or failed recovery, are converted into new tasks and quality checks.
5. **Workflow recording:** Longer tasks include records of launching jobs, inspecting logs, updating plans, and recovering from errors.

The stated aim is to teach reusable behaviors for state inspection, feedback interpretation, error recovery, and multi-step execution.

#### 3.2. Benchmark evaluation

The model was evaluated on 16 benchmarks covering general agentic tasks, search and research, workspace productivity, professional work, software engineering, terminal use, machine-learning engineering, and cybersecurity. Comparisons were made with several other contemporary language models, including DeepSeek V4 Pro, KIMI K3, Qwen 3.8 Max, GLM 5.3, GPT 5.6 sol, and Claude Opus 5.

The protocols used different execution frameworks, time limits, tools, judges, and numbers of runs. For example, some evaluations used OpenHands or Claude Code, while others used in-house search harnesses or AgentCompass. Several benchmarks relied on model-based judging, and some used multiple independent runs.

#### 3.3. Development-process case study

The collaboration analysis used **769 task records from 56 participants**, combined with agent logs. The study examined AI use, estimated effort without AI, proposal and decision attribution, interventions after difficulties, and revisions to AI-generated outputs.

The analysis included:

- 739 tasks with a clear response about AI use.
- 455 completed AI-assisted tasks with usable estimates of feasibility without AI.
- 567 decisions concerning goals, methods, parameters, or acceptance criteria.
- 588 tasks with recorded difficulties.
- 627 tasks with a known disposition for the main AI output.

The authors also measured the ratio of logged agent actions to human prompts over time for a cohort of 22 participants.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Benchmark performance

Atria Dawn achieved the highest reported score among the listed comparison models on five benchmarks:

- **AutomationBench:** 53.8.
- **BFCL v4:** 77.0.
- **CyberGym:** 86.5.
- **DeepSearchQA:** 96.0.
- **BrowseComp:** 92.5.

It ranked near the top on several additional benchmarks. It scored 66.4 on SkillsBench, 68.2 on Workspace-Bench-Lite, and 65.0 on Workspace-Bench. It obtained 81.9 on WideSearch, 51.1 on DeepResearch Bench II, 41.2 on Tau3-Bench Banking, 1,583 on GDPval, and 50.3 on JobBench.

In coding-related tasks, its strongest relative result was CyberGym, where it exceeded the next listed result by 2.0 points. It scored 86.2 on MLE-bench Lite, 59.6 on SWE-bench Pro, and 78.3 on Terminal-Bench 2.1. However, it did not lead every benchmark. Other models scored higher on professional deliverables, terminal tasks, software engineering, and several workspace and research evaluations. The paper therefore supports a conclusion of broad competitiveness rather than uniform dominance.

#### 4.2. Demonstrated workflow capabilities

The selected cases illustrate sustained tool-mediated behavior, although they are demonstrations rather than estimates of average performance.

In a weather-forecasting task, the model processed more than 100 GB of data, implemented a vision-transformer-based network with over 400 million parameters, and trained it for 45,000 steps to model 69 meteorological variables. The paper shows a generated interface but does not report forecast accuracy for this case.

In an optimization task involving Gated Delta Network decoding, the agent responded to measured performance changes. It identified launch overhead, changed from Triton to native CUDA, and revised configurations after regressions. Seven representative batch sizes produced a 1.46 ratio between summed baseline and candidate latencies. However, only 52 of 54 formal workloads had passed at the time of reporting, so the ratio was not presented as a final benchmark result.

Other cases involved building a MiniOS system, generating CAD assemblies, preparing reports and presentations, analyzing scientific literature, and diagnosing and repairing a vulnerability in an isolated cybersecurity environment. The evidence generally consisted of executable traces, artifacts, tests, or re-tests.

#### 4.3. Feasibility and delegation

Among 455 completed AI-assisted tasks, participants judged **151 tasks, or 33.2%, infeasible without AI** when scope, quality requirements, and other resources were held constant. These tasks involved 27 of the 56 participants. The result suggests that AI assistance sometimes expanded the set of tasks that participants could undertake, rather than merely reducing the time required for work that was already feasible.

The ratio of agent actions to human prompts increased over the observed period. The daily median rose from **11.0 to 28.5** between August 7 and September 4, 2026. The increasing ratio indicates that each human prompt was associated with a longer chain of agent activity. The widening interquartile range shows that this pattern varied across participants.

#### 4.4. Decision-making and revision

The division of responsibility was asymmetric. AI proposed methods or parameters in **55.4%** of the relevant decisions, but humans made the final choice in **85.5%** of method and parameter decisions. Humans also made the final choice in **93.4%** of goal or scope decisions and **81.9%** of acceptance-criteria decisions.

For tasks rated infeasible without AI, humans selected the final goal in **95.4%** of cases. These findings indicate that heavy dependence on AI for execution did not correspond to AI control over research direction.

When difficulties occurred, **76.0%** of 588 tasks moved forward through human intervention, while agents recovered independently in 23.0% and 1.0% remained unresolved. Human assistance usually involved adding context or clarifying requirements, or diagnosing the problem and changing the method. Direct editing and takeover were uncommon.

Of 627 tasks with known output dispositions, 56.5% involved substantive revisions. In 75.4% of those cases, the agent performed the revisions after receiving human feedback; humans directly edited the output in 19.2%. Thus, human input generally took the form of judgment and steering rather than manual replacement of the agent’s work.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance lies in treating agentic AI performance and the organization of AI-assisted research as related but distinct questions. It provides evidence that capable agents can execute long workflows, generate candidate methods, respond to feedback, and produce externally checkable artifacts. At the same time, the development records show that human researchers continued to determine goals, select methods, interpret failures, and decide whether work should continue.

For scientific and engineering organizations, this suggests a shift in labor allocation. Human effort may move away from routine implementation and toward problem selection, experimental design, evidence assessment, and oversight. AI could also make previously infeasible projects accessible to small teams or individual researchers.

The findings also identify risks. Increasing numbers of agent actions per human prompt can make oversight more difficult because human decisions govern longer and less inspectable chains of activity. Shared model priors may lead multiple agents to explore similar approaches, creating scale without sufficient diversity. In addition, benchmark improvement may not measure whether a system has acquired the ability to generate valuable new research questions.

The paper therefore argues for stronger evaluation of research capability, explicit authority-allocation protocols, mechanisms for preserving diverse exploration, and methods that convert experience into durable model capabilities rather than merely storing retrievable records.

Several limitations qualify the conclusions. The model’s training details are described at a high level, the benchmark comparisons rely on heterogeneous harnesses and model-based judges, and selected demonstrations are not representative success-rate estimates. The collaboration study is observational and based partly on participant self-reports, with no independent control condition. Finally, the evidence does not establish recursive self-improvement or superintelligence. Its contribution is instead to document an intermediate stage in which AI agents increasingly perform research work while humans retain substantial responsibility for direction and accountability.
