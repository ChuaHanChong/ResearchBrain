# The Great March 100: 100 Detail-oriented Tasks for Evaluating Embodied AI Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> The Great March 100 (GM-100) benchmark provides a collection of 100 systematically designed, detail-oriented tasks for evaluating embodied AI agents, addressing limitations in current evaluation approaches. Experiments demonstrate that existing Vision-Language-Action models achieve low success rates on these challenging tasks, though a strong inverse correlation is observed between action prediction error and physical task completion.

## Problem

- Existing robot learning datasets suffer from a lack of task diversity, primarily focusing on a narrow range of common actions, leading to biased model training.
- Complex, multi-step, or rare 'long-tail' interactions crucial for versatile embodied agents are often underrepresented in current benchmarks.
- The absence of standardized evaluation tasks across different studies hinders objective comparison and assessment of progress in embodied AI.

## Method

- Developed a systematic task design framework leveraging insights from ==human-object interaction primitives== and 'how-level affordances', deliberately avoiding biases from perceived real-world utility.
- Utilized a large language model (==Qwen3==) to generate and refine 100 detail-oriented tasks, followed by LLM-based executability scoring and human expert filtering to ensure feasibility and diversity.
- Collected over 13,000 teleoperated trajectories for the ==GM-100== tasks on two distinct robotic platforms (Agilex Cobot Magic and Dobot Xtrainer) to provide a diverse dataset.

## Results

- The 100 GM-100 tasks are feasible for human operators but prove highly challenging for current VLA models, with the best-performing model (π0.5) achieving an average Success Rate of only **24.9%** on the Xtrainer platform.
- GM-100 effectively differentiates the capabilities of various VLA models, with π0.5 consistently outperforming Diffusion Policy and π0 across nearly all evaluated tasks.
- A strong inverse correlation was observed between normalized action prediction error (MSE) and Partial Success Rate (PSR), indicating that models with lower prediction errors achieve higher physical task completion rates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current robot learning benchmarks overemphasize common actions, resulting in models with significant biases and limited generalization capabilities.
> - A principled approach to task design, integrating human-object interaction knowledge and low-level manipulation understanding, is essential for creating truly challenging and comprehensive benchmarks.
> - High-precision action modeling is a prerequisite for robust physical interaction in complex tasks, as evidenced by a strong inverse correlation between action prediction error and real-world task success.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied AI and robot learning has witnessed an exponential surge in development, fueled by advancements in imitation learning and large-scale data collection. Recent years have seen the emergence of numerous datasets and methodologies, such as Open X-Embodiment, Agibot, RoboCOIN, BridgeData V2, and others, accumulating millions of trajectories across various robotic platforms. Concurrently, Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (e.g., RT-2, OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$, GR00T) have gained prominence, aiming to integrate perception, language understanding, and physical control into unified networks.

Despite these significant strides, the authors identify a critical gap in the existing research landscape: the lack of systematic and principled task design within these datasets and evaluation benchmarks. Current approaches often suffer from several limitations:
1. **Lack of Diversity:** Datasets tend to overemphasize a narrow range of common tasks and behaviors, frequently focusing on simple actions like "pick and hold" (as illustrated by the word cloud and verb frequency analysis in Figure 1). This leads to trained models exhibiting significant biases, limiting their generalization capabilities to more complex or novel real-world scenarios.
2. **Insufficient Coverage of Long-Tail Behaviors:** Complex, multi-step, or rare interactions, which are crucial for truly versatile embodied agents, are often underrepresented.
3. **Absence of Standardized Evaluation:** The fragmented nature of evaluation tasks across different studies makes fair and comprehensive comparison between novel methods challenging, hindering objective progress assessment in the field.

"The Great March 100" positions itself as a direct response to these shortcomings. Rather than merely contributing another large-scale dataset, it shifts the focus to the quality and diversity of task design. By introducing 100 *detail-oriented* tasks that span a wide spectrum of interactions, including long-tail behaviors, GM-100 aims to provide a much-needed standardized, diverse, and challenging benchmark. This work seeks to elevate the standard of evaluation, enabling researchers to accurately gauge the differentiated performance of advanced VLA models and ultimately push the boundaries of general-purpose robotic intelligence. It strives to initiate a "robot learning Olympics," a unified and rigorous competition ground for assessing embodied AI agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the Great March 100 (GM-100) is to address the systemic deficiencies in existing robot learning datasets and evaluation methodologies, which currently hinder the development of truly capable and generalizable embodied AI agents. The authors highlight that the prevailing focus on data scale over principled task design has led to biased models and an inability to comprehensively assess robotic capabilities.

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Systematic Task Design:** To move beyond subjective criteria and develop a systematic framework for designing robot tasks. This framework emphasizes physical common sense and "how-level affordances" (low-level manipulation knowledge) as the sole standards, deliberately avoiding human biases stemming from perceived real-world utility.
2. **Promote Diversity and Complexity:** To create a benchmark consisting of 100 detail-oriented tasks that significantly expand the coverage of interactions beyond common behaviors. This includes a strong emphasis on diverse, complex, and long-tail behaviors, which are crucial for evaluating the generalization abilities of robotic agents.
3. **Comprehensive Evaluation:** To provide a robust and challenging set of tasks that can effectively differentiate the performance of various robotic learning methods, particularly Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This aims to establish a more equitable and insightful evaluation standard for the field.
4. **Foundation for "Robot Learning Olympics":** To lay the initial groundwork for a long-term vision of a "robot learning Olympics," a standardized, ambitious, and globally recognized benchmark for embodied AI systems.
5. **Open-Sourcing and Community Engagement:** To release the task designs, collected data, and evaluation procedures to foster transparency, reproducibility, and collaborative development within the embodied AI research community.

In essence, GM-100 is motivated by the need for a more thoughtful, diverse, and standardized approach to robot task definition and evaluation, aiming to accelerate the progress towards general-purpose, human-like robotic intelligence.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for constructing GM-100 is highly structured, combining systematic analysis, linguistic models, and human expertise to generate a diverse and challenging set of tasks.

#### 3.1. Task Design and Generation

The process begins by analyzing existing robot tasks from prior works like Agibot and Open X-Embodiment. Duplicates are removed, and tasks are categorized by their semantic meanings. This initial analysis (Figure 1) reveals a clear bias towards common actions like "pick and hold," motivating the need for broader coverage.
The core of the GM-100 task design is based on **human action propriety**, aiming to create tasks that cover a wide range of interactions, including the "coupling of actions" and the representation of "long-tail, rare but important actions." The authors explicitly state they avoid designing tasks based on perceived real-world utility to mitigate human bias, instead relying on "physical common sense and low-level manipulation knowledge (how-level affordance)."

The task generation pipeline (Figure 2) consists of several steps:
1. **Human-Object Interaction Primitive Selection:** Insights from human-object interaction studies (HAKE, PaStaNet, OCL) are utilized to select representative interaction primitives, spanning both high- and low-frequency activities.
2. **LLM-based Generation (Qwen3):** A large language model, Qwen3, is employed. Carefully designed prompts, incorporating action primitives and objects, guide the model to:
 - Perform word sense disambiguation for selected action primitives.
 - Enumerate objects semantically and physically relevant to each primitive.
 - Synthesize concrete task instances and refine their textual descriptions for clarity.
3. **Hybrid Task Filtering:**
 - **LLM Executability Scoring:** Qwen3 is first used to automatically score the robot executability of the generated candidate tasks.
 - **Human Expert Selection:** Five human experts then conduct a final selection, serving as the "gold standard." This step ensures tasks are feasible with current hardware constraints and amenable to teleoperation-based data collection.
4. **Task Instantiation:** High-priority tasks are further refined with specific interaction details, object selection (e.g., from Taobao.com), and clear criteria for task completion, including definitions for partial success. Template videos of humans completing these tasks are recorded to guide data collection.
Ultimately, 100 such tasks are selected to form the first version of the GM-100 benchmark.

#### 3.2. Data Collection

A medium-sized dataset of **over 13,000 trajectories** is collected for the GM-100 tasks.
- **Hardware Platforms:** Two distinct robotic platforms are used:
 - **Agilex Cobot Magic:** A Mobile-Aloha-like robot with a forward-reaching arm structure and a head-mounted camera.
 - **Dobot Xtrainer:** An Aloha-like platform featuring an inward-folding arm structure and a top-down camera view.
 This dual-platform approach provides diversity in kinematic structures and camera perspectives.
- **Method:** Trajectories are collected via **teleoperation** by human operators.
- **Data Distribution:** For each task, 100 trajectories are collected with diverse initial conditions and designed perturbations for training. An additional 30 trajectories with similar distributions are collected to serve as aligned test cases for evaluation, ensuring consistency across models. All 100 tasks are collected on Cobot Magic, and 10 tasks are collected on Xtrainer for the initial release.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup and Evaluation

- **Baseline Models:** Several prominent robot learning models are evaluated: Diffusion Policy (DP), $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$, and GR00T. DP is trained from scratch, while VLA models like $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$ are fine-tuned on the collected 100 trajectories per task until convergence.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Success Rate (SR):** The standard metric, measuring the percentage of tasks successfully completed within a fixed number of attempts. Test cases are aligned using the 30 dedicated test trajectories.
 - **Partial Success Rate (PSR):** For complex, multi-step tasks, subtasks and subgoals are defined. PSR measures the percentage of these subtasks successfully achieved, offering a more granular assessment of performance, especially when overall SR is low.
 - **Action Prediction Error:** Mean Squared Error (MSE) and L1 loss are calculated between predicted and ground truth actions within a specific prediction window on test trajectories. This metric assesses the model's fidelity in replicating expert demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results from GM-100 clearly demonstrate its efficacy as a challenging and differentiating benchmark for embodied AI agents.

#### 4.1. Task Feasibility and Challenge

- The data collection process, yielding over 13,000 teleoperated trajectories across two robotic platforms, confirms that the 100 GM-100 tasks are **feasible to execute** by human operators.
- However, the performance of baseline models indicates that these tasks are **sufficiently challenging** for current VLA models. As shown in Table 1 (Real-world Performance on Xtrainer), average Success Rates (SR) are low: DP at 1.6%, $\pi_0$ at 4.4%, and $\pi_{0.5}$ at 24.9%. Even the best-performing model, $\pi_{0.5}$, achieves a modest SR, underscoring the difficulty presented by the diverse and detail-oriented nature of GM-100 tasks. The Partial Success Rate (PSR) provides a more nuanced view, with $\pi_{0.5}$ reaching an average PSR of 53.9% on Xtrainer, suggesting models can often complete parts of complex tasks even if full completion is rare. Figure 4 further visually confirms this challenging nature across tasks on the Cobot Magic platform.

#### 4.2. Differentiation of Model Performance

- GM-100 effectively differentiates the capabilities of various baseline models. On the Xtrainer platform (Table 1), $\pi_{0.5}$ consistently outperforms both DP and $\pi_0$ across almost all evaluated tasks, achieving significantly higher SR and PSR. For instance, in Task 0007, $\pi_{0.5}$ achieves a PSR of 90.0% and SR of 50.0%, while DP shows 6.2% PSR and 0.0% SR. This clear distinction highlights GM-100's ability to expose performance differences among leading embodied AI approaches.

#### 4.3. Correlation between Action Prediction Error and Physical Success

- Table 2 presents the action prediction errors (MSE and L1 loss), revealing that $\pi_{0.5}$ consistently exhibits lower errors compared to DP and $\pi_0$.
- Crucially, Figure 5 visualizes a strong inverse correlation between Normalized MSE (action prediction error) and Partial Success Rate (PSR). Models with lower action prediction errors (bars extending further to the left) tend to achieve higher physical success rates (bars extending further to the right).
- Specifically, $\pi_{0.5}$ minimizes prediction error while maximizing PSR across nearly all tasks, indicating that its architectural refinements and training objectives effectively capture the underlying distribution of successful trajectories. Conversely, Diffusion Policy (DP) generally shows larger action prediction deviations, which directly translate to diminished PSRs (mostly below 25%).
- This finding suggests that for the challenging tasks in GM-100, especially in the real-world Xtrainer environment, high-precision action modeling is a prerequisite for robust and successful physical interaction. The results validate that models which minimize training loss more effectively also yield superior performance in real-world deployment.

In summary, the GM-100 tasks are verified to be implementable and critically challenging for current methods, providing a strong benchmark for distinguishing the capabilities of embodied AI agents. The robust correlation between offline prediction accuracy and online physical success further validates the design principles and utility of the benchmark.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Great March 100 represents a significant contribution to the field of embodied AI and robot learning, with profound potential impacts on future research and development.

1. **Elevating Benchmarking Standards:** GM-100 addresses a critical gap in the existing research landscape by offering a systematically designed and diverse benchmark. Unlike prior efforts that often prioritize data scale but lack task diversity, GM-100 provides 100 "detail-oriented" tasks that cover a wide range of interactions and, importantly, long-tail behaviors. This shift from quantity-over-quality to principled, diverse task design will be instrumental in pushing the capabilities of robotic agents beyond rote execution of common actions.
2. **Driving Generalization and Robustness:** By forcing models to contend with complex, novel, and less-frequent interactions, GM-100 is poised to stimulate research into more generalizable, robust, and adaptable embodied AI systems. The demonstrated challenge of these tasks for current state-of-the-art VLA models highlights the significant room for improvement, thereby directing future innovations towards truly human-like manipulation and interaction.
3. **Facilitating Fair Comparisons:** The standardized nature of GM-100's tasks and evaluation metrics enables researchers to conduct more objective and equitable comparisons between different robot learning methods and VLA architectures. This will accelerate progress by clearly identifying superior approaches and focusing community efforts on common, well-defined challenges, akin to the impact of benchmarks like ImageNet for computer vision.
4. **Foundation for a "Robot Learning Olympics":** The paper explicitly frames GM-100 as the "first step towards a robot learning Olympics." This ambitious vision suggests a long-term roadmap for evolving and expanding the benchmark (GM-X series), fostering a competitive yet collaborative environment that drives the field forward with clear goals and metrics.
5. **Promoting Open Science and Community Collaboration:** The open-sourcing of task designs, collected data, and evaluation procedures (via RHOS.ai) is crucial. By acknowledging the difficulties of maintaining a perfectly "fair" centralized physical testing environment, the authors propose a community-driven paradigm. This encourages researchers to upload their own results and evidence, fostering transparency, reproducibility, and collective oversight. This approach can build trust and accelerate validation of new models and methods.
6. **Insights into Model Capabilities:** The strong inverse correlation between action prediction error and physical success observed in GM-100 provides valuable insights into the necessary characteristics of high-performing embodied agents. It underscores the importance of precise action modeling for robust physical interaction, guiding future architectural and training objective designs for VLA models.

In conclusion, GM-100 is not merely another dataset; it is a foundational initiative to redefine how embodied AI agents are evaluated. By setting a higher standard for task diversity and challenge, it promises to be a crucial catalyst in advancing the intelligence, adaptability, and real-world applicability of robotic systems.
