# Learning to Walk in Costume: Adversarial Motion Priors for Aesthetically Constrained Humanoids

> [!summary] Summary
> A research collaboration led by UCLA developed a locomotion system for the aesthetically-driven Cosmo humanoid robot, using Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) and a robust sim-to-real transfer pipeline. This enabled stable and natural walking on hardware despite the robot's disproportionately large head and physical movement constraints.

## Problem

- Entertainment humanoids often feature non-standard morphologies, disproportionate mass distributions, and restricted movement ranges due to aesthetic shells.
- The Cosmo robot specifically has a disproportionately large head (16% of total mass), leading to a high and shifted center of mass, making it inherently top-heavy and challenging to balance.
- Aesthetic design choices limit onboard vision and restrict the robot's joint ranges of motion, complicating control and requiring specialized solutions beyond conventional methods.

## Method

- ==Employed Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP)== to train a policy that imitates human motion capture data, promoting natural, human-like movement, with a discriminator distinguishing between robot-generated and reference motions.
- Utilized a comprehensive simulation environment (Isaac Sim for analysis, Isaac Gym for parallel training) to develop policies, integrating human and robot-specific motion references.
- Implemented a robust sim-to-real transfer pipeline using extensive domain randomization (physics, external perturbations, sensor noise) and a tailored PD controller for hardware deployment.

## Results

- Achieved stable standing, robust push-recovery (regaining stability within 2 seconds), and natural, human-like walking on the physical Cosmo robot, reproducing coordinated shoulder swings and subtle head oscillations.
- Successfully managed Cosmo's top-heavy mass distribution while keeping joint torques within safe operating limits (±20 Nm) during locomotion, demonstrating respect for hardware limitations.
- Ablation studies confirmed that specific reward components (e.g., stumble prevention) and motion references (e.g., standing pose) were essential for preventing hazardous motions and enabling smooth, safe locomotion.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - AMP can effectively adapt to and induce compensatory behaviors for eccentric robot morphologies, such as Cosmo's top-heavy design, while maintaining human-like motion styles.
> - Specialized safety rewards (e.g., foot stumble prevention) and diverse motion references (standing, model-based, expressive human-like gaits) are crucial for hardware protection and achieving both stability and expressiveness.
> - Cosmo's extreme mass distribution results in significantly higher Center of Mass (CoM) deviation (5x more than a biomechanically optimized robot), confirming the substantial inherent instability challenge.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are increasingly deployed in environments designed for human interaction, performing tasks ranging from basic locomotion to complex manipulation. The traditional approach to humanoid control has largely relied on model-based methods, which require precise kinematic and dynamic models and extensive manual tuning. While effective for well-defined tasks, these methods can be sensitive to deviations from their design assumptions and often struggle with highly dynamic or varied conditions.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as an alternative, demonstrating the ability to generate agile and dynamic behaviors, such as running and parkour, without explicit model programming. However, pure RL approaches typically necessitate complex reward shaping to achieve desired behaviors and often produce motions that lack natural, human-like qualities. Imitation learning offers a complementary strategy by leveraging human motion capture data to imbue robots with more natural and aesthetically pleasing movement patterns. A notable advancement in this area is Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), which integrates the realism of imitation learning with the adaptability of RL by using an adversarial training framework. AMP enables robots to learn physically plausible and stylized movements from reference motion data, often simplifying reward engineering by learning motion characteristics directly.

This work specifically addresses a developing niche within humanoid robotics: entertainment applications. Unlike humanoids designed primarily for industrial or research tasks, entertainment robots prioritize aesthetic appeal, often resulting in non-standard morphologies, disproportionate mass distributions, and constrained movement ranges due to protective or cosmetic shells. These design choices pose significant challenges for conventional and even many existing learning-based control methods, which typically assume more biomechanically optimized designs and unhindered sensing. This paper extends the application of AMP and sim-to-real transfer techniques to such a challenging platform, demonstrating their efficacy under conditions that are more demanding than those typically found in general humanoid locomotion research. It contributes to understanding how learning-based methods can enable functional performance while respecting aesthetic-driven design constraints in real-world entertainment robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and natural-looking locomotion system for Cosmo, a custom-built humanoid robot specifically designed for entertainment applications. The motivation for this work stems directly from the unique and significant challenges presented by Cosmo's aesthetic-driven design, which deviates considerably from the standard humanoid morphology typically addressed in robotics research.

Specifically, Cosmo embodies the following challenges:
- **Disproportionate Mass Distribution:** The robot possesses a disproportionately large head, constituting 16% of its total mass (4 kg out of 25 kg). This results in a significantly elevated and shifted center of mass, making the robot inherently top-heavy and challenging to balance, especially during dynamic locomotion.
- **Limited Sensing Capabilities:** Aesthetic shells and design choices restrict the integration of an onboard vision system, requiring the control policy to rely exclusively on proprioceptive feedback from motor chains and an onboard state estimator.
- **Restricted Movement Range:** Protective and aesthetic coverings, particularly on the feet and around joints, impose considerable physical constraints on the robot's range of motion, complicating the generation of effective and safe gaits.

Given these constraints, the conventional model-based control methods often require extensive, platform-specific tuning and may prove brittle or insufficient. Pure reinforcement learning approaches might generate physically plausible movements but often lack the natural, human-like qualities desired for entertainment applications. Therefore, the core motivation is to demonstrate that modern learning-based methods, specifically Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), can effectively overcome these aesthetic-driven design difficulties.

The key objectives are:
1. To develop a learning-based locomotion control system that can achieve stable standing and walking on a humanoid robot with an extreme and atypical mass distribution, a configuration rarely explored in existing literature.
2. To establish a robust sim-to-real transfer pipeline tailored for robots with aesthetic shell constraints, ensuring safe deployment on physical hardware while preserving both performance and preventing damage to valuable components. This includes developing tailored domain randomization techniques and specialized reward structures.
3. To empirically demonstrate that AMP-guided reinforcement learning can generate natural and stable walking behaviors even on robotic platforms with significant mechanical limitations and design choices that prioritize visual appeal over ease of control.

Ultimately, the motivation is to bridge the gap between aesthetic design requirements and functional locomotion capabilities in entertainment humanoids, offering a promising direction for future robots in interactive entertainment environments that must balance visual appeal with real-world dynamic performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a comprehensive methodology combining motion capture data, advanced reinforcement learning with adversarial priors, extensive simulation, and a tailored sim-to-real transfer pipeline to address the locomotion challenges of the Cosmo humanoid robot.

**A. Hardware Platform: Cosmo**
Cosmo is a custom-built humanoid robot with 28 degrees of freedom (DOFs): 10 in the legs, 8 in the arms, 2 in the head, and 8 in the hands. Its defining feature is a disproportionately large head, weighing 4 kg (16% of the robot's 25 kg total mass), which significantly elevates its center of mass. The robot relies solely on proprioceptive feedback. Westwood Robotics actuators (Panda Bear Plus for high-torque joints, Koala Bear Muscle Build for others) provide precise torque control. The design incorporates carbon fiber reinforcement for the head and spring steel flexures in the feet for compliance, balancing aesthetics with structural integrity within constrained volumes.

**B. Motion Retargeting**
To leverage human motion capture data, an essential step was to adapt these motions to Cosmo's unique kinematics. The CMU Mocap Dataset was utilized. This involved:
1. Building a custom animation rig in Blender that matched Cosmo's proportions.
2. Using the Rokoko plugin in Blender to retarget human motion data onto this custom rig. The process involved averaging limb movements to fit the robot’s kinematic structure.
3. Addressing potential issues where aesthetic shells, particularly on the feet, could clip due to the retargeting not accounting for their width.

**C. Imitation Learning with Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP)**
The locomotion problem was framed as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The agent learns a policy $\pi(a|s)$ to map observations $s$ to actions $a$, maximizing an expected discounted return.
The core of the learning approach is Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP):
1. **Discriminator Network:** A discriminator network $D_{\phi}(s)$ is trained to distinguish between states generated by the robot's policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and states from a reference human motion dataset $M$.
2. **Policy Reward:** The policy incorporates the discriminator's output as a reward term, $r_{AMP}(s_t) = \log D_{\phi}(s_t)$, encouraging the policy to generate motions that are visually similar to the reference data. The discriminator and policy are trained adversarially.
3. **Network Architecture:** The policy network uses 3 layers (512, 256, 128 units), while the critic and discriminator use 2 layers (256, 128 units), all with ELU activations.
4. **Observation Space:** The observation vector $s$ includes proprioceptive information: base linear velocity ($v_{base}$), angular velocity ($\omega_{base}$), normalized joint positions ($q - q_{default}$), joint velocities ($\dot{q}$), projected gravity orientation ($g_{proj}$), previous actions ($a_{prev}$), base height ($h_{base}$), and command signals ($c_{cmd}$). Base kinematics are estimated via an invariant extended Kalman filter. Command ranges for forward, lateral, and yaw velocities are defined (e.g., [−0.3, 0.9] m/s for forward velocity).
5. **Action Space:** The actions are target joint positions for all actuated DOFs, which are translated into torques by a higher-frequency PD controller.
6. **Reward Scheme:** The total reward function combines task-oriented terms with the AMP style reward, organized into three groups:
 - **Imitation Reward:** $- \log D_{\phi}(s)$ from the AMP discriminator.
 - **Motion Quality Rewards:** Penalizes large joint rate changes to ensure smooth movements.
 - **Safety Rewards:** Designed to protect the robot's aesthetic shells and optimize foot interactions. This includes foot stumble prevention and foot orientation rewards (e.g., keeping feet flat to the floor).
 - **Task Rewards:** Direct objectives for locomotion, such as matching desired linear and angular velocities.
 Reward coefficients are adjustable for curriculum learning. Simplified convex hulls are used for collision modeling during training.

**D. Simulation Suite**
A comprehensive simulation environment was established using NVIDIA's Isaac Sim for initial analysis and Isaac Gym for massively parallel training.
1. **Pose Stability Analysis (Isaac Sim):** The team performed static analysis by evaluating various standing poses. Stability was determined by whether the projected gravity vector remained within the support polygon (convex hull of foot-ground contact points) during joint sweeps. This analysis quantitatively compared Cosmo’s stability challenges against a biomechanically optimized robot (ARTEMIS), revealing a five-fold greater CoM deviation for Cosmo during equivalent motions.
2. **Push-Recovery/Balancing Policy:** An intermediate policy was developed for push recovery by training the robot to maintain a statically stable pose while subjected to random external forces. This stage was crucial for identifying sensitive platform parameters and efficient gain tuning.
3. **Walking Policy:** Diverse motion references were combined:
 - Human motion capture clips (e.g., "swaggy" walking, standing poses) for expressiveness.
 - Experimental motion data from a hierarchical quadratic programming-based inverse dynamics whole-body controller running on Cosmo, providing robot-specific dynamics information and continuous velocity ranges for safe, low-speed transitions. This hybrid approach created a natural curriculum, progressing from controlled, lower-speed movements to more expressive gaits.

**E. Sim-to-Real Transfer**
To bridge the reality gap between simulation and hardware:
1. **Low-Level Control:** A PD controller ($\tau = K_p(q_{target} - q) + K_d(\dot{q}_{target} - \dot{q})$) translates policy actions (target joint positions) into motor torques. A gain-tuning protocol focused on joint tracking, vibration, and torque constraints, with more aggressive tuning for torso-related joints to manage the head's mass.
2. **Domain Randomization (Table III):**
 - **Physics Randomization:** Varying contact friction coefficients, robot base mass perturbations, PD gain multipliers, and actuator lag.
 - **External Perturbations:** Applying periodic random external forces (linear and angular pushes) with tuned magnitudes and intervals.
 - **Calibrated Sensor Noise Injection:** To enhance policy robustness against real-world sensor inaccuracies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrated that the proposed learning-based system, incorporating AMP and a comprehensive sim-to-real pipeline, enabled stable standing and walking behaviors on the Cosmo humanoid robot, despite its challenging morphology.

**A. Pose Stability Analysis (Isaac Sim)**
Static stability analysis in Isaac Sim revealed Cosmo's inherent instability. Out of four tested standing poses, only two maintained stability throughout their joint range of motion. Quantitative comparison with the ARTEMIS humanoid showed that Cosmo's center of mass (CoM) deviated by 30 mm during joint movements in a stable pose, which is five times greater than ARTEMIS's 6 mm deviation for equivalent motions (Figure 6). This finding confirmed the significant challenge posed by Cosmo's top-heavy design and informed the selection of the initial pose for control development.

**B. Training Results (Isaac Gym)**
Training experiments in Isaac Gym evaluated performance based on reward components (closer to 1.0 being better) and AMP discriminator loss (closer to 0 for better style matching).

1. **Balancing Policy (Head Mass Sensitivity):** A systematic evaluation of balancing policy performance by perturbing the head mass in the URDF file (Table IV) revealed a non-linear relationship. An optimal head mass of 3.2 kg achieved the best average performance (0.677), suggesting that moderate top-heaviness provided pronounced proprioceptive signals while remaining manageable. Performance degraded with head masses of 4.2 kg (0.658), 5.2 kg (0.613), and 6.2 kg (0.653), indicating that excessive mass overwhelmed the policy's compensation capabilities. A lighter head (2.2 kg, 0.660) also presented challenges, as the controller struggled to generalize.

2. **Walking Policy (Reward Structure and AMP Coefficient):**
 - **Reward Structure:** A balanced reward distribution [0.35, 0.35, 0.4] (Motion Quality, Task, Safety) achieved the best overall average performance (0.721) with strong safety metrics (0.693). Altering the distribution, such as prioritizing task performance [0.5, 0.3, 0.2], improved individual task scores (0.757) but significantly reduced safety (0.567) (Table IV).
 - **AMP Style Coefficient:** A lower AMP style coefficient (0.4) resulted in optimal overall performance (0.733) and excellent safety (0.720). Higher coefficients (0.7, 0.9) showed diminishing returns in safety (0.696, 0.679, respectively) but produced more human-like motion (discriminator loss improved from -0.420 to -0.372). This indicated a trade-off between robust physical performance and fidelity to human-like style.

**C. Sim-to-Real Transfer (Real Hardware)**
The AMP-trained policies successfully transferred from simulation to the physical Cosmo robot.

1. **Balancing Policy:** The robot demonstrated robust balancing and push-recovery capabilities (Figure 8). Upper body tracking data showed effective head stabilization with minimal deviations even under significant external perturbations up to 0.15 m/s. Lower body tracking indicated sophisticated joint behaviors, with hip joints maintaining stability and ankle pitch exhibiting controlled oscillations during disturbances, mimicking human-like ankle-prioritized recovery strategies. The robot regained stability within 2 seconds after multi-directional perturbations, validating the domain randomization and reward structure.

2. **Walking Policy:** The walking policy achieved natural, human-like locomotion on hardware (Figure 9). The robot reproduced coordinated shoulder swings and subtle head oscillations characteristic of human walking. Body-local velocity data showed purposeful joint oscillations and x-direction velocity fluctuations, reaching peaks of 0.4 m/s, authentically reproducing human walking gait cycles. The policy successfully managed Cosmo's mass distribution while keeping joint torques within safe operating limits (±20 Nm), demonstrating respect for hardware limitations and shell integrity.

**D. Ablation Studies**
Ablation studies provided evidence for the necessity of specific methodological components:

1. **Motion Styles (Figure 10):**
 - **Without standing pose reference:** The robot exhibited a "jumping" behavior when transitioning to walking, significantly raising its CoM and increasing instability and energy consumption. This validated the need for a standing reference.
 - **Without model-based reference:** The policy generated ineffective, high-frequency stepping with poor foot trajectory control. The robot struggled to accelerate smoothly from standstill to walking speeds, highlighting that proper foot trajectories required explicit reference motions beyond reward-based compensation.

2. **Reward Component Analysis (Figure 11):** Specialized rewards beyond basic imitation, motion quality, and task objectives were crucial for managing foot trajectories and impact forces.
 - Configurations lacking stumble prevention or foot height rewards resulted in faster but hazardous motions with dangerous impact force spikes.
 - The full reward baseline achieved controlled foot trajectories and moderate contact forces, distributing forces over longer periods (e.g., a step taking approximately 12 control ticks, correlating to a biologically plausible gait frequency of ~1.9 Hz). These specialized rewards were essential for protecting the robot's physical components from damaging torque spikes and enabling safer walking.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of humanoid robotics, particularly in the emerging domain of entertainment robotics. Its primary significance lies in demonstrating the effectiveness of integrating physics-based simulation, Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), and robust domain randomization to enable dynamic locomotion in robots with challenging, non-standard morphologies.

The work's impact can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Addressing Aesthetic-Driven Design Challenges:** The successful implementation on Cosmo, a robot with a disproportionately large head (16% of total weight), complex collision geometries, and restrictive foot shells, validates that AMP-guided reinforcement learning can effectively adapt to severe aesthetic design constraints. This is a critical step for entertainment robots, where visual appeal often dictates design choices that would otherwise be considered detrimental to control. The ability to achieve natural, stable motion despite these challenges expands the practical applicability of humanoids in fields where form is as important as function.

2. **Validation of AMP for Eccentric Morphologies:** This study extends the known capabilities of the AMP framework by demonstrating its viability with eccentric robot morphologies. It shows that AMP can induce behavioral adaptations, such as conservative ankle strategies and increased stabilization efforts, while maintaining a human-like motion style. This broadens the scope of AMP beyond biomechanically optimized robots, suggesting its potential for a wider range of custom robotic platforms.

3. **Importance of Specialized Reward Engineering and Motion References:** The research rigorously highlights that a combination of specialized safety rewards and diverse motion references (e.g., standing, model-based, and expressive human-like gaits) is essential. Specialized rewards protect hardware from damage during deployment, a crucial aspect for valuable, custom-built robots. Multiple motion references provide both expressiveness and stability, enabling a natural curriculum from safe, basic movements to dynamic, stylized gaits. This detailed analysis of reward components and motion styles offers practical guidelines for future learning-based control system designs.

4. **Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The success of the sim-to-real transfer pipeline, even for a robot with such demanding aesthetic and structural constraints, underscores the effectiveness of the employed domain randomization techniques. This capability is vital for accelerating development cycles and minimizing risks associated with physical hardware testing.

While the approach prioritizes stability, platform safety, and natural motion over maximal speed (achieving 0.5-0.7 m/s gaits), this trade-off is well-aligned with the requirements of entertainment applications, where expressiveness and hardware preservation often hold greater value than raw agility. This work therefore establishes a promising path forward for the creation of robots that can seamlessly balance visual appeal with robust real-world performance in interactive and entertainment-focused environments.

Future research directions include comparing these imitation learning approaches against traditional model-based controllers in the context of entertainment robotics, and exploring cross-embodiment transfer to assess the broader applicability of these methods across diverse aesthetic morphologies.
