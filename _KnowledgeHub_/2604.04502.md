# Veo-Act: How Far Can Frontier Video Models Advance Generalizable Robot Manipulation?

> [!summary] Summary
> A hierarchical framework named Veo-Act integrates advanced video generation models for high-level planning with vision-language-action policies for low-level execution in robotic manipulation. This system achieved a 2.8x improvement in overall task success in challenging simulated environments and a 3.2x improvement in real-world scenarios compared to VLA baselines, demonstrating enhanced instruction following and robustness.

## Problem

- Achieving generalizable real-world object manipulation across diverse object geometries and open-ended environments.
- High costs and effort associated with collecting large-scale robotic manipulation datasets for VLA models, often leading to knowledge degradation when introducing action modalities.
- Current video generation models struggle with accuracy during contact-rich physical interactions, limiting their direct utility for precise robotic control.

## Method

- A hierarchical framework, **Veo-Act**, which leverages a frontier video generation model (Veo-3) for high-level motion planning by generating future visual trajectories from natural language instructions.
- A multi-head inverse dynamics model (IDM) that simultaneously predicts robot actions from video frames and an interaction indicator (gate value) indicating the need for reactive control.
- A gated switching mechanism that delegates fine-grained, contact-rich interactions to a high-performing low-level Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy, returning control to the planned trajectory when interactions are complete.

## Results

- Veo-Act improved overall task success by 2.8x in challenging simulated environments and 3.2x in real-world settings compared to the π0.5 VLA baseline.
- The framework successfully addressed scenarios with confounding factors like invisible wrist cameras, similar-object distractors, and pass-by interactions, where baseline policies experienced significant performance degradation.
- Ablation studies highlighted the contributions of the DINOv3 visual encoder, STEM-OB noise augmentation, and the multi-head IDM design to the system's overall robustness and performance, particularly in aligning action prediction with interaction detection.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Frontier video generation models possess strong zero-shot capabilities for high-level planning and semantic understanding but lack the low-level precision required for contact-rich interactions.
> - A hierarchical control framework effectively combines the semantic generalization strengths of video models with the precise execution capabilities of VLA policies.
> - A jointly trained multi-head inverse dynamics model with an interaction detector enables robust and intelligent switching between high-level video-guided planning and low-level reactive control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has long grappled with the challenge of achieving generalizable real-world object manipulation across diverse object geometries and open-ended environments. Recent advancements have seen Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, often built upon large Vision-Language Models (VLMs), emerge as a promising paradigm for developing generalist robot policies. However, the development of robust VLA models faces two significant hurdles: the immense cost and effort associated with collecting large-scale robotic manipulation datasets, and the difficulty in preserving and transferring the rich prior knowledge embedded in pretrained VLMs to downstream VLA policies without suffering from knowledge degradation or catastrophic forgetting, particularly when introducing an explicit action modality.

In parallel, video generation models have undergone rapid development, now demonstrating the capacity to produce highly realistic video frames and a nascent understanding of physical dynamics. Researchers have begun exploring the use of these models to inform generalist robot policies. Some approaches leverage video models to predict future visual sequences, subsequently employing inverse dynamics models (IDMs) to infer corresponding robot actions. Others utilize the internal representations learned by video models to guide policy learning. While these methods have shown some degree of generalization in robotic manipulation, their performance has often been constrained by the capabilities of publicly available video models, which typically lag behind commercial foundation models. A critical limitation observed in these approaches is the inaccuracy of video generation models during contact-rich physical interactions, which can adversely affect downstream policy performance, especially in complex dexterous manipulation scenarios.

This paper positions itself at the intersection of these two areas, explicitly asking how far the most powerful contemporary video generation models can advance robotic manipulation. It first investigates the zero-shot capabilities of an advanced video generation model (Veo-3) combined with an IDM, identifying its strengths in generating task-level trajectories and following instructions, but also its limitations in low-level control accuracy during contact-rich interactions. Building on this observation, the work proposes a hierarchical framework, Veo-Act, designed to integrate the strengths of advanced video models for high-level planning with the precision of VLA policies for low-level execution, thereby addressing the identified shortcomings in a synergistic manner. This approach aims to leverage the inherent generalization capabilities of video models by operating on their native input-output formats, a method proposed to circumvent the knowledge degradation issues often encountered when adapting VLMs to VLA tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to comprehensively investigate the extent to which state-of-the-art video generation models, specifically Veo-3, can support and advance generalizable robotic manipulation capabilities. This central inquiry is pursued through two main phases of investigation.

The initial motivation stems from the rapid advancements in video generation models and their demonstrated capacity for understanding physical dynamics. The authors first explore a zero-shot approach where Veo-3 predicts future image sequences based on current robot observations and task instructions. An inverse dynamics model (IDM), trained solely on self-supervised random-play data without human supervision or expert demonstrations, then recovers the corresponding robot actions. The rationale for this initial investigation is the hypothesis that if a video model can accurately generate physically plausible future motions in image space, an IDM should be capable of translating these visual trajectories into executable robot actions. This approach is intended to assess the direct utility and generalization capabilities of frontier video models in a robotic control context.

Through this initial investigation, a key observation emerged: while the "Veo-3+IDM" approach consistently generates approximately correct task-level trajectories and faithfully adheres to instructions, its low-level control accuracy proved insufficient for reliably solving most manipulation tasks, particularly those involving contact-rich interactions. This limitation motivated the second, and primary, objective of the research: to develop a framework that mitigates these low-level precision issues while retaining the high-level generalization benefits of video models.

The core motivation for developing Veo-Act, the hierarchical framework, is to capitalize on the observed strengths of video models—their capacity for high-level planning and instruction following—while compensating for their weaknesses in precise physical interaction. The authors specifically note that the "video prediction + inverse dynamics" paradigm preserves the full generalization capabilities of video models by operating directly on their native input-output formats, in contrast to VLM-based VLA policies which often experience knowledge degradation when an action modality is introduced. Therefore, Veo-Act aims to leverage Veo-3 as a high-level motion planner to provide robust semantic guidance and task-level trajectories, and to delegate the execution of fine-grained, contact-rich interactions to a high-performing VLA policy (π0.5), which acts as a low-level executor. An inverse dynamics model is further enhanced with an interaction detector to intelligently switch control between these two components, thereby combining their respective advantages for improved instruction-following and overall task success.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the development and evaluation of Veo-Act, a hierarchical planning and control framework designed to integrate advanced video generation models with low-level robot policies.

**A. Video Generation**
The framework initiates by leveraging a frontier video generation model, specifically Veo-3.1-fast, as a high-level motion planner. Given an initial observation image (I0) and a natural language task instruction (prompt), the video model generates a future visual trajectory, denoted as I∗0:n = {I∗1, I∗2..., I∗n}. This sequence of synthesized frames represents the intended future evolution of the scene and provides a high-level motion prior in image space. The generated video is converted into a frame trajectory by sampling or decoding frames at a fixed rate, which then serves as the conditioning signal for subsequent inverse dynamics.

**B. Multi-Head Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM)**
A critical component of Veo-Act is a multi-head inverse dynamics model. This IDM is designed to perform two simultaneous functions: mapping image transitions to robot actions and predicting an interaction indicator (gate value). The architecture utilizes DINOv3 as the visual encoder, chosen for its spatial comprehension capabilities. At each timestep t, the IDM processes the previous and current frames (It-1, It) and the robot state (st). The model features two separate MLP heads:
- **Action Head**: Predicts an executable action chunk a∗0:n-1 = {a∗0, a∗1..., a∗n-1} by inferring inverse dynamics over the entire generated video trajectory I∗0:n.
- **Interaction Detector Head**: Predicts a scalar gate value Gt ∈ online during execution, indicating the likelihood of requiring reactive low-level policy intervention.
The IDM is trained end-to-end. The action head is supervised using a Huber loss for robust regression of robot poses, while the interaction detector is supervised via Binary Cross Entropy to classify switching triggers (grasp steps labeled 1, non-grasp steps labeled 0). The training dataset comprises 300k simulated frame-pair samples of random robot motions and grasp/release actions, augmented with 100k purely random-motion simulations and 150k real-world samples. STEM-OB is applied for observation-level noise augmentation to enhance robustness and reduce the sim-to-real gap.

**C. Action Smoothing**
To address potential noise or infeasible high-frequency components in the directly predicted action sequences, a temporal smoother is applied to the action chunk (a∗0:n-1 → ¯a∗0:n-1) before execution. This module can be implemented using techniques such as moving-average filtering or spline-based interpolation, ensuring a smooth and executable action trajectory.

**D. Hierarchical Planning and Execution**
After video generation and action chunk conversion, the robot enters a step-by-step execution stage.
- **Instruction-Following Stage**: By default, the system follows the planned actions by popping them from a queue.
- **Gated Switching**: In parallel, at every timestep, the IDM's interaction detector head computes Gt from real-time observations. If Gt consistently exceeds a predefined threshold (e.g., τ=0.5) for a short persistence window, control switches to the low-level policy.
- **Low-Level Execution Stage**: When enabled, a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy (π0.5 in this case) takes current image and robot state as input to output reactive actions (at = πVLA(at | It, st)), controlling dexterous manipulation.
- **Switch-Back Mechanism**: If Gt falls below τ consistently, control switches back to the planned action queue. To ensure stability and prevent repeated re-entry into the same interaction region, the system prunes remaining planned actions corresponding to the interaction interval and resumes from the next segment where the predicted gate is below the threshold. Execution continues with alternation between planned chunks and reactive control until task termination.

**E. Variants for Comparison**
To isolate the effects of different components, two variants are considered:
1. **Pure IDM Execution**: The low-level policy is removed, and the smoothed action chunk is executed throughout the episode without gating. This variant assesses the video prior and IDM mapping in isolation.
2. **Simultaneous Control**: The planned action chunk controls arm pose components (position, rotation), while the low-level policy controls hand/gripper components. This provides continuous, factorized control without discrete switching.

**F. Experimental Setup**
Experiments are conducted in both simulation (high-fidelity IsaacLab) and on a real robot (7-DoF arm with a 12-DoF dexterous hand, equipped with global and wrist-mounted RGB cameras). The global-view camera is used for video generation and IDM prediction, while the wrist camera assists low-level policy execution.
Evaluation tasks involve object-placement. Three "confounded settings" are designed to expose specific instruction-following failures in the VLA baseline:
- **Simulation**: Wrist-camera invisible, similar-object distractors, and pass-by interaction. Each setting includes a Control condition (without confounding factor) and an Experimental condition (with confounding factor).
- **Real Robot**: Similar-object distractors, pass-by interaction, and richer semantics (compositional language instructions).
The baseline for comparison is π0.5, which also serves as the low-level policy within Veo-Act. An additional comparison is made with VPP (Video Prediction Policy) in simulation.

**G. Evaluation Metrics**
Two metrics are used:
1. **Instruction-following success**: Whether the end-effector reaches the specified target object, based on a distance threshold.
2. **Overall task success**: Whether the target object is successfully placed into the specified container and becomes static, based on placement and speed thresholds.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the capabilities of Veo-Act across a range of manipulation tasks in both simulation and real-world environments, particularly in scenarios designed to challenge conventional VLA policies.

**Initial Zero-Shot Capabilities of Video Models**:
The initial investigation revealed that while advanced video models like Veo-3 can consistently generate approximately correct task-level trajectories and follow instructions faithfully, their low-level control accuracy is insufficient for reliably solving most manipulation tasks, especially those requiring contact-rich interactions. The "Pure IDM Execution" variant further supported this, achieving non-trivial instruction-following success in simpler settings but consistently low overall task success, indicating a limitation in precise grasping and object transfer rather than semantic understanding.

**Overall Performance of Veo-Act vs. Baselines**:
- **Simulation Results (Table I, Fig. 5)**:
 - The confounding factors (wrist-camera invisible, similar-object distractors, pass-by interaction) significantly degraded the performance of the π0.5 baseline, with instruction-following success dropping by 51.5% to 91.9% and overall success dropping by 58.8% to 100% in experimental conditions compared to control.
 - Veo-Act demonstrated substantial improvements over π0.5 under these challenging experimental conditions. For instance, in "wrist-camera invisible," Veo-Act improved instruction-following success from 0.37 to 0.83 and overall success from 0.33 to 0.67. In "pass-by interaction," where π0.5 nearly failed (0.03 instruction-following, 0.00 overall), Veo-Act achieved 0.50 instruction-following and 0.47 overall success.
 - Aggregating overall success across the three simulation settings under experimental conditions, Veo-Act achieved a 2.8x improvement over the baseline (0.69 vs. 0.24). When considering both control and experimental conditions, Veo-Act achieved a 60.0% weighted total improvement (0.80 vs. 0.50).
 - The video-based method VPP generally performed worse than both π0.5 and Veo-Act, especially in experimental conditions, highlighting the limitations of relying solely on direct video-to-action translation.

- **Real-Robot Results (Table I, Fig. 6)**:
 - Veo-Act showed consistent and substantial gains over π0.5 in real-world scenarios.
 - For "similar-object distractors," Veo-Act improved instruction-following success from 0.56 to 0.94 and overall success from 0.50 to 0.75.
 - In "pass-by interaction," Veo-Act achieved a 4.00x improvement in instruction-following (0.23 to 0.92) and a 5.7x improvement in overall success (0.15 to 0.85).
 - For tasks requiring "richer semantics," Veo-Act improved instruction-following success by 4.5x (0.21 to 0.95) and overall success by 7.2x (0.11 to 0.79).
 - Aggregating overall success across the three real-robot settings, Veo-Act yielded a 3.2x weighted total improvement (0.79 vs. 0.25).

- **Overall Aggregated Results (Simulation + Real Robot)**:
 - Across all simulation and real-robot trials, Veo-Act achieved an overall success rate of 0.80 compared to the baseline's 0.45, representing a 78.4% weighted total improvement.

**Ablation Study (Table II, Fig. 7)**:
An ablation study on the "wrist-camera invisible" experimental setting investigated the impact of architectural choices:
- Using a ResNet backbone instead of DINOv3 for the IDM slightly reduced performance (85.1% overall success relative to full Veo-Act), indicating the benefit of DINOv3's visual encoding.
- Training without noise augmentation (STEM-OB) also led to a slight reduction (79.1% overall success), suggesting the importance of robustness techniques.
- A single-head IDM (without a separate interaction detector) matched the instruction-following success but achieved lower overall success (85.1% relative to full Veo-Act). This suggests that the multi-head design, which jointly learns action prediction and interaction detection, provides better alignment for stage switching and improved end-to-end execution.

**Failure Analysis (Table IV)**:
Analysis of failure cases revealed that for the "Pure IDM" approach, interaction failures (57 out of 89 total failures) were the dominant cause, significantly outweighing video generation (28) and guidance failures (4). In contrast, Veo-Act substantially reduced interaction failures (6 out of 28 total failures), although video generation failures (21) remained a contributing factor. This supports the core hypothesis that while video models offer strong semantic guidance, robust dexterous interaction critically benefits from a specialized low-level policy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant step towards leveraging advanced video generation models for generalizable robotic manipulation, outlining a practical pathway for their integration into robot learning systems. The proposed Veo-Act hierarchical framework addresses a critical limitation of current video-based approaches: their insufficient precision during contact-rich physical interactions, while simultaneously capitalizing on their emerging strengths in high-level semantic understanding and instruction following.

The work's significance lies in several aspects:
- **Demonstrating the Generalization of Frontier Video Models**: The study empirically demonstrates that advanced video generation models like Veo-3 already possess strong capabilities for generating task-level trajectories and understanding complex instructions in a zero-shot manner. This highlights their potential as robust high-level planners, capable of interpreting broad semantic intent from natural language prompts.
- **Addressing Precision Limitations Hierarchically**: By proposing a hierarchical architecture, Veo-Act effectively mitigates the low-level control accuracy issues inherent in current video generation models. This "divide and conquer" strategy, where video models plan and VLA policies execute precise interactions, showcases a successful method for combining complementary strengths of different AI paradigms in robotics.
- **Substantial Performance Improvements**: Veo-Act consistently outperforms a strong VLA baseline (π0.5) in challenging simulation and real-world scenarios, particularly those designed to induce semantic or perceptual failures in VLA policies. The observed 2.8x to 7.2x gains in success rates across various confounding factors signify a notable advancement in reliable robot control under distribution shifts and complex instructions.
- **Insights into System Design**: The ablation studies and failure analyses provide valuable insights into the architectural choices, such as the efficacy of the multi-head inverse dynamics model for aligned action prediction and interaction detection, and the crucial role of noise augmentation for robustness. The clear identification of interaction failures as a dominant bottleneck for pure video-based methods underscores the necessity of the hierarchical design.

The potential impact of this research is substantial for the field of robot learning:
- **Enhanced Generalizability**: By leveraging the web-scale knowledge embedded in large video models, robots can potentially achieve greater generalization across diverse tasks and environments, reducing the dependency on extensive, domain-specific robotic datasets.
- **Reduced Data Requirements**: The ability to infer high-level plans from video models, especially with IDMs trained on self-supervised random-play data, could significantly reduce the need for costly human demonstrations or expert supervision for new tasks.
- **New Directions for Robot Learning**: This work suggests a promising direction for future research where the development of more capable video models directly translates into improvements in robotic manipulation, particularly if these models can enhance their physical fidelity during contact-rich interactions. It also paves the way for a clearer division of labor in robot control architectures, where different AI modules specialize in different levels of abstraction.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
The authors acknowledge several limitations. Veo-Act's performance remains sensitive to the fidelity and stability of the underlying video generation model. Minor variations in scene elements (robot appearance, camera viewpoint, layout) can lead to different video predictions, thereby undermining the reliability of downstream planning. Furthermore, inverse dynamics alone, when derived from current video models, is insufficient for robustly solving complete manipulation tasks, especially during contact-rich interaction phases.

Future work, as suggested by the authors, should focus on improving video generation models themselves. This includes developing models that exhibit stronger consistency under distribution shifts and substantially improved accuracy in modeling contact-rich physical interactions. Achieving these advancements would further empower the video planning + inverse dynamics paradigm, potentially enabling it to support an even broader range of manipulation behaviors with minimal reliance on learned low-level control policies.
