# CoCo-InEKF: State Estimation with Learned Contact Covariances in Dynamic, Contact-Rich Scenarios

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from ETH Zurich and Disney Research developed CoCo-InEKF, a state estimator for legged robots that integrates a differentiable Invariant Extended Kalman Filter with a learned module predicting continuous contact velocity covariances. The system, trained end-to-end using state-error losses, demonstrated superior linear velocity estimation and filter consistency in dynamic movements and multi-contact interactions in both simulation and real-world experiments.

## Problem

- Traditional contact-aided state estimators often rely on a simplistic binary (on/off) interpretation of contact, which inadequately captures complexities like partial contact or directional slippage.
- Existing learning-based contact detection methods for state estimation typically require supervised training data with contact labels, which are often derived from error-prone heuristics.
- Current proprioceptive state estimation struggles with highly dynamic and multi-contact interactions, leading to reduced accuracy, particularly for linear velocity, and potential estimator divergence.

## Method

- A ==differentiable Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF)== is employed, which permanently incorporates all predefined contact candidate points into its state to maintain a constant and differentiable computation graph.
- A lightweight neural network, ==`ContactNet`==, predicts continuous, per-contact-point velocity covariances from proprioceptive sensor history, providing a nuanced representation of contact confidence instead of binary states.
- The entire **CoCo-InEKF** architecture is trained end-to-end using ==Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT)== with an L2 loss on the body linear velocity, removing the dependency on hand-labeled contact ground truth.

## Results

- CoCo-InEKF achieved the lowest linear velocity Absolute Trajectory Error (RMSE = **0.046 m/s** for dancing, **0.099 m/s** for ground motions) in simulation, outperforming all baseline methods, including InEKF with ground-truth contacts.
- The method demonstrated improved filter consistency, with a higher percentage of Normalized Estimation Error Squared (NEES) values (**52.1%** for combined core state) within **95% confidence bounds** compared to baseline InEKF methods.
- In real-world online control loop tests on a bipedal robot, CoCo-InEKF enabled high success rates for trained dance motions (**95%**) and unseen challenging motions (**100%** for pirouette and moonwalk), surpassing even MoCap-based state estimators.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning continuous contact velocity covariances enables a more expressive and accurate representation of complex contact conditions (e.g., partial contact, anisotropic slippage) compared to binary contact states.
> - End-to-end training of a differentiable filter purely on state error eliminates the need for heuristic contact labels, allowing the model to learn optimal noise characteristics directly from data and improve generalization.
> - Automated contact candidate selection simplifies the application of the method to new robot morphologies while maintaining performance comparable to hand-picked configurations, demonstrating robustness to exact point placement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Proprioceptive state estimation, which relies solely on onboard sensors like Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) and joint encoders, is a critical component for robust feedback control in legged robots, especially during dynamic movements or in environments where exteroceptive sensors (e.g., cameras, LiDAR) are unreliable. A common strategy in this field is to use Kalman filters (such as Extended Kalman Filters or Invariant Extended Kalman Filters – InEKFs) that fuse IMU data with leg odometry. These methods often assume discrete contact points on the robot are stationary in the world frame when in contact.

However, the efficacy of these traditional filtering approaches is significantly dependent on accurate contact state detection and the strict validity of the non-slip assumption. Existing methods for contact detection often employ force thresholding, probabilistic fusion, or integrate planned contact states. These techniques frequently rely on hand-crafted heuristics and user-defined thresholds, which can be brittle in varied and dynamic scenarios, particularly when dealing with partial contact or directional slippage. While some research has explored adaptive modulation of contact velocity covariance, these often still involve heuristic rules.

To address these limitations, data-driven methods, particularly end-to-end supervised learning approaches using neural networks (e.g., Transformers or MLPs), have emerged. These methods aim to learn direct mappings from raw sensor inputs to state trajectories. While they have shown promise in areas like pedestrian pose estimation and can enable impressive reinforcement learning controllers, they have typically underperformed traditional filters in terms of state estimation accuracy and often lack the guarantees and consistency of model-based approaches, particularly when encountering out-of-distribution data.

Hybrid approaches seek to combine the strengths of both model-based filtering and data-driven learning. These methods integrate learnable components into classical filtering frameworks, for instance, by predicting measurement inputs, refining input residuals, estimating input noise, or learning Kalman gains. In the context of legged robotics, hybrid methods have focused on learning contact states. For example, some approaches use neural networks to classify binary contact states based on labeled data or learn slip states to heuristically modulate contact velocity covariance. However, these still frequently depend on supervised learning with ground-truth contact labels, which themselves can be derived using heuristics, or reduce complex contact conditions to binary classifications.

CoCo-InEKF positions itself within this hybrid landscape. It addresses the limitations of binary contact state assumptions and the need for heuristic ground-truth contact labels by proposing a differentiable InEKF that leverages learned continuous contact velocity covariances. This approach aims to provide more nuanced contact confidence modulation and enables end-to-end training of the learning module without relying on hand-labeled contact data, thereby offering a more robust and adaptable state estimation solution for highly dynamic and contact-rich locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust proprioceptive state estimation method for legged robots capable of operating in highly dynamic, contact-rich scenarios. The authors aim to overcome several specific limitations of existing approaches:

1. **Addressing the binary contact state assumption:** Traditional contact-aided state estimators often rely on a binary (on/off) interpretation of contact, which is insufficient to capture the complexities of real-world interactions. These complexities include partial contact, varying contact forces, and directional slippage. The paper is motivated by the need for a more nuanced representation of contact confidence that can account for a spectrum of conditions from firm contact to no contact, including anisotropic slippage.

2. **Eliminating the reliance on heuristic ground-truth contact labels:** Previous learning-based contact detection methods for state estimation often require supervised training data with labeled contact states. These labels are typically derived from heuristics or thresholds, which can introduce errors, limit generalizability, and necessitate expert tuning. The authors aim to develop a system that can be trained end-to-end using only state-error losses, thereby removing the dependency on such heuristic contact labels.

3. **Improving robustness and accuracy in challenging motions:** Current methods can struggle with highly dynamic and multi-contact interactions, leading to estimator divergence or reduced accuracy. The motivation is to achieve superior state estimation performance, particularly for linear velocity, and improved filter consistency in scenarios like dancing and complex ground interactions, both in simulation and on real hardware.

4. **Maintaining efficiency for real-time operation:** While end-to-end learning methods can be computationally intensive, and traditional filtering approaches may involve complex computations, the goal is to develop a hybrid solution that offers an improved accuracy-efficiency trade-off, enabling real-time execution on resource-constrained onboard robot computers.

5. **Simplifying deployment on new robots:** Hand-picking contact candidate points for state estimators can be a laborious and expert-dependent process. The authors are motivated to propose an automated method for contact candidate selection, aiming for performance comparable to hand-picked configurations and demonstrating insensitivity to the exact placement of these points.

By addressing these motivations, the CoCo-InEKF seeks to advance the state of proprioceptive state estimation by integrating learned, continuous contact velocity covariances into a differentiable InEKF framework, trained purely on state error.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CoCo-InEKF methodology integrates a learned contact module with a differentiable Invariant Extended Kalman Filter (InEKF). The core idea is to replace binary contact state assumptions with continuously modulated contact velocity covariances.

**A. Differentiable InEKF Architecture:**
The CoCo-InEKF is based on the contact-aided InEKF formulation but introduces a key modification to make it differentiable. Unlike standard approaches that dynamically add and remove contact points from the state, CoCo-InEKF permanently maintains the positions of all predefined contact candidate points as part of the filter's state. This results in a constant computation graph, where all operations (matrix multiplications, inversions, and group operations) are differentiable, which is essential for end-to-end training.

The state vector `x` includes base orientation, base linear velocity, base position, `N` contact positions, and IMU gyro and acceleration biases. The process model for contact positions (`W_dot_p_C_i`) is defined by a zero-mean Gaussian noise term (`B_w_C_i`) in the IMU frame, with a predicted covariance `B_Sigma_C_i`. This noise term allows the model's confidence in a contact point being stationary to be continuously adjusted by the learned module. The remaining components of the InEKF (prediction and correction steps, IMU state forward integration, and bias modeling) follow the standard formulation as detailed by Hartley et al.. Leg kinematics are used to form the measurement model, relating joint position measurements to the estimated state.

**B. Learned Contact Module:**
A lightweight neural network, referred to as the learned contact module or `ContactNet`, is responsible for predicting the per-contact-point velocity covariances (`B_Sigma_C_i`) in the body frame. To ensure that the output is a valid, symmetric, positive-semidefinite matrix, the network predicts the six elements of a lower-triangular 3x3 matrix `L`, from which the covariance `B_Sigma_C_i = LL^T` is computed.

The input to this module is a history of proprioceptive sensor inputs of length `H`. These inputs include IMU angular velocity (`B_omega`), linear acceleration (`B_a`), joint positions (`q`), joint velocities (`dot_q`), actuator torque measurements (`tau`), relative contact positions (`B_p_B->C_i`), and relative contact velocities (`B_v_B->C_i`). Crucially, internal InEKF state estimates are excluded from the contact module's inputs to maintain a clean separation and avoid complex feedback loops. The input history is normalized along the time dimension. For computational efficiency, an MLP-based architecture is chosen over CNNs, allowing real-time operation on the robot's onboard system.

**C. End-to-End Training via Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT):**
The entire CoCo-InEKF architecture, including the neural contact module and the differentiable InEKF, is trained end-to-end using BPTT.
1. **Data Aggregation:** Training data is collected by forward-simulating a pre-trained control policy in parallel across multiple (E=1280) environments. During these rollouts, ground-truth states and sensor measurements are recorded.
2. **Environment Randomization:** Domain randomization is applied, varying friction, terrain shapes, and disturbance forces, to enhance the model's generalization capabilities.
3. **Rollout and Loss Computation:** For each learning iteration, a buffer of `L` time steps of proprioceptive and ground-truth state data is used. CoCo-InEKF processes these `L` time steps, with the contact module predicting covariances that are then fed into the InEKF.
4. **Loss Function:** The training objective is an L2 loss on the body linear velocity in the body frame: `L(x, hat_x) = || W_R_B^T W_v_B - W_hat_R_B^T W_hat_v_B ||_2^2`. This loss focuses on an observable quantity of the InEKF and aligns with the requirements of downstream control policies, avoiding issues related to global drift in position and orientation.
5. **Optimization:** Gradients are backpropagated from the computed state errors at all time steps through the differentiable InEKF to update the contact module's parameters using the Adam optimizer. The InEKF's internal state and covariance are maintained across consecutive rollouts up to a maximum episode length, allowing it to naturally drift away from the ground-truth state, which aids in learning robustness.

**D. Automated Contact Candidate Selection:**
To simplify the application of CoCo-InEKF to new robot morphologies, an automated method for generating contact candidate points is proposed. Given a target number of `N` candidates and a set of robot rigid bodies, the method samples 10 times `N` points on each mesh surface. From this combined set, `N` points are selected using farthest point sampling, starting from a random seed and a nominal robot pose. This method aims to distribute points effectively, typically at extremities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluated CoCo-InEKF against several baselines in simulation and real-world experiments on a bipedal robot named Lima, using two scenarios: dynamic dancing motions (foot contact only) and ground motions (full-body ground contact). Metrics included Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) for linear velocity, position, and orientation, as well as Normalized Estimation Error Squared (NEES) for filter consistency and real-world success rates.

**A. Simulation Experiments:**

1. **Contact Covariances:** Visualization during a forward-walking gait showed that the learned contact covariances, particularly their total standard deviation, align with ground-truth contact events and instantaneous contact point velocities. The directionality of the covariance ellipsoids generally agreed with contact candidate displacement, suggesting the model learns to constrain tangential directions with low covariance magnitudes. This indicates that the continuous covariance representation captures more nuanced contact conditions than binary states.

2. **Dancing Motions:** In the dancing scenario, CoCo-InEKF achieved the lowest linear velocity ATE (RMSE = 0.046 m/s) compared to all baselines, including InEKF with ground-truth contacts (0.176 m/s), Hybrid Baseline methods (0.121-0.123 m/s), and end-to-end SET models (0.279-0.286 m/s). The InEKF with heuristic contacts exhibited significant divergence (2.675 m/s). While CoCo-InEKF's position and orientation ATE were slightly higher than some InEKF baselines, the focus was on body-frame velocity, which is critical for control.

3. **Ground Motions:** For contact-rich ground motions, CoCo-InEKF again demonstrated the lowest linear velocity ATE (RMSE = 0.099 m/s), matching the performance of the much larger SET models (0.096-0.107 m/s) and significantly outperforming other InEKF and Hybrid baselines (0.363-4.448 m/s). It also showed substantial reductions across position and orientation error metrics compared to all other InEKF-based baselines. The results suggested that the velocity covariance representation allows the method to leverage information even from contact candidates not perfectly in contact.

**B. Ablation Studies:**

1. **Architecture and Inputs:** Replacing the CNN with an MLP and reducing hidden layer sizes in the Hybrid Baseline significantly improved inference speed (5x speedup) while maintaining or slightly improving performance. CoCo-InEKF with its MLP architecture outperformed all Hybrid Baselines and SET models in terms of linear velocity ATE (0.046 m/s) while maintaining low inference times (0.14 ms for NN, 0.42 ms for full SE). The SET baselines were computationally expensive and had higher RMSE. Increasing the sensor history size (H=150 vs. H=20) led to performance degradation and increased inference time across all evaluated methods.

2. **Training Configurations:** The BPTT unroll size (L) affected performance. A shorter buffer (L=64) resulted in significant performance deterioration (RMSE = 0.066 m/s compared to 0.046 m/s for L=128), indicating an inability to capture longer-horizon effects. A longer buffer (L=256) also showed slightly worse performance (RMSE = 0.051 m/s), potentially due to vanishing/exploding gradients or reduced training iterations within the fixed time limit.

3. **Number of Contact Candidates:** For CoCo-InEKF, increasing the number of contact points from 4 to 10 and then to 18 positively correlated with improved estimation accuracy (RMSE decreased from 0.134 m/s to 0.069 m/s). This came at an expense of computational cost, though the method still remained efficient. SET baselines showed limited improvement beyond 10 contacts.

4. **Automated Contact Candidate Selection:** The automated selection method yielded performance comparable to, or in some cases better than, hand-picked contact configurations for both dancing and ground motions. There was minimal variation across different random samples generated by the automated method, indicating its robustness.

**C. Statistical Filter Consistency:**
The Normalized Estimation Error Squared (NEES) metric was used to assess filter consistency. CoCo-InEKF demonstrated a higher percentage of NEES values within the 95% confidence bounds (52.1% for combined core state, 71.1% for velocity, 59.8% for position, 68.1% for orientation) compared to all baseline InEKF methods. This indicates that CoCo-InEKF matches or exceeds the consistency of an InEKF utilizing ground-truth contact information and significantly improves consistency over heuristic or learned binary contact classification baselines, which were often underconfident.

**D. Real-World Experiments:**

1. **Offline Evaluation (Ground Motions):** On a 10-minute real-world ground-motion dataset, CoCo-InEKF achieved the lowest linear velocity ATE (RMSE = 0.0805 m/s) and position ATE (RMSE = 0.2019 m/s), outperforming all baselines. This suggests a low sim-to-real gap for the approach.

2. **Online Evaluation (Dancing Motions):** In live control loop tests with a VMP controller at 600 Hz, CoCo-InEKF enabled the highest success rates for both trained dance motions (95%) and unseen challenging motions like pirouette (100%) and moonwalk (100%). This performance exceeded even the MoCap-based state estimator (92% for trained, 90% for pirouette, 100% for moonwalk) and other real-time capable baselines.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CoCo-InEKF method represents an advancement in proprioceptive state estimation for legged robots, particularly in dynamic and contact-rich environments. Its significance stems from several key aspects:

1. **Enhanced Robustness through Continuous Contact Covariances:** By replacing binary contact states with continuous, learned contact velocity covariances, CoCo-InEKF provides a more expressive and nuanced representation of contact confidence. This allows the system to effectively account for complex contact conditions such as partial contact, varying force magnitudes, and directional slippage, which traditional binary approaches often fail to capture. This leads to more stable and accurate state estimates, as demonstrated by the improved ATE values across various motion types.

2. **Autonomous Training without Heuristic Labels:** The end-to-end training of the neural contact module via backpropagation through time, using only state-error losses, eliminates the need for manually labeled or heuristically derived ground-truth contact information. This significantly simplifies the training process, reduces reliance on expert knowledge, and allows the model to learn optimal noise covariances directly from data, potentially leading to better generalization.

3. **Improved Accuracy-Efficiency Trade-off:** The proposed method achieves high accuracy, particularly in linear velocity estimation, while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for real-time operation on resource-constrained robot hardware (600 Hz control loop). This positions CoCo-InEKF as a practical solution for deploying agile and robust controllers on physical robots.

4. **Demonstrated Filter Consistency:** The analysis of Normalized Estimation Error Squared (NEES) indicates that CoCo-InEKF improves the statistical consistency of the filter, matching or exceeding the performance of an InEKF with privileged ground-truth contact information. This suggests that the filter's reported uncertainty more accurately reflects the actual estimation error, which is crucial for reliable control and planning.

5. **Simplified Deployment and Generalization:** The development of an automated contact candidate selection procedure, coupled with the method's insensitivity to exact contact point placement, streamlines the application of CoCo-InEKF to new robot morphologies. This reduces the setup time and expertise required, facilitating broader adoption.

The potential impact of CoCo-InEKF is substantial for the field of legged robotics. By providing more accurate, consistent, and robust state estimates, it can enable robots to perform more complex, dynamic, and agile maneuvers, such as dancing, navigating varied terrains, and executing multi-contact interactions, with greater stability and less susceptibility to failures. This could lead to advancements in areas such as robot exploration, search and rescue, and industrial applications where dynamic and contact-rich locomotion is required.

Future work could involve incorporating real-world data or increasing training diversity to further improve performance, applying the framework to diverse robot morphologies, and integrating these proprioceptive estimates with exteroceptive sensors (LiDAR or vision) for global drift correction, paving the way for even more capable autonomous systems.
