# Safe Task Planning with Long-Term Graph Memory for Embodied Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> SafeMem is a safe task-planning framework for embodied agents under partial observability. It builds an object-centric semantic graph memory from egocentric RGB-D observations, using a large multimodal model plus SAM for nodes and depth for relations, then an LLM risk predictor scans that memory and triggers conservatism-modulated replanning. Average safe success rate on IS-Bench rises from 0.17 to 0.59.

## Problem

- LLM and VLM planners lack physical risk awareness because pretraining data contains no real-world interaction, so they emit high-level actions that damage robots and environments.
- Prompt-based safety methods such as chain-of-thought reminders and learned cognition prompts only mitigate currently visible hazards, assuming full observability that egocentric robots never have.
- Long-context VLMs fail to reliably retain previously seen hazards across long-horizon interaction, so occluded or out-of-view dangers like a power strip get forgotten.

## Method

- ==Long-term graph memory==: an LMM proposes object nodes and relations, SAM masks refine them through ==IoU matching== and trimming, and depth-derived 3D bounding boxes correct spatial edges.
- Graph update via ==mask-based node alignment== with ==bipartite matching==, retention of unmatched invisible nodes across occlusion, and ==action-aware edge pruning== plus insertion driven by the previous executed action.
- ==Safety-aware planning loop==: an ==LLM risk predictor== reads the simplified graph and candidate action, emits a ==REPLAN signal== with explanations, and a conservative factor gates replanning probability against deadlock.

## Results

- On IS-Bench under egocentric partial observability, SafeMem averages **0.59** safe success rate versus **0.17** for chain-of-thought, **0.13** for the cognition-prompt baseline, and **0.00** for the base planner.
- Real-world Franka Panda trials averaged over **10** runs hit **1.0** safe success on all four hazard categories, while every baseline drops to **0.0** or **0.1**.
- Memory ablations: graph **0.59** safe success beats object-list **0.17**, retrieval-augmented text **0.38**, and full image history **0.13**; dropping invisible-object retention collapses it to **0.21**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured memory beats raw history: feeding all past images to a long-context model hurt risk prediction more than having no memory module at all.
> - Relational edges carry the safety signal; a bare object list recovers little, so hazards live in object-to-object relations rather than object presence.
> - Probabilistic conservatism trades safety for liveness; a 0.2 skip rate caused roughly a third of violations, so critical domains need a halting fallback.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses safety in high-level task planning for embodied robots controlled by large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs). Recent advances in pretrained multimodal models have enabled robots to interpret natural-language instructions and generate zero-shot sequences of executable actions. These systems provide semantic reasoning and generalization across tasks without requiring extensive task-specific training data. However, their safety performance remains unreliable because the models are primarily pretrained on Internet-scale data rather than physical interaction and embodied failure scenarios.

The paper is situated at the intersection of four research areas: robot task and motion planning, multimodal foundation models, memory for embodied agents, and safety-aware robotics. Classical planners use symbolic representations and search, while reinforcement-learning and imitation-learning approaches learn task policies from interaction or demonstrations. LLM- and VLM-based planners provide a more flexible alternative, but commonly rely on the current visual observation, a textual action history, or prompt-based safety reminders.

Existing safety-oriented methods have addressed collision avoidance, chain-of-thought safety reasoning, learned safety prompts, uncertainty estimation, and explicit safety rules. These methods generally perform better when hazards are visible at the time an action is selected. The paper focuses on a less-developed problem: safety under partial observability. An egocentric robot camera may show a hazard early in an interaction, after which the hazard becomes occluded or moves outside the field of view. A planner that reasons only from the current frame may then forget or overlook the hazard. SafeMem is proposed as an explicit, structured memory system intended to preserve information about objects and their spatial relationships over long interactions.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Central Objective

The primary objective is to improve the Safe Success Rate of embodied agents by allowing them to identify hazards that are not visible in the current observation but were observed earlier. The authors seek to preserve safety-critical information while retaining the flexible task-planning capabilities of a VLM.

The system is designed to satisfy two requirements simultaneously:

1. The robot must complete the user’s task.
2. The robot must avoid violating a corresponding safety condition, such as keeping water away from an electrical power strip or removing flammable objects before activating a stove.

The paper formalizes task planning as a partially observable Markov decision process. The robot receives a single egocentric RGB-D observation rather than the complete environment state. Its high-level actions are selected from a predefined set of executable skills.

#### 2.2. Motivation

The motivating example concerns watering a plant near a power strip. The instruction tells the robot to retrieve water from a container inside a cabinet and use a watering can to water the plant. The power strip is visible in an earlier view but is absent from the robot’s later observation. A conventional VLM planner may therefore pour water into the plant without accounting for the electrical hazard. A safety reminder in the prompt does not guarantee that the model will retain and apply the earlier observation over a long sequence of actions.

The authors argue that raw image histories and long textual contexts are insufficiently structured for reliable hazard reasoning. A graph representation can instead maintain persistent object identities and explicit relations, such as “power strip NEAR plant.” This representation is intended to provide a compact global description of the environment and support targeted risk assessment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Overall SafeMem Architecture

SafeMem is a closed-loop framework composed of perception, graph-memory construction, graph updating, risk prediction, and VLM planning. At each decision step, the robot:

1. Processes the current RGB-D observation.
2. Constructs a frame-level semantic graph.
3. Merges that graph with the persistent memory from previous steps.
4. Generates a candidate action with a VLM planner.
5. Evaluates the candidate action with an LLM-based risk predictor.
6. Replans if a hazard is detected.
7. Executes the accepted action and obtains a new observation.

The planner outputs one high-level atomic action at a time, such as `PLACE_INSIDE`, `POUR_INTO`, `TOGGLE_ON`, or `MOVE_RIGHT`. These actions are grounded in a predefined low-level skill set.

#### 3.2. Graph Memory Construction

The environment is represented as a graph \(G=(V,E)\). Nodes correspond to object instances and contain a class label, persistent instance identifier, image bounding box, and segmentation mask. Edges represent semantic relations between objects. The relation vocabulary includes `ON`, `IN`, `ABOVE`, `NEAR`, `ATTACHED TO`, `PART OF`, and `HOLDING`.

The graph is initialized using two complementary models. Gemini 2.5 Flash receives the RGB image and generates object categories and candidate relationships. SAM 2.0 independently produces segmentation masks. The system matches language-model-detected objects to SAM masks using intersection-over-union. Low-overlap matches are discarded, and duplicate matches are removed. This gives the graph more accurate object localization than relying on the multimodal model alone.

Depth data are used to refine spatial relations. Mask pixels are back-projected into three-dimensional points using the camera’s intrinsic parameters and depth values. The system calculates axis-aligned 3D bounding boxes and applies geometric rules for relations such as `ON`, `IN`, `ABOVE`, `NEAR`, and `ATTACHED TO`. For example, an `ON` relation requires suitable vertical contact and horizontal overlap, while `NEAR` is based on the distance between 3D bounding boxes and an object-scale-dependent threshold. Semantically based relations that cannot be reliably inferred from geometry are retained from the multimodal model.

#### 3.3. Long-Term Graph Updating

At every new observation, the current graph is merged with the previous memory. Nodes are aligned across time using mask overlap and bipartite matching. Matched objects retain their persistent identities, while newly observed objects receive new identifiers. Objects that are temporarily unmatched are retained in memory, allowing the system to preserve information about occluded or out-of-view hazards.

Relations from the current observation replace outdated relations where the relevant objects are visible. Relations involving invisible objects are preserved unless they concern an object affected by the previous action. This pruning is intended to avoid retaining relations that may have changed after an interaction. The system also updates relations using action semantics. For example, after placing an object into a container, it explicitly adds an `IN` relation. Thus, the graph combines perception-based updates with known effects of executed skills.

#### 3.4. Risk Prediction and Replanning

The risk predictor receives a simplified semantic version of the graph, the task instruction, and a proposed action. Bounding boxes and masks are omitted because the predictor primarily requires object identities and relations. The LLM is instructed to determine whether the action is safe and to identify the explicit graph relations supporting any hazard explanation.

If the action is considered unsafe, the predictor returns a `REPLAN` signal and a textual explanation. The VLM planner then generates another candidate action using the current image, action history, task information, available skills, and the risk explanation. This loop continues until the risk predictor accepts an action or the system stops replanning.

A conservative factor \(\eta\) controls whether the robot follows a replan signal. The default value is 0.8, meaning that a detected risk is acted upon probabilistically rather than deterministically. The authors introduce this factor to reduce the possibility of deadlock between the planner and risk predictor. They also evaluate \(\eta=1\), which always follows risk-based replanning but can increase latency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Under Partial Observability

The authors evaluate SafeMem on IS-Bench using modified egocentric RGB-D observations. The simulated tasks cover electrical shock, fire, falling, and food-contamination hazards. The partial-observability condition is constructed so that a hazard is visible earlier but later becomes occluded or unavailable from the current view.

SafeMem achieves an average Success Rate of 0.79 and an average Safe Success Rate of 0.59. Its SR is comparable to SaP at 0.79 and lower than some individual baseline-category results, but its SSR is substantially higher than SaP at 0.13, CoT at 0.17, the base VLM planner at 0.00, and SafeMem without graph memory at 0.21.

Across hazard categories, SafeMem obtains SSR values of 0.67 for electrical hazards, 0.50 for fire hazards, 0.67 for falling hazards, and 0.50 for food contamination. These results support the claim that persistent relational memory is particularly relevant when the hazardous object is no longer visible.

The version without graph memory still improves over several prompt-based baselines, indicating that a separate risk-prediction module provides some safety benefit even when it reasons from the current image alone. However, the difference between 0.21 and 0.59 average SSR indicates that explicit long-term memory is the major contributor.

#### 4.2. Simulation Under Full Observability

Under the fully observable setting, SafeMem achieves an average SR of 0.88 and SSR of 0.67. The corresponding SSR values are 0.13 for SaP, 0.21 for CoT, 0.21 for the base planner, and 0.42 for SafeMem without graph memory.

SafeMem records category-level SSR values of 1.00 for electrical hazards, 0.50 for fire hazards, 0.83 for falling hazards, and 0.33 for food contamination. The results suggest that the framework remains useful even when hazards are currently visible, although performance varies across categories.

#### 4.3. Real-World Robot Experiments

The system is deployed on a seven-degree-of-freedom Franka Panda arm with a RealSense D405 RGB-D camera. Four tabletop tasks correspond to the four simulated hazard categories. Each method is evaluated over ten trials per task.

SafeMem achieves SR and SSR of 1.00 for all four tasks. The baselines generally achieve high task-completion rates but very low safety rates. SaP has an SSR of 0.00 across all tasks. CoT also has 0.00 SSR, while the base planner achieves only 0.10 for fire and falling hazards and 0.00 for the other tasks. SafeMem without graph memory obtains SSR values of 0.30, 0.00, 0.00, and 0.00 across the four categories.

These results show a recurring separation between task completion and safe completion. The models can often complete the requested manipulation while violating a safety constraint. SafeMem reduces this gap in the reported real-world scenarios, although the small number of tasks and trials limits the strength of general conclusions.

#### 4.4. Ablation Findings and Computational Cost

Memory ablations show that the graph representation is more effective than full image history, retrieval-augmented textual memory, or an object-only list. The graph obtains an average SSR of 0.59, compared with 0.13 for full history, 0.38 for RAG, and 0.17 for object-list memory. The comparison between the graph and object-list memory indicates that explicit object relationships are important.

Removing invisible-object retention reduces SSR to 0.21, while removing action-aware edge updates reduces it to 0.38. With \(\eta=0.8\), the system reports six safety violations out of 17 trials and an SSR of 0.59. With \(\eta=1\), violations decrease to three out of 17 and SSR rises to 0.67. However, SafeMem increases average planning cost from approximately 3,980 tokens and 10.33 seconds per step for the base planner to 9,425 tokens and 17.62 seconds per step.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

SafeMem contributes an explicit mechanism for carrying safety-critical relational information across partially observed interactions. Its main significance is not simply the use of an LLM safety prompt, but the separation of memory, risk assessment, and action generation. This design could be relevant to household robots, laboratory automation, and other settings where hazards may be temporarily hidden and actions have physical consequences.

The method also demonstrates the value of combining multimodal semantic understanding, segmentation, depth-based geometry, and action-conditioned state updates. The graph representation provides an interpretable basis for risk explanations and can support debugging or human inspection.

Several limitations remain. SafeMem can reason only about hazards that appear in current or historical observations. It cannot reliably infer risks from completely unseen objects, hidden dynamics, or broader physical contexts. Its closed-set object and relation vocabularies may restrict deployment in unfamiliar environments. Graph construction depends on the accuracy of Gemini, SAM, depth sensing, and mask association; errors can produce missing or incorrect relations. The risk predictor is also constrained to use explicit graph relations and may fail when a hazard requires multi-step causal reasoning.

Finally, the probabilistic replanning mechanism can accept an action flagged as unsafe, as shown by the remaining violations at \(\eta=0.8\). Increasing conservatism reduces violations but increases latency and may cause unnecessary interruption. The real-world evaluation is limited to a single robot platform, four constructed tasks, and ten trials per task. Therefore, the results support the utility of long-term graph memory in the tested conditions but do not establish performance across the wider range of environments and hazards encountered in physical robotics.
