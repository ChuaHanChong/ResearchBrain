# Learning from Failures: Correction-Oriented Policy Optimization with Verifiable Rewards

> [!summary] Summary
> Correction-Oriented Policy Optimization (CIPO) introduces a method to enhance large language models' reasoning and error correction capabilities by converting on-policy failed trajectories into explicit, directional supervision. This approach led to a 4.55% improvement in mathematical reasoning and a 1.43% gain in code generation over existing baselines, alongside substantial boosts in error correction performance across various tasks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) methods often face challenges due to sparse and binary reward signals, making effective credit assignment difficult in complex, multi-step reasoning tasks.
- Current RLVR approaches underutilize information from failed trajectories, uniformly penalizing all errors without differentiating between error modes or proximity to correct solutions, leading to ambiguous optimization signals.
- Previous solutions to address sparse rewards often rely on auxiliary models or external supervision, which can introduce additional costs, biases, or limit the generalizability of the trained models.

## Method

- **CIPO** transforms ==on-policy failed trajectories== into valuable, correction-oriented supervision by training the policy to refine its own erroneous attempts into correct solutions.
- The method incorporates an ==adaptive replay ratio== to balance learning from failures with retaining existing capabilities and uses ==risk-averse reward shaping== to prevent capability regressions during correction.
- A ==difficulty-aware preference mechanism== prioritizes replaying prompts within a 'medium-difficulty' range, focusing training on the most informative samples for efficient learning.

## Results

- CIPO improved mathematical reasoning accuracy by an average of **+4.55%** (to **64.38%**) across six benchmarks and code generation accuracy by **+1.43%** (to **54.27%**) on LiveCodeBench v6 compared to GRPO.
- The method expanded the intrinsic reasoning capacity of models, demonstrated by a **+6.12%** higher pass@32 on competition-style mathematical benchmarks and a monotonically increasing performance trajectory during training.
- CIPO substantially enhanced error correction ability, boosting correction rates by **+7.74%** on mathematical tasks (CriticBench) and **+4.20%** on code debugging (DebugBench), with these correction capabilities generalizing to out-of-domain reasoning tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Converting ambiguous failure signals into precise, directional guidance by requiring the model to 'self-correct' its errors yields a richer and denser learning signal for policy optimization.
> - It is possible to derive fine-grained, corrective supervision directly from the model's own on-policy failed rollouts, eliminating the need for external annotations, process reward models, or LLM-based critics.
> - Adaptive control mechanisms for replay ratios and risk-averse reward shaping are crucial for maintaining learning stability and preventing the model from 'unlearning' correct behaviors while acquiring new error-correction skills.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has become a prominent paradigm for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs), particularly in domains such as mathematical reasoning and code generation. This approach benefits from automatically verifiable reward signals derived from on-policy rollouts, allowing for scalable training without extensive human annotation.

Despite its utility, existing RLVR methods, including algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), face limitations. These methods typically operate on a reinforce-suppress paradigm, where successful trajectories are reinforced and failed trajectories are penalized. However, the sparse and binary nature of verifiable rewards often leads to ambiguous optimization signals and weak credit assignment, especially in long-horizon reasoning tasks. All failures are treated uniformly as negative signals, regardless of their specific error modes or their logical proximity to a correct solution. This approach can result in the underutilization of potentially valuable information embedded in partially correct reasoning steps within failed trajectories, potentially hindering effective exploration and leading to suboptimal generalization.

Previous research efforts to address these challenges have involved integrating auxiliary models or additional supervisory signals. These include process reward models (PRMs) that evaluate intermediate steps, LLM-based critics for corrective feedback, or methods leveraging environmental feedback to guide credit assignment via teacher model distributions (e.g., Self-Distillation Policy Optimization, SDPO). However, these solutions often introduce their own drawbacks, such as the need for additional manual labeling, increased computational resources, or the potential for auxiliary models to introduce noise, biases, or suppress epistemic uncertainty, thereby undermining generalizability.

This work, Correction-OrIented Policy Optimization (CIPO), is positioned as a task-agnostic solution that aims to overcome these limitations. It seeks to provide fine-grained, directional optimization signals without requiring external annotations, process reward models, or LLM-based critics. By focusing on transforming the information from the model's own failed trajectories into explicit correction-oriented supervision, CIPO extends the RLVR paradigm to improve both learning effectiveness and the model's intrinsic error-correction capabilities, while operating strictly within the on-policy data distribution of the model.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the effectiveness of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) training for large language models (LLMs) by converting on-policy failed trajectories into valuable, correction-oriented supervision.

The motivation for this objective stems from several identified limitations of current RLVR approaches:

1. **Sparse and Binary Rewards:** RLVR relies on simple binary outcomes (success or failure) for reward signals. This sparsity provides limited information, particularly in complex, multi-step reasoning tasks, making credit assignment difficult. A failed long reasoning chain offers little indication of where the error occurred or how to correct it.
2. **Weak Credit Assignment and Ambiguous Optimization Signals:** When a trajectory fails, current methods uniformly penalize the entire sequence. This "reinforce-suppress" paradigm treats all failures identically, failing to differentiate between various error modes. For instance, a minor miscalculation in the final step is penalized as severely as a fundamental logical flaw, despite the former containing substantially more useful intermediate reasoning. This ambiguity leads to inefficient learning, as the model does not receive explicit guidance on how to fix specific errors.
3. **Underutilization of Failed Trajectories:** Failed rollouts often contain partially correct reasoning or "near-miss" attempts that hold valuable learning signals. Existing methods typically discard or merely suppress these trajectories, wasting potentially rich information that could guide the model towards correct solutions.
4. **Reliance on External Signals in Prior Solutions:** Previous attempts to address sparse rewards have involved integrating external process reward models or LLM-based critics. These solutions introduce challenges related to the cost of manual labeling, increased computational overhead, and the potential for auxiliary models to introduce noise, biases, or limit generalizability. The aim is to achieve similar benefits without incurring these external dependencies.
5. **Enhancing Intrinsic Reasoning and Correction Capabilities:** Beyond improving overall success rates, the authors are motivated to foster the LLM's intrinsic reasoning capacity and its ability to self-correct. By training the model to refine its own erroneous attempts, the objective is not only to make it more accurate but also more robust and capable of debugging, thus extending its practical applicability.

In summary, the core motivation is to move beyond the simplistic "reinforce-suppress" model by intelligently leveraging failed attempts to provide specific, directional, and internally generated learning signals. This approach is intended to make RLVR training more efficient, stable, and capable of truly improving the LLM's underlying reasoning and error-correction abilities without relying on external or costly annotations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Correction-OrIented Policy Optimization (CIPO) is proposed as an extension to the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) paradigm. Its core methodology involves transforming on-policy failed trajectories into corrective supervisory signals without the need for external information or annotations.

#### 3.1. Overall Procedure

CIPO operates through an iterative cycle of generation and correction-oriented replay, as depicted in Figure 2. At each training step, the policy $\pi_{\theta}$ is optimized using two distinct data streams:
1. **Base Stream:** This stream consists of standard on-policy rollouts ($y_i$) generated from original queries ($x \sim D$). These are evaluated for correctness using the verifiable reward function.
2. **Correction Stream:** This stream involves refinement rollouts ($y'_i$) generated by conditioning the policy on the original query ($x$) concatenated with a previously generated (and potentially erroneous) trajectory ($y$). The concatenation uses a specific template to instruct the model to provide a solution, referencing the candidate solution ($y_{cand}$). This process is referred to as constructing correction pairs.

The objective is then jointly optimized with a standard RLVR objective, specifically GRPO in this implementation. This joint optimization is designed to convert ambiguous suppression signals from failures into precise directional guidance, as illustrated in Figure 1. By successfully refining an error into a correct solution, the model establishes a distinct gradient path between the failure mode and the desired goal state. This process inherently differentiates failure modes; "near-miss" attempts are more likely to be refined successfully than fundamentally flawed ones, providing richer and denser signals.

#### 3.2. Adaptive Replay with Risk-Averse Shaping

To ensure stable and efficient learning, CIPO incorporates two complementary mechanisms to manage the integration of failed trajectories:

- **Adaptive Replay Ratio:** A dynamic replay ratio, $\rho_t \in [\rho_{min}, \rho_{max}]$, is maintained to balance learning from failed trajectories with retaining previously acquired capabilities. This ratio dictates the mixture of successful and failed trajectories in the correction stream. It is adjusted based on the model’s recent retention performance on recycled successful samples. If performance degrades or continues to decline, the proportion of successful trajectories is increased. If performance remains stable and high, more emphasis is placed on failed trajectories. This feedback-based mechanism (detailed in Algorithm 3 in Appendix A.2) aims to prevent policy degradation by controlling the curriculum at a macro level.
- **Risk-Averse Reward Shaping:** This mechanism introduces an asymmetric penalty to explicitly constrain against capability regressions. A specific penalty, $R_{risk}(x, y, y') = R(x, y') - \lambda_{risk} \cdot I[R(x, y) = 1 \land R(x, y') = 0]$, is applied when the conditioning trajectory ($y$) was correct, but the new response ($y'$) is incorrect. This penalty prioritizes the preservation of existing correct behaviors while allowing for the acquisition of new ones, providing micro-level guidance to prevent the model from "unlearning" correct solutions when attempting to correct errors.

#### 3.3. Difficulty-Aware Trajectories Preference

To improve training efficiency, CIPO employs a mechanism that prioritizes replaying prompts within a "medium-difficulty" range. This is based on the premise that consistently solved (too easy) or consistently failed (too hard) prompts may offer limited learning signals and waste computational resources. The set of prioritized prompts $X_{med}$ is defined as those where the empirical pass rate $\hat{P}(x)$ falls between specified thresholds, $\delta_{low}$ and $\delta_{high}$ (Eq. 2). Algorithm 2 in Appendix A outlines this strategy, which samples from the full distribution $D$ as a fallback if insufficient medium-difficulty prompts are available. This approach aims to focus learning on the most informative samples, aligning with the "zone of proximal development" concept.

#### 3.4. Training Objective

The combined objective function ($J_{CIPO}(\theta)$) integrates both the base and correction rollouts:

$J_{CIPO}(\theta) = E_{x \sim D} \left[ \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^{m} A^{(i)} \log \pi_{\theta}(y^{(i)}|x) \right] + \lambda E_{(x,y_c,r_c) \sim X_{rec}} \left[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} A'^{(i)} \log \pi_{\theta}(y'^{(i)}|x, y_c) \right]$

Here, advantages ($A^{(i)}$ and $A'^{(i)}$) are computed separately within each group of rollouts (base and correction). The correction rewards ($\tilde{r}_i$) are shaped by the risk-averse mechanism. The hyperparameter $\lambda > 0$ controls the relative importance of the correction rollouts.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details (Section 4.1)

- **Training Datasets:** DeepScalerR (40,000 math problems) and curated verifiable prompts from AM-DeepSeek-Distilled-40M (370,000 Python code problems).
- **Base LLMs:** Qwen3-4B (mathematical reasoning) and Seed-Coder-8B (code generation), both in instruct mode.
- **Framework:** VERL framework for RL training.
- **Training Configuration:** Batch size of 128 questions, 8 responses per question during rollout (temperature=1.0, top-p=1.0, max 4096 tokens). Learning rate $1 \times 10^{-6}$, KL loss coefficient $1 \times 10^{-4}$. Trained for 500 steps.
- **CIPO Specific Hyperparameters:** $\lambda=1$, correction batch size of 128, 8 correction rollouts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across 11 benchmarks in mathematical reasoning and code generation demonstrated that CIPO consistently outperformed strong baselines.

#### 4.1. Reasoning Performance

CIPO exhibited significant improvements in the model's reasoning capabilities:
- **Mathematical Reasoning (Qwen3-4B):** CIPO achieved an average accuracy of 64.38% across six mathematical benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, MATH500, Minerva, Olympiad), representing a 4.55% improvement over GRPO. The gains were particularly pronounced on challenging datasets like AIME24 and AIME25.
- **Code Generation (Seed-Coder-8B):** CIPO demonstrated stable improvements on LiveCodeBench v6 and LeetCode problems, achieving an average accuracy of 54.27%, outperforming GRPO by 1.43%.
- **Computational Budget:** Under matched computational budgets, CIPO still surpassed GRPO (Batch Size=256) by 4.72% on average, indicating that the performance gains are primarily attributable to the algorithmic design rather than increased resource allocation.

#### 4.2. Expansion of Intrinsic Reasoning Capabilities (Pass@K)

The evaluation of pass@K metrics, which assesses the model's ability to generate a correct solution within a fixed number of samples, suggests that CIPO genuinely expands the model's intrinsic reasoning capacity rather than merely redistributing probability mass over existing correct answers:
- **Mathematical Tasks (Pass@32):** CIPO outperformed vanilla GRPO by 6.12% on competition-style mathematical benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23).
- **Code Generation Training Dynamics (Pass@8):** CIPO maintained a robust and monotonically increasing performance trajectory throughout training, in contrast to the saturation and fluctuation observed in the GRPO baseline. This indicates continuous exploration of diverse solutions and a substantial enhancement of reasoning capacity.

#### 4.3. Enhanced Correction Ability

CIPO substantially improved the model's ability to detect and rectify errors:
- **CriticBench (Math):** CIPO boosted the correction rate by 7.74%, surpassing GRPO by 4.67%.
- **DebugBench (Code):** CIPO achieved a 4.20% gain in code debugging performance, outperforming GRPO (+2.53%). The performance matched Claude-Sonnet-4 and surpassed Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, with consistent gains across different completeness settings ("w/ Intent," "w/ Full Intent," "w/o Intent").

#### 4.4. Cross-Scenario Generalization of Error Correction

The error correction capabilities acquired through CIPO training demonstrated transferability to out-of-domain reasoning tasks:
- **Out-of-Domain Tasks (CriticBench):** A model trained solely on mathematical data using CIPO showed substantial correction gains in symbolic and algorithmic reasoning. CIPO also enhanced critique performance across all out-of-domain categories. This suggests that CIPO fosters general critique and correction capabilities rather than task-specific overfitting, enabling transfer to new reasoning environments.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study

Ablation studies were conducted to ascertain the contribution of CIPO's key components:
- **On-policy vs. Offline Replay:** CIPO, with its on-policy correction mechanism, significantly outperformed a variant that relied solely on offline replay of trajectories collected at initialization. CIPO showed an additional 3.91% gain, confirming the importance of dynamically correcting errors within the current policy distribution.
- **Adaptive-control:** Removing the adaptive replay ratio mechanism and using a fixed 1:1 ratio for mixing successful and failed trajectories resulted in a 4.19% performance drop compared to the full CIPO model. This validates the importance of adaptively balancing exploration (from failures) and exploitation (retaining successes).
- **Risk-averse Reward Shaping:** The removal of risk-averse reward shaping led to the most severe performance degradation, with overall accuracy plummeting by 6.97%. Performance declines were observed across all datasets, indicating the mechanism's critical role in preventing capability regressions and maintaining model stability.
- **Difficulty-aware Preference:** Removing the difficulty-aware preference mechanism, which prioritizes prompts with moderate pass rates, led to consistent performance declines across all benchmarks. This supports the hypothesis that focusing on a specific learning difficulty range improves training efficiency.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces CIPO, a method that addresses notable challenges within the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) paradigm for large language models. The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

**Enhanced Learning from Failures:** CIPO offers a novel approach to converting failed trajectories from mere negative signals into actionable, correction-oriented supervision. This directly counters the issues of sparse binary rewards and weak credit assignment that limit traditional RLVR methods. By differentiating failure modes through refinement sampling, CIPO extracts richer and more directional learning signals, which can lead to more efficient and targeted policy optimization.

**Improved Intrinsic Reasoning Capabilities:** The empirical results, particularly the higher pass@K gains and sustained performance improvement during training, suggest that CIPO contributes to a genuine expansion of the model's intrinsic reasoning capacity. This is distinct from simply redistributing probability mass over existing correct solutions. Models trained with CIPO may therefore be capable of solving a broader range of complex problems independently.

**Development of Error Correction Skills:** CIPO explicitly trains the model's ability to correct its own errors. This is a crucial advancement, as it enables LLMs to not only generate solutions but also to identify and rectify flaws in their outputs, a capability that extends beyond initial generation performance. This "self-debugging" skill has practical implications for robustness and reliability in applications like code generation and complex problem-solving.

**Efficiency and Scalability:** A key advantage of CIPO is its ability to derive supervisory signals from the model's own on-policy failures without requiring external human annotations, process reward models, or LLM-based critics. This reduces reliance on costly and potentially biased auxiliary systems, making the training process more scalable and self-sufficient within the RLVR framework. The adaptive replay and difficulty-aware preference mechanisms further enhance training efficiency and stability.

**Robust Generalization:** The demonstrated generalizability of correction capabilities across in-domain and out-of-domain tasks (e.g., math-trained models improving in symbolic and algorithmic reasoning) suggests that CIPO fosters more fundamental and transferable error-correction and critique skills, rather than merely task-specific overfitting. This implies that models trained with CIPO could be more versatile across different reasoning environments.

In summary, CIPO provides a systematic, internally driven mechanism for LLMs to learn from their mistakes. This approach could lead to the development of more capable, robust, and autonomous AI systems that are better equipped to handle complex reasoning tasks and self-diagnose issues, thereby broadening the applicability and reliability of LLMs in various domains.
