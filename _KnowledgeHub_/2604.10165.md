# MoRI: Mixture of RL and IL Experts for Long-Horizon Manipulation Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> The MoRI framework integrates Reinforcement Learning and Imitation Learning within a Mixture-of-Experts architecture for complex, long-horizon robotic manipulation tasks. It achieved an average success rate of 97.5% across four real-world tasks, reducing training time by 21.0% and human intervention by 85.8% compared to baseline methods.

## Problem

- Traditional Imitation Learning (IL) policies suffer from compounding errors and distribution shift in novel states, limiting robustness for precise or novel interactions.
- Pure Reinforcement Learning (RL) approaches have low sample efficiency, slow convergence, and high costs/risks for extensive real-world exploration in complex, long-horizon tasks.
- Existing hybrid methods often lack full validation for long-horizon tasks, can incur high computational costs, or still require substantial human intervention during refinement.

## Method

- **MoRI** employs a two-stage approach: advantage-weighted offline pre-training for policy initialization and ==Mixture-of-Experts== (==MoE==) driven online fine-tuning.
- A gating network dynamically selects between a Behavioral Cloning (BC) expert for coarse movements and a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) expert for fine-grained manipulations, leveraging action variance as a confidence measure.
- The SAC expert's exploration is regularized by the BC policy to maintain safety and efficiency, while a Discrete BC (DBC) policy handles gripper control.

## Results

- MoRI achieved an average success rate of **97.5%** across four complex tasks, outperforming individual BC (**37.5%**) and RL (**77.5%**) experts, and a baseline RL model (**57.5%**).
- The framework reduced average training time by **21.0%** (from 273.8 to 216.3 minutes) and decreased human intervention by **85.8%** compared to the baseline.
- Analysis showed the BC expert was chosen for deterministic, coarse motions, while the RL expert was increasingly engaged for fine-grained manipulations, with its call proportion rising from 50-**60%** to 70-**80%** over training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically assigning sub-tasks to specialized IL and RL experts based on action variance can significantly improve performance and efficiency in long-horizon manipulation.
> - BC regularization of the RL policy is crucial for safe and sample-efficient exploration in physical robots, preventing divergence and ensuring motion continuity during expert transitions.
> - A dedicated Discrete BC (DBC) policy for gripper control offers superior robustness and convergence compared to Deep Q-Networks (DQN) for multi-step discrete actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation tasks, particularly those involving long horizons, often necessitate a combination of coarse, high-level movements and precise, contact-rich manipulations. The current research landscape in policy acquisition for such tasks is primarily dominated by two paradigms: Imitation Learning (IL) and Reinforcement Learning (RL).

**Imitation Learning (IL)** offers an efficient approach to derive policies from expert demonstrations. It is effective for acquiring fundamental behaviors and can provide a strong initial policy. However, IL policies are inherently limited by the distributional coverage of the demonstration data. This limitation can lead to compounding errors and distribution shift during execution, making IL less robust for tasks requiring high precision or novel environmental interactions.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL)**, in contrast, optimizes policies through autonomous trial-and-error exploration guided by reward signals. RL agents can learn to perform tasks beyond the scope of initial demonstrations and exhibit robustness, particularly in contact-rich scenarios. Recent advancements in Human-in-the-Loop RL (HIL-RL) have allowed real-time human intervention to refine policies on physical robots. Despite these benefits, traditional RL often suffers from low sample efficiency, slow convergence, and the high cost and potential for hardware damage during extensive real-world exploration. Managing complex, long-horizon tasks further compounds these challenges for pure RL approaches.

To address the limitations of standalone IL and RL, hybrid methods have emerged. These typically involve:
- **Offline pre-training with online fine-tuning:** Utilizing offline RL (e.g., Implicit Q-Learning, Advantage-Weighted Actor-Critic) to initialize policies from existing datasets, followed by online interaction for refinement. While improving sample efficiency, these methods are not always fully validated on long-horizon tasks and can still face high computational costs, especially with Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.
- **Residual RL frameworks:** Building a lightweight RL module on top of a pre-trained Behavioral Cloning (BC) policy to provide corrective adjustments.
- **Iterative offline and online RL:** Combining these stages, sometimes with diffusion policies, to reduce human intervention, though convergence times can remain a bottleneck (e.g., 14 hours per task).

**Mixture of Experts (MoE)** architectures represent another relevant area, focusing on decomposing complex tasks into specialized sub-tasks managed by different "experts." MoE has been applied in various robotic contexts, such as multi-skill learning for quadrupedal robots and real-time response scenarios like microsurgery. However, the application of MoE to dynamically schedule heterogeneous experts (like IL and RL) for long-horizon manipulation, particularly by leveraging action variance for gating, has been less explored.

This work fits into this landscape by proposing a novel hybrid framework that combines the strengths of IL and RL within an MoE architecture. It seeks to provide a more efficient, robust, and less intervention-intensive solution for complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks by dynamically assigning sub-tasks to the most suitable learning paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient and robust framework for robotic manipulation that can effectively handle complex, long-horizon tasks requiring both coarse movements and fine-grained, contact-rich manipulations. The authors aim to overcome the inherent limitations of standalone Imitation Learning (IL) and Reinforcement Learning (RL), as well as the shortcomings of existing hybrid methods.

The key motivations and objectives include:

- **Mitigating Compounding Errors and Distribution Shift in IL:** While IL offers efficient policy derivation from demonstrations, its performance is constrained by the data distribution, leading to error accumulation when encountering novel states. The research aims to integrate RL to allow the agent to explore beyond demonstrated data and refine policies.
- **Addressing Low Sample Efficiency and High Cost of RL:** Pure RL often requires extensive trial-and-error exploration, which is time-consuming, sample-inefficient, and potentially damaging in real-world robotic deployments. The authors are motivated to accelerate convergence and reduce the need for costly real-world interactions.
- **Reducing Human Intervention:** Human-in-the-Loop RL (HIL-RL) can improve policy performance but still demands significant human effort. A core objective is to minimize human intervention during policy refinement and autonomous operation.
- **Improving Robustness and Generalization for Complex Tasks:** Many tasks involve a sequence of diverse sub-tasks. A single learning paradigm often struggles to balance efficiency, accuracy, and generalization across such varied requirements. The research aims to create a system that can robustly adapt to different phases of a complex task.
- **Accelerating Convergence with Hybrid Training:** The authors seek to leverage the benefits of both offline pre-training (for quick initial policy acquisition) and online fine-tuning (for adaptation to real-world dynamics and overcoming data limitations).
- **Dynamic Specialization of Experts:** The motivation extends to dynamically assigning different parts of a task to specialized experts. Specifically, for coarse, deterministic movements where human demonstrations are reliable, an IL expert would be suitable. For fine-grained, uncertain, or contact-rich manipulations requiring exploration and adaptation, an RL expert would be more appropriate.
- **Ensuring Exploration Safety and Efficiency:** Unconstrained RL exploration can be unsafe for physical robots. The authors aim to regularize the RL policy with knowledge from IL to guide exploration within safer and more relevant regions of the action space, thereby increasing sample efficiency and reducing risks.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for a practical, efficient, and robust solution for real-world long-horizon robotic manipulation that balances the strengths of IL and RL through a dynamic, specialized architecture while minimizing human effort and training time.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, MoRI (Mixture of RL and IL Experts), addresses long-horizon manipulation tasks by integrating Behavioral Cloning (BC) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) within a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture. The system operates in two stages: offline pre-training and online fine-tuning.

**A. Stage I: Advantage-Weighted Offline Pre-training**
This initial stage aims to provide a robust policy initialization using a limited dataset of 20 human demonstration samples (`D_demo`). This pre-training phase mitigates the inefficiencies of random exploration in RL and the initial fragility of IL. The MoE component remains untrained during this stage to prevent overfitting.

1. **Behavioral Cloning (BC) Policy Initialization:**
 - A BC policy, $\pi_{BC}(a|s)$, is trained using a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss on the `D_demo` dataset. This establishes a baseline for coarse motions.
 - $L_{BC}(\theta_{bc}) = E_{(s,a)\sim D_{demo}} ||\pi_{\theta_{bc}}(s) - a ||^2_2$
 - Here, $\theta_{bc}$ are the parameters for the BC policy, $\pi_{\theta_{bc}}(s)$ is the predicted end-effector pose increment, and $a$ is the target end-effector pose increment from demonstrations.

2. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Policy Initialization:**
 - The RL policy is initialized using the Advantage-Weighted Actor-Critic (AWAC) algorithm on `D_demo`. This approach balances knowledge from demonstrations with exploratory capabilities.
 - **Critic Network Optimization:** The critic network $Q_{\phi}(s, a)$ is optimized by minimizing an MSE loss.
 - $L_Q(\phi) = E_{(s,a,s',r)\sim D_{demo}} [ (Q_{\phi}(s, a) - y)^2 ]$
 - The target value $y = r(s, a) + \gamma E_{a'\sim\pi_{\theta_{rl}}(a|s)} [Q_{\bar{\phi}}(s', a')]$ employs clipped double-Q learning to mitigate overestimation.
 - **Actor Network Optimization:** The actor network $\pi_{\theta_{rl}}(a|s)$ is updated via advantage weighting.
 - $L_{RL}(\theta_{rl}) = -E_{(s,a)\sim D_{demo}} [ \log \pi_{\theta_{rl}}(a|s) \exp( \frac{1}{\lambda} A(s, a)) ]$
 - $A(s, a)$ is the advantage function, and $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter scaling the weighting intensity.

3. **Discrete BC (DBC) Policy Initialization for Gripper Control:**
 - To address slow convergence in discrete gripper control with Deep Q-Networks (DQN), a discrete BC policy $\pi_{DBC}(a_g|s)$ is introduced. This policy is trained with a cross-entropy objective on `D_demo` for gripper actions ($a_g$: open, hold, closed).
 - $L_{DBC}(\theta_{dbc}) = -E_{(s,a_g)\sim D_{demo}} [ \log \pi_{\theta_{dbc}}(a_g|s) ]$

**B. Stage II: Mixture-of-Experts Driven Online Fine-tuning**
The online stage continuously refines the policies and the MoE gating network using three evolving datasets:
- `D_demo`: Initialized with offline data, updated with human interventions.
- `D_success`: Builds upon `D_demo` by incorporating successful trajectories from online training.
- `D_replay`: An empty buffer at the start, stores real-time interaction data, including MoE-selected actions and human corrections.

1. **BC and DBC Expert Update:**
 - The loss functions $L_{BC}(\theta_{bc})$ and $L_{DBC}(\theta_{dbc})$ remain the same as in Stage I, but training data $D_{bc}$ is uniformly sampled from `D_success` and `D_demo`.

2. **Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) Expert Update:**
 - The RL component transitions to a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) framework for online optimization.
 - **Critic Loss:**
 - $L_Q(\phi) = E_{(s,a,s',r)\sim D_{rl}} [ (Q_{\phi}(s, a) - (r + \gamma E_{a'\sim\pi_{\theta_{rl}}(a|s)} [Q_{\bar{\phi}}(s', a')]))^2 ]$
 - $D_{rl}$ is sampled with equal probability from `D_replay` and `D_demo`.
 - **Actor Loss with BC Regularization:**
 - $L_{RL}(\theta_{rl}) = -E_{s\sim D_{rl}} [ E_{a\sim\pi_{\theta_{rl}}(a|s)} [Q_{\phi}(s, a)] + \alpha H(\pi_{\theta_{rl}}(\cdot | s)) - \beta ||\pi_{\theta_{rl}}(s) - \pi_{\theta_{bc}}(s) ||^2_2 ]$
 - This loss balances Q-value maximization, entropy regularization (controlled by $\alpha$) for exploration, and a crucial BC regularization term (controlled by $\beta$). The regularization term ensures the RL agent's exploration remains close to the BC policy's distribution, promoting safety and efficiency.

3. **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Gating Network Optimization:**
 - A gating network $g_{\psi}(s)$ determines weights $w_{bc}$ and $w_{rl}$ for the BC and RL experts, respectively, such that $w_{bc} + w_{rl} = 1$.
 - The MoE loss function $L_{MoE}(\psi)$ guides expert routing, incorporating several terms:
 - **Weighted Variance Loss:** $w_{bc} \cdot \sigma_{bc}(s) + w_{rl} \cdot \sigma_{rl}(s)$, where $\sigma_{bc}(s)$ and $\sigma_{rl}(s)$ are the variance of action outputs from each expert. This term uses variance as a proxy for expert confidence.
 - **Specialization Loss:** $\alpha \cdot (0.5 - |w_{bc} - 0.5|)$, encouraging experts to specialize.
 - **Load Balancing Loss:** $\beta \cdot [(E[w_{bc}] - 0.5)^2 + (E[w_{rl}] - 0.5)^2]$, promoting balanced utilization of experts.
 - **Entropy Regularization:** $\gamma \cdot (-H(g_{\psi}(\cdot | s)))$, preventing premature convergence of the gating mechanism.
 - $D_{bc}$ is used for MoE optimization.

4. **Dynamic Expert Action Selection:**
 - The MoRI strategy employs a hard selection mechanism:
 - $a = \pi_{\theta_{bc}}(s)$, if $w_{bc} > w_{rl}$
 - $a = \pi_{\theta_{rl}}(s)$, otherwise
 - This selected end-effector action is then combined with the discrete gripper command $a_g$ from the DBC expert to form the final control output.

The method focuses on a robotic arm (Franka Research 3) with visual (three cameras) and multi-modal sensor data (end-effector poses, velocities, contact forces). The action space is 7-dimensional (6D incremental end-effector pose + binary gripper command). A binary classifier, trained on human demonstrations, provides reward feedback.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The MoRI framework was evaluated across four complex real-world manipulation tasks: "Place Block in Drawer," "Put Towel in Lidded Box," "Insert Two Sockets," and "Double-Fold the Towel." The experiments addressed specific research questions to assess the framework's performance, efficiency, and underlying mechanisms.

**Q1. Does MoRI enhance manipulation performance relative to the baseline RL model?**
MoRI demonstrated enhanced performance compared to ConRFT, a baseline RL model. On average, MoRI reduced training time by 21.0% (from 273.8 minutes for ConRFT to 216.3 minutes). MoRI achieved an average success rate of 97.5% across all tasks, significantly higher than ConRFT's 57.5%. The method also showed improved data utilization and reduced reliance on human demonstrations. The proportion of demonstration data in MoRI's replay buffer averaged 7.0%, compared to 49.6% for ConRFT, while the proportion of successful autonomous trajectories increased by 111.7% to 78.3%. Learning curves indicated faster convergence for MoRI with fewer total interventions.

**Q2. Can MoRI outperform its individual sub-experts?**
MoRI consistently outperformed its individual sub-experts (BC and RL policies). The average success rate for MoRI was 97.5%, substantially higher than BC's 37.5% and RL's 77.5%. In terms of efficiency, MoRI required an average of 161.4 steps per episode, fewer than BC (206.0 steps) and RL (174.9 steps). This suggests that MoRI’s adaptive scheduling effectively leverages the complementary strengths of RL and BC.

**Q3. In what manner does MoRI schedule different experts?**
Analysis of the drawer storage task revealed a dynamic scheduling pattern. The BC expert was primarily selected for deterministic, coarse movements with low fitting variance, such as opening or closing the drawer. In contrast, the RL expert was more frequently engaged for fine-grained manipulations, such as picking and placing, which are sensitive to target randomness and benefit from dense Q-value guidance, exhibiting lower variance in such contexts. Over the course of training, the proportion of RL expert calls increased from 50%–60% to 70%–80%. This shift is attributed to MoRI's load balancing being tied to the dataset distribution. As RL-driven exploration and human interventions evolve the action distribution, the RL expert, bolstered by accumulated data and Q-network guidance, demonstrated superior adaptability, leading to its increased utilization.

**Q4. How does BC regularization influence performance and the smoothness of expert switching?**
Ablation studies on the drawer storage task demonstrated the importance of BC regularization. Without it, human intervention increased from 5.5% to 45.4%, and training duration extended from 142 minutes to 221 minutes. The success rate dropped from 100% to 70%, and the autonomous success ratio decreased from 85.4% to 41.9%. These results indicate that BC regularization restricts RL exploration to reasonable regions, mitigating slow convergence. Furthermore, BC regularization contributed to motion continuity during expert transitions. With regularization, the end-effector position change during expert switching was approximately 0.017 m. Without regularization, position shifts exceeded 0.029 m during RL-to-BC transitions, with a standard deviation of 0.031 m, indicating significantly less smooth transitions.

**Q5. How does the performance of the gripper expert differ between DQN and DBC?**
The Discrete BC (DBC) expert for gripper control significantly outperformed a Deep Q-Network (DQN) alternative. While DQN resulted in a slightly shorter average episode length (132.1 vs. 161.4 steps for MoRI with DBC), its average success rate was only 72.5%, compared to DBC's 97.5%. DQN also required a higher intervention rate (23.4% vs. 7.0%) to achieve a lower autonomous success rate (60.0% vs. 78.3%) and took longer to converge (323.3 minutes vs. 216.3 minutes). For tasks involving multiple gripper switches, such as putting a towel in a lidded box, the DQN expert failed to converge, highlighting DBC's robustness for complex gripper control sequences.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MoRI framework represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly for long-horizon and complex tasks. Its primary significance lies in demonstrating an effective integration of Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Imitation Learning (IL) through a dynamic Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture.

**Key Contributions and Impact:**

- **Improved Efficiency and Reduced Intervention:** MoRI reduces training convergence time by 21% and decreases human intervention by 85.8% compared to a baseline. This addresses critical limitations of real-world RL, where extensive human supervision and long training durations are common bottlenecks. The ability to achieve high success rates (97.5%) with minimal human input has practical implications for deploying robots in various industrial and service settings.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** By dynamically switching between IL for coarse, deterministic movements and RL for fine-grained, uncertain manipulations, MoRI capitalizes on the strengths of each paradigm. This allows the system to adapt to diverse operational requirements, from storage tasks to high-precision assembly and flexible object manipulation, leading to a robust policy that consistently outperforms individual experts.
- **Safe and Efficient Exploration:** The IL-based regularization applied to the RL component is crucial for maintaining safety during exploration on physical robots. By constraining the RL agent's search space to reasonable regions informed by expert demonstrations, MoRI improves sample efficiency and mitigates the risk of hardware damage or catastrophic failures, which are common concerns in real-world RL.
- **Practical Solution for Hybrid Learning:** The framework provides a practical and validated approach for balancing policy efficiency and stability in manipulation. The dynamic expert fusion strategy, guided by action variance, offers a generalizable method for integrating disparate learning algorithms to handle task decomposition effectively.
- **Advancements in Gripper Control:** The introduction of Discrete BC (DBC) for gripper control demonstrates a more robust and efficient alternative to traditional DQN methods for discrete actions, especially in multi-step manipulation sequences. This finding has direct implications for improving the reliability of gripper operations in complex tasks.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge limitations regarding generalization and autonomous recovery under significant perturbations. Future work aims to:
- **Hierarchical Systems with Vision-Language Models (VLMs):** Evolve the dual-expert architecture into a hierarchical system, leveraging VLMs for high-level reasoning and zero-shot transfer, which could improve generalization to novel tasks and environments.
- **World Models for Simulated Imagination:** Incorporate World Models to partially replace physical trials with simulated imagination, reducing the intensive human labor and operational costs associated with real-world training.
- **Online Continual Learning and Sparse Expert Activation:** Integrate these concepts to manage even longer-horizon tasks and facilitate more autonomous, large-scale deployment of robotic systems.

Overall, MoRI demonstrates a viable pathway for combining the strengths of different learning paradigms to address persistent challenges in robotic manipulation, moving closer to autonomous and highly capable robotic systems in complex environments.
