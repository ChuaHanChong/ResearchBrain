# Programmable Synchronization Graphs for Adaptive and Fault-Tolerant Modular Miniature Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> A programmable synchronization-graph framework enables adaptive and fault-tolerant coordination in modular miniature robots. This system employs signed graph links to program gait phase and an online learning algorithm to adapt inter-module coupling, achieving stable synchronization in heterogeneous physical collectives and demonstrating superior fault tolerance compared to centralized leader-follower control.

## Problem

- Coordinating numerous imperfect actuator-sensor modules in miniature robots under tight constraints on power, cost, volume, and reliability in constrained environments.
- Scalability challenges with conventional centralized control architectures, which struggle to maintain high-bandwidth communication and leader-follower coordination as system size and functional complexity increase.
- The difficulty of dynamically programming or learning phase relationships, gait modes, and network connectivity in traditional analog oscillator systems without requiring physical redesign.

## Method

- Introduced a **programmable synchronization-graph** framework where each actuator-sensor pair acts as a network node and locomotor coordination is encoded through graph coupling based on the ==Kuramoto model==.
- Implemented fixed ==intra-subgraph links== for coherent motion within groups of actuators (e.g., left/right side) and a small number of ==signed inter-subgraph links== to program phase relationships between these groups, enabling gait selection.
- Developed an online **Contextual Bandit Edge-selection (CBE)** algorithm with an ==Upper Confidence Bound (UCB)== rule to autonomously select inter-subgraph links, adapting to achieve target phase states and compensating for hardware variability.

## Results

- Successfully synchronized heterogeneous physical actuators using Kuramoto coupling and programmed gait-like phase relationships (in-phase, out-of-phase, and intermediate) by varying the sign composition of inter-subgraph links.
- Demonstrated that sparse d-regular graph topologies (e.g., degree **3**) for **N=8** modules maintain synchronization, and that increasing graph degree from **3** to **6** improved fault tolerance from **2-3** to approximately **5** module deactivations before desynchronization.
- The online CBE algorithm adaptively formed inter-subgraph connections to achieve target phase states, and the graph-based controller reduced the worst-case phase-error RMSE by approximately **threefold** (from **1.88 ± 0.13 rad** to **0.65 ± 0.22 rad**) compared to a centralized leader-follower approach during sequential module deactivation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Network graph topology, specifically the composition and signs of inter-subgraph links, can function as a direct, programmable control layer for dynamically adjusting gait phase in modular robot collectives.
> - Sparse d-regular graph topologies are sufficient to maintain synchronization in modular robots, demonstrating that dense connectivity is not always required, and increased graph connectivity directly correlates with enhanced fault tolerance to module loss.
> - Distributed, graph-based control without a privileged leader offers a more robust control paradigm against module failure, avoiding single points of vulnerability present in centralized leader-follower architectures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Miniature robots are being developed for applications in constrained, cluttered, and communication-degraded environments, such as inspection, search-and-rescue, environmental monitoring, and security. As these systems decrease in size and increase in functional complexity, conventional control architectures face scalability challenges. Centralized computation, high-bandwidth communication, and leader-follower coordination become difficult to maintain under tight constraints in terms of power, cost, volume, and reliability. For modular miniature robots, this becomes a coordination problem: how to transform many imperfect actuator-sensor modules into a coherent locomotor system despite actuator variability, sensor drift, limited communication, and potential loss of individual modules.

One approach to address this is distributing capability across physically coupled modules. Modular and self-reconfigurable robotic systems offer versatility, scalability, and fault tolerance, enabling system-level functions that a single miniature unit might not achieve alone. Research has shown that changing morphology and inter-unit connectivity in small-scale collective robots can adapt locomotion and task performance. However, once capability is distributed, the primary design challenge shifts from constructing individual modules to establishing coordination, synchronization, and planning rules that allow the collective to function coherently with limited communication and connection strength.

Embodied and physical computing offer a potential solution, as computation can be partially carried out by the robot's body dynamics and interactions, reducing reliance on a large, centralized processor. Oscillator-based controllers, including central pattern generators (CPGs) and analog oscillator circuits, are particularly effective for rhythmic locomotion due to their ability to generate coordinated motion through simple, robust dynamics. However, a limitation of analog oscillator systems is their programmability and scalability. Frequency and phase relationships are often hard-wired by mechanical architecture and material properties, meaning reprogramming may require redesign rather than simple parameter tuning. Furthermore, fabrication tolerances can introduce oscillator mismatches that degrade synchronization as the system size increases.

This context highlights a gap in modular miniature robotics: a need for a controller that retains the robustness and natural coordination of oscillator networks but also allows for programmable or learned phase relationships, gait modes, network connectivity, and recovery behavior after fabrication. Such a controller should allow the network graph itself—its connectivity, edge signs, and adaptive link-selection rules—to serve as a programmable control object. Centralized policies can provide coordination but are vulnerable to single points of failure and often assign fixed roles like leader and follower. Decentralized approaches, such as CPGs and local behavioral rules, reduce fragility but do not inherently provide a general, topology-level method to program sparse coupling, switch gaits, and adapt links in response to performance. This work aims to fill this gap by proposing and experimentally demonstrating a framework where graph topology functions as a programmable control layer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation for this research is to address the challenge of coordinating numerous imperfect modules in modular miniature robots operating in constrained, cluttered, and communication-degraded environments, particularly under tight limits on computation, communication, and reliability. The goal is to achieve scalable function without relying on a privileged leader, prescribing a fixed gait template, or requiring dense communication.

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a programmable synchronization-graph framework:** Introduce a novel control framework where each actuator-sensor pair is represented as a network node, and locomotor coordination is encoded through graph coupling.
2. **Separate control functions for gait programming:** Implement fixed intra-subgraph links to establish coherent motion within groups of heterogeneous actuators (e.g., left or right side) and a small number of signed inter-subgraph links to program the phase relationships between these groups, thereby enabling gait-like motion selection.
3. **Validate synchronization in heterogeneous physical systems:** Experimentally demonstrate that graph coupling can effectively synchronize heterogeneous physical actuators despite inherent differences in their free-running frequencies.
4. **Demonstrate gait phase control through signed links:** Show that the sign composition of a limited number of inter-subgraph links can control the phase difference between actuator groups, producing in-phase, out-of-phase, and intermediate gait-like coordination.
5. **Investigate sparse network topologies:** Explore whether sparse d-regular graph topologies can preserve synchronization while reducing the number of required links, thus addressing the scalability limitations of dense, all-to-all coupling.
6. **Quantify fault tolerance based on network connectivity:** Determine how graph connectivity (specifically, graph degree) influences the robustness of the system to module loss, by measuring the number of module deactivations tolerated before desynchronization occurs.
7. **Implement and validate online edge learning:** Develop and test an online learning algorithm (Contextual Bandit Edge-selection with Upper Confidence Bound) that can autonomously select inter-subgraph links to guide the system towards target phase states, allowing for adaptive control in response to hardware variability.
8. **Compare robustness with centralized control:** Benchmark the fault tolerance of the proposed graph-based controller against a centralized leader-follower control architecture, specifically focusing on avoiding single-point failure modes and improving worst-case performance during module deactivation.

Ultimately, the research aims to establish programmable network topology as a practical and compact control layer for enabling gait phase programming, online adaptation, and robustness to unit loss in modular miniature robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a programmable synchronization-graph framework and validates it through a series of experiments using physically coupled modular robots.

**Robot Platform:**
The robot collective consists of modular units, each containing two C-shaped actuators (one left, one right) and a corresponding rotary encoder to report instantaneous angle. In the graph representation, each actuator-sensor pair functions as a "sensorimotor node." Thus, a platform with N robot modules comprises 2N network nodes. The physical module design and soft mechanical links were adopted from prior work by the authors. Mechanical coupling between modules was achieved using simple 3D-printed backbone elements of varying stiffness (elastic yellow and stiff black) to prevent entanglement and minimize uncontrolled mechanical synchronization during floor locomotion experiments.

**Graph Representation and Dynamics:**
The network graph is conceptually partitioned into left and right subgraphs, corresponding to the actuators on each side of the robot platform. This partitioning reflects the typical requirement for actuators on the same side to coordinate, while the relative phase between sides dictates gait-like motion.
The phase dynamics of each node *i* are governed by the Kuramoto model. Initial experiments utilized Equation (2) for intra-subgraph coupling, and Equation (3) for combined intra- and inter-subgraph coupling:
- $ \frac{d\theta_i}{dt} = \omega_i + \frac{k}{N} \sum_{j \in \text{in-subgraph}} \sin(\theta_j - \theta_i) $ (Equation 2, intra-subgraph)
- $ \frac{d\theta_i}{dt} = \omega_i + \frac{k}{N} \sum_{j \in \text{in-subgraph}} \sin(\theta_j - \theta_i) + \frac{g}{M} \sum_{l \in \mathcal{N}(i)} \sin(\theta_l - \theta_i) $ (Equation 3, combined)
Here, $\omega_i$ is the uncoupled (free-running) rotation frequency of unit *i*, *k* is the intra-subgraph coupling strength, *g* is the inter-subgraph coupling strength, and $\mathcal{N}(i)$ denotes the set of neighbors of node *i* in the opposite subgraph.
To emulate actuator variability in miniature robots, each actuator was assigned a natural frequency drawn from a Gaussian distribution (mean 0.8 Hz, standard deviation 0.2 Hz), and PWM values were calibrated to match these frequencies.

**Control Implementation:**
The graph-local update law was implemented on an ESP32 coordinator. This coordinator computed timing updates based on phase differences along graph edges and broadcasted commands to the modules, typically every 75 ms via ESP-NOW. This centralized infrastructure allowed for controlled experiments and repeatability, while still validating a *graph-local* control law, meaning the information exchanged between modules (phase differences) is the same as what would be exchanged in a truly distributed system. It specifically avoided imposing a leader trajectory or a fixed global gait template.

**Experimental Procedures:**

1. **Graph Coupling for Synchronization:**
 - Setup: N=7, 8, and 9 modules were placed in stationary holders to isolate electronic graph coupling from unintended mechanical synchronization.
 - Procedure: Each left and right subgraph was initialized with all-to-all Kuramoto coupling; no inter-subgraph coupling was present. The intra-subgraph coupling strength *k* was increased from 0 to 200 in steps of 5. For each non-zero *k*, the network ran for 8 seconds to assess synchronization.
 - Metric: Synchronization was quantified using the order parameter *r* (Equation 1), which approaches unity for synchronized systems. Critical coupling was defined as the point where *r* exceeded 0.90.

2. **Signed Inter-subgraph Links for Gait Phase Programming:**
 - Setup: N=9 modules, with all-to-all intra-subgraph couplings fixed. Six inter-subgraph links were introduced between predefined left-right pairs.
 - Procedure: The sign composition of these six inter-subgraph links was varied from all-negative to all-positive, one by one.
 - Metric: The relative phase difference between the left and right subgraphs was measured.
 - Floor Locomotion: For five coupled robot modules, this phase programming was tested on the floor. With positive inter-subgraph coupling, gallop-like (in-phase) coordination was expected; with negative coupling, trot-like (out-of-phase) coordination was expected.

3. **Sparse Topology and Coupling Burden:**
 - Setup: N=8 robot modules. Each left and right subgraph was initialized as a connected d-regular graph.
 - Procedure: The degree *d* was varied from 3 to 7 (d=7 being all-to-all connectivity). Ten random connected graphs were tested for each *d* in stationary holders.
 - Metric: The steady-state order parameter was measured to determine if synchronization was preserved with fewer links.

4. **Graph Connectivity and Fault Tolerance:**
 - Setup: N=8 robot modules, using d-regular networks (d=3 to 7).
 - Procedure: Randomly selected modules were "deactivated" (stopped sending/receiving updates; their last measured angles were used in calculations for active nodes). Deactivated nodes continued to move with their inherent dynamics.
 - Metric: The number of deactivated modules required to drive at least one subgraph's order parameter below a desynchronization threshold (*r* < 0.6, after having been > 0.8) was quantified across 20 trials for each degree *d*.

5. **Online Edge Learning (Contextual Bandit Edge-selection - CBE):**
 - Setup: A five-module network with randomized intrinsic frequencies and internally synchronized subgraphs (fixed intra-subgraph links). Initially, all inter-subgraph links were removed.
 - Algorithm: A Contextual Bandit Edge-selection (CBE) algorithm with an Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) rule was implemented. The algorithm evaluated candidate signed inter-subgraph links (positive or negative connections between corresponding left and right actuators).
 - Procedure: When a candidate link was activated, the network ran for 1.35 seconds, and the resulting phase difference between left and right subgraphs was measured. A "reward" was calculated based on the reduction in average phase error relative to a target phase. Links yielding a positive reward were kept; otherwise, they were removed. A lock-in condition required the phase error to remain within a prescribed tolerance (0.50 radians) for two consecutive decision cycles.

6. **Deactivation Performance Comparison (CBE vs. Leader-Follower - LF):**
 - Setup: The same five-robot platform was used for both CBE and LF control, aiming for synchronized out-of-phase motion (target $\pi$ phase difference). The robots were grouped mechanically (Robot #1 isolated, 2-3 attached, 4-5 attached).
 - LF Implementation: Robot #1 was designated as the leader. Its left leg was positively coupled to the left legs of followers and negatively to their right legs, and vice-versa for its right leg. The leader's internal L-R link was undirected; all other links were directed from leader to followers.
 - Deactivation Test: Robots 1 to 5 were deactivated sequentially for 10 seconds each, then reactivated for 10 seconds. This sequence was repeated for both algorithms over 10 trials.
 - Metric: A performance metric ($F_i$) was defined as the time-averaged absolute value of the difference vector between the complex order parameters of the left and right legs ($|r_L - r_R|$) during deactivation. A reduction in this metric indicated performance loss. Additionally, the Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of the phase difference deviation from the target $\pi$ was calculated and compared, after subtracting baseline errors.

Data processing involved filtering raw angle data (low-pass, band-pass) and rolling averaging for order parameter and phase difference calculations, as detailed in the Materials and Methods section.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the programmable synchronization-graph framework:

1. **Graph Coupling Synchronizes Heterogeneous Modules:** Experiments with N=7, 8, and 9 robot modules demonstrated that intra-subgraph Kuramoto coupling successfully synchronized heterogeneous physical actuators. As the coupling strength (*k*) increased, the order parameter (*r*) within both left and right subgraphs converged towards unity, indicating robust synchronization despite differences in individual actuator free-running frequencies. The critical coupling strength required for synchronization was observed to increase with the spread in natural frequencies of the actuators.

2. **Signed Inter-subgraph Links Program Gait Phase:** A small number of signed inter-subgraph links effectively programmed the relative phase between the left and right actuator groups. When all six inter-subgraph links were positive, the system converged to in-phase motion (e.g., gallop-like coordination). Conversely, when all links were negative, the system achieved an approximately $\pi$-shifted (out-of-phase) state, corresponding to trot-like coordination. Mixtures of positive and negative links produced intermediate phase offsets, indicating that gait phase can be directly controlled by a low-dimensional edge variable rather than pre-assigned module identities or handcrafted timing patterns. This phase programming was validated in floor locomotion experiments with a five-module robot, where distinct in-phase and out-of-phase gaits were observed.

3. **Sparse Topology Reduces Coupling Burden:** Investigation of sparse d-regular graph topologies for N=8 robot modules showed that synchronization could be maintained with fewer links compared to all-to-all coupling. Networks with lower degrees (e.g., d=3) still achieved high order parameters, demonstrating that dense connectivity is not strictly necessary for synchronization and that the coupling burden can be reduced while preserving coordinated motion.

4. **Graph Connectivity Determines Failure Tolerance:** Experiments on module loss revealed a direct relationship between graph connectivity (degree *d*) and robustness. Lower-degree networks (e.g., d=3) typically desynchronized after two to three module deactivations, while higher-degree networks (e.g., d=6) tolerated approximately five deactivations, approaching the resilience of all-to-all connectivity (d=7). Desynchronization events were often characterized by the emergence of a single "drifter" module, while the remaining active units maintained partial synchronization. This finding indicates that network topology is a critical determinant of fault tolerance in modular robot collectives.

5. **Online Edge Learning Adapts Inter-subgraph Coupling:** The Contextual Bandit Edge-selection (CBE) algorithm, utilizing an Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) rule, successfully learned to select inter-subgraph links online. In a five-module network, the algorithm adaptively formed signed inter-subgraph connections to drive the phase difference towards desired target values. It demonstrated the ability to clear existing links and re-learn new configurations when target phases were changed. This adaptive process selected a reduced set of effective links, tuning the graph to the existing hardware state and avoiding reliance on fixed link patterns, which may also contribute to reducing computational load.

6. **Graph-Based Control Outperforms Centralized Leader-Follower Control in Fault Tolerance:** A comparative study between the graph-based CBE controller and a centralized leader-follower (LF) control algorithm under module deactivation revealed that the CBE controller exhibited superior robustness. The LF controller showed a significant single-point vulnerability: deactivating the designated leader robot resulted in a substantially larger degradation in performance (measured by an order-parameter-based metric) compared to deactivating any follower robot. In contrast, the CBE controller maintained a more uniform performance across robot deactivations, consistent with the absence of a privileged leader. Quantitatively, the graph-based CBE controller reduced the worst-case phase-error RMSE by approximately threefold (from 1.88 ± 0.13 rad for LF to 0.65 ± 0.22 rad for CBE) and also achieved a lower average phase error (0.48 ± 0.07 rad for CBE versus 0.64 ± 0.05 rad for LF).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a programmable synchronization-graph framework that represents a step towards advanced control for modular miniature robots, offering potential significance and impact in several areas:

1. **Enabling Scalable and Resilient Miniature Robotics:** The work addresses a fundamental challenge in miniature robotics: coordinating large numbers of imperfect modules under severe constraints on computation, communication, and reliability. By demonstrating that coordination can emerge from programmable network topology rather than centralized control, it provides a pathway for constructing more scalable and resilient modular robot collectives.

2. **Flexible and Adaptive Gait Programming:** Unlike traditional oscillator-based controllers whose phase relationships are often hard-wired, this synchronization-graph framework allows for dynamic programming of gait-like coordination patterns. The ability to tune phase differences from in-phase to out-of-phase motion simply by changing the signs of a small number of inter-subgraph links offers practical flexibility for adapting robot locomotion to different tasks or environments post-fabrication.

3. **Enhanced Fault Tolerance:** The explicit demonstration that graph connectivity directly influences the system's robustness to module loss is significant. Designing networks with specific degrees can engineer desired levels of fault tolerance, allowing robot collectives to maintain coherent function even when individual modules fail or become disconnected. This is critical for operation in hazardous or unpredictable environments where module loss is probable.

4. **Distributed Intelligence and Online Adaptation:** The successful implementation of an online edge-selection algorithm (CBE) to learn optimal inter-subgraph links is a key contribution. This capability allows the robot collective to adapt its coupling structure in real-time to achieve target phase states, compensating for intrinsic hardware variability or environmental changes. This moves towards a more intelligent, self-configuring system that does not rely solely on predefined control parameters.

5. **Reduced Computational and Communication Burden:** The findings regarding sparse d-regular topologies indicate that synchronization can be maintained with significantly fewer links than all-to-all coupling. Coupled with the CBE algorithm's ability to identify a reduced set of effective links, this framework has the potential to lower the computational and communication demands on individual modules, which is vital for power- and resource-constrained miniature robots.

6. **Alternative to Centralized Control Vulnerabilities:** The comparative analysis demonstrating the superior fault tolerance of the graph-based CBE controller over a centralized leader-follower architecture highlights a critical advantage. By avoiding a single point of failure (a privileged leader), the proposed framework offers a more robust control paradigm for distributed robotic systems.

While the current implementation uses a centralized coordinator for experimental control, the underlying graph-local update laws are amenable to fully distributed computation. This research lays a foundation for future autonomous modular-robot architectures where robustness and adaptability emerge intrinsically from the topology and dynamics of the robot network itself, rather than from external instruction or a vulnerable centralized policy. This shift in design paradigm—making the synchronization graph the central control object—could lead to more capable and reliable miniature robotic systems for diverse applications.
