# Co-STAR: Cognitive Stimulation Therapy by an Autonomous Robot for Dementia -- A One-Week In-Home Study

> [!summary] Summary
> Co-STAR, an autonomous robot developed by Imperial College London researchers, delivered personalized Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (iCST) to people living with dementia in their homes. A one-week in-home study showed that participants completed an average of 3.44 sessions, with 6 out of 9 households meeting or exceeding the benchmark of two sessions per week, and the system achieved an average SUS score of 80.8, indicating good usability.

## Problem

- Evidence-based Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (CST) and Individualized CST (iCST) for people living with dementia (PwDs) face significant barriers to widespread in-home delivery.
- Challenges include a shortage of trained professionals, substantial time and training demands on informal caregivers, and low adherence rates in community settings.
- Prior socially assistive robot (SAR) research often focused on non-dementia populations or controlled environments, with limited autonomous, multi-session, one-to-one therapy in PwDs' homes.

## Method

- Developed **Co-STAR**, an autonomous ==socially assistive robot== designed to deliver personalized Individual Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (iCST) in the homes of PwDs.
- The system uses a Misty II robot, a tablet interface, and a custom software architecture with advanced ==speech processing== for ==multimodal interaction==, guided by stakeholder consultations.
- Five distinct iCST activities were designed to stimulate various cognitive processes, with personalization elements (e.g., incorporating personal details) to enhance engagement.

## Results

- Participants completed an average of **3.44** sessions over one week, with **6 out of 9** households meeting or exceeding the benchmark of **2** sessions per week, demonstrating feasibility for in-home delivery.
- The system achieved an average System Usability Scale (SUS) score of **80.8** (above the average of **68**), indicating good usability, and participants exhibited high positive attitudes toward robots.
- Caregiver unavailability and technical failures were the leading causes of missed sessions, while personalization of activity content significantly enhanced engagement and led to longer, more detailed responses from users.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Autonomous robots can deliver evidence-based iCST in home settings, achieving adherence levels comparable to or exceeding typical caregiver-led iCST benchmarks, suggesting a scalable approach for dementia care.
> - Interaction with therapeutic SARs in home environments often involves a "triadic" model (PwD, caregiver, robot), highlighting the critical supportive role of family members in session initiation and technical management.
> - Personalization of content, adaptable sensory outputs, and cultural customization (e.g., voice accent) are crucial for enhancing engagement, perceived trust, and overall user experience in home-based SAR deployments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (CST) and its individualized variant (iCST) are recognized as evidence-based, non-pharmacological interventions for people living with dementia (PwDs). CST is recommended by national dementia care guidelines, with randomized control trials demonstrating improvements in cognition and quality of life, approximating a six-month delay in cognitive decline for mild-to-moderate dementia. Despite these documented benefits, the widespread application of iCST in home settings is hindered by several practical challenges. These include a shortage of trained professionals, the significant time commitment and training required of informal caregivers, and the competing responsibilities and stress experienced by caregivers, leading to suboptimal adherence rates in real-world deployments. For example, a large multicenter trial reported that only 40% of iCST dyads achieved the recommended minimum of two sessions per week.

Socially Assistive Robots (SARs) represent an emerging technological solution with the potential to address these gaps. SARs are designed to engage individuals through social interaction, offering structured, consistent, and personalized interactions. Their capacity to deliver repeated interactions without exhibiting human frustrations positions them as a suitable platform for dementia interventions.

Prior research has explored the use of robots for cognitive support in older adults. Studies such as CARMEN focused on compensatory cognitive training for individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) in home settings. Other work has investigated autonomous social robots combining music therapy, reminiscence, and cognitive games for groups of PwDs in nursing homes, or robot-delivered cognitive stimulation games for community-dwelling older adults and individuals with MCI in various settings, including retirement villages and through randomized controlled trials. These studies have demonstrated robots' ability to engage older persons, support social interaction, personalize content, and reduce neuropsychiatric symptoms or improve cognitive functioning.

However, the current literature exhibits several limitations that this research aims to address. A majority of existing SAR studies for cognitive support have been conducted with older adults without a dementia diagnosis or with MCI, rather than specifically with PwDs. Furthermore, many deployments have occurred in controlled environments such as day centers or residential facilities, with very few involving autonomous, multi-session interactions in the PwD's home. Studies that did involve PwDs often focused on group activities or generic companionship rather than one-to-one delivery of structured, evidence-based CST protocols. This paper contributes to the field by presenting an in-home deployment of an autonomous social robot specifically designed to deliver evidence-based iCST to PwDs, and by providing a detailed examination of factors influencing multi-session engagement in this context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged efficacy of Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (CST) and Individualized CST (iCST) in improving the quality of life and cognitive functions for people living with dementia (PwDs), contrasted with the practical barriers preventing its consistent and widespread in-home delivery. These barriers include a limited number of trained professionals, the significant time and effort demands placed on informal caregivers, and consequently, low adherence rates in community settings. The researchers identified a clear need for scalable solutions that could overcome these challenges and extend access to evidence-based cognitive therapy to PwDs in their own homes.

Socially Assistive Robots (SARs) were identified as a promising technology to address this gap. SARs possess characteristics that make them suitable for delivering dementia interventions, such as the ability to provide structured and consistent interactions, personalize content, and interact without exhibiting impatience or frustration during repetitive tasks, unlike human caregivers.

Against this background, the key objectives of this study were:

- **To develop and deploy a novel robotic system (Co-STAR)** capable of autonomously delivering personalized Individual Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (iCST) in the homes of PwDs. This involved translating evidence-based iCST principles into a robotic platform that offers multimodal interaction through speech and a tablet-based visual interface, focusing on activities supporting memory recall, reasoning, and reminiscence.
- **To evaluate the feasibility and initial effectiveness of this autonomous, in-home robot-led iCST** over a one-week period with a cohort of PwDs. This involved measuring participant engagement, session adherence, and the general usability and acceptance of the system in an unsupervised home environment.
- **To provide an in-depth examination of the factors influencing multi-session interaction engagement and adherence** for PwDs with a social robot. This included investigating the role of family members and friends in supporting the system's use, exploring participants' motivations for engagement, and identifying barriers to consistent use, such as technical issues or perceived system limitations.
- **To capture nuanced behavioral and contextual insights** into how PwDs interact with the robotic system, their perceptions of the therapy, and the social dynamics that arise within the home setting during robot-led sessions. This aimed to inform future design guidelines for SARs in home-based therapeutic contexts.
- **To demonstrate the potential of socially assistive robots** as a scalable approach to expand access to dementia care, particularly for structured, evidence-based interventions like CST, which have historically been limited by resource constraints.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research involved the development, refinement, and a one-week in-home deployment of a socially assistive robot named Co-STAR, designed to deliver personalized Individual Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (iCST).

**System Development:**
The Co-STAR system's design was informed by prior stakeholder consultations involving 16 dementia stakeholders, including formal and informal caregivers. These consultations yielded design guidelines concerning the structure of robot-based therapy and essential design goals, such as encouraging connection over correction, positive reinforcement, cognitive stimulation rather than drill-and-practice, prioritizing opinions over facts, supporting user autonomy, incorporating privacy-by-design, facilitating personalization, and encouraging family involvement. Initial versions of the system were tested in 30-minute sessions with care professionals and PwDs, with feedback incorporated into the final system.

**iCST Activities:**
Five distinct iCST activities were developed to stimulate various cognitive processes, adhering to the seven design principles identified. These activities included:
1. **Popular Places:** Displayed images of landmarks to aid memory recall.
2. **Famous Faces:** Involved recognizing notable people to strengthen memory associations.
3. **Common Sayings:** Required completing familiar proverbs to promote language activation.
4. **Word Association:** Asked users to identify thematic connections among images to reinforce semantic memory.
5. **Object Categorisation:** Tasked users with grouping related objects.
Word Association and Common Sayings were repeated with different content to provide seven days of unique sessions. Prior to deployment, personal details (e.g., jobs, travels, hobbies) shared by participants were embedded into interactions for personalization, such as incorporating visited places into the Popular Places activity.

**iCST Session Structure:**
Each session was designed to last 30 minutes. Participants initiated sessions by pressing a "start" button on a tablet interface after unlocking it. The robot greeted the participant, introduced the day's activity with a "fun fact," and displayed pictures or text on the tablet. It then asked questions related to the visuals. A text-to-speech system captured PwD utterances, and text matching was used to identify answers. The robot provided positive reinforcement for correct answers and supportive feedback (e.g., "You're close") followed by the correct answer for incorrect responses. Sessions concluded with a brief wrap-up, thanks, and a summary of discussed items.

**Hardware Components:**
Co-STAR integrated a Misty II robot, a microphone for audio input, a mini-CPU as the primary processing unit, a tablet for visual display, and a tablet holder. A router facilitated local internet connection for system components and speech-to-text algorithms. All components were housed in a custom enclosure for safety and portability. The Misty robot performed gestures within this enclosure. Data was stored locally within the home to maintain privacy; no video data was collected.

**Software Architecture:**
The software architecture combined speech-based communication with visual assistance and adaptive personalization. It utilized OpenAI's text-to-speech model for natural voice generation and WhisperD, a fine-tuned variant of Whisper, for robust speech-to-text processing tailored for dementia speech, handling filler words and disfluencies. All robot dialogue text was pre-generated. A central controller managed session scheduling, activity sequencing, and data flow. An Android-based application served as the dual-purpose user interface, displaying temporal information when idle and presenting activity prompts, text, and images during sessions for multimodal engagement.

**User Study Design:**
- **Ethical Approval:** The study received ethical approval (ethics no: 7091501).
- **Recruitment:** Nine PwDs with a clinical dementia diagnosis were recruited through dementia care managers and independent living centers, ensuring participants had sufficient capacity to provide informed consent, supported by caregiver consent where applicable. Participants averaged 79.7 years of age (5 female, 4 male).
- **Deployment:** The robot was deployed in each participant's home for one week, with daily use requested. On the first day, the lead researcher was present to assist with initial setup and sessions, clarifying any misconceptions. A "cheat sheet" outlining system use was also provided.
- **Measures:**
 - **Usage Data:** Primary measure was the number of completed sessions and session length, logged by the system.
 - **Pre-study Questionnaires:** Demographics Questionnaire, General Attitudes Towards Robots Scale (GAToRS), and questions on technology familiarity were administered.
 - **Post-study Assessments:** Participants completed a System Usability Scale (SUS) to evaluate system experience. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with PwDs to gather qualitative insights on usability, acceptance, and perceived value. Where possible, family caregivers completed a questionnaire to offer complementary perspectives.
- **Data Analysis:** Interview transcripts were subjected to thematic analysis using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework. Findings were triangulated across interviews, caregiver feedback, interaction logs, and system recordings to enhance credibility. Quantitative data from SUS and GAToRS questionnaires, as well as usage logs, were analyzed to provide numerical context.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded quantitative data on system usage and usability, alongside rich qualitative insights into participant and caregiver experiences.

**Usage and Adherence:**
- **Session Completion:** Participants completed a total of 31 sessions over the one-week study period (63 potential sessions across 9 participants), averaging 3.44 sessions per person. This represents approximately half of the expected daily sessions. Six out of nine households met or exceeded the common iCST benchmark of two sessions per week. Gideon completed the most sessions (8), while Henry completed none.
- **Session Length:** The average session duration was 32 minutes and 42 seconds, closely aligning with the target of 30-minute sessions. Individual session lengths varied, ranging from 23m54s to 65m24s, influenced by the length of user responses.
- **Reasons for Participation:** Participants enrolled primarily for three reasons: the potential for cognitive improvements (4 participants), caregiver commitment to help a loved one (4 participants), and altruism towards dementia research (1 participant). A desire to keep the robot for longer was expressed by four PwDs and two caregivers.
- **Reasons for Missed Sessions:** The most frequent cause for missed sessions was caregiver unavailability (10 sessions), particularly affecting participants reliant on support. Technical failures (8 sessions), such as power loss or unstable internet, were the second most common issue. Loss of interest (7 sessions), difficulties with the interface (6 sessions, e.g., tablet navigation), and competing life priorities (6 sessions, e.g., visiting grandchildren) also contributed to non-adherence.

**Perceptions of the System:**
- **Quantitative Usability:** The System Usability Scale (SUS) was completed by six participants, yielding an average score of 80.8 (SD ≈ 25.1), which is above the established average of 68, indicating good usability.
- **Attitudes Towards Robots (GAToRS):** Eight participants completed the GAToRS. Average scores indicated high positive trust and attitudes towards robots (P+ M=4.53, SD=2.01), low negative trust (P- M=1.00, SD=0.00), high perception of positive societal impact of robots (S+ M=6.25, SD=0.90), and moderate perception of negative societal impact (S- M=3.45, SD=1.64).
- **Qualitative Feedback:** Participants reported enjoying the system, particularly when activities stimulated memories and provided enjoyable challenges. Companionship and social ripple effects were also appreciated. The most consistent criticism was the robot's perceived scriptedness and weak responsiveness, leading to impressions that it was "machine-like." Some found the content repetitive, and slow system startup or response times were noted as barriers to engagement. While the physical appearance of the robot was generally appealing, some participants expressed a desire for more advanced capabilities, such as mobility.

**Social Context and Family Role:**
- **Caregiver Impact:** Participants living with caregivers completed more sessions (Mean=4.3) than those living alone (Mean=2.8). Caregivers frequently played a crucial role in session initiation, handling technical issues, and providing prompts, suggesting a "triadic" interaction model (PwD, caregiver, robot). Caregiver unavailability was a direct cause of missed sessions.
- **Social Validation:** Participants often expressed curiosity about others' use of Co-STAR and shared their experiences with neighbors or friends, indicating a desire for social validation and community building around the technology.
- **Family Bonding:** Sessions presented opportunities for deeper conversations and shared memory reconstruction between PwDs and their caregivers. Examples included PwDs recounting previously unknown personal memories sparked by activity prompts, fostering family bonding.

**Role of Personalization:**
- **Sensory Preferences:** Users requested adjustments to system settings such as volume and screen brightness, highlighting the need for adaptable sensory outputs.
- **Adaptive Cognitive Challenge:** Participants exhibited diverse preferences for activity difficulty. Individuals with higher educational backgrounds sometimes found tasks too simple, while others were motivated by challenging activities, underscoring the necessity of personalized cognitive challenge.
- **Content Relevance:** Personalization of activity content, such as incorporating familiar places from a participant's life, significantly enhanced engagement and led to longer, more detailed responses.
- **Voice Accent Familiarity:** Co-STAR's American accent was a source of discomfort and perceived trust reduction for some British participants, although others reported no concerns, indicating the importance of local accent customization.

**Case Studies:**
The report presented four case studies illustrating varied engagement patterns:
- **Gideon:** A tech-positive, highly motivated user who exceeded adherence targets, appreciating the cognitive benefits and actively advocating for the technology within his community. His missed sessions were due to technical issues he introduced.
- **Georgia:** Highly motivated but completed only one session due to competing life priorities (visiting grandchildren, recent move), demonstrating that motivation alone is insufficient without practical integration.
- **Benjamin:** Willing to engage but significantly limited by technical barriers (unreliable Wi-Fi), highlighting the need for seamless technological integration.
- **Priscilla:** Initially apprehensive but achieved reliable use with consistent support from her daughter, illustrating the critical role of caregiver assistance in overcoming initial barriers and fostering engagement, ultimately leading to improved mood and cognitive stimulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study represents an advancement in the application of socially assistive robots for dementia care, particularly in demonstrating the feasibility and potential of autonomous, in-home delivery of evidence-based Individual Cognitive Stimulation Therapy (iCST) for people living with dementia (PwDs). By deploying Co-STAR directly in participants' homes for a week, the research moves beyond controlled laboratory or institutional settings, addressing a significant gap in the literature regarding real-world, multi-session autonomous robot interaction with PwDs.

The findings indicate that an autonomous robot can deliver structured cognitive work at an adherence level comparable to or, in some aspects, higher than typically observed in caregiver-led iCST. The average adherence rate of approximately half the expected daily sessions, with 6 out of 9 households meeting the benchmark of ≥ 2 sessions per week, suggests a promising foundation for a scalable approach to dementia care. This is particularly significant given the persistent shortage of trained professionals and the recognized burden on informal caregivers, which limit access to traditional iCST.

The research provides novel and detailed insights into the complex factors influencing sustained engagement with SARs for PwDs in a home environment. A key finding is the often "triadic" nature of the interaction, involving the PwD, their caregiver, and the robot. This highlights the critical, supportive role of family members in initiating sessions, managing technical issues, and mediating the interaction, even with an "autonomous" system. This insight is crucial for future SAR design, suggesting the need for systems that explicitly support both autonomous and caregiver-assisted modes, as well as multi-user turn-taking and joint decision-making.

The qualitative data underscores the perceived cognitive, social, and emotional benefits reported by participants and caregivers, including cognitive stimulation, mood improvement, companionship, and the fostering of family bonding through shared reminiscence. The observation that participants and caregivers expressed a desire to continue using Co-STAR beyond the study period indicates a level of acceptability and perceived value that extends beyond initial novelty.

The study also identifies actionable design considerations for future therapeutic robots:
- **Enhanced Responsiveness and Personalization:** The criticism regarding scriptedness, slow responses, and content repetitiveness points to the need for more dynamic, context-aware, and continuously personalized interactions to maintain long-term engagement and perceived competence.
- **Technical Robustness:** The significant impact of technical failures (e.g., Wi-Fi disconnections) on adherence highlights the necessity for seamless integration into existing home network ecosystems and robust, user-friendly operation.
- **Cultural and Sensory Customization:** The feedback on voice accent preferences and sensory adjustments (volume, brightness) emphasizes the importance of localizing robot characteristics and providing customizable settings to enhance comfort and trust.
- **Privacy-by-Design:** Reinforcing the explicit communication of privacy measures is crucial for building user trust, especially in sensitive home environments.

Beyond dementia care, the insights gleaned from this work, particularly concerning multi-session engagement, caregiver involvement, and personalization in an unsupervised home setting, can inform the design and deployment of SARs in other home-based therapeutic contexts, such as rehabilitation, mental health support, and chronic disease management. The study lays groundwork for future long-term deployments with larger sample sizes to comprehensively track cognitive changes and assess the enduring benefits of robot-assisted iCST.
