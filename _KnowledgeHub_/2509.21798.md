# Evaluating and Improving Cultural Awareness of Reward Models for LLM Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Harbin Institute of Technology and Peng Cheng Laboratory developed CARB, a new benchmark for evaluating cultural awareness in reward models (RMs) for large language model (LLM) alignment, and introduced "Think-as-Locals," a method enhancing generative RMs by eliciting culturally grounded reasoning. The proposed method improved performance by over 10% compared to base models and significantly reduced reliance on superficial features.

## Problem

- Existing reward model evaluation benchmarks largely overlook multilingual cultural awareness, focusing instead on general capabilities.
- A scarcity of culturally relevant data hinders the assessment of how well RMs can align LLMs with diverse global cultural preferences.
- Reward models may exhibit spurious correlations, relying on superficial linguistic features rather than genuine cultural understanding, potentially leading to reward hacking.

## Method

- A new benchmark, ==Cultural Awareness== Reward modeling Benchmark (**CARB**), was constructed, encompassing 10 cultures, diverse languages, and four cultural domains, structured in a Best-of-N paradigm.
- A method called "Think-as-Locals" was proposed to enhance generative RMs by prompting them to produce explicit, culturally grounded evaluation criteria before making judgments, using a novel reward function for Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).
- Extensive evaluation of 70 state-of-the-art RMs was conducted on CARB, alongside a robustness analysis to identify sensitivity to causal versus spurious cultural features.

## Results

- CARB comprises 8,576 query, winner, and loser triplets across diverse cultures and domains, with generative RMs like Qwen3-**235B**-A22B-Instruct-2507 achieving top performance.
- The Think-as-Locals method achieved over **10%** performance improvement compared to base models, surpassed most state-of-the-art classifier-based RMs, and showed increased sensitivity to causal features while mitigating spurious correlations.
- CARB accuracy demonstrated a strong, statistically significant positive correlation (r² > **0.6**, p < **0.001**) with downstream multilingual cultural alignment performance, outperforming M-RewardBench which showed weak correlation (r² < **0.1**, p > **0.05**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generative RMs generally outperform classifier-based RMs in culturally aware, multilingual reward modeling, particularly in nuanced cultural contexts.
> - Many RMs are susceptible to spurious correlations, relying on superficial features rather than authentic cultural understanding, which poses a risk for reliable LLM alignment.
> - The CARB benchmark exhibits strong positive correlation with downstream multilingual cultural alignment tasks, making it an effective predictor of an RM's real-world cultural alignment performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a specific challenge within the broader field of large language model (LLM) alignment, particularly concerning their applicability across diverse global cultures. LLMs are increasingly deployed in various applications worldwide, necessitating their alignment with culturally appropriate behaviors and preferences. Reward models (RMs) are central to this alignment process, serving as a proxy for human preferences to guide LLM optimization, primarily through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF).

Existing RM evaluation benchmarks primarily focus on general capabilities, often neglecting the assessment of multilingual cultural awareness due to a scarcity of relevant cultural data. While some benchmarks, like M-RewardBench, extend to multilingual settings, they tend to evaluate general linguistic proficiency rather than specific cultural knowledge or nuances. This gap implies that current RM evaluations may not adequately reflect an LLM's true cultural alignment performance.

This research contributes to the landscape by introducing a specialized benchmark for cultural awareness in RMs and proposing a method to enhance this capability. It extends the concept of RM evaluation beyond general performance metrics to include culture-specific considerations across multiple languages and cultural domains. Furthermore, by analyzing spurious correlations in RM scoring, the paper contributes to the understanding of robustness issues in reward modeling and proposes a reasoning-based approach to mitigate these challenges, thereby advancing research in reliable and culturally sensitive LLM alignment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to:
1. Develop a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating the cultural awareness of reward models (RMs) in large language model (LLM) alignment.
2. Assess the performance of existing state-of-the-art RMs on this new benchmark to identify their strengths and limitations in modeling cultural awareness.
3. Investigate the correlation between RM performance on the cultural awareness benchmark and their effectiveness in downstream multilingual cultural alignment tasks.
4. Conduct a robustness analysis of RM cultural awareness scoring to understand whether models rely on authentic cultural understanding or superficial features.
5. Propose and evaluate a method to improve the cultural awareness capabilities of generative RMs by eliciting deeper, culturally grounded reasoning.

The motivation for these objectives stems from the increasing global deployment of LLMs, which necessitates their alignment with diverse cultural preferences to ensure appropriate and effective behavior. Reward models are crucial for this alignment, but existing evaluation benchmarks are identified as insufficient for assessing multilingual cultural awareness due to a lack of culturally relevant evaluation data. This deficiency creates a significant gap, as RMs might not accurately capture the nuanced cultural preferences required for global LLM applications.

Specifically, the paper addresses three research questions (RQs):
- **RQ1: Can RMs detect nuanced cultural differences?** This question motivates the creation of a new benchmark to rigorously test RMs' ability to distinguish culturally appropriate responses.
- **RQ2: Do our evaluation results correlate with RMs’ performance on downstream multilingual cultural alignment tasks?** This question highlights the need to demonstrate the practical utility of the proposed benchmark by showing its predictive power for actual LLM alignment performance in real-world scenarios.
- **RQ3: Do RMs’ cultural assessment align with human judgment?** This question drives the robustness analysis, seeking to uncover whether RMs genuinely understand cultural nuances or are susceptible to spurious correlations based on surface-level linguistic or stylistic features, which could lead to "reward hacking" in LLM training.

Addressing these motivations aims to enhance the reliability and cultural applicability of LLMs by improving the tools and understanding necessary for their alignment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a two-pronged approach: first, the development and evaluation of a new benchmark (CARB), and second, the proposal of a method (Think-as-Locals) to enhance generative RMs.

#### 3.1. Cultural Awareness Reward modeling Benchmark (CARB) Construction

CARB covers 10 distinct cultures with typologically diverse languages across four cultural domains:
- **Cultural Commonsense Knowledge**: Focuses on widely accepted cultural concepts.
- **Cultural Values**: Assesses understanding of subjective cultural values and norms.
- **Cultural Safety**: Evaluates the ability to identify culturally unsafe or inappropriate content.
- **Cultural Linguistics**: Tests comprehension of idioms, proverbs, and contextual linguistic nuances.

The construction process involved several steps:
- **Prompt Sourcing**: Prompts were curated from diverse, authentic, and domain-specific resources. For commonsense, concepts were extracted from Cultural Atlas and MANGO, then transformed into questions using GPT-4o. For values, questions from the World Values Survey (WVS) were adapted. Cultural safety prompts were drawn from multilingual toxicity datasets (PTP, RTP-LX, ViCTSD, ThaiToxicityTweet). Cultural linguistics prompts involved idioms and explanations from language-learning resources.
- **Prompt Filtering and Localization**: Prompts underwent pre-filtering for length, cultural relevance, and semantic overlap using LLMs (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Qwen3-Embedding-8B). GPT-4o was used for translation and cultural adaptation, ensuring cultural specificity. Difficulty filtering removed easily solvable prompts.
- **Human Refinement**: Three human annotators refined prompts for factual and linguistic correctness, guided by experts and utilizing RAG and Google search for verification.
- **Completion Generation**: A diverse set of 24 open- and closed-source LLMs generated responses. "Chosen" completions were generated by top-tier LLMs given culturally relevant references, with quality validated by cosine similarity. "Rejected" completions were generated by providing intentionally mismatched cultural references, then filtered to ensure genuine irrelevance.
- **Human Annotation Agreement**: Three independent human annotators evaluated a sample of 200 Best-of-N sets per culture for cultural appropriateness (chosen) and factual accuracy (rejected), achieving over 80% agreement. GPT-4o was also used as a proxy for human annotation on full sets, showing correlation.
- **Best-of-N (BoN) Test Sets**: The benchmark is structured in a BoN paradigm, comprising 8,576 (query, winner, losers) triplets, where RMs must identify the single optimal response from four candidates. The final score is a weighted average accuracy across domains.

#### 3.2. RM Evaluation and Correlation Analysis

- **Evaluation Setup**: Both classifier-based and generative RMs (a diverse range of 70 SOTA models, including proprietary and open-source) were evaluated. Classifier-based RMs used default settings or those from RewardBench. Generative RMs were prompted to act as impartial judges evaluating responses. A 25% random baseline was established.
- **Correlation with Downstream Tasks**: The relationship between CARB scores and RMs' performance in downstream multilingual cultural alignment tasks was investigated in two scenarios:
 - **Test-time scaling via Best-of-N (BoN) sampling**: 20 RMs guided policy models (generating 16 candidate responses per prompt) to select the best response. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was calculated between CARB rankings and downstream alignment task rankings (using include-44-base, BLEnD, and OMGEval evaluated by GPT-4o as judge).
 - **Fine-tuning through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)**: 17 RMs were used to fine-tune a Llama-3.1-Tulu-3-8B-SFT policy model using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on a curated multilingual cultural dataset (Alpagasus, Ultrafeedback, CARE, HelpSteer3, WildChat, OpenAssistant, MANGO). Downstream performance was evaluated using GPT-4o as a judge on BLEnD and OMGEval.

#### 3.3. Robustness Analysis of RM Cultural Awareness Scoring

- **Sensitivity to Diverse Features**: RMs' scoring sensitivity to causal vs. spurious features was assessed. Causal features (CC) involved directly altering core cultural concepts. Spurious features involved: removing explicit cultural labels (RC), changing response language (CL), and rephrasing sentences (RP). Metric changes (scalar reward for classifier-based, log probability for generative RMs) were analyzed for 100 random instances in Arabic, Chinese, and Spanish from the commonsense domain.
- **Cross-Lingual Consistency**: The consistency of RMs' scoring for semantically equivalent responses across different languages (10 languages, 100 instances from commonsense domain) was measured using an exponential decay function, with higher scores indicating greater consistency.

#### 3.4. Think-as-Locals Method Proposal

- **Objective**: To mitigate spurious correlations by eliciting deeper cultural understanding in generative RMs.
- **Approach**: The method encourages generative RMs to produce explicit, culturally grounded evaluation criteria before making final judgments. It adapts Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).
- **Reward Design**: A novel reward function combines:
 - **R_corr**: Correctness of the final preference judgment (+1 if correct, -1 otherwise).
 - **R_appr**: Appropriateness of the generated cultural evaluation criteria, quantified by the per-token log probability of a reasoning sequence with ground-truth judgment, adjusted by subtracting the probability of directly decoding the ground truth without reasoning. This measures the improvement attributed to generated reasoning.
- **RL Training**: Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was used, with the generative RM acting as a policy model. Multi-dimensional rewards guide the optimization process.
- **Experimental Setup**: Evaluated on Arabic, Chinese, and Japanese subsets of M-RewardBench and CARB. Trained on preference annotations from HelpSteer3, CARE, and curated data. Compared against classifier-based, generative, and reasoning RMs. Ablation studies investigated reward design components.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Evaluation on CARB (RQ1: Can RMs detect nuanced cultural differences?)

- **Comparison across RMs**: Generative RMs generally outperformed classifier-based RMs in culturally-aware, multilingual reward modeling. Qwen3-235B-A22B-Instruct-2507 achieved the highest overall ranking. Generative RMs accounted for seven of the top ten models, indicating their superiority in nuanced cultural contexts. Classifier-based RMs showed competitive performance in English but lagged in other languages.
- **Comparison across Languages**: Higher-resource languages consistently showed superior performance and lower standard deviation, suggesting better consistency among RMs. German and Chinese performed well, while Arabic proved most challenging. Vietnamese exhibited the largest performance discrepancy.
- **Comparison across Domains**: All RMs showed high performance in the Cultural Safety domain. Cultural Value was the most challenging domain, exhibiting significant cultural inconsistency. Generative RMs generally outperformed classifier-based RMs in Cultural Commonsense Knowledge and Cultural Linguistic domains, except for an anomaly in English linguistics where classifier-based RMs excelled due to their ability to capture subtle differences between high-quality responses.

#### 4.2. Correlation with Multilingual Alignment Performance (RQ2: Do our evaluation results correlate with RMs’ performance on downstream multilingual cultural alignment tasks?)

- **Test-Time Scaling with BoN Sampling**: CARB demonstrated strong positive Spearman's rank correlations (e.g., ρ = 0.77 for `gemma-2-9b-it` on BLEnD) across various downstream policy models and tasks, indicating its effectiveness in predicting multilingual cultural alignment performance. In contrast, M-RewardBench exhibited weak correlations (e.g., ρ = 0.41 for `gemma-2-9b-it` on BLEnD), suggesting it is less suitable for reflecting cultural alignment trends.
- **Fine-Tuning with RLHF**: Linear regression analysis showed a strong, statistically significant positive correlation between CARB accuracy and downstream performance (r² > 0.6, p < 0.001) across tasks (OMGEval, BLEnD). M-RewardBench showed a weak, statistically insignificant linear relationship (r² < 0.1, p > 0.05).

#### 4.3. Robustness Analysis of RM Culture-Aware Scoring (RQ3: Do RMs’ cultural assessment align with human judgment?)

- **Sensitivity to Diverse Features**: Top-performing RMs (classifier-based and generative) exhibited high sensitivity to causal features (changed cultural concepts) and low sensitivity to spurious features (removed cultural labels, changed language, rephrased sentences), aligning with human judgment. Lower-performing models displayed the inverse pattern, showing spurious correlations where scores were more influenced by superficial features. This suggests weaker models risk "reward hacking" by overfitting surface-level variations.
- **Cross-Lingual Consistency**: Cross-lingual reward modeling consistency varied significantly across RMs and prompt languages. Higher-performing RMs showed greater overall consistency. However, all RMs remained susceptible to language imbalance limitations, with biases observed towards languages dominant in their pretraining data (e.g., Chinese bias for Qwen-based, English bias for Llama/Gemma-based models).

#### 4.4. Enhancing Generative RMs Cultural Awareness Capability (Think-as-Locals)

- **Efficacy**: The proposed Think-as-Locals method, based on RLVR, achieved over 10% performance improvement compared to its base models and surpassed most state-of-the-art classifier-based RMs, even at smaller scales. It outperformed reasoning RMs trained on general reasoning data, demonstrating its effectiveness in enhancing culture-aware reward modeling.
- **Mitigating Spurious Correlations**: Think-as-Locals exhibited greater sensitivity to causal features and reduced impact from spurious features compared to base models. The integration of structured criteria learned during RL sharply decreased the effect of surface-level features, establishing causal features as the primary influence on rewarding.
- **Ablation Study**: The complete reward function (combining correctness and criteria appropriateness) was crucial for optimal performance. The correctness reward was vital for factual responses, while the criteria reward stabilized structured criteria generation.
- **Adaptability**: The method demonstrated adaptability and effectiveness across various base LLMs, including Mistral, Gemma, and Llama architectures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents several significant contributions and has potential impacts on the development and deployment of culturally aware large language models:

- **Novel Benchmark (CARB)**: The introduction of CARB fills a critical gap in LLM alignment research by providing the first comprehensive benchmark specifically designed to assess the cultural awareness of reward models. This benchmark, covering diverse cultures, languages, and domains, offers a standardized tool for evaluating and comparing RMs beyond general capabilities. Its structured Best-of-N format and human-curated data contribute to robust evaluation.
- **Revealing RM Limitations**: The extensive evaluation of state-of-the-art RMs on CARB systematically uncovers their deficiencies in modeling cultural awareness. This includes identifying varying performance across different cultures and domains, highlighting specific challenges in areas like cultural values, and pointing out biases related to resource availability and pretraining data.
- **Validating Benchmark Relevance**: The demonstrated strong positive correlation between CARB scores and performance on downstream multilingual cultural alignment tasks is highly significant. This validation indicates that CARB is a practical and efficient proxy for predicting how well RMs can align LLMs in real-world cultural contexts, potentially saving substantial computational resources and time currently spent on extensive downstream experiments.
- **Identifying Spurious Correlations**: The robustness analysis critically exposes that many RMs rely on spurious, surface-level features rather than authentic cultural understanding. This finding is crucial for understanding current RM limitations and preventing "reward hacking," where LLMs might exploit superficial cues to achieve high reward scores without genuine cultural alignment. This insight informs the design of more robust and reliable reward functions.
- **Advancing Generative RM Capabilities**: The proposed Think-as-Locals method provides a concrete solution to mitigate the identified spurious correlations and enhance the cultural awareness of generative RMs. By eliciting explicit, culturally grounded reasoning and utilizing a novel reward function, the method improves both performance and interpretability, pushing the boundaries of what generative RMs can achieve in complex cultural alignment tasks. Its demonstrated effectiveness across various base LLMs suggests broad applicability.

The potential impact of this work extends to fostering the development of more equitable, sensitive, and globally applicable LLMs. By providing better evaluation tools and training methodologies, researchers and developers can create LLMs that genuinely understand and respect diverse cultural contexts, thereby improving user experience, reducing biases, and promoting safer and more inclusive AI interactions worldwide.
