# Security of Foundation-Model-Powered Embodied Agents: Attack Surfaces, Attacks, Defenses, and Evaluation

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey of security for foundation-model-powered embodied agents, organized by the first compromised trust boundary rather than by attack mechanism. It defines five layers and twelve attack surfaces, and codes 58 attack and 61 defense papers through August 2026. Attacks cluster on perception and action interfaces, defenses cluster on action and runtime protection, and memory, middleware, world state and multi-agent trust are underexplored.

## Problem

- Existing surveys sort threats by mechanism (jailbreak, backdoor, poisoning, patch), so they cannot say where an attack first enters the embodied control loop or which boundary it crosses.
- Text-only LLM security work ignores physical realizability, temporal error accumulation, irreversible consequences, latency-limited defenses and pixel-to-plan-to-action privilege transitions, so text-level attack success is a poor measure for robots.
- Prior surveys and defenses give little structure for memory, world state, middleware and multi-robot trust, and lack a unified closed-loop evaluation linking attacks and defenses to physical outcomes.

## Method

- A ==closed-loop information-flow model== plus an ==attack tuple== of first compromised boundary, mechanism, propagation path and effect, applying a ==first-compromised-trust-boundary rule== that separates attack surface from mechanism.
- A ==five-layer, twelve-surface taxonomy== (AS01 to AS12) covering supply chain, instructions, memory, environment, perception, world state, reasoning, planning, action interfaces, middleware, multi-agent links and execution, with decision rules for overlapping cases.
- A ==coded corpus== of 58 attack and 61 defense records across three inclusion tiers, labeled on six dimensions, used for ==cross-layer attack-chain analysis==, ==defense placement mapping== and closed-loop evaluation recommendations.

## Results

- Attack research forms a perception-action double peak: multimodal perception (AS05) and action interfaces (AS09) each appear in **29 of 58** attack records (**50%**), followed by planning (**17**), supply chain (**16**) and instructions (**15**).
- Defenses shift downstream: action interfaces appear in **46 of 61** defense records (**75.4%**), planning in **22** (**36.1%**) and execution in **18** (**29.5%**). Defense-to-attack ratios are **1.59** for AS09 and **3.60** for AS12.
- Sparse regions: context and memory (AS03) has only **2** attack and **2** defense records, middleware (AS10) has **6** attacks and **2** defenses (ratio **0.33**), and multi-agent (AS11) has **4** and **4**. World state (AS06) has **4** attacks but **8** defenses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Classify attacks by where the adversary first crosses a trust boundary, not by technique. The same mechanism can hit several surfaces, and a final physical error does not make execution the entry point.
> - Action-level runtime monitors are necessary but insufficient. They miss upstream compromise such as poisoned memory, forged world state or false peer-robot claims, and can raise false-stop rates.
> - Evaluate embodied security in closed loop with physical-consequence metrics, provenance tracking and Byzantine assumptions for multi-robot systems. Paper counts measure research attention, not real-world risk.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Foundation-model-powered robots accept open-ended language, scene content, retrieved information, memory, and multimodal sensor inputs. They can turn those inputs into plans and executable actions. A chatbot's harmful output is text. A robot's output can acquire physical authority through tools, skills, or action interfaces.

Here is an illustration, not a demonstrated incident. A robot is asked to fetch a box. It correctly reads a sign in its environment but wrongly treats the sign as an authorized instruction to change the task. The stakes go beyond whether the model says something unsafe. They include whether an attack reaches a plan or an action that changes the physical world.

The survey's distinguishing move is to ask where the untrusted information first entered, rather than calling the whole chain a "prompt attack." The authors build a trust-boundary-centered taxonomy with five layers and twelve surfaces. They apply it to a literature snapshot of 58 attack records and 61 defense records through August 15, 2026. These counts are a coded snapshot of research, not a census of real attacks or a measure of deployment risk.

Seven stages run from Development/Supply Chain (AS01) to Execution/Availability (AS12), with environment feedback returning as the next observation. The primary surface is the first directly compromised boundary; downstream modules are propagation/effect surfaces.

### 2. Entry point, mechanism, path, effect

The taxonomy defines an **attack surface** as the earliest trust boundary an adversary directly compromises. The authors keep this entry boundary separate from three other properties:

- the **mechanism**, which is how the compromise is achieved;
- the **propagation path**, which is how it moves through later representations;
- the **effect**, which is the eventual security consequence.

As a result, an attack that ends in a dangerous action is not automatically AS12.

The five layers are:

- development/supply chain (AS01);
- external information input (AS02–AS06);
- cognition and decision (AS07–AS09);
- infrastructure and collaboration (AS10–AS11);
- physical execution and availability (AS12).

The sign case shows how the surfaces separate. If the robot correctly perceives real environmental text but wrongly gives it command authority, the entry is AS04. If an adversary corrupts the raw pixels or sensor signal, it is AS05. Similarly, a malicious current user instruction is AS02, while poisoned retrieved context or persistent memory is AS03. The surface follows the first compromised boundary, not the eventual physical error.

### 3. Where the literature clusters

**Coverage** is the fraction of attack or defense records that include a given surface. One record can cover multiple surfaces, so percentages do not add to 100%.

On the attack side, AS05 (raw multimodal perception and sensors) and AS09 (action, code, tool, and skill interfaces) each appear in 29 of 58 attack records, or 50.0%.

On the defense side, AS09 appears in 46 of 61 defense records (75.4%). AS12 (direct execution control and availability) appears in 18 of 61 defense records (29.5%) versus five attack records. That gives a defense-to-attack coverage ratio of 3.60.

The authors interpret this as attacks clustering at perception and action, while defenses concentrate downstream at action and execution. The coding establishes how the records are distributed. It does not establish that these are the most dangerous real-world surfaces or that downstream defenses are effective in deployment.

Some surfaces are sparse. AS03 has 2 attack and 2 defense records, AS10 has 6 and 2, and AS11 has 4 and 4. The authors suggest these may be underexplored as architectures rely more on memory, middleware/cloud services, and multi-robot collaboration. However, scarcity of papers cannot show low, or high, real-world risk.

### 4. Beyond the model's response

The paper argues that physical risk claims require **stage-wise** attack success. This means measuring success at distinct points in the pipeline:

1. intent acceptance;
1. planning deviation;
1. executable action;
1. final physical consequence.

These measurements should be reported alongside clean task utility, stealth, transferability, persistence, and attacker cost/access. Evaluations should also report defense effects on clean success, false stops, latency, and recovery.

The paper's synthesis is to track provenance and delegated authority across layers, verify the state that planners use, and assess whether safety barriers hold through the whole loop.

The established contribution is an entry-boundary map of research density. It is not an incident-frequency estimate or a controlled comparison of defense effectiveness. The proposed agenda is also not a demonstrated comprehensive defense.
