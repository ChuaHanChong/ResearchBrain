# Graph-level Representation Learning with Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures

> [!summary] Summary
> Graph-JEPA introduces the first Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture for self-supervised graph-level representation learning, addressing limitations of existing methods by predicting latent representations of target subgraphs from context subgraphs. The architecture achieves state-of-the-art or competitive performance on various graph classification and regression benchmarks, demonstrating enhanced expressivity and up to 8x faster pre-training.

## Problem

- Existing contrastive graph self-supervised learning (SSL) methods rely on complex data augmentations and computationally expensive negative sampling.
- Generative graph SSL models often overfit to high-level details and may learn suboptimal representations for diverse downstream tasks, a problem exacerbated by graph data's non-Euclidean nature.
- Adapting Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) to graphs requires defining effective context/target signals and an optimal latent prediction task for capturing hierarchical graph concepts.

## Method

- **Graph-JEPA** partitions input graphs into 1-hop neighborhood-expanded ==subgraphs== using METIS, which are then embedded by a GINE model along with positional embeddings.
- It designates a context subgraph and multiple target subgraphs, processing them with separate Transformer encoders; the target encoder uses Exponential Moving Average (EMA) updates and a stop-gradient mechanism.
- A novel latent prediction objective transforms target subgraph embeddings into 2D hyperbolic coordinates, which a two-layer MLP predicts from the context embedding and target positional encoding.

## Results

- Graph-JEPA achieved state-of-the-art or competitive performance on 5 of 8 TUD graph classification datasets and strong results on ZINC regression, outperforming most existing graph SSL methods and demonstrating high expressivity (**98.77%** accuracy on EXP non-isomorphic graphs).
- Demonstrated significant computational efficiency during pre-training, being up to 2.5 times faster than Graph-MAE and 8 times faster than MVGRL on larger datasets.
- The novel latent prediction objective based on hyperbolic angles proved more effective than Euclidean or Poincaré distance-based objectives, inducing beneficial implicit hierarchical organization in the latent space.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - JEPAs provide a robust self-supervised learning paradigm for graphs by predicting in the latent space, avoiding the complexities of negative samples, intricate augmentations, and direct raw data reconstruction.
> - Projecting high-dimensional latent graph embeddings onto 2D hyperbolic coordinates allows for implicitly learning hierarchical relationships between subgraphs without the computational and stability issues of full high-dimensional hyperbolic embeddings.
> - Applying stop-gradient and EMA updates to the target encoder, coupled with a simpler predictor network, is crucial for preventing representation collapse and ensuring stable, meaningful representation learning within the self-distillation framework.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Graph data structures are prevalent across various scientific and social domains, abstracting complex relationships. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have emerged as a standard solution for processing such data. However, the majority of GNN applications rely on supervised learning, requiring extensive ground-truth labels. The increasing volume and complexity of graph data in fields like bioinformatics and social networks render manual labeling laborious and costly, motivating significant research into unsupervised graph representation learning.

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has become a prominent paradigm in this area, generating supervisory signals directly from the input data. Existing SSL approaches for graphs generally fall into two categories:

1. **Invariance-based (Contrastive Learning):** These methods optimize models to produce similar embeddings for different augmented "views" of an input signal, typically requiring positive and negative sample pairs. While effective, they necessitate sophisticated data augmentation strategies and the processing of negative samples, which can be computationally intensive and memory-demanding. Examples in the graph domain include InfoGraph, GraphCL, MVGRL, and AD-GCL.

2. **Generative-based:** These methods typically reconstruct masked or corrupted portions of the input data using autoencoding or autoregressive models. Generative models, such as Graph Auto-Encoders (GAEs) and Masked Autoencoders (GraphMAE, S2GAE), aim to learn the underlying data distribution. However, they can be prone to overfitting, face training challenges like mode collapse, and may learn representations that are not consistently optimal for downstream tasks, particularly in the complex, non-Euclidean space of graphs.

Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) have recently been introduced as a self-supervised technique that combines aspects of both contrastive and generative models. JEPAs predict a latent representation of a target signal from a context signal in the latent space, avoiding the need for negative samples and complex data augmentations (characteristic of contrastive methods) while sidestepping the direct reconstruction of raw data (characteristic of generative methods) and its associated overfitting issues. JEPAs have been successfully applied in other domains such as images (I-JEPA), video (V-JEPA), and audio (A-JEPA).

This work positions itself at the intersection of self-supervised graph representation learning and the emerging JEPA paradigm. The authors propose "Graph-JEPA," the first architecture designed to implement modern JEPA principles for learning graph-level representations. It addresses specific challenges inherent in graph data, such as defining appropriate context and target signals, designing an effective latent prediction task that aligns with the hierarchical nature of graph concepts, and ensuring the learned representations are expressive and semantic for various downstream tasks. The work aims to offer an efficient and robust alternative to existing self-supervised graph learning methods by leveraging the advantages of JEPAs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate a Graph Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture (Graph-JEPA) for self-supervised learning of graph-level representations. This central goal is driven by several specific motivations:

1. **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Self-Supervised Graph Learning:** The authors identify key challenges in current graph SSL paradigms. Contrastive learning methods are motivated by their reliance on complex, often non-trivial, data augmentation strategies and the computational burden of sampling and processing negative examples. Generative models, while offering an alternative, often suffer from overfitting to high-level data details and learning representations that are not consistently beneficial for diverse downstream tasks, a problem exacerbated by the non-Euclidean nature of graph data. Graph-JEPA seeks to bypass these issues by predicting in the latent space, thereby avoiding explicit reconstruction and the need for negative samples or complex augmentations.

2. **Developing a Graph-Specific JEPA Formulation:** While JEPAs have shown promise in other data modalities, adapting them to the graph domain presents unique challenges. The paper specifically aims to address:
 - **Context and Target Extraction:** For graphs, defining what constitutes a "context" (x) and "target" (y) signal for prediction is not straightforward. The work proposes a masked modeling approach where an input graph is partitioned into subgraphs, and the latent representation of masked target subgraphs is predicted from a context subgraph.
 - **Optimal Latent Prediction Task:** The hierarchical nature often implicit in graph-level concepts is a crucial characteristic. The authors hypothesize that a standard latent reconstruction objective (common in generic JEPAs) might be suboptimal for capturing this hierarchy. They are motivated to devise an alternative prediction objective that explicitly encourages the learning of hierarchically consistent concepts within the latent space.

3. **Learning Expressive and Semantic Graph Representations:** A core motivation is to ensure that Graph-JEPA learns representations that are not only effective but also highly semantic and expressive. This includes the ability to perform well on standard graph classification and regression tasks, as well as to differentiate between non-isomorphic graphs that are challenging for simpler graph isomorphism tests (like 1-WL).

4. **Improving Computational Efficiency:** By eliminating the need for complex data augmentation and negative sampling, the authors aim to develop a self-supervised learning framework that is more computationally efficient and easier to train compared to many existing contrastive graph SSL methods.

5. **Preventing Representation Collapse:** JEPAs, being self-distillation-based, are susceptible to representation collapse, where the model learns trivial, uninformative embeddings. The research is motivated to integrate established techniques (e.g., stop-gradient, momentum updates, simpler predictor parametrization) to ensure stable and meaningful representation learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Graph-JEPA operates by dividing an input graph into subgraphs, embedding these subgraphs, and then predicting the latent representation of selected target subgraphs from a context subgraph. The process involves four main stages: Spatial Partitioning, Subgraph Embedding, Context and Target Encoding, and Latent Target Prediction.

### 4. Spatial Partitioning

The initial step involves partitioning the input graph G = (V, E) into *p* non-overlapping subsets of nodes, {V1..., Vp}. This partitioning aims to embed a notion of locality and spatial awareness into the representations. The METIS graph clustering algorithm is employed, which partitions the graph to minimize edge cuts between clusters, thereby maximizing intra-cluster density and capturing local properties and community structures.
To address potential issues of lost edges and disconnected subgraphs from non-overlapping partitions, a 1-hop neighborhood expansion is applied. Each partition Vi is expanded to include its 1-hop neighbors from the original graph, forming G'i = (V'i, E'i). This allows information aggregation from neighboring regions and preserves signal propagation, crucial for broader graph-level pattern capture.

### 5. Subgraph Embedding

Each expanded subgraph G'i is then processed by a Graph Neural Network (GNN), specifically a GIN operator with support for edge features (GINE), to learn node embeddings. These node embeddings within each subgraph are subsequently mean-pooled to generate a fixed-size vector representation for each subgraph, denoted as {h1..., hp}.
To provide additional contextual information, positional embeddings are incorporated for each subgraph. This is achieved by taking the maximum Random Walk Structural Embedding (RWSE) across all nodes within a subgraph. The RWSE for a node *v* (Pv) is defined by the probabilities of returning to *v* after *k* steps of a random walk. For a subgraph *l*, its RWSE (Pl) is calculated as the element-wise maximum of the RWSEs of its constituent nodes (Pl = max_v∈Vl Pv). This assigns a global positional context to each subgraph, acting as an anchor for the predictor network.

### 6. Context and Target Encoding

In each training iteration, one random subgraph is chosen as the context (x), and *m* other random subgraphs are designated as targets (Y = {y1..., ym}). The context subgraph *x* is processed by a context encoder (Ec), yielding a latent representation *zx*. Similarly, the target subgraphs *Y* are processed by a target encoder (Et), producing a set of latent representations *Zy*. Both encoders are parametrized by Transformer encoder blocks; the target encoder specifically uses Hadamard self-attention, while the context encoder omits self-attention. A stop-gradient operation is applied to the target encoder's output, and its weights are updated via an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the context encoder's weights. This mechanism, combined with a simpler predictor network, is crucial for preventing representation collapse during self-distillation.

### 7. Latent Target Prediction (Novel Objective)

Instead of directly predicting high-dimensional latent codes, Graph-JEPA devises a novel objective to embed hierarchical information. Each target subgraph's latent embedding *Zyl* is transformed into a representation of a hyperbolic angle. The mean of the elements in *Zyl* defines a scalar *αyl*. This *αyl* is then used to compute 2D coordinates on the unit hyperbola: *ψyl = [cosh(αyl), sinh(αyl)]*.
The predictor module, a two-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP), is tasked with predicting these 2D hyperbolic coordinates (`hat_ψyl`) for each target subgraph. The prediction is conditioned on the context embedding *zx* and the target subgraph's positional encoding *Pl* (hat_ψyl = W2(σ(W1(zx + Pl) + b1)) + b2). The learning objective is a smooth L1 loss between the predicted (`hat_ψyl`) and the true (`ψyl`) hyperbolic coordinates (Eq. 6). This low-dimensional regression task is designed to balance the expressivity of high-dimensional embeddings with the hierarchical organization inherent to hyperbolic geometry, forcing the context encoder to understand hierarchical relationships between subgraphs.

### 8. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Graph-JEPA was conducted across various graph datasets and tasks, demonstrating its performance, efficiency, and robustness.

**Downstream Performance on TUD and ZINC Datasets:**
Graph-JEPA achieved competitive results on eight TUD benchmark datasets (PROTEINS, MUTAG, DD, REDDIT-BINARY, REDDIT-MULTI-5K, IMDB-BINARY, IMDB-MULTI) for graph classification and on the ZINC dataset for graph regression.
- It established state-of-the-art performance as a pre-trained backbone on five of the eight datasets and ranked second on another.
- Graph-JEPA consistently outperformed a fully supervised Graph Isomorphism Network (F-GIN) on all classification datasets, indicating the effectiveness of its self-supervised pre-training.
- It surpassed the performance of most contrastive (InfoGraph, GraphCL, MVGRL, AD-GCL) and generative (GraphMAE, S2GAE) graph SSL methods.
- Node-level self-predictive methods like BGRL showed comparatively lower performance for graph-level tasks, which is consistent with their design.
- The results exhibited some variance, attributed by the authors to the randomness in subgraph partitioning, susceptibility to representation collapse (especially with smaller, similar subgraphs), and the training protocol where the model was retrained for each cross-validation fold.

**Expressivity in Distinguishing Non-Isomorphic Graphs (EXP dataset):**
On the synthetic EXP dataset, designed to test the model's ability to distinguish non-isomorphic graphs that are indistinguishable by the 1-WL test, Graph-JEPA achieved an accuracy of 98.77% (±0.99). This performance is comparable to a fully supervised Graph-MLP-Mixer (100%) and significantly superior to other supervised GNNs (GCN, GatedGCN, GINE, GraphTransformer). This finding suggests that Graph-JEPA learns highly expressive graph representations capable of capturing fine-grained structural differences.

**Exploration of Graph-JEPA Latent Space:**
An ablation study comparing the proposed hyperbolic angle objective with Euclidean and Poincaré (hyperbolic) distance-based objectives revealed that the Graph-JEPA approach generally yielded the best performance.
- Euclidean distance performed less effectively.
- Poincaré embeddings, while conceptually aligned with hierarchy, faced computational instability and expense in higher dimensions. The proposed method provided a balance, leveraging the benefits of hyperbolic organization without the high-dimensional stability issues.
- Qualitative analysis (3D t-SNE) demonstrated a noticeable change in the curvature of the embedding space with the Graph-JEPA objective, providing visual evidence for the induced implicit hyperbolicity.

**Model Efficiency:**
Graph-JEPA demonstrated notable efficiency advantages during pre-training on larger datasets (IMDB, REDDIT-M5).
- It was observed to be up to 2.5 times faster than Graph-MAE and 8 times faster than MVGRL, indicating promising scaling behavior and the inherent efficiency of latent self-predictive models that avoid negative sampling and complex augmentations.

**Additional Ablation Studies:**
- **MLP Parametrization:** Replacing Transformer encoders with MLPs resulted in slightly reduced but still strong performance, indicating architectural flexibility while confirming the advantage of Transformer-based encoders in terms of overall performance and reduced variance.
- **Positional Embedding:** Using node-level (global) RWSEs consistently outperformed relative patch-level RWSEs. Global RWSEs provided a less ambiguous context to the predictor.
- **Random Partitioning:** While METIS-based partitioning was advocated, experiments showed that using random subgraphs for context and targets also yielded strong performance, highlighting the robustness of the JEPA architecture to the initial input generation.

**Long-Range Graph Benchmark (LRGB) Datasets:**
On the Peptides-struct dataset (multi-variate graph regression), Graph-JEPA achieved competitive results (Test MAE 0.305 ± 0.002), closely approaching supervised state-of-the-art models and outperforming simpler GNN baselines. Ablations confirmed the benefit of the positional embedding and the hyperbolic latent objective.
However, on the Peptides-func dataset (multi-label graph classification), Graph-JEPA's performance (Test AP 0.263 ± 0.003) significantly lagged behind supervised baselines. The authors suggest this disparity is due to Graph-JEPA's strength in capturing smooth global features (beneficial for regression) but its limitations in capturing localized, specific interactions crucial for multi-label classification with low correlation between labels.

### 9. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Graph-JEPA, marking the first architecture that applies modern Joint-Embedding Predictive Architecture principles to graph-level self-supervised learning. This work carries several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of graph representation learning:

1. **New Paradigm for Self-Supervised Graph Learning:** Graph-JEPA offers a distinct and effective alternative to traditional contrastive and generative approaches in self-supervised graph representation learning. By operating directly in the latent space and leveraging a predictive objective, it sidesteps the complexities of negative sampling, intricate data augmentations, and the overfitting tendencies often associated with reconstructing high-dimensional data distributions. This can simplify the development and training of SSL models for graphs.

2. **Enhanced Representation Quality and Expressiveness:** The empirical results demonstrate that Graph-JEPA learns highly semantic and expressive graph representations. Its ability to outperform supervised GIN models on various tasks and, notably, to distinguish non-isomorphic graphs that challenge the 1-WL test, indicates a capacity to capture rich structural and semantic information. This is crucial for applications requiring subtle discrimination between graph structures.

3. **Implicit Hierarchical Understanding:** The novel latent prediction objective, which projects high-dimensional latent codes onto 2D hyperbolic coordinates, implicitly imbues the learned representations with hierarchical organization. This approach provides a practical way to leverage the benefits of hyperbolic geometry for hierarchical concept learning without the computational and stability issues of full high-dimensional hyperbolic embeddings, which is particularly relevant for complex, hierarchical graph data.

4. **Improved Efficiency and Scalability:** Graph-JEPA exhibits superior computational efficiency compared to several existing graph SSL methods. By removing the need for memory-intensive negative samples and complex data augmentations, it offers a more scalable solution for pre-training on large graph datasets, potentially making advanced graph representation learning more accessible.

5. **Broad Applicability:** The model's competitive performance across a diverse range of graph classification and regression tasks on various benchmark datasets suggests its generalizability as a pre-training method for different types of graph data and downstream applications.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future investigations into JEPAs in the graph domain. It opens avenues for extending the framework to node-level and edge-level self-supervised learning, exploring theoretical properties of Graph-JEPA's expressiveness, and further optimizing the geometry of the latent space for graph-specific challenges. The insights into the limitations observed on tasks like Peptides-func also highlight important directions for developing more versatile architectures that can capture both global and localized interactions effectively.

In conclusion, Graph-JEPA represents a notable advancement in self-supervised graph representation learning, offering a robust, efficient, and expressive framework that effectively addresses several limitations of prior approaches.
