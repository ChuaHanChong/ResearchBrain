# Can LLMs Learn to Reason Robustly under Noisy Supervision?

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper presents a systematic analysis of noisy label mechanisms within the Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) framework for large language models. It introduces Online Label Refinement (OLR), a novel method that dynamically corrects potentially noisy training labels using the model's own generated majority answers, leading to consistent performance improvements in mathematical and general reasoning tasks across various noise conditions.

## Problem

- Training reasoning-oriented LLMs with RLVR relies on large volumes of perfectly curated data, which is difficult to acquire in real-world scenarios.
- Existing noisy label learning methods are not directly applicable to RLVR due to its near-infinite label space, on-policy data generation, and conditional label influence.
- A lack of systematic analysis and mitigation strategies for noisy labels specifically within the RLVR paradigm.

## Method

- Defined a taxonomy for noisy labels in RLVR, distinguishing between 'inactive' (not rollout-feasible) and 'active' (rollout-feasible) noisy labels based on their impact.
- Developed Online Label Refinement (OLR), a self-correcting mechanism that identifies and replaces noisy labels with model-generated majority answers based on two criteria: a positive convergence slope of the answer's pass-rate and historical consistency.
- Integrated OLR into the Group Relative Policy Optimization (==GRPO==) training process, allowing the model to dynamically refine its training data after an initial early learning phase.

## Results

- OLR achieved average gains of **3.6%** on in-distribution mathematical benchmarks and **3.3%** on out-of-distribution tasks under inactive noisy labels, maintaining performance at **50%** noise comparable to **10%** noise.
- Under active noisy labels, OLR produced average gains of **3.9%** on mathematical benchmarks and **4.6%** on out-of-distribution tasks, preventing model collapse observed in baselines.
- The method successfully reduced the proportion of original noisy labels by approximately **30%** and introduced minimal new errors (below **5%**), demonstrating high efficacy in label refinement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Identified 'Early Correctness Coherence,' a phenomenon where accuracy on both clean and noisy samples increases similarly during early RLVR training due to positive cross-sample coupling.
> - Active noisy labels are more detrimental to RLVR training than inactive noisy labels, as they can be reinforced and skew the policy.
> - The model's emergent reasoning capabilities, specifically its ability to generate consistent majority answers from rollouts, can be leveraged for effective self-correction of noisy training labels.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LLMs) has seen significant advancements, particularly with the emergence of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) as a paradigm for training reasoning-oriented models. Algorithms such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) and its variants, exemplified by models like DeepSeek-R1, have demonstrated effectiveness in domains requiring clear ground truth, such as mathematics and code. This success, however, is predicated on the availability of large quantities of meticulously curated data with perfect labels.

In practical applications, the acquisition of such high-quality datasets is often hindered by expert scarcity and the limitations of verifiers, leading to the unavoidable presence of noisy labels in training data. While noisy label learning is a well-established area in traditional supervised classification, existing techniques typically focus on static datasets and limited output label spaces. These methods are not directly applicable to RLVR, which operates with a near-infinite label space and on-policy data generation dynamics. A critical characteristic unique to most RLVR algorithms is that a label's influence on training is conditional on the current policy's ability to generate rollouts that realize that label. This distinction implies that not all incorrect labels contribute to loss in the same way, unlike in traditional classification.

This paper addresses this gap by presenting a systematic analysis of noisy label mechanisms specifically within the RLVR framework. It is positioned as an initial exploration into robust training of LLMs for reasoning tasks under such noisy supervision, where previous research has largely remained unaddressed. The proposed method, Online Label Refinement (OLR), is presented as the first approach designed to mitigate noisy labels in this specific context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to initiate a systematic analysis of noisy label mechanisms in the context of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). This overarching goal is motivated by the recognized dependence of successful RLVR models on large quantities of perfectly labeled data, which is often not available in real-world scenarios due to expert scarcity and limitations of verifiers. The unavoidable presence of noisy labels necessitates an understanding of their impact on RLVR training, especially given the unique characteristics of this paradigm compared to traditional supervised learning.

Specifically, the authors are motivated to:

1. **Formally Define Noisy Labels in RLVR:** A key motivation is to move beyond the traditional classification-centric view of noisy labels and establish a specific taxonomy relevant to RLVR's on-policy generation and near-infinite label space. This led to the distinction between "inactive noisy labels," which are incorrect but cannot be generated by the current policy (thereby primarily reducing data efficiency), and "active noisy labels," which are incorrect but can be generated and reinforced by the policy (posing a more significant risk of skewing the model towards incorrect distributions).
2. **Investigate Training Dynamics under Noisy Supervision:** The research aims to observe and theoretically characterize how RLVR models learn when exposed to noisy data. This led to the identification of the "Early Correctness Coherence" phenomenon, where accuracy on both clean and noisy samples increases similarly during early training stages before diverging. The motivation here is to understand if and how a model's intrinsic learning capabilities might implicitly address or reveal correct answers even within noisy datasets.
3. **Develop a Novel Mitigation Strategy:** Building upon the insights gained from the analysis, particularly the "Early Correctness Coherence" phenomenon, a core objective is to propose a method for robust training. The motivation for "Online Label Refinement (OLR)" is to leverage the model's own emerging reasoning capabilities – specifically, its ability to generate consistent "majority answers" from rollouts – to dynamically self-correct or refine potentially noisy training labels. This approach is intended to provide a self-improving mechanism that reduces reliance on externally perfect labels.
4. **Evaluate Robustness and Generalizability:** The research seeks to comprehensively evaluate the proposed OLR method across various conditions. This includes testing its effectiveness under a range of noise ratios (from 0.1 to 0.9) and against both inactive and active noisy label settings. Furthermore, evaluation across multiple in-distribution mathematical reasoning benchmarks and out-of-distribution tasks (open-domain, graduate-level science, academic reasoning) is intended to ascertain the method's robustness and its capacity to generalize to diverse reasoning challenges.

In summary, the motivation stems from the practical challenges of applying RLVR due to noisy data, driving a systematic investigation into the problem and the development of a data-driven, self-correcting solution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves a multi-faceted approach, combining problem formulation, theoretical analysis, and the development and extensive empirical evaluation of a novel technique.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Noisy Label Taxonomy

The authors formally define the standard RLVR problem, where a policy $\pi_\theta(y|x)$ generates solutions $y$ for prompts $x$. Training data is generated on-policy via $K$ rollouts. The policy is updated using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which computes advantages $A(x,y^{(k)})$ based on binary rewards $r(x,y)$.
A central contribution is the classification of noisy labels within this framework:
- **Rollout Feasibility:** A solution $y$ is defined as rollout-feasible if $\pi_\theta(y|x) > 0$.
- **Inactive Noisy Label:** An incorrect label $\tilde{y}(x) \ne y^*(x)$ that is not rollout-feasible ($\pi_\theta(\tilde{y}|x) = 0$). These labels reduce data efficiency but do not actively misdirect the policy.
- **Active Noisy Label:** An incorrect label $\tilde{y}(x) \ne y^*(x)$ that is rollout-feasible ($\pi_\theta(\tilde{y}|x) > 0$). These are more detrimental as they can be reinforced and skew the policy.

#### 3.2. Analysis of Early Correctness Coherence Phenomenon

Empirical experiments (Figure 2) revealed a phenomenon termed "Early Correctness Coherence," where accuracy on both clean and noisy samples increases similarly in early training, before diverging. The authors attribute this to "positive cross-sample coupling," where learning from clean samples inadvertently benefits noisy ones due to shared model parameters.
Theorem 3.4 (formalized as Theorem A.1 in the Appendix) provides a theoretical basis for this observation. It posits that under certain conditions (initial rollout bias favoring correct answers, positive cross-sample coupling, and a sufficiently low noise ratio $\rho < \rho_c$), the log-ratio of the probability of the correct solution to the noisy solution, $L_t(x)$, increases over early training epochs. This implies that correct answers can emerge with increasing probability even for noisy samples.

#### 3.3. Online Label Refinement (OLR)

Motivated by the Early Correctness Coherence, the authors propose Online Label Refinement (OLR) as a mechanism to dynamically correct potentially noisy labels. OLR operates by replacing an original noisy label $\tilde{y}(x)$ with a model-generated majority answer $\hat{y}_t(x)$ when specific criteria are met. The process involves:
1. **Rollout Generation and Majority Answer Identification:** At each epoch $t$, for each prompt $x$, the policy generates $K$ rollouts $Y_t(x)$. From these, the majority answer $y_{maj,t}(x)$ and its empirical pass rate $p_{maj,t}(x)$ are computed.
2. **History Tracking:** A trajectory history $H_t(x)$ is maintained, recording the majority answer and pass rate at each epoch for each sample.
3. **Label Refinement Criteria (applied after an initial early learning phase of $T$ epochs):**
 - **Positive Convergence Slope ($S_t(x)$):** The linear regression slope of the majority answer's pass-rate trajectory over time is calculated. A slope $S_t(x) > \delta_{slope}$ indicates increasing model confidence and convergence towards that answer.
 - **Historical Consistency ($C_t(x)$):** The current majority answer $y_{maj,t}(x)$ must match the historical majority $y_{hist,t}(x)$, which is the most frequently occurring majority answer in the sample's history $H_t(x)$. This filters out transient or accidental majority answers.
4. **Label Replacement:** If both $S_t(x) > \delta_{slope}$ and $C_t(x)$ are true, the effective label $\hat{y}_t(x)$ used for reward computation in GRPO is replaced by $y_{maj,t}(x)$. Otherwise, the original label $\tilde{y}(x)$ is retained.
Theorem 3.5 provides a theoretical justification for OLR, demonstrating that it can select correct labels with high probability, reduce the effective noise ratio, and increase the tolerable noise threshold.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Base Model:** Qwen3-4B-Base was primarily used, with additional experiments on Qwen3-8B-Base and Deepseek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B to assess scalability and versatility.
- **Dataset and Noise Injection:** 800 instances from DAPO-Math were used for training, with noise ratios ranging from 0.1 to 0.9. A larger 4,000-instance dataset was used for scalability tests. Inactive noise was generated by replacing correct labels with unlikely strings. Active noise was generated dynamically during inference by replacing correct labels with incorrect answers produced by the model itself in an on-policy manner.
- **Evaluation Benchmarks:**
 - **In-distribution (ID):** AIME 2024, AIME 2025, AMC, MATH-500, Minerva, OlympiadBench (mathematical reasoning). Metrics included avg@32 for AIME/AMC and pass@1 for others.
 - **Out-of-distribution (OOD):** ARC-c, GPQA-diamond, MMLU-Pro (covering open-domain, graduate-level science, and academic reasoning).
- **Baselines:** Two categories were compared:
 - Unsupervised methods (e.g., TTRL, Co-Reward, Self-Certainty, Token-Level Entropy, Sentence-Level Entropy).
 - Traditional noisy label learning methods and regularization techniques (e.g., Confidence Penalty, Label Smoothing, Small-loss Selection, Random Selection).
- **Hyperparameters:** Key OLR parameters were $\delta_{slope}=0.05$ and early learning phase $T=5$. Other training parameters included 8 rollouts ($K$), a learning rate of 1e-6, and a KL loss weight of 1e-3.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding noisy supervision in RLVR and the effectiveness of the proposed Online Label Refinement (OLR) method.

#### 4.1. OLR Performance Across Noise Ratios

OLR demonstrated consistent performance improvements across the tested noise ratios, ranging from 0.1 to 0.9, and under both inactive and active noisy label conditions.
- **Inactive Noisy Labels:** OLR achieved average gains of 3.6% on in-distribution mathematical benchmarks and 3.3% on out-of-distribution tasks. At a 50% inactive noise ratio, OLR maintained performance levels comparable to those observed at 10% noise, indicating its robustness.
- **Active Noisy Labels:** This scenario presented a more significant challenge, leading to a sharp decline and potential collapse of models without OLR. However, OLR still produced average gains of 3.9% on mathematical benchmarks and 4.6% on out-of-distribution tasks.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Baseline Methods

Under a 50% noise ratio, OLR generally outperformed a range of baseline methods.
- **Unsupervised Methods:** Most unsupervised baselines resulted in severe model degradation. The most effective among them, TTRL and Co-Reward, only marginally improved over naive noisy training on in-distribution tasks. OLR achieved average gains of 5.0% and 8.0% over these methods in in-distribution settings under active and inactive noise, respectively, and outperformed the best unsupervised method by 3.3% on out-of-distribution tasks.
- **Traditional Noisy Label Learning Methods:** Many traditional methods proved ineffective in the RLVR context. Confidence Penalty showed some improvement in active noisy label settings, but others, notably small-loss selection, harmed performance. OLR surpassed the best of these methods by an average of 6.1% on in-distribution benchmarks.

#### 4.3. Training Dynamics and Early Correctness Coherence

Analysis of training dynamics (Figure 3, 6) under 50% active noise revealed:
- **Early Correctness Coherence:** In the initial learning phase, the majority answer accuracy for both clean and noisy samples increased significantly (e.g., from 50% to over 60% for active noise, and over 70% for inactive noise). This supported the theoretical premise that the model learns to produce correct answers even for noisy samples early on.
- **OLR's Impact:** After OLR was applied, accuracy further increased (e.g., to over 80% for active noise), indicating that OLR successfully leverages and builds upon this early learning.
- **Label Selection Efficacy:** Majority answers from OLR-selected samples achieved high accuracy (over 90%), while unselected samples, retaining original labels, showed lower accuracy. OLR reduced the proportion of original noisy labels by approximately 30% and introduced minimal new errors (below 5%).
- **Positive Pass Rate Slope:** The slope of the pass rate for majority answers remained positive throughout training, corroborating the continuous emergence of correct answers through cross-sample coupling.
- **Balanced Selection:** OLR selected over 40% of samples from both clean and noisy sets, indicating it did not disproportionately favor simple examples.

#### 4.4. Scalability and Versatility

When tested with a larger dataset (4,000 samples at 50% noise ratio), OLR continued to enhance reasoning performance, yielding average improvements of 4.6% on in-distribution benchmarks and 2.0% on out-of-distribution benchmarks across both noise scenarios. Experiments on different model sizes (Qwen3-8B-Base) and types (Deepseek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B) further demonstrated OLR's versatility, showing consistent performance gains.

#### 4.5. Parameter Sensitivity and Ablation Studies

- **Parameter Sensitivity:** OLR exhibited robustness to variations in the slope threshold ($\delta_{slope}$) and the duration of the early learning phase ($T$). Optimal performance was observed at $\delta_{slope}=0.05$ and $T=5$, representing a balance between capturing reliable trends and avoiding premature or insufficient label refinement.
- **Ablation Study:** Removing either the "Positive Convergence Slope" criterion or the "Historical Consistency" criterion consistently led to a substantial drop in performance across all settings. This confirms that both criteria are complementary and essential for OLR's effectiveness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding and practical application of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) in scenarios involving imperfect data. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized in several key areas:

#### 5.1. Foundational Understanding of Noisy Labels in RLVR

The paper provides a fundamental contribution by offering the first systematic analysis of noisy label mechanisms specific to RLVR. By classifying noisy labels into "active" and "inactive" categories based on their rollout feasibility, the authors establish a crucial taxonomy. This distinction is vital because it highlights how RLVR's on-policy generation fundamentally alters the impact of noisy labels compared to traditional supervised learning. This new framework serves as a basis for future theoretical and empirical investigations into data quality within RLVR.

#### 5.2. Discovery and Characterization of Early Correctness Coherence

The identification and theoretical analysis of the "Early Correctness Coherence" phenomenon reveal an intrinsic learning dynamic of LLMs under noisy RLVR. The finding that correct answers can emerge even from noisy samples during early training stages, attributed to positive cross-sample coupling, provides an optimistic perspective on the robustness of these models. This insight motivates the development of self-correction mechanisms that leverage the model's own evolving understanding.

#### 5.3. Development of a Novel Self-Correcting Mechanism (OLR)

Online Label Refinement (OLR) represents a significant methodological advancement as the first proposed technique for denoising labels during RLVR training. Unlike traditional noisy label learning methods, OLR is tailored to RLVR's generative nature and on-policy dynamics. By utilizing the model's own policy-generated majority answers, guided by convergence slope and historical consistency, OLR offers a data-driven and self-improving approach to label refinement. This mitigates the dependence on perfectly clean labels, which are often scarce.

#### 5.4. Enhanced Robustness and Reasoning Performance

The experimental results demonstrate that OLR consistently and significantly improves the reasoning performance of LLMs across a wide range of noise ratios (from 0.1 to 0.9) and under both inactive and the more challenging active noisy label conditions. The ability of OLR to maintain performance even at high noise levels, where naive RLVR models often collapse, attests to its practical robustness. This enhancement in performance, both on in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks, indicates that OLR can improve the reliability and generalization capabilities of RLVR-trained LLMs in real-world applications.

#### 5.5. Practical Implications for LLM Training

The introduction of OLR offers a pragmatic solution to a critical bottleneck in the scalable training of reasoning-oriented LLMs: the cost and difficulty of obtaining abundant, perfectly labeled data. By enabling models to effectively learn from noisy supervision, OLR lowers the data quality requirements, potentially accelerating the development and deployment of advanced LLMs in various domains. The negligible computational overhead of OLR further enhances its practicality.

#### 5.6. Broader Applicability and Future Research Directions

The principles underlying OLR, particularly the leveraging of intrinsic model consistency and emergent correctness, may extend beyond RLVR. The authors suggest potential applications in other domains where noisy label learning remains underexplored, such as Visual Language Models (VLM) and agent systems. This research therefore provides a foundational contribution that could inspire similar self-correction mechanisms across a broader spectrum of AI paradigms, impacting the general robustness and autonomy of future generative AI systems.
