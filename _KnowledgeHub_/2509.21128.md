# RL Squeezes, SFT Expands: A Comparative Study of Reasoning LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The University of Tokyo quantitatively compared reasoning in large language models after Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL), establishing that RL prunes incorrect reasoning paths and concentrates functionality, while SFT expands the diversity of correct trajectories and distributes functional importance. This work provides a mechanistic explanation for the empirical success of combined SFT+RL training strategies in enhancing LLM reasoning.

## Problem

- Existing research predominantly relies on outcome-based metrics like Pass@k, overlooking the qualitative changes in the underlying reasoning processes of LLMs.
- The distinct and complementary roles of SFT and RL in shaping LLM reasoning mechanisms remain largely unclarified, leading to 'trial-and-error' approaches in current training methodologies.
- A debate persists regarding whether RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) develops new reasoning capabilities or primarily elicits existing ones from pre-trained base models.

## Method

- The authors developed a novel analytical framework to investigate LLM reasoning paths at two granularities: a ==trajectory-level analysis== and a ==step-level analysis==.
- Trajectory-level analysis involved clustering unique correct and incorrect reasoning outputs based on character n-gram similarity (chrF) to quantify their diversity.
- Step-level analysis constructed =='reasoning graphs'== by segmenting responses into logical expressions, embedding them into a shared space, and then analyzing their global topological properties and local ==graphlet structures==.

## Results

- RL consistently reduced the number of unique incorrect trajectories and, notably, also reduced the number of unique correct trajectories, which helps explain why base models might outperform RL models at high Pass@k values.
- RL models exhibited significantly steeper exponential decay rates (e.g., ~**2.5 times** higher than Base models) for reasoning graph metrics, indicating a concentration of key functionalities into fewer steps.
- SFT models showed flatter decay rates (e.g., reduced to about **one-third** of Base models), signifying a more uniform distribution of reasoning functionalities and a shift from modular to globally connected graph structures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RL 'squeezes' reasoning by pruning incorrect trajectories and concentrating functional importance into a smaller, more specialized subset of reasoning steps, leading to a hub-centralized graph structure.
> - SFT 'expands' reasoning by increasing the diversity of correct trajectories and distributing functional importance more uniformly across a broader range of reasoning steps, creating a globally connected graph.
> - The two-stage SFT+RL procedure achieves maximal Pass@1 performance by first leveraging SFT to expand the repertoire of correct solution paths, then using RL to effectively compress and eliminate incorrect ones.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The advent of advanced large language models (LLMs) and their open-sourcing has intensified research into post-training methods for enhancing reasoning abilities, particularly in complex logical tasks such as mathematics and coding. The two predominant learning paradigms in this area are Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) with verifiable rewards (RLVR).

SFT typically involves training an LLM to imitate "teacher" policies by maximizing log-likelihood, often using reasoning traces generated by human annotators or more capable models. Conversely, RL aims to maximize expected rewards by optimizing the probability of producing correct solutions in tasks where outcomes can be verified.

Previous research has explored the nature of capabilities introduced by RLVR. Some studies suggest that RLVR primarily elicits pre-existing capabilities of the base model rather than developing novel ones. For instance, investigations using the Pass@k metric, which measures the probability of finding at least one correct solution among *k* samples, have indicated that base models can eventually surpass RL models in Pass@k as *k* increases, implying that the underlying reasoning capabilities might already exist in the base model's broader sampling distribution. Additionally, it has been theorized that RLVR cannot exceed the inherent support of the base model, and advanced reasoning mechanisms like backtracking are only amplified if already present.

Despite these observations, current state-of-the-art models for mathematical and coding reasoning often employ a two-stage training approach: SFT followed by RL. This method, exemplified by models like ProRL and AceReason built upon DeepSeek-R1 distillation checkpoints, suggests complementary roles for SFT and RL. However, the distinct contributions of each method to the *reasoning process itself*, beyond overall accuracy metrics, have remained largely underexplored. Existing analyses often focus on outcome-based metrics like Pass@k, without delving into the qualitative and quantitative changes within the LLM's step-by-step reasoning. This paper positions itself within this gap, aiming to systematically investigate how SFT and RL fundamentally sculpt the reasoning process.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate how Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) distinctively shape the reasoning processes of large language models (LLMs), moving beyond traditional accuracy-based evaluations. The authors aim to provide a nuanced understanding of why the current two-stage training approach, involving SFT followed by RL, has proven effective in developing advanced reasoning LLMs.

The motivation stems from several observations in the current landscape of LLM reasoning:
1. **Limited understanding of underlying mechanisms:** While SFT and RL are widely adopted for enhancing reasoning, the precise mechanisms by which they influence the internal reasoning process of LLMs are not fully understood. Previous work primarily focused on output accuracy (e.g., Pass@k), which reveals *what* the models achieve but not *how* their reasoning trajectories are altered.
2. **Conflicting observations on RL's role:** Some studies suggest RL might merely re-prioritize existing capabilities of base models rather than generating new ones, an idea supported by observations that base models can match or exceed RL models' Pass@k at high *k*. This raises questions about the intrinsic impact of RL on reasoning diversity and strategy.
3. **Empirical success of two-stage training:** State-of-the-art reasoning LLMs often utilize a sequence of SFT followed by RL. However, the synergistic effects and distinct roles of each stage in this successful "recipe" are often discovered through trial-and-error, lacking a principled explanation. Understanding these roles could lead to more efficient training methodologies.

To address these motivations, the paper introduces a novel analysis framework to:
- **Quantify reasoning paths:** Develop metrics and methods to measure and compare the structure of reasoning trajectories.
- **Capture qualitative changes:** Identify how the characteristics of these reasoning paths evolve under SFT and RL training.
- **Investigate at multiple granularities:** Analyze the reasoning process at two levels:
 - **Trajectory-level:** Examining complete reasoning outputs as distinct paths.
 - **Step-level:** Decomposing reasoning into individual logical steps represented as nodes in a "reasoning graph."

By systematically studying these aspects across different model sizes and domains, the authors aim to delineate the shared and distinct characteristics imprinted by RL and SFT, thereby offering empirical insights into the efficacy of current training practices and guiding future research directions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a comparative study across various LLM training stages (Base, RL-only, SFT-only, SFT+RL) and model sizes (1.5B, 7B, 14B parameters) on mathematical and code reasoning tasks. The methodology is bifurcated into trajectory-level and step-level analysis.

#### 3.1. Models and Datasets

The study utilized models from the Qwen2.5-Math and Llama-3.1 families, specifically:
- **Base Models:** Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, Qwen2.5-7B, Llama-3.1-8B.
- **RL Models:** Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Oat-Zero, Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Oat-Zero, Qwen-2.5-14B-SimpleRL-Zoo, Qwen2.5-7B-SimpleRL-Zoo, Llama-3.1-8B-SimpleRL-Zoo. These models underwent RL training (e.g., using GRPO or Dr.GRPO) from their respective base models.
- **SFT Models:** DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B. These were fine-tuned using DeepSeek-R1's distillation data.
- **SFT+RL Models:** Nemotron-Research-Reasoning-Qwen-1.5B, AceReason-Nemotron-7B, AceReason-Nemotron-14B, AceReason-Nemotron-1.1-7B. These represent models that received RL training after an initial SFT stage.

Evaluation datasets included AIME24, AIME25, and AMC23 for mathematical reasoning, and HumanEval for code generation.

#### 3.2. Sample Generation

For each problem and model, 256 independent samples (M=256) were generated using a temperature of 0.6, top *p* of 0.95, and a maximum response length of 16000 tokens. Specific prompt templates (Qwen, Nemotron-Qwen, R1, Llama templates) were adapted per model to ensure consistency with prior work and proper stop token generation. Pass@k metrics were computed to establish baseline performance.

#### 3.3. Trajectory-Level Analysis

This level treats entire reasoning outputs as single trajectories.
- **Unique Trajectory Identification:** For each problem and model, the M sampled outputs were split into correct and incorrect sets based on verifiable rewards. Pairwise similarities between trajectories within each set were computed using the chrF metric (character n-gram F-score, with β=2), which is robust to morphological variations.
- **Clustering:** Hierarchical clustering (UPGMA) was applied to the similarity matrices, with a similarity threshold of 60, to determine the number of unique correct and incorrect trajectory clusters. This quantifies the diversity of reasoning paths.

#### 3.4. Step-Level Analysis

This delves into the granularity of individual reasoning steps.
- **Reasoning Graph Construction:**
 - **Sentence Segmentation:** Model responses were segmented into sentences using punctuation and newline characters, with rules for merging short segments and splitting long ones.
 - **Sentence Embedding:** Each sentence was mapped to a d-dimensional vector using BGE-large-en-v1.5 (d=1024).
 - **Node Definition:** A global set of sentence embeddings from all models and problems was created. K-means clustering (K=2000, L2 distance, 10 restarts, 300 iterations) was applied to these embeddings to define K "nodes" in a shared semantic space. This allows direct comparison of graph properties across models.
 - **Edge Formation:** For each trajectory, a sequence of node transitions was derived. Consecutive identical cluster assignments were merged to avoid self-loops. Directed edges were formed between distinct consecutive nodes, with edge weights reflecting inter-cluster distance and transition frequency.
- **Global Reasoning Graph Structure Analysis:**
 - **Decay Rate Analysis:** For each model's integrated reasoning graph (G*l*), node visitation frequency, node degree (in-degree + out-degree), and betweenness centrality were computed. Rank plots of these metrics were generated, and their exponential decay rate (β) was estimated by linear regression on log-linear plots. This quantifies the concentration or distribution of functionality.
 - **Topological Metrics:** Eight standard graph metrics were calculated: edge density, normalized clustering coefficient, assortativity, modularity, Freeman centralization, normalized average path length, global efficiency, and algebraic connectivity. These metrics characterize the overall connectivity, community structure, and robustness of the reasoning graphs.
- **Local Reasoning Graph Structure Analysis:**
 - **Graphlet Analysis:** The proportions of 4-node graphlets (G3-G8, representing small induced subgraphs) were counted within the integrated reasoning graphs. This reveals local structural motifs, such as cyclic patterns indicative of backtracking.

#### 3.5. Ablation Studies

- **Reasoning Graph Construction:** Ablations were performed on the 7B models (AIME24) varying the number of clusters (K=1000, 3000), distance metric (cosine distance), and sentence embedding model (GTE-base-en-v1.5).
- **Graph Sparsification:** To assess robustness to graph density, edges were sparsified by retaining only the top-10 or top-20 closest edges based on L2 norm, and decay rates were re-evaluated.
- **SFT Data Type:** The SFT model definition was also evaluated using a dataset with single response SFT (s1k-1.1 dataset) to confirm the generalizability of findings regarding SFT's effects.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Trajectory-Level Analysis: RL Squeezes, SFT Expands Unique Reasoning Trajectories

The trajectory-level analysis, examining the diversity of correct and incorrect reasoning paths, yielded distinct effects for RL and SFT:
- **RL's effect:** Across all model sizes and datasets (AIME24, AIME25, AMC23, HumanEval), applying RL to either a Base model or an SFT model consistently and dramatically reduced the number of unique *incorrect* trajectories. This indicates that RL effectively compresses undesirable reasoning paths, concentrating probability mass on correct solutions. However, RL also reduced the number of unique *correct* trajectories, providing an explanation for observations that Base models may surpass RL models in Pass@k at large *k* (as RL narrows the diversity of successful paths).
- **SFT's effect:** When applied to a Base model, SFT significantly increased the number of unique *correct* trajectories. This suggests that SFT enables the model to learn and generate novel, successful solution strategies not present in the Base model. Crucially, SFT was observed to preserve a non-negligible number of *incorrect* trajectories, implying it does not inherently guarantee improvements in Pass@1.
- **SFT+RL Synergy:** The two-stage SFT+RL procedure exhibited a complementary mechanism: SFT initially expands the repertoire of correct reasoning paths, and subsequent RL then effectively suppresses the remaining incorrect paths. This combination leads to maximized Pass@1 performance by both acquiring new strategies and refining them.

#### 4.2. Step-Level Analysis: Global Reasoning Graph Structure and Functionality

The step-level analysis, focusing on the topological characteristics of reasoning graphs constructed from individual sentences, revealed how RL and SFT modulate graph structure and functionality:
- **Exponential Decay Rates (RL Squeezes, SFT Expands Functionality):** Rank plots for node visitation frequency, degree, and betweenness centrality consistently followed approximate exponential laws.
 - **RL:** Training with RL consistently increased the exponential decay rate (β) by approximately 2.5 times compared to Base models. This indicates that RL consolidates key graph functionalities (e.g., high visitation, many connections, central positions) into a smaller subset of highly prominent reasoning steps (nodes).
 - **SFT:** In contrast, SFT reduced the exponential decay rate (β) to approximately one-third. This suggests that SFT homogenizes reasoning functionalities, distributing importance and activity more uniformly across a larger number of reasoning steps.
 - This "squeezing" by RL and "expanding" by SFT pattern was consistent across model sizes and observed in both mathematical and code domains.
- **Global Topological Metrics:**
 - **Base Models:** Reasoning graphs of Base models were characterized by high modularity, low global efficiency, and low algebraic connectivity. This implies a community-structured graph with distinct, loosely connected clusters of reasoning steps, limiting global exploration.
 - **RL Models:** RL transformed the Base model graphs into hub-centralized structures, exhibiting high edge density, low clustering coefficient, low assortativity, and high Freeman centralization. This suggests a graph optimized for efficient traversal through a few highly connected "hub" nodes.
 - **SFT/SFT+RL Models:** These models displayed low modularity, high global efficiency, and high algebraic connectivity. Their reasoning graphs are characterized by high robustness and superior reachability, lacking distinct community boundaries and facilitating more globally connected reasoning.
- **Correlation with Performance:** Global efficiency and algebraic connectivity were positively correlated with the Pass@1/Pass@k ratio (indicating less benefit from increased sampling), while modularity was negatively correlated. This suggests that graph structures facilitating efficient traversal across the entire graph are associated with stronger single-attempt reasoning performance.
- **Ablation Study Consistency:** The observed trends in exponential decay rates and global topological metrics remained consistent even when varying graph construction parameters (number of clusters, distance metric, sentence encoder) and under graph sparsification.

#### 4.3. Step-Level Analysis: Local Reasoning Graph Structure (Graphlets)

- **Increased Cyclic Structures:** Both RL and SFT were found to convert local acyclic graph structures (G3, G4 graphlets) into more cyclic ones (G7, G8 graphlets). This suggests that both training paradigms introduce or enhance local reasoning patterns indicative of backtracking and verification mechanisms.
- **Limited Explanatory Power of Local Structures:** While local graphlet proportions showed similar changes across RL, SFT, and SFT+RL models (relative to Base), these similarities did not fully account for the significant performance disparities observed between these models. This implies that global topological characteristics play a more critical role in overall reasoning performance than local structural motifs alone.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to a deeper understanding of how different post-training strategies, Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT), fundamentally alter the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). The findings provide a novel "reasoning path perspective" that moves beyond accuracy metrics to analyze qualitative and quantitative changes in reasoning trajectories and their underlying graph structures.

The key significance and potential impact areas include:

1. **Explaining Two-Stage Training Efficacy:** The study offers empirical justification for the current state-of-the-art practice of applying SFT followed by RL. It clarifies that SFT plays a crucial role in *expanding* the diversity of correct reasoning trajectories, essentially teaching new solution strategies. Subsequently, RL *compresses* and refines these trajectories, suppressing incorrect paths and concentrating the model's probability mass on highly effective solutions. This interpretation provides a principled understanding of the synergy observed in top-performing models.

2. **Addressing Prior Observations:** The findings offer explanations for previously observed phenomena:
 - The observation that RL can reduce the number of *correct* trajectories clarifies why base models might, in some cases, exhibit competitive Pass@k performance at large *k* compared to RL-trained models (which might sacrifice diversity for Pass@1 concentration).
 - The finding that SFT preserves incorrect trajectories provides an interpretation for "pseudo-reasoning" paths induced by SFT-only models, as SFT focuses on imitation rather than strict correctness filtering.

3. **Guiding Data Curation Strategies:** The distinct mechanisms of RL and SFT suggest implications for how training data for reasoning tasks should be constructed. Understanding which types of reasoning paths are expanded or compressed by each method can inform the design of more targeted and effective datasets for SFT (e.g., focusing on diverse correct demonstrations) and for RL (e.g., providing signals for path pruning).

4. **Informing Efficient Learning Approaches:** The step-level analysis, particularly the observation that RL consolidates functionality into fewer, highly central reasoning steps while SFT distributes it, opens avenues for more efficient training. Future work could explore:
 - **Targeted RL:** Applying RL specifically to "functional steps" (e.g., hub or central nodes) in the reasoning graph might further improve performance and reduce computational costs.
 - **Exploration-Exploitation Balance:** Investigating whether exploration bonuses in RL primarily prevent the "squeezing" phenomenon (diversity collapse) or actively encourage "expansion" akin to SFT's role.

5. **Advancing Graph-Based Reasoning Analysis:** The introduction and validation of a shared sentence embedding space for constructing reasoning graphs across different models represent a methodological advance. This allows for direct comparison of topological properties, which could become a standard tool for analyzing LLM reasoning behaviors. The identified correlations between global graph metrics (e.g., high global efficiency, low modularity) and reasoning performance (high Pass@1/Pass@k) highlight the importance of graph structure for effective problem-solving. Incorporating such graph metrics as process rewards in RL could be a promising direction.

In summary, this research provides a nuanced, mechanistic view of how RL and SFT shape LLM reasoning. By articulating their complementary roles and the structural changes they induce, it offers actionable insights for designing more effective training regimes and data engineering strategies for future reasoning LLMs.
