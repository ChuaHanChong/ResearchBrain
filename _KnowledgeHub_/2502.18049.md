# Recursive Learning Without Collapse: A Weighting-Based Stabilization Framework

> [!summary] Summary
> This work studies recursive generative-model training where each round mixes newly collected real data with synthetic data from the previous model, and proposes a weighted training scheme that assigns separate weights to the two sources. Across Gaussian estimation, generalized linear models and nonparametric distribution estimation, the optimal real-data weight follows one unified expression, equal to the reciprocal of the golden ratio when real and synthetic sample sizes match.

## Problem

- Prior collapse analyses reuse one fixed real dataset, so synthetic samples carry no information beyond the original samples and cannot improve the final model.
- Data-accumulation remedies keep error bounded but pile up excessive synthetic data, leaving the final model worse than one trained on real data alone.
- Empirical evidence that modest synthetic mixing with fresh real data helps had no theory: no optimal mixing ratio, weighting rule, or collapse threshold.

## Method

- **Fresh data augmentation framework**: each step trains only on a newly collected real batch plus synthetic data from the previous model, with no retention of past real datasets.
- ==Weighted training scheme== places weight w on real terms and 1-w on synthetic terms, instantiated as ==weighted mean and covariance estimators==, ==weighted MLE== for generalized linear models, and ==weighted empirical CDF==.
- A formal definition of ==collapse== as ==estimator inconsistency==, plus finite-sample and asymptotic analysis giving unified limiting error C(w,k) and optimal weight w* = (sqrt(k^2+4k) - k)/2.

## Results

- Theory shows covariance error diverges when the real-data weight falls below **1 - sqrt((m-1)/(m+1))**, while the optimal weight cuts error to a factor **(sqrt(k^2+4k) - k)/2** below real-data-only estimation.
- Simulations with **100 samples** over **1,000 recursive steps** and **1,000 replications** confirm divergence at weight 0 for Gaussian, linear, logistic and Poisson settings; the CDF error saturates at **1/6**.
- Sweeping the weight from **0.2 to 0.8** puts the error minimum near **0.62** across all models, in simulation and on the Adult dataset with **500 real and 500 synthetic samples** per round.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Keep real and synthetic samples distinguishable and weight them separately: naive pooling by sample count is always suboptimal, and arbitrarily so when synthetic data dominate.
> - Synthetic data only help above a weight threshold; below it, mixing actively degrades efficiency relative to fitting on the fresh real batch alone.
> - The guarantee rests on knowing each sample's provenance, so unlabeled web-scale corpora, where watermarks or detectors fail, break the scheme's core assumption.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A model is retrained repeatedly while new real data arrives, but old real data cannot be retained or reused. The previous model can still supply synthetic samples. The risk is treating those samples as interchangeable with fresh observations. The authors propose training on each new real-data batch **and** synthetic samples from the previous model, with separate weights for the two sources rather than letting their sample counts determine their influence. They motivate the setting partly by privacy and retention limits. They say it is most relevant when raw historical data, and informative summaries compatible with future training, are unavailable.

Paper Figure 4 illustrates the fresh-data augmentation framework: a new real batch and synthetic data from a preceding model feed the next model, which then generates synthetic data for a later round. It is a schematic, not a measured performance result.

### 2. Weight sources, not sample counts

In [*Self-Consuming Generative Models Go MAD*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2307.01850), Alemohammad et al. studied a fresh-data loop in image generation. They reported empirically that modest amounts of synthetic data can help, while too much can hurt. The present paper derives optimal weights and error behavior for specified statistical models and metrics.

At each round, the procedure combines the newly collected real batch with synthetic samples from the previous model. Each batch is normalized by its own size. The real-data objective then gets weight $w$, and the synthetic objective gets $1-w$. Thus $w$ is a training-objective weight, not necessarily the raw fraction of samples. The paper studies this scheme in Gaussian estimation, generalized linear models (GLMs), and nonparametric cumulative-distribution estimation.

The key comparison is against ordinary pooled training. In the Gaussian mean result, pooling real and synthetic datasets directly implies a real-data weight of $n/(n+m)$, which is generally not the optimal weight. The authors establish strict suboptimality of that implicit weight for Gaussian mean estimation. In their GLM setting, they show lower asymptotic scaled error for the optimal weight than for naive mixing, under the same real and synthetic sample sizes.

These results concern estimation error. For parametric models, "model collapse" means the limiting expected squared error stays bounded away from zero as the number of rounds grows, for any initial sample size. Stability also has conditions. For Gaussian covariance estimation, finite-sample stability depends on a sufficient real-data weight.

Simulation comparison of naive mixing (blue solid) and optimal weighting (orange dashed), plotting estimation error against $k=n/m$ for Gaussian mean, Gaussian variance, linear, logistic and Poisson regression, and CDF estimation. The orange curve is below the blue curve across the displayed panels and ratios. Each panel has its own vertical scale.

### 3. Why 0.618 appears

Consider Gaussian mean estimation with equal numbers of real and synthetic samples per round ($n=m$). The optimal constant real-data weight is the reciprocal of the golden ratio, about **0.618**. At that weight, the expected squared Euclidean error in the infinite-iteration limit is **0.618 times** the error from using only the current real-data batch ($w=1$). This is an exact theoretical comparison for Gaussian mean estimation. The other results have their own qualifications:

- For Gaussian covariance, the optimal-weight result is asymptotic in sample size.
- For GLMs, it concerns asymptotically scaled parameter-estimation error.
- For nonparametric CDF estimation, it is a large-sample result under the Cramér–von Mises criterion.

The stated optimality is for constant-in-time weights. In simulations with $n=m=100$, the authors report a minimum near 0.62 across their estimation tasks. They also report an application to the Adult tabular dataset, with errors lowest near the golden-ratio reciprocal.

Simulated estimation error versus real-data weight $w$ with equal real and synthetic sample sizes ($n=100$, $k=1$), for six estimation tasks. Each curve is U-shaped, with a minimum near 0.62. The axes have different scales.

### 4. Synthetic data as a carrier

In this limited-retention setting, the useful question is how to combine the two sources, not whether synthetic data are inherently good or bad. Separate, optimized weights beat pooling in the analyzed cases. The evidence comes from statistical analyses of stylized estimation problems, simulations, and a tabular-data application. It does not include tests of large language models or image generators. The framework also relies on distinguishing real from synthetic samples, and the authors note that provenance may be unreliable in practice.
