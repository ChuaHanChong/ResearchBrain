# Making Sense of Vision and Touch: Self-Supervised Learning of Multimodal Representations for Contact-Rich Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> A self-supervised network fuses RGB images, force-torque readings, and proprioception into a compact 128-d multimodal representation, trained by predicting action-conditional optical flow, next-step contact, and cross-modal time alignment. Frozen, this representation feeds a shallow TRPO policy with a compliant operational-space controller, solving 2mm-clearance peg insertion in simulation and on a real Kuka arm, and transferring across peg shapes.

## Problem

- Classical contact-rich assembly controllers assume accurate state estimation and hand-specified contact configurations, so entirely new policies are needed for each new peg geometry.
- Most learned manipulation policies close the loop around a single modality, usually vision, and deep RL on raw high-dimensional input is too sample-hungry for real robots.
- Prior vision-plus-touch work targets grasp-stability classification, or needs known object CAD models, demonstrations, or a pre-specified manipulation graph, and reconstruction objectives overfit.

## Method

- Modality encoders: ==FlowNet-style 6-layer CNN== for 128x128 RGB, ==5-layer causal convolutions== over a 32x6 force-torque window, MLP for end-effector pose and velocity, fused by a 2-layer MLP into a 128-d ==multimodal representation==.
- Three ==self-supervised losses==: ==action-conditional optical-flow prediction== with ==endpoint-error loss==, binary ==next-step contact prediction==, and ==cross-modal temporal-alignment classification==, all trained end-to-end without manual labels.
- Frozen representation feeds a 2-layer ==TRPO== policy emitting 3D Cartesian displacements at 20Hz, converted by a trajectory generator, ==impedance PD==, and ==operational-space controller== into 200Hz compliant joint torques.

## Results

- In simulated square-peg insertion the full model reaches about **80%** completion and **71%** full insertion, while removing vision, haptics, or both leaves every baseline below **5%** completion.
- On the real Kuka arm, policies trained for **300 episodes** (about **5 hours**) succeed on **92%** (round), **73%**, and **71%** of **100** evaluation trials across three peg shapes.
- Zero-shot policy transfer to unseen hexagonal and square pegs gives **62%** success; reusing only the representation and retraining the policy raises this to **81%** and **92%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling perception from control, learning the representation once then freezing it, cuts policy-learnable parameters to 3% and makes real-robot RL practical in hours.
> - Action-conditional surrogate targets like flow and contact prediction beat raw reconstruction for control, since they force the latent state to encode action-relevant dynamics.
> - Modality value is stage-dependent: vision drives reaching and hole search, haptics dominates once contact begins, so ablations must measure per-stage rather than aggregate success.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is continuously striving for more autonomous and versatile manipulation capabilities, particularly in unstructured environments. Contact-rich tasks, such as assembly, packing, or key insertion, represent a significant challenge due to the complex and often unpredictable physical interactions between the robot, objects, and environment. Humans perform these tasks effortlessly by integrating multiple sensory modalities, primarily vision and touch. However, endowing robots with similar capabilities has proven difficult.

Historically, robot manipulation policies for contact-rich tasks have often relied on manually designed controllers, typically using haptic feedback and force control. While these methods can be effective for specific, well-defined tasks (e.g., peg-in-chamfered-hole insertion), they struggle with generalization to novel geometries, configurations, or clearances, requiring entirely new policies for minor variations.

The advent of learning-based methods, particularly deep reinforcement learning (DRL), has offered a promising alternative, allowing robots to learn complex behaviors from data rather than explicit programming. However, DRL often faces two major hurdles in real-world robotic applications:
1. **Sample Complexity:** DRL algorithms typically require an enormous amount of interaction data to learn effective policies. Collecting this data on physical robots is time-consuming, expensive, and can cause wear and tear.
2. **Modality Integration:** Most learned manipulation policies tend to close the control loop around a single modality, predominantly vision. While some works have explored multimodal data, they often remain task-specific or rely on pre-specified manipulation graphs and CAD models, limiting their scalability and real-world applicability. Crucially, fusing heterogeneous data (vision, haptics, proprioception) with vastly different dimensions, frequencies, and characteristics into a unified, actionable representation is non-trivial.

This paper positions itself directly at the intersection of these challenges. It aims to bridge the gap between human-like multimodal sensing and robust robot control for contact-rich tasks. Specifically, it addresses the limitations of sample efficiency in DRL for real robots and the difficulty of fusing heterogeneous sensor data. By proposing a self-supervised learning approach for multimodal representation, the authors tackle the sample complexity problem by decoupling representation learning from policy learning. This allows for efficient data collection for representation learning (without task-specific labels) and then using a compact, relevant representation to accelerate DRL.

The work builds upon existing research in multimodal representation learning, which has explored fusing vision, range, audio, and haptic data for various inference tasks (e.g., grasp stability, object recognition). However, many of these approaches focus on classification objectives. This paper extends the concept to action-conditional predictive representations for *control*, leveraging the temporal concurrency and interdependence of sensor streams, an idea explored in audio-visual contexts but less commonly for vision-touch in manipulation control. The paper also acknowledges the difficulty of simulating contact dynamics with high fidelity, thus motivating real-robot learning but with methods that mitigate the associated challenges.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary goal of this research is to enable a robot to perform complex, **contact-rich manipulation tasks** by effectively leveraging **multimodal feedback from vision and touch**. The authors articulate several key objectives:

1. **Learn a generalizable policy:** Develop a robot policy that can effectively combine sensory inputs from vision and touch to complete tasks requiring physical interaction. This policy should generalize over variations within the same task, such as different object geometries, configurations, and clearances.
2. **Achieve robustness:** Ensure the learned policy is robust to external perturbations and sensor noise, which are common in real-world unstructured environments.
3. **Improve sample efficiency for real robots:** Overcome the notorious sample complexity of deep reinforcement learning (DRL) algorithms, making them practical for deployment on physical robots. This is a critical motivation, as the high cost and time of data collection on real hardware hinder widespread adoption of DRL in robotics.
4. **Develop a compact multimodal representation:** Learn a low-dimensional, shared representation of high-dimensional and heterogeneous sensory inputs (RGB images, force-torque readings, proprioception) that encodes action-relevant information. This representation should serve as an effective input for policy learning.
5. **Utilize self-supervision:** Eliminate the need for laborious human annotation by designing self-supervised learning objectives for training the multimodal representation model. This further enhances the practicality and scalability of the approach.

In essence, the core motivation stems from the observed human ability to seamlessly integrate vision and touch for intricate manipulation, a capability largely absent in robots due to algorithmic and practical limitations. The authors aim to endow robots with a similar ability through a learning-based framework that is both effective and efficient enough for real-world deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology tackles the problem in two distinct, sequential stages: first, learning a self-supervised multimodal representation, and second, learning a reinforcement learning policy based on this fixed representation. This decoupling is crucial for addressing sample efficiency.

#### 3.1. A. Multimodal Representation Learning with Self-Supervision

The first stage focuses on learning a compact, 128-dimensional feature vector from raw, high-dimensional sensory data. This is achieved through a neural network architecture trained with self-supervision.

1. **Modality Encoders:**
 - **RGB Images (Vision):** A 6-layer Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), inspired by FlowNet, encodes 128x128x3 RGB images into a 128-dimensional feature vector.
 - **Force-Torque (F/T) Readings (Haptics):** The last 32 readings from a 6-axis F/T sensor (32x6 time series) are processed by a 5-layer causal convolutional network, producing a 64-dimensional feature vector.
 - **Proprioception:** Current end-effector position and velocity are encoded by a 2-layer Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) into a 32-dimensional feature vector.
2. **Multimodal Fusion Module:** The feature vectors from all three encoders are concatenated and passed through a 2-layer MLP to generate the final 128-dimensional multimodal representation.
3. **Self-Supervised Prediction Objectives:** To train this network without manual labels and ensure the representation is action-relevant, three self-supervised objectives are used:
 - **Action-Conditional Optical Flow Prediction:** Given the current sensory data and the *next robot action* (end-effector motion, encoded by a 2-layer MLP), the network predicts the optical flow in the visual stream. Ground truth optical flow is automatically generated using known robot kinematics and geometry. This objective encourages the representation to capture dynamic, action-related visual information. Loss: Endpoint Error (EPE).
 - **Action-Conditional Contact Prediction:** Similar to optical flow, the network predicts whether the end-effector will make contact with the environment in the next control cycle, conditioned on the next action. Ground truth contact states are derived from simple heuristics applied to F/T sensor readings. This objective ensures the representation encodes crucial haptic interaction information. Loss: Cross-entropy.
 - **Temporal Alignment Prediction:** The network predicts whether visual and haptic sensor streams are temporally aligned. During training, a mix of aligned and randomly shifted multimodal data is presented. This objective exploits the natural concurrency of sensory streams, encouraging the fusion module to learn meaningful correlations. Loss: Cross-entropy.

The entire representation learning network is trained end-to-end by minimizing a sum of these three loss functions via stochastic gradient descent on a dataset of robot trajectories.

#### 3.2. B. Policy Learning and Controller Design

The second stage involves learning a robot policy that takes the fixed, compact multimodal representation as input.

1. **Policy Learning:**
 - **Algorithm:** Trust-Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) is chosen for its stability and success in continuous control, formulating the task as a model-free reinforcement learning problem.
 - **Policy Network:** A shallow 2-layer MLP maps the 128-d multimodal representation to a 3D displacement (∆x) of the robot end-effector in Cartesian space.
 - **Sample Efficiency Strategy:** Crucially, during policy learning, the parameters of the multimodal representation model are *frozen*. This significantly reduces the number of learnable parameters for the policy (to 3% of the entire model), substantially improving sample efficiency—a key contribution for real-robot training.
2. **Controller Design:** A hierarchical controller ensures robust and compliant execution of policy commands.
 - **Policy Output Frequency:** The policy provides Cartesian end-effector displacement commands (∆x) at a relatively low frequency (20 Hz), limited by the representation model's forward pass.
 - **Robot Torque Control:** The robot executes torque commands (τu) at a high frequency (200 Hz).
 - **Trajectory Generator:** Interpolates the low-bandwidth policy commands into high-bandwidth desired end-effector positions, velocities, and accelerations (ξt) at 200 Hz.
 - **Impedance PD Controller:** Computes a task space acceleration command (au) to track the interpolated trajectory while providing compliance.
 - **Operational Space Controller:** Uses known robot kinematic and dynamic models to transform the Cartesian-space acceleration command (au) into joint torque commands (τu) via the end-effector Jacobian. This direct torque control provides intrinsic compliance, which is vital for safe and successful contact-rich manipulation.
3. **Reward Design:** A staged reward function is used to guide the reinforcement learning process, simplifying exploration by rewarding progress through different sub-tasks (reaching, alignment, insertion, completion).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted in both simulation and on a real Kuka LBR IIWA robot, effectively demonstrate the value and capabilities of the proposed multimodal representation learning approach.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Experiments: Ablative Study

An ablative study was performed in simulation using a square peg insertion task to evaluate the contribution of each sensory modality:
- **"Full model"** (vision + haptics + proprioception)
- **"No vision"** (haptics + proprioception)
- **"No haptics"** (vision + proprioception)
- **"No vision & No haptics"** (proprioception only)

**Key Results:**
- **Superior Performance of Full Model:** The "Full model" significantly outperformed all baselines, achieving nearly an 80% completion rate for the peg insertion task. In contrast, all baseline methods had completion rates below 5%.
- **Complementary Roles of Modalities:**
 - **"No vision"** performed poorly, often failing to find the box or hole, indicating vision's crucial role in initial localization and alignment.
 - **"No haptics"** showed better initial success (reaching and sometimes inserting) than "No vision," localizing the hole visually. However, it struggled to complete full insertions (few episodes reached the bottom of the hole), implying haptic feedback is critical for the fine-grained control required during insertion itself.
 - **"No vision & No haptics"** performed the worst, rarely achieving more than simply touching the box through random exploration.
- The training curves clearly illustrated that the full multimodal input leads to faster learning and higher average episode returns.

#### 4.2. B. Real Robot Experiments

The "Full model" was evaluated on a real Kuka LBR IIWA robot for peg insertion tasks using various 3D-printed pegs (round, triangular, semicircular, hexagonal, square) with tight clearances (around 2mm).

**Key Results:**
- **High Success Rates on Trained Pegs:** For round, triangular, and semicircular pegs (where a specific representation model and policy were trained), the robot achieved high success rates, ranging from 71% to 92% completion. This demonstrates the practical feasibility and effectiveness of the approach on real hardware, despite the complexities of sensor synchronization, delays, and real-world dynamics.
- **Generalization to Novel Peg Shapes (Transfer Learning):**
 - **Policy Transfer:** Taking the representation model and policy trained for the triangular peg and applying it directly to novel hexagonal and square pegs (without further training) still yielded over 60% success rates. This indicates that the learned policy and multimodal representation capture generalizable manipulation skills.
 - **Representation Transfer with New Policy:** When the representation model from the triangular peg was used, but a *new policy* was trained specifically for the hexagonal and square pegs, the success rates increased significantly (19% for hexagonal, 30% for square), reaching 81% and 92% respectively. This highlights that the learned multimodal representation effectively generalizes across different task instances, serving as a robust feature extractor for new policies.
- **Efficiency:** Policy training on the real robot required approximately 5 hours (300 episodes, 1000 steps each), which is a remarkably efficient learning time for complex DRL tasks on physical hardware, largely attributable to the frozen, pre-trained representation.
- **Robustness:** The policy demonstrated robustness to sensory noise (e.g., camera occlusion) and external physical perturbations to the robot arm, effectively recovering and completing the task.
- **Learned Strategy:** The robot typically learned a sequence of behaviors: reaching the box, sliding over its surface to search for the hole, aligning the peg, and finally performing insertion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in robot manipulation, particularly for contact-rich tasks in unstructured environments, with several key implications:

1. **Enabling Practical DRL on Real Robots:** The core contribution of decoupling self-supervised representation learning from policy learning effectively addresses the major bottleneck of sample inefficiency in DRL for real-world robotics. By freezing the representation during policy training, the learning process becomes substantially faster and more practical, making complex DRL policies accessible for real robot deployment.
2. **Highlighting the Criticality of Multimodal Sensing:** The ablative studies rigorously demonstrate that both vision and haptics are indispensable for complex contact-rich tasks like peg insertion. Vision provides the initial global context for localization and coarse alignment, while haptics is crucial for fine-grained contact understanding and precise insertion. This work reinforces the need for true multimodal integration in advanced robotic systems, moving beyond single-modality limitations.
3. **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The ability of the learned multimodal representations and policies to generalize to novel peg geometries and configurations, even with minimal further training, is a major step towards versatile robots. The demonstrated robustness to external perturbations and sensor noise further increases the applicability of these systems in dynamic and uncertain real-world settings.
4. **Foundation for Autonomous Manipulation:** This work lays a strong foundation for developing more autonomous robots capable of performing intricate assembly, manufacturing, and service tasks that currently require human dexterity. By automatically learning robust behaviors from sensory data, robots can adapt to variations and complexities without extensive manual reprogramming.
5. **New Paradigm for State Representation:** The proposed self-supervised objectives, particularly action-conditional predictions of optical flow, contact, and temporal alignment, offer a novel and effective way to learn control-relevant state representations. This paradigm reduces reliance on costly manual labeling, which is a common hurdle in machine learning applications.

In conclusion, this paper successfully demonstrates a practical and efficient framework for endowing robots with human-like visual and haptic integration for complex manipulation. Its impact extends to reducing the development cycle for robotic tasks, increasing robot autonomy and versatility, and opening new avenues for research into richer multimodal learning and more complex interaction behaviors. Future work, as suggested by the authors, including extending to 6-DoF control and incorporating additional modalities like depth and sound, indicates a clear path towards even more capable and perceptive robotic agents.
