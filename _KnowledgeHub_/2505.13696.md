# Building spatial world models from sparse transitional episodic memories

> [!summary] Summary
> A computational framework called the Episodic Spatial World Model (ESWM) was developed to construct coherent spatial world models using only sparse, disjoint episodic memories. This approach enables zero-shot exploration and near-optimal navigation in novel environments, adapting quickly to changes by updating an external memory bank.

## Problem

- Existing computational world models typically require continuous, sequential observations to build environmental representations.
- Integrating fragmented or non-sequential experiences into coherent spatial maps remains a challenge for current AI systems.
- Rapidly adapting to dynamic environmental changes is difficult for models where environmental knowledge is embedded in fixed parameters.

## Method

- The **ESWM** (==Episodic Spatial World Model==) framework was introduced, which is ==meta-trained== to infer missing components of sparse, ==one-step transitional episodic memories==.
- It operates on an ==external memory bank== containing unordered, disjoint (source state, action, end state) tuples.
- Evaluated architectures included ==Transformer, LSTM, and Mamba==, with the Transformer-based **ESWM-T** demonstrating superior performance.

## Results

- **ESWM-T** achieved higher prediction accuracy for missing transition elements compared to other architectures and the sequence-based TEM-T baseline.
- An ESWM-based explorer visited **16.8%** more unique states than EPN, and a navigator achieved **96.8%** success (**18%** higher than EPN) and **99.2%** path optimality with minimal memories.
- The system rapidly adapted to new obstacles, maintaining **93%** navigation success by only updating the memory bank, outperforming EPN (**72%**) and an RL agent (**56%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The latent space of ESWM spontaneously forms a geometric map that accurately reflects the environment's topology and adapts dynamically to new memories or structural alterations.
> - Learning from sparse, disjoint memories, rather than continuous trajectories, allows for better generalization to diverse environmental structures and unseen states.
> - Decoupling the learned reasoning mechanism from an external, editable memory bank enables rapid adaptation to environmental changes without model retraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of computational models that can construct internal representations of environments, often referred to as "world models" or "cognitive maps," is a prominent area in artificial intelligence and neuroscience-inspired computing. This work addresses a specific challenge within this field: the ability to build flexible spatial maps from fragmented and non-sequential experiences.

Traditional computational world models, particularly in reinforcement learning, frequently rely on continuous sequences of observations and actions for training. Examples include models leveraging recurrent neural networks (e.g., Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018; Hafner et al., 2019; Whittington et al., 2020) that learn dynamics from trajectories. Other approaches may incorporate fixed, pre-designed circuitry for spatial information processing (e.g., Wang et al., 2023) or depend on iterative update procedures (e.g., Stachenfeld et al., 2017) which can limit their adaptability to environmental changes. These models typically internalize environmental knowledge into their weights, making them less flexible when encountering disjoint experiences or structural alterations in the environment.

Neuroscience research provides evidence that biological systems, such as animals, possess the capacity to integrate disparate experiences into coherent spatial representations. The medial temporal lobe (MTL), for instance, is implicated in both spatial processing (e.g., place cells, grid cells) and the formation of episodic memories. Studies suggest that the MTL integrates overlapping episodic memories into structured relational networks, facilitating inferences about spatial relationships beyond direct, continuous perception (Eichenbaum, 2004; McKenzie et al., 2014). This biological mechanism allows for the construction of cognitive maps from non-sequential data, which contrasts with many existing computational models.

This paper positions itself by drawing inspiration from these neuroscience insights to address the limitations of current world models. Specifically, it seeks to develop a framework that can learn spatial structure from *sparse, disjoint episodic memories* rather than continuous trajectories. This contrasts with models like the Tolman-Eichenbaum Machine (TEM) (Whittington et al., 2020, 2022), which factorize sensory and structural information but presuppose a common structural template and operate on sequential trajectories. The authors aim to bridge the gap between biological cognitive mapping capabilities and computational models by enabling direct inference of spatial structure from independent, non-sequential memories, thereby enhancing adaptability and generalization in dynamic or partially observed environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed ability of animals to construct mental maps from fragmented experiences, a capability fundamental for navigation, exploration, and planning. While cognitive neuroscience points to the medial temporal lobe's role in integrating episodic memories to form spatial representations, most computational world models struggle with scenarios involving sparse or disjoint observations, as well as rapid adaptation to environmental changes. This disparity highlights a limitation in current artificial intelligence systems, which often require extensive, continuous data to build environmental models.

The central question addressed by this work is: Can a neural network rapidly construct a coherent spatial map of an environment using only sparse and disjoint episodic memories?

To investigate this, the paper sets out the following key objectives:

1. **Introduce a Novel Framework:** To develop the Episodic Spatial World Model (ESWM), a computational framework designed to infer spatial structure solely from independently acquired episodic experiences, specifically sparse, one-step transitions. This objective aims to demonstrate a departure from conventional models that rely on continuous sensory sequences.
2. **Characterize Latent Space Geometry:** To evaluate whether the latent space of ESWM forms a geometric map that accurately reflects the topology of the environment. A secondary aspect of this objective is to ascertain if this latent map dynamically adapts to the incorporation of new memories and to structural changes within the environment, such as the introduction of obstacles.
3. **Enable Zero-Shot Downstream Tasks:** To demonstrate that the learned world model within ESWM can facilitate near-optimal strategies for exploration of novel environments and navigation between arbitrary points. A key criterion for this objective is that these capabilities should emerge without the need for additional task-specific training (zero-shot performance).
4. **Demonstrate Scalability:** To assess ESWM's applicability and performance in more complex and realistic settings. This includes continuous environments with high-dimensional inputs, such as those involving egocentric multi-dimensional observations and continuous actions.
5. **Assess Adaptability to Environmental Changes:** To evaluate ESWM's capacity for rapid adjustment to structural modifications in an environment. This objective focuses on the model's ability to update its understanding by simply editing a minimal number of relevant episodic memories, without necessitating retraining of its core parameters.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the Episodic Spatial World Model (ESWM), a framework designed to construct spatial maps from sparse, disjoint episodic memories.

#### 3.1. Episodic Spatial World Model (ESWM) Formalism

ESWM is defined as a function `f` that takes a memory bank `M` and a partially masked query transition `q` as input, and predicts the complete transition `q*`. An episodic memory is represented as a one-step transition tuple `(ss, a, se)`, denoting a source state, an action, and an end state.

#### 3.2. Memory Bank Construction

For each environment, a memory bank `M` is constructed as an unordered set of transitions designed to be:
- **Disjoint:** Transitions do not form a continuous trajectory.
- **Spanning:** The transitions collectively cover all locations in the environment.
- **Minimal:** (For specific experiments) Removing any transition would disconnect the graph, ensuring a sparse representation. The paper also explores non-minimal memory banks.
These memory banks are algorithmically generated to sparsely cover environments (Extended Methods C.3).

#### 3.3. Task Formulation

The core task for ESWM is to infer any missing component (source state `ss`, action `a`, or end state `se`) of an unseen but plausible query transition `q` (i.e., not present in `M`). The query `q` is partially masked, with `ss`, `a`, or `se` randomly omitted with equal probability. The model predicts the masked element given the other two and the memory bank `M`.

#### 3.4. Training Procedure (Meta-Learning)

ESWM is trained using a meta-learning approach. In each training trial:
1. An environment `e` is randomly sampled, including a random assignment of states to spatial locations to prevent memorization of specific state-location mappings.
2. A disjoint memory bank `M` is sampled from `e`.
3. A plausible query transition `q` (not in `M`) is sampled from `e`.
4. A masking choice (ss, a, or se) for `q` is made randomly.
This process exposes the model to a vast number of environment configurations, promoting generalization rather than memorization. To handle partial observations and unobserved regions, some memories are deliberately removed in certain trials, and the model is trained to output an "I don't know" category for unsolvable queries.

#### 3.5. Model Architectures

Three neural network architectures for `f` were evaluated:
1. **ESWM-T:** An encoder-only Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) without positional encoding.
2. **ESWM-LSTM:** A Long Short-Term Memory network (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997).
3. **ESWM-MAMBA:** A Mamba architecture (Gu & Dao, 2024).

Each transition component (`ss`, `a`, `se`) is independently projected into a shared high-dimensional embedding space. These embeddings are then averaged to form a single input token for each transition. The Transformer model processes a concatenated sequence of memory bank and query tokens. LSTM and Mamba models receive a randomly permuted sequence of memory tokens, with the query token appended. Three separate linear heads are used to read out predictions for `ss`, `a`, and `se` from the final layer's output. The training objective uses cross-entropy loss, with equal weight assigned to predictions for `ss`, `a`, and `se`.

#### 3.6. Evaluation Environments

ESWM's performance was evaluated across three settings of increasing complexity:
1. **2D Grid Environments:** Discrete, hexagonally structured grids. State-location mappings are randomized per environment.
 - **Open Arena:** 19 locations, 64 compositional 6-bit binary states. Used to test generalization to unseen states, with predictions broken into binary classification per bit.
 - **Random Wall:** 36 locations, unique integer states, with randomly shaped walls. Used to test generalization to unseen structural configurations and includes "I don't know" classification for masked regions.
2. **MiniGrid Environments:** 9x9 layouts with egocentric multi-dimensional observations (5x5 view of colored objects) and discrete egocentric actions (turn left/right, go straight). Challenges self-localization from partial views.
3. **Simulated 3D Indoor Environments (ProcThor):** Large-scale, procedurally generated 3D scenes. Features high-resolution RGB first-person observations (224x224) and continuous egocentric actions (Δxy, Δθ). States are embedded using a pre-trained vision model (DINOV3), and predictions involve discretized actions and cosine similarity for end-state representations.

#### 3.7. Downstream Task Evaluation

- **Exploration:** An ESWM-based algorithm is developed where the agent selects actions with the highest predicted uncertainty (highest "I don't know" probability) to explore novel environments. It performs multi-step lookahead when confident in immediate actions.
- **Navigation:** A Dijkstra-style planning algorithm is employed, leveraging ESWM's ability to predict the end state of any given transition to simulate future trajectories and find paths from a source to a goal state.
- **Adaptability to Changes:** ESWM's memory-reasoning decoupling allows for rapid adaptation. When environmental changes occur, outdated transitions are removed from the memory bank, and exploration is triggered to acquire new information before replanning.

#### 3.8. Baselines

The Tolman-Eichenbaum Machine (TEM-T) was used as a sequence-based baseline for core prediction tasks, receiving a minimal spanning trajectory rather than disjoint memories. For navigation and exploration, the Episodic Planning Network (EPN) (Ritter et al., 2020) and a basic A2C-trained RL agent were used as baselines.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding ESWM's capacity to build spatial world models from sparse episodic memories and its utility in downstream tasks.

#### 4.1. Spatial Environment Modeling from Episodic Memories

- **Architectural Performance**: In the Open Arena environment, ESWM-T (Transformer-based) demonstrated higher performance in predicting missing transition elements, while ESWM-LSTM and ESWM-MAMBA exhibited overfitting on training states and struggled with unseen states (Figure 2a). This suggests that the attention mechanism inherent in Transformer architectures is conducive to learning generalizable world models from episodic memories in this context. Conversely, the sequence-based TEM-T model, while outperforming non-Transformer ESWM variants, showed lower performance compared to ESWM-T, indicating an advantage in directly inferring from episodic memories rather than relying on segregated encoding of structure and memories.
- **Generalization to Structures**: In the Random Wall environment, all ESWM models achieved the task, though Transformer-based models maintained a notable performance gap over other architectures. TEM-T, which embeds structural knowledge in its weights, performed less effectively on environments with varying structures (Figure 2b). This suggests ESWM's strategy of inferring structure dynamically from external memories contributes to better generalization across diverse structural layouts.
- **Handling Duplicate States and Memory Density**: ESWM-T was shown to handle environments with duplicate states when provided with a trajectory context (Figure 7). Additionally, ESWM's performance improved with denser, non-minimal memory banks, even when these were out-of-distribution during testing (Figure 4c), and exhibited some robustness to noisy memory banks, with prediction accuracy drops ranging from 1% to 10%.

#### 4.2. Emergence of Spatial Maps in Latent Space

- **Geometric Alignment**: ISOMAP projections of ESWM's latent space consistently revealed internal representations that align with the spatial layout of the environment (Figure 3a, b). In obstacle-free environments, the manifold exhibited smooth, continuous shapes, while the introduction of obstacles created localized discontinuities corresponding to blocked physical space. This structure demonstrated robustness across various obstacle shapes, partial environmental observations, and different architectural types (Transformer, LSTM, Mamba) (Figure 10, 11, 12).
- **Memory Integration and Adaptation**: ESWM demonstrated the ability to integrate disconnected memory clusters into a coherent map by aligning them along inferred wall or boundary shapes (Figure 3c, d, 13). Quantitative analysis showed a strong correlation (R² = 0.89) between path lengths in ESWM's latent space and physical space (Figure 16b). A linear classifier could discriminate relative distances between states with 93.42% accuracy, and the sRSA metric was significantly higher for trained ESWM compared to an untrained model (0.78 vs. 0.5).
- **Contrast with Navigation Agents**: Notably, these structured spatial representations were not observed to emerge in episodic navigation agents like EPN (Figure 10, 11), suggesting that ESWM's specific learning objective of predicting masked transitions contributes to this map-like internal organization.

#### 4.3. Integration of Overlapping Memories

- **Uncertainty and Path Length**: ESWM's prediction uncertainty (measured by entropy) was observed to increase with the shortest path length required to integrate memories to solve a query (Figure 4a).
- **Impact of Shortcuts**: Introducing shortcut memories that reduced the integration path length significantly altered the prediction distribution (Figure 4b), indicating active integration of available information.
- **Generalization to Scale**: ESWM generalized to larger environments (from 19 to 37 locations in Open Arena) with above-chance prediction accuracy (s_s: 59%, a: 90%, s_e: 55%).

#### 4.4. Exploration in Novel Environments

- An ESWM-based exploration algorithm, which prioritizes actions leading to high "I don't know" predictions, adopted an efficient zig-zag strategy.
- In the Random Wall environment, this strategy explored 16.8% more unique states than EPN (Figure 5a) and achieved 96.48% of the performance of an oracle agent with full obstacle knowledge.
- The collected memory banks from exploration supported prediction accuracies comparable to procedurally generated ones.

#### 4.5. Navigation with Minimal Spanning Episodic Memories

- An ESWM-based agent, utilizing its internal world model for planning, achieved a 96.8% navigation success rate and 99.2% path optimality in Random Wall, outperforming EPN by 18% and 21% respectively (Figure 5b). This was achieved with significantly fewer memories than EPN.
- The learned spatial maps in ESWM's latent space could be leveraged as a heuristic for guided imagination (A* planning), improving planning efficiency by 57% over Dijkstra's algorithm while maintaining path optimality (Figure 16a, c).

#### 4.6. Fast Adaptation to Environmental Changes

- ESWM's decoupling of memory from reasoning allowed for rapid adaptation to structural changes without retraining. By updating the external memory bank (removing outdated transitions and acquiring new ones through exploration), ESWM maintained a 93% navigation success rate after the introduction of new obstacles (Figure 5c). This performance exceeded that of EPN (72%) and a baseline RL agent (56%) under similar conditions.

#### 4.7. Scaling ESWM

- **MiniGrid**: ESWM demonstrated strong predictive performance and constructed coherent internal map representations in MiniGrid environments with egocentric, multi-dimensional observations (Figure 6a).
- **ProcThor**: In the more complex ProcThor 3D indoor environments with high-resolution RGB observations and continuous actions, ESWM learned to predict both state vectors and action parameters effectively (Figure 6b).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work introduces the Episodic Spatial World Model (ESWM), which presents a method for constructing coherent spatial world models from sparse and fragmented one-step transitions. Its significance lies in several areas:

**Advancement in World Modeling:** ESWM addresses limitations of existing world models by demonstrating the capacity to learn from disjoint, non-sequential episodic data. Unlike many prior models that depend on continuous trajectories or pre-defined spatial circuitry, ESWM infers spatial structure dynamically from a memory bank. This approach enables rapid acquisition of environmental knowledge and adaptability, which are often challenging for models that embed environmental specifics directly into their fixed weights or require iterative parameter tuning. The model's ability to operate on independently stored and updated transitional memories provides a mechanism for rapid adjustment to environmental modifications, a capability where many current models exhibit limitations.

**Neuroscience-Inspired AI:** The research is informed by neuroscience principles regarding the medial temporal lobe's role in integrating episodic memories for spatial cognition. By computationally demonstrating how a model can build flexible cognitive maps from fragmented experiences, it offers a plausible computational framework for such biological processes. The emergence of coherent, geometric spatial maps within ESWM's latent space, even without explicit training for navigation, supports the hypothesis that a general world model can underpin various spatial behaviors.

**Enhanced Generalization and Adaptability:** ESWM's meta-training on diverse, randomized environments, combined with its memory-based reasoning, promotes strong generalization to unseen states, structures, and even larger environments. Its capacity for zero-shot exploration and near-optimal navigation in novel environments, without requiring task-specific training, suggests a degree of flexibility and generalization not consistently achieved by other methods. The ability to quickly adapt to environmental changes (e.g., new obstacles) by simply updating the memory bank, rather than retraining the model, is a notable step towards more robust and flexible autonomous systems.

**Potential Societal Impact:** The theoretical and methodological contributions of this work have potential implications for real-world applications in robotics and autonomous systems.
- **Autonomous Navigation and Exploration:** ESWM's ability to rapidly map novel environments, navigate efficiently, and adapt to changes could contribute to the development of more robust and intelligent autonomous agents for tasks such as environmental monitoring, disaster response, or planetary exploration.
- **Problem-Solving and Decision-Making:** By providing a framework for flexible spatial reasoning, this work could also advance AI systems capable of more complex planning and decision-making in dynamic physical spaces.

**Limitations and Future Directions:** The paper acknowledges that the current work primarily utilizes highly controllable, theoretical environments. Future research could explore extending ESWM to more complex and realistic settings, such as robotic navigation with natural, high-dimensional sensory information and more intricate physics.

The work underscores the importance of responsible development and deployment of autonomous systems, especially given potential applications in critical areas, and highlights the need for continued evaluation and adherence to ethical guidelines.
