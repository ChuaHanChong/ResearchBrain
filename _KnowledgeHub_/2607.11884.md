# Mixture of Frames Policy: Multi-Frame Action Denoising for Bimanual Mobile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The Mixture of Frames (MoF) Policy introduces a diffusion-based visuomotor policy that performs synchronized action denoising across multiple coordinate frames for bimanual mobile manipulation tasks. This approach achieves an average success rate of 66.8% in simulation, an 11.7 percentage point improvement over a single-frame Mixture-of-Experts baseline, by adaptively leveraging frame-specialized denoisers.

## Problem

- Traditional diffusion-based visuomotor policies rely on a single, fixed action frame, which is often suboptimal for complex bimanual and mobile manipulation.
- The most suitable coordinate frame for representing an action can change significantly throughout a task or across different sub-tasks, making a single-frame approach inefficient.
- Transforming noisy intermediate diffusion states across frames is challenging with standard 6D rotation representations, as it requires lossy orthogonalization that alters the diffusion state.

## Method

- A **Mixture of Frames (MoF) Policy** integrates a ==Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)== architecture, where each expert is a ==denoiser== specialized for a particular coordinate frame.
- A ==synchronized multi-frame denoising== procedure transforms noisy action samples into expert frames, denoises them, and fuses the predictions back in a canonical frame.
- A novel ==column-vector rotation representation== for ==SE(3)== actions is introduced, enabling exact and differentiable transformations of noisy intermediate diffusion states without ==orthogonalization==.

## Results

- MoF-MoE achieved an average success rate of **66.8%** in simulation, surpassing single-frame baselines by up to **16.5 percentage points** and often exceeding the 'Oracle Frame' performance.
- Router analysis demonstrated adaptive, phase-dependent frame selection, with different expert frames dominating at various stages of complex tasks like 'Threading'.
- Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of the column-vector SE(3) action representation, as methods requiring orthogonalization resulted in complete policy failure (**0.0%** success), and validated the importance of the auxiliary denoising loss and expert set complementarity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - No single coordinate frame is universally optimal across all bimanual mobile manipulation tasks or their phases; adaptive frame selection significantly improves policy performance.
> - Synchronized multi-frame denoising, rather than merely ensembling multiple denoisers, is crucial for leveraging the complementary strengths of different action representations.
> - Preserving the integrity of noisy rotation components during inter-frame transformations is critical for the stability and effectiveness of diffusion-based multi-frame policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation, particularly for complex bimanual and mobile tasks, often involves coordinating actions across various coordinate frames. Traditional visuomotor policies, such as those based on diffusion models, typically commit to a single, predefined action frame. This approach can lead to suboptimal performance when the optimal frame for representing an action distribution varies throughout a task or across different sub-tasks. For instance, local grasping motions may be simpler in an end-effector frame, while tasks involving object transport or maintaining an object's upright orientation might be better expressed in a base-aligned frame.

Prior research has explored multi-frame reasoning in robot learning. This includes methods that mine observation frames for point-cloud policies, compose low-dimensional frame-conditioned skills from demonstrations, or adopt relation-centric representations for bimanual manipulation. However, these efforts have largely remained under-explored within the context of modern diffusion-based visuomotor policies, which have shown promise in learning complex manipulation behaviors.

This paper addresses the limitation of single-frame action representations in diffusion policies by introducing a mechanism for synchronized action denoising across multiple coordinate frames. It builds upon the success of diffusion policies by integrating a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) paradigm, which traditionally partitions experts along behavioral dimensions (e.g., task identity, skill abstraction, denoising stages, action modes, or sensing modalities). This work distinguishes itself by formulating the MoE over coordinate frames, thereby allowing for the adaptive fusion of frame-specific action representations, a challenge that has been less investigated in the field of diffusion policies for robot manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a diffusion-based visuomotor policy that can adaptively leverage multiple coordinate frames for action representation, thereby improving performance in bimanual mobile manipulation tasks. The authors identify a significant limitation in existing diffusion policies: their reliance on a single, fixed action frame. This limitation arises because robotic manipulation tasks are inherently multi-frame, meaning that the most suitable coordinate system for representing an action can change depending on the specific phase or nature of the manipulation.

The motivation behind the Mixture of Frames (MoF) Policy stems from several observations:
- **Task-Dependent Frame Optimality**: The paper posits that no single coordinate frame is universally optimal for representing actions across all bimanual mobile manipulation tasks or even within different phases of a single complex task. For example, grasping a cup handle is more naturally compact in a gripper frame, exhibiting invariance to the absolute pose of the gripper-object pair. Conversely, transporting a filled cup while maintaining its upright position is better expressed in a base-aligned frame to ensure coherence with the mobile platform and gravity.
- **Complexity of Action Distributions**: When a policy commits to a single predefined action frame, the denoiser must model the entire action distribution within that frame, regardless of whether that distribution is inherently complex or only appears complex due to the chosen parameterization. This can impose unnecessary learning challenges for the policy.
- **Under-exploration in Diffusion Policies**: While multi-frame reasoning has been explored in other robot learning contexts, its application to modern diffusion-based visuomotor policies, where denoising occurs iteratively on noisy action states, remains largely unaddressed. The authors argue that frames should not only be provided as observations but also define alternative action spaces where denoising can be performed directly.
- **Synchronization Challenge**: A naive approach of running independent diffusion processes in different frames and fusing final actions is problematic due to potential mode mismatches in multimodal action distributions. This motivates a synchronized approach where experts contribute to a single diffusion trajectory.
- **Technical Challenge of Noisy Rotations**: Transforming intermediate noisy diffusion states across frames poses a technical challenge, as standard 6D rotation representations (e.g., storing the first two rows of a rotation matrix) require orthogonalization, which is a non-linear, lossy projection that alters the diffusion state. A representation that supports exact, differentiable transformations of noisy rotations is required for consistent multi-frame denoising.

By addressing these points, the research aims to enable policies to reason over multiple coordinate frames simultaneously, reducing the need for manual frame selection and potentially improving robustness and generalization across diverse manipulation scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Mixture of Frames (MoF) Policy is a diffusion-based visuomotor policy designed to perform synchronized action denoising across multiple coordinate frames. The methodology centers on three main components: a consistent multi-frame denoising procedure, a transformation-compatible action representation, and a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The policy operates within a visuomotor imitation learning setup for bimanual mobile manipulation. It receives observations (RGB images and proprioception) and predicts an action chunk over a future horizon. Each action timestep includes target poses for the left and right end-effectors and gripper commands. The set of task-relevant coordinate frames, $\mathcal{F} = \{F_m\}$, includes left/right end-effector frames and a base frame. Rigid transformations between any two frames are known from proprioception. Unlike standard diffusion policies that fix a single action frame $F^\star$, MoF maintains multiple frame-specialized denoisers.

#### 3.2. Consistent Multi-Frame Denoising

MoF keeps all frame experts synchronized by operating on a single diffusion trajectory in a designated canonical frame ($F_c$). At each denoising step ($k$):
1. A noisy action sample $x_k^c$ in the canonical frame is transformed into each expert's frame $F_m$ using an action-state operator $\text{}^m\mathcal{T}_c(\cdot)$.
2. Each frame-specialized denoiser $\epsilon_{\theta_m}$ predicts the noise in its native frame based on the transformed noisy action and current observation.
3. The predicted noise vectors from each expert are transformed back to the canonical frame using a noise-vector operator $\text{}^c\mathcal{T}_m(\cdot)$.
4. These aligned noise predictions are then fused in the canonical frame to produce a combined noise estimate $\hat{\epsilon}^c$. The fusion can be achieved either by a learned router that predicts weights $w_m$ for each expert, or by fixed uniform weights ($w_m = 1/|\mathcal{F}|$).

This synchronized approach ensures that all experts contribute to refining the same underlying action trajectory, thus avoiding mode-mismatch issues that could arise from independent diffusion processes. The fused noise prediction $\hat{\epsilon}^c$ is then used by a standard diffusion scheduler (e.g., DDIM).

During training, the objective is to minimize the squared error between the fused canonical noise prediction and the true canonical noise $\epsilon^c$:
$L_{\text{mix}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{k, \epsilon^c, o} \left[ \left\| \epsilon^c - \sum_{F_m \in \mathcal{F}} w_m \text{}^c\mathcal{T}_m \left( \epsilon_{\theta_m} \left( \text{}^m\mathcal{T}_c(x_k^c), o; k \right) \right) \right\|^2 \right]$.
An auxiliary denoising loss $L_{\text{aux}}$ is also added to directly supervise each expert in its own frame against its corresponding transformed noise target ($\text{}^m\mathcal{T}_c(\epsilon^c)$). This helps stabilize training, especially for experts with small router weights. The final training objective is $L = L_{\text{mix}} + \lambda_{\text{aux}} L_{\text{aux}}$.

#### 3.3. Transformation-Compatible Action Representation

A critical aspect of MoF is its ability to transform intermediate noisy diffusion states, whose rotation components generally do not lie on the SO(3) manifold. Standard 6D rotation representations (e.g., using the first two *rows* of a rotation matrix) would require orthogonalization to form a valid rotation matrix before transformation, which is a nonlinear and lossy projection that alters the diffusion state.

To address this, MoF introduces a **column-vector rotation representation**. For an action timestep, the pose/action channels in the canonical frame $F_c$ are represented as $x_c = [p_c, c_1^c, c_2^c, g]$, where $p_c \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the end-effector position, $c_1^c, c_2^c \in \mathbb{R}^3$ are the first two *columns* of the end-effector orientation matrix, and $g$ is the gripper command.
When re-expressing this action in an expert frame $F_m$, the known frame-change rotation $\text{}^m R_c$ is applied. For each stored column $c_i^c$, the transformed column becomes $c_i^m = \text{}^m R_c c_i^c$. This operation is well-defined even when $c_1^c$ and $c_2^c$ are noisy vectors, as it only involves left-multiplication of 3D vectors by a rotation matrix, without requiring orthogonalization or projecting onto SO(3).
The action-state operator $\text{}^m\mathcal{T}_c$ translates position channels and rotates position and rotation-column channels. The noise-vector operator $\text{}^m\mathcal{T}_c$ similarly rotates position and rotation-column noise vectors, but does not apply a translation offset, respecting the nature of noise in the action space.

#### 3.4. Expert Action Parameterizations

MoF is instantiated with four experts, chosen to capture complementary action structures:
- **Left End-Effector Frame ($F_l$)**: Actions relative to the left gripper.
- **Right End-Effector Frame ($F_r$)**: Actions relative to the right gripper.
- **Base-Relative Frame ($F_b$)**: Rotations and translation directions are in the mobile-base frame, but each arm's translation is re-centered at its observation-step end-effector position, balancing body-alignment with reduced absolute translation variance.
- **Per-Arm Relative-Trajectory Frame ($F_{rt}$)**: Each arm's future motion is represented in that arm's own current end-effector frame, removing both global translation and global orientation, making local end-effector-centric motions compact.

These experts are conditioned on visual features (shared) and proprioceptive inputs (transformed into each expert's frame). Two variants are evaluated: MoF-MoE (with a learned router for weights) and MoF-Ensemble (with uniform weights).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents findings from both simulation and real-world experiments to evaluate the MoF policy.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

Nine bimanual manipulation tasks from BiGym and DexMimicGen benchmarks were used for evaluation.

**Q1. Effect of Action Frame Choice on Performance:**
- **Task-Dependent Performance**: Single-frame diffusion policies demonstrate a substantial and task-dependent variation in performance based on the chosen action frame. For example, the base frame achieved the highest average success rate (61.0%), but was not consistently the best for all tasks. The left frame was best for two tasks, while the right frame and relative trajectory frame each excelled in one task.
- **Performance Gap**: The difference between the "Oracle Frame" (best single frame per task, 63.8% average) and the "Worst Frame" (worst single frame per task, 48.8% average) averaged 15.0 percentage points, with some tasks exhibiting larger differences (e.g., Threading at 28.4 points). This indicates that an unfavorable frame choice can significantly hinder policy performance. These results collectively support the motivation for policies that can reason across multiple frames.

**Q2. MoF vs. Baselines:**
- **Improved Average Success Rate**: Both MoF variants outperformed single-frame baselines on average. MoF-MoE achieved an average success rate of 66.8%, representing an 11.7 percentage point improvement over MoE-DP (a Mixture-of-Experts module in the conditioning pathway but single-frame action denoising) and a 16.5 percentage point improvement over standard Diffusion Policy (DP). MoF-Ensemble achieved a comparable 65.9% average success rate.
- **Beyond Oracle Frame**: MoF-MoE matched or surpassed the Oracle Frame baseline on most tasks. Notably, it improved performance on "Threading" by 7.7 percentage points.
- **Router Dynamics**: Analysis of MoF-MoE's router weights during the "Threading" task revealed a phase-dependent frame usage. The relative-trajectory expert dominated early in the episode (arm-centric reaching), while the base-relative expert took over later (coordinated bimanual insertion). This adaptive frame switching, not available to single-frame baselines, is attributed to MoF-MoE exceeding Oracle Frame on this task.
- **Multi-Frame Denoising vs. Multiple Denoisers**: The "Single-Frame Ensemble" baseline, which averaged four diffusers operating in the same coordinate frame, performed substantially worse than MoF variants (55.9% vs. 66.8% for MoF-MoE). This suggests that the performance gains derive from multi-frame action denoising, rather than merely from ensembling multiple denoisers.
- **Router Modulation Correlation**: A positive correlation (Spearman $\rho = +0.75$) was observed between the router modulation amplitude (how much expert weights typically swing within an episode) and MoF-MoE's performance advantage over Oracle Frame. Tasks with higher router modulation (e.g., Threading, Drawer Cleanup) showed greater gains, while the task with minimal modulation (Box Cleanup) was the only instance where MoF-MoE underperformed Oracle Frame. This indicates that the router's adaptive frame selection across task phases contributes to performance improvements.

**Q3. Ablation Study:**
- **Canonical Frame Robustness**: Changing the canonical frame had a modest impact on performance, with the largest average drop being 2.5 percentage points. This suggests MoF is robust to the arbitrary choice of the canonical frame.
- **Expert Set Complementarity**: Removing the best-performing single frame from the expert set reduced average performance by 6.2 percentage points, indicating that the expert frames offer complementary denoising structures and are not redundant.
- **Transformation-Compatible Representation Validation**: Applying orthogonalization before frame transformation (as would be necessary with a row-vector 6D representation) resulted in significant performance degradation. MoF-MoE dropped by 7.0 percentage points, and MoF-Ensemble completely collapsed to 0.0% success. This validates the necessity of the introduced column-vector SE(3) action representation for exact, differentiable transformations of noisy diffusion states.
- **Auxiliary Loss Importance**: Removing the per-expert auxiliary denoising loss reduced average performance by 8.0 percentage points, highlighting its role in ensuring all experts remain meaningful during training, especially when router weights are small.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

MoF-MoE was evaluated on two real-world bimanual mobile manipulation tasks using the HoMMI framework, comparing it against constituent single-frame baselines. The base frame expert was excluded due to data collection limitations.

- **Pouring Task**: The robot picks up two cups, transfers a cube, and places them. MoF-MoE achieved 85% success, with failures primarily in initial cup pickup. Router analysis indicated alternating use of Left and Rel Traj experts during pick-and-place, with the Left expert dominating during pouring, suggesting dynamic frame selection based on task phase. Single-frame baselines showed distinct failure modes: Left (15%) struggled with right cup pickup and pouring; Right (70%) failed with left cup pickup and placement; Rel Traj (5%) exhibited instability during precise pickups and pouring.
- **Serving Task**: The robot navigates with a plate, grasps a cup, and places it. MoF-MoE achieved 70% success, with failures concentrated at certain table distances. Router weights indicated the Left expert consistently dominating, suggesting that reasoning relative to the stably held plate was optimal throughout the task. Single-frame baselines also showed distinct failures: Left (55%) had grasping failures due to cup shifting during long navigation; Right (0%) exhibited high instability during navigation; Rel Traj (60%) struggled with bimanual coordination during grasping.

Overall, MoF-MoE demonstrated improved robustness and performance over single-frame baselines in real-world scenarios, leveraging adaptive multi-frame reasoning to handle dynamic coordination requirements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Mixture of Frames Policy (MoF) introduces a novel approach to visuomotor policy learning for bimanual mobile manipulation by enabling synchronized action denoising across multiple coordinate frames. This work addresses a fundamental limitation in existing diffusion-based policies, which typically rely on a single, fixed action representation.

The significance of MoF is demonstrated by several key findings:
- **Improved Robustness and Performance**: MoF-MoE consistently outperforms single-frame baselines and even surpasses the theoretical "Oracle Frame" (the best single-frame policy for a given task) on several complex bimanual manipulation tasks in simulation and the real world. This indicates that the adaptive fusion of frame-specialized denoisers can yield more robust and effective policies.
- **Adaptive Frame Selection**: The learned router in MoF-MoE dynamically adjusts the influence of different frame experts based on the task progression. This ability to adaptively select the most suitable action frame for different phases of a task (e.g., arm-centric reaching vs. coordinated bimanual manipulation) allows the policy to leverage compact and invariant action distributions, which simplifies the learning problem.
- **Technical Advancement in Diffusion Models**: The introduction of a column-vector SE(3) action representation is a critical technical contribution. This representation enables exact and differentiable transformation of noisy intermediate diffusion states across frames without requiring orthogonalization, thus preserving the integrity of the diffusion process. This technical detail is essential for the practical implementation and stability of multi-frame denoising within diffusion policies.
- **Generalized Approach**: MoF provides a more generalized framework for robotic manipulation, moving beyond the need for manual, task-specific selection of an optimal action frame. By incorporating multiple frame experts, it automates a challenging aspect of policy design.

The potential impact of this research is substantial, particularly in the field of mobile manipulation:
- **Enhanced Real-World Deployment**: The demonstrated improvements in real-world tasks like pouring and serving suggest that MoF can lead to more reliable and robust robotic systems capable of handling complex, multi-phase manipulation scenarios with dynamic coordination requirements.
- **Reduced Data Requirements**: By representing actions in frames where their distributions are simpler, MoF could potentially reduce the amount of demonstration data required to learn complex behaviors, as the policy does not need to learn to disentangle frame-induced complexity.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: MoF lays a foundation for future work in several areas:
 - **Scaling to Larger Models**: While current experiments focus on smaller diffusion policies, the framework is theoretically adaptable to larger vision-language-action (VLA) models, potentially extending its benefits to more generalized manipulation capabilities.
 - **Learning or Discovering Frames**: Future research could explore methods for automatically learning or discovering optimal task-relevant frames, rather than relying on a designer-specified set.
 - **Robustness to Frame Uncertainty**: Investigating how frame uncertainty affects multi-frame denoising in real-world deployments could lead to more robust policies in less controlled environments.
 - **Behavior-Aware Routing**: Optimizing router weights with behavior-aware objectives, beyond just denoising loss, could further enhance execution quality.

In summary, MoF represents a step towards developing more intelligent and adaptable robotic manipulation policies that can intrinsically reason about and utilize the inherent multi-frame nature of physical tasks.
