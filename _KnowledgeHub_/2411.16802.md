# Leveraging Foundation Models To learn the shape of semi-fluid deformable objects

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Alstom Transports and the University of Burgundy developed a model-free method for characterizing semi-fluid deformable objects, like weld pools, by leveraging foundation models to automatically generate shape and keypoint labels. Their teacher-student framework achieved a mean Euclidean Distance of 13.4 pixels for keypoint estimation, outperforming several baselines in industrial welding scenarios.

## Problem

- Traditional robotic manipulation primarily focuses on rigid objects, with limited robust methods for complex fluid or semi-fluid deformable objects.
- Existing deformable object characterization methods often require extensive, costly manual pixel-level labeling, which is impractical for dynamic or amorphous shapes like weld pools.
- Manipulating semi-fluid objects is challenging due to their highly dynamic topological surface changes and complex physical models that are difficult to predict or model accurately.

## Method

- A teacher-student framework is employed where foundation models (DINO and SAM2) act as the 'teacher' to automatically generate pixel-level object masks and keypoint heatmaps.
- DINO generates attention maps to guide SAM2 in segmenting the deformable object, and an algorithm extracts keypoints from the contour based on normal line angular variation for heatmap creation.
- A lightweight ResNet-Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE) serves as the 'student,' trained via knowledge distillation to reconstruct the teacher-generated masks and heatmaps for efficient inference.

## Results

- The DINO-SAM teacher network achieved a mean IoU of **75.26%** for object mask retrieval, demonstrating robust 'ground truth' generation for weld pools with appropriate prompt heuristics.
- The VAE student network, trained with knowledge distillation from the DINO-SAM teacher, achieved a mean Euclidean Distance (mED) of **13.4** pixels for keypoint estimation on weld pool images.
- The proposed method outperformed several state-of-the-art baselines for keypoint estimation, including Active Contour (mED **85.74**), KCF (mED **53.08**), SIFT (mED **32.8**), and a standalone CNN (mED **18.92**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Foundation models like DINO and SAM2 can effectively generalize to provide 'ground truth' labels for novel, amorphous objects such as weld pools, significantly reducing the need for manual data annotation.
> - Distilling knowledge from powerful, pre-trained foundation models into a smaller, more efficient generative network enables robust, model-free characterization suitable for real-time industrial applications.
> - Characterizing semi-fluid deformable objects by identifying keypoints based on contour normal line angular variation provides a stable and reliable feature representation despite high dynamic topological changes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The robotic manipulation of objects has traditionally focused on rigid bodies, where their shape and properties remain constant. However, an increasing demand for robots to interact with real-world, everyday objects, alongside advancements in machine learning and computer vision, has propelled research into deformable objects. The broader landscape of deformable object manipulation encompasses a variety of materials, from linear objects like ropes, through fabrics and soft tissues, to granular materials. Researchers have employed diverse representation methods, including continuous geometrical entities (surfaces, curves) and discrete representations (meshes, skeletons, landmark points).

While significant progress has been made in characterizing and manipulating non-fluid deformable objects such as clothes and ropes, these methods often rely on pixel-level information derived from segmentation networks. Such networks typically require extensive training on manually labeled datasets, which can be labor-intensive and challenging to scale. Furthermore, many existing solutions are highly application-specific, limiting their generalization to different object types or tasks.

A particular challenge lies in the characterization and manipulation of fluid or semi-fluid deformable objects, such as weld pools or glue. These objects exhibit high-dynamical topological surface changes and their shapes are governed by complex physical models, making their deformation difficult to predict or model. Current approaches for fluid manipulation are either highly simplified (e.g., treating a semi-fluid object as a single particle) or rely on complex model-based methods.

This paper addresses this specific gap by proposing a model-free approach that is particularly suited for fluid and semi-fluid deformable objects. It aims to overcome the limitations of manual labeling and domain-specific solutions by leveraging large-scale foundation models. By doing so, it contributes to the development of more generalized and data-efficient methods for robotic interaction with a broader range of complex, deformable materials, moving beyond the well-explored domains of rigid or easily characterized non-fluid deformable objects.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for robustly characterizing semi-fluid deformable objects, specifically weld pools, to define stable features that can inform subsequent motion control strategies. The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in manipulating such objects:

1. **Complexity of Fluid Deformations**: Fluid and semi-fluid objects like weld pools exhibit highly dynamic and unpredictable topological surface changes. Their shapes are influenced by numerous process parameters (e.g., robot speed, welding position, geometry type) and are governed by complex physical models, such as visco-elasticity, making it difficult to predict or model their deformations accurately. Traditional model-based approaches, while sometimes effective, often require detailed physical understanding and extensive computational resources.
2. **Limitations of Existing Deformable Object Characterization**: While methods exist for non-fluid deformable objects (e.g., fabrics, ropes), they are often not directly applicable to fluid-like materials. Many state-of-the-art approaches, even for softer objects like dough or tissues, rely on precise pixel-level information from segmentation networks. These networks, in turn, necessitate manually labeled data for training, a process that is costly, time-consuming, and prone to human error, especially for amorphous or dynamically changing shapes.
3. **Lack of Generalization**: A significant drawback of many current solutions is their application-oriented nature. Methods developed for specific tasks or objects often fail to generalize to other types of deformable materials or manipulation scenarios. The unpredictability of semi-fluid objects further exacerbates this generalization problem, driving the need for model-free characterization.
4. **Industrial Application Need**: The paper is motivated by a real-world industrial application: the state and shape estimation of weld pools in robotic welding. Accurate characterization of the molten metal pool is a critical prerequisite for advanced control strategies, such as feature-based visual servoing, which can enhance welding quality and process automation. The absence of existing benchmarks or datasets for this specific challenge further highlights the motivation to develop a practical solution.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of the research are:
- To propose a model-free method for characterizing fluid deformable objects based on their contour shapes, avoiding the need for complex physical modeling.
- To leverage the generalization capabilities of foundation models (specifically DINO and SAM2) to automatically extract pixel-level information (object masks) and representative keypoints from images, thereby eliminating the need for extensive pre-training or manually labeled datasets for the target object.
- To implement a teacher-student framework where knowledge from these powerful foundation models (teacher) is distilled into a smaller, more efficient generative network (student) suitable for inference in real-time industrial applications.
- To validate the proposed approach on a challenging, real-world industrial dataset of weld pools captured under harsh conditions, demonstrating its effectiveness in a practical setting.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology employs a teacher-student framework to characterize semi-fluid deformable objects, specifically weld pools, by learning to generate heatmaps representing keypoints along the object's contour.

**Overall Framework (Fig. 1):**
The core of the approach is a teacher-student architecture. The "teacher" component, composed of two foundation models, extracts the shape and keypoints of the deformable object, which are then used as "ground truth" labels. This knowledge is subsequently distilled into a "student" generative network, which is lighter and capable of efficient inference.

**Teacher Network Pipeline (DINO-SAM for Ground Truth Generation) (Fig. 2, Algorithm 1):**
The teacher network is responsible for generating two pieces of information: the object's mask and a heatmap indicating keypoint locations.

1. **DINO for Attention Map Generation**: The input image of the deformable object (e.g., weld pool) is first processed by the DINO (Self-Distillation with No Labels) model. DINO, a self-supervised vision transformer, is capable of generating attention maps that highlight salient regions within an image. Six such attention maps are retrieved.
2. **Prompt Extraction for SAM2**: The average of these six attention maps is computed. This average attention map is then thresholded based on its mean value to identify regions with the highest attention. These high-attention regions are used as spatial prompts for the subsequent SAM2 model.
3. **SAM2 for Object Mask Retrieval**: The original image, along with the extracted DINO prompts, is fed into SAM2 (Segment Anything Model 2). SAM2 is a prompt-based foundation model that generates a pixel-level segmentation mask for the specified prompts. This mask provides the precise boundary and shape of the object.
4. **Heatmap Generation (Algorithm 1)**:
 - **Contour Extraction**: The contour of the object is extracted directly from the DINO-SAM generated mask.
 - **Normal Line Angle Calculation**: The Sobel operator is applied to the object mask to determine the gradient directions, which are then used to calculate the angle ($\theta_i$) of the normal lines at each point ($p_i=(x_i, y_i)$) along the contour $C$.
 - **Identification of High Variation Points**: Points where the angular variation between consecutive normal lines ($|\theta_i - \theta_{i-1}|$) exceeds a predefined threshold ($\lambda$) are identified as potential keypoints. These points represent areas of significant curvature or shape change. This set of points is denoted as $P$.
 - **K-means Clustering**: K-means clustering is applied to the identified points in $P$ to group them into $k$ clusters ($P_j$). Each cluster is considered a region of interest.
 - **Gaussian Heatmap Creation**: For each cluster $P_j$, a 2D Gaussian distribution ($f_j$) is defined. The center ($\mu_j$) of this Gaussian is typically the centroid of the cluster, and the covariance matrix ($\Sigma_j$) is calculated from the points within the cluster.
 - **Final Heatmap Composition**: The final ground truth heatmap ($F$) is computed as the mixture (sum) of all individual Gaussian distributions $F_j$. This heatmap represents the most probable locations of the object's keypoints.
5. **Teacher Output Formulation**: The output of the teacher framework, serving as the ground truth for student training, is a concatenated matrix of shape (W, H, 2), where the first channel is the generated heatmap and the second channel is the DINO-SAM object mask.

**Student Network (ResNet-Variational Auto-Encoder - VAE):**
The student network is designed to be a lighter generative model capable of learning the representations generated by the teacher.

1. **Architecture**: A Variational Auto-Encoder (VAE) architecture is utilized, featuring a ResNet backbone for the encoder component. VAEs are chosen for their ability to learn latent probabilistic representations and generate new data with similar characteristics.
2. **Encoding**: The input image of the weld pool is encoded by the ResNet-based encoder into a continuous latent probabilistic space.
3. **Decoding**: The decoder then reconstructs the target output from this latent vector. The output layer consists of a fully connected convolutional layer, producing a two-channel matrix of the same dimensions as the input image (W, H, 2). These two channels correspond to the reconstructed DINO-SAM mask and the calculated heatmap, respectively.
4. **Reconstruction Objective**: The student network is trained to reconstruct the mask and heatmap generated by the teacher, rather than the original image itself. This approach is intended to accelerate convergence and enable the network to focus on learning the specific shape and keypoint information, as it directs the network's attention to a single zone and shape within the image.

**Data Acquisition and Dataset:**
To validate the methodology, an industrial application involving weld pool characterization was chosen.
- **Setup**: A custom dataset was generated by equipping a camera to an industrial robot that was performing welding operations.
- **Conditions**: The dataset captured weld pools under various industrial conditions, including changes in welding geometry (fillet joint / Tee joint), robot speed, and process parameters, along with external perturbations, which induce significant deformations in the molten pool.
- **Data**: Nine video sequences of the weld pool were acquired. Table I details the specific welding parameters used (wire feed rate, voltage, current, arc length correction, robot velocity).
- **Labeling**: The dataset was manually labeled with keypoints representing the weld pool's shape, primarily for validating the performance of the student network. A subset of the sequences was also manually labeled for segmentation to evaluate the DINO-SAM teacher framework's mask retrieval capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated both the teacher network's ability to generate accurate pixel-level information and the student network's performance in learning to estimate keypoints.

**Teacher Network Performance (DINO-SAM for Mask Retrieval):**
The performance of the DINO-SAM framework in retrieving accurate object masks was quantified using the Intersection Over Union (IoU) metric. An ablation study was conducted to assess different configurations and heuristics for optimizing SAM2 detections:
- **SAM2 without prompts**: Achieved a mean IoU (mIoU) of 35.37%, with an upper quartile of 59%. This indicated that SAM2 alone did not consistently generate reliable masks for the specific weld pool objects.
- **SAM2 with a single DINO prompt**: Showed a mIoU of 34.82%. While the upper quartile improved, the overall mIoU remained similar, suggesting that a single, unrefined prompt was insufficient.
- **DINO-SAM with Heuristics**: By implementing heuristics such as clustering the thresholded mean attention map, applying DBSCAN for optimal bounding box proposal, and filtering prompt points, a significant improvement was observed. This configuration yielded a mIoU of **75.26%**, with an upper quartile of 85%. This result indicated that with appropriate prompt generation and refinement, the DINO-SAM teacher could reliably retrieve pixel-level masks of the deformable object, providing a robust source of "ground truth" for the student.

**Student Network Performance (ResNet-VAE for Keypoint Estimation):**
The student model, a VAE with a ResNet backbone, was trained for 100 epochs. Its performance was evaluated by comparing its predicted heatmap centers to manually labeled keypoints, using the Euclidean distance. The average Euclidean distance over the dataset and for all heatmaps is denoted as mED.
- **Our Method (DINO-SAM Teacher + VAE Student)**: Achieved an overall mean Euclidean Distance (mED) of **13.4 pixels**, with a standard deviation of 3.1. This value is presented as acceptable for the target application.

**Comparison with Baselines (Table II):**
The proposed method was benchmarked against several relevant state-of-the-art techniques for similar problems:
- **Active Contour**: mED of 85.74 pixels.
- **Kernelized Correlation Filters (KCF)**: mED of 53.08 pixels.
- **Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT)**: mED of 32.8 pixels.
- **Coherent Point Drift (CPD) + Active Contour**: mED of 29.61 pixels.
- **Coherent Point Drift (CPD)**: mED of 26.59 pixels.
- **Fully Connected Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)**: mED of 18.92 pixels.
- **Variational Autoencoder (VAE) (without foundation models/DINO-SAM teacher)**: mED of 14.55 pixels.

The proposed method, integrating the DINO-SAM teacher with the VAE student, demonstrated the lowest mean Euclidean Distance (13.4 pixels) among all tested baselines. This indicates that the knowledge distillation from the foundation models enabled the student network to achieve a more accurate and stable estimation of keypoints compared to traditional and other deep learning approaches.

**Qualitative and Quantitative Discussion:**
- **Qualitative Evaluation**: The teacher-student framework provided stable estimations of the deformable object's shape without the requirement for prior manual labeling. However, the automatically generated masks from DINO-SAM sometimes suffered from ambiguity. This was attributed to erroneous prompt points, which could arise from the presence of impurities or intrusions (e.g., grease) in the industrial environment, occasionally leading to lower IoU values.
- **Quantitative Evaluation**: The mED of 13.4 pixels was considered small and acceptable. The paper acknowledged two main factors contributing to this error:
 1. **Human Factor in Labeling**: Validation images were manually labeled with keypoint centers, which introduces bias and potential inaccuracies into the ground truth for evaluation. Defining perfectly accurate labels for highly deformable and fluid-like objects is inherently challenging.
 2. **Lack of Visual Features**: Some frames in the dataset exhibited a lack of distinct visual features due to noise or process instability, which could lead to lower fidelity in keypoint predictions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation of deformable objects, particularly addressing the challenging category of semi-fluid materials. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Manipulation of Complex Deformables**: The work addresses a critical gap in robotic manipulation by providing a model-free method for characterizing highly-deformable fluid and viscoelastic objects, such as weld pools. This class of objects has historically been difficult to handle due to their unpredictable deformations and the limitations of traditional physical modeling approaches or application-specific solutions. By offering a robust characterization, the research paves the way for advanced robotic control strategies for these materials.

2. **Leveraging Foundation Models for Data Efficiency**: A key innovation is the effective integration of large-scale pre-trained foundation models (DINO and SAM2) as a "teacher" for knowledge distillation. This approach circumvents the need for extensive, manually labeled datasets—a major bottleneck in many deep learning applications for novel objects or domains. The ability of foundation models to generalize to unseen objects allows for automatic generation of pixel-level "ground truth" (masks and heatmaps), significantly reducing data annotation efforts for new applications. This strategy can accelerate the development of robotic systems for diverse tasks involving deformable objects.

3. **Industrial Application and Real-World Relevance**: The successful validation of the method on a real-world industrial dataset of weld pools in robotic welding underscores its practical applicability. Accurate characterization of the molten pool is crucial for process control (e.g., visual servoing) and ensuring weld quality. The method's demonstrated performance in harsh industrial conditions suggests its potential for immediate deployment and impact in manufacturing and automation sectors, improving the autonomy and precision of welding robots.

4. **Efficient Inference with Student Networks**: By distilling knowledge into a lighter generative network (ResNet-VAE), the proposed framework ensures that inference can be performed efficiently. This is vital for real-time robotic applications where computational resources and latency are critical constraints. The student model offers a balance between accuracy and computational cost, making it suitable for online control loops.

5. **Foundation for Advanced Control**: The stable and accurate estimation of keypoints, as represented by the heatmaps, provides critical feedback for closed-loop control systems. This information can be directly integrated into visual servoing or other feature-based control algorithms, enabling robots to adapt to the dynamic changes in the deformable object's shape during manipulation, leading to more robust and precise operations.

**Potential Future Directions**:
The paper outlines several avenues for future research, which further highlight its potential impact:
- **Teacher Improvement with Human-in-the-Loop**: Enhancing the teacher's performance through active learning techniques, incorporating human feedback, could refine the automatically generated ground truth labels and address ambiguities.
- **Integration with Control Tasks**: A direct next step is to integrate the retrieved shape information (weld pool heatmaps) into actual control tasks, such as visual servoing, to demonstrate practical manipulation capabilities.
- **Addressing Labeling Uncertainty**: Adopting automatically generated ground truth labels for evaluation, rather than biased manual annotations, could provide a more consistent and objective assessment of the system's performance.
- **Temporal Information Integration**: Utilizing sequential generative models like LSTM-VAE for the student network could enable the preservation and exploitation of temporal information, potentially improving prediction stability and accuracy for dynamic processes.

In conclusion, this research not only advances the understanding and capabilities for manipulating complex deformable objects but also offers a practical, data-efficient framework adaptable to various industrial scenarios, leveraging the capabilities of modern foundation models.
