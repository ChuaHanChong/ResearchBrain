# Learning Personalized Safety Interventions for Haptic Human-Robot Shared Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Boston University developed a Learning from Haptics (LfH) framework that personalizes safety interventions in haptic human-robot shared control by learning user-preferred force responses from sparse demonstrations. The framework, leveraging differentiable optimization, significantly reduced the error between generated and desired haptic forces in simulations and demonstrated adaptable safety guidance on physical hardware.

## Problem

- Existing haptic shared control systems rely on predefined or manually tuned intervention parameters, often failing to accommodate diverse user safety preferences or application scenarios.
- Manually tuning Control Barrier Function (CBF) response parameters is challenging due to the implicit and non-linear relationship between parameters and the resulting haptic force feedback.
- Current learning approaches in human-robot interaction primarily focus on command mappings or skill acquisition, rather than personalizing how an optimization-based safety controller communicates its interventions.

## Method

- A **Learning from Haptics (LfH)** framework is proposed, comprising teleoperation data recording, replay-based preference specification, and parameter learning via ==differentiable optimization==.
- Users provide sparse preference demonstrations by modifying the magnitude of recorded haptic forces during replay, which are then interpolated into continuous desired force trajectories using ==Gaussian Processes==.
- The framework employs differentiable ==Control Barrier Function-Quadratic Programs (CBF-QPs)== to learn personalized CBF response gains by backpropagating through the QP solution, matching generated haptic forces to user preferences.

## Results

- In AirSim simulations, the framework reduced the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between generated and desired haptic forces by up to **23.1%** compared to a nominal CBF baseline.
- Learning semantically grouped CBF gains for different obstacles further improved preference matching, demonstrating a **4.6%** additional MSE reduction over a globally learned gain.
- Hardware experiments with a DJI Tello drone successfully deployed learned obstacle-specific haptic interventions, adapting force feedback in real-time to reflect user-specified preferences.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Differentiable optimization allows for direct learning of implicit control parameters (CBF response gains) from user demonstrations, overcoming the challenges of manual tuning.
> - Learning to personalize the *communication* of safety through haptic feedback preserves the operator's direct control authority while enhancing usability, rather than overriding commands or core safety logic.
> - Sparse, replay-based preference specification, combined with Gaussian Process interpolation, can effectively guide the learning of continuous haptic force trajectories without requiring dense or complex annotations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of human-robot interaction, specifically focusing on haptic shared control (HSC) for teleoperation in safety-critical applications. Teleoperation allows human operators to control robots in environments that are hazardous or challenging for fully autonomous systems. HSC enhances safety by conveying safety-related information to the operator through force feedback, thereby preserving human decision-making authority.

Historically, haptic guidance systems employed predefined intervention strategies, often derived from explicitly designed safety metrics such as parametric risk fields, time-to-impact, or dynamic kinesthetic boundaries. More recently, Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) have been adopted to generate haptic safety interventions. In CBF-based HSC, a virtual safety controller computes a constraint-compliant reference, and the deviation between this reference and the operator's command is rendered as haptic force feedback. This approach maintains the operator's direct control authority while implicitly communicating safety information.

A limitation of existing CBF-based HSC systems is their reliance on manually tuned response parameters. These parameters govern the timing and strength of safety interventions, and their selection significantly influences constraint feasibility, conservativeness, and overall closed-loop behavior. Different parameter values can lead to varied intervention behaviors, which may need to adapt based on application scenarios or individual operator preferences. However, manual tuning is complex and time-consuming because the relationship between optimization parameters and haptic feedback is implicitly defined by the CBF-based quadratic program (CBF-QP) solution.

This work addresses the challenge of personalizing haptic safety interventions by integrating machine learning with CBF-based control. While previous learning approaches in human-robot interaction have focused on learning human-to-robot command mappings or improving operator skill acquisition, this paper aims to learn how an existing optimization-based safety controller should communicate its intervention through haptic feedback. Similarly, existing work combining learning with CBFs typically focuses on improving safety certificates, identifying uncertain system dynamics, or optimizing autonomous control policies. In contrast, this research assumes the safety controller is available and learns the response parameters that shape the haptic intervention perceived by the human operator.

The paper builds on advances in differentiable optimization, which allow optimization-based controllers to be embedded directly into learning architectures. Specifically, it leverages the concept of differentiable quadratic programs (QPs) and CBF-QPs. Unlike prior differentiable CBF methods that learn barrier functions, system dynamics, or autonomous policies, this framework uses the differentiable CBF-QP as an implicit model to learn user-preferred safety intervention parameters. This positions the work as a contribution to the personalization and usability aspects of safety-critical shared control, offering a method to adapt haptic guidance to individual user preferences without requiring extensive manual tuning or modifying the fundamental safety guarantees of the CBF structure.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of current haptic shared control (HSC) systems, particularly those relying on Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) for safety interventions. These systems typically employ predefined intervention strategies that are determined by fixed response parameters.

The key motivations can be detailed as follows:
- **Lack of Personalization:** Existing haptic guidance systems often fail to accommodate the diverse safety preferences of individual users or the specific requirements of various application scenarios. For instance, an operator might prefer an earlier and stronger intervention near a moving obstacle like a pedestrian, but a weaker and less intrusive intervention near a static obstacle. Predefined parameters cannot adapt to such nuanced preferences.
- **Difficulty of Manual Parameter Tuning:** The response parameters of CBFs (e.g., $k_1, k_2$ in exponential CBFs) significantly influence how and when a safety intervention is communicated through haptic feedback. However, obtaining a desired haptic behavior by manually adjusting these parameters is challenging. The mapping from these optimization parameters to the resulting haptic feedback is implicitly defined by the solution of a CBF-based quadratic program (CBF-QP). Consequently, even minor parameter adjustments can lead to unintuitive changes in the haptic behavior, making manual tuning a time-consuming process that demands considerable expertise. This constitutes a significant usability barrier for deploying CBF-based HSC systems.
- **Preserving Operator Authority:** The objective is not to replace the human operator's control but to enhance safety and intuitiveness. The framework aims to personalize the *communication* of safety through haptics, rather than overriding the operator's commands or learning an end-to-end control policy. This approach ensures the operator retains full control authority over the robot.

To address these motivations, the authors established the following key objectives and contributions:
- **Propose a Learning from Haptics (LfH) Framework:** Develop a framework that enables users to personalize optimization-based safety interventions for HSC. This framework is designed to learn user-preferred force responses from sparse demonstrations, eliminating the need for direct manual tuning of CBF response parameters.
- **Formulate Personalized Safety Intervention Learning as a Differentiable Optimization Problem:** Embed the CBF-QP directly into the learning pipeline as a differentiable optimization layer. This allows the framework to adapt CBF response parameters by backpropagating through the QP solution, enabling direct learning from user demonstrations while preserving the structured CBF-based intervention mechanism.
- **Validate the Framework Across Different Safety Representations and Platforms:** Conduct evaluations using both hardware-in-the-loop simulation and real-world hardware experiments. This objective includes demonstrating that the framework can learn personalized haptic safety interventions compatible with diverse underlying safety representations, such as FPV perception-based semantic constraints and Signed Distance Field (SDF)-based constraints. The validation aims to confirm that the framework can generate haptic guidance reflecting demonstrated preferences while maintaining the intuitive interaction of haptic shared control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Learning from Haptics (LfH) framework consists of three main stages: teleoperation and data recording, replay-based preference specification and parameter learning, and deployment with the learned CBF response gains.

#### 3.1. Control Barrier Function (CBF)-based Haptic Shared Control (HSC)

The framework is built upon a second-order CBF formulation for acceleration-controlled robot dynamics, specifically a double integrator model, where robot state $x = [x_p, x_v]^T$ includes position and velocity, and control input $u$ is acceleration.
- **Safety Definition:** A safe set $\mathcal{H}$ is defined as the zero superlevel set of a continuously differentiable function $h(x) \geq 0$.
- **CBF Constraint:** Safety is enforced using an exponential CBF, which dictates $\ddot{h} \geq -k_1 h - k_2 \dot{h}$, where $k_1, k_2 > 0$ are the CBF response gains. These gains determine the dynamic response, influencing the timing and aggressiveness of the safety intervention.
- **CBF-QP:** The control input $u_{cbf}$ that minimally deviates from a reference control $u_{ref}$ while satisfying the CBF constraint is computed by solving a quadratic program (QP):
 $u_{cbf} = \text{arg min}_{u \in \mathbb{R}^d} \frac{1}{2} \|u - u_{ref}\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \text{(CBF constraints)}$.
- **Haptic Force Feedback:** The haptic force feedback $F_{cbf}$ perceived by the user is defined as $F_{cbf} = k_f (u_{cbf} - u_{ref})$, where $k_f > 0$ is a scaling factor. Critically, $u_{cbf}$ is used *only* for generating haptic feedback; the actual control input applied to the robot remains $u_{ref}$, which is derived from the operator's desired velocity $x_{vd}$. This ensures the operator retains direct control.

#### 3.2. Data Collection and Preparation

1. **Initial Teleoperation and Recording:** An initial teleoperation session is conducted using nominal CBF response gains $\Theta_0$. During this session, the robot's state $x^{(j)}$, the operator's reference input $u_{ref}^{(j)}$, active constraint data $C^{(j)}$, the nominal haptic force $F_{cbf,0}^{(j)}$, and synchronized first-person-view (FPV) observations are recorded for $N$ samples.
2. **Replay-based Preference Specification:** To avoid requiring dense annotations, users provide preferences through a replay interface. The operator reviews the recorded FPV sequence and the corresponding haptic feedback (visualized and physically rendered). At selected time instants $t_i$, the operator sparsely modifies *only the magnitude* of the recorded force, using a force-scaling factor $\rho_i$, while preserving its direction. This yields sparse preference demonstrations $D_{spar} = \{(t_i, \rho_i)\}_{i=1}^M$.
3. **Gaussian Process (GP) Interpolation:** Gaussian Process regression is employed to interpolate these sparse force-scaling annotations into a continuous desired force trajectory. A GP model for the time-varying force scale $\rho(t)$ is used with a prior mean $\mu_{\rho}(t)=1$ (ensuring nominal behavior in unannotated regions) and a radial basis function kernel. The operator can adjust the kernel length scale $r$ to control the temporal influence of each demonstration. The posterior mean $\hat{\rho}(t)$ is evaluated at each sample time $t_j$ to construct the desired force trajectory $F_{wish}^{(j)} = \hat{\rho}(t_j) F_{cbf,0}^{(j)}$. The posterior variance $\sigma_{\rho}^2(t)$ quantifies confidence and is used for weighted training.

#### 3.3. Learning from Haptics (Differentiable Optimization)

1. **Learnable Parameters:** The learnable parameters are a set of grouped CBF response gains $\Theta = \{\theta_s\}_{s \in S_{learn}}$, where each group $s$ is associated with gains $\theta_s = [k_{1,s}, k_{2,s}]^T$. These groups correspond to semantic categories of obstacles or constraints.
2. **Learning Objective:** The personalized response gains $\Theta^\star$ are obtained by minimizing a loss function that quantifies the discrepancy between the generated haptic force $F_{cbf}^{(j)}(\Theta)$ and the desired force $F_{wish}^{(j)}$. The sample-wise preference loss is $\ell_j(\Theta) = \|F_{cbf}^{(j)}(\Theta) - F_{wish}^{(j)}\|_2^2$. The total loss over the trajectory is $L(\Theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N w_j \ell_j(\Theta)$, where $w_j$ are sample weights (e.g., uniform, semantic-informativeness, or GP-based $1/(\sigma_\rho^2(t_j) + \epsilon)$).
3. **Regularization:** A regularization term $R(\Theta) = \lambda \|\Theta - \Theta_0\|_2^2$ is added to discourage large deviations from the nominal gains, improving numerical stability.
4. **Differentiable CBF-QP:** The core of the learning process is to optimize $\Theta$ by minimizing $L(\Theta) + R(\Theta)$, subject to $\Theta$ remaining in an admissible set $\Omega$ (gains positive and bounded). Since $F_{cbf}^{(j)}(\Theta)$ depends implicitly on $\Theta$ through the solution of the CBF-QP, gradients are computed by backpropagating through a differentiable CBF-QP layer. The QP is expressed in matrix form $u_{cbf} = \text{arg min } \frac{1}{2} \|u - u_{ref}\|^2 \text{ s.t. } Au \leq B(\Theta)$, where $\Theta$ influences the vector $B(\Theta)$. The gradients of the optimal QP solution with respect to $B(\Theta)$ (and thus $\Theta$) are derived from the Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions. CVXPYLayers and PyTorch are used for this implementation.

#### 3.4. Safety Constraint Construction

The framework is compatible with different safety representations, provided they yield the barrier function $h_i$, its gradient $\partial_{x_p} h_i$, and its Hessian $\partial^2_{x_p} h_i$.
- **FPV Perception-Based (AirSim Simulation):** Constraints are generated from synchronized RGB, depth, and semantic-label images. Pixels within a region of interest are grouped by semantic labels. For each group, a representative depth $d_i$ and a local clearance-increasing direction $n_i$ (from camera ray and world rotation) are computed. The barrier value is $h_i = d_i - d_{safe}$, and its derivatives are approximated as $\dot{h}_i \approx n_i^T x_v$ and $\ddot{h}_i \approx n_i^T u$.
- **Signed Distance Field (SDF)-based (Hardware Experiment):** For known obstacles, SDF values $d_{SDF,i}(x_p)$ are pre-computed on a grid. The barrier function is $h_i(x_p) = d_{SDF,i}(x_p) - d_{safe}$. Its spatial derivatives $\nabla d_{SDF,i}(x_p)$ and $\nabla^2 d_{SDF,i}(x_p)$ are obtained by interpolating from the discrete SDF.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of the proposed framework was conducted through two distinct experimental settings: an AirSim hardware-in-the-loop simulation and a physical hardware demonstration.

#### 4.1. AirSim Simulation Evaluation (FPV Perception-Based Constraints)

This experiment aimed to quantitatively evaluate how well the learned CBF response gains could reproduce demonstrated haptic preferences.
- **Experimental Setup:** A simulated aerial robot (drone) was teleoperated using a Geomagic Touch haptic device, with the operator receiving only a first-person-view (FPV). AirSim provided synchronized RGB, depth, and semantic labels for constructing FPV perception-based CBF constraints, where constraints were grouped by semantic labels (e.g., 'car', 'tree').
- **Force Preference Matching:** The framework was evaluated based on the Mean Error and Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the generated haptic force and the operator's demonstrated preference over informative samples (where at least one constraint was active and produced a non-zero haptic response).
 - The **Nominal CBF** baseline (without learning) exhibited an MSE of 1.8786.
 - Learning a single global gain pair for all constraints (**Global DiffQP**) reduced the MSE to 1.5147, representing a 19.4% reduction compared to the nominal CBF.
 - Learning separate gain pairs for the two most informative semantic groups (**Top-K grouped**) further reduced the MSE to 1.4451. This constituted a 23.1% reduction relative to the nominal CBF and an additional 4.6% reduction compared to Global DiffQP. This result suggests that semantic grouping improved the capture of varied intervention preferences.
 - Variants using semantic-informativeness weighting (**Top-K semantic-weighted**, MSE 1.4457) and GP-based weighting (**Top-K GP-weighted**, MSE 1.5747) performed similarly or slightly less effectively than the uniformly weighted Top-K grouped model. The GP-weighted variant's higher MSE was attributed to potentially overemphasizing sparse edit points.
 - A residual mismatch was observed, attributed to the representation of frame-dependent modifications by a compact set of grouped gains.
- **Visual Analysis:** Visual comparisons of force trajectories (Figure 4) indicated that forces regenerated using the learned gains more closely followed the demonstrated preferences, especially during periods when the operator explicitly modified the intervention strength.

#### 4.2. Hardware Demonstration (SDF-Based Constraints)

This study focused on qualitatively demonstrating the deployment of the learned response gains in a physical aerial teleoperation loop and assessing compatibility with SDF-based constraints.
- **Experimental Setup:** A DJI Tello drone was teleoperated via a Geomagic Touch device in an environment with a rectangular and two circular obstacles. An OptiTrack motion-capture system provided drone and obstacle states, which were used to construct SDF-based CBF constraints. Each obstacle was assigned a distinct constraint group for learning.
- **Personalized Hardware Intervention:**
 - An initial teleoperation session was conducted with nominal CBF response gains.
 - The operator then reviewed the recorded FPV and specified preferences: a stronger intervention near obstacle A, no intervention associated with obstacle B, and a weaker intervention near obstacle C. These sparse demonstrations were used to learn obstacle-specific CBF response gains.
 - In a subsequent flight using the learned gains, the drone's haptic feedback became stronger near obstacle A, was suppressed near obstacle B, and remained weaker near obstacle C, consistently reflecting the operator's demonstrated preferences.
- **Deployment and Compatibility:** This experiment demonstrated the framework's ability to deploy learned obstacle-specific response gains on a physical drone with a haptic device. It also confirmed compatibility with SDF-based constraint construction, showcasing the framework's adaptability to different safety representations. Due to the inherent variability of manual teleoperation across separate flights, this was presented as a qualitative deployment demonstration rather than a precise frame-wise force comparison.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses a notable challenge in haptic human-robot shared control: the personalization of safety interventions. The work holds several points of significance and potential impact:

- **Enabling User-Preferred Safety Interventions:** The primary significance lies in providing a method for users to specify their preferred haptic safety intervention behaviors. This contrasts with traditional systems that rely on predefined or manually tuned parameters, which often fail to account for individual user preferences or context-specific requirements. By allowing users to "teach" the system their preferences through sparse demonstrations, the framework promotes a more intuitive and user-centric approach to human-robot interaction in safety-critical tasks.

- **Reducing Design and Tuning Effort:** The framework largely eliminates the need for manual, trial-and-error tuning of complex CBF response parameters. Manually tuning these parameters is often time-consuming and requires significant expertise due to the non-linear and implicit relationship between parameters and the resulting haptic feedback. The proposed differentiable optimization approach automates this process, potentially reducing development time and barriers to deploying personalized shared control systems.

- **Preservation of Shared Control Authority:** A key aspect of the method is that it learns how the safety controller *communicates* its intervention through haptic feedback, rather than learning to override the operator's commands or replace the underlying safety logic. This maintains the operator's direct control authority over the robot, which is critical for tasks requiring human judgment and adaptability, while enhancing safety through implicit guidance.

- **Versatility with Safety Representations:** The demonstrated compatibility with both FPV perception-based semantic constraints and SDF-based constraints indicates the framework's generalizability. This suggests that the learning approach is not tied to a specific method of obstacle detection or safety boundary definition, making it applicable to a broader range of robotic systems and environments.

- **Advancement in Learning-Enabled Control:** By integrating differentiable optimization with CBF-based control in a novel way—learning intervention *response parameters* from haptic demonstrations—the research contributes to the growing field of learning-enabled safety-critical control. This approach differs from typical applications that learn system dynamics, barrier functions, or entire control policies, focusing instead on refining the human-robot interface for safety.

- **Potential for Broader Applications:** The ability to personalize haptic feedback could enhance the usability and safety of teleoperation in various domains, including remote manipulation in hazardous environments, search and rescue operations, assistive robotics, and potentially advanced driver-assistance systems. More generally, it offers a pathway to more adaptable and user-friendly human-robot shared autonomy systems.

Future work outlined, such as investigating nonlinear parameterizations and generalization across scenarios, suggests avenues for further enhancing the adaptability and robustness of such personalized haptic interfaces, potentially leading to more sophisticated and practical implementations.
