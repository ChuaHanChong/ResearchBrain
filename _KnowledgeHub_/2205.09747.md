# HandoverSim: A Simulation Framework and Benchmark for Human-to-Robot Object Handovers

> [!summary] Summary
> HandoverSim is a PyBullet simulation benchmark for human-to-robot object handovers. Human giver motion is replayed from the DexYCB motion-capture dataset by directly actuating hand and object poses, with active and passive release triggers on gripper contact. It defines 1,000 scenes, four train/val/test splits, and efficacy, efficiency, and safety metrics. Baseline results correlate positively with a real-world user study.

## Problem

- Handover evaluation typically needs a real human in the loop, making experiments expensive, slow, and hard for other groups to reproduce.
- Handover studies use divergent objects, settings, and metrics, so cross-study comparison of receiver policies is effectively impossible.
- Existing robotics simulators either omit humans entirely or use passive, cost-function-driven avatars lacking realistic captured hand motion and physical handover contact.

## Method

- Motion replay driving: hand and grasped object get base ==actuators== so captured trajectories are directly commanded, avoiding learning a dexterous control policy; hand-object collision disabled to suppress capture-noise artifacts.
- Release model: ==active release== fires after 0.1 seconds of continuous contact with both gripper finger surfaces; ==passive release== fires on contact with other robot parts, after which the object is purely physical.
- ==Gym-API== benchmark: 9-D joint-position action space, spherical goal region success test, three failure conditions (human contact, drop, timeout), and four splits S0-S3 probing unseen subjects, handedness, and grasped objects.

## Results

- On S0, Yang et al. reaches **64.58%** success versus OMG Planner **62.50%**, GA-DDPG hold **50.00%**, and GA-DDPG without hold **36.81%**, the weakest baseline.
- Failure modes separate sharply: OMG Planner causes **27.78%** human-contact failures but only **1.39%** timeouts, while GA-DDPG hold has **4.86%** contact and **25.69%** timeout.
- Simulation ranking matches a real user study: Yang et al. beats GA-DDPG in real success (**82%** vs **80%**) and approach time (**10.7s** vs **12.1s**), matching simulated trends.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Replaying captured human motion with directly actuated objects sidesteps dexterous-hand control entirely, a cheap route to realistic human agents in simulation.
> - Aggregate success rate hides safety: methods with similar success can differ two-fold in human-contact failures, so multi-objective metrics are essential.
> - Simulated rankings can track real-world rankings even when absolute numbers offset, since perception latency and cooperative human partners are absent in simulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The seamless and safe exchange of objects between humans and robots is a fundamental capability in human-robot interaction (HRI), with implications for domestic, assistive, and collaborative robotics. Despite ongoing research, progress in human-robot object handovers faces two primary challenges: the high cost and reproducibility issues associated with real-world evaluations requiring human participation, and the lack of standardized experimental settings and metrics, which hinders cross-study comparisons.

The broader robotics and machine learning communities have increasingly adopted standardized datasets and simulation benchmarks to accelerate progress and improve reproducibility. This approach has been successful in domains such as computer vision, object manipulation, and navigation. While efforts exist to create simulation environments for various robotic tasks, including those involving human-like agents (e.g., Assistive Gym for assistive robotics, Watch-and-Help for social intelligence), simulating realistic human-robot physical interaction, particularly for contact-rich tasks like object handovers, remains an active research area. Existing human simulations often either lack high-fidelity physics or are driven by learned motions rather than captured human data.

Specifically for object handovers, prior work has focused on various aspects, including intent communication, grasp planning, perception, and motion control. Some benchmarks exist at the vision level (e.g., large-scale human-to-human handover datasets for grasp prediction) or for specific object categories in real-world settings. However, a comprehensive simulation benchmark that enables physics-simulated evaluation of the full human-to-robot (H2R) handover process across a diverse range of objects, using standardized protocols and metrics, has been largely absent.

HandoverSim addresses this gap by introducing a simulation framework and benchmark specifically for H2R object handovers. It extends previous efforts in creating standardized benchmarks for robotics by focusing on the unique challenges of simulating realistic human motion and interaction in handover scenarios, leveraging motion capture data to drive the human giver's actions within a physics-based simulation environment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research were to develop a simulation framework and benchmark that could facilitate standardized, reproducible, and scalable research in human-to-robot object handovers. The motivation stemmed from several key challenges inherent in the current state of HRI handover research:

- **Cost and Reproducibility of Evaluation:** Traditional evaluation of handover systems often necessitates real human participants, making experiments expensive, time-consuming, and difficult to reproduce consistently across different research groups or even within the same group over time. A simulation-based environment offers a pathway to mitigate these issues by providing a controlled and repeatable testing ground.
- **Lack of Standardization:** Research in human-robot handovers frequently employs disparate experimental setups (e.g., types of objects, environmental configurations) and evaluation metrics. This heterogeneity complicates the comparison of different approaches and impedes the clear assessment of progress within the field. The establishment of a standardized benchmark, complete with unified protocols and metrics, was identified as crucial for enabling fair comparisons and fostering cumulative research.
- **Realistic Human Simulation:** A significant challenge in creating a valuable handover simulation is accurately representing the human giver's behavior. Previous simulation efforts for human-robot interaction often simplify human models or rely on less realistic motion generation. For object handovers, the realism of human hand motion, particularly during grasping and offering, and the subsequent interaction with the robot, is critical. The motivation was to incorporate realistic human motion to create a more relevant and challenging simulation environment for robot policies.
- **Focus on Human-to-Robot (H2R) Paradigm:** The work specifically targets the H2R handover paradigm, where the robot acts as the receiver. This scenario presents particular challenges for the robot, as it must adapt to the human's offering behavior.
- **Scalable Training and Evaluation:** A simulation environment can enable the generation of large amounts of data, which is beneficial for training data-intensive machine learning and reinforcement learning algorithms. By creating a benchmark, the authors aimed to provide an environment where robot policies could be trained and rigorously evaluated on a diverse set of scenarios, reducing the dependency on real-world data collection for early development stages.

In summary, the motivation was to provide a robust, reproducible, and standardized platform for developing and evaluating robot policies for human-to-robot object handovers, addressing the limitations of real-world experimentation and the absence of comprehensive simulation tools for this specific HRI task.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HandoverSim framework and benchmark were constructed using the PyBullet physics engine, focusing on simulating the "human-to-robot" (H2R) handover scenario. The environment includes a human giver and a robot arm receiver, facing each other with an object on a table between them.

**Simulation of Human Giver's Motion:**
A key methodological component was to simulate realistic human motion. The authors leveraged the DexYCB dataset, a motion capture dataset of human subjects grasping objects and offering them to an imagined partner. This dataset provided frame-wise 3D poses of both the human hand (represented by the deformable MANO mesh model) and 20 YCB objects.
To reproduce this motion in PyBullet, the hand model was imported as an articulated rigid body. Rather than learning a complex dexterous hand control policy, which is computationally intensive and challenging, the authors chose to augment the object models with actuators. This allowed for direct control of the object's 6D pose, moving it simultaneously with the human hand according to the captured trajectories. This approach circumvented the complexities of high-fidelity dexterous hand physics and control while maintaining the visual and kinematic realism of the human's offering gesture. Collision detection between the human hand and the object was disabled to prevent simulation artifacts arising from minor interpenetration in the motion capture data. The "pre-handover" phase involved replaying the captured motion, with the human hand and object maintaining their final pose, simulating a human waiting for the robot.
The human's motion in this simulation is non-adaptive, meaning it does not react to the robot's actions. This assumption is noted to be applicable in scenarios where the human is focused on other tasks. The simulation also does not model realistic human hand motion during or immediately after object release due due to data limitations.

**Object Release Mechanism:**
The "physical handover" phase, where the object is released, was simulated using two conditions:
- **Active Release:** Triggered when the object maintains continuous contact with both gripping surfaces of the robot gripper for 0.1 seconds, indicating a successful grasp by the robot.
- **Passive Release:** Triggered if the object is not in contact with the gripper's gripping surfaces but has continuous contact with any other part of the robot's body for 0.1 seconds. This simulates involuntary drops due to robot strikes.
Upon release, the object becomes passive, subjected only to robot forces and gravity.

**Robot Receiver and Task Formalization:**
The robot model used is a Franka Emika Panda with a 7-DoF arm and a 2-DoF parallel-jaw gripper. The task is formalized as a standard reinforcement learning problem using the OpenAI Gym API. The robot observes the environment state (e.g., object pose, point cloud) and generates a 9-dimensional continuous action for the arm and gripper joint positions, which are executed via a PD controller.

**Success and Failure Conditions:**
- **Success:** Defined by three simultaneous conditions: 1) gripper fingers contacting the object, 2) gripper link position within a predefined spherical goal region in front of the robot, and 3) these conditions holding continuously for 0.1 seconds.
- **Failure:** Defined by three distinct causes: 1) **Contact:** Any part of the robot body touching the human hand, indicating an unsafe handover. 2) **Drop:** Gripper not holding the object, and the object contacting the table/other objects or falling below the table height. 3) **Timeout:** Exceeding a maximum episode duration of 13 seconds. An episode terminates upon success or any failure, preventing successful completion after a failure event.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
The benchmark measures efficacy, efficiency, and safety:
- **Efficacy:** Success rate and failure rates (categorized by contact, drop, and timeout).
- **Efficiency:** Mean completion time for successful episodes, divided into execution time (robot physical movement) and planning time (policy function computation).
- **Safety:** Failure rate due to robot-human contact.

**Benchmark Environment Statistics and Setups:**
The benchmark uses 1,000 motion capture sequences from DexYCB, corresponding to 10 subjects, 20 objects, and 5 trials per subject-object pair. Two objects ("002 master chef can" and "036 wood block") were removed due to gripper capacity limitations, resulting in 18 objects for evaluation.
Four evaluation setups (S0-S3) were designed to test generalization capabilities:
- **S0 (Default):** Standard train/validation/test split across all subjects and objects.
- **S1 (Unseen Subjects):** Splits data by subjects (7/1/2 for train/val/test).
- **S2 (Unseen Handedness):** Splits data by the hand used (Right for train, Left for val/test).
- **S3 (Unseen Grasping):** Splits data by objects (14/2/2 for train/val/test).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated three categories of baselines on HandoverSim, assuming perfect perception (ground-truth states available) to focus on planning and control capabilities. The baselines included a motion planning method, a task planning method, and a pre-trained reinforcement learning policy, none of which were specifically trained on HandoverSim.

**Baselines Evaluated:**
- **OMG Planner:** A joint motion and grasp planner that selects a goal and generates a trajectory. It uses pre-generated grasp poses and does not account for the human hand. It operates open-loop, waiting for the human to stop before planning.
- **Yang et al.:** A reactive H2R handover system employing point cloud-based grasp generation and task planning. For this benchmark, it bypassed grasp generation, directly using pre-generated grasps.
- **GA-DDPG:** A neural network policy trained with reinforcement learning for grasping static objects. It takes segmented point clouds as input and outputs target joint positions. Two variants were tested: "hold" (waiting for human to stop) and "w/o hold" (no waiting).

**Performance Analysis (Focus on S0 - Default Setup):**

1. **OMG Planner:**
 - **Efficacy:** Achieved a competitive success rate of 62.50%. Failure cases were dominated by robot-human contact (27.78%), consistent with its lack of human hand awareness. Object dropping (8.33%) and timeout (1.39%) rates were the lowest among baselines.
 - **Efficiency:** Exhibited the highest mean accumulated time for both execution (8.309s) and planning (1.414s), indicating a slower operational speed.

2. **Yang et al.:**
 - **Efficacy:** Showed a comparable success rate (64.58%) to the OMG Planner. Its robot-human contact failure rate was significantly lower (17.36%), attributed to its tendency to approach objects from the front, away from the human hand. However, the higher acceleration and deceleration led to a higher object drop rate (11.81%).
 - **Efficiency:** Achieved competitive mean accumulated execution time (4.864s) and the lowest planning time (0.036s), reflecting its reactive nature.

3. **GA-DDPG (hold variant):**
 - **Efficacy:** Recorded a lower success rate (50.00%) compared to the OMG Planner and Yang et al., likely due to the ambiguity of point cloud input versus ground-truth object poses and pre-generated grasps. It had a higher timeout rate (25.69%), often due to mis-grasps, but a much lower robot-human contact rate (4.86%) as mis-grasps often occurred before close proximity to the hand.
 - **Efficiency:** Execution time was comparable to Yang et al. (7.139s), but planning time was slightly slower (0.142s) due to point cloud processing.

4. **GA-DDPG (w/o hold variant):**
 - **Efficacy:** Achieved the lowest success rate (36.81%) among all baselines. Failures often resulted from the gripper contacting the human hand or object while in motion, as the policy was not trained for moving objects.
 - **Efficiency:** Demonstrated the lowest mean accumulated execution and total time (4.664s execution, 4.796s total) due to not being forced to wait.

**Generalization Across Setups (S1, S2, S3):**
The trends observed in S0 generally held across the other three setups (S1, S2, S3), which tested generalization to unseen subjects, handedness, and objects, respectively. The OMG Planner and Yang et al. generally maintained higher success rates, while GA-DDPG variants showed lower performance but also reduced contact failures.

**Correlation with Real-World Evaluation:**
The paper reported a positive correlation between performance on HandoverSim and a real-world user study from a prior work comparing Yang et al. and GA-DDPG.
- **Success Rate:** Yang et al. slightly outperformed GA-DDPG in both the real-world study (82% vs. 80%) and HandoverSim (e.g., 62.78% vs. 55.00% on S1).
- **Efficiency:** Yang et al. demonstrated lower approach times in the real world (10.7s vs. 12.1s) and lower mean accumulated times in HandoverSim (e.g., 4.758s vs. 6.927s on S1). Subjective user fluency scores also aligned with this trend.

**Observed Offsets between Real and Simulation:**
- **Higher Success in Real World:** Real-world success rates were consistently higher than in HandoverSim (e.g., GA-DDPG: 80% real vs. 55.00% sim on S1). This was attributed to human cooperativeness in real scenarios, where users might adjust object pose to prevent failures.
- **Lower Efficiency in Real World:** Real-world efficiency (approach time) was consistently lower (longer) than in HandoverSim (e.g., Yang et al.: 10.7s real vs. 4.758s sim on S1). This offset was explained by two factors: the absence of perception latency in HandoverSim (baselines used ground-truth state) compared to real systems, and the use of simpler control modules in HandoverSim versus potentially more sophisticated, latency-inducing control stacks in real-world systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

HandoverSim represents a significant contribution to the field of human-robot interaction and robotic manipulation by introducing the first dedicated simulation benchmark for human-to-robot object handovers. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Standardization and Reproducibility:** By providing a unified environment, standardized protocols, and consistent evaluation metrics across diverse handover scenarios, HandoverSim directly addresses the existing challenges of comparing different HRI handover approaches. This standardization is expected to improve the reproducibility of research, allowing for more objective and direct comparisons of new algorithms.
- **Accelerated Research and Development:** The simulation framework enables scalable training and evaluation of robot policies. Researchers can iterate on new algorithms, especially data-intensive machine learning and reinforcement learning methods, without the need for expensive, time-consuming, or safety-critical real-world experiments in early development stages. This could significantly accelerate the pace of innovation in handover robotics.
- **Focus on Realistic Human Motion:** The integration of motion capture data (from DexYCB) to drive the human giver's motion in simulation is a key methodological advancement. This approach contributes to a more realistic and challenging environment for robot policies, as the robot must contend with human behaviors derived from real-world interaction, rather than simplified or procedurally generated motions.
- **Benchmarking Planning and Control:** By assuming perfect perception (ground-truth states), the initial benchmark results primarily assess the planning and control capabilities of robot policies. This allows for a modular evaluation, isolating the challenges of physical interaction from those of perception, which can be integrated in future work.
- **Credibility through Real-World Correlation:** The demonstrated positive correlation between performance in HandoverSim and a real-world user study provides initial validation of the benchmark's fidelity and relevance. While offsets exist, the consistent trends suggest that advancements made in the simulated environment can translate to practical improvements in real-world HRI systems.
- **Identification of Future Research Directions:** The detailed analysis of baseline performance highlights specific areas for improvement. For example, the high contact failure rate of the OMG Planner indicates a need for human-aware motion planning, while the GA-DDPG's performance on moving objects points to the need for policies capable of adapting to dynamic human actions. The framework can serve as a foundation for developing adaptive and safe handover strategies.
- **Foundation for Broader HRI Research:** HandoverSim provides a platform that can be extended to explore more complex HRI challenges, such as integrating advanced perception modules, modeling adaptive human behavior, or investigating more nuanced interaction dynamics. The open-sourced code further promotes community engagement and collaborative development.

In conclusion, HandoverSim offers a critical tool for advancing human-to-robot object handovers by providing a robust, reproducible, and realistic simulation environment that can facilitate the development and standardized evaluation of future robotic systems capable of seamless and safe physical interaction with humans.
