# WholeBodyWAM: Learning Whole-Body World Action Models with Scalable Motion Priors

> [!summary] Summary
> WholeBodyWAM is a humanoid Video-Motion-Action world-action model that pretrains a language-conditioned Motion Expert via flow matching on UniMotion-4K, a 4.1K-hour corpus of human and humanoid motion canonicalized into a root-free 63D space, then fuses it with Video and Action Experts through asymmetric Mixture-of-Transformers attention. On six real TianGong 3.0 whole-body tasks it scores 72.2%, beating GR00T N1.7 by 11.4 points.

## Problem

- Humanoid foundation policies mostly follow the VLA paradigm, mapping current observations to commands without explicitly modeling future dynamics, so they lack predictive foresight for long, coordinated whole-body behavior.
- Existing world action models emphasize future visual dynamics, ignoring the temporal evolution of the robot's own articulated body, which is itself control-relevant world state for high-DoF humanoids.
- Target-robot humanoid demonstrations are expensive, while abundant human and humanoid motion lacks executable actions; prior motion models treat generation or tracking as the end task, not predictive control supervision.

## Method

- **UniMotion-4K**: 1.19M sequences (4.1K hours) from 11 sources (HowTo100M via ==GVHMR==, EgoDex, AMASS, BONES-SEED, humanoid data) canonicalized into ==root-free 63D== local rotations of 21 joints, with per-humanoid MLP adapters trained on ==GMR==-retargeted pairs.
- **Stage I Motion Pretraining**: a 30-block language-conditioned ==Motion Expert== uses continuous ==flow matching== to predict 32 future motion frames from 16 history frames, without any target-robot action supervision.
- **Stage II Video-Motion-Action post-training**: Wan2.2-5B ==Video Expert==, pretrained Motion Expert, and ==DiT== Action Expert interact via asymmetric ==Mixture-of-Transformers== attention that hides future video tokens from motion and action streams; video is dropped at inference.

## Results

- Averages **72.2%** normalized task score over six real TianGong 3.0 tasks, beating GR00T N1.7 (**60.8%**), π0.5 (**58.1%**), FastWAM (**41.5%**), and τ0-WM (**39.4%**), best or tied on every task.
- Removing motion pretraining drops the score to **59.1%**; blocking Motion-to-Action attention drops it to **46.3%**; plugging the pretrained Motion Expert into τ0-WM lifts it from **39.4%** to **62.7%**.
- Scaling pretraining from 0 to **4K+** hours cuts motion error **27.5%** (**1.127°** to **0.817°** MPJRE) and raises task score **57.1%** to **67.6%**; with 50% demos it beats full-data FastWAM (**46.9%** vs **42.9%**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - For high-DoF robots, the robot's own future body configuration is a control-relevant world state worth predicting alongside pixels, not just proprioceptive input.
> - Action-free, embodiment-agnostic motion corpora from human video can scale a predictive prior; a root-free articulated representation plus small per-robot adapters bridges human and humanoid bodies.
> - Prediction supervision alone is not enough: a predicted modality helps only when action queries can attend to it, so auxiliary objectives need an explicit information path to action.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

WHOLEBODYWAM addresses the intersection of humanoid robotics, vision-language-action models, world models, and large-scale human-motion modeling. Recent humanoid-control systems increasingly use hierarchical architectures in which a high-level foundation model interprets language and visual observations, while a lower-level reinforcement-learning or motion-tracking controller executes locomotion, balance, and whole-body behavior. Many of these systems follow the vision-language-action, or VLA, paradigm and primarily map current observations to robot commands.

World Action Models (WAMs) extend this approach by explicitly predicting future states or future visual representations in addition to actions. The motivation is that predictive information can help a robot reason about temporally extended interactions, object movement, posture transitions, and task consequences. Existing WAM research has largely focused on future visual dynamics or general manipulation, while humanoid whole-body control introduces an additional requirement: the robot’s own articulated body must evolve coherently over time. Torso orientation, arm configuration, lower-body posture, locomotion, and balance are coupled, particularly during bending, kneeling, carrying, and object transfer.

The paper’s central contribution is to treat future whole-body motion as a predictive modality alongside future scene dynamics. Rather than attempting to use human motion directly as robot actions, the authors use large-scale human and humanoid motion to pretrain a transferable motion prior. This prior is later adapted to a particular humanoid through target-robot demonstrations. In this respect, the work differs from conventional motion-generation or motion-retargeting studies: motion data are used primarily as predictive supervision for a world-action model, not as the final control output.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The paper has three closely related objectives.

First, it seeks to determine whether heterogeneous human and humanoid motion can provide useful supervision for humanoid world-action modeling without requiring executable action labels for the target robot. Collecting whole-body demonstrations on a full-sized humanoid is expensive, difficult to teleoperate, and limited in behavioral diversity. Human videos, motion-capture datasets, egocentric recordings, and data from other humanoid platforms offer substantially more motion examples, but their body models, joint structures, coordinate systems, and action interfaces differ.

Second, the authors aim to construct a shared motion representation that permits learning across embodiments. They propose UniMotion-4K, a corpus containing approximately 1.19 million sequences, 444 million motion frames, and more than 4,100 hours of data from 11 sources. The dataset includes Internet videos, egocentric human data, native 3D human-motion datasets, and humanoid datasets. The representation must retain information about articulated posture and coordination while avoiding dependence on any one robot’s joint layout.

Third, the work evaluates whether a pretrained whole-body motion prior improves downstream robot performance, generalization, and data efficiency. The authors hypothesize that a model that predicts future body configurations can provide useful intermediate information to action generation, especially for tasks involving posture changes, locomotion, and coordinated manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. UniMotion-4K and Motion Unification

The authors canonicalize all motion sources into a root-free 63-dimensional representation. Each frame contains local rotations for 21 selected joints, represented as three-dimensional axis-angle vectors. Global root translation, root orientation, and body shape are excluded. This enables common modeling across humans and robots, although embodiment-specific global movement remains in the robot’s native action space.

For exocentric Internet videos, the authors detect and track people, segment temporally coherent clips, and recover 3D motion using GVHMR. For egocentric sources such as EgoDex, available body-motion annotations are converted directly; where explicit motion is unavailable, the authors recover motion from visual and motion cues. Native 3D datasets, including AMASS, Motion-X, HumanML3D, BONES-SEED, and BEDLAM, are mapped to the common 21-joint convention.

Humanoid data are handled through embodiment-specific adapters. The authors use GMR to retarget parametric human motion to each humanoid, producing paired native robot states and unified 63D motion. A three-layer multilayer perceptron, with two 128-dimensional hidden layers and ReLU activations, learns a mapping from each robot’s native joint-state space to the shared representation. A frozen adapter is also trained for the target TianGong 3.0 robot.

The initial data pool exceeds 30,000 hours, but the authors apply filtering for body truncation, occlusion, failed tracking, insufficient motion, invalid numerical values, implausibly large rotations, discontinuities, and unstable root movement. The final dataset contains 1,196,017 retained sequences. The largest sources are HowTo100M with 501,746 sequences, EgoDex with 314,839, HIW-500 with 176,768, and BONES-SEED with 135,246.

Language annotations are retained from structured datasets when available. For video-derived sequences, Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct generates motion-focused descriptions emphasizing posture, limb coordination, locomotion, temporal transitions, and object interaction.

#### 3.2. Stage I: Motion Pretraining

Stage I trains a language-conditioned Motion Expert to predict future whole-body motion. The model contains 30 transformer blocks with 512-dimensional tokens and a fourfold feed-forward expansion. A 48-frame training sample consists of 16 historical motion frames and 32 future frames. The future sequence is corrupted with Gaussian noise, and the model learns a continuous velocity field using flow matching. At inference, future motion is generated by integrating this learned field from noise toward a predicted trajectory.

The language instruction is encoded using a frozen Wan2.2 text encoder. Stage I does not use target-robot actions or target-robot demonstrations. Its purpose is to learn general task-conditioned dynamics, including how body configurations change during activities such as bending, lifting, walking, kneeling, and placing objects.

#### 3.3. Stage II: Video-Motion-Action Post-Training

Stage II adapts the motion prior to TianGong 3.0 and integrates it with two other components: a Video Expert and an Action Expert. The Video Expert is initialized from Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, while the Motion Expert is initialized from Stage I. The Action Expert is a 30-layer diffusion-transformer model that predicts 32-step action chunks in a 34-dimensional robot action space.

The three experts interact through layer-wise Mixture-of-Transformers attention. Each modality retains separate parameters, but its query, key, and value representations can exchange information with permitted modalities. The attention structure is asymmetric. Motion and action queries can attend to the current visual observation and to each other, but they cannot attend to future visual tokens. Future visual latents are therefore predicted during training without providing privileged future information to the motion or action streams.

The joint objective combines three flow-matching losses: future visual-latent prediction, future motion prediction, and action-chunk prediction. All three losses receive equal weight. The Motion Expert remains trainable during Stage II, allowing its general prior to adapt to the target embodiment and task distribution.

#### 3.4. Closed-Loop Robot Control

During deployment, the target robot supplies an egocentric RGB image and proprioceptive state. The frozen embodiment adapter maps the recent robot state history into the shared 63D motion space. The latest 16 motion states are used as conditioning. The model predicts a 32-frame future motion trajectory and a 32-step executable action chunk using four Euler integration steps.

Upper-body, head, and waist commands are sent directly to the robot. Locomotion and lower-body commands are processed by robot-specific reinforcement-learning controllers responsible for walking, balance, kneeling, and posture transitions. The action chunk is executed at 30 Hz, after which new observations are obtained and the policy replans. Predicted future motion is not fed back into the history and is not directly retargeted into robot commands. Future video prediction is omitted during deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The real-world evaluation uses six TianGong 3.0 tasks: Toy Pickup, Laundry Loading, Pillow Transfer, Kneeling Toy Storage, Toy Transfer, and Box Transfer. Each task has 100 training demonstrations and 20 evaluation rollouts per method. Performance is measured using normalized milestone-based task scores rather than binary success rates.

WHOLEBODYWAM obtains an average score of **72.2%**. Its task scores are 61.3% for Toy Pickup, 85.0% for Laundry Loading, 56.7% for Pillow Transfer, 90.0% for Kneeling Toy Storage, 66.7% for Toy Transfer, and 73.3% for Box Transfer. The strongest listed baseline is GR00T N1.7 at 60.8% average, while π0.5 reaches 58.1%, FastWAM 41.5%, and τ0-WM 39.4%. The authors report that WHOLEBODYWAM matches or exceeds the best baseline on each individual task.

Ablations indicate that both pretraining and Motion-to-Action information flow are important. Removing motion pretraining reduces the average score from 72.2% to 59.1%. Retaining the pretrained Motion Expert but preventing action queries from attending to motion features reduces performance further to 46.3%. In a separate transfer experiment, adding the pretrained Motion Expert to τ0-WM increases its score from 39.4% to 62.7%, suggesting that the motion prior is not restricted to the proposed architecture.

The scaling experiments show improvements with more motion-pretraining data. Across the evaluated tasks, increasing pretraining from zero to more than 4,000 hours reduces average future-motion MPJRE from 1.127 degrees to 0.817 degrees, a 27.5% reduction. The corresponding average robot task score rises from 57.1% to 67.6% in the scaling-study configuration. On a held-out UniMotion-4K split, the 4,000-hour model reduces 16-frame-horizon MPJRE from 1.855 degrees for a static baseline to 1.324 degrees, and 32-frame-horizon error from 3.220 to 2.573 degrees.

The model also shows improved robustness to controlled distribution shifts. With a 15-centimeter basket displacement in Toy Pickup, WHOLEBODYWAM scores 55.0%, compared with 46.3% for GR00T N1.7. With unseen toy colors and shapes in Kneeling Toy Storage, it scores 75.0%, compared with 63.3% for GR00T N1.7.

Motion pretraining improves data efficiency. Relative to models without pretraining, it raises average scores by 4.2, 11.2, and 10.5 percentage points when using 20%, 50%, and 100% of the target-robot demonstrations, respectively. With half of the demonstrations, the model pretrained on more than 4,000 hours reaches 46.9%, exceeding FastWAM trained with the full demonstration set at 42.9%.

Inference requires 363 milliseconds end to end on an NVIDIA A100 GPU. This is slower than τ0-WM at 313 milliseconds but faster than the reported DreamZero and LingBot-VA configurations and 1.45 times faster than FastWAM. The authors attribute this efficiency partly to omitting future-video generation at deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper provides evidence that large-scale motion data can serve as transferable predictive supervision for humanoid control, even when the data do not contain target-robot actions. Its main conceptual contribution is to model the robot’s future articulated configuration as part of the predictive world state. This is relevant to tasks where success depends on coordinated posture, balance, locomotion, and manipulation rather than isolated end-effector movement.

The UniMotion-4K construction also addresses a practical bottleneck in humanoid learning: the limited availability of high-quality, full-body robot demonstrations. A shared, root-free representation and embodiment adapters could support pretraining across multiple platforms, potentially reducing the amount of robot-specific data required for new systems.

Several constraints remain. The shared representation excludes global root translation and orientation, so locomotion and whole-body spatial dynamics are not fully learned in the motion prior. Human motion recovered from Internet video may contain estimation errors, and the dataset includes source overlap and heterogeneous quality despite filtering. The robot experiments focus on one humanoid platform, six tasks, and a common low-level controller, so the extent of transfer to other embodiments and environments is not established. Nevertheless, the reported ablations, scaling trends, and data-efficiency results support the paper’s central claim that scalable whole-body motion pretraining can improve predictive modeling and downstream humanoid control.
