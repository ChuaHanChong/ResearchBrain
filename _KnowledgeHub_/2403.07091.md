# Sim-to-Real gap in RL: Use Case with TIAGo and Isaac Sim/Gym

> [!summary] Summary
> A short use-case study comparing Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim for sim-to-real RL on the TIAGo mobile manipulator. The authors enable self-collision checking, repair the simplified collision mesh, match each simulator's joint PD controller to the real robot's ros_control JointGroupPosition PID, and benchmark step responses. An RL home-to-zero policy trained in simulation transfers to the real arm with visibly similar motion.

## Problem

- Collecting reinforcement-learning data on real robot fleets is costly, slow, and needs constant human supervision to catch unexpected or unsafe scenarios during runs.
- GPU simulators speed data generation but only approximate reality, so policies trained inside them need adaptation before they run on physical manipulators.
- Simulator defaults simplify meshes into convex shapes and disable self-collision checks, so out-of-the-box robot models do not match real hardware behaviour.
- Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim share a physics engine but expose different controller stacks, and their comparative fidelity on a real mobile manipulator was untested.

## Method

- Model fixes: ==self-collision checking== is switched on in the simulator settings and the TIAGo mesh is edited to remove spurious self-collisions introduced by convex mesh simplification.
- Control-pipeline matching: both Isaac simulators drive joints through a ==PD tracker== whose gains come from per-joint stiffness and damping, aligned against the real TIAGo's ==ros_control JointGroupPosition PID==.
- Fidelity benchmark: step inputs are injected per joint and simultaneously across all seven arm DOFs, with accumulated position error and joint velocity traces used as the sim-versus-real gap metric.
- Policy training: an ==RL== agent is trained for 100K epochs in each simulator with a reward of negative absolute joint position, driving the arm from Home to the fully extended zero pose.

## Results

- Isaac Gym tracked the real arm more closely under simultaneous multi-joint steps, with total accumulated error of **114.3006 rad** versus **152.0785 rad** for Isaac Sim.
- Per-DOF errors favoured Isaac Gym on the distal joints, notably DOF 5 (**43.6909** vs **11.9117** reversed on proximal joints) and DOF 7 (**12.7499** vs **57.0088 rad**).
- Velocity traces showed Isaac Sim violating joint-level velocity constraints, the likely cause of its larger deviation; Isaac Gym instead responded slower, with DOF 7 error converging later than DOF 1.
- The home-to-zero policy transferred to hardware and produced similar motion, yet the two simulators yielded different joint trajectories despite identical reward and **100K** training epochs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simulator defaults are a hidden sim-to-real gap: convex mesh simplification and disabled self-collision must be corrected before any policy fidelity claim is meaningful.
> - Single-joint step responses can look identical while multi-joint coordination diverges, so fidelity should be validated with simultaneous excitation, not isolated actuator tests.
> - Sharing a physics engine does not imply matched behaviour: identical rewards and training budgets across Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim produced different learned trajectories.
> - Whether joint-level velocity and effort constraints are actually enforced is a more useful simulator selection criterion than nominal engine or vendor equivalence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses one of the most significant challenges in modern robotics and artificial intelligence: the **sim-to-real gap** (or Sim2Real). Reinforcement Learning (RL) has shown immense promise in enabling robots to learn complex manipulation and navigation tasks autonomously. However, training RL agents directly on physical robots is often prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and potentially damaging to the hardware. This has led to a paradigm where policies are primarily learned in simulated environments.

The broader research landscape recognizes that while simulators offer a safe, fast, and scalable platform for data generation and policy training, they are inherently imperfect representations of reality. Differences in physics engines, sensor noise, control latencies, and environmental dynamics mean that a policy trained in simulation often performs poorly, or even fails, when deployed on a real robot. Bridging this gap is crucial for the widespread adoption of RL in real-world robotic applications.

Historically, RL data collection has evolved from single-robot, real-world training, which is slow and costly, to multi-robot setups for parallel data collection (as referenced by Levine et al. and Kalashnikov et al.), which is still expensive. The advent of sophisticated software libraries like OpenAI's Gymnasium and Stable-baselines, coupled with physics simulators like Mujoco, made simulation-based training more accessible. The most recent advancement, and the direct focus of this paper, is the emergence of high-performance, GPU-accelerated simulators such as NVIDIA's Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim. These simulators promise unprecedented speed and scalability for training, enabling the generation of vast amounts of synthetic data.

This paper positions itself at the forefront of this evolution by critically evaluating the fidelity of these state-of-the-art GPU-accelerated simulators for sim-to-real transfer with a complex mobile manipulator. By comparing Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim against a real TIAGo robot, the authors contribute to the empirical understanding of the "sim-to-real gap" inherent in these popular platforms. Their work provides practical insights into the challenges and potential of using these advanced simulators for developing robust robot control policies, directly informing researchers and practitioners striving for successful sim-to-real deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **explore and evaluate policy-learning approaches for sim-to-real transfer in the context of robotic manipulation using a TIAGo mobile manipulator robot**. Specifically, the authors aim to:

1. **Assess the fidelity and suitability of two state-of-the-art GPU-accelerated simulators, Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim, both developed by Nvidia, for robotic RL tasks.** This involves a direct comparison of their behavior against the real TIAGo robot.
2. **Identify and analyze discrepancies in control architectures and physical responses between the simulators and the real robot.** This includes examining how joint controls are implemented and how the robot reacts to inputs in each environment.
3. **Demonstrate successful sim-to-real transfer of an RL-trained policy for a specific manipulation task (moving the arm from a "Home" to a "fully extended" position), despite the identified differences.** The goal is to show that a policy learned in simulation can still produce functionally similar movements on the physical robot.
4. **Focus on achieving collision-less movement** in both simulated and real environments, highlighting a crucial aspect for practical robotic safety and deployment.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from the inherent difficulties and costs associated with training RL models on physical robots. The authors highlight that real-world training is economically expensive, time-consuming due to slow robot movements and environment preparation, and requires constant supervision to prevent unexpected scenarios or damage. Simulators offer a promising alternative by making the process faster and cheaper, especially with the advent of GPU-supported platforms like Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim. However, the fundamental challenge remains: simulators are simplifications of reality. Therefore, the core motivation is to understand and mitigate the "sim-to-real gap" to leverage the benefits of simulation for real-world robotic applications effectively. By directly comparing and identifying the sources of this gap in modern simulators, the research aims to pave the way for more reliable and efficient RL-driven robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts an empirical comparative methodology, focusing on a specific robotic platform and two prominent simulators.

**Robotic Platform:** The **TIAGo mobile manipulator robot** from PAL Robotics is used as the target hardware. This choice is significant as TIAGo is a complex, multi-jointed robot commonly used in research and commercial applications, providing a realistic use case for evaluating sim-to-real transfer challenges.

**Simulators Under Evaluation:**
The study focuses on two NVIDIA simulators:
- **Isaac Gym:** Known for its high-performance GPU-based physics simulation, enabling parallel training of thousands of environments.
- **Isaac Sim:** Built on the NVIDIA Omniverse platform, offering a more feature-rich environment with advanced rendering and robotics development tools.

**Methodological Steps:**

1. **Robot Model Preparation:** The authors first addressed challenges in simulating the TIAGo robot. This involved:
 - Modifying simulator settings to enable **self-collision checking**, which is often disabled by default for computational efficiency.
 - Adjusting the robot's mesh models to prevent spurious self-collisions caused by simulator-specific mesh simplifications.

2. **Control Architecture Analysis and Comparison:** A detailed comparison of the control pipelines across the real TIAGo and the two simulators was conducted:
 - **Isaac Gym & Isaac Sim:** Both rely on a similar PD (Proportional-Derivative) controller structure, where `k_p` and `k_d` values are derived from joint stiffness and damping parameters. They support position, velocity, and effort controls. Isaac Sim offers a wider variety of off-the-shelf controllers, including joint and Cartesian space control.
 - **Real TIAGo:** Utilizes the `ros_control` framework within ROS (Robot Operating System). For this study, the `JointGroupPosition` controller was employed, which is a PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative) controller, chosen for its similarity to Isaac's PD control.

3. **Simulator Response Evaluation (Step Input Experiment):** To quantify the differences in physical response, the authors performed a controlled experiment:
 - A **simultaneous step input** was applied to each joint of the TIAGo arm in the real robot, Isaac Gym, and Isaac Sim.
 - The **responses of each Degree of Freedom (DOF)** were recorded and compared, focusing on positional trajectories and velocities.
 - **Accumulated errors** (deviation from the real robot's steady-state position) were calculated for each DOF to provide a quantitative measure of simulator fidelity.

4. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Policy Training and Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
 - An RL model was trained in both Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim for a specific task: moving the TIAGo arm from a "Home" position to a "fully extended" (zero joint position) position.
 - The same **reward function (reward = -|DOF_value|)** and training parameters (100,000 epochs) were used for consistency.
 - The resulting learned policies were then deployed on the real TIAGo robot.
 - The **movements generated by the RL-trained policies** in simulation and on the real robot were qualitatively and quantitatively compared.

This methodical approach allowed for a systematic comparison, moving from low-level control analysis to high-level policy transfer, providing comprehensive insights into the sim-to-real gap for this specific robot and these particular simulators.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, highlighting both the capabilities and the discrepancies of Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim when applied to sim-to-real transfer with the TIAGo robot.

#### 4.1. Simulator Responses to Step Inputs

The comparison of responses to simultaneous step inputs across the TIAGo's arm joints revealed clear differences:

- **Isaac Gym vs. Isaac Sim:** When multiple joints received step inputs concurrently, the individual joint responses in both simulators were similar, but the *overall arm behavior* differed.
- **Fidelity to Real TIAGo:**
 - **Isaac Gym** generally produced responses that *resembled the real TIAGo more closely* than Isaac Sim (Figure 3). This suggests a better overall physical fidelity in terms of how the multi-joint system responds.
 - However, Isaac Gym exhibited a *slower response*, possibly due to how it prioritizes or processes joint movements in a hierarchical tree. This was hypothesized based on the slower convergence of errors in DOF 7 compared to DOF 1.
 - **Accumulated Errors (Table 1):** Isaac Gym consistently showed *smaller accumulated errors* across most DOFs compared to Isaac Sim (e.g., total accumulated error of 114.3006 rad for Isaac Gym vs. 152.0785 rad for Isaac Sim). This quantitative measure further supports Isaac Gym's superior fidelity in this specific test.
 - **Isaac Sim Issues:** Isaac Sim's larger deviations were attributed to its apparent difficulty in *respecting velocity constraints at a joint level* (Figure 4). This could lead to unrealistic joint speeds or accelerations in certain scenarios, impacting its overall realism.

#### 4.2. Trained Model Performance and Sim-to-Real Transfer

The RL policies trained in each simulator for the "Home-to-zero" arm movement task showed:

- **Successful Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The paper states that the presented results demonstrate "successful sim-to-real transfer," showcasing "similar movements executed by an RL-trained model in both simulated and real setups." This indicates that, despite the simulator differences, a functional policy could be learned in simulation and deployed on the real robot.
- **Policy Differences Despite Same Training:** Even though both models were trained with the identical reward function (reward = -|DOF_value|) and for the same number of epochs (100,000), the *movement patterns observed differed* between the policies trained in Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim (Figure 5). This visually demonstrates that the underlying simulator physics and control behaviors, as identified in the step input experiment, directly influence the learned policy's trajectory and execution style. The subtle discrepancies in simulator fidelity thus translate into distinct learned behaviors, even for a relatively simple task.

In summary, the key findings underscore that while both Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim enable sim-to-real transfer, their internal dynamics and control implementations lead to noticeable differences in robot behavior. Isaac Gym demonstrated higher fidelity to the real TIAGo in multi-joint dynamic responses and lower accumulated errors, albeit with a potentially slower overall system response. Isaac Sim, while powerful, showed specific issues with respecting joint velocity constraints, which likely contributed to its higher error and different learned policy trajectories.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant importance for the broader fields of robotics, reinforcement learning, and simulation-based development. Its potential impacts are multifaceted:

1. **Validation of State-of-the-Art Simulators:** The paper provides a direct empirical comparison of two leading GPU-accelerated simulators, Isaac Gym and Isaac Sim, against a real-world mobile manipulator. This is crucial for validating the claims of these platforms and understanding their practical limitations. For researchers and developers, this provides valuable data to inform their choice of simulation environment for specific robotic tasks.

2. **Highlighting the Enduring Sim-to-Real Gap:** Despite advancements in simulation technology, the study explicitly identifies and quantifies the "sim-to-real gap" even with sophisticated tools. The observed differences in control responses and learned policies, despite identical training parameters, serve as a reminder that simulators, while powerful, are still abstractions. This emphasizes the continued need for robust sim-to-real transfer techniques such as domain randomization, domain adaptation, or reality gap mitigation strategies.

3. **Guidance for Simulator Development:** By pinpointing specific issues, such as Isaac Sim's challenges with velocity constraints or Isaac Gym's potential hierarchical processing slowdowns, the research offers actionable feedback for the developers of these simulators (NVIDIA). Addressing these identified discrepancies could significantly improve simulator fidelity and, consequently, the effectiveness of sim-to-real transfer.

4. **Informing Robot Learning Strategies:** The finding that different simulators lead to distinct learned policies, even for the same task and reward function, has profound implications for robot learning. It suggests that the choice of simulator is not merely an implementation detail but can fundamentally shape the resulting robot behavior. This encourages researchers to be more critical of their simulation environments and to consider how simulator-specific dynamics might influence the generalizability and robustness of learned policies on real hardware.

5. **Practical Implications for Robotics Engineers:** For practitioners deploying RL-trained policies on robots like TIAGo, this work provides practical insights. It highlights the importance of thorough validation of simulator behavior against the real robot early in the development cycle. Understanding these nuances can help in fine-tuning simulation parameters, designing more robust reward functions, or even selecting a simulator that better matches the real robot's dynamic characteristics.

In conclusion, this paper successfully demonstrates the feasibility of sim-to-real transfer with modern GPU-accelerated simulators for a complex robotic platform. More importantly, it systematically exposes the subtle yet significant differences between these simulators and reality, offering critical insights into the ongoing challenges of the sim-to-real gap. The work serves as a valuable guide for future research, simulator development, and practical applications in reinforcement learning for robotics.
