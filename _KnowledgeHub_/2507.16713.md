# A Pragmatist Robot: Learning to Plan Tasks by Experiencing the Real World

> [!summary] Summary
> PRAGMABOT enables a robot to learn task planning through direct interaction with the real world, integrating VLM-driven self-reflection via short-term memory and cumulative knowledge from long-term memory. This approach achieved an 84% average task success rate and led to emergent intelligent behaviors like tool use in challenging manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Large language and vision-language models (LLM/VLM) struggle to translate abstract reasoning into physically feasible plans for real-world robots due to their lack of embodied experience.
- Existing methods for robotic task planning often rely heavily on human supervision or extensive pre-training, limiting autonomous learning and adaptation in dynamic environments.
- Robots frequently fail at complex manipulation tasks because VLMs lack the ability to effectively evaluate action outcomes and self-reflect on failures for online course correction.

## Method

- A closed-loop framework using a VLM for task planning and a success detector for real-time feedback on action outcomes.
- Short-term memory (STM) enables the VLM to self-reflect on failures within an episode, facilitating online adaptation and generating revised plans.
- Long-term memory (LTM) stores summarized successful experiences, which are retrieved via ==Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)== to inform planning for new tasks and generalize learned behaviors.

## Results

- Achieved an **84%** average task success rate across four manipulation tasks with self-reflection via STM, compared to **35%** for a baseline without this capability.
- Demonstrated emergent intelligent behaviors, including autonomously pushing aside obstructions, repositioning contained objects, and using a sponge as a tool to push small items.
- Improved single-trial success rates to **79.5%** on twelve diverse real-world scenarios by utilizing LTM and RAG, significantly outperforming methods without cumulative experience.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating VLM-driven self-reflection with short-term memory allows robots to adapt to failures online and exhibit emergent intelligent behaviors, such as creative tool use and obstruction handling.
> - Cumulative long-term memory, effectively leveraged through RAG, significantly improves a robot's first-attempt success rates and enables generalization of learned strategies to unseen, similar scenarios.
> - An on-demand image annotation module enhances VLM spatial reasoning for precise low-level skill execution, leading to more accurate grasping and pushing actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The broader research landscape for this work is situated at the intersection of large language models (LLMs), vision-language models (VLMs), and robotics, particularly in the domain of autonomous task planning.

Recently, LLMs have demonstrated capabilities in reasoning and plan generation across various domains, leading to their application in robotic task planning where they interpret natural language instructions and generate action sequences. However, a significant limitation arises from LLMs being trained predominantly on text data, which does not inherently align with the continuous, high-dimensional sensor streams and physical embodiment of real-world robotic systems. This discrepancy can lead to plans that are theoretically sound but practically infeasible for a robot with specific physical constraints and skill sets.

To address the limitations of text-only input, the field has progressed towards multimodal approaches, specifically VLMs. These models integrate visual and textual data, enabling robots to reason about visual inputs and operate in closed-loop settings. While VLMs offer improved multimodal reasoning and visual understanding, they are still primarily trained on general internet data and may not intrinsically account for a robot’s unique physical embodiment, manipulation capabilities, or environmental limitations. For instance, a VLM might suggest grasping an object that is partially occluded, a task challenging or impossible for a robot despite being intuitively plausible for a human.

Existing research has explored various strategies to bridge this gap:
- **Grounding LLMs in affordances:** Methods like SayCan integrate LLM outputs with trained visual affordance networks, which require substantial training resources.
- **Memory-based systems with human feedback:** Frameworks such as DROC and BUMBLE incorporate memory modules to improve decision-making, often relying on dense human supervision for feedback and error correction. BUMBLE, for example, combines short-term memory for replanning with long-term memory of human-annotated failures.
- **Autonomous failure recovery through self-reflection:** Systems like REFLECT and Reflexion enable LLM/VLM agents to analyze their own failures and adapt subsequent actions. Reflexion introduced "verbal reinforcement learning," where LLMs improve performance via in-context learning from self-reflective experiences without explicit parameter updates. However, these methods have largely been evaluated in simulated environments or rely on external, hand-engineered scene descriptors, often overlooking the complexities of real-world robotic interaction and visual self-evaluation.
- **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG):** Approaches like RAP propose using RAG with a memory of diverse experiences for simulated LLM agents, but they typically do not combine this with self-reflection from VLMs in embodied systems.
- **Enhanced skill execution:** Other works focus on improving precise action parameters using mask-based techniques or object-centric annotations, usually for specific tasks like grasping in tabletop settings.

PRAGMABOT distinguishes itself within this landscape by focusing on how physical robots can *visually* evaluate action outcomes, *self-reflect* on failures, and *construct long-term memory* to guide future planning—all through real-world experience and without relying on explicit human annotation or dense human feedback. It integrates verbal reinforcement learning principles with VLMs for embodied tasks, introduces RAG for selective retrieval of self-reflective experiences, and incorporates an on-demand image annotation module for enhanced spatial reasoning, thus addressing the alignment issue between internet-trained models and real-world robotic capabilities. Table I in the paper illustrates this differentiation by highlighting PRAGMABOT's unique combination of self-reflection, learning by experiencing, interactive replanning, creative tool use, and unified visual feedback.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed disconnect between the advanced reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) and the practical realities of real-world robotic systems. While these models excel at understanding natural language and processing visual information based on vast internet-scale data, they are not inherently designed to account for the physical embodiment, specific skill sets, and inherent limitations of a robot operating in a dynamic environment.

For instance, an internet-trained VLM might confidently propose an action that appears logical in a general context (e.g., grasping a partially obscured object), but which a robot cannot execute successfully due to its physical gripper design, limited perception under occlusion, or kinematic constraints. This leads to a critical question: How can a robot align the abstract reasoning of a VLM with its own unique physical capabilities to achieve more robust and successful task planning?

The key objectives of the PRAGMABOT framework are:

1. **Enable Learning from Real-World Experience:** To develop a system where robots can autonomously learn and improve their task planning abilities through direct interaction with the physical world, rather than relying solely on pre-trained models or extensive human supervision. This includes learning from both successes and failures.
2. **Integrate Self-Reflection and Online Adaptation:** To equip the robot with mechanisms for self-reflection, allowing it to analyze the outcomes of its actions (especially failures), identify the reasons for unsuccessful attempts, and adapt its subsequent behavior within an ongoing task. This aims to reduce repetitive errors and enable dynamic replanning.
3. **Facilitate Cumulative and Generalizable Learning:** To establish a system for storing and leveraging past experiences to inform future task planning. This involves summarizing lessons learned from completed tasks and making them accessible for new, similar scenarios, thereby fostering lifelong learning and improved first-attempt success rates.
4. **Ground VLM Planning with Embodied Knowledge:** To develop a method that effectively incorporates a robot's own capabilities and limitations into the VLM's planning process. This ensures that the generated action sequences are not only semantically correct but also physically feasible and optimized for the robot's specific hardware.
5. **Enhance Spatial Understanding for Precise Action Execution:** To improve the VLM's ability to reason about spatial details and object interactions, leading to more accurate and nuanced parameter selection for low-level manipulation skills (e.g., choosing the correct part of an object to grasp, or the precise location to push an item).

By addressing these objectives, PRAGMABOT aims to create a more "pragmatic" robot, one that learns to plan tasks in a manner grounded in its real-world experiences and physical capabilities, thereby moving beyond abstract, disembodied reasoning. This approach is motivated by the principles of verbal reinforcement learning, where in-context learning from self-reflective experiences can improve agent performance without the computational cost of model fine-tuning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PRAGMABOT operates as a closed-loop system, enabling a robot to learn task planning through iterative interaction with its environment. The framework is structured around a VLM acting as a central processing unit for perception, planning, and memory management, incorporating both short-term and long-term memory mechanisms. The core components are detailed below, as illustrated in Figure 2 and Algorithm 1 of the paper.

**A. Task Planner (P)**
The Task Planner is implemented using a VLM (specifically, gpt-4o). Its function is to interpret a natural language instruction (I) from the user and, conditioned on the current RGB observation (o_t), generate the next action (a_t). An action consists of a specific low-level skill (π_k) and its associated parameters (e.g., object to pick, location to place). The VLM leverages its visual-language understanding and common-sense reasoning to formulate these actions. Initially, this planner may lack intrinsic knowledge of the robot's physical capabilities, making real-world interaction crucial for its improvement.

**B. Success Detector (R)**
The Success Detector, also implemented with the same VLM, provides critical feedback on the outcome of an executed action. After an action (a_t) is performed, R compares the observation before (o_t) and after (o_{t+1}) the action to generate a feedback signal (r_{t+1}). This signal includes:
- A binary indicator of whether the action was successful.
- A binary indicator of whether the overall task is complete.
- A semantic description of any changes observed in the scene.
This detailed feedback is crucial for the robot's self-reflection and subsequent planning adjustments.

**C. Short-Term Memory (STM) and Online Adaptation (m)**
The STM is a dynamic log that records the robot's interactions within a single task episode. It stores a sequence of executed actions (a_τ) and their corresponding feedback signals (r_{τ+1}) from the Success Detector. When an action fails, the Task Planner (P) accesses the STM to perform self-reflection. This process involves:
- Analyzing the sequence of past actions and their outcomes.
- Identifying potential causes for failure.
- Formulating a revised plan or corrective action.
This self-reflection manifests as a "linguistic gradient" within the VLM's chain-of-thought process, guiding the generation of a new, more effective action without requiring parameter updates to the VLM itself. For example, if a robot fails to grasp an object due to an obstruction, the STM allows it to reflect, "Previous attempts to pick up the apple directly have failed. The apple is next to a cylindrical container, which might be causing interference. To create more space and ensure a successful grasp, I will push the can to the right, away from the apple." This mechanism facilitates online adaptation, enabling the robot to learn from immediate failures and exhibit emergent behaviors such as creative tool use. The STM is reset upon task completion.

**D. Long-Term Memory (LTM) and Experience Summarization (M)**
To enable cumulative learning across different tasks, PRAGMABOT employs a persistent LTM. Upon successful completion of a task, the STM episode is summarized by a VLM-based Experience Summarizer (S) and stored in the LTM. Each entry in the LTM is a key-value pair (K, E):
- **Key (K):** A scenario description that combines the user instruction (I) with a natural language description of the initial scene (generated by a VLM Scene Describer D from the initial observation o_0). This provides a rich, semantic index for future retrieval.
- **Value (E):** The summarized experience from the successful STM episode.
Keys (K) are embedded into dense vectors using a text embedding model (OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large) and cached for efficient lookup via cosine similarity. This process allows the system to transform transient episodic memories into reusable, generalized knowledge.

**E. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**
When confronted with a new task, the Task Planner leverages the LTM through a RAG mechanism. A retrieval key (K') is constructed from the current instruction (I) and the initial scene description (D(o_0)). This key's embedding is used to query the LTM for the top-k most similar past experiences. These retrieved experiences (K_i, E_i) are then incorporated into the VLM's planning prompt. This in-context learning mechanism allows the VLM to draw on relevant prior knowledge, making its planning decisions more informed and grounded in actual robot capabilities and limitations in new but related scenarios.

**F. Enhanced Skillset with Image Annotations**
To enable precise low-level manipulation, PRAGMABOT integrates an on-demand image annotation tool. This tool addresses the challenge of identifying specific, semantically meaningful locations for actions beyond simple geometric grasp planning.
- **Object Segmentation:** Given an instruction and an RGB frame, the VLM first selects a skill and determines if image annotation is required. Open-vocabulary segmentation is performed using Grounded SAM (which combines Grounding DINO and SAM) to generate an initial object mask.
- **Candidate Location Generation:** If the VLM requests further annotation, the tool overlays candidate location masks on the image. For placing, farthest-point sampling (FPS) is applied to the segmented mask to generate potential drop-off points. For pushing, candidate goal masks indicating end positions are drawn.
- **VLM Selection:** The VLM then evaluates these annotated options and selects the most appropriate location for the current action.
- **Grasping:** For grasping, the segmented point cloud is passed to AnyGrasp, which generates grasp hypotheses with confidence scores. If annotation is deemed beneficial by the VLM, FPS is used within the object mask to generate numbered location masks. The final grasp (g*) is selected by maximizing a product of the AnyGrasp confidence score (s_conf) and a location score (s_loc) based on the Euclidean distance to the VLM-chosen location, after filtering for kinematic feasibility using Pinocchio.

The overall algorithm ensures that the robot continuously learns and refines its planning capabilities by integrating real-world feedback into its decision-making process, combining online adaptation with cumulative experience.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of PRAGMABOT focused on four challenging robotic tasks, extended to twelve real-world scenarios, using a legged manipulator (ANYmal with a 6-DoF arm). The VLM used was gpt-4o, and the text embedding model was OpenAI's text-embedding-3-large.

**A. Evaluation of Short-Term Memory (STM) and Self-Reflection**
To assess the impact of STM and self-reflection, PRAGMABOT was compared against a baseline (CaP-V) that lacks STM and simply selects actions based on current vision and instruction.
- **Increased Task Success Rates:** PRAGMABOT, with STM-based self-reflection, achieved an average task success rate of 84% across four challenging manipulation tasks, compared to 35% for CaP-V (Table II). This demonstrates that the ability to reflect on failures and adapt actions online significantly improves task completion.
- **Emergent Intelligent Object Interactions:** The robot exhibited intelligent behaviors not explicitly programmed:
 - **Obstruction Handling:** When instructed to "put the apple on the plate" and faced with an obstructing container, the robot initially failed to grasp the apple. Through reflection, it autonomously decided to push the container aside before retrying the grasp, which then succeeded.
 - **Object Repositioning:** Tasked with "collect the bowl" that contained an apple, the robot first attempted to lift the bowl, causing the apple to fall. Upon reflection, it revised its plan to "first move the apple to the table before picking up the bowl," leading to successful task completion.
 - **Creative Tool Use:** For the "move tiny candy to banana" task, direct pushing with the gripper failed due to insufficient contact. The robot reflected on this failure and autonomously chose to use a sponge as a tool to push the candy more effectively. Similarly, after cracking an egg by grasping, the VLM learned to push fragile objects instead.
- **VLM Success Detector Accuracy:** The VLM success detector demonstrated a false negative rate of 6.67% and a false positive rate of 5% in object-picking tasks, indicating generally reliable feedback for self-reflection.

**B. Evaluation of Long-Term Memory (LTM) and Generalization**
After completing the initial tasks, successful STM episodes were summarized into an LTM containing 100 entries. This LTM was then used to evaluate PRAGMABOT's ability to leverage past experiences for new tasks, compared to COME (a VLM-based method without LTM access).
- **Improved Single-Trial Success Rates:** In the same four initial scenes, PRAGMABOT with LTM and RAG achieved a significantly higher average single-trial success rate of 90% compared to 19% for COME (Table III). This indicates effective recall and application of learned solutions.
- **Generalization to Unseen Scenarios:** The LTM demonstrated effective generalization to eight new, structurally similar scenarios (e.g., "put tennis ball in box (mug obstructs)," "move screw (towel nearby)"). Across these 12 tasks, PRAGMABOT achieved an average single-trial success rate of 79.5% with LTM and RAG, substantially higher than COME's 22%. Examples include immediately using a towel to push a screw and repositioning an apple before picking up a milk carton, directly applying knowledge learned from previous tasks.
- **Failure Analysis:** Out of 91 total trials, 19 initial failures were analyzed (Figure 6):
 - 8 failures were attributed to low-level action execution issues (e.g., poor grasp generation, inaccurate depth estimates).
 - 7 failures stemmed from VLM reasoning errors, primarily when the VLM overrode or downweighted retrieved LTM experiences due to perceived misalignment with current visual observations.
 - 4 failures occurred due to RAG failing to retrieve a relevant experience from the LTM.

**C. Ablation Study of Memory Retrieval**
An ablation study on the memory retrieval module evaluated the accuracy of the first planned action across 12 tasks under three settings: random retrieval, providing the entire LTM, and RAG-based retrieval (Figure 7).
- **RAG Superiority:** RAG-based retrieval achieved the highest first-action planning accuracy at 89% (with gpt-4o).
- **Ineffectiveness of Random Retrieval:** Randomly selecting 5 memories yielded the lowest accuracy (17%).
- **Degradation with Full LTM:** Providing the entire LTM improved accuracy to 74% but was less effective than RAG, suggesting that irrelevant or distracting information can degrade VLM performance.
- **Efficiency:** RAG significantly reduced prompt token count (by a factor of 7.5 compared to full LTM) and, consequently, monetary cost. While gpt-4o-mini showed similar trends, its performance gains were less pronounced, but with lower latency.

**D. Ablation Study of Image Annotation Module**
The impact of the on-demand image annotation module was evaluated for grasping and pushing tasks (Figure 8).
- **Improved Grasping Success:** For objects with complex shapes requiring specific grasp points (e.g., drumsticks, skewers), image annotation significantly improved grasping success rates. Without annotation, a geometric grasp planner like AnyGrasp often favored larger, but incorrect, surfaces.
- **Reduced Pushing Errors:** Image annotation consistently reduced the distance error to target locations in pushing tasks, indicating more precise control.
- **Latency:** The annotation process introduced an average latency of 5.15 seconds when using gpt-4o.

In summary, PRAGMABOT demonstrated that integrating self-reflection with short-term memory and leveraging retrieval-augmented long-term memory significantly enhances a robot's ability to plan and execute complex tasks in real-world environments, showing improved success rates, emergent behaviors, and generalization capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PRAGMABOT framework introduces a methodology for robotic task planning that has several areas of significance and potential impact:

1. **Autonomous Embodied Learning:** The work presents a step towards enabling robots to autonomously learn and improve task planning by interacting with the real world. This shifts the paradigm from models that are merely "used" by robots to systems that "learn" from their own embodied experiences, aligning abstract reasoning with physical capabilities.
2. **Efficiency in Learning and Deployment:** PRAGMABOT leverages verbal reinforcement learning and in-context learning, which means the VLM's parameters do not need to be updated. This eliminates the need for extensive, costly training data collection or fine-tuning, making the framework resource-efficient and potentially easier to deploy in diverse real-world robotic systems.
3. **Enhanced Task Robustness and Failure Recovery:** The integration of short-term memory (STM) and self-reflection allows robots to adapt to unforeseen circumstances and recover from action failures online. This capability for interactive replanning and problem-solving within an ongoing task contributes to more robust and reliable robotic operation, moving beyond brittle pre-programmed sequences.
4. **Cumulative Knowledge and Generalization:** The long-term memory (LTM) coupled with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) enables the robot to accumulate experience over time and apply lessons learned from past successful tasks to new, even previously unseen, but structurally similar scenarios. This promotes generalization and improves first-attempt success rates, reducing the need for repeated trial-and-error.
5. **Emergent Intelligent Behaviors:** The framework facilitates the emergence of intelligent behaviors, such as creatively using unmentioned objects as tools or repositioning obstructions, without explicit programming. This suggests a pathway towards more flexible and adaptive robotic intelligence in unstructured environments.
6. **Improved Action Grounding:** The on-demand image annotation module significantly enhances the VLM's ability to reason about spatial details and object parts, leading to more precise and semantically appropriate execution of low-level skills like grasping and pushing. This helps overcome limitations of purely geometric planners by incorporating higher-level visual-semantic understanding.
7. **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the need for multimodal input (tactile, auditory), strategies for scaling and pruning large memory databases, more sophisticated retrieval mechanisms (e.g., MMR), and memory sharing among heterogeneous robots, highlight clear directions for future research. This positions PRAGMABOT as a foundational work for advancing adaptive, experience-driven robotic intelligence.

In essence, PRAGMABOT contributes to the development of more capable and autonomous robots that can learn from their own real-world interactions, making them more resilient, efficient, and adaptable to complex tasks in dynamic human environments.
