# GraspIT: A Dataset Bridging the Sim-to-Real gap and back for Validated Grasping SE(3) Pose Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> GraspIT introduces a comprehensive dataset and generation system for robotic manipulation, providing 316,160 annotated RGB-D frames, physically-validated 6-DoF grasp poses, and a bidirectional sim-to-real link. The system rigorously validates over 2.2 million grasp candidates through a four-stage slip test, identifying that 11.46% are kinematically unfeasible and 38.84% fail under dynamic perturbations.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation systems often struggle with generalizing grasp tasks to novel objects and out-of-distribution scenes due to a lack of physically grounded visual representations.
- Existing grasp datasets frequently rely on analytical grasp quality metrics (e.g., force-closure) which show limited correlation with real-robot success under dynamic conditions.
- Current datasets typically lack comprehensive annotations, such as validated grasp quality, robot reachability filtering, and a robust, bidirectional sim-to-real bridge.

## Method

- A generation system, **GraspIT**, was developed, featuring a closed ==Real↔Sim loop== to produce fully annotated RGB-D corpora with an emphasis on quality and physical validation.
- A '==Robot School==' implements a ==four-stage physical slip test== to validate grasp candidates dynamically, assigning a continuous quality score that penalizes positional and orientational slip.
- ==Robot-in-the-loop reachability filtering== is integrated, using a Franka Panda arm via ==cuRobo==, to ensure all labeled successful grasps are kinematically feasible and collision-free.

## Results

- The initial GraspIT release provides **316,160** annotated RGB-D frame sets, including **264,960** from simulated scenes and **51,200** from real-world captures paired with synthetic views.
- Over **2.2 million** grasp candidates were validated using the Robot School, with an overall mean quality score of **0.72 ± 0.30**, where **82.94%** were classified as 'good' grasps.
- The rigorous validation process revealed that **11.46%** of initial grasp candidates failed due to robot-reachability issues, and **38.84%** of candidates failed under the dynamic pendulum swing test.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Traditional analytical grasp quality metrics are insufficient for predicting real-world grasp success, as they often fail to capture dynamic failure modes like object oscillation or pendulum swings.
> - Integrating explicit robot kinematic planning and a multi-stage physical slip test provides a more robust and actionable evaluation of grasp quality than purely geometric or force-closure methods.
> - A principled bidirectional Real↔Sim registration link is essential for leveraging the scalability of simulation for grasp annotation while maintaining relevance and transferability to real-world sensor data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in GraspIT addresses challenges in robotic manipulation, particularly the robust grasping of novel objects and the generalization capabilities of robotic systems. Decades of research have not fully solved the problem of robotic manipulation of everyday objects such as picking, placing, and stacking. Recent advancements in large-scale imitation learning systems, including RT-2, π0, and Octo, have demonstrated multi-task instruction-following in controlled laboratory environments. However, these systems exhibit limitations in generalization when confronted with objects, scenes, or lighting conditions that deviate from their training distributions.

A contributing factor to this generalization issue is the absence of physically grounded visual representations. The encoders employed by these systems, often trained on semantic similarity objectives (e.g., CLIP, SigLIP), do not inherently encode metric geometry, 3D object understanding, or the physical principles necessary for predicting the success of a proposed grasp or manipulation action.

Collecting the extensive training data required to address this deficit is resource-intensive. Accurate 6-DoF grasp annotation typically necessitates either large-scale real-robot execution or the use of a physics simulator. Furthermore, acquiring real RGB-D observations requires dedicated physical setups, and establishing a reliable link between simulation-derived annotations and real sensor data requires a precise calibration pipeline. Existing datasets in the field generally do not provide these components simultaneously.

**Related Work Analysis:**

- **6-DoF Object Pose Datasets:** Datasets like those consolidated in the BOP benchmark (e.g., LineMOD, YCB-Video, T-Less, HomebrewedDB) provide calibrated RGB-D frames with CAD models and 6-DoF ground truth. While valuable for detection and shape reasoning, they lack manipulation-specific annotations such as grasping labels, robot reachability information, and explicit physical object properties. The MVIP dataset expands to industrial multi-view settings with object weight and language properties but focuses on recognition rather than manipulation.
- **Grasping Datasets:** Early datasets such as Cornell and Jacquard offer 2D planar grasp rectangles, which represent a simplification insufficient for complex 6-DoF grasp configurations needed in practical manipulation. GraspNet-1Billion, a significant 6-DoF benchmark, provides real RGB-D imagery and a billion analytically scored grasp candidates. However, its force-closure metric has been shown to correlate moderately with real-robot success under dynamic conditions, and it lacks robot-reachability filtering, meaning many high-scoring candidates might be kinematically infeasible. ACRONYM executes millions of grasp candidates in a physics simulator but lacks visual observations, uses non-textured meshes leading to a sim-to-real gap, and does not model robot kinematics.
- **Learning-Based Grasp Quality Estimation:** Approaches like Dex-Net 2.0 and 4.0 use deep learning to predict grasp success from depth images, building on synthetic data. While demonstrating sim-to-real transfer for planar grasps, they rely on force-closure as ground truth, inheriting its limitations. Dex-Net 4.0 reported a wide range of real-robot success (9–98%) depending on the quality score, which underscores the need for more robust grasp validation. Contact-GraspNet generates 6-DoF grasps from point clouds using GraspNet-1B. A common limitation across these methods is the absence of validated candidate reachability via robot trajectory planning and a Real↔Sim registration link, which GraspIT aims to provide.
- **Robot Demonstration Datasets:** Large-scale imitation learning datasets such as BridgeData V2, DROID, and Open X-Embodiment have driven the development of generalized manipulation policies. However, they typically lack explicit 3D geometry, CAD models, or fine-grained grasp quality scores, focusing on "what to do" rather than "why it works physically." ManiSkill2 provides simulation-based demonstrations with object metadata but emphasizes task-success trajectories over physical object properties or a Real↔Sim connection.
- **Geometry-Aware Visual Pretraining:** Recent research indicates that incorporating explicit geometric knowledge into visual encoders can enhance downstream robotic perception, addressing a measurable physical reasoning deficit in current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. GraspIT is positioned to support pretraining that targets this object-level physical grounding gap with directly manipulable, physics-validated labels for robot tabletop manipulation tasks.
- **Sim-to-Real Data Generation:** Techniques like domain randomization and photorealistic rendering are standard for pose estimation and detection training. Robot-assisted annotation pipelines exist for 6-DoF labels in real-world settings. GraspIT extends this by providing a Real↔Sim loop that explicitly validates simulation-generated grasp quality annotations for transfer to real sensor data through metric 3D registration.

GraspIT's contribution lies in integrating these missing elements into a single, comprehensive system designed to serve as a pretraining foundation for advanced object manipulation models, thereby enhancing the visual representations consumed by VLAs and diffusion policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of GraspIT is to provide a dataset and a generation system that addresses critical limitations in existing approaches to robust robotic grasping. The work is motivated by the observation that current robotic systems struggle with generalizing manipulation tasks to novel objects and out-of-distribution scenes, largely due to a lack of physically grounded visual representations and an insufficient bridge between simulated and real-world data.

The detailed motivations and specific objectives are as follows:

- **Motivation for Physically Validated Grasp Quality:** Current state-of-the-art imitation learning systems and grasp planning algorithms often rely on analytically derived grasp quality metrics, such as force-closure. These metrics, however, frequently demonstrate a limited correlation with actual robot success in dynamic real-world scenarios, particularly under perturbations. They tend to overlook critical failure modes such as slipping under gravity, object oscillation, or pendulum-like swings during manipulation. The objective is to move beyond these analytical approximations by directly validating grasp candidates through physical simulations that account for dynamic interactions.
- **Motivation for Robot-in-the-Loop Reachability:** Many existing grasp datasets generate grasp candidates without considering the kinematic constraints and workspace limits of a real robot arm. This results in a significant number of "valid" grasps that are not actionable by a deployed robotic system, necessitating additional filtering or planning in practical applications. The motivation is to integrate robot kinematic planning directly into the data generation process, ensuring that all labeled successful grasps are genuinely reachable and collision-free for a specified robot model.
- **Motivation for a Principled Real↔Sim Bridge:** The domain gap between synthetic data and real-world observations remains a challenge for robotic learning. Training models exclusively on synthetic data often leads to poor transfer performance to real-world robots, while real-world data collection is costly and difficult to annotate at scale. The motivation is to establish a robust and bidirectional link that enables the seamless transfer of simulation-validated annotations to real-world sensor data, and vice versa. This aims to leverage the scalability of simulation for annotation while grounding these labels in real-world geometry.
- **Motivation for Comprehensive and Scalable Annotations:** Current datasets often provide only a subset of necessary annotations (e.g., only RGB-D, only 6-DoF poses, or only limited physical properties). Comprehensive data, including photorealistic RGB-D, precise instance segmentation, 6-DoF object poses, detailed physical properties, and physically validated grasp scores, is essential for training advanced perception and manipulation models. Furthermore, the dataset needs to be scalable and extensible to continuously introduce novel objects and scene configurations to prevent overfitting and support long-term research. The motivation is to create a living, expandable system that produces arbitrarily large, fully annotated RGB-D corpora with minimal manual labeling.
- **Motivation for Open and Reproducible Infrastructure:** The complexity of setting up and reproducing advanced data generation pipelines can be a barrier to entry for many researchers. Providing open-source tools and a containerized environment (Docker) facilitates reproducibility, encourages community contributions, and enables the progressive integration of existing datasets and expansion to new robot models, environments, or sensor modalities.

In summary, GraspIT aims to provide a physically grounded and realistically validated dataset foundation for robotic manipulation, addressing the limitations of existing datasets in terms of grasp quality validation, robot reachability, sim-to-real transfer, and comprehensive annotation, all within an open and extensible framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GraspIT is developed as an expandable generation system rather than a static benchmark, employing a closed Real↔Sim loop to produce annotated RGB-D corpora. The methodology is guided by four design principles: Quality over Quantity, Robot-in-the-loop Reachability, Real↔Sim Bidirectional Link, and Open and Extendable Infrastructure.

#### 3.1. Design Philosophy

1. **Quality over Quantity:** GraspIT emphasizes the physical validation of each grasp candidate through a four-stage slip test, departing from analytic scoring.
 - **Continuous Quality Score (s ∈):** A base score `b` is assigned based on the last successfully completed stage:
 - `0.00`: Trajectory planning to closure failed.
 - `0.25`: Trajectory OK; lift failed.
 - `0.50`: Lift OK; horizontal oscillation failed.
 - `0.75`: Horizontal oscillation OK; pendulum swing failed.
 - `1.00`: All four stages passed.
 - **Slip Penalty:** The final score `s` is further penalized by subtracting `1/12` for each stage `i` where positional slip `|∆p_i| > 2 cm` or orientational slip `|∆θ_i| > 10°`. The score is clamped between 0 and 1. A grasp is classified as "good" if `s ≥ 0.50` and "bad" otherwise. This multi-level scoring system aims to capture nuances in grasp reliability beyond simple success/failure.
2. **Robot-in-the-loop Reachability:** Before any physical slip test, every grasp candidate is validated for collision-free trajectory planning using a Franka Panda arm via cuRobo. Candidates that require motion through obstacles or exceed workspace limits are discarded, ensuring that all labeled positive grasps are actionable by a robot.
3. **Real↔Sim Bidirectional Link:** Real-world scene geometry is captured and registered into NVIDIA Isaac Sim. Subsequently, simulation-validated annotations are back-projected onto all real camera frames. This approach allows a single real-world capture session to yield both real RGB-D images and an unlimited number of photorealistic synthetic views, all sharing a consistent annotation coordinate frame.
4. **Open and Extendable Infrastructure:** All generation tools are packaged as Docker containers and made open-source. This facilitates reproducibility, extension, and community contributions, allowing for the integration of existing datasets or expansion to new robot models, environments, or sensor modalities.

#### 3.2. Simulation Branch: Scene Generation

Tabletop scenes are procedurally generated within NVIDIA Isaac Sim.
- **Object Sampling:** 10 to 20 objects are randomly selected from the ABC dataset (approximately 10,000 curated CAD meshes covering various geometries) and custom real assets.
- **Object Scaling and Placement:** Objects are randomly scaled to a maximum side length ranging from 2% to 20% of the maximum table side length. They are then randomly oriented, spawned 20-40 cm above the table, and allowed to settle under gravity physics, creating physically plausible scene configurations.
- **Scene Variety:** Ten different table models with domain-randomized Physically Based Rendering (PBR) textures, HDR lighting, and ambient occlusion are used to ensure visual diversity across scenes.
- **Camera Views and Rendering:** For each scene, 256 camera poses are sampled along a hemispherical trajectory at distances of 1-2 meters from the table center. Frames are rendered at 1920x1080 resolution, providing photorealistic RGB images, metric depth with structured-light noise, per-instance semantic segmentation, ground-truth 6-DoF object poses, and complete CAD point clouds.

#### 3.3. Annotation Modalities

Each GraspIT frame includes a comprehensive set of annotations:
- **RGB-D:** Photorealistic color images and metric depth with simulated structured-light noise.
- **Instance Segmentation:** Per-object binary masks derived from the Isaac Sim scene graph.
- **6-DoF Object Pose:** Ground-truth SE(3) transform for every visible object.
- **Camera Calibration:** Extrinsic and intrinsic parameters for each view.
- **3D Point Cloud:** Full CAD surface geometry for objects.
- **Physical Properties:** Geometric centroid (as a proxy for center-of-mass under uniform density), spatial extent, visibility percentage, and convex-hull volume fraction.
- **Validated 6-DoF Grasps:** Slip-test quality scores for each candidate grasp, including translation and orientation (with the pose origin between fingertips).
- **Grasping and Slip-Test Trajectories:** Robot trajectories associated with each grasp attempt, categorized by the reached slip-test stage, enabling full demonstration capturing for policy learning.

#### 3.4. Real Branch: Capture, Scan, and Register

- **Object Scanning:** Physical objects are 3D scanned using TRELLIS, a generative image-to-3D reconstruction model. This process involves capturing high-resolution RGB images under controlled lighting from multiple viewpoints to reconstruct high-fidelity textured 3D assets with PBR material attributes. Absolute scale is recovered by fitting the reconstructed mesh to real-world scene geometry using multi-view ICP and precise camera poses from the robot's forward kinematics. The initial release incorporates 100 real objects scanned via this pipeline.
- **Real Scene Capture:** A UR-5 robotic arm, equipped with an Intel RealSense D405 camera (1280x720), executes a predefined hemispherical scanning trajectory of 256 joint configurations. An eye-in-hand calibration determines the rigid transform between the end-effector and the camera, allowing camera poses in world coordinates to be derived from forward kinematics.
- **Annotation Transfer:** Ground-truth object poses are obtained through ICP-refined CAD fitting in the top-down reference frame and then propagated to all 256 real views using relative camera transforms. The registered scene is subsequently imported into Isaac Sim, where additional synthetic views are rendered using the real camera intrinsics. Per-instance segmentation masks are back-projected onto the real frames, and grasps are annotated using the Robot School's slip test.

#### 3.5. Robot School: Physically Validated Grasp Annotation

- **Candidate Generation and Filtering:** For each object, initial grasp candidates are generated via antipodal point sampling on a down-sampled surface point cloud (1000 points). These candidates undergo several filtering stages:
 - Geometric antipodal filtering (approximately 10% pass).
 - Collision checking and trajectory planning for a Franka Panda arm using cuRobo and Lula. Kinematically infeasible configurations (e.g., approaches through obstacles, beyond workspace limits) are discarded. This stage passes approximately 88.54% of the remaining candidates, reducing the pool to about 307 candidates per object. A Franka Hand gripper with 50mm finger length and 80mm opening width is used, with the grasp pose origin between the fingertips and orientation defining the approach.
- **Four-stage Slip Test:** Surviving candidates are evaluated by parallel Franka Panda instances in Isaac Sim, each executing the grasp through four sequential physical perturbations. A position slip exceeding 2 cm or an orientation slip exceeding 10 degrees at any stage results in failure and contributes to the continuous quality score defined in Eq. 2. The four stages are:
 1. **Trajectory + Close:** The robot attempts to reach the gripper to antipodal force-closure contact. Failure indicates a geometric collision, insufficient contact, or initial slip.
 2. **Lift:** The grasped object is vertically displaced by 20 cm under gravity. Failure suggests insufficient friction or gripping force. The simulation uses a default friction coefficient (μ_s = 1.0) and linearly scales object weight with volume (1000 kg/m³).
 3. **Horizontal Oscillation:** The grasped object undergoes linear perturbation in the horizontal plane to test rotational instability.
 4. **Pendulum Swing:** Joint 5 of the robot arm rotates to test resistance to moment arms created by off-center mass.
This staged protocol aims to identify failure modes beyond static force-closure analysis, providing granular insights into grasp robustness.
- **Demonstration Readiness:** The GraspIT Robot School can capture and stream robot states, camera observations, and action commands for both successful and failed demonstrations. This functionality supports policy learning techniques like behavior cloning, offering a foundation for exploring and fine-tuning manipulation policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The initial release of the GraspIT dataset provides a large-scale collection of annotated data designed for robotic manipulation tasks. The statistics derived from the generation process highlight the characteristics and validation rigor of the dataset.

**Dataset Scale and Content (GraspIT v1 Initial Release):**
- **Simulated Scenes:** The dataset comprises 1,035 unique simulated tabletop scenes generated in NVIDIA Isaac Sim. Each scene is rendered from 256 distinct hemispherical camera poses, totaling 264,960 annotated frame sets.
- **Real-world Scenes:** An additional 100 real-world scenes are included. For each real scene, 256 real RGB-D views are captured, and an equivalent 256 synthetic views are generated via the Real↔Sim link, resulting in 51,200 annotated frame sets (25,600 real, 25,600 synthetic).
- **Total Annotated Frames:** The complete initial release provides 316,160 annotated RGB-D frame sets.
- **Image Resolution:** Simulated frames are generated at 1920x1080 pixels, while real-world frames are at 1280x720 pixels.
- **Object Diversity:** The simulated scenes incorporate 7,396 unique object identities drawn from the ABC dataset's 10,000 object pool (approximately 73% coverage). The real scenes feature 100 distinct physical objects.
- **Scene Complexity:** Each scene contains an average of 13.9 ± 2.7 objects, with a range of 8 to 19 objects.

**Grasp Candidate Evaluation and Quality:**
- **Total Candidates:** A total of 2,275,352 grasp candidates were generated and subjected to the Robot School's validation process.
- **Good vs. Bad Grasps:**
 - 1,887,185 candidates (82.94%) were annotated as "good" (continuous quality score `s ≥ 0.50`). The mean score for good grasps was `s_good = 0.85`.
 - 388,167 candidates (17.06%) were annotated as "bad" (continuous quality score `s < 0.50`). The mean score for bad grasps was `s_bad = 0.13`.
- **Overall Grasp Score:** The mean quality score across all validated grasps was `s = 0.72 ± 0.30`.
- **Grasps per Object/Scene:** On average, there were 307 ± 649 grasps evaluated per object-scene combination, and 2198 ± 1866 grasps per scene. Good grasps averaged 1823 ± 1612 per scene and 255 ± 567 per object, while bad grasps averaged 375 ± 423 per scene and 52 ± 155 per object.

**Pipeline Ablation Statistics (Failure Analysis):**
The staged validation process provides insights into the failure modes of grasp candidates:
- **Trajectory Planning Failure (s = 0.00):** A significant portion, 260,663 candidates (11.46% of all initial candidates), failed at the initial trajectory planning stage. This indicates that robot-reachability filtering is a genuine bottleneck and effectively prunes kinematically infeasible grasps.
- **Lift Failure (s = 0.25):** 33,029 candidates (1.45%) failed during the vertical lift stage, indicating issues with insufficient friction or gripping force to counteract gravity.
- **Horizontal Oscillation Failure (s = 0.50):** 167,549 candidates (7.36%) failed under horizontal perturbations, demonstrating rotational instability. Of the candidates that passed trajectory planning, 88.48% survived this stage.
- **Pendulum Swing Failure (s = 0.75):** 883,733 candidates (38.84%) failed the pendulum swing test, indicating susceptibility to off-center mass moments. Of the candidates that passed trajectory planning, 94.26% survived this stage.
- **Full Success (s = 1.00):** 835,903 candidates (36.74%) passed all four stages without significant slip.
- **Penalized Large Slip (s < 0.5):** 94,475 candidates (4.15%) were identified as "bad" due to excessive slip, even if they nominally passed two or more stages. This category underscores the value of the continuous quality score and its slip penalty, as these grasps would otherwise be incorrectly labeled as good by a simpler stage-count metric.

**Computational Cost:**
The generation of the GraspIT dataset utilized Nvidia RTX6000 GPUs. A single GPU can generate a batch of 100 scenes in less than a week. The full generation of the initial GraspIT release took approximately 2-3 months on a single GPU, with the majority of this time dedicated to robot trajectory computation and grasp evaluation.

**Comparison with Other Datasets:**
The paper explicitly compares GraspIT's annotation strategy with other grasping datasets, highlighting its unique combination of physics-validated grasps, robot-reachability filtering, continuous quality scores, visual observations, and a bidirectional Sim↔Real link, which are not simultaneously present in previous works. This includes a demonstration that antipodal force-closure alone is insufficient, and the added path planning and staged slip-test filtering contribute to identifying genuinely high-quality grasps.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GraspIT dataset and its associated generation system represent a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, primarily by addressing several long-standing limitations in existing data resources.

**Bridging Critical Gaps:** GraspIT is presented as the first dataset to simultaneously offer photorealistic RGB-D observations at scale, robot-reachability-filtered grasps, staged and physically-validated quality scores that include explicit slip-penalty calibration, comprehensive 3D object shape information (including occluded geometry), and a bidirectional Real↔Sim registration link. This integrated approach directly addresses the identified deficits in previous datasets, which often lacked one or more of these crucial components.

**Advancing Grasp Quality Assessment:** The implementation of a four-stage physical slip test, coupled with a continuous quality score that penalizes excessive positional and orientational slip, provides a more rigorous and realistic evaluation of grasp quality. This method moves beyond traditional static force-closure metrics, which often fail to predict success under dynamic perturbations. The slip test explicitly captures failure modes such as object oscillation and pendulum swings, offering a more nuanced understanding of grasp robustness. The inclusion of "bad" grasp examples, particularly the 94,475 "penalized" near-misses, provides valuable "hard negatives" for training grasp-quality regressors. These candidates appear geometrically plausible but fail under realistic conditions, offering crucial data for developing models that can anticipate subtle failure modes and for applications such as out-of-distribution detection and data curation.

**Enhancing Pretraining for Robotic Models:** The physically grounded labels (e.g., precise object shape, geometric centroid, validated grasp quality) are designed to improve the visual representations learned by Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and diffusion policies. Existing analyses have indicated a "depth-estimation deficit" in current VLAs compared to geometric foundation models. GraspIT's data aims to reduce this deficit by providing explicit physical understanding at the encoder level during pretraining, thereby enabling better object affordance understanding and ultimately improving generalization for downstream task-specific fine-tuning.

**Scalability and Object Diversity:** By leveraging the large ABC CAD library (with 7,396 unique objects in the initial release) and employing a probabilistic scene generation approach, GraspIT ensures high object diversity. Each new generation run introduces novel object combinations, which can mitigate the risk of object-identity overfitting observed in datasets with smaller object pools (e.g., GraspNet, YCB-Video). This scalability supports the development of more generalizable manipulation models.

**Extensible and Open Infrastructure:** The open-source, Docker-containerized pipeline offers a robust and accessible framework for the research community. It allows for the reproduction of the dataset, extension with new scenes, objects, and annotations, and potential integration with existing datasets (e.g., BOP, GraspNet) to upgrade their analytic annotations to GraspIT's physically-validated labels. This open approach fosters collaborative development and continuous growth of the data foundation.

**Facilitating Policy Learning:** The ability to stream high-resolution demonstrations, including robot states, camera observations, and action commands, for both successful and failed grasp attempts, offers direct utility for policy learning methodologies. This capability supports behavior cloning and reinforcement learning, providing rich data for model training, identifying challenging scenarios, and developing more robust manipulation policies.

**Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The Real↔Sim registration pipeline provides a principled method for transferring high-quality, simulation-derived annotations to real sensor data. This directly addresses the domain gap, offering a mechanism for generating vast amounts of accurately labeled synthetic data that is explicitly validated for real-world relevance, thereby reducing the reliance on costly and time-consuming manual annotation in physical environments.

**Acknowledged Limitations:** The authors transparently acknowledge several limitations of the current release, including the absence of a comprehensive downstream evaluation, restriction to parallel-jaw grippers, reliance on a single robot model (Franka Panda) for reachability filtering, lack of full task-level demonstrations (though grasp trajectories are provided), reliance on generated physical properties (e.g., CoM from CAD), and potential inherent offsets in the Real↔Sim scanning and registration process. These limitations also provide clear directions for future research and community contributions.

Overall, GraspIT establishes a foundation for physically grounded robot learning, with the potential to significantly advance the capabilities of robotic systems in perceiving, understanding, and manipulating objects in diverse and unstructured environments.
