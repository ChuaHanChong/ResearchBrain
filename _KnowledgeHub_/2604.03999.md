# Dynamic Whole-Body Dancing with Humanoid Robots -- A Model-Based Control Approach

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed a model-based control framework that enables humanoid robots to perform dynamic, whole-body dance motions by applying future state prediction in both offline trajectory optimization and online model predictive control. This approach facilitates expressive movement generation and ensures robust stability against real-world disturbances, successfully enabling four full-size Kuavo 4Pro robots to complete a 4-minute live public dance performance.

## Problem

- Achieving dynamic, expressive, and stable whole-body dance motions in humanoid robots without conservative movements.
- Computational challenges of applying long-horizon trajectory optimization to complex dance sequences with frequent and aperiodic contact transitions.
- Maintaining robot stability and robustness against real-world disturbances like sensor noise and uneven surfaces during dynamic execution.

## Method

- A two-stage framework: offline motion generation and online motion execution, both leveraging future state prediction for proactive control.
- Offline motion generation involves human motion capture, geometric retargeting (Quadratic Programming), and dynamic retargeting via centroidal dynamics-based Trajectory Optimization.
- Online execution uses Centroidal ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) for real-time adaptation, **Whole-Body** Control (WBC) for joint torque commands, and multi-source state estimation.

## Results

- Offline dynamic motion retargeting effectively enforced joint torque constraints and modulated momentum, preventing instability during large stride and arm swing movements.
- Online MPC with a **1.2**-second prediction horizon successfully adapted to disturbances by proactively adjusting swing-foot placement, maintaining robot balance where shorter horizons failed.
- Four Kuavo 4Pro humanoid robots successfully performed a 4-minute live public dance, demonstrating the framework's robustness in complex, real-world environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Longer prediction horizons in offline trajectory optimization are crucial for generating dynamically feasible and expressive dance motions by enabling proactive momentum regulation and coordinated limb movements.
> - Proactive swing-foot placement adjustments, facilitated by longer prediction horizons in online MPC, are vital for robustly adapting to real-world disturbances and maintaining balance.
> - A hierarchical, model-based approach effectively bridges human motion demonstrations to stable robot execution, addressing the trade-off between motion expressiveness and stability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robot control aims to enable robots to perform complex, human-like movements. A significant area within this field is the generation of expressive whole-body motions, particularly for entertainment applications like dance. This work contributes to overcoming challenges associated with achieving dynamic, stable, and expressive dance movements in humanoid robots.

Previous research has explored various methodologies for robot motion generation and control. Reinforcement Learning (RL) frameworks have been used to imitate human dance motions from motion capture (MoCap) data. However, these often require extensive reward function design and parameter tuning, along with lengthy retraining phases for modifications. Optimization-based methods, such as those employing Inverse Kinematics (IK) or operational-space inverse dynamics within Quadratic Programming (QP) frameworks, have also been investigated. While these approaches can incorporate dynamic constraints like Zero Moment Point (ZMP) limits or joint torque limits, they often only consider the robot's current state, leading to conservative motions that struggle with large momentum variations.

Trajectory Optimization (TO) and Model Predictive Control (MPC) offer a way to address these limitations by anticipating future states over a prediction horizon. While TO has been successfully applied to dynamic motions like jumping and running in humanoid robots, its application to dance is challenging due to the long-horizon sequences involved, coupled with frequent and aperiodic contact transitions. These factors can significantly increase computational complexity and make it difficult to generate feasible motions without appropriate reference trajectories. Simplistic dynamic models, such as the Linear Inverted Pendulum Model (LIPM), simplify the problem but limit the range and dynamism of achievable motions by neglecting angular momentum and assuming constant Center of Mass (CoM) height. More complex whole-body dynamic models are computationally intensive for direct TO.

This research integrates a hierarchical optimization-based approach, combining geometric motion retargeting with centroidal dynamics-based Trajectory Optimization and Model Predictive Control. It leverages human MoCap data to provide well-shaped reference trajectories, addressing the computational challenges of direct TO for complex dance sequences. By focusing on future state prediction in both offline planning and online execution, the work aims to overcome the conservatism of instantaneous optimization and the instability caused by real-world disturbances, thereby enabling more dynamic, expressive, and robust whole-body dance motions on full-size humanoid robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an integrated model-based framework capable of generating and executing dynamic, whole-body dance motions on humanoid robots. This framework aims to enable robots to perform expressive movements that emulate human dance, ultimately enhancing their artistic capabilities and potential for entertainment applications.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in achieving dynamic dance with humanoid robots:

- **Dynamic Motion Complexity:** Dance involves high-speed, nonlinear, and coupled movements of all limbs, leading to significant changes in angular and linear momentum. Precise coordination and momentum regulation are crucial for maintaining balance, which is a complex control problem.
- **Expressiveness vs. Stability:** Traditional motion generation methods often prioritize stability, which can lead to conservative motions lacking the expressiveness required for dance. Achieving both dynamic expressiveness and robust stability simultaneously is a key challenge.
- **Computational Feasibility for Long Sequences:** Dance motions typically involve long sequences with frequent and aperiodic contact transitions. Directly applying computationally intensive whole-body trajectory optimization to such extended sequences is often prohibitive.
- **Robustness to Real-world Disturbances:** In real-world environments, factors like sensor noise, actuator errors, and uneven surfaces (e.g., soft carpets) can introduce tracking errors, potentially leading to instability or falls. Robots need to adapt to these disturbances in real time.
- **Bridging the Gap between Planning and Execution:** Offline planned motions often struggle to account for real-world uncertainties. A robust online control mechanism is required to bridge this gap and ensure stable execution.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a framework that leverages future state prediction in both its offline motion generation and online motion execution stages. This predictive capability is motivated by the need to:
1. Generate dynamically feasible and expressive trajectories by proactively coordinating limb movements and regulating momentum over a horizon.
2. Enable robust online execution by anticipating potential instabilities and actively adjusting foot placement to maintain balance in response to real-time disturbances.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates a two-stage process: offline motion generation and online motion execution, both utilizing future state prediction.

#### 3.1. A. Offline Motion Generation

This stage focuses on creating dynamically feasible and expressive whole-body dance trajectories for the robot. It consists of three substages:

1. **Human Demonstration Acquisition:**
 - **Method:** An optical Motion Capture (MoCap) system (NOKOV) is used to record human dance demonstrations.
 - **Details:** 12 infrared cameras track 53 reflective markers placed on key body segments (head, torso, arms, waist, legs, feet) of a human demonstrator at 100 Hz. The software reconstructs 3D spatial trajectories and associates them with a human skeleton model to compute the position and orientation of each body link.
 - **Output:** High-quality kinematic data of human dance movements.
 - **Manual Annotation:** Contact states (double support, left single support, right single support) are manually annotated for each time step, providing a crucial contact schedule for subsequent optimization and control.

2. **Geometric Motion Retargeting:**
 - **Purpose:** To adapt the captured human motions to the robot's different body structure (e.g., limb lengths, joint configurations).
 - **Method:** Formulated as a Quadratic Programming (QP) problem.
 - **Details:**
 - **Scaling:** The human skeleton is proportionally scaled to match the robot's limb lengths after manually establishing joint correspondences.
 - **Optimization Objective:** Minimize the spatial positional errors between the robot joints and their corresponding scaled human joints. The weighted QP formulation minimizes the squared L2 norm of the task error `e_i`, which is the twist logarithm of the relative pose between the robot's current link frame and the human's target link frame.
 - **Weighting Strategy:** Higher weights are assigned to the pelvis and feet to maintain whole-body configuration, while lower weights are given to intermediate leg and arm joints to accommodate morphological differences and preserve motion style.
 - **Output:** Robot joint trajectories that are kinematically adapted but may not be dynamically feasible.

3. **Dynamic Motion Retargeting via Trajectory Optimization (TO):**
 - **Purpose:** To refine the geometrically retargeted trajectories to ensure dynamic feasibility by satisfying dynamic constraints.
 - **Method:** Implemented using a Model Predictive Control (MPC) approach in a receding-horizon fashion.
 - **Details:**
 - **Computational Efficiency:** The long dance sequences are broken into shorter optimization windows to reduce computational load. The optimizer processes a window, applies the first-step adjustment, and then shifts the window.
 - **Dynamics Model:** A centroidal dynamics model is adopted, which maps linear and angular momentum of the multi-body system to the centroidal frame via the Centroidal Momentum Matrix (CMM). The Newton-Euler equation at the robot's CoM describes the forces and torques acting on the system.
 - **Optimal Control Problem (OCP):** An OCP is solved over a finite horizon to minimize a stage cost `L(x, u, t)` subject to system dynamics, equality, and inequality constraints.
 - **Stage Cost:** `L(x, u, t)` is a weighted squared L2 norm combining tracking errors of the robot's state `x` (CoM velocity, angular momentum, joint positions) to a reference `x_ref` (from geometric retargeting) and the magnitude of control inputs `u` (contact forces, joint velocities).
 - **Weighting Strategy:** In the state weight matrix `Q`, highest weights are on base position/orientation and centroidal momentum for stability and coordinated motion. Leg joints are moderately weighted for foot placement, while arm joints are lower-weighted. In the input weight matrix `R`, joint velocities receive higher weights for smooth actuation, and contact forces the lowest.
 - **Constraints:**
 - **System Dynamics:** Eqs. (2) and (3) relating limb motion to centroidal momentum.
 - **Equality Constraints:** Zero-velocity for stance feet (no slippage), zero contact force and reference trajectory tracking for swing feet.
 - **Inequality Constraints:** Contact force within friction cone, joint limits.
 - **Solver:** The Pinocchio library's centroidal dynamics interface computes reference states. The optimization iterations are set to 200.
 - **Output:** Dynamically feasible and expressive whole-body trajectories.

#### 3.2. B. Online Motion Execution

This stage focuses on real-time execution of the planned motions, adapting to disturbances to maintain stability. It comprises three components:

1. **Centroidal Model Predictive Control (MPC):**
 - **Purpose:** Continuously optimizes reference robot state trajectories in real time to track the desired dance motions and adapt to disturbances.
 - **Method:** Uses a receding horizon approach, consistent with the OCP formulation from offline TO.
 - **Details:**
 - **Real-time Adaptation:** Dynamically adjusts the reference trajectory at 50 Hz based on real-time state feedback.
 - **Proactive Adjustment:** When instability is predicted, the controller proactively adjusts swing-foot placement to reshape the support polygon and keep the ZMP within stable bounds.
 - **Computational Efficiency:** The solver is warm-started with the previous time step's solution, and only one solver iteration is executed per control cycle.
 - **Output:** Updated reference targets (joint position/velocity, centroidal momentum, contact force, torso orientation, joint acceleration, CoM, swing-foot trajectories) for the Whole-Body Control.

2. **Whole-Body Control (WBC):**
 - **Purpose:** To compute joint torque commands to track the reference motion trajectories generated by the centroidal MPC.
 - **Method:** Formulated as a weighted QP problem running at 500 Hz.
 - **Details:**
 - **Optimization Objective:** Simultaneously optimizes joint accelerations, joint torques, and contact forces to achieve multiple weighted tasks (e.g., stance leg joint motion tracking, swing leg joint motion tracking, base angular motion tracking, arm joint motion tracking, CoM tracking, contact force tracking).
 - **Constraints:** Robot dynamics, friction cones, and joint limits.
 - **Weighting Strategy:** Stance-leg joint motion tracking is given the highest weight to prevent contact slip.
 - **Actuation:** A PD control scheme with a feedforward torque term (computed by QP) generates the final joint torque commands. MPC trajectories are linearly interpolated for high-frequency WBC execution.

3. **State Estimation:**
 - **Purpose:** Provides accurate real-time feedback of the robot's state to close the control loop.
 - **Method:** Fuses multi-source sensor data.
 - **Details:** Orientation is estimated from Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) data (gyroscope and accelerometer). Base position and velocity are derived by fusing accelerometer measurements with joint state feedback based on the kinematic model.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The framework was validated through simulations and hardware experiments using the full-size humanoid robot Kuavo 4Pro (1.66 m height, 55 kg mass, 6 DoF legs, 7 DoF arms). The evaluation focused on dynamic whole-body dance motions involving synchronized arm swinging and leg stepping.

#### 4.1. A. Dynamic Motion Retargeting (Offline)

1. **Enforcement of Dynamic Constraints:**
 - Geometric motion retargeting alone often produced dynamically infeasible trajectories, particularly violating joint torque limits (e.g., knee joint torque).
 - The dynamic motion retargeting module, by employing TO, explicitly enforced joint torque constraints. This resulted in adjustments (e.g., reducing knee joint motion amplitude) that kept torques within limits, ensuring dynamic feasibility.

2. **Momentum Modulation:**
 - During dynamic motions with large leg strides and arm swings, geometric retargeting often led to imbalance due to excessive angular momentum, causing the ZMP to exit the support polygon.
 - The dynamic retargeting module effectively modulated momentum. It reduced the angular velocity of the swing leg and arms and generated a larger forward contact force, which applied a compensatory torque to the CoM. This active regulation maintained ZMP within the support polygon, ensuring stable posture during large stride movements.

3. **Influence of Prediction Horizon on Dynamic Capability:**
 - The duration of the prediction horizon in TO significantly affected the dynamic capability of the generated motion.
 - Longer prediction horizons (e.g., beyond 0.6 s, with a substantial increase between 0.6 s and 1.0 s) led to higher average velocities of both the swing leg and the CoM.
 - Shorter horizons resulted in more conservative motions, as the optimizer, without anticipating future events like contact transitions, reduced limb speeds to ensure short-term stability.
 - Longer horizons allowed for better anticipation of dynamic changes throughout the entire motion, enabling a trade-off between stride velocity and stability, thereby generating more expressive movements.

#### 4.2. B. Online Motion Execution

1. **Adaptation to Real-world Disturbances:**
 - The ability of the controller to adapt to disturbances and maintain stability was found to be dependent on the prediction horizon of the online MPC.
 - A short prediction horizon (0.6 s) resulted in failure to maintain balance, with the knee joint exceeding its position limit. This was attributed to insufficient foresight, preventing the controller from planning future foot placements and relying excessively on joint-level adjustments.
 - A longer prediction horizon (1.2 s) enabled successful execution. The controller proactively adjusted swing-foot placement based on anticipated future states, effectively reshaping the support polygon and keeping the ZMP centered within the *adjusted* support polygon, rather than the reference one. This demonstrated an increased stability margin and more effective disturbance response.

2. **Computational Cost vs. Horizon Length:**
 - While longer prediction horizons improved robustness, they increased computational cost.
 - An average solve time of 20.64 ms for a 1.2 s prediction horizon was measured. This speed allowed the controller to operate close to the target real-time frequency of 50 Hz, balancing foresight with computational feasibility.

#### 4.3. C. Public Performance

- The integrated framework was successfully deployed in a four-minute live public dance performance at the 2025 Zhongguancun (ZGC) Forum.
- A team of four Kuavo 4Pro humanoid robots performed dynamic whole-body dance motions.
- Challenges included the inherent complexity of dynamic whole-body movements and unstructured environmental disturbances (e.g., soft carpets causing foot sinking and altered support polygons).
- Despite these challenges, the robots completed the entire performance without any failures, indicating the robustness and effectiveness of the proposed model-based framework in a real-world, public setting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents an integrated model-based framework that enables humanoid robots to perform dynamic, whole-body dance motions with enhanced expressiveness and robustness. The core contribution lies in the systematic application of future state prediction across both offline motion generation and online execution stages.

The findings highlight the critical role of the prediction horizon:
- In **offline motion generation**, a longer prediction horizon allows the trajectory optimizer to proactively coordinate limb movements and regulate momentum, leading to dynamically feasible and more expressive dance motions compared to conservative, instantaneous optimization methods.
- In **online motion execution**, a longer prediction horizon empowers the MPC controller to anticipate potential instabilities and proactively adjust swing-foot placement. This mechanism significantly improves the robot's ability to adapt to real-world disturbances, maintaining balance and preventing joint limit violations or falls, as demonstrated on uneven surfaces.

The successful deployment of a team of four full-size humanoid robots in a four-minute live public performance under challenging environmental conditions signifies a validation of the framework's practical applicability and robustness. This achievement contributes to the advancement of humanoid robot control in several ways:
- **Enhanced Motion Capabilities:** It demonstrates the feasibility of generating and executing complex, high-dynamic dance movements, pushing the boundaries of humanoid robot agility and artistic expression.
- **Improved Robustness:** The proactive foot placement adjustment mechanism, enabled by longer prediction horizons in MPC, offers a more resilient control strategy against real-world uncertainties compared to purely reactive approaches.
- **Integrated Pipeline:** The work provides a comprehensive pipeline from human demonstration to real-world execution, which can serve as a foundation for developing other complex human-like behaviors in robots.

Potential impacts of this work include:
- **Entertainment and Art:** Humanoid robots could be deployed in performances, shows, or interactive artistic installations, leveraging their anthropomorphic design for expressive communication.
- **Advanced Robotics Research:** The framework's principles, particularly the use of future state prediction for complex, long-horizon tasks, could be adapted for other challenging locomotion and manipulation tasks in humanoid robots, such as dexterous object handling during dynamic movement or navigating complex terrains.

Despite its demonstrated effectiveness, the authors acknowledge limitations. The framework currently requires manual intervention for reference motion adjustment and contact schedule annotation, limiting its scalability. Additionally, the computational cost of the online MPC, while managed for this specific dance, may pose challenges for even more highly dynamic movements requiring longer horizons or faster control rates. Future work aiming to automate motion retargeting with dynamic feasibility considerations, automatic contact schedule extraction, and developing faster MPC frameworks could further enhance the framework's capabilities and broaden its applicability.
