# Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper systematically studies using powerful language models as automated judges for evaluating other LLMs, introducing the MT-bench and Chatbot Arena benchmarks for human-preference-aligned assessment. It demonstrates that GPT-4, when used as a judge, exhibits high agreement with human evaluations, provides insights into LLM judge biases, and advocates for a hybrid evaluation framework to holistically measure LLM capabilities and human alignment.

## Problem

- Existing LLM benchmarks primarily assess core capabilities and are ill-suited to evaluate human alignment or performance in open-ended, multi-turn conversational scenarios.
- Human evaluation, while the gold standard for assessing human preference, is prohibitively slow, expensive, and non-scalable, hindering rapid LLM development.
- There was a lack of systematic study and validation of using powerful LLMs as automated judges, despite its emerging practical use.

## Method

- The paper systematically investigates and validates **LLM-as-a-judge**, using powerful LLMs like GPT-4 to automatically evaluate other LLMs (via ==pairwise comparison==, ==single-answer grading==, and ==reference-guided grading==), particularly for conversational quality.
- It introduces two new human-preference-focused benchmarks: **MT-bench**, a ==multi-turn question set==, and **Chatbot Arena**, a ==crowdsourced== platform for large-scale, in-the-wild human preference data collection.
- The authors identify and propose mitigation strategies for common LLM judge biases, including ==position bias==, ==verbosity bias==, and limitations in reasoning/math.

## Results

- GPT-4 demonstrated high agreement with human judgments, reaching **85%** for non-tie votes on MT-bench (expert humans) and **87-95%** on Chatbot Arena (crowdsourced humans), comparable to inter-human agreement.
- Position bias in LLM judges can be significantly reduced by swapping response orders, and GPT-4 showed robustness to verbosity bias with only an **8.7%** failure rate.
- Reference-guided judging reduced GPT-4's failure rate on math questions from **70%** to **15%**, and fine-tuning an open-source model (Vicuna) as a judge improved its consistency from **16.2%** to **65%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Powerful LLMs, particularly GPT-4, can serve as effective automated judges for evaluating other LLMs, showing high agreement with human preferences.
> - LLM judges are susceptible to biases like position and verbosity bias, which can be mitigated through methods such as response swapping and reference-guided prompting.
> - A hybrid evaluation framework combining traditional capability benchmarks with human-preference-focused benchmarks (like MT-bench and Chatbot Arena) offers a more comprehensive assessment of LLMs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical and rapidly evolving challenge in the field of Large Language Models (LLMs): effective and scalable evaluation, particularly for conversational AI assistants. The proliferation of LLM-based chat assistants, heavily influenced by models like ChatGPT, has led to a paradigm shift in how users interact with AI. These models, often fine-tuned with supervised instruction following and Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF), exhibit remarkable conversational and instruction-following abilities that users strongly prefer.

However, the authors highlight a fundamental discrepancy: this heightened user preference does not consistently translate to improved scores on traditional LLM benchmarks. Existing benchmarks, such as MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) and HELM (Holistic Evaluation of Language Models), primarily focus on measuring LLMs' "core capabilities" on confined, often closed-ended tasks like multi-choice knowledge questions or code completion. They are ill-suited to assess "alignment with human preference" in open-ended, multi-turn dialogues, which are central to user satisfaction with chat assistants. This gap leaves researchers and developers without robust, scalable methods to evaluate whether their models are truly "aligned" with human expectations in real-world conversational contexts.

Into this landscape, the paper introduces "LLM-as-a-judge" as a promising solution. While the concept of using powerful LLMs to evaluate others had been explored concurrently by other groups (e.g., OpenAI's Evals, AlpacaFarm), and in the authors' own earlier blog post, this paper provides the first systematic and comprehensive study of its usage, advantages, and limitations. It aims to formalize LLM-as-a-judge as a viable, scalable, and explainable alternative or complement to human evaluation.

Furthermore, the paper contributes significantly to the benchmarking landscape by introducing two novel, human-preference-focused benchmarks: MT-bench and Chatbot Arena. These are designed to bridge the gap between traditional capability-focused benchmarks and the need for evaluating human alignment. MT-bench offers a controlled, multi-turn question set, while Chatbot Arena provides large-scale, crowdsourced "in-the-wild" human preference data. By validating LLM-as-a-judge against these human-centric benchmarks, the work positions itself as a foundational study for a new era of LLM evaluation, advocating for a hybrid framework that combines the strengths of both traditional and preference-based approaches. This shift is crucial for guiding the development of truly helpful and aligned AI assistants.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the observed mismatch between the perceived utility of advanced LLM-based chat assistants by human users and their performance on conventional evaluation benchmarks. While models fine-tuned with human feedback are overwhelmingly preferred by users for their instruction-following and conversational abilities, traditional benchmarks often fail to capture this "human alignment" or "preference." Human evaluation, the gold standard for assessing such preferences, is prohibitively slow and expensive, hindering rapid model development and iteration.

Against this backdrop, the paper sets forth several key objectives:

1. **Systematically Study LLM-as-a-Judge:** The primary objective is to conduct a thorough and systematic investigation into the efficacy, advantages, and limitations of using strong LLMs (like GPT-4) as automated judges for evaluating other LLMs. This includes examining potential biases inherent in LLM-based evaluation.
2. **Validate LLM-as-a-Judge against Human Preferences:** A crucial goal is to empirically verify the agreement between judgments made by powerful LLM judges and actual human preferences. This involves comparing LLM judgments with both controlled expert human evaluations and large-scale crowdsourced human preferences.
3. **Identify and Mitigate Biases in LLM-as-a-Judge:** The authors aim to meticulously identify and analyze various biases that could affect LLM judges, such as position bias (favoring responses based on their display order), verbosity bias (favoring longer responses), and self-enhancement bias (favoring one's own model's output). Subsequently, the objective is to propose and test practical solutions to mitigate these biases.
4. **Develop and Release Novel Human Preference Benchmarks:** To facilitate robust evaluation, the paper aims to introduce two new, publicly available benchmarks:
 - **MT-bench:** A high-quality, multi-turn question set designed to challenge chat assistants on their conversational and instruction-following abilities across diverse categories.
 - **Chatbot Arena:** A crowdsourced platform for anonymous battles between LLMs, allowing the collection of diverse, real-world human preferences in an open-ended setting.
5. **Advocate for a Hybrid Evaluation Framework:** The paper seeks to demonstrate that traditional capability-based benchmarks and new preference-based benchmarks (with LLM-as-a-judge) are complementary. The objective is to argue for the adoption of a hybrid evaluation framework that comprehensively assesses both core capabilities and human alignment, enabling faster and more effective LLM development.
6. **Publicly Release Resources:** A practical objective is to publicly release the MT-bench questions, expert human votes, and thousands of conversations with human preferences from Chatbot Arena to foster further research and transparent benchmarking in the community.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a multi-pronged methodology combining the development of novel evaluation benchmarks, a systematic analysis of LLM judges, and rigorous agreement evaluation against human preferences.

#### 3.1. LLM-as-a-Judge Variations

The paper explores three primary variations for employing LLMs as judges:
- **Pairwise comparison:** The judge LLM is presented with a user question and two candidate answers (Assistant A and Assistant B) and instructed to choose the better one or declare a tie. This method is intuitive and mimics human comparison.
- **Single answer grading:** The judge LLM evaluates a single answer against a user question and assigns an absolute score (e.g., 1-10). This approach offers higher scalability for many models but may struggle with subtle distinctions or consistency without a stable internal rubric.
- **Reference-guided grading:** For specific tasks like math or factual reasoning, the judge is provided with a reference solution alongside the candidate answers. This aims to improve accuracy by providing a correct ground truth.

#### 3.2. Development of New Human Preference Benchmarks

Recognizing the limitations of existing benchmarks, the authors introduce two complementary benchmarks:
- **MT-bench:** A carefully constructed dataset of 80 high-quality, multi-turn questions categorized into 8 common use cases (writing, roleplay, extraction, reasoning, math, coding, STEM knowledge, humanities/social science knowledge). Its multi-turn nature is crucial for assessing conversational abilities and instruction following in complex scenarios.
- **Chatbot Arena:** A crowdsourced online platform enabling anonymous "battles" between two LLMs. Users interact with both models for the same question and vote for their preferred response, revealing model identities only after voting. This platform generates a large volume of "in-the-wild" human preference data across diverse, unrestricted real-world queries.

#### 3.3. Identification and Mitigation of LLM Judge Limitations

The study systematically investigates several potential biases and limitations:
- **Position Bias:** The tendency of an LLM judge to favor responses presented in a particular position (e.g., first or second). The authors test this by swapping the order of responses. Mitigation strategies include:
 - **Swapping positions:** Calling the judge twice with swapped answer orders and requiring consistent results for a definitive verdict, or randomizing positions at scale.
 - **Few-shot judging:** Providing examples of good judgments to guide the LLM.
- **Verbosity Bias:** The inclination to favor longer, more verbose responses regardless of actual quality. This was tested using a "repetitive list" attack, where an answer was made artificially verbose without adding new information.
- **Self-enhancement Bias:** The potential for an LLM judge to implicitly favor answers generated by itself (or models it is closely related to). This was statistically examined by comparing win rates across different judges.
- **Limited Reasoning and Math Capability:** LLM judges can make incorrect judgments in math and reasoning tasks, even when they could solve the problem independently. Mitigation methods explored:
 - **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Judge:** Prompting the judge to first independently solve the question step-by-step before evaluating the candidates.
 - **Reference-guided Judge:** Providing a correct reference answer within the prompt to help the judge.

#### 3.4. Multi-turn Judging

For MT-bench's multi-turn questions, the authors explored optimal prompt design. They found that displaying the *complete conversation history* in a single prompt (rather than breaking turns into separate prompts) was essential for the LLM judge to accurately grasp context and previous turns.

#### 3.5. Agreement Evaluation Setup

To validate LLM-as-a-judge, the authors conducted extensive agreement studies:
- **MT-bench:** Answers from 6 models (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude-V1, Vicuna-13B, Alpaca-13B, LLaMA-13B) were collected. Human evaluations involved 58 "expert-level" human labelers (mostly graduate students) providing around 3,000 votes, ensuring controlled, high-quality human preferences.
- **Chatbot Arena:** A random sample of 3,000 single-turn votes from over 30,000 collected arena interactions was used, involving 2,114 unique crowd judges, providing diverse "in-the-wild" human preferences.
- **Metrics:** Agreement (probability of two judge types agreeing) and average win rate were used to quantify alignment.

#### 3.6. Exploring Open-source LLM Judges (Appendix F)

Beyond proprietary LLMs, the study investigated fine-tuning an open-source model (Vicuna-13B) on the Chatbot Arena human preference data to act as a judge, aiming for a more cost-effective and transparent evaluation solution.

This comprehensive methodological approach allows for both a deep understanding of LLM-as-a-judge's mechanics and biases, as well as a robust validation of its performance against the gold standard of human preference.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several significant findings regarding the feasibility and characteristics of using LLMs as judges for conversational AI models:

#### 4.1. LLM-as-a-Judge Biases and Mitigation Effectiveness

- **Position Bias:** All LLM judges (Claude-v1, GPT-3.5, GPT-4) exhibited a strong position bias, primarily favoring the first presented answer. GPT-4 showed the highest consistency (65%) among zero-shot judges when swapping positions. The conservative mitigation approach of swapping positions and requiring consistent verdicts significantly reduces this bias. Interestingly, position bias was found to be less prominent when there was a large performance disparity between the two models being judged, suggesting judges are more confident when differences are clear. Few-shot prompting also increased consistency.
- **Verbosity Bias:** Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 were highly susceptible to verbosity bias, failing in over 90% of "repetitive list" attack cases. GPT-4, however, demonstrated significantly better robustness, with a failure rate of only 8.7%, indicating its superior ability to discern actual content quality over mere length.
- **Self-enhancement Bias:** While some proprietary models like GPT-4 (10% higher win rate for itself) and Claude-v1 (25% higher win rate for itself) showed a tendency to favor their own outputs, the study could not definitively determine the extent of self-enhancement bias due to data limitations and the difficulty of controlling for style vs. quality changes.
- **Limited Reasoning and Math Capability:** LLM judges, even GPT-4, exhibited limitations in accurately grading math and reasoning questions. They could sometimes be misled by incorrect answers provided, even if capable of solving the problem independently. However, mitigation strategies proved highly effective:
 - **Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting:** While helpful, LLM judges could still be influenced by incorrect answers in the context.
 - **Reference-guided judging:** This method significantly improved accuracy, reducing the failure rate for GPT-4 on math questions from 70% (with default prompt) to 15%. This highlights the importance of providing ground truth for certain types of questions.

#### 4.2. High Agreement between GPT-4 and Human Preferences

- **MT-bench (Expert Humans):** GPT-4, using both pairwise comparison and single-answer grading, demonstrated remarkably high agreement with human experts. For non-tie votes, the agreement rate between GPT-4 and humans reached **85%**. This is notably comparable to, or even slightly higher than, the agreement rate *among humans themselves* (81%), indicating that GPT-4's judgments closely align with the majority human consensus. Furthermore, humans deemed GPT-4's judgments reasonable in 75% of cases where their initial choice differed, and 34% were willing to change their choices after seeing GPT-4's rationale.
- **Chatbot Arena (Crowdsourced Humans):** Similar high agreement (87-95% for non-tie votes) was observed between GPT-4 and crowdsourced human preferences on this large-scale, in-the-wild dataset. GPT-4 was also more "affirmative" (fewer tie votes) than other LLM judges.
- **Correlation with Performance Disparity:** The agreement between GPT-4 and human judgments progressively increased with the performance disparity between the two models being compared, reaching nearly 100% agreement when one model significantly outperformed the other.

#### 4.3. Model Differentiation and Win Rates

- LLM judges' average win rate curves for different models (GPT-4, GPT-3.5, Claude, Vicuna, Alpaca, LLaMA) closely mirrored human preferences on both MT-bench and Chatbot Arena.
- MT-bench effectively differentiated model capabilities, particularly in multi-turn interactions. Proprietary models like GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 significantly outperformed open-source models (Vicuna, LLaMA) across most categories, especially in reasoning, math, and coding, where Vicuna showed noticeable weaknesses.

#### 4.4. Complementarity of Benchmarks

- The evaluation of LLaMA and Vicuna variants on MMLU, TruthfulQA, and MT-bench demonstrated that fine-tuning on high-quality dialogue data (like ShareGPT for Vicuna) consistently improved performance on MT-bench (human alignment) and MMLU, but not necessarily TruthfulQA.
- The results confirmed that no single benchmark could comprehensively determine model quality, underscoring the need for a **hybrid evaluation framework** that combines traditional capability-based benchmarks with new preference-based benchmarks using LLM-as-a-judge.

#### 4.5. Vicuna as a Judge (Preliminary)

- Zero-shot Vicuna-13B performed poorly as a judge (low consistency, high error rate).
- However, fine-tuning Vicuna-13B on human votes from Chatbot Arena significantly improved its performance, achieving 65% consistency (up from 16.2%) and a classification accuracy matching GPT-4 agreement (85.5% excluding ties). This shows promising potential for open-source LLM judges.

In essence, the findings robustly validate GPT-4 as a highly reliable and scalable judge that can approximate human preferences with an agreement level comparable to inter-human agreement, despite some identifiable biases that can be mitigated.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries substantial significance for the future of Large Language Model development and evaluation, offering solutions to critical challenges in the field. Its potential impacts are multi-faceted:

#### 5.1. Scalable and Cost-Effective LLM Evaluation

The most immediate and impactful contribution is the establishment of LLM-as-a-judge as a viable, scalable, and explainable method for evaluating LLM-based chat assistants. Human evaluation, while the gold standard, is a bottleneck due to its cost, time, and labor intensity. By demonstrating that powerful LLMs like GPT-4 can achieve human-level agreement in judging other models, this work provides a crucial alternative. This allows for:
- **Faster Iteration Cycles:** Developers can rapidly evaluate changes to their models, accelerating the development and refinement of aligned LLMs.
- **Reduced Costs:** Automating evaluation significantly cuts down the expenses associated with human labeling.
- **Explainable Judgments:** Unlike simple scores from traditional metrics, LLM judges can provide detailed explanations for their decisions, offering valuable insights for model improvement.

#### 5.2. Revolutionizing LLM Benchmarking

The paper fundamentally reshapes the benchmarking landscape for LLMs. By introducing MT-bench and Chatbot Arena, it provides robust, human-preference-centric datasets and platforms that capture crucial aspects of LLM performance—multi-turn conversation, instruction-following, and human alignment—that traditional benchmarks overlook. The proposed "hybrid evaluation framework" that combines conventional capability-based benchmarks with preference-based LLM-as-a-judge evaluations is poised to become a new industry standard. This holistic approach ensures models are not only intelligent but also user-friendly and aligned with human expectations.

#### 5.3. Fostering Open Science and Reproducibility

The public release of MT-bench questions, expert human votes, and 30,000 conversations with human preferences from Chatbot Arena is a major contribution to the open-source community. This data empowers researchers worldwide to conduct their own evaluations, compare models fairly, and build upon the methodology. It promotes transparency and reproducibility in a field that has seen increasing reliance on proprietary models and evaluation methods.

#### 5.4. Deeper Understanding of LLM Behavior and Biases

The systematic study of LLM judge biases (position, verbosity, self-enhancement, reasoning limitations) offers critical insights into the internal workings and potential pitfalls of using LLMs for evaluative tasks. By identifying these biases and proposing mitigation strategies (e.g., swapping positions, reference-guided prompting), the research contributes to the development of more robust and reliable AI systems, not just for judging, but for other complex tasks as well.

#### 5.5. Guiding Future LLM Development

The findings highlight the distinct strengths and weaknesses of various LLMs (e.g., GPT-4's superior reasoning and alignment compared to open-source models like Vicuna). This provides valuable guidance for developers and researchers working on improving LLMs, emphasizing areas like multi-turn conversational abilities, complex reasoning, and ethical alignment. The preliminary success of fine-tuning Vicuna as an open-source judge also paves the way for cheaper, accessible evaluation tools.

#### 5.6. Broader Societal Impact

By enabling more effective evaluation, this work indirectly contributes to the development of higher-quality and more helpful AI chat assistants. This can enhance user experiences across various applications. However, the authors appropriately acknowledge that the study focuses on "helpfulness" and largely neglects "safety" aspects (honesty, harmlessness), which are equally crucial for societal impact. The methods proposed in this paper, by extension, can be adapted for evaluating these safety metrics. The proliferation of advanced chat assistants, facilitated by better evaluation, also raises broader societal considerations, such as potential job displacement and the need for new skill development, which require careful consideration by policymakers and society as a whole.

In conclusion, "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge" is a landmark paper that provides a rigorous empirical foundation for an LLM-based evaluation framework. Its methodologies and findings are set to significantly influence how LLMs are developed, assessed, and deployed, accelerating progress in creating more human-aligned and capable AI systems.
