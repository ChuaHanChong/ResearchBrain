# KINO: A Keyframe Interface for VLM Planning and Whole-Body Control in Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> KINO is a hierarchical humanoid loco-manipulation framework that uses motion keyframes as the interface between a VLM planner and an RL whole-body controller. The VLM selects the next keyframe from a labeled library, an IK retargeter adapts it to object pose and size, and a keyframe-conditioned PPO policy reaches it. Saliency-based keyframe sampling lifts end-to-end success from 44% to 92% in simulation, with hardware deployment on a Unitree G1.

## Problem

- Motion-tracking loco-manipulation controllers like OmniRetarget, HDMI, and ResMimic need a complete kinematic reference trajectory at inference time, which real-world deployment rarely provides.
- End-to-end VLAs for humanoids need large robot-specific action-labeled datasets and must output high-dimensional, high-frequency whole-body actions to keep balance and manage contacts.
- Hierarchical VLM-plus-RL stacks lack a good interface: VLMs reason in open-ended language, while RL controllers need precise structured goals; prior keyframe controllers relied on hand-specified keyframes.

## Method

- **Keyframe-conditioned whole-body policy**: ==PPO== with an ==asymmetric actor-critic== and separate value functions for tracking, sparse goal-reaching, and regularisation rewards, conditioned on target root, joint, and object poses.
- **Saliency-based keyframe sampling**: training goals come from ==low-pass-filtered body-acceleration peaks==, with periodic gait peaks filtered out and object-acceleration events added, so stage transitions like pick and place are well represented.
- **VLM planner with scene alignment**: Qwen3.6-27B picks a labeled keyframe from a library via ==constrained JSON== using execution feedback; a bounding-box normalised transform plus ==damped least-squares IK== retargets it to object pose and size.

## Results

- Saliency-based sampling raises end-to-end box pick-and-place success from **44%** to **92%** over 100 simulated trials versus uniform 1.5 s sampling, with pick **63%** to **95%** and place **63%** to **93%**.
- Saliency sampling cuts pick-stage object error from **0.170 m** to **0.043 m** and place-stage root error from **0.162 m** to **0.067 m**, though place-stage object error rises to **0.227 m**.
- VLM keyframe queries average under **0.2 s** and retargeting about **5 ms**; the policy places boxes outside training-data coverage and runs one- and two-handed tasks on a real G1.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Train low-level goal-conditioned policies on the goal distribution the planner will actually emit; uniform temporal sampling underweights rare stage transitions and silently caps hierarchical success.
> - Restricting a VLM to discrete selection from a labeled pose library, rather than continuous action output, uses its reasoning without any robot-specific planner training data.
> - Caution: the manually built keyframe library, third-person camera, and mocap object poses limit scalability; onboard perception and automatic library construction remain unsolved for in-the-wild use.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid loco-manipulation combines locomotion, balance, whole-body coordination, perception, task reasoning, and physical interaction with objects. It is more demanding than conventional stationary manipulation because the robot must simultaneously control its feet, torso, arms, and balance while navigating through an environment and manipulating objects.

Recent research has progressed along two partially separate directions. The first uses reinforcement learning (RL), imitation learning, or motion tracking to produce dynamically feasible humanoid movements. These methods can generate locomotion and whole-body behaviors, particularly when trained from human motion or teleoperation data. However, many motion-tracking systems require a complete reference trajectory at inference time. Such a requirement is difficult to satisfy for a robot operating in an unstructured environment, where the desired motion depends on the current scene and task.

The second direction uses vision-language models (VLMs) and vision-language-action (VLA) models for interpreting instructions and planning actions. VLA systems attempt to map images and language directly to robot actions, but whole-body humanoid control presents a difficult scaling problem. The action space is high-dimensional, balance must be maintained at high frequency, and large robot-specific datasets are needed to learn reliable motor outputs.

KINO addresses the interface between these two levels. Rather than asking a VLM to generate joint commands or requiring an RL controller to interpret language, it uses motion keyframes as an intermediate representation. A VLM selects a semantically meaningful pose, such as approaching an object, crouching to pick it up, carrying it, or placing it. A low-level RL policy then generates the continuous whole-body motion needed to reach that pose. In this respect, the work is related to prior keyframe-based control and hierarchical VLM planning, but it extends these ideas to humanoid loco-manipulation and includes object-relative retargeting and closed-loop failure recovery.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to develop a hierarchical system that can execute long-horizon humanoid loco-manipulation tasks from language instructions while avoiding direct end-to-end learning from language to motor actions.

The authors identify three central problems. First, a high-level planner must understand the task, scene, object identity, and required sequence of subtasks. For example, a command to pick up and transport a box requires more than a single manipulation action; it involves approaching the object, positioning the body, grasping it, moving to a destination, and releasing it.

Second, the low-level controller must produce physically feasible whole-body behavior. The controller must account for balance, foot placement, joint limits, contact, object motion, and the robot’s dynamics. RL is well suited to this control problem, but conventional low-level policies generally require structured numerical goals rather than natural-language instructions.

Third, the interface between planning and control must be sparse but expressive. A complete motion trajectory provides too much information and limits generalization, while a generic language command provides insufficient geometric detail for a controller. The paper proposes that keyframes provide a suitable compromise: they encode semantic stages using target robot and object poses, while allowing the learned policy to fill in the continuous motion between them.

A further objective is to improve the distribution of low-level training goals. The authors argue that uniformly sampled keyframes from manipulation trajectories tend to overrepresent periods of walking or approaching and underrepresent important transition events such as grasping, lifting, or placing. They therefore introduce a saliency-based sampling method intended to expose the policy more frequently to task-critical states.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Hierarchical System Architecture

KINO consists of four main components: a VLM planner, a keyframe library, a scene retargeting module, and a keyframe-conditioned whole-body RL policy.

The user supplies a language instruction, while the system provides the VLM with a third-person visual observation and structured execution context. The VLM selects the next keyframe from a predefined library. The selected keyframe is then adapted to the current scene, including the observed position, orientation, and dimensions of the object. Finally, the RL policy executes the resulting goal.

The VLM is not required to predict continuous actions. It returns a constrained JSON response containing the keyframe name, whether an object should be manipulated, and whether the task is complete. This restricts the output space and makes the planner directly compatible with the downstream modules.

The planner operates in an event-driven closed loop. A new VLM query is issued after the robot and object become stationary and the previous goal has had sufficient time to execute. The context includes the previous keyframe, whether it succeeded, current robot and object poses, the target object pose, and controller diagnostics such as goal errors. If execution fails, the VLM can resend the same keyframe or choose a recovery keyframe.

#### 3.2. Keyframe-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning Policy

The low-level policy is formulated as a goal-conditioned RL controller. At each control step, the actor receives the robot’s local linear and angular velocity, projected gravity, joint positions and velocities, object position and orientation, the previous action, and the next keyframe. The policy uses a history of the previous ten observations.

A keyframe contains the target root position and orientation, target joint angles, and, when applicable, a target object position and orientation. Object-related targets are set to zero when the keyframe does not involve object manipulation.

The policy uses an asymmetric actor-critic architecture. During training, the critic receives privileged information unavailable to the deployed actor, including reference motion states, body and object states, goal-relative errors, and remaining time to the target. This additional information assists value estimation while preserving an actor input that can be obtained in real-world operation.

Training uses proximal policy optimization (PPO), with rewards divided into three groups: reference tracking, keyframe goal reaching, and regularization. Reference-tracking terms encourage the robot to follow root, joint, body, and object motion from the reference database. The goal reward is activated at the target time and encourages the policy to reach the selected keyframe. Regularization penalizes excessive torque, rapid action changes, joint acceleration, and foot slipping. Separate value functions are used for the three reward groups to reduce interference between objectives.

The policy outputs 29 target joint positions for the Unitree G1 humanoid, which are tracked using a proportional-derivative controller. Domain randomization is applied to friction, robot and object mass, center-of-mass offsets, actuator gains, joint parameters, actuator delay, observation noise, and external pushes to improve transfer from simulation to hardware.

#### 3.3. Saliency-Based Keyframe Sampling

The training data include 52 locomotion clips from AMASS, 105 two-handed box manipulation clips from OmniRetarget, and 120 internally recorded one-handed bucket manipulation clips. The authors augment manipulation motions by varying object positions and orientations and adapting the robot and object trajectories.

Instead of relying only on uniformly spaced keyframes, KINO detects salient events using acceleration. It calculates an aggregate body-acceleration signal, smooths it with a low-pass filter, and identifies local maxima. These maxima often correspond to rapid transitions such as lifting or placing.

The method then removes candidates caused primarily by periodic leg motion. Separate left- and right-leg acceleration profiles are analyzed to identify regular alternating locomotion patterns. Object motion is incorporated by detecting peaks in vertical object acceleration. Robot-event candidates near these object events are retained, and additional object-event candidates are added if needed. The final set includes the start and end frames as well as the filtered robot and object events.

#### 3.4. Scene Retargeting and Path Planning

A library keyframe represents a nominal robot-object relationship from the source motion. To adapt it to a new object, the method aligns the source object frame with the target object frame and scales normalized positions according to the target object’s dimensions. End-effector and robot-root positions are transformed relative to the object, while the root’s vertical position is treated separately.

After transformed end-effector targets are generated, the remaining robot configuration is recovered through iterative damped least-squares inverse kinematics. The solver minimizes end-effector position errors and weighted foot-position residuals, helping maintain ground contact.

For locomotion over distances beyond the training examples, KINO constructs intermediate keyframes along a tangent-aligned circular arc or a shortest valid Dubins path. Curvature is limited to 3 m⁻¹, corresponding to a minimum turning radius of approximately 0.33 meters. Intermediate goals are selected subject to maximum heading changes of 45 degrees and maximum displacements of 2 meters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Performance

The system was evaluated on two pick-transport-place task types: two-handed box manipulation and one-handed bucket manipulation. In simulation, the VLM selected different keyframes according to the object and task description, including one- or two-handed manipulation stages.

For box placement, the robot’s final object position was considered successful when it was within 0.3 meters of the specified target. Success was evaluated over a grid of target positions. The reported success regions extended beyond the spatial contour represented in the training data, indicating that the keyframe-conditioned policy could execute placements at locations not directly covered by the reference trajectories.

The paper does not provide a single aggregate success percentage for this spatial generalization experiment, but it presents the success map as evidence that intermediate path planning and sparse goals can support placement beyond the training distribution.

#### 4.2. Effect of Keyframe Sampling

The main quantitative ablation compares uniform sampling with the proposed saliency-based sampling over 100 trials. The saliency-based approach produced the following success rates:

- Approach: 99%, compared with 97% for uniform sampling.
- Pick: 95%, compared with 63%.
- Place: 93%, compared with 63%.
- End-to-end task: 92%, compared with 44%.

Thus, the largest differences occurred at manipulation stages and in complete task execution. The results support the authors’ argument that uniform sampling underrepresents semantically important events.

Goal-reaching errors also generally improved. For the approach stage, root-position error decreased from 0.125 ± 0.050 meters to 0.113 ± 0.047 meters, and joint RMSE decreased from 0.099 ± 0.007 to 0.075 ± 0.004 radians. During picking, object-position error decreased from 0.170 ± 0.094 to 0.043 ± 0.035 meters. During placing, root-position error decreased from 0.162 ± 0.048 to 0.067 ± 0.018 meters.

The saliency-based method did not improve every metric. Placement object-position error increased from 0.082 ± 0.026 to 0.227 ± 0.043 meters. The authors attribute this to an unstable reference placement pose in which the box was too close to the robot. The policy compensated by matching the robot root position while placing the object farther away.

#### 4.3. Hardware Demonstrations

The complete pipeline was deployed on a Unitree G1 humanoid. Robot state was obtained from onboard sensing, while object-relative pose was measured using an external motion-capture system. The hardware demonstrations included two-handed box pickup and placement and one-handed bucket pickup and transport.

The reported VLM, Qwen3.6:27B, produced keyframe outputs in less than 0.2 seconds on a single RTX 4090 GPU. Mean retargeting time was approximately 5 milliseconds. These timings allowed the high-level planner to operate without imposing a substantial delay between subtasks. The experiments also demonstrated VLM-guided recovery after a keyframe failure by resending a prior goal.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

KINO contributes a concrete interface between semantic task planning and continuous humanoid control. Its keyframes preserve enough structure for whole-body execution while avoiding the need for the VLM to generate high-frequency actions. This separation may reduce the amount of robot-specific data required for high-level planning compared with end-to-end VLA systems.

The saliency-based sampling result is also relevant to RL training design. The improvement from 44% to 92% in reported end-to-end simulation success suggests that the distribution of goal states can substantially affect whether a sparse-goal policy handles long-horizon manipulation. The retargeting mechanism further provides a way to reuse a limited set of reference motions across objects with different poses and dimensions.

The work remains limited by its manually constructed and labeled keyframe library. Scaling to many objects and tasks would require automated extraction and semantic labeling. In addition, the planner currently depends on third-person images and external motion capture for object pose estimation. Real-world operation using only onboard cameras, especially egocentric RGB-D sensing, would introduce additional perception and uncertainty challenges. The experiments also focus on a narrow set of box and bucket tasks, and the hardware results are primarily demonstrations rather than a broad statistical evaluation.

Overall, the paper presents a hierarchical design that connects VLM reasoning, geometric retargeting, and RL-based whole-body control. Its potential impact lies in providing a reusable mechanism for expanding humanoid task repertoires without requiring a separate end-to-end action dataset for every new instruction or object configuration.
