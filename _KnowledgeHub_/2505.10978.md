# Group-in-Group Policy Optimization for LLM Agent Training

> [!summary] Summary
> GiGPO introduces a two-level advantage estimation structure to improve Large Language Model agent training in complex, multi-turn environments. This reinforcement learning algorithm consistently surpasses prior RL methods and prompting techniques across diverse benchmarks like ALFWorld and WebShop, demonstrating enhanced success rates and tool efficiency with negligible additional computational cost.

## Problem

- Existing group-based RL algorithms, while efficient for large-scale LLM training, are primarily limited to single-turn tasks.
- Multi-turn, long-horizon environments present a significant 'credit assignment problem' due to sparse or delayed rewards across many interaction steps.
- Current methods fail to provide the fine-grained, per-step feedback necessary for effective policy refinement in complex, multi-step agent interactions.

## Method

- Introduces **GiGPO**, a Group-in-Group Policy Optimization algorithm featuring a ==two-level advantage estimation== structure for LLM agents.
- Macro credit assignment utilizes episode-level relative advantages based on total trajectory returns, guiding overall task performance.
- Micro credit assignment dynamically forms step-level groups from recurring 'anchor states' to compute relative advantages for individual actions based on discounted returns, enabling fine-grained feedback without extra LLM rollouts.

## Results

- GiGPO agents achieved over **90%** success on ALFWorld and demonstrated >**13%** performance gain over GRPO for **1.5B** models, consistently outperforming other RL methods and prompting techniques.
- The algorithm showed strong average accuracy (**47.2%** for **7B** models) and improved tool efficiency on WebShop and multi-turn search-augmented QA tasks.
- Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of both episode-level and step-level advantages, with the step-level grouping mechanism introducing minimal computational overhead, less than **0.002%** of total training time.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Effective multi-turn LLM agent training requires a hierarchical approach to credit assignment, combining both episode-level and fine-grained step-level feedback.
> - Recurring environment states across trajectories can be leveraged to efficiently construct step-level action groups for micro-level advantage estimation, minimizing computational cost.
> - The core benefits of group-based reinforcement learning (critic-free, low-memory, stable convergence) can be maintained while enabling precise policy refinement in complex, long-horizon tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models has rapidly evolved from static question-answering systems to versatile, autonomous agents capable of perceiving, reasoning, and acting in open-ended environments. This transformation has led to LLMs powering applications such as embodied assistants in simulated homes, web navigators, and explorers in interactive games. A critical component in enabling these advanced capabilities is Reinforcement Learning, which has become a standard recipe for post-training LLMs, yielding frontier models like OpenAI o1 and DeepSeek R1.

Within RL, **group-based RL algorithms** such as RLOO, GRPO, and DAPO have emerged as particularly effective for large-scale LLM training. These methods offer appealing properties: they are critic-free, require low memory, and exhibit stable convergence, making them scalable to large models and batch sizes. Their success, however, has primarily been confined to **single-turn tasks**, such as mathematical reasoning or code generation, where rewards are immediate and credit assignment is straightforward.

The present work directly addresses a critical limitation of these existing group-based RL methods: their **limited scalability to multi-turn LLM agent training**. In multi-turn environments, agent-environment interactions unfold over many steps, often characterized by sparse or delayed rewards. This makes the crucial task of assigning credit to individual actions across a long sequence of steps (known as the credit assignment problem) significantly more challenging. Naively applying existing group-based RL algorithms in such settings often collapses step-level distinctions, undermining their effectiveness. For instance, RAGEN, which uses a trajectory-level GRPO, faces scalability challenges in long-horizon tasks like ALFWorld.

GiGPO positions itself as a solution to this gap. It aims to introduce **fine-grained, per-step credit assignment** for multi-turn LLM agents *while simultaneously preserving* the core advantages of group-based RL—being critic-free, low-memory, and exhibiting stable convergence. This differentiates GiGPO from classical RL algorithms like PPO, which require additional value networks and incur higher computational overhead, or other complex approaches involving Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) as seen in some works like ArCHer and AgentQ. By building directly on the strengths of group-based RL and extending them to multi-turn scenarios, GiGPO represents a significant step towards more efficient and effective training of sophisticated LLM agents for complex, long-horizon tasks. The paper also highlights that GiGPO's hierarchical structure is orthogonal to other group-based RL advancements, suggesting it can integrate with and enhance existing techniques like DAPO.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to overcome the inherent limitations of existing group-based reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms when applied to the training of Large Language Model (LLM) agents in multi-turn, long-horizon environments. The authors precisely articulate the core problem: current group-based RL methods, while effective for single-turn tasks due to their critic-free, low-memory, and stable convergence properties, struggle with **fine-grained credit assignment** in scenarios where actions unfold over many steps and rewards are sparse or delayed.

This critical challenge stems from the nature of LLM agents operating in external environments. Their interactions can span dozens of decision steps and thousands of tokens, making it difficult to attribute success or failure to specific intermediate actions. A single, episode-level reward—as used in vanilla group-based RL—provides insufficient feedback to accurately adjust the policy at individual steps. The authors pose the fundamental question that motivates their work: **"Can we preserve the critic-free, low-memory, and stable convergence properties of group-based RL while introducing fine-grained credit assignment for multi-turn LLM agent training?"**

To address this, the paper's key motivation is to design an RL algorithm that can provide a more granular understanding of an agent's performance, not just at the end of an episode, but at each critical decision point. This fine-grained feedback is essential for refining an agent's policy in complex tasks that demand long-horizon planning and decision-making. GiGPO is proposed as the solution, aiming to achieve this without introducing the computational complexity (e.g., auxiliary value models, additional rollouts) that has historically plagued other RL approaches for multi-turn agent training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GiGPO, or Group-in-Group Policy Optimization, introduces a novel two-level advantage estimation structure to enable fine-grained credit assignment for LLM agents while retaining the efficiency benefits of group-based RL.

The methodology begins with a standard problem setup: an LLM agent, parameterized by policy $\pi_\theta$, interacts with an environment to complete multi-step tasks. At each time step $t$, the agent observes a state $s_t$, generates a textual action $a_t$, and receives a reward $r_t$ and the next state $s_{t+1}$. A complete interaction forms a trajectory $\tau$. The challenge lies in sparse or delayed rewards, making credit assignment difficult.

#### 3.1. Episode-Level Relative Advantages (Macro Credit Assignment)

GiGPO starts by applying a coarse-grained, episode-level credit assignment similar to vanilla GRPO.
1. **Trajectory Collection:** For a given task $x$ and identical initial states, the LLM agent, using its current policy $\pi_{\theta_{old}}$, rolls out $N$ complete trajectories, forming a group $\{\tau_i\}_{i=1}^N$.
2. **Total Return:** Each trajectory $\tau_i$ receives a total return $R(\tau_i) = \sum_{k=1}^T r_k^{(i)}$, serving as a holistic measure of task completion. In sparse reward settings, this often simplifies to a binary success/failure signal.
3. **Episode Group:** These trajectories and their returns are organized into an episode-level group $G_E$.
4. **Episode Relative Advantage ($A_E$):** For each $\tau_i$, its advantage $A_E(\tau_i)$ is computed by normalizing its total return $R(\tau_i)$ against the group's mean and a normalization factor $F_{norm}$. The paper explores using standard deviation (as in GRPO) or a fixed factor of 1 (which results in a rescaled Leave-One-Out estimator, RLOO).
This macro-level advantage guides the policy to develop coherent, trajectory-wide behaviors that maximize overall task performance.

#### 3.2. Step-Level Relative Advantages (Micro Credit Assignment)

To provide fine-grained feedback, GiGPO introduces a novel mechanism to form step-level groups without incurring additional LLM rollouts.
1. **Anchor State Grouping:** The key insight is that in groups of trajectories starting from identical conditions, many environment states naturally recur across episodes or even within a single trajectory. GiGPO identifies all *distinct environment states* that appear in the collected trajectories, labeling them as "anchor states" ($\tilde{s}$).
2. **Dynamic Group Construction:** For each unique anchor state $\tilde{s}$, a step-level group $G_S(\tilde{s})$ is dynamically constructed. This group comprises all instances of actions $a_t^{(i)}$ taken from that specific anchor state $\tilde{s}$ across all trajectories. This process is offline and involves lightweight key-based grouping using hashmaps, thus avoiding any extra LLM forward passes.
3. **Discounted Return for Steps:** Instead of relying solely on immediate rewards $r_t^{(i)}$, each action $a_t^{(i)}$ within $G_S(\tilde{s})$ is associated with its discounted future return $R_t^{(i)} = \sum_{k=t}^T \gamma^{k-t} r_k^{(i)}$, where $\gamma$ is the standard RL discount factor. This captures the long-term impact of an action.
4. **Step Relative Advantage ($A_S$):** For each action $a_t^{(i)}$ in $G_S(\tilde{s})$, its step relative advantage $A_S(a_t^{(i)})$ is calculated by normalizing its discounted return $R_t^{(i)}$ against the mean and $F_{norm}$ of returns within that specific step-level group $G_S(\tilde{s})$.
This micro-level advantage provides precise feedback on the relative quality of individual actions taken from the same environment state, crucial for refining decisions in long-horizon tasks.

#### 3.3. Group-in-Group Policy Optimization Objective

The two levels of advantage signals are combined into a single "group-in-group" advantage:
$A(a_t^{(i)}) = A_E(\tau_i) + \omega \cdot A_S(a_t^{(i)})$
where $\omega \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$ is a weighting coefficient that balances the contribution of episode-level and step-level advantages. This combined advantage guides a clipped policy optimization objective, similar to PPO, which includes an importance sampling ratio and a KL-divergence penalty to maintain proximity to a reference policy $\pi_{ref}$. This ensures stable updates while leveraging the fine-grained credit signals.

The GiGPO algorithm is summarized in Algorithm 1 (Appendix D), highlighting that the additional steps for step-level grouping and advantage computation are lightweight, preserving the overall computational efficiency of group-based RL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluations demonstrate GiGPO's significant capabilities and efficiency across a range of challenging agentic tasks.

#### 4.1. Performance on ALFWorld and WebShop

GiGPO consistently outperforms strong baselines on both the ALFWorld embodied environment and the WebShop web navigation task, using Qwen2.5-1.5B/3B/7B-Instruct models:
- **Superiority over Prompting and Closed-Source LLMs:** GiGPO-trained agents achieve significantly higher success rates than models relying solely on prompting (ReAct, Reflexion) and even surpass the performance of powerful closed-source models like GPT-4o and Gemini-2.5-Pro in many scenarios. For instance, on ALFWorld, GiGPO achieves >86% success for 1.5B and >90% for 7B, significantly higher than Gemini-2.5-Pro's 60.3%.
- **Outperformance of Prior RL Methods:** GiGPO consistently outperforms PPO (an actor-critic method requiring an additional value network), RLOO, and the direct predecessor, GRPO. Specifically, GiGPO
 w/o std
 achieves performance gains of **>13% on ALFWorld and >10% on WebShop over GRPO at 1.5B**, and **>12% on ALFWorld and >9% on WebShop at 7B**. This highlights its effectiveness in providing precise credit assignment in long-horizon tasks.
- **Normalization Factor ($F_{norm}$):** The choice of normalization factor (standard deviation or 1) is found to be task-dependent. For more difficult tasks (e.g., Look, Pick2 in ALFWorld, and WebShop), setting $F_{norm}=1$ (equivalent to a rescaled RLOO) tends to yield higher success rates, suggesting that standard deviation scaling can sometimes exaggerate gradients from difficult samples or imbalanced groups, harming stability.

#### 4.2. Performance on Search-Augmented QA Tasks

GiGPO demonstrates strong and consistent gains on multi-turn search-augmented QA tasks (NQ, TriviaQA, PopQA, HotpotQA, 2Wiki, MuSiQue, Bamboogle), reaching **42.1% (3B) and 47.2% (7B)** average accuracy. It significantly outperforms previous strong baselines like Search-R1 and StepSearch.
- **Tool Efficiency:** GiGPO-trained agents are remarkably tool-efficient, requiring fewer tool calls on average (e.g., ∼0.9 calls for single-hop and ∼1.6 calls for multi-hop tasks with the 7B model), matching state-of-the-art results while suppressing redundant queries effectively.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

An ablation study confirms the criticality of both components of GiGPO's hierarchical advantage:
- **Removing Episode-Level Advantages (GiGPO w/o A_E):** Leads to a substantial performance drop, indicating that the overall trajectory-level signal is essential for long-range coherence.
- **Removing Step-Level Advantages (GiGPO w/o A_S):** Also results in pronounced declines, especially on complex tasks, demonstrating the necessity of fine-grained, per-step feedback for effective policy refinement.
This confirms that the combination of both levels of advantage is the primary driver of GiGPO's performance gains.

#### 4.4. Dynamics of Step-Level Group

Analysis of step-level group dynamics in ALFWorld shows:
- Over **65% of states recur** across trajectories throughout training, validating the premise of anchor state grouping.
- In early training, large group sizes ($|G_S(\tilde{s})| \ge 10$) are frequent, reflecting exploratory, often redundant, or ineffective actions.
- As training progresses, the distribution of group sizes tightens around the rollout group size (N=8), indicating that the agent learns more consistent and purposeful decision-making, avoiding common dead ends and repetitive loops.

#### 4.5. Computational Budget

A detailed breakdown of training time confirms GiGPO's efficiency:
- The additional components for anchor state grouping (hashmap lookups) and step-relative advantage computation (simple arithmetic) incur **minimal overhead (< 0.002% of total per-iteration training time)**.
- GiGPO shares identical GPU memory usage and LLM rollout costs with GRPO, as it remains critic-free and operates with a single actor LLM.
This confirms that GiGPO achieves significant performance gains without compromising the computational efficiency inherent in group-based RL.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Group-in-Group Policy Optimization (GiGPO) marks a significant advancement in the training of Large Language Model (LLM) agents, particularly for long-horizon, multi-turn tasks. Its core significance lies in its ability to **resolve the challenging credit assignment problem** in these complex environments without sacrificing the highly desirable properties of traditional group-based RL algorithms.

The potential impact of GiGPO is substantial:

1. **Enabling Advanced LLM Agents:** By providing fine-grained, per-step credit signals alongside global episode-level feedback, GiGPO allows LLM agents to learn more effectively in environments characterized by sparse or delayed rewards. This fosters the development of more intelligent, robust, and adaptable agents for real-world applications such as embodied AI (e.g., robotics, virtual assistants), web navigation, and sophisticated tool-use scenarios.
2. **Efficiency and Scalability:** GiGPO preserves the critic-free, low-memory, and stable convergence properties that make group-based RL ideal for large-scale LLM training. Its anchor state grouping mechanism is particularly impactful, as it enables fine-grained credit assignment without the prohibitive computational cost of additional rollouts or auxiliary value networks typically required by other RL approaches (like PPO). This makes advanced agent training more accessible and practical, even for models with billions of parameters. The demonstration of minimal additional time cost (< 0.002%) is a crucial validation of its efficiency.
3. **Versatile Applicability:** The method's success across diverse benchmarks—from embodied tasks in ALFWorld, to web interactions in WebShop, to multi-turn tool-augmented QA, and even multimodal settings with VLM agents—highlights its broad applicability. This versatility suggests GiGPO could become a foundational algorithm for training agents in various domains.
4. **Orthogonality and Extensibility:** The paper notes that GiGPO's hierarchical core is orthogonal to existing group-based RL advancements. This means it can be combined with other techniques (e.g., DAPO's dynamic sampling and clipping methods), allowing for further performance enhancements and contributing to a richer ecosystem of RL algorithms for LLMs.
5. **Open-Source Contribution:** The release of the `verl-agent` framework alongside the paper is a significant contribution to the research community. This highly scalable framework for multi-turn LLM agent training will accelerate further research and enable practitioners to experiment with and deploy advanced agent capabilities more readily. It provides a robust platform for future work in this rapidly evolving field.
6. **Insights into Agent Learning:** The analysis of step-level group dynamics offers valuable insights into how LLM agents learn. The observed shift from redundant behaviors to more consistent, purposeful decision-making demonstrates GiGPO's ability to shape complex agent policies effectively.

While the reliance on exact state matching for anchor grouping is a potential limitation in highly noisy or continuous environments, the paper mitigates this by noting that GiGPO gracefully degrades to GRPO in the extreme case of no matches. Furthermore, the mention of similarity-based grouping and future exploration of more robust state-matching strategies (e.g., embedding-based representations) indicates a clear path for continued improvement.

In conclusion, GiGPO represents a significant step forward in making advanced LLM agent training both more effective and more efficient. By enabling fine-grained control over learning without compromising scalability, it broadens the horizons for developing highly capable AI agents that can operate autonomously in complex, dynamic environments.
