# HomeRobot: Open-Vocabulary Mobile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> HomeRobot introduces Open-Vocabulary Mobile Manipulation (OVMM) as a unified problem for household robots, presenting the first reproducible real-world benchmark with a complementary simulation environment and open-source software stack. Baseline experiments reveal that current open-vocabulary perception systems are a primary bottleneck, leading to low overall success rates (e.g., 15-20% in real-world scenarios) for the complex end-to-end task.

## Problem

- Mobile manipulation robots were typically limited to predefined object sets and constrained environments, hindering generalization to novel items and complex multi-room homes.
- A lack of standardized hardware platforms and software stacks made it difficult to reproduce results and compare different approaches fairly across research labs.
- Research in embodied AI often addressed fragmented sub-problems (e.g., navigation or manipulation) rather than integrating perception, navigation, and manipulation for complex, open-vocabulary tasks.

## Method

- Formalizes the Open-Vocabulary Mobile Manipulation (OVMM) task, requiring robots to find, pick, and place any object in any unseen home environment based on natural language commands.
- Establishes the first reproducible benchmark for OVMM, comprising a large-scale simulation environment using Habitat HSSD and a real-world component utilizing the standardized Hello Robot Stretch platform.
- Provides the HomeRobot open-source software framework with unified APIs for both simulated and real-world execution, designed to facilitate modular development and sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- Overall success rates in simulation dropped significantly from 5.1-14.8% with ground-truth perception to 0.4-0.6% when using the DETIC open-vocabulary object detector.
- Real-world experiments showed very low overall success rates (15% for heuristic baseline, 20% for RL baseline), primarily limited by the robustness of open-vocabulary perception systems.
- While reinforcement learning (RL) policies demonstrated improved performance in specific manipulation sub-tasks (Gaze, Pick, Place), integrating these skills with noisy perceptual inputs proved challenging, highlighting the need for more robust, integrated perception-action systems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robust open-vocabulary perception in cluttered, real-world environments is currently the most critical bottleneck for successful end-to-end mobile manipulation.
> - While individual sub-skills like grasping and placement can be improved with learned policies, their integration into a full system reveals the immense challenge posed by noisy perception and the sim-to-real gap.
> - The HomeRobot benchmark serves as a crucial tool for exposing current limitations and driving future research towards more robust and integrated perception-action systems for household robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of household robotic assistants capable of understanding and interacting with human environments has long been a "north star" for the robotics field. This ambition has spurred specialized research in areas such as robot vision, manipulation, navigation, and language understanding. However, the integration of these disparate capabilities into a single, cohesive, and generalizable system remains a formidable challenge. This paper introduces "Open-Vocabulary Mobile Manipulation" (OVMM) as a crucial, holistic problem definition that requires the seamless integration of all these sub-fields.

Prior research in mobile manipulation has often simplified the problem setting due to technical limitations. These simplifications include:
- **Limited Object Sets:** Robots were typically trained and tested on a small, predefined set of objects, hindering generalization to novel items encountered in real homes.
- **Constrained Environments:** Many benchmarks focused on small, single-room environments or used discrete action spaces, which do not reflect the complexity and continuous nature of real-world multi-room homes.
- **Lack of Reproducibility:** Varied robot platforms, custom environments, and proprietary software stacks made fair comparison and reproduction of results across different labs incredibly difficult.

Recent advancements, particularly in vision-language models like CLIP and large language models (LLMs), have opened new avenues for robots to generalize beyond pre-defined categories and understand open-vocabulary commands. However, these successes have largely been demonstrated in isolated tasks (e.g., open-vocabulary navigation or manipulation) and still suffer from the reproducibility challenge.

HomeRobot addresses these limitations head-on. It positions itself as the **first reproducible mobile manipulation benchmark for the real world with an associated simulation component**. Its key differentiators and contributions to the broader research landscape are:
- **Holistic OVMM Definition:** It defines and tackles the full problem of finding, picking, and placing *any* object in *any unseen* home environment, moving beyond fragmented sub-problems.
- **Large-Scale, Diverse Environments:** Leverages the Habitat Synthetic Scenes Dataset (HSSD) with 200+ human-authored, cluttered, multi-room 3D home scenes for simulation, offering a significant leap in environmental complexity compared to prior works (e.g., Habitat 2.0, ALFRED, RoboTHOR).
- **Open Object Sets:** Crucially, it incorporates a large and diverse object dataset (2,535 objects from 129 categories) with explicit splits for seen vs. unseen categories and instances, pushing generalization capabilities.
- **Standardized Hardware for Reproducibility:** By focusing on the affordable and compliant Hello Robot Stretch, which is already used in many research labs, HomeRobot provides a common hardware platform. This is a significant improvement over challenges where participants use varied custom robots.
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The benchmark is explicitly designed to support sim-to-real transfer, providing identical APIs across both simulated and real-world settings. This bridges the notorious sim-to-real gap, which has historically limited the practical impact of simulated robotics research.
- **Comprehensive Software Stack:** The HomeRobot library offers a multi-purpose robotics stack for the Hello Robot Stretch, not just for OVMM, but also for sub-tasks like navigation, object-goal navigation, and manipulation learning. This is unique compared to other benchmarks that might provide only a simulation environment or specific datasets.

In essence, HomeRobot aims to consolidate and advance the field by providing the necessary infrastructure, benchmarks, and a common platform to foster reproducible research on complex, integrated mobile manipulation tasks in realistic home environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching motivation behind HomeRobot is the long-held aspiration to develop **truly useful household robotic assistants**. For robots to be effective helpers in human environments, they must possess a set of foundational capabilities that, when integrated, enable them to perform everyday tasks with a high degree of adaptability. This leads directly to the core problem defined in this paper: **Open-Vocabulary Mobile Manipulation (OVMM)**.

The key objectives of this research are multi-faceted:

1. **Define and Formalize OVMM:** To establish a clear, challenging, and relevant task definition for the robotics community, expressed as "Move (object) from the (start\_receptacle) to the (goal\_receptacle)" with novel objects in unseen environments.
2. **Develop a Reproducible Benchmark:** To provide a standardized evaluation framework for OVMM, addressing the common challenge of reproducibility in robotics research. This involves:
 - **Simulation Component:** Creating a large-scale, diverse, and interactive simulation environment using Habitat and the HSSD dataset with a wide variety of objects and multi-room layouts. This allows for rapid iteration, training, and testing.
 - **Real-World Component:** Establishing an equivalent real-world benchmark in a controlled apartment environment using a standard, affordable hardware platform (Hello Robot Stretch) to enable practical validation and deployment.
3. **Introduce the HomeRobot Software Framework:** To open-source a comprehensive software stack that facilitates research in both simulated and physical environments. This framework is designed with identical APIs across sim and real settings to enable seamless transfer of policies and supports modular development of perception, navigation, and manipulation components.
4. **Implement and Evaluate Baseline Policies:** To provide initial heuristic and reinforcement learning (RL) baselines for the OVMM task, demonstrating their performance in both simulation and the real world. This serves to highlight current capabilities and, crucially, identify key bottlenecks and areas for future improvement.
5. **Drive Future Research:** By establishing a robust benchmark and an accessible platform, the authors aim to encourage further research into full-stack integrated mobile manipulation systems, particularly focusing on how to bridge the sim-to-real gap and improve generalization with open-vocabulary perception.

The core motivation is to move beyond isolated sub-problems in robotics and enable the development of truly capable mobile manipulators that can assist humans with arbitrary tasks in complex, unstructured home environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HomeRobot project employs a two-pronged approach: a comprehensive **simulation benchmark** and a complementary **real-world benchmark** supported by a unified **software framework**.

#### 3.1. The HomeRobot OVMM Benchmark

- **Task Definition:** The OVMM task is formalized as: "Move (object) from the (start\_receptacle) to the (goal\_receptacle)." The robot must navigate to the `start_receptacle`, grasp the specified `object` (which is small and manipulable), then navigate to the `goal_receptacle` (a large piece of furniture), and place the object on it. This requires tackling perception, language understanding, navigation, and manipulation.
- **Robot Platform:** The **Hello Robot Stretch with DexWrist** is chosen for the real-world component. Its affordability (~$25,000 USD), 6 Degrees of Freedom (DoF) manipulation, human-safe design, and widespread use in academia make it an ideal standardized platform for reproducible research.
- **Objects and Receptacles:**
 - **Objects:** A large dataset of 2,535 manipulable household objects from 129 categories. These are split into "seen" (categories/instances present in training) and "unseen" (unseen instances of seen categories or entirely unseen categories) for evaluation, emphasizing open-vocabulary generalization.
 - **Receptacles:** 21 common household receptacle categories (e.g., tables, chairs, sofas), all of which are "seen" during training, but their instances and locations can be novel at test time.
- **Scenes:**
 - **Simulation:** Uses a subset of 60 human-authored 3D home scenes from the Habitat Synthetic Scenes Dataset (HSSD) within the AI Habitat simulator. These are multi-room, cluttered, and realistic. Training, validation, and test splits (38, 12, 10 scenes, respectively) ensure evaluation on unseen environments. Objects are procedurally placed ensuring navigability and reachability.
 - **Real-World:** A controlled 3-room apartment environment with documented layouts and robot start positions to ensure reproducibility. Objects are a mix of seen and unseen categories.
- **Scoring:** Success is broken down into four stages, with partial credit for each: Finding object on `start_receptacle`, successfully picking up object, finding `goal_receptacle`, and placing object on `goal_receptacle`. Overall success requires all four.

#### 3.2. The HomeRobot Library

HomeRobot is an open-source software framework facilitating research on the Stretch robot in both simulated and real environments.
- **Unified APIs:** Provides identical APIs for controlling the robot across both simulation (`home_robot_sim`) and hardware (`home_robot_hw`), simplifying sim-to-real transfer.
- **Modular Design:** Separates perception and action components, allowing researchers to develop and swap out modules for high-level states (e.g., semantic maps, segmented point clouds) and high-level actions (e.g., go-to-goal, pick-object).
- **Architecture:** A desktop PC with a GPU handles computationally intensive perception and planning, while the Stretch robot runs low-level control and SLAM, connected via a dedicated consumer-grade router for low-latency communication.

#### 3.3. Baseline Agent Implementation

Two families of agents are implemented as baselines for OVMM, using a high-level state-machine (`OVMMAgent`) that calls a sequence of skills:
1. **FindObj/FindRec:** Locating the target object on the start receptacle, or the goal receptacle.
2. **Gaze:** Moving close to the object and orienting the head for a good grasp view.
3. **Pick:** Picking up the object.
4. **Place:** Placing the object on the goal receptacle.

- **Perception Backend:** Both baselines rely on **DETIC**, an open-vocabulary object detector, to provide object segmentation from RGB images at test time.
- **Heuristic Baseline:**
 - **Navigation:** Extends prior work using a semantic mapping module (Hector SLAM for pose, DETIC for segmentation, 2D semantic map construction), a frontier-based exploration policy, and a Fast Marching Method based navigation planner. It prioritizes finding the object on the start receptacle, then exploring frontiers.
 - **Grasping:** A simple, heuristic top-down grasping policy based on voxelizing the object's point cloud and identifying clear paths for the gripper fingers. This was chosen for transparency, reproducibility, and ease of installation over more complex learned models.
 - **Placement:** A heuristic placement strategy that projects the detected receptacle segmentation mask onto a point cloud and chooses a stable surface location for object release.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Baseline:**
 - Each of the four core skills (FindObj, FindRec, Gaze, Place) is trained as a separate policy using **DDPPO** in the Habitat simulator.
 - **Observation Space:** Policies receive depth, ground-truth semantic segmentation (during training), proprioceptive sensors (joints, gripper state), and CLIP embeddings of goal objects. RGB is available but not directly used by the RL policies during training.
 - **Action Space:** Discrete actions for navigation skills (FindObj, FindRec), and continuous actions for manipulation skills (Gaze, Place) to allow for fine-grained control.
 - **Reward Function:** Dense rewards are used for distance reduction and alignment for navigation and gaze, while sparse rewards are given for successful pick and place actions.
 - **Manipulation Mode:** For both baselines, the Stretch robot transitions between "navigation mode" (camera forward) and "manipulation mode" (camera towards arm, base as lateral DoF) when performing pick and place.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provide crucial insights into the performance of current approaches for OVMM and highlight the significant challenges that remain.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results (Table 3)

- **Ground Truth Perception vs. DETIC:** A dramatic drop in performance is observed when switching from ground-truth (GT) semantic segmentation (simulator-provided perfect masks) to DETIC segmentation (real-world perception noise).
 - With GT perception, overall success rates range from 5.1% to 14.8%.
 - With DETIC, overall success rates plummet to 0.4% to 0.6%. This stark difference underscores that **perception is currently the primary bottleneck** for end-to-end mobile manipulation.
- **RL vs. Heuristic Baselines:**
 - **Navigation (FindObj/FindRec):** With GT perception, RL policies generally outperform heuristic methods in navigation efficiency. However, when using noisy DETIC input, the **heuristic FindObj policy sometimes outperforms RL**, suggesting its robustness to noisy predictions, possibly by aggregating information in a 2D semantic map. RL navigation struggled with long-horizon exploration when object cues were absent.
 - **Manipulation (Gaze/Pick/Place):** RL policies show superior performance in `Pick` and `Place` with GT perception, particularly benefiting from the learned `Gaze` policy, which helps achieve better alignment for grasping.
 - **Overall:** Despite better sub-skill performance, integrating these skills with noisy perception proves incredibly challenging, leading to very low overall success rates with DETIC. The low rates (even with GT) highlight the inherent difficulty of the full OVMM task.
- **Skill Invocation and Efficiency (Table 5 & 6):**
 - RL-trained navigation (FindObj) policies, while sometimes more efficient if cues are present, can take many more steps (up to 539.1 average steps) with DETIC perception, implying they "give up" or get lost when perception is unreliable. Heuristic methods tend to terminate faster or explore more systematically.
 - The learned Gaze and Place policies, while effective with GT, also struggle significantly with DETIC input, leading to prolonged execution times or failures.
- **Seen vs. Unseen Object Categories (Table 7):** Because both baselines rely on DETIC for open-vocabulary perception, which is pre-trained on a vast dataset, there is no dramatic difference in performance between "seen category, unseen instance" (SC,UI) and "unseen category, unseen instance" (UC,UI) objects. The generalization comes from the pre-trained model rather than specific training on the HomeRobot dataset.

#### 4.2. Real-World Results (Table 4)

- **Overall Success:** Real-world success rates are extremely low for both baselines: 15% for heuristic and 20% for RL. This mirrors the low performance seen in simulation with DETIC and validates the sim-to-real gap, primarily driven by perception challenges.
- **RL slightly better:** The RL baseline achieved a slightly higher success rate (20% vs. 15%) primarily due to better performance in the `Pick` and `Place` sub-tasks. The learned `Gaze` skill in RL helped achieve better alignment for grasping, and the `Place` skill was more precise, reducing instances of objects falling off receptacles.
- **Perception as Dominant Failure Mode:** As in simulation, misclassification and unreliable detection by DETIC were significant causes of failure in the real world. Near-miss collisions also contributed to failures.

**Summary of Findings:**
The results clearly indicate that HomeRobot OVMM is an extremely challenging benchmark. While RL shows promise for improving specific sub-skills like grasping alignment and precise placement, the overall success of both heuristic and RL approaches is severely limited by the performance of current open-vocabulary perception systems when deployed in noisy, complex real-world environments. The gap between ground-truth and real-world perception is immense, underscoring the need for more robust, integrated perception-action systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "HomeRobot: Open-Vocabulary Mobile Manipulation" paper makes several significant contributions to the field of embodied AI and robotics, with substantial potential impact:

1. **Defines a Crucial Frontier Problem:** By formalizing Open-Vocabulary Mobile Manipulation (OVMM) as a holistic task, the paper clearly articulates a key next step for developing truly useful household robots. This integrated problem requires simultaneous advancements across perception, navigation, language understanding, and manipulation, pushing the boundaries of current research.
2. **Establishes the First Reproducible Real-World Benchmark for OVMM:** This is perhaps the most impactful contribution. The lack of standardized hardware and environments has long hampered fair comparisons and progress in robotics. By adopting the affordable Hello Robot Stretch and defining a controlled real-world testbed, HomeRobot enables researchers worldwide to replicate experiments and directly compare algorithms, fostering faster innovation.
3. **Provides a Comprehensive Open-Source Software Stack:** The HomeRobot library, with its unified APIs for both simulation and real-world execution, significantly lowers the barrier to entry for mobile manipulation research. This accelerates development cycles, allows for rapid prototyping in simulation, and seamless deployment on hardware, democratizing access to complex robotics research.
4. **Highlights Key Research Bottlenecks:** The extensive baseline experiments, particularly the stark performance drop when moving from ground-truth to noisy real-world perception (DETIC), unequivocally identify **robust open-vocabulary perception in cluttered environments** as the most critical current limitation. This directs future research efforts towards improving perception for real-world deployment, potentially integrating temporal information or 3D scene representations (like ConceptFusion explored in the appendix).
5. **Encourages Integrated System Design:** The low overall success rates of the baselines, even with strong individual sub-skills, demonstrate that the challenge lies not just in optimizing individual components but in their seamless integration and robust performance under uncertainty. This promotes a shift towards end-to-end, full-stack robotic system development.
6. **Catalyst for Future Competitions and Innovations:** The planned NeurIPS 2023 HomeRobot OVMM competition leverages this benchmark to inspire the community to tackle these complex challenges. Such competitions often serve as powerful catalysts for rapid advancements, much like ImageNet revolutionized computer vision or RoboCup influenced robot soccer.

In summary, HomeRobot is more than just a research paper; it's an infrastructure project designed to accelerate progress towards ubiquitous household robots. By defining a clear problem, providing a reproducible benchmark, open-sourcing a robust software framework, and identifying critical challenges, it sets a strong foundation for the next generation of mobile manipulation research and has the potential to significantly impact both academic understanding and practical applications of embodied AI. Future work will likely focus on improving robust perception, long-horizon planning, and more complex language understanding to make robots truly useful assistants in our homes.
