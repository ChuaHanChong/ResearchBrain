# Reward as Observation: Learning Reward-Based Policies for Rapid Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> Reward-based policy acts on only a history of reward-action pairs, never raw observations, so it transfers zero-shot between environments that share dynamics, reward and actions but have completely different observations. An LSTM over reward-action history is trained with PPO plus expert guidance. It reaches 66-95% of state-based performance, transfers 2D-to-3D and into Habitat-Sim, and serves as a DAgger teacher.

## Problem

- Standard deep RL policies are tightly coupled to their training observation space, so any camera, rendering or modality change forces full retraining and discards learned task knowledge.
- Robotics transfer work, such as sim-to-real and rapid motor adaptation, mostly targets dynamics shift, leaving fixed-dynamics transfer across arbitrarily different observation spaces largely unaddressed.
- Representation-learning and distillation transfer methods need some alignment or mapping between source and target observations, which fails when the two spaces differ arbitrarily in modality or dimensionality.

## Method

- ==Reward-based MDP==: replaces the observation space with a history of recent reward-action pairs, making source and target MDPs identical whenever they share reward and action spaces.
- ==LSTM reward-action encoder== trained with ==PPO== plus a ==guidance loss== that regresses actions toward a pretrained state-based expert, stabilizing exploration under scalar-only observability.
- ==DAgger transfer==: the reward-based policy is deployed zero-shot in the target environment as a teacher that labels states for an observation-based student, which needs no reward at inference.

## Results

- Reward-based policies reach **95%**, **66%** and **70%** of state-based performance on Pointmass, Cartpole and Car Racing; on Pointmass, performance falls from **98.7%** at 1D to **57%** at 6D.
- Zero-shot transfer under color-palette swaps keeps performance nearly unchanged, for example Cartpole **758.7** vs **755.5**; the same policy transfers 2D-to-3D Car Racing and to Stretch navigation in two HM3D scans.
- As a DAgger teacher, it reaches near-optimal 3D Car Racing within 70K steps where pixel PPO stays slow; with a learned image estimator, reward-based Cartpole scores **736** vs state-based **189** (random **193**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - When only the observation changes, a shared scalar like reward can bridge environments without any observation alignment, turning perception transfer into a far simpler estimation problem.
> - Estimating a one-dimensional reward from images is much more data-efficient than recovering the full state, so transfer interfaces should target the lowest-dimensional sufficient signal.
> - Reward-only control requires dense rewards and degrades roughly exponentially with state dimension, so it suits low-dimensional tasks as a teacher, not as a general final policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Reusing a policy without its visuals

A policy trained on one kind of input may be unusable when the same task is presented through a different observation space, forcing costly retraining. Byrd et al. give the transferable policy neither camera image. It reads only recent rewards and its own actions. If source and target share the task’s reward and actions, the policy can be reused without aligning their observations. In the authors’ concrete contrast, a policy is trained on a cheap 2D racing view. The same reward-and-action policy then runs in a 3D view, or in a simulated scanned-home setting, without being fed the new visuals. The clean 2D-to-3D racing test deliberately matches transition dynamics.

Project overview video. The authors describe the demonstration as training in a cheap 2D simulator and deploying unchanged in a 3D renderer and on a Stretch robot in scanned homes.

### 2. A policy that reads the task signal

A reward-based policy receives a history of recent reward/action pairs and emits an action. It does not take the original observation as input. The authors’ proposed rationale is that reward changes in response to actions can guide corrections across visual representations. In a driving example, the policy keeps going when reward remains good and adjusts when it drops. This is intuition for the method, not proof that reward suffices in every task.

For training, an MLP embeds the reward/action pairs, an LSTM processes their temporal sequence, and PPO trains the policy and value networks. A pre-trained observation-based expert also supplies action labels through a supervised guidance loss. This guidance is intended to support productive exploration when reward-only feedback is limited.

Fig. 3: During training, reward/action history feeds the reward-based policy, expert actions provide guidance, and PPO trains the policy. At test time, the policy is used with the reward/action signal. The diagram shows architecture; transferable behavior is the paper’s claim.

The approach has conditions. The signal must be dense and informative, and reward-only control has less information than observation-based control. For Car Racing, the paper reports reward-based performance at 70% of state-based performance.

### 3. From a 2D teacher to target-image learning

Suppose source and target share state, actions, dynamics, and reward. Then the reward/action input sequence is independent of the original observation format, so the reward-based policy can be deployed without observation alignment.

The reward-based policy can also teach a policy that uses the target images. With DAgger (dataset aggregation), the student visits target states and the reward-based teacher labels those visits. The resulting student uses target observations at inference and does not need reward then. In 3D Car Racing, a high-dimensional pixel environment, this student reaches near-optimal performance in 70,000 target-environment steps. The comparison baseline learns from pixels from scratch with a CNN-LSTM. The result shows fewer target training steps. It does not measure elapsed time or total computation, including source training.

Fig. 7: Reported learning curves for 3D Car Racing, comparing DAgger (red) with pixel-based learning from scratch (green). The DAgger curve rises quickly; the from-scratch curve improves more slowly. These are not elapsed-time or total-compute measurements.

### 4. Estimating reward instead of state

If reward is unavailable in the target, one alternative is to estimate the source policy’s full state from target images. On image-observation Cartpole, both estimators used 100,000 supervised target-image samples. In 100-trial evaluations, the policy with a learned five-dimensional state estimator scored 189.41 ± 12.54, near the random policy’s 192.78 ± 36.91. The reward-based policy with a learned reward estimator scored 735.83 ± 65.48, compared with its original-domain score of 836.49 ± 4.46. This supports the authors’ claim that reward estimation was easier in this experiment. Their explanation, that a scalar is easier to estimate than a five-dimensional state, is their interpretation.

### 5. A bridge, not a shortcut

In these experiments, reward/action history carried useful task guidance across visual changes and helped train a target-specific visual policy. The bridge depends on a shared, informative reward and the paper’s transfer assumptions. Transfer when dynamics change remains untested.
