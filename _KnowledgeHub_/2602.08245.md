# STEP: Warm-Started Visuomotor Policies with Spatiotemporal Consistency Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> STEP (Warm-Started Visuomotor Policies with Spatiotemporal Consistency Prediction) drastically reduces the inference latency of diffusion-based visuomotor policies by integrating spatiotemporal consistency prediction and adaptive perturbation. This approach achieves high task success rates with as few as two denoising steps, enabling real-time robotic control with end-to-end latencies as low as 20ms on real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Diffusion policies for robotic control offer strong generative capabilities but suffer from high inference latency due to numerous iterative denoising steps, hindering real-time deployment.
- Existing acceleration methods often compromise action quality, lack consistent low latency, or fail to simultaneously address both spatial (distributional alignment) and temporal (smoothness) consistency.
- Real-world robotic systems using these policies can experience 'execution deadlocks' where minimal action variations lead to robot stagnation, especially with static friction.

## Method

- The **STEP** framework employs a lightweight predictor to warm-start the ==diffusion policy=='s inference at an intermediate step, generating spatiotemporally consistent initial actions.
- A velocity-aware perturbation injection mechanism is introduced to detect and counteract execution stagnation by adaptively adding controlled noise to actions when needed.
- Theoretical analysis demonstrates the local contractivity of the diffusion reverse process when initialized with the warm-start, providing guarantees for stable error reduction and convergence.

## Results

- Achieved success rates comparable to vanilla DDPM (100 steps) using only 2-4 denoising steps in simulation, while significantly improving efficiency and performance over existing acceleration methods.
- Reduced real-world end-to-end inference latency to 20ms on a 5-DoF robot (**105.7x** faster than vanilla DDPM, **4.8x** faster than DDIM), maintaining high success rates (e.g., **95%** PickNPlace with 2 steps).
- The velocity-aware perturbation mechanism reduced average episode execution time by **59%** in real-world tasks and prevented task failures due to robot stagnation, showing optimal performance within specific parameter ranges.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly enforcing both spatial (state-conditioned distributional alignment) and temporal (smoothness between consecutive actions) consistency during warm-start is crucial for maintaining action quality and achieving low latency.
> - Adaptive perturbation, driven by velocity-aware stagnation detection, is essential for robust real-world robotic deployment, preventing execution deadlocks and ensuring continuous actuation.
> - A theoretically grounded warm-start strategy allows diffusion policies to retain their generative expressiveness and high success rates with significantly fewer denoising steps, effectively improving the latency-quality trade-off.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a challenge within the field of visuomotor control in robotics, specifically concerning the deployment of diffusion policies. Diffusion policies have recently emerged as a method for robotic manipulation, offering capabilities such as modeling the distribution of action sequences and capturing multimodality. This contrasts with conventional regression-based policies that typically predict single-step actions. The generative formulation of diffusion policies allows for enhanced action stability and higher success rates in complex tasks by formulating action generation as a progressive denoising process from Gaussian noise towards a target action distribution.

However, a significant limitation of standard diffusion policies is their inference latency. Generating high-quality actions often requires a large number of iterative denoising steps (e.g., up to 100 steps), which hinders their application in real-time, closed-loop robotic control systems. Existing research efforts to accelerate diffusion-based action generation generally fall into three categories:
1. **Accelerated numerical solvers:** Methods like DDIM, DPM-Solver, and DPM-Solver++ aim to reduce sampling steps by reformulating the reverse stochastic diffusion process as a deterministic or higher-order ordinary differential equation (ODE). While these methods reduce iterations, they still require repeated evaluations of the policy network, limiting efficiency in high-frequency control.
2. **Direct prediction approaches:** Techniques such as CP, OneDP, BRIDGER, and NO-Diffusion attempt to bypass iterative denoising with a small number of direct prediction or consistency-based refinement steps. These methods learn to map latent or noisy distributions to target action distributions. However, the capacity of the lightweight predictors used often restricts their ability to perform complex distribution transformations, potentially leading to degraded action quality or reduced robustness.
3. **Action reuse strategies:** Methods like RTI-DP, SDP, RNR-DP, and Falcon exploit temporal continuity by warm-starting the diffusion process with previous actions. This strategy aims to reduce inference iterations by leveraging the smoothness of dynamics. However, their effectiveness can be limited under rapid state changes, as the initializations are not always guaranteed to align with the current system state, potentially compromising action quality.

This work positions itself by observing that existing acceleration methods often struggle to simultaneously preserve action quality and achieve consistently low latency. The proposed STEP method seeks to advance the state of the art by developing a warm-start mechanism that addresses these joint challenges, particularly focusing on ensuring both distributional alignment (spatial consistency) and temporal smoothness across successive control steps, while maintaining the generative capabilities of the original diffusion policy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to reduce the inference latency of diffusion-based visuomotor policies without compromising action quality or stability, thereby enabling their practical deployment in real-time, high-frequency closed-loop control systems. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and addresses limitations identified in the broader research landscape:

1. **Addressing High Inference Latency:** Diffusion policies, despite their generative capabilities, inherently involve an iterative denoising process. This process can require a substantial number of steps (e.g., up to 100 in vanilla DDPM) to produce high-quality action sequences, leading to significant inference latency. This latency is a critical barrier for real-time control, especially on resource-constrained platforms. The research aims to develop a mechanism that drastically reduces the required denoising steps.
2. **Ensuring Action Quality and Robustness:** While various acceleration methods exist, many either reduce sampling steps at the cost of action quality or robustness, or they replace the diffusion process with lightweight predictors that have limited capacity for complex distribution transformations. A key motivation is to maintain the generative expressiveness and high action quality characteristic of full diffusion policies, even with fewer inference steps.
3. **Achieving Consistent Low Latency:** Some existing acceleration methods, particularly action reuse strategies, depend on smooth dynamics and may not reliably guarantee action quality under rapid state changes. This variability in performance implies inconsistent latency or quality. The research aims for a solution that provides consistently low latency across diverse and dynamic scenarios.
4. **Integrating Spatial and Temporal Consistency:** The authors identify that prior work tends to focus on either temporal consistency (smoothness between consecutive actions) or spatial consistency (alignment with the state-conditioned target action distribution), but not both simultaneously.
 - **Temporal warm-starts** (e.g., RTI-DP, Falcon) promote smoothness but may not align with the current state's optimal action distribution, requiring extra denoising steps to correct the mismatch.
 - **Spatial warm-starts** (e.g., BRIDGER) aim for state-conditioned alignment but lack explicit constraints on temporal smoothness, potentially leading to oscillatory behaviors.
 The core motivation is to develop a mechanism that explicitly enforces **spatiotemporal consistency**, ensuring warm-start actions are both smooth over time and distributionally close to the current optimal actions.
5. **Preventing Execution Stagnation in Real-World Scenarios:** In practical robotic deployments, especially with high-frequency control, policies can enter "execution deadlocks" where actions exhibit minimal variation, insufficient to overcome static friction or control dead zones. This leads to the robot stalling. A specific objective is to implement a mechanism to detect and counteract such stagnation, ensuring continuous and effective actuation.
6. **Providing Theoretical Guarantees:** Beyond empirical performance, the authors are motivated to provide a theoretical analysis to support the stability and convergence of their proposed warm-start strategy. Demonstrating that the prediction induces a locally contractive mapping during diffusion refinement would offer robust guarantees for error reduction and stable control.

In summary, the key objectives are to develop a lightweight, warm-started diffusion policy framework that provides rapid, stable, and high-quality visuomotor control by explicitly integrating spatiotemporal consistency prediction and adaptive perturbation, supported by theoretical analysis.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, STEP (Warm-Started Visuomotor Policies with Spatiotemporal Consistency Prediction), enhances diffusion-based visuomotor policies by integrating a spatiotemporal consistency prediction mechanism and a velocity-aware perturbation injection mechanism, complemented by theoretical analysis.

#### 3.1. Spatiotemporal Consistency Framework

The method defines two types of consistency:
- **Temporal Consistency:** This refers to the smoothness of actions across consecutive control steps. A warm-start action $\tilde{a}_t$ at time $t$ satisfies temporal consistency if its difference from the previously executed action $a_{t-1}$ is bounded: $\| \tilde{a}_t - a_{t-1} \| \le \epsilon_t$. This is crucial for physical continuity and stable control frequency.
- **Spatial Consistency:** This characterizes the alignment between the warm-start action and the state-conditioned target action distribution $p(a|s_t)$. A warm-start action $\tilde{a}_t$ satisfies spatial consistency if its distance to the high-probability action manifold $M(s_t)$ of $p(a|s_t)$ is bounded: $\text{dist}(\tilde{a}_t, M(s_t)) \le \epsilon_s$. This ensures the action is relevant to the current system state.

STEP explicitly enforces both spatial and temporal consistency, referred to as **spatiotemporal consistency**, during warm-start initialization.

#### 3.2. Spatiotemporal Consistency Prediction Mechanism

The core of STEP is a lightweight predictor designed to generate high-quality warm-start actions.
- **Predictor Definition:** The predictor $f_{\theta}: \mathcal{O} \times \mathcal{A}^H \rightarrow \mathcal{A}^H$ takes the current observation $o_t$ and the previous timestep's action sequence $A_{t-H}$ (where $H$ is the planning horizon) as input, and outputs a predicted action sequence $\hat{A}_t = f_{\theta}(o_t, A_{t-H})$.
- **Architecture:** The predictor is implemented as a multi-layer Transformer with cross-attention. Historical actions and current observations are first projected into a shared embedding space (128-dimensional). Cross-attention mechanisms then integrate temporal context by using the action sequence as the query and observations as key/value. The output is processed by layer normalization and an output projection to match the action sequence dimensionality. Evaluations indicated that two cross-attention blocks provided good performance with low latency.
- **Training:** The predictor is trained separately in a supervised manner using a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss: $L_{\text{pred}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\| \hat{A}_t - A_t \|_2^2]$. This objective encourages $\hat{A}_t$ to approximate the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[A_t | o_t, A_{t-H}]$.
- **Inference Process:** During inference, the predicted sequence $\hat{A}_t$ is used to warm-start the diffusion policy's reverse process at an intermediate step $K' < K$ (where $K$ is the total denoising steps). Instead of starting from pure Gaussian noise, the diffusion process is initialized with $\tilde{A}_{K'} = \sigma \hat{A}_t + \sigma_t \epsilon_t$, where $\epsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$. The reverse denoising then proceeds from step $K'$ down to 0 to produce the final action sequence $A_t$. The executed sequence $A_t$ is cached for the next timestep's predictor input, maintaining temporal continuity.

#### 3.3. Velocity-aware Perturbation Injection Mechanism

This mechanism is designed to prevent execution deadlocks, particularly in real-world deployments where robots might stall due to insufficient actuation for minimal action variations.
- **Stagnation Detection:** The method measures the action variation between consecutive timesteps, $\Delta A_t = A_{\text{cache}} - A_{t-2H}$. Execution stagnation is detected if the norm of this variation falls below a threshold $\epsilon_a$, indicated by $I_t = \mathbb{I}(\|\Delta A_t\| < \epsilon_a)$.
- **Adaptive Perturbation:** Based on the detected stagnation, the action scaling factor $\sigma$ and perturbation magnitude $\sigma_t$ for the warm-start are adaptively adjusted:
 $\sigma = \begin{cases} \sigma_{\text{scale}}, & \text{if } I_t = 1 \\ 1, & \text{if } I_t = 0 \end{cases}$
 $\sigma_t = \begin{cases} \sigma_{\text{stall}}, & \text{if } I_t = 1 \\ 0, & \text{if } I_t = 0 \end{cases}$
 Here, $\sigma_{\text{scale}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{stall}}$ are hyperparameters. This formulation injects controlled actuation excitation (noise) only when stagnation is detected, otherwise preserving the original policy behavior. A value of $\epsilon_a = 0.01$ is used. $\sigma_{\text{stall}}$ is typically small in simulation but larger in real-world tasks to counteract static friction and dead zones.

#### 3.4. Local Contractivity Analysis

The theoretical analysis provides a guarantee for the convergence of action errors during diffusion refinement when initialized with a warm-start.
- **Unified Reverse Update:** The analysis applies to common diffusion solvers (DDPM, DDIM, DPM-Solver) which can be expressed with a unified reverse update: $A_{k-1} = \mu_k(A_k, o_t) + \xi_k$. The focus is on the contraction property of the mean mapping $\mu_k$.
- **Lipschitz Continuity:** The key assumption is that the denoising network $\epsilon_{\theta}(A, k, o_t)$ is L-Lipschitz continuous in $A$ within a neighborhood of the data manifold. Under this assumption and with standard noise schedules, the reverse posterior mean $\mu_k$ is also shown to be Lipschitz continuous with a constant $c_k < 1$.
- **Contraction Property:** The analysis demonstrates that if the denoising network is Lipschitz continuous and the noise schedule satisfies certain conditions, the reverse diffusion process becomes locally contractive. This implies that the error between the warm-start initialization $\tilde{A}_{K'}$ and the optimal action $A_{K'}$ decays exponentially as the diffusion process proceeds from $K'$ down to 0: $\| \tilde{A}_0 - A_0 \| \le (\prod_{k=1}^{K'} c_k) \| \tilde{A}_{K'} - A_{K'} \|$. The spatiotemporal predictor ensures that the warm-start lies within this contraction neighborhood, enabling stable and efficient inference with reduced denoising steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of STEP was conducted on nine simulated benchmarks (Push-T, five RoboMimic tasks, three ManiSkill2 tasks) and two real-world robotic tasks (PickNPlace, StackCube). The evaluation focused on task success rate (Score) and inference latency (Time).

#### 4.1. Simulation Results (State-based and Image-based)

- **Comparison with Standard Diffusion (DDPM):** STEP with 2 steps achieved success rates comparable to vanilla DDPM (100 denoising steps) on the RoboMimic benchmark for both state-based and image-based conditions. On the contact-rich Push-T benchmark, STEP with 4 steps approached DDPM's score. This indicates that STEP can preserve performance while significantly improving efficiency.
- **Comparison with Numerical Solvers (DDIM, DPM-Solver++):**
 - Against DDIM, STEP with 4 steps showed comparable performance and inference speed, and notably higher scores on challenging tasks like RoboMimic ToolHang. With 2 steps, STEP maintained stable performance while achieving lower latency.
 - Against DPM-Solver++, STEP consistently delivered substantially better performance with both 2 and 4 steps. Even in extreme settings like single-step denoising on ManiSkill2, STEP demonstrated a higher probability of success compared to DPM-Solver++.
- **Comparison with Distillation Methods (CP):** STEP achieved slightly higher scores on simpler tasks with similar inference time budgets and provided substantial improvements (21-56% score gains) on more challenging tasks like Push-T and ToolHang compared to CP. The authors also highlight that STEP is more parameter-efficient than methods like OneDP, requiring fewer trainable parameters for comparable or better performance (0.98M for STEP vs. 251.51M for OneDP).
- **Comparison with Noise Prediction (BRIDGER):** STEP achieved higher average scores with 2 and 4 steps on RoboMimic benchmarks. Specifically, on Push-T and ToolHang, STEP showed 2-56% higher scores, suggesting increased robustness and generalization under aggressive step reduction.
- **Comparison with Action Reuse (RTI-DP, Falcon, RNR-DP):** On RoboMimic, STEP generally achieved higher scores and lower inference latency than RTI-DP and Falcon. On ManiSkill2, STEP achieved lower inference latency than RNR-DP for comparable scores, noting that STEP generates 8 actions per inference while RNR-DP generates one.
- **Overall Pareto Frontier Advancement:** The results across various baselines indicate that STEP consistently improved the trade-off between inference latency and success rate, allowing for high performance with fewer denoising steps.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments

- **Tasks and Setup:** Experiments were conducted on a 5-DoF SO-ARM101 robot with a 1-DoF gripper and RGB camera for PickNPlace and StackCube tasks. Policies were trained on 20 teleoperated demonstrations and evaluated over 20 episodes. Inference was performed on an NVIDIA RTX 2050 GPU.
- **Performance:** Vanilla DDPM achieved 100% success on both tasks but with high latency (approx. 4200-4300ms). DDIM with 8 steps maintained high success but collapsed at 4 or fewer steps. In contrast, STEP consistently achieved high task success rates (e.g., 95% on PickNPlace and 80% on StackCube with 2 steps) with significantly lower end-to-end inference latency (20ms). This resulted in speedups of 105.7x over vanilla DDPM and 4.8x over DDIM (at comparable success rates).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Velocity-aware Perturbation Injection:** Real-world experiments demonstrated that this mechanism significantly reduced the average episode execution time by 59% (e.g., from 32s to 20s for PickNPlace with 2 steps). Without perturbation ($\sigma_{\text{stall}}=0$), the policy consistently failed due to execution stagnation. The study identified an optimal range for $\sigma_{\text{stall}}$ (e.g., 1.0-1.4 for 100% success) balancing exploration and control stability. It also showed that a moderate predictor retention ratio ($\sigma_{\text{scale}}=0.2 \text{ or } 0.4$) combined with appropriate perturbation yielded higher success rates.
- **Consistency Types:** An ablation on average success rate with 2 steps (Image-based, RoboMimic) demonstrated that Spatiotemporal Consistency (STC) achieved an average score of 0.69, outperforming Spatial Consistency (SC) at 0.53 and Temporal Consistency (TC) at 0.36. This supports the hypothesis that combining both forms of consistency is beneficial.
- **Predictor Error and K' Selection:** The empirical validation of Lipschitz continuity and contraction coefficient $c_k < 1$ confirmed the theoretical guarantees. An analysis of the predictor's Mean Action Relative Error (MARE) and MSE bounds guided the selection of optimal warm-start steps $K'$, typically found to be in the range of 1 to 4 steps.

#### 4.4. Generalization

- **Transformer-based Diffusion Policy:** STEP showed improved success rates (e.g., 0.98 vs 0.46 on Push-T, 0.99 vs 0.77 on Lift) when applied to a Transformer-based diffusion policy with 2 steps compared to DDIM.
- **Flow-matching-based VLA ($\pi_{0.5}$):** With 1 denoising step plus STEP, the method achieved comparable average scores (0.963) to a 10-step baseline (0.972) on various VLA tasks (Spatial, Object Goal, Long).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper, STEP, offers a notable advancement in making diffusion-based visuomotor policies more practical for real-world robotic applications. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging the Performance-Efficiency Gap:** The primary impact is the demonstration that high-quality, multimodal action generation from diffusion policies can be maintained while drastically reducing inference latency. By allowing diffusion policies to operate effectively with as few as 2 denoising steps, STEP addresses a critical bottleneck that has limited their deployment in real-time, closed-loop robotic control. This effectively pushes the Pareto frontier, enabling simultaneous improvements in both performance and efficiency.
2. **Enabling On-Device Deployment:** The significantly reduced inference latency (e.g., 20ms on an RTX 2050 GPU for real-world tasks) makes diffusion policies viable for execution on resource-constrained edge devices. This capability is crucial for ubiquitous deployment of embodied intelligence systems where low-power, high-frequency control is required without relying on powerful off-board computation.
3. **Enhanced Robustness through Spatiotemporal Consistency:** The explicit integration of both spatial and temporal consistency in the warm-start mechanism contributes to more stable and robust policy execution. This structured initialization ensures that the diffusion process starts closer to a valid, smooth, and task-relevant action distribution, which is particularly beneficial under aggressive step reduction or dynamic environmental changes. This contrasts with methods that only address one aspect of consistency or rely on less informed warm-starts.
4. **Improved Real-World Reliability with Adaptive Perturbation:** The velocity-aware perturbation injection mechanism is a practical innovation that directly addresses a common failure mode in real-world robotics: execution stagnation due to minimal actuation. By adaptively introducing excitation only when needed, the method ensures continuous and effective robot motion, significantly enhancing task completion rates and overall reliability in physical environments where factors like static friction are prevalent.
5. **Theoretical Foundation for Warm-Starting:** The theoretical analysis demonstrating local contractivity of the diffusion reverse process with the warm-start provides a mathematical justification for the observed empirical gains. This theoretical grounding offers confidence in the stability and convergence properties of the method, indicating that initialization errors decay exponentially, which is important for dependable control system design.
6. **Generalizability and Complementary Approach:** The demonstrated effectiveness of STEP across diverse simulation benchmarks, real-world tasks, and its generalization to different policy architectures (e.g., Transformer-based diffusion, flow-matching-based VLA models) indicates its broad applicability. The method's design as a post-hoc acceleration technique that does not require retraining the base diffusion policy suggests it can complement other advancements in diffusion models, potentially being combined with other model compression or flow-matching techniques for even further gains.

In conclusion, STEP represents a practical and theoretically grounded approach to accelerate diffusion policies, making them more suitable for real-time robotic manipulation. Its impact extends to enabling wider adoption of these powerful generative models in robotics by resolving key efficiency and reliability challenges.
