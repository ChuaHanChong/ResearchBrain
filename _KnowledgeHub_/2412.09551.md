# Video Creation by Demonstration

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Google DeepMind and Cornell University developed δ-Diffusion, a self-supervised, two-stage approach for video creation by demonstrating actions from a source video onto a target context image. This method enhances action concept transfer and content consistency by using pre-trained video foundation models and an appearance bottleneck, achieving up to 1.00 human preference rate over baselines.

## Problem

- Existing controllable video generation methods rely on explicit signals that are either too abstract or too laborious, limiting user expressiveness for interactive video creation.
- Traditional action transfer techniques struggle with significant misalignment (appearance, subject, environment) between demonstration and target videos, making direct transfer and paired training data collection challenging.
- Generalizing complex, context-dependent actions in diverse video content is difficult, as many advanced video generation methods are domain-specific.

## Method

- Introduced **δ-Diffusion**, a two-stage, ==self-supervised training== approach for "Video Creation by Demonstration," which takes a context image and a demonstration video to generate a new video.
- Developed an "appearance bottleneck" module that uses a pre-trained ==video foundation model== (==VideoPrism==) and a feature predictor to extract action-rich control latents with minimal appearance information.
- Employed a self-supervised training paradigm where the model reconstructs subsequent video frames from an initial frame, applying action-preserving augmentations to the demonstration video to mitigate appearance leakage.

## Results

- The δ-Diffusion appearance bottleneck consistently improved action concept transferability, outperforming raw features and temporal normalization on instance-wise metrics like Hit@k and Embedding Cosine Similarity across multiple datasets.
- Human evaluators favored δ-Diffusion for visual quality, action transferability, and context consistency, showing up to **0.83** preference over WALT (text-to-video) and up to **1.00** preference over MotionDirector.
- The method successfully demonstrated auto-regressive generation, coherently concatenating multiple distinct demonstration videos to simulate complex, multi-step tasks within a consistent environment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging pre-trained video foundation models for extracting implicit latent action controls offers greater flexibility and expressiveness in video generation compared to relying on explicit signals.
> - An "appearance bottleneck" that subtracts per-frame appearance features from spatiotemporal representations effectively isolates action concepts, enabling cleaner action transfer without appearance leakage.
> - A self-supervised training approach, utilizing augmentations to create misalignment between demonstration and target, is crucial for learning robust action transfer when paired training data is scarce.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of generative artificial intelligence, specifically within controllable video generation. It positions itself as an advancement towards building interactive world simulators.

Current controllable video generation methods predominantly rely on explicit control signals such as text prompts, moving keypoints, dense depth maps, or segmentation maps. These explicit signals can either be abstract, thereby limiting user expressiveness, or difficult to acquire, hindering user experience in interactive video creation. This paper introduces an alternative approach utilizing implicit latent control, aiming for increased flexibility and expressiveness in generating general videos.

The work falls into the category of Image+Video-to-Video (I+V2V) generation, where the objective is to animate a still input image conditioned on signals derived from another video. This contrasts with earlier I+V2V methods that typically extract explicit signals like keypoints or embeddings from a conditioning video. The paper notes that extracting implicit signals is comparatively less explored in the existing literature, with examples like MotionDirector, which fine-tunes a model per demonstration, or Genie, which learns reusable latent action codes from video games. $\delta$-Diffusion differentiates itself by generating videos without optimization during inference and by applying to real-world visual domains using more abstract action concepts.

Furthermore, this work addresses limitations in traditional action transfer or retargeting techniques. These methods often assume a degree of alignment between the source and target videos, for instance, a consistent pose or camera distance. Such assumptions do not hold in the proposed "Video Creation by Demonstration" setting, where the demonstration video and the target context can be misaligned. This misalignment presents challenges for directly transferring low-level motion patterns and also for generating paired training data.

A key aspect of this research is the leveraging of out-of-the-box video foundation models, such as VideoPrism, for controlling I+V2V generation. While video foundation models have shown strong performance in various video understanding tasks by extracting rich representations, their application for latent control in video generation is presented as a novel direction by the authors. The use of a self-supervised training paradigm is also a notable contribution, enabling the model to learn from unlabeled video data, which is crucial given the difficulties in acquiring paired demonstration and target data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable machines to simulate a realistic visual world with fine-grained action controls, using a visual demonstration and a specified environment. This leads to the introduction of "Video Creation by Demonstration," a novel video creation experience.

The core motivation stems from several limitations observed in existing video generation approaches:
- **Limitations of Explicit Control Signals**: Contemporary controllable video generation largely relies on explicit control signals (e.g., text, keypoints, depth maps, segmentation masks). These signals are often either too abstract to convey detailed action nuances or too laborious to acquire for general video content. This restricts user expressiveness and makes interactive video creation challenging. The authors are motivated to move beyond these explicit controls towards a more implicit and flexible latent control mechanism.
- **Domain Specificity vs. Generalizability**: Many advanced video generation techniques are tailored to specific domains, such as gaming, dancing, or talking-head videos. Actions in general videos are highly complex and context-dependent, presenting significant challenges in transferring action concepts across varied environments, subjects, and objects. The objective is to develop a system capable of handling the inherent complexities and contextual variations found in general video content.
- **Handling Misalignment**: Traditional action transfer methods often presuppose a degree of alignment between the source (demonstration) and target (context) scenes or actions. This assumption is frequently violated in real-world scenarios. The authors are motivated to create a system that can effectively transfer action concepts even when there is substantial misalignment in appearance, subject, object, or environment between the demonstration and the target context.
- **Addressing Data Scarcity for Paired Training**: The misalignment problem makes it difficult to collect paired training data (i.e., a demonstration video showing an action and a corresponding target video showing the same action in a different context). This scarcity motivates the development of a self-supervised training paradigm that can effectively learn from readily available unlabeled video data.
- **Leveraging Video Foundation Models**: With the emergence of powerful video foundation models capable of deep semantic understanding, the authors are motivated to explore how these models can be utilized to extract robust action concepts and contextual information, rather than relying on simpler visual features.

In pursuit of these objectives, the research aims to unlock new capabilities for video generation and manipulation, moving beyond the constraints of explicit control signals to allow for more nuanced and flexible content creation, ultimately paving the way for advanced interactive world simulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces **$\delta$-Diffusion**, a two-stage, self-supervised training approach designed for "Video Creation by Demonstration."

#### 3.1. Task Formulation

The system takes two inputs: a context image ($I$) providing the initial scene, and a demonstration video ($V$) providing the action concepts. The objective is to generate a video ($\hat{V}$) that logically continues from $I$ and executes the action concepts observed in $V$, provided these actions are compatible with the context image.

#### 3.2. Method Overview

The $\delta$-Diffusion model, represented as G, generates a video $\hat{V}$ conditioned on the context image $I$ and action latents ($\delta_V$) extracted from the demonstration video $V$. The process involves two main components:
1. **Extracting Action Latents**: A pre-trained video foundation model (vision encoder F) processes the demonstration video $V$ to obtain spatiotemporal semantic representations. An "appearance bottleneck" module then refines these representations to extract action-rich control latents ($\delta_V$) with minimal appearance information.
2. **Training the Generation Model**: The generation model G, a diffusion model, is trained in a self-supervised manner. During training, both the context image $I$ and the demonstration video $V$ are sampled from the same video sequence. Specifically, $I$ is the starting frame, and $V$ is the subsequent clip, which G is tasked to reconstruct.

#### 3.3. Extracting Action Latents (Detailed)

This crucial step aims to isolate action concepts from appearance information.
- **Spatiotemporal Representations**: A pre-trained video encoder (VideoPrism) extracts temporally-aggregated semantic representations ($z \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times N \times D}$) from the demonstration video $V$, where $T$, $N$, and $D$ are temporal, spatial, and feature dimensions, respectively. These representations inherently contain both appearance and action information.
- **Appearance Bottleneck**: This module, comprising a feature predictor P and a removal operator, isolates action information.
 - **Feature Predictor P**: This is a per-frame estimator (a stack of Transformer encoder blocks) that takes per-frame spatial representations (from $V$) as input. P is trained to predict the best-effort approximation of the temporally-aggregated representation ($z_t$) for each individual frame, denoted as $h_t$. The design principles are: (i) P extracts minimal action information, as it operates on single frames; (ii) $h_t$ is aligned with the $z_t$ space. P is trained by minimizing the $L_1$ difference between $z_t$ and $h_t$ over a dataset.
 - **Removal Operator**: The action control latents ($\delta_V$) are computed by subtracting the concatenated per-frame representations $h_t$ from the full spatiotemporal representations $z$: $\delta_V = z - [h_0, h_1, \ldots, h_{T-1}]$. This difference is posited to represent the "temporal surprisal" or collective action concepts discernible only across a sequence of frames.

#### 3.4. Training $\delta$-Diffusion (Detailed)

The generation model G is trained using a self-supervised approach.
- **Data Sampling**: For each training step, a sequence of $T+1$ frames ($v_{0:T}$) is sampled from a single video. The first frame ($v_0$) serves as the context image $I$. The subsequent frames ($v_{1:T}$) serve both as the demonstration video (for latent extraction) and as the ground-truth target for reconstruction.
- **Appearance Leakage Mitigation**: To ensure G learns to transfer actions rather than copy appearances, additional action-preserving random augmentations (e.g., spatial cropping) are applied to $v_{1:T}$ (creating $V'$) before extracting action latents $\delta_{V'}$. This creates a misalignment between the demonstration and target, forcing G to generalize.
- **Generation Model Architecture**: A latent diffusion model (LDM), specifically WALT, is adopted for G. It tokenizes $v_0$ and $v_{1:T}$ into a low-dimensional latent space. The LDM is trained with a denoising loss, where the prediction target is velocity. It is conditioned on corrupted latents of $v_{1:T}$, $v_0$, and $\delta_{V'}$.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

- **Vision Encoders**: VideoPrism (Base, 0.1B parameters) is used. Its factorized encoder design allows direct input of per-frame spatial representations to the appearance bottleneck.
- **Feature Predictor P**: Composed of 4 Transformer encoder blocks from ViViT (1024 hidden dimensions, 8 heads). Trained for 30k iterations with Adam optimizer and L1 loss.
- **Video Generation Model G**: WALT (Large, 0.3B parameters) generates 16 frames at 128x128 resolution. The context image is passed natively. The $\delta_V$ latents are projected to 2048-dimensional vectors, replacing text embeddings. G is initialized with a pre-trained image+text-to-video checkpoint and fine-tuned for 500k iterations. Classifier-free guidance is used during inference.
- **Auto-regressive Generation**: For longer videos, the method supports auto-regressive generation, where the last frames of a generated segment become the context for the next segment. Self-conditioning and control signal masking are used during training, and classifier-free guidance at inference.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of $\delta$-Diffusion was assessed through extensive experiments on diverse video datasets using both machine and human evaluations.

#### 4.1. Datasets and Evaluation Setup

Experiments were conducted on Epic Kitchens 100, Something-Something v2 (SSv2), and Fractal datasets, chosen for their rich human-object interactions and state changes. Ego4D was additionally used during the training of the feature predictor P.
- **Machine Evaluation**: Utilized Fréchet Video Distance (FVD) for overall generation fidelity, Embedding Cosine Similarity (ES) (using I3D features) for action concept transfer quality, and Retrieval Hit@k (Hit@100, 500, 1k) to measure the similarity between generated and reference videos in a retrieval context.
- **Human Evaluation**: Five human raters assessed visual quality (VQ), action transferability (AT), and context consistency (CC) through side-by-side comparisons of generated videos. Preference rates favoring $\delta$-Diffusion over baselines were computed.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study on Appearance Bottleneck

An ablation study investigated the contribution of the appearance bottleneck by comparing it with two variants:
- "None": No bottleneck applied, using raw spatiotemporal features $z$.
- "Temp. Norm.": Temporal normalization, where the temporal average of $z$ is subtracted from $z$.
- "Ours": The proposed appearance bottleneck ($z - h$).

**Results**:
- **Quantitative**: $\delta$-Diffusion consistently outperformed "None" and "Temp. Norm." variants on instance-wise metrics (Hit@k and ES) across all three datasets (SSv2, Epic Kitchens 100, Fractal). This indicates that the appearance bottleneck significantly improves action concept transferability. FVD results were not consistently superior, suggesting that the method maintains comparable visual quality while enhancing action faithfulness.
- **Qualitative**: Visual comparisons demonstrated that both "None" and "Temp. Norm." approaches suffered from appearance leakage (e.g., textures or colors from the demonstration video appearing in the generated video), while the proposed appearance bottleneck successfully preserved the input context and transferred action concepts without such leakage.

#### 4.3. Main Results: Comparison with Baselines

$\delta$-Diffusion was compared against two relevant baselines:
- **WALT (image+text)**: A text-to-video generation model conditioned on an image and a text prompt (ground truth caption used for fair comparison).
- **MotionDirector (image+text+video)**: A method that uses image, text, and video as conditional signals, learning per-instance appearance and motion via LoRA fine-tuning.

**Results**:
- **Human Evaluation**: $\delta$-Diffusion was favored by human raters across all datasets and evaluation rubrics (VQ, AT, CC).
 - Against WALT, $\delta$-Diffusion achieved preference rates ranging from 0.70 to 0.83.
 - Against MotionDirector, $\delta$-Diffusion achieved even higher preference rates, ranging from 0.86 to 1.00. This indicates a clear superiority in human perception of visual quality, action transfer, and context consistency.
- **Qualitative Comparisons**: Visual examples demonstrated that MotionDirector frequently produced failures, possibly due to difficulties in optimizing its LoRA parameters. WALT, while maintaining content consistency, often generated actions that did not genuinely align with the demonstration video, attributed to the limited expressiveness of text descriptions for complex actions. In contrast, $\delta$-Diffusion consistently produced videos with faithful action execution and good content preservation.

#### 4.4. Auto-Regressive Generation

The method demonstrated the ability to auto-regressively generate coherent video sequences by conditioning on a concatenation of multiple distinct demonstration videos. This involved using the last frames of a previously generated segment as the context for the next segment. This capability suggests potential for simulating complex, multi-step tasks in a consistent environment.

#### 4.5. Controllability by Demonstration

Supplementary material illustrates the model's controllability, showing how different demonstration videos applied to the same context image lead to varied yet relevant generated actions.

#### 4.6. Failure Cases

The paper identifies three primary failure modes:
- **Incomplete Action Semantics**: Generated actions may not fully capture the semantic intent of the demonstration (e.g., placing an object "in-between" instead of "next-to").
- **Appearance Leakage with Fast Changes**: Rapid appearance changes in the demonstration video (e.g., fast object rotation) can still lead to some appearance leakage in the generated video.
- **Inconsistent Generations with Mismatched Contexts**: Significant mismatches in perspective or elements between the demonstration video and context image can result in inconsistent or visually illogical generations (e.g., generating an additional hand due to a perspective mismatch).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research on "Video Creation by Demonstration" and the introduction of $\delta$-Diffusion present several significant contributions and potential impacts in the field of generative AI:

- **Novel Video Creation Paradigm**: The work introduces a new method for controllable video generation that utilizes demonstration videos as direct driving control signals for transferring action concepts. This approach bridges a gap between abstract control signals (like text prompts) and highly detailed, often difficult-to-acquire, explicit controls. This could simplify interactive video creation workflows and expand creative possibilities for users.

- **Pioneering Use of Video Foundation Models for Latent Control**: The study is presented as the first to leverage out-of-the-box video foundation models (such as VideoPrism) for implicit latent control in video generation. This demonstrates a new application for these powerful models beyond traditional video understanding tasks, suggesting their broader utility in generative contexts.

- **Effective Self-Supervised Training**: The development of a novel self-supervised training paradigm for $\delta$-Diffusion addresses the challenge of acquiring paired training data for action transfer tasks in misaligned contexts. This approach enables learning from large amounts of unlabeled video data, making the method scalable and robust.

- **Enhanced Controllability and Expressiveness**: By moving towards implicit latent control via an appearance bottleneck, $\delta$-Diffusion offers greater flexibility and expressiveness in generating diverse video content compared to methods relying on explicit signals. This allows for more nuanced and fine-grained control over generated actions and dynamics.

- **Advancements Towards Interactive World Simulation**: The demonstrated capability for auto-regressive generation, where a sequence of distinct demonstration videos can coherently drive the simulation of a complex task in a consistent environment, signifies a step towards building more sophisticated interactive world simulators and generative interactive environments. This could have implications for robotics, virtual reality, and synthetic data generation for various applications.

**Broader Impact**:
The work is noted to improve the ease of interactive generation, potentially fostering new creative tools and experiences. It also contributes to world modeling by simulating not only action transfer but also the plausible effects of these actions on the entire scene, including interactions not explicitly present in the demonstration.

**Potential Negative Impact**:
The authors acknowledge that the work shares potential negative societal impacts common to controlled video generation technologies. These include the reinforcement of existing dataset biases, the misuse of generated content for creating misleading or inappropriate materials (e.g., deepfakes), and privacy concerns related to individuals appearing in generated content without their explicit consent. The paper stresses the importance of vigilance and responsible implementation to mitigate these risks and ensure ethical use of the technology.

**Limitations and Future Work**:
Despite its capabilities, $\delta$-Diffusion in its current form may not always strictly adhere to physical realism in complex scenes. This is identified as an area for future exploration, with potential alleviation through scaling up the generation model.
