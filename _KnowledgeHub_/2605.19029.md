# Distributionally Robust Control via Stein Variational Inference for Contact-Rich Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A new control framework called Stein Variational Distributionally Robust Optimization (SV-DRO) integrates Stein Variational Inference with model-based control to adaptively model task-sensitive parameter uncertainty in contact-rich robotic manipulation. The approach achieves up to a 3x improvement in robustness and higher task success rates (e.g., 93.25% for bimanual pushing and 100% for within-hand positioning) compared to existing methods, even with broad uncertainty and limited feedback.

## Problem

- Existing data-driven robotic control methods demand extensive data and computation, and often lack "zero-shot" robustness in uncertain environments.
- Traditional model-based controllers are limited in their capacity to flexibly represent and adapt to diverse task-relevant uncertainties, especially in contact-rich scenarios.
- Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) can be overly conservative, leading to suboptimal task performance, and typically requires strong assumptions about known reference distributions or direct uncertainty samples for latent physical parameters.

## Method

- The approach formulates the control problem as a ==Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO)== problem, addressing parametric uncertainty.
- **Stein Variational Inference (SVI)** is used to generate and deterministically evolve a set of parameter particles, which adaptively model a task-aware posterior distribution of uncertainties based on their impact on task optimality.
- These task-sensitive particles are then integrated into a ==receding horizon Model Predictive Control (MPC)== loop, where the controller minimizes the cost while penalizing the ==optimality gap== exposed by the evolved parameter variations.

## Results

- SV-DRO achieved higher task success rates (e.g., **93.25%** for bimanual pushing and **100%** for within-hand positioning), demonstrating up to a **3x** improvement in robustness compared to existing model-based control methods.
- The framework showed more stable DRO convergence with lower task errors across broad parameter discrepancies, and practical online tractability (**0.130 ± 0.008 s**/MPC step), scaling linearly with particle size/horizon on CPU, near-constant on GPU.
- Hardware experiments confirmed the ability of SV-DRO to adapt online to sudden, discrete parameter shifts, such as changes in object mass distribution during a contact-rich serving task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adaptively shaping the uncertainty distribution through Stein Variational Inference (SVI) based on task-aware sensitivities allows the controller to prioritize parameter variations most critical for task success.
> - The deterministic evolution of parameter particles via SVI provides more stable and consistent convergence for Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) objectives compared to stochastic sampling.
> - Robust manipulation can be achieved by modeling task-relevant uncertainty through an optimality gap, enabling effective control even with broad initial parameter uncertainty and limited feedback.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reliable robotic manipulation, particularly in environments characterized by contact-rich interactions, necessitates control policies that can effectively represent and adapt to various forms of uncertainty. The current research landscape in this area is broadly divided into data-driven and classical model-based approaches, each with inherent strengths and limitations that this work seeks to address.

Modern data-driven methods, such as reinforcement learning (RL) and policy optimization, mitigate uncertainty by training policies on large datasets and extensive computation. These methods offer adaptability and the potential for implicit learning from experience. However, their performance can degrade significantly with a limited number of training samples, and they often require substantial computational resources and data coverage across all possible parameter uncertainties to guarantee high performance. Approaches like domain randomization, which train policies over a distribution of system parameters, are often task-agnostic, as the prior distribution for sampling is fixed and does not adapt to the specific control objective during execution.

In contrast, classical model-based controllers are generally computationally efficient and offer a degree of reliability. Their primary limitation, however, lies in their restricted ability to flexibly represent task-relevant uncertainty, which can hinder performance in dynamic and uncertain contact-rich scenarios. Existing uncertainty-aware model-based approaches include:
- **Sampled trajectory ensembles:** These methods aim to mitigate parametric uncertainty by optimizing controls over average performance derived from multiple sampled trajectories. They can be limited by the number of samples available and may not robustly adapt to uncertainty under insufficient sampling.
- **Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO):** DRO formulates control as an optimization problem against worst-case probability distributions within a defined ambiguity set, offering theoretical robustness. However, these methods can be overly conservative, leading to suboptimal task performance due to the variability of object dynamics in contact-rich settings. Conventional DRO formulations often rely on estimating a parametric reference distribution or assume direct access to samples from the uncertain process, which is restrictive when uncertainty pertains to latent physical parameters (e.g., mass, inertia, friction, geometry) that cannot be directly sampled or observed with sufficient feedback.

Stein Variational Inference (SVI), particularly Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD), is a particle-based method for approximating complex probability distributions without requiring a specific parametric variational family. It has been previously applied in robot control for maintaining multi-modal control particles, optimizing constrained trajectory distributions, and jointly inferring controls and dynamics parameters online. However, these applications often focus on estimation-driven system identification, requiring informative state-transition measurements, which may be sparse or indirectly related to latent contact parameters.

This research aims to bridge the gap between model-based efficiency and flexible uncertainty modeling by proposing an approach that expands the capabilities of model-based control. It introduces a novel framework that uses SVI to adaptively model uncertainty, thereby enhancing robustness in contact-rich manipulation tasks without the performance compromises associated with conservative worst-case methods or the data/compute demands of large-scale data-driven approaches. The work specifically differentiates itself by using SVI to evolve parameter particles based on task-aware sensitivities, rather than solely for system identification from state feedback or relying on fixed, potentially overly conservative, uncertainty sets.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to enhance the capabilities of model-based control for robotic manipulation by introducing a more flexible uncertainty modeling approach that maintains performance while exactly adapting to uncertainty.

The motivation for this research stems from several limitations observed in existing robotic manipulation control strategies when dealing with uncertainty, particularly in contact-rich environments:

1. **Limitations of Data-Driven Methods:** Modern data-driven control, while adaptive, often requires vast amounts of data and computational resources. Their performance rapidly deteriorates with fewer training samples, and they struggle to achieve robust "zero-shot" manipulation without extensive coverage of all possible parameter uncertainties. This poses a challenge for deploying robots in novel or rapidly changing environments.

2. **Limitations of Classical Model-Based Methods:** Traditional model-based controllers are computationally efficient and offer reliability but are constrained by their ability to represent and adapt to diverse task-relevant uncertainties. Their fixed uncertainty models or reliance on strong priors can lead to brittle performance in dynamic, contact-rich interactions where physical parameters (e.g., mass, friction, inertia) are often unknown or variable.

3. **Conservatism of Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO):** While DRO offers robustness by optimizing against worst-case distributions within an ambiguity set, this worst-case formulation can result in overly conservative control policies that compromise desired task performance. Additionally, many DRO formulations require pre-defined parametric reference distributions or direct access to uncertainty samples, which is often not feasible for latent physical parameters in contact-rich manipulation. The implicit assumption that the true distribution lies within a small neighborhood of a known reference (KL-ball ambiguity set) may not hold for weak priors or in highly uncertain scenarios.

4. **Inadequacy of Sample-Based Ensemble Approaches:** Methods leveraging sampled trajectory ensembles to mitigate uncertainty often compute only average performance and struggle to adapt robustly when the number of available samples is limited or when the range of parameter uncertainty is broad.

To address these limitations, the key objectives are:

- To reformulate the robotic manipulation problem under parametric uncertainty as a Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) problem.
- To integrate Stein Variational Inference (SVI) into this DRO framework, proposing a novel deterministic formulation that allows for flexible and exact adaptation to uncertainty.
- To enable control policies to explicitly model and reason over task-sensitive parameter uncertainty. This means the controller should focus its resources on uncertainty directions that directly impact task success, rather than optimizing against all possible perturbations or merely fitting recent state feedback.
- To develop controllers that demonstrate high reliability and robustness without compromising task performance, especially in contact-rich manipulation tasks with broad parametric uncertainty and limited feedback.
- To demonstrate that this approach can achieve improved robustness compared to existing model-based control methods across a range of tasks.

Ultimately, the motivation is to develop zero-shot manipulation controllers that can actively adapt to physical uncertainty in real-world settings, offering a balance of performance, robustness, and computational efficiency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed Stein Variational Distributionally Robust Optimization (SV-DRO), expands upon conventional model-based control by integrating Stein Variational Inference (SVI) within a Distributionally Robust Control (DRO) framework. This integration aims to create a control policy that can adapt to parameter uncertainty without compromising performance or relying on strong prior assumptions.

The approach begins by formulating the control problem under parametric uncertainty as a DRO problem. The objective is to find a control trajectory $\tau = \{(x_k, u_k, c_k)\}$ that minimizes a task-dependent cost $J_\theta(\tau)$, subject to system dynamics $x_{k+1} = f_\theta(x_k, u_k)$ and contact models $c_{k+1} \in C(x_k)$, where $\theta \in \Theta$ represents uncertain physics model parameters. The Lagrangian $L(\tau, \theta)$ encapsulates this cost and constraints.

A standard DRO problem seeks to minimize the worst-case expected Lagrangian over a set of plausible distributions $P$, typically defined as an $\epsilon$-neighborhood around a reference distribution $q$ (e.g., via KL-divergence, $D_{KL}[p||q] \le \epsilon$). This is commonly approximated, for sufficiently large $\beta$, as an expectation and variance of the Lagrangian under $q$. However, the paper highlights that this approach often relies on strong assumptions about the availability and quality of the reference distribution $q$ (e.g., estimated via maximum-likelihood or requiring direct samples), which are often not met for latent physical parameters in contact-rich manipulation or with weak priors.

To address these limitations, the core innovation of SV-DRO lies in using Stein Variational Inference to generate and evolve a set of parameter particles that explicitly capture task-sensitive uncertainty, thereby eliminating the need for a fixed ambiguity set or strong assumptions about the reference distribution.

**A. Variational Approximation of Task-Dependent Parameter Posterior:**
Instead of relying on a pre-defined or estimated distribution $q$, SV-DRO infers a *task-aware parameter posterior* $p'(\theta)$. This posterior is derived by considering how parameter variations affect task optimality.
1. **Optimality Gap:** The concept of an "optimality gap," $\delta L(\tau^*, \theta) = L(\tau^*, \theta) - L(\tau^*, \theta^*)$, is introduced. Here, $\tau^*$ is the solved optimal trajectory, and $\theta^*$ is the true (unknown) parameter. Since $\theta^*$ is unknown, the particle mean $\bar{\theta} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \theta_i$ is used as a stable, low-variance reference to define the optimality gap. This gap measures how much the Lagrangian (and thus task performance) changes with respect to different parameter realizations.
2. **Likelihood and Posterior:** A likelihood $p(O|\theta) = \exp(-\delta L(\tau^*, \theta))$ is defined, where $O$ represents the parameter-dependent outcome in task optimality. This likelihood, combined with a prior $p(\theta)$, forms the task-aware posterior $p'(\theta) \propto p(O|\theta)p(\theta)$. The log-posterior is then $\log p'(\theta) = \log p(\theta) + \delta L(\tau, \theta) + \text{constant}$. This formulation directly links parameter uncertainty to its impact on task performance.
3. **Stein Variational Gradient Descent (SVGD):** A set of $N$ parameter particles $\{\theta^i_t\}$ is initialized from a prior $p(\theta)$. These particles are then iteratively evolved using SVGD. The update rule for each particle is $\theta^i_{t+1} \leftarrow \theta^i_t + \alpha \phi^*(\theta^i_t)$, where $\alpha$ is a step size and $\phi^*(\cdot)$ is the steepest descent direction that minimizes the KL-divergence between the empirical particle distribution and the target posterior $p'(\theta)$. $\phi^*(\cdot)$ is approximated using Monte Carlo sampling as:
 $\phi^*(\cdot) \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \left[k(\theta^i_t, \cdot)\nabla_{\theta} \log p'(\theta^i_t) + \nabla_{\theta} k(\theta^i_t, \cdot)\right]$.
 A Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel $k(\theta, \hat{\theta}) = \exp(-\|\theta - \hat{\theta}\|^2/h)$ is typically used. This process ensures that particles move towards high-probability regions of the task-aware posterior while maintaining diversity through a kernel-induced repulsion term. The outcome is a particle set that emphasizes parameter variations most critical for task success.

**B. Stein Variational DRO Formulation:**
The evolved parameter particles are then incorporated into the DRO objective. The expected Lagrangian $E_q[L(\tau, \theta)]$ is empirically approximated using the evolved particle set:
$E_q[L(\tau, \theta)] \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N L(\tau, \theta^i_t)$.
This is further decomposed into:
$\approx L(\tau, \bar{\theta}) + \gamma \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \delta L(\tau, \theta^i_t)$,
where $\bar{\theta}$ is the empirical mean of the current particle set, and $\gamma \in (0, 1]$ is a tuning parameter to balance optimization over nominal parameters versus parameter variations. This objective minimizes the cost with respect to the mean parameter while also penalizing the optimality gap exposed by the task-sensitive particles.

**C. Receding Horizon Control (Algorithm 1):**
The SV-DRO framework operates within a receding horizon Model Predictive Control (MPC) loop:
1. At each time step $t$, the controller solves the optimization problem for an optimal trajectory $\tau^*$ using the current set of $N$ evolved particles. This involves minimizing the combined objective $L(\tau, \bar{\theta}) + \gamma \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \delta L(\tau, \theta^i_t)$.
2. The first $H$ control inputs from $\tau^*$ are applied to the physical system.
3. After executing $H$ controls, a new state $\tilde{x}$ is measured from the actual dynamics.
4. The parameter particles $\{\theta^i_t\}$ are updated using SVGD based on the task-aware posterior gradients derived from the current optimality gap. This means particles are only updated at the lower feedback frequency (after $H$ steps), distinguishing it from estimation-driven methods requiring continuous state feedback.
5. The process repeats until the task is complete.

This approach allows the controller to dynamically reshape its understanding of uncertainty based on how it impacts task performance, focusing computational effort on critical uncertainty directions. It aims to achieve robust control without the conservatism of worst-case methods or the data intensity of pure learning-based techniques, particularly in scenarios with sparse or indirect feedback on latent parameters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper evaluates the proposed Stein Variational Distributionally Robust Control (SV-DRO) approach across various contact-rich manipulation tasks, comparing its performance against several baselines. The findings address five specific research questions (Q1-Q5).

**Experimental Setup:**
The experiments were conducted on two primary contact-based control problems:
1. **Bimanual Push-T:** A bimanual manipulator pushes a T-shaped block with unknown mass and inertia to a goal pose.
2. **Dynamic Within-Hand Positioning:** A robot manipulates an object (e.g., a cup) with unknown mass distribution (mass and inertia) and friction coefficient to a goal state within its tray hand.
A hardware demonstration involving within-hand serving of a wine glass with changing mass distribution was also performed.
Baselines included: Ensemble Model Predictive Control (EMPPI), Dual Stein Variational control (DuST-MPC), Conventional DRO, and a naive MPC using the prior mean. Initial parameters for all methods were sampled from a bounded uniform distribution. For EMPPI and DRO, parameters were resampled after each MPC planning cycle, while SV-DRO evolved samples via SVGD.

**Q1) Can our proposed approach improve task success rate for manipulation tasks under broad parameter uncertainty?**
**A1. Improved Performance Under Uncertainty:**
SV-DRO demonstrated a significant improvement in robustness and task performance.
- **Quantitative Results (Table I):** SV-DRO achieved the highest success rates across both tasks: 93.25% (≤ 10 cm) and 84.38% (≤ 1 cm) for Bimanual Push-T, and 100% for both tolerance levels in Dynamic Within-Hand Positioning. This represents up to a 3x improvement in robustness compared to existing model-based methods. Completion times were also competitive or superior (e.g., 12.00 s for within-hand positioning). In contrast, conventional DRO performed poorly, especially in Push-T (3.13% success for ≤ 10 cm), due to its conservative nature. EMPPI and DuST-MPC showed moderate success but were outperformed by SV-DRO.
- **Qualitative Results (Fig. 3, 4, 5):**
 - Figure 3 illustrates SV-DRO coordinating two end-effectors for Push-T despite high uncertainty in block mass distribution, avoiding task-sensitive actions.
 - Figure 4 demonstrates SV-DRO's reliability in continuous within-hand positioning with unknown cup mass distribution and tray friction over a 3.5-minute run, successfully achieving the desired tolerance (within 1.5 cm of goal) in 100% of trials after random perturbations.
 - Figure 5 presents a hardware demonstration where SV-DRO successfully adapts to a sudden, discrete shift in parameter (mass distribution change when a wine glass is filled with water during a serving task). This empirically indicates robustness beyond the stationary parameter assumptions of the theoretical guarantees.

**Q2) How does Stein variational inference impact the convergence of the DRO problem?**
**A2. SVI Improves Task-Aware Convergence:**
- Figure 6 shows the convergence of the DRO objective for the within-hand positioning task. SV-DRO exhibited significantly lower variance and more consistent convergence compared to baselines, even with varying initial parameter priors. This stability is attributed to the deterministic evolution of parameters by SVGD, which guides particles towards robust regions. EMPPI, relying on stochastic task-agnostic random sampling, showed high variations in loss, while conventional DRO converged to a worse-performing, risk-averse controller.

**Q3) Does the task-aware parameter inference via Stein Variational DRO benefit over existing uncertainty mitigating approaches?**
**A3. Task-Aware Inference Improves Manipulation:**
- Figure 7 illustrates the relationship between initial parameter discrepancy (distance from prior mean to true parameter) and the final object position error for the within-hand positioning task. SV-DRO consistently maintained lower task error across a wide range of parameter discrepancies. This indicates that SV-DRO effectively reshapes the uncertainty distribution towards *task-relevant* parameters, focusing on those that most affect the optimality gap, rather than merely converging to the true physical parameters. This allows for robust control even when the initial prior is far from the true parameters. Conversely, DRO and EMPPI performance degraded as the initial parameter mean moved further from the true parameter, highlighting their task-agnostic uncertainty representations.

**Q4) How does the choice of kernel affect the performance of Stein Variational DRO?**
**A4. Choice of Kernel for SV-DRO:**
- Table II shows the impact of different SVGD kernels (RBF, constant, Inverse Multiquadric (IMQ)) on within-hand positioning. While all kernels achieved 100% success, their completion times varied. The IMQ kernel resulted in the fastest completion time (8.78 s), outperforming RBF (12.93 s) and the constant kernel (16.54 s). This suggests that the IMQ kernel, with its stronger long-range particle interactions, is more effective at preserving particle diversity and preventing premature mode collapse in task-sensitive regions of the posterior, thereby improving the efficiency of SV-DRO.

**Q5) What is the computation scaling for our Stein variational DRO approach with respect to particle parameter size and planning horizon?**
**A5. Computational Scaling of SVI:**
- Figure 8 analyzes the computational time scaling. On a CPU, SV-DRO showed linear scaling with both parameter sample size ($N$) and planning horizon ($t_h$). However, with GPU parallelization, the scaling with particle size became nearly constant (approximately 77 μs), and scaling with planning horizon was sublinear.
- Table IV provides a wall-clock time comparison per MPC step. SV-DRO had a wall-clock time of 0.130 ± 0.008 s, which is comparable to conventional DRO (0.123 s) and well within the typical MPC planning horizon (0.375 s). This demonstrates that despite the additional SVGD particle update, SV-DRO maintains practical online computational tractability.

In summary, the main findings indicate that SV-DRO significantly improves robustness and reliability in contact-rich manipulation tasks under broad parameter uncertainty. It achieves this by adaptively modeling task-sensitive uncertainty through Stein variational inference, leading to more stable convergence, effective adaptation to discrepancies, and efficient computation compared to existing model-based and ensemble approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that enhances the robustness and reliability of robotic manipulation in the presence of significant parametric uncertainty, particularly in contact-rich scenarios. The integration of Stein Variational Inference (SVI) with Distributionally Robust Optimization (DRO) provides a novel approach that addresses several limitations of existing control methodologies, potentially impacting the field in multiple ways.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**

1. **Flexible and Adaptive Uncertainty Modeling:** The central contribution is the ability to model uncertainty in a flexible and adaptive manner using SVI. Unlike conventional DRO, which often relies on fixed ambiguity sets or strong assumptions about the underlying uncertainty distribution, SV-DRO dynamically shapes the parameter uncertainty towards "task-aware" posterior distributions. This ensures that the controller focuses computational resources on parameter variations that most directly affect task success, moving beyond overly conservative worst-case formulations.
2. **Enhanced Robustness and Performance in Contact-Rich Tasks:** The experimental results demonstrate a substantial improvement in robustness (up to 3x) and task success rates across challenging contact-rich manipulation problems like bimanual Push-T and dynamic within-hand positioning. This is particularly significant for tasks involving intermittent contact and sliding, which are highly sensitive to latent dynamics and difficult for methods requiring persistent, informative state feedback for system identification.
3. **Efficiency and Adaptability with Limited Feedback:** SV-DRO offers a model-based solution that retains computational efficiency while adapting to uncertainty with limited feedback. The particle updates via SVGD occur at a lower frequency (after executing multiple controls) and are driven by task optimality gaps rather than continuous state-transition measurements. This makes the approach suitable for scenarios where sensory feedback on latent physical parameters is sparse, indirect, or noisy, avoiding the data and compute intensity of many data-driven learning methods.
4. **Zero-Shot Adaptation to Parameter Shifts:** The hardware demonstration involving a sudden change in object mass distribution (wine glass filling) illustrates the approach's ability to adapt to abrupt posterior shifts online. This indicates a potential for robust "zero-shot" control under changing environmental conditions without requiring extensive offline training or retraining.
5. **Improved Convergence and Stability:** The analysis shows that SV-DRO's deterministic particle evolution via SVI leads to more stable convergence of the DRO objective with less variance compared to stochastic sampling-based baselines. This predictability contributes to more reliable controller behavior.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Deployment in Real-World Robotics:** The ability of robots to reliably perform tasks in uncertain, unstructured environments is a major challenge. SV-DRO’s enhanced robustness and adaptability could facilitate the deployment of robotic manipulators in practical applications where object properties or environmental conditions are not precisely known or can change dynamically, such as manufacturing, logistics, domestic assistance, and space exploration.
- **Reduced Development Costs:** By enabling more robust performance with limited training samples and without the need for extensive data collection or continuous system identification, SV-DRO could potentially reduce the development time and cost associated with deploying new manipulation skills.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work establishes a foundation for future research at the intersection of robust control and probabilistic inference. It opens avenues for exploring more complex uncertainty classes, structured priors, and integration with advanced learning techniques in a principled, model-aware manner.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The authors acknowledge several limitations:
1. **Model Error from Overlooked Parameters:** The model-based nature of SV-DRO requires prior knowledge of the parameter uncertainty class. If critical model parameters are overlooked or inaccurately modeled (e.g., complex frictional forces), overall performance can be adversely affected.
2. **Model Complexity:** The necessity of constructing reduced-order models to maintain computational tractability can be a bottleneck in general open-world settings. Bridging the gap between interpretable model-based approaches and flexible model-free methods remains an open challenge.
3. **Sensitivity to Informative Priors:** The prior distribution significantly influences SVGD updates. Highly informative priors could inadvertently suppress the exploration of task-critical uncertainty directions. Future work will investigate structured priors to characterize this trade-off.
4. **Theoretical Guarantees for Dynamic Parameters:** While empirical results demonstrated adaptation to changing parameters (e.g., the wine glass task), the theoretical convergence guarantees for SVI currently assume fixed physical parameter targets. Extending these guarantees to scenarios with moving parameter targets is a direction for future research.

Despite these limitations, the SV-DRO framework represents a step towards more intelligent and reliable robotic manipulation under uncertainty, combining the interpretability and efficiency of model-based control with the flexibility of modern probabilistic inference techniques.
