# A Survey of On-Policy Distillation for Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A comprehensive survey outlines On-Policy Distillation (OPD) for Large Language Models (LLMs), introducing a unified theoretical framework based on f-divergences and a three-dimensional taxonomy for existing methods. The work details how OPD mitigates exposure bias, reducing error accumulation from quadratic to linear, and explores various methodologies and future research directions for efficient and capable LLM distillation.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) demand extensive computational resources, creating barriers to widespread deployment.
- Off-policy knowledge distillation methods face "exposure bias," where student models accumulate quadratic errors in autoregressive generation due to train-test mismatches.
- The rapidly growing field of On-Policy Distillation (OPD) for LLMs lacked a comprehensive, unified framework to organize diverse methods and identify key challenges.

## Method

- Formalizes ==autoregressive language generation== within a ==Markov Decision Process (MDP)== framework to precisely define and quantify ==exposure bias==.
- Proposes a unified theoretical framework for ==On-Policy Distillation (OPD)== using generalized ==f-divergence== to characterize various objective functions.
- Introduces a novel ==three-dimensional taxonomy== to categorize OPD methods based on ==feedback signal==, ==teacher access==, and ==loss granularity==.

## Results

- On-Policy Distillation (OPD) consistently improves performance by mitigating exposure bias, transforming error accumulation from quadratic to linear in sequence length, particularly for multi-step reasoning tasks.
- The survey unifies diverse OPD methods under a generalized f-divergence framework, highlighting that adaptive divergence approaches often outperform fixed choices by balancing diversity and coherence.
- A novel three-dimensional taxonomy provides a structured classification of OPD methods based on feedback signal, teacher access, and loss granularity, clarifying the design space and identifying open research problems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - On-policy learning fundamentally addresses exposure bias, reducing error accumulation in autoregressive generation from quadratic (O(T^2)) to linear (O(T)) by allowing students to learn from their own generated trajectories.
> - The specific choice of f-divergence (e.g., Forward KL for mode-covering vs. Reverse KL for mode-seeking) is crucial, influencing the student's output diversity and coherence.
> - The distinction between knowledge distillation and reinforcement learning is dissolving, with increasing integration into hybrid frameworks that leverage stable imitation from KD and exploration from RL.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated rapid advancements in capabilities such as reasoning, code generation, and multilingual performance. This progress, however, necessitates substantial computational resources for training, limiting their development to organizations with extensive infrastructure. Knowledge distillation (KD), initially introduced by Hinton et al. (2015) for model compression and knowledge transfer, has evolved to become a core technique for transferring capabilities from large, frontier models to smaller, more deployable student models across varying scales and architectural families. Examples include distilling a 671B mixture-of-experts teacher into dense students ranging from 1.5B to 70B parameters, preserving complex reasoning abilities.

The prevalent method for large-scale KD involves off-policy imitation, where a student model is trained to match a teacher's next-token distribution over a fixed dataset. This approach suffers from a structural limitation: during training, the student conditions on flawless prefixes generated by the teacher, but at inference time, it must generate autoregressively from its own potentially imperfect outputs. This discrepancy, termed "exposure bias," leads to compounding errors along the generated sequence. This phenomenon is analogous to the compounding error observed in interactive imitation learning (Ross et al., 2011), where the discrepancy can amplify quadratically with sequence length (O(ϵT²)). Such errors are particularly detrimental in reasoning tasks, where an early misstep can undermine an entire solution chain.

On-Policy Distillation (OPD) emerged to address this exposure bias by shifting the training data sampling distribution. Instead of relying on a static corpus, OPD trains the student on sequences generated by its own evolving policy. The teacher then provides feedback on these student-generated sequences, transforming distillation into an iterative process of correction. This approach has rapidly transitioned from research into practical applications within LLM development. Despite its adoption, the literature on OPD remains dispersed across various research communities—knowledge distillation, reinforcement learning (RL), and imitation learning—lacking a unified theoretical and empirical treatment. Existing surveys of LLM distillation have generally retained a classical compression framing, often treating off-policy and on-policy methods as interchangeable rather than distinct paradigms with different theoretical properties and failure modes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this survey are rooted in the challenges and fragmented understanding surrounding On-Policy Distillation (OPD) for Large Language Models (LLMs).

**Motivation:**
1. **Addressing Exposure Bias in Knowledge Distillation:** The primary motivation for OPD stems from the inherent structural weakness of traditional off-policy knowledge distillation. In off-policy methods, LLMs are trained on teacher-generated text, typically conditioning on "flawless" prefixes. However, during inference, the student must generate text autoregressively, conditioning on its own "imperfect" outputs. This distributional mismatch between training and inference leads to compounding errors, which can significantly degrade performance, especially in tasks requiring long reasoning chains (e.g., mathematical proofs, code generation). OPD aims to mitigate this by training the student on its own generated sequences, allowing the teacher to provide feedback on the states the student actually encounters during deployment.
2. **High Cost of Frontier LLMs:** The rapid advancement in LLM capabilities has coincided with a substantial increase in training costs, making frontier models accessible to only a few organizations. Knowledge distillation, and particularly OPD, is motivated by the need to efficiently transfer these advanced capabilities from large, costly teacher models to smaller, more deployable student models, thereby reducing inference costs and democratizing access to powerful AI.
3. **Fragmented Research Landscape:** The literature on OPD is scattered across the knowledge distillation, reinforcement learning, and imitation learning communities. This fragmentation results in disparate notations, benchmarks, and taxonomies of failure modes, hindering a cohesive understanding of the field. A unified treatment is needed to consolidate these diverse perspectives.
4. **Lack of Unified Theoretical and Practical Account:** There is an absence of a comprehensive mathematical framework that unifies the on-policy dynamics connecting these different research threads. Furthermore, a systematic comparison of OPD's white-box, black-box, and teacher-free instantiations is lacking, making it difficult for researchers and engineers to select appropriate methods based on practical constraints.

**Key Objectives of the Survey:**
1. **Formalize OPD as f-divergence minimization:** To provide a unified theoretical foundation, the survey aims to recast the transition from off-policy to on-policy distillation as a sequential decision-making problem, formalizing core OPD algorithms as instances of f-divergence minimization over student-sampled trajectories. This framework is intended to establish a common analytical vocabulary and clarify relationships between methods.
2. **Develop a design-centric taxonomy:** The survey seeks to organize the field not by superficial similarities, but along three fundamental design axes: objective function design, signal source architecture, and training dynamics optimization. This categorization is designed to provide a clear, one-method-one-category classification that highlights specific design choices.
3. **Consolidate theoretical and empirical insights on failure modes:** The report aims to synthesize existing empirical records into a coherent understanding of when and why OPD breaks down. This includes identifying recurring failure modes (e.g., the flawed prefix trap, self-play saturation, diversity collapse) and describing formal conditions under which OPD offers improvements over off-policy alternatives.
4. **Provide a cross-regime comparative analysis:** The survey intends to offer a comparative analysis of white-box, black-box, and self-distillation regimes within the unified framework, summarizing methods and documenting factors that map deployment constraints (like teacher access and compute budgets) to applicable OPD strategies.
5. **Identify Open Problems:** The survey concludes by outlining open research questions, such as distillation scaling laws, the incorporation of uncertainty-aware teacher feedback, and the extension of OPD to agent-level distillation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

As a survey paper, the methodology employed by the authors involves a comprehensive review, synthesis, and systematic organization of existing research on On-Policy Distillation (OPD). The approach is structured around formalizing the core concepts and then using this formalization to classify and analyze diverse methods.

1. **Formalization of On-Policy Distillation:** The survey establishes a unified mathematical framework by formalizing OPD as an f-divergence minimization problem. The core objective function is defined as:
 `min_θ E_{y~π_mix} [ Σ_{t=1}^{|y|} D_f(p_T(·|x, y_<t), p_θ(·|x, y_<t))]`
 where `p_θ` is the student policy, `p_T` is the teacher policy, `π_mix` is a mixture policy for sampling trajectories (often including the student's own current policy), and `D_f` is an f-divergence. This framework highlights that the choice of f-divergence (e.g., Forward KL, Reverse KL, Jensen-Shannon Divergence) determines the implicit weighting of likelihood ratios, influencing whether the student's distribution is mode-covering or mode-seeking. This formalization also grounds the concept of exposure bias within the DAgger theorem, demonstrating how on-policy sampling can reduce compounding error from O(ϵT²) to O(ϵT).

2. **Development of a Design-Centric Taxonomy:** The authors organize the vast and fragmented literature on OPD along three orthogonal design axes:
 - **Objective Functions and Optimization:** This axis categorizes methods based on the mathematical quantity optimized by the student. It includes fixed divergence objectives (e.g., GKD, DistiLLM), adaptive divergence objectives (e.g., EOPD, AOPD) that adjust based on local distributional geometry, and RL-augmented objectives (e.g., G-OPD, RLKD) that incorporate external reward signals.
 - **Signal Source and Teacher Architecture:** This axis differentiates methods based on where the supervisory signal originates. It distinguishes between external teacher distillation (further subdivided into white-box logit supervision with full access to teacher distributions, and black-box/API-constrained methods relying on text outputs or scalar scores) and self-distillation (where the model generates its own training signal, typically through privileged information or by exploiting internal asymmetries).
 - **Training Dynamics and Efficiency:** This axis covers strategies for stabilizing and accelerating the on-policy training loop. Categories include token and sample weighting (e.g., TIP, SCOPE), curriculum and difficulty adaptation (e.g., PACED, TCOD), and compute optimization (e.g., FOPD, Lightning-OPD).

3. **Consolidation of Success Conditions and Failure Modes:** The survey systematically gathers empirical evidence and theoretical explanations for when OPD is effective and when it encounters difficulties. It identifies key success conditions, such as compatible reasoning patterns between teacher and student, and the teacher providing new, transferable capabilities. Concurrently, it catalogues recurring failure modes, including the "flawed prefix trap" (where teacher feedback on student errors becomes unreliable), self-play saturation (mode collapse in self-distillation), diversity collapse, and the calibration-capability gap (where students become overconfident despite improved performance). Agentic collapse in multi-turn settings is also detailed.

4. **Cross-Regime Comparative Analysis:** The authors conduct a comparative analysis across different teacher access regimes (white-box, black-box, self-distillation). This involves summarizing representative methods within each category (using tables for comparison) and discussing the trade-offs and selection factors relevant to each regime. For instance, white-box access allows for dense token-level supervision, while black-box access necessitates sequence-level or reward-based signals.

5. **Integration with RL and Imitation Learning:** The survey explicitly formalizes the connection between OPD and KL-constrained reinforcement learning, demonstrating that many OPD objectives can be viewed as special cases of policy optimization with different reward definitions and constraint strengths. This integration bridges previously separate research communities and suggests that advancements in one field can often be transferred to the other.

6. **Review of Applications and Open Problems:** The methodology extends to reviewing practical industrial deployments across various domains (e.g., reasoning, code, multimodal, agentic systems) and highlighting key open research problems, such as the development of distillation-specific scaling laws, uncertainty-aware teacher feedback, and lifelong distillation.

Through this multi-faceted approach, the survey aims to provide a structured, comprehensive, and theoretically grounded understanding of OPD, moving beyond a collection of individual techniques to a unified scientific discipline.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey synthesizes extensive research on On-Policy Distillation (OPD), presenting a unified framework and organizing methods along three design axes. The main findings and results are summarized below:

#### 4.1. Unified Theoretical Framework

The survey formalizes OPD as **f-divergence minimization over student-sampled trajectories**. This framework unites various OPD algorithms, showing that different choices of f-divergence (Forward KL, Reverse KL, JSD) lead to distinct optimization behaviors (mode-covering, mode-seeking, symmetric). This formalization highlights that OPD directly addresses the **exposure bias** problem by having the student generate its own training data, mirroring deployment conditions, thereby reducing compounding error from O(ϵT²) (off-policy) to O(ϵT) (on-policy) as per the DAgger theorem.

#### 4.2. Taxonomy of Methods

The literature is organized along three core design axes, demonstrating the breadth and interconnections of OPD techniques:

1. **Objective Functions and Optimization:**
 - **Fixed Divergence Objectives:** Early methods (e.g., GKD, MiniLLM, DistiLLM) applied standard f-divergences (FKL, RKL, JSD) or their skewed variants (DistiLLM) uniformly. Reverse KL is often preferred for reasoning tasks (mode-seeking), while Forward KL for diverse generation (mode-covering). Sequence-level Reverse KL can be reformulated as policy gradient RL (MiniLLM), linking distillation to RL.
 - **Adaptive Divergence Objectives:** Recognizing that a single divergence is suboptimal for heterogeneous token distributions within a sequence, adaptive methods (e.g., EOPD, AOPD) dynamically select or blend divergences based on local signals like teacher entropy or advantage sign. This allows for fine-grained control, e.g., applying FKL on high-entropy (uncertain) teacher tokens and RKL on low-entropy (certain) ones.
 - **RL-Augmented Objectives:** These methods (e.g., G-OPD, RLKD, AlignDistil) integrate external reward signals beyond the teacher's next-token distribution. G-OPD formalizes OPD as KL-constrained RL, enabling "reward extrapolation" where students can surpass teacher capabilities. Other approaches use structural rewards (RLKD) or DPO-style preference objectives (AlignDistil) to provide task-level guidance.

2. **Signal Source and Teacher Architecture:**
 - **External Teacher Distillation:**
 - **White-Box Logit Supervision:** This involves full access to the teacher's token-level probability distributions (e.g., DSKD for cross-architecture alignment, MPD for reasoning compression, BRTS for teacher rollout selection). It enables the densest supervision but requires co-hosting the teacher model.
 - **Black-Box and API-Constrained Distillation:** When only text outputs or scalar scores are available (e.g., Lion, GAD, OVD), methods resort to adversarial distribution matching, verbal feedback curricula, or verbal score distillation. ROPD introduced rubric-based semantic scoring, showing high sample efficiency without logit access.
 - **Self-Distillation:** The model itself generates the supervisory signal.
 - **Privileged Information (PI):** The student serves as both teacher and student, with the teacher variant conditioned on privileged information (e.g., ground-truth answers in OPSD, retrieved documents in GATES, constitutional instructions, or future observations). This internalizes behaviors without an external strong model.
 - **Pure Self-Distillation:** Extracts training signals from the model’s own output distribution without auxiliary input, often by exploiting rollout diversity or architectural self-training (e.g., SDFT for forgetting resistance, MTP-SD for architectural improvement).
 - **External Feedback:** Self-distillation is augmented with external signals from verifiers, environments, or multi-agent interactions to prevent self-reinforcing errors and ground learning (e.g., SD-ZERO, SDPO, RLSD).

3. **Training Efficiency and Stabilization:**
 - **Token and Sample Weighting:** Addresses the "flawed prefix trap" where teacher feedback on student errors becomes unreliable. Methods like TIP and SCOPE assign adaptive weights to tokens or entire rollouts based on reliability, entropy, or divergence, concentrating learning on informative signals. SOD applies step-level reweighting for tool-integrated reasoning.
 - **Curriculum and Difficulty Adaptation:** Proactively selects training prompts to maximize learning efficiency. PACED uses a Beta-kernel to sample prompts at the student's "competence frontier." TCOD applies a temporal curriculum for multi-turn tasks, progressively expanding supervision horizons. Off-policy cold-starts (Li et al., 2026j) are often used to reduce initial "thinking-pattern mismatch."
 - **Compute Optimization:** Aims to reduce the substantial overhead of on-policy generation. Techniques include prefix truncation (FOPD), offline teacher caching (Lightning-OPD), asynchronous generation-training decoupling (NPD), speculative knowledge distillation (SKD), and dynamic trajectory pruning (Prune-OPD).

#### 4.3. Success Conditions and Failure Modes

- **Success Conditions:** Effective OPD requires compatible reasoning patterns between teacher and student, and the teacher must offer genuinely new capabilities. Optimal learning occurs when there's an "exploitable gap" where the teacher is sufficiently better but not entirely mismatched with the student's initial capabilities.
- **Failure Modes:** Common pathologies include the "flawed prefix trap" (unreliable teacher signals on OOD student contexts), gradient SNR collapse (no useful signal on very hard/easy prompts), "epistemic suppression" (loss of uncertainty awareness in self-distillation), "self-play saturation" (mode collapse in self-distillation without external grounding), "diversity collapse" (precision over recall), "calibration-capability gap" (overconfidence despite improved accuracy), and agentic collapse in multi-turn settings (e.g., trajectory structure erosion, teacher dynamics collapse).

#### 4.4. Industrial Deployments and Emerging Domains

- **Industrial Patterns:** OPD is increasingly integrated into LLM development. Common patterns include two-phase pipelines (off-policy SFT + on-policy refinement, e.g., Qwen3), model consolidation (merging multiple experts into one, e.g., DeepSeek-V4, KAT-Coder-V2), multi-budget reasoning (ORBIT), and agentic distillation (TCOD, MAD-OPD).
- **Emerging Domains:** OPD is being applied to multimodal LLMs (e.g., VOLD, CORD, VISD), GUI agents (LiteGUI), embodied intelligence and physical reasoning (OPD-AV, VLA-OPD), medical agents (Healthcare AI Gym), protein sequence design (ProteinOPD), hardware description generation (RWOPD), and test-time adaptation (TTS), demonstrating its versatility beyond text-only, single-turn generation.

#### 4.5. Unified Theoretical Perspectives

OPD, RL, and preference optimization are shown to occupy a **continuous spectrum** of optimization objectives. The f-divergence framework subsumes many, and the equivalence of OPD to KL-constrained RL provides a bridge between these fields. Analysis suggests that KD+RL hybrid approaches leverage KD for gradient stability and RL for exploration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey of On-Policy Distillation (OPD) for Large Language Models (LLMs) contributes to the scientific understanding and practical application of advanced model training techniques. Its significance and potential impact span several dimensions:

#### 5.1. Scientific Significance

1. **Unified Framework:** The survey's primary contribution is providing a unified theoretical framework for OPD, formalizing it as f-divergence minimization over student-sampled trajectories. This addresses the fragmentation of the field across knowledge distillation, reinforcement learning, and imitation learning communities, offering a common language and understanding that can facilitate cross-pollination of ideas.
2. **Systematic Taxonomy:** The proposed design-centric taxonomy, organizing methods by objective function, signal source, and training dynamics, clarifies the relationships between diverse techniques. This systematic categorization helps researchers identify unexplored design spaces and understand the trade-offs inherent in different OPD approaches.
3. **Comprehensive Analysis of Failure Modes:** By consolidating success conditions and recurring failure modes (e.g., flawed prefix trap, self-play saturation, epistemic suppression), the survey provides critical diagnostic insights. This understanding is instrumental for designing more robust and stable OPD pipelines, moving beyond trial-and-error to principled mitigation strategies.
4. **Bridging Research Communities:** The explicit formal connection between OPD and KL-constrained reinforcement learning is a significant theoretical bridge. This connection implies that advances in RL (e.g., trust regions, variance reduction) can be directly applied to OPD, and vice versa, accelerating progress in both fields.
5. **Setting the Research Agenda:** By identifying key open problems such as distillation scaling laws, uncertainty-aware teacher feedback, and agent-level distillation, the survey lays out a clear agenda for future research, guiding efforts toward addressing the most pressing challenges and advancing the state of the art.

#### 5.2. Practical Significance and Potential Impact

1. **Cost Reduction and Accessibility:** OPD enables the transfer of capabilities from large, costly frontier models to smaller, more efficient student models. This significantly reduces inference costs, making advanced LLM capabilities more accessible to a broader range of organizations and users, including those with limited computational resources. Self-distillation methods, in particular, allow for capability improvement without external teacher models, further democratizing access.
2. **Enhanced LLM Capabilities:** OPD addresses the exposure bias inherent in off-policy training, leading to student models that are more robust and perform better in deployment, especially on complex reasoning, code generation, and multi-turn agentic tasks where compounding errors are prevalent. This translates to more reliable and capable AI systems in real-world applications.
3. **Improved Training Stability and Efficiency:** The insights into training dynamics, including token/sample weighting, curriculum learning, and compute optimization techniques, provide practical guidelines for improving the stability and efficiency of OPD pipelines. This helps reduce the substantial computational overhead of on-policy training, making it a more viable option for industrial-scale deployments.
4. **Guidance for Industrial Deployment:** The survey documents various industrial deployment patterns (e.g., two-phase pipelines, model consolidation, multi-budget reasoning, agentic distillation, safety infrastructure). This provides practitioners with proven strategies and architectural considerations for integrating OPD into their LLM development workflows.
5. **Advancements in Emerging Domains:** The application of OPD to multimodal, embodied, medical, and privacy-preserving AI domains highlights its versatility. This suggests that the core principles of OPD can be adapted to tackle novel challenges in diverse scientific and engineering fields, leading to breakthroughs in specialized AI applications.
6. **Ethical and Safety Implications:** OPD offers mechanisms for controlled knowledge transfer, allowing for the selective distillation of aligned behaviors and safety constraints from teacher models. This is crucial for developing safer AI systems, particularly in contexts like multilingual safety alignment. However, the survey also implicitly points to the risk of misuse if powerful capabilities are transferred without adequate safety measures.
7. **Standardization of Evaluation:** By highlighting reproducibility challenges and the need for standardized benchmarking protocols, the survey advocates for practices that can improve the comparability and reliability of research findings in the rapidly evolving LLM landscape.

In summary, this survey not only consolidates existing knowledge but also provides a structured framework that can guide future research and development, positioning OPD as a critical and evolving component in the ecosystem of large language models.
