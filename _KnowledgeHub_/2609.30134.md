# Training-free Behavior Cloning

> [!summary] Summary
> Behavior Predictive Control (BPC) builds imitation policies without end-to-end training: it keeps raw demonstrations, retrieves windows via a learned action-aware metric, reconstructs the live observation-action history over a Hankel-style bank to carry coefficients into the demonstrated future, and adds a closed-form random-feature residual. It is competitive with pi-0.5, beating it on some tasks, while fitting in seconds and running at 75 Hz on a Jetson Orin Nano.

## Problem

- Neural behavior cloning compresses demonstrations into weights, so individual actions cannot be attributed to examples and deleting a bad demonstration requires costly refitting.
- Fitting or adapting diffusion and vision-language-action policies takes hours of server-class GPU compute, blocking rapid deployment, frequent revision, and resource-constrained onboard hardware.
- Existing retrieval policies keep demonstrations explicit but select or average nearby actions, never reconciling mismatch between the live history and recorded trajectories.

## Method

- ==Future-informed retrieval== fits a ==Mahalanobis metric==, by action ridge regression or sparsemax-weighted Fisher LDA over ==random Fourier features==, ranking demonstration windows whose histories predict similar expert continuations.
- A regularized ==Hankel-based action-continuation prior== solves a sum-to-one least-squares fit of coefficients reconstructing the live history, then applies those same coefficients to the retrieved future action blocks.
- A shared-support residual correction pools random Fourier features of retrieved observations, actions, and query differences, and a ridge-regression linear head predicts the additive action correction.

## Results

- On real xArm6 tasks **BPC** scored **34/40** on Switch versus **8/40** for fine-tuned pi-0.5, with comparable Coffee (**36 vs 39**) and Drawer (**38 vs 40**).
- Across eight MimicGen visuomotor tasks **BPC** averaged **73%** success, near pi-0.5's **77%** and SDP's **76%**, far above retrieval baseline DARP's **52%**.
- Ablations drop mean success from **73%** to **45%** without the nonlinear correction, **62%** without history-conditioned continuation, and **65%** without learned retrieval; fitting takes under **3 minutes** at **200k** windows.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Signed affine coefficients over a local support can correct first-order history mismatch that nonnegative kNN averaging cannot, at the cost of amplified curvature error.
> - Keeping raw data in the policy buys provenance, editability, and free task-progress estimates, capabilities that architectural compression removes rather than trades away.
> - Retrieval over histories mitigates perceptual aliasing that single-observation policies suffer, but coverage gaps remain fatal: no recovery action exists outside the demonstration bank.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

In the xArm Switch task, the robot must carry a bag of beans to the plate opposite its starting plate. Remembering where it came from matters while the bag is in transit. One way to handle this is to keep robot demonstrations available at run time instead of compressing them into a single end-to-end policy. The robot then consults recent history and stored demonstrations, rather than relying only on a single current observation.

Behavior Predictive Control (BPC) takes this approach. On Switch, it succeeded in 34 of 40 trials, versus 8 of 40 for fine-tuned π0.5. Both methods used the same demonstrations and visuomotor inputs in this xArm experiment. The authors attribute much of this gap to BPC's history-based orientation. The comparison does not isolate history as the only cause, however: they also cite temporal filtering as a way to disambiguate progress.

### 2. From examples to a next action

Plain retrieval is brittle because a live robot's history may not exactly match any stored trajectory. BPC's key move is to find demonstration windows that help explain the robot's recent observations and actions. It then reuses those same windows to continue the demonstrated actions and adds a fitted correction.

Each online step has three stages:

1. **Retrieve.** BPC retrieves windows using a learned, action-aware metric, so histories are judged partly by the futures they predict.
1. **Continue.** It chooses coefficients that make a weighted combination of the windows' pasts reconstruct the live observation–action history. It then applies those same coefficients to the windows' following actions.
1. **Correct.** It adds a correction fitted on the same retrieved support, using pooled random Fourier features of candidate observations, actions, and observation differences. Here, a random Fourier feature is a fixed feature mapping that lets a linear head express a nonlinear correction.

This produces a one-step action, and the controller repeats the process at the next time step. The reconstruction is regularized, and the coefficients can be signed. This makes BPC different from nearest-neighbor copying or ordinary positive-weight averaging. BPC therefore avoids end-to-end policy training, but it still fits some components.

Conceptual pipeline, not a measured result. Live history (images, proprioception, and past actions) enters on the left. Stage 1 retrieves K neighbors. Stage 2 fits coefficients to continue actions from those windows. Stage 3 pools random Fourier features and uses a linear head to predict a correction δu(t), which is added to the action prior to form the outgoing action.

### 3. Does reconstructing history matter?

The MimicGen ablation tests whether the added mechanism is more than a new way to retrieve neighbors. It covers eight tabletop simulation tasks and reports success rates in percent over 300 rollouts per task. The reported mean averages the eight task rates.

Each ablation removes one component:

- **No nonlinear correction (NNC)** keeps only the base policy.
- **No history-conditioned continuation (NHC)** replaces history-conditioned continuation with uniform averaging over k-nearest neighbors.
- **No learned retrieval (NLR)** selects top-K candidates by raw input-space L2 distance.

Full BPC's mean was 73%. Removing the nonlinear correction yielded 45%, removing history-conditioned continuation yielded 62%, and removing learned retrieval yielded 65%. The strongest measured loss came from removing the residual correction.

These are success-rate means, and they do not prove that any one component alone caused all of BPC's gains. Scores also vary by task: full BPC's Nut result is only 13%. The ablation supports component importance in this setup. It does not show universal necessity or guarantee successful control.

Success percentages for NHC, NNC, NLR, and BPC (Ours) on Square, Stack, Coffee, Hammer, Mug, Nut, Stack3, and Thread. Mean summarizes the eight named tasks and is not a ninth task. BPC is the common reference; it is not best on every individual task.

### 4. Data stays in the loop

BPC keeps a window's supporting history and its coefficients. As a result, its action is linked to explicit demonstrations rather than only opaque network weights. The authors present this as a route to inspecting provenance and revising the bank.

The approach has clear limits. BPC depends on demonstration coverage and on histories that identify the right action. Large signed coefficients can amplify approximation error. The analysis also does not guarantee closed-loop stability or task completion after correction.
