# Validate on Sim, Detect on Real -- Model Selection for Domain Randomization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Intel Labs and Technion introduced VSDR, a method for efficiently selecting the best sim2real policies for robotic tasks by combining simulation-based validation with out-of-distribution detection using minimal real-world observations. The approach achieved higher policy ranking accuracy than individual components or baselines, demonstrating robust performance with as few as 64 real-world observations.

## Problem

- The high cost, time, and safety concerns associated with evaluating numerous trained sim2real policies directly on physical robots, creating a bottleneck in development.
- Bridging the sim-to-real gap using Domain Randomization (DR) leads to a combinatorial explosion of hyper-parameters and many candidate policies, making selection difficult.
- Existing policy selection methods often require extensive real-world data or are task-specific, lacking generalizability and data efficiency.

## Method

- A combined policy score, **VSDR** (Validate on Sim, Detect on Real), multiplies normalized ==simulation validation== and real-world ==out-of-distribution (OOD) detection== scores, favoring policies that perform well in both.
- Policies are validated in simulation on a held-out set of ==domain-randomization (DR)==-generated environments to assess generalization capability.
- Real-world OOD scores are computed by fitting ==Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)== to feature activations from simulated training data and then evaluating the ==log-likelihood== of minimal real-world observations under these GMMs.

## Results

- VSDR consistently outperformed individual components and baselines (OPC/SoftOPC) in ranking accuracy, achieving better Spearman correlation with ground-truth rankings in **86-100%** of evaluated cases.
- The method demonstrated robust performance across different real-world data collection protocols (Expert, Sparse-expert, Initial), effectively using as few as **64** real-world observations.
- Richer randomization (DR-heavy) simulation validation led to superior VSDR rankings (up to **53%** improvement over sim validation alone without DR), and earlier GMM-fitting layers (e.g., image encoder, `fc0`) improved Spearman correlation by **38%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining simulation-based validation with real-world OOD detection provides a more accurate and robust policy ranking than either component used individually, leveraging their complementary information.
> - Policies that perceive the real world as 'in-distribution' (lower OOD score) relative to their diverse simulated training environments exhibit better sim2real transfer.
> - Using feature activations from earlier neural network layers (e.g., image encoder) for GMM fitting yields more effective, highly data-efficient OOD detection, accurately ranking policies with merely dozens of easily obtainable real-world observations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of robotic control and reinforcement learning (RL), specifically addressing challenges related to "sim2real" transfer. Sim2real involves training control policies in simulated environments and then deploying them on physical robots. This approach is motivated by the high cost, safety concerns, and time consumption associated with training RL policies directly on real hardware. Modern simulators offer advantages such as parallelization and realistic physical interactions, making simulation-based training attractive.

However, a fundamental challenge in sim2real is the "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in simulation may not perform optimally in reality due to discrepancies between the simulated and real environments. Domain Randomization (DR) is a prominent technique developed to bridge this gap. DR involves training policies across a diverse set of randomly generated simulated environments, varying attributes like textures, lighting, and dynamics. The objective is to encourage policies to learn robust features that generalize well to unseen real-world conditions.

A practical issue arises from the extensive hyper-parameter tuning required for both RL algorithms (e.g., learning rate, batch size) and DR methods (e.g., randomized elements, randomization type, frequency of randomization). This combinatorial explosion often leads to a large number of trained policies. Selecting the most effective policy for real-world deployment from this large set typically necessitates costly and time-consuming real-world evaluation of each candidate policy. This bottleneck significantly impedes the development and application of sim2real techniques.

Existing related work includes offline RL methods that propose metrics to evaluate policies using previously collected data, often assuming that only the policy differs between training and testing. Off-Policy Classification (OPC) is a notable baseline that uses a trained critic to classify successful or unsuccessful real trajectories. However, such methods often require large amounts of specific real-world data. Concurrently, Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection, also known as novelty or outlier detection, has been extensively studied, with approaches ranging from classical methods like one-class SVMs to modern deep generative models. Recent work in image domains leverages deep network feature activations and models their distributions (e.g., Gaussian, flow-based models) to identify OOD samples. This paper integrates concepts from OOD detection and offline policy evaluation to address the specific problem of efficient policy selection in sim2real settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for ranking and selecting the best sim2real policies without requiring extensive and costly real-world execution. The authors identify a critical bottleneck in the practical application of sim2real methods: the need to evaluate numerous trained policies on a real robot to determine their actual performance, which can be prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. For instance, the robotic grasping task used in their experiments requires 60-75 minutes to evaluate a single policy, and typical deep RL workflows with DR can generate hundreds of such policies.

The core motivation is to enable a faster policy evaluation cycle that does not necessitate running policies on a physical robot. To achieve this, the authors propose an evaluation method guided by three desiderata:
1. **Require only easy-to-obtain real-world data**: The method should minimize the effort and resources needed to collect data from the real environment.
2. **Accurate ranking of policies based on their real-world performance**: The method's ranking should correlate strongly with actual performance on the real robot.
3. **Generally applicable to a wide range of robotic tasks**: The approach should not be task-specific but rather broadly usable across different robotic applications.

The central idea is to utilize a predefined, policy-independent set of real-world observations to evaluate all candidate policies, employing out-of-distribution (OOD) detection techniques. The underlying hypothesis is that if a DR-trained policy is truly robust to domain variations, the real-world environment should be perceived as "in-distribution" relative to the diverse simulated environments it was trained on. Consequently, policies that exhibit lower OOD scores (i.e., the real world appears less novel or out-of-distribution) are hypothesized to transfer better. This OOD-based evaluation serves as a "unit test" before any real-world policy execution.

However, the authors acknowledge that OOD scores alone can be inaccurate and sensitive to the specific OOD detection method or its parameters. To address this limitation, their main contribution is a combined policy score, termed Validate on Sim, Detect on Real (VSDR). This score integrates the OOD detection on real-world data with an evaluation in simulation on held-out DR environment attributes. The motivation for this combination is the hypothesis that these two components are complementary: simulation validation gauges a policy's performance within the randomized domain space, while OOD detection assesses how well the policy's learned features align with real-world observations. The combined score aims to leverage the strengths of both, providing a more robust and accurate ranking.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, VSDR (Validate on Sim, Detect on Real), combines two components: validation in a simulated environment and out-of-distribution (OOD) detection using real-world observations. The overall process involves generating two scores for each policy and then merging them into a single VSDR score.

#### 3.1. Validation in Simulation

For each policy $\pi_i$, a "validation score" ($s_i$) is computed by evaluating its performance on a set of simulated domains specifically held out from the policy's training process. These validation domains are sampled from a predefined DR distribution, $P_{DR}^v(\phi)$. Unlike training where DR parameters might change frequently, during validation, a new domain $\phi_k$ is drawn only at the beginning of an episode and remains fixed for its duration, mirroring a static real-world setting. The performance metric (e.g., cumulative reward, success rate) on these validation episodes serves as the simulation validation score. The rationale is that a policy robust to DR should perform well across a range of unseen but similar simulated domains, indicating its generalization capability.

#### 3.2. OOD Detection in Real World

The second component measures how "novel" real-world observations are with respect to the observations encountered during a policy's training. This involves two sub-steps: real-world data collection and OOD detection.

1. **OOD Detection Mechanism**: Inspired by prior work, the method uses a policy's neural network to detect OOD. For each policy $\pi_i$, it is run in its *trained* simulation setting to collect a dataset of feature activations ($A_S$) from a specific layer (e.g., output of an image encoder or an MLP layer). A parametric model, specifically an $N$-component Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), is then fitted to these simulated feature activations using maximum likelihood. This GMM represents the distribution of "in-distribution" features for that policy. Subsequently, real-world observations ($D_R$) are fed through the same policy network to obtain their feature activations ($A_R$) from the *same* layer. The log-likelihood of $A_R$ under the fitted GMM ($G_i$) is calculated, yielding an OOD score ($r_i$). A lower log-likelihood indicates that the real-world observations are more out-of-distribution, suggesting a poorer sim2real transfer.

2. **Real-World Data Collection Protocols**: The authors propose three protocols for collecting real-world observations ($D_R$) for OOD evaluation, emphasizing that this data should be independent of the RL training process and easy to obtain:
 - **Expert**: A comprehensive set of observations sampled from full demonstrations of an expert (e.g., scripted or teleoperated) successfully solving the task.
 - **Sparse-expert**: A sparsely sub-sampled subset of observations from the Expert protocol.
 - **Initial**: Only observations from the initial state of each episode, randomly sampled from the initial state distribution ($P_{init}$).
 These protocols are designed to gather task-relevant states that a policy might visit, enabling an assessment of visual domain shift without requiring the policy to actively execute actions on the real robot.

#### 3.3. Merging the Validation and OOD Scores

To combine the simulation validation score ($s_i$) and the real-world OOD score ($r_i$) into a single VSDR score, a two-step process is used:
1. **Min-max normalization**: Both score vectors (S for simulation validation and R for OOD) across all policies are min-max normalized to a range (e.g.,). This ensures that both components contribute proportionally to the final score despite potentially different scales.
2. **Multiplication**: The normalized scores ($s'_i$ and $r'_i$) for each policy are multiplied to produce the final VSDR score: $VSDR_i = s'_i \cdot r'_i$. The motivation for multiplication is to favor policies that perform well in *both* simulation validation and OOD detection, reflecting the complementary nature of these two evaluation aspects.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

The method was evaluated on a robotic grasping task, specifically the "Lift" task from Robosuite, using a 7DoF Franka Panda robotic arm.
- **Input/Output**: The agent receives observations from a fixed Intel RealSense D415 RGB camera (224x224 image after processing) and proprioceptive data (joint angles, end-effector pose, gripper position). Actions consist of 3D Cartesian position changes, vertical axis rotation, and a binary gripper command.
- **RL Algorithm**: Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) was used, with a ResNet18 encoder for image processing followed by 3-layer MLPs for actor and critic networks.
- **Policies and DR Configurations**: 60 policies were trained, originating from 20 distinct DR configurations (3 seeds per configuration). DR configurations varied significantly in terms of randomization intensity (e.g., "DR-heavy" vs. "DR-mild"), types of visual elements randomized (textures, lighting, camera), texture sources (programmatically generated, DTD dataset), and the frequency of applying new domains during training (e.g., once per episode, every 1/2/5 simulation steps). "DR-off" represented no randomization.
- **Baselines**: The proposed VSDR was compared against SoftOPC and OPC (Off-Policy Classification) metrics.
- **Ground Truth (GT) Evaluation**: Each policy's real-world performance was assessed over 49 grasp attempts (a 7x7 grid of initial cube positions), with three metrics: cumulative Reward, Success rate, and Strict-Success rate. This comprehensive real-world evaluation spanned approximately 130 hours of robot interaction. The Spearman rank correlation coefficient ($\rho$) between the method's ranking and the GT ranking served as the primary evaluation metric.
- **Hyper-parameter Exploration**: Extensive testing was performed across different validation in simulation types (DR-heavy, DR-mild, DR-off), five different neural network layers for GMM fitting (img_encoder, fc0, relu0, fc1, relu1), various numbers of GMM components (1, 2, 3, 5, 10), and the three real-world data collection protocols (Expert: 704 observations; Sparse-expert: 100 observations; Initial: 64 observations).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research conducted an extensive evaluation to assess the effectiveness and robustness of the VSDR method, comparing it against baselines and analyzing its sensitivity to various hyper-parameters.

#### 4.1. VSDR Performance Against Individual Components and Baselines

- **VSDR vs. Individual Components**: The combined VSDR score demonstrated superior performance compared to using either simulation validation or OOD detection independently. VSDR rankings achieved better Spearman rank correlation with ground-truth (GT) rankings in 87% of cases when compared to rankings based solely on validation in simulation. Similarly, VSDR improved correlation in 86% of cases compared to rankings based only on OOD detection in the real world (GMM-only rankings). This outcome supports the hypothesis that the two components provide complementary information.
- **VSDR vs. Baselines (OPC and SoftOPC)**: VSDR consistently outperformed the Off-Policy Classification (OPC) and SoftOPC baselines, achieving better correlation in 100% of the evaluated cases. This result is significant given that the baselines were provided with a substantial advantage: they were computed using 2940 real-world episodes (approximately 53,000 transitions) collected during the GT evaluation itself, essentially training on ground-truth data. In contrast, VSDR utilized only dozens of real-world observations collected independently of the trained policies. The study also noted that combining the simulation validation score with OPC/SoftOPC improved these baselines, but VSDR still yielded better correlations in 92% and 88% of cases, respectively, further emphasizing its robust performance and practical advantage.
- **Real-world Consistency**: The real-world GT evaluation itself showed high consistency, with Spearman correlations between two independent evaluation cycles ranging from 0.93 to 0.98 across different performance metrics (Reward, Success, Strict-Success). This indicates the reliability of the ground-truth rankings used for evaluation.

#### 4.2. Analysis of Hyper-parameter Choices

- **Data Collection Protocol for Real-World Observations**: The choice among Expert (704 observations), Sparse-expert (100 observations), and Initial (64 observations) data collection protocols for OOD detection yielded similar aggregate results. This indicates that VSDR is robust to the specific method of real-world data collection and is highly data-efficient, requiring merely dozens of observations. The study also found that sub-sampling different sets of observations within the Sparse-expert protocol did not significantly impact results, demonstrating robustness to minor variations in the collected data.
- **Choice of Neural Network Layer for GMM Fitting**:
 - Fitting GMMs to ReLU feature activations generally resulted in slightly worse VSDR rankings; 26 out of 30 cases where VSDR performed worse than sim validation only were linked to ReLU layers.
 - Using feature activations from earlier layers in the network (e.g., `img_encoder` and `fc0`) tended to produce better rankings. VSDR rankings based on GMMs fitted to these early layers improved Spearman correlation over sim validation only by an average of 38%, compared to an average of 11% improvement when using subsequent layers. This suggests that more abstract, higher-level features closer to the output might be less sensitive to domain shifts relevant for sim2real transfer.
- **Validation in Simulation Settings**:
 - Using richer and more complex randomization (DR-heavy) for simulation validation led to better VSDR rankings, indicating that harder and more diverse simulated domains are more effective predictors for sim2real transfer.
 - The benefit of combining OOD (GMM-only) scores with simulation validation decreased as the complexity of the DR setting in simulation validation increased. For "DR-off" (no randomization), VSDR improved over sim validation only by an average of 53%; for "DR-mild," the average improvement was 8%; and for "DR-heavy," it was 5%.
 - Conversely, when using DR-heavy and DR-mild for simulation validation, VSDR almost always improved over GMM-only rankings (99% of cases). However, with DR-off, VSDR improved GMM-only rankings in only 60% of cases. These findings further underscore the complementary nature of the two scores, with OOD detection being particularly valuable when simulation validation alone is weak (e.g., without DR).
- **Number of GMM Components**: The number of components in the GMM (1, 2, 3, 5, 10) had a significant impact when policies were ranked solely based on GMM fitting, with 1- and 2-component GMMs performing noticeably worse. However, when these GMM scores were combined with simulation validation scores to form VSDR scores, the impact of the number of components on ranking accuracy was significantly reduced. This suggests that the strong information content from simulation validation helps mitigate the sensitivity of the OOD score to this specific hyper-parameter.

In summary, the empirical results confirm that VSDR's components are complementary. The simulation validation performance provides a robust baseline, and OOD detection in the real world contributes additional useful information, especially when simulation validation is less discriminative.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of sim2real transfer in robotics by addressing a critical practical bottleneck: the efficient and accurate selection of policies for real-world deployment. The VSDR method offers several notable implications:

1. **Reduced Real-World Evaluation Cost and Time**: The most immediate impact of VSDR is its ability to accurately rank sim2real policies using only a minimal amount of real-world data (dozens of observations). This drastically reduces the need for extensive, time-consuming, and potentially unsafe real-robot experiments for policy selection. This efficiency gain can accelerate the development and iteration cycles for robotic learning systems.
2. **Improved Practicality of Sim2Real**: By making policy selection more accessible, VSDR enhances the practical applicability of sim2real methodologies, especially for complex tasks involving deep reinforcement learning and domain randomization, where many candidate policies are often generated.
3. **Robustness and Data Efficiency**: The method's demonstrated robustness across different real-world data collection protocols (Expert, Sparse-expert, Initial) and its low data requirement (as few as 64 observations) make it a highly practical tool for researchers and engineers.
4. **Complementary Evaluation Strategy**: The finding that combining simulation validation with real-world OOD detection leads to superior and more stable policy rankings highlights the value of a multi-faceted evaluation strategy. This principle could inform future approaches to model selection in other domains facing similar distribution shift challenges.
5. **Generalizability**: While evaluated on a vision-based robotic grasping task, the underlying principles of VSDR—leveraging simulation performance and detecting real-world novelty in feature space—suggest potential for broad applicability across a wide range of robotic tasks, particularly those involving image-based observations.

Potential future work, as noted by the authors, includes extending this approach to domains where physical properties (e.g., mass, friction) are randomized using DR, beyond the current focus on visual appearance. This would broaden the scope and impact of the VSDR framework for more comprehensive sim2real transfer challenges. Overall, VSDR represents a step towards making sim2real transfer more amenable to rapid development and deployment in real-world robotic applications.
