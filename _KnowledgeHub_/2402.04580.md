# A Comprehensive Survey of Cross-Domain Policy Transfer for Embodied Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey of cross-domain policy transfer for embodied agents. It formalizes domains, domain-dependent MDPs, and transfer via a policy-mapping operator, then splits domain gaps into appearance, viewpoint, dynamics, and morphology, plus multi-gap settings. Methods are grouped into five families: source-domain manipulation, correspondence/correction learning, domain-invariant distributions, domain-invariant features, and hierarchical control. It closes with five open challenges.

## Problem

- Target-domain data collection is costly and unsafe, so policies are trained in simulation or lab source domains whose environments and embodiments diverge from deployment.
- Existing transfer approaches are fragmented across gap types, learning paradigms, and data assumptions, obscuring the connections and differences between strategies and blocking a holistic view.
- No prior review unifies notation for cross-domain settings; terms like embodiment, morphology, and modality gaps are used inconsistently, and most methods target only one gap.

## Method

- Formal scaffolding: definitions of a domain as environment, embodiment, and their dynamics; a ==domain-dependent MDP==; and cross-domain transfer as a ==policy-space map h== whose target return must beat the untransferred source policy.
- A gap taxonomy separating environment-side ==appearance== and ==viewpoint gaps== (jointly perception gaps) from ==dynamics gaps== and embodiment-side ==morphology/modality gaps==, with a fifth multi-gap category for compound settings.
- A methodology axis cutting across the taxonomy: ==source-domain manipulation== (randomization, calibration), ==correspondence and reward/dynamics correction learning==, ==domain-invariant distribution matching==, ==domain-invariant feature and skill learning==, and ==hierarchical high-level/low-level decoupling==.

## Results

- No new experiments; this is a review. Its evidence is organizational: **four** gap categories plus a multi-gap setting, **five** methodology families, and **five** open-challenge directions covering the surveyed literature.
- Comparative analysis credits source-domain randomization and calibration with industrial-scale success but flags under-modeling of non-rigidity, gear dead zones, wear-and-tear, and rolling friction as failure modes.
- State-distribution-matching methods are judged to handle dynamics and morphology gaps only, failing under visual mismatch and long-horizon tasks; feature-invariance methods lack the temporal alignment correspondence learning provides.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diagnose the gap before picking a method: appearance/viewpoint gaps distort observations, dynamics gaps distort transitions, morphology gaps distort state and action spaces themselves.
> - Invariant features are reusable but temporally blind; explicit state-action correspondences align MDPs in time. Long-horizon tasks need the progress awareness only temporal alignment supplies.
> - Caution: most work assumes a static target domain and two domains only. Wear-and-tear, multi-source data, and off-domain policy evaluation remain unaddressed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The burgeoning fields of robot learning and embodied AI, which integrate advances from computer vision, decision-making, and natural language processing, face a critical challenge: the insatiable demand for large quantities of high-quality, unbiased training data. Collecting such data in real-world target domains (e.g., autonomous driving scenarios, industrial robot control) is often prohibitively costly, time-consuming, and fraught with safety concerns. Consequently, researchers frequently resort to leveraging data from more accessible and cost-effective source domains, such as simulations or controlled laboratory environments, for rapid iteration and model development.

However, a fundamental hurdle arises from the inherent "domain gaps" – significant discrepancies between these source domains and the real-world target domains. These gaps can manifest in various ways, from visual appearances and sensor configurations to underlying physical dynamics and even the morphology of the embodied agents themselves. Policies trained solely in source domains often perform poorly or fail entirely when deployed in target environments, a phenomenon commonly referred to as the "sim-to-real gap."

Cross-domain policy transfer has emerged as a crucial research direction to bridge these gaps, aiming to learn policies that are robust and transferable from source to target domains. Despite its growing importance, the existing body of work in this area is highly fragmented. Researchers have tackled diverse types of domain gaps using a multitude of learning paradigms, data constraints, and underlying assumptions. This fragmentation has created a complex and opaque research landscape, making it difficult for new and even experienced researchers to grasp the connections and differentiations among various strategies, identify overarching insights, and pinpoint promising future directions.

This survey paper addresses this fragmentation head-on by providing the first comprehensive and systematic review of cross-domain policy transfer methods for embodied agents. It seeks to bring order to the chaos, offering a unified framework and a holistic understanding of the field. By clearly categorizing domain gaps and systematically classifying existing methodologies, the authors provide an invaluable resource that distills years of disparate research into a coherent narrative. This work is essential for accelerating progress in embodied AI, as it lays the groundwork for more principled and integrated approaches to developing real-world deployable robotic systems, moving beyond ad-hoc solutions to a more structured scientific inquiry.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this comprehensive survey stems from the critical need to enable the real-world deployment of embodied agents, which is currently hampered by significant data bottlenecks and the pervasive problem of domain gaps. The high cost, inefficiency, and safety risks associated with collecting vast amounts of data directly in target environments force researchers to rely on readily available source domains like simulators. However, policies trained in these controlled environments inevitably suffer from performance degradation when transferred to the real world due to intricate environmental and embodiment discrepancies.

Given the scattered and specialized nature of existing research in cross-domain policy transfer, the authors identify a pressing need for a unified and holistic understanding of the field. The current fragmentation, arising from diverse domain gap types, varied learning paradigms, and distinct data constraints, obscures underlying connections and differentiations, making it challenging for researchers to gain a comprehensive overview and identify promising avenues for new research.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of this survey are clearly articulated:

1. **Systematic Review and Unification:** To conduct the first comprehensive review of existing cross-domain policy transfer methods for embodied agents, unifying related notations and definitions to establish a common language for the field.
2. **Nuanced Categorization of Domain Gaps:** To provide a clear and fine-grained classification of different types of domain gaps, distinguishing between appearance, viewpoint, dynamics, and morphology gaps, and discussing their interconnections. This taxonomy serves as a foundational framework for understanding the problem space.
3. **Consolidation of Methods:** To organize and consolidate the vast diversity of existing approaches by mapping them to the identified domain gap categories, offering a structured view of how different methods address specific challenges.
4. **Extraction of Methodological Insights:** To distill and present overarching methodological insights and design considerations common across various approaches, providing a high-level understanding of the core techniques employed in cross-domain policy transfer.
5. **Identification of Open Challenges and Future Directions:** To summarize the limitations of current paradigms by outlining open challenges that remain beyond their capabilities and discussing potential future research directions, thereby serving as a roadmap for the field's advancement.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to provide a critical resource that illuminates the current state of cross-domain policy transfer, offers new insights, and ultimately accelerates future research and development in embodied AI, paving the way for more robust and versatile real-world robotic solutions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors undertake a systematic and comprehensive survey approach to organize and synthesize the fragmented landscape of cross-domain policy transfer for embodied agents. Their methodology is structured around several key steps:

1. **Unifying Terminology and Definitions:** The survey begins by establishing a foundational understanding, providing unified notations and definitions relevant to cross-domain settings. This includes formal definitions for a "Domain" (encompassing environment, embodiment, and their dynamics) and a "Domain-Dependent Markov Decision Process (D-MDP)." Crucially, "Cross-Domain Policy Transfer" is precisely defined in terms of improving target domain performance of a policy obtained from a source domain via a transfer method. This initial step ensures clarity and consistency throughout the review.

2. **Categorization of Domain Gaps:** A core component of their approach is the development of a nuanced taxonomy of domain gaps. They identify and meticulously differentiate four primary categories:
 - **Appearance Gaps:** Visual discrepancies (e.g., colors, textures, lighting) in observations.
 - **Viewpoint Gaps:** Inconsistencies in sensor configurations (e.g., camera positions/angles).
 - **Dynamics Gaps:** Differences in interaction dynamics between the embodiment and environment, or within the embodiment itself (e.g., friction, stiffness).
 - **Morphology Gaps:** Variations in the physical design of the robot (e.g., joint types, limb dimensions), which can also lead to dynamics and state/action space mismatches.
 They also discuss "visual gaps" (combining appearance and viewpoint), "perception gaps" (broader, including other sensors), "modality gaps" (variations in sensing/actuation modalities), and "embodiment gaps" (a more ego-centric term for internal physical properties and morphology). This detailed breakdown is crucial for systematically classifying methods.

3. **Mapping Methods to Domain Gaps:** The authors then consolidate a vast array of existing research papers, organizing them according to which of the four main domain gap categories they primarily address. For each category, they provide an overview of the challenges posed by that specific gap and detail the common strategies and specific techniques developed to mitigate it. This structured organization allows readers to quickly identify relevant methods for particular domain gap scenarios.

4. **Extraction of Overarching Methodological Insights:** Beyond categorizing by domain gap, the survey distills the underlying principles and common design considerations that span across different problem settings. They identify five overarching methodological paradigms:
 - **Source Domain Manipulation:** Altering the source environment (e.g., simulators) through techniques like domain randomization or system identification to better match target conditions.
 - **Learning Domain Correspondences / Corrections:** Developing mapping functions or corrective terms to align visual observations, states, actions, or rewards between domains.
 - **Identifying Domain-Invariant Distributions:** Seeking to match high-level data distributions (e.g., state visitation) across domains, based on assumptions about optimal policies.
 - **Learning Domain-Invariant Features:** Extracting abstract, task-relevant representations (e.g., semantic features, skills, sub-goals) that are robust to domain changes.
 - **Building Hierarchical Control Paradigms:** Decoupling policies into domain-independent high-level components and domain-specific low-level components, often combined with meta-learning.
 This classification provides a high-level conceptual framework for understanding the core strategies employed in the field.

5. **Identification of Open Challenges and Future Trends:** Finally, the authors look forward, summarizing the current limitations of existing approaches and outlining several critical open challenges. These include handling diverse sensor/actuator modalities, effectively leveraging multi-source data, enabling continual target fine-tuning, navigating the generalization-adaptation trade-off, and developing reliable off-domain policy evaluation metrics. This forward-looking perspective highlights crucial areas for future research and innovation.

By employing this systematic review, rigorous categorization, and insightful synthesis, the authors effectively bring clarity and structure to a highly complex and fragmented research area, providing a valuable resource for both newcomers and seasoned researchers in embodied AI.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

As a comprehensive survey, this paper's main "findings" are not experimental results but rather the systematic organization, classification, and synthesis of existing knowledge, coupled with the identification of key trends and open problems. The core contributions lie in its structured taxonomy of domain gaps, the categorization of prevailing methodologies, and the delineation of future research directions.

#### 4.1. Categorization of Domain Gaps

The survey provides a clear, fine-grained classification of domain gaps, a critical step toward understanding the challenges in policy transfer:

- **Appearance Gaps:** These arise from visual discrepancies between domains (e.g., sim vs. real textures, lighting). Methods predominantly focus on visual domain adaptation, including image-to-image translation (e.g., CycleGAN-inspired approaches, real-to-sim translation), learning canonical domain-invariant representations (e.g., semantic segmentation), and domain randomization/visual data augmentation, which aim to make the policy robust to visual variations.
- **Viewpoint Gaps:** These stem from different sensor configurations (e.g., camera angles, positions). Approaches include third-person imitation learning, context translation models that convert observations from one viewpoint to another, implicit learning of action effects in image space, contrastive learning for viewpoint-invariant features, and meta-learning for fast adaptation, or hierarchical setups to separate viewpoint-agnostic goals from embodiment-specific control.
- **Dynamics Gaps:** These reflect inconsistencies in the physical interactions between the agent and its environment, or within the agent's internal components (e.g., friction, stiffness). Solutions involve traditional system identification (though often data-intensive), modifying source domain configurations through dynamics randomization (passive or active), learning reward corrections or dynamics ratios for RL, and state/transition alignment based on optimal policies inducing similar state trajectories across domains.
- **Morphology Gaps:** These occur when target embodiments differ in design (e.g., joint types, limb lengths) from source agents, leading to differences in state/action spaces and dynamics. Favored solutions are hierarchical designs that decouple morphology-specific low-level control from morphology-agnostic high-level planning. Morphology-aware models, often using Transformer architectures, are emerging, and large-scale pre-training over diverse morphologies is explored for generalization.
- **Cross-Multi-Gap Policy Transfer:** This addresses scenarios where multiple types of gaps exist simultaneously. Common strategies include correspondence learning across domains (mapping states and actions), learning domain-invariant features (e.g., task-progress aware, skill-based representations), and leveraging large foundation models (e.g., vision-language models like CLIP or RT-X) for universal domain alignment and scene understanding.

#### 4.2. Overarching Methodological Insights

The survey synthesizes five high-level methodological categories that encapsulate how researchers tackle domain gaps:

- **Source Domain Manipulation:** Direct modification of the source environment (e.g., simulators) to make it more representative of the target. This includes large-scale randomization of visual and dynamic parameters, and model calibration using limited target data. It is powerful for white-box source domains but can be resource-intensive and limited by simulation fidelity.
- **Learning Domain Correspondences / Corrections:** Constructing explicit or implicit mappings (e.g., image-to-image translation, state/action mapping, reward/dynamics ratio correction) between source and target domains. This allows leveraging unpaired data but can face challenges in accuracy and consistency.
- **Identifying Domain-Invariant Distributions:** Aligning statistical properties of data, particularly state visitation distributions, between domains. This is often based on the assumption that optimal policies generate similar trajectories regardless of underlying dynamics, but may not directly address visual gaps or long-horizon tasks.
- **Learning Domain-Invariant Features:** Deriving abstract representations that are robust to domain-specific variations, such as semantic segmentation, skills, or sub-goals. This promotes flexibility and data efficiency but can be prone to uninformative mappings or require sophisticated temporal alignment techniques. Large pre-trained models are powerful tools here.
- **Building Hierarchical Control Paradigms:** Decoupling the control problem into higher-level, domain-agnostic planning and lower-level, domain-specific execution. This modularity simplifies domain gap modeling and facilitates adaptation, often leveraging meta-learning for fast fine-tuning.

#### 4.3. Open Challenges and Future Trends

The survey concludes by outlining critical areas for future research:

- **Different Sensor and Actuator Modalities:** Developing transfer methods for drastically different state and action spaces, moving beyond expert demonstrations to leverage diverse, suboptimal, "in-the-wild" data, potentially through large foundation models like Octo.
- **Multi-Source Data Sharing:** Integrating data from multiple, diverse source domains to overcome data scarcity, requiring flexible interfaces and expressive feature spaces.
- **Continual Target Fine-Tuning:** Designing policies that can adapt to evolving target domains (e.g., due to wear-and-tear) by continually integrating new, sporadically available target data, leading to generalist, multi-skill policies.
- **Generalization and Adaptation Trade-Off:** Achieving a balance between broad generalization across many domains and high-performance adaptation to a specific target domain. This is where powerful foundation models and language-based alignment show promise.
- **Off-Domain Policy Evaluation:** Developing reliable theoretical and empirical criteria to evaluate policy transferability using accessible source domains and limited target data, crucial for safe and efficient real-world deployment.

In summary, the survey provides a clear conceptual map of cross-domain policy transfer, highlighting the diversity of challenges and solutions, while rigorously outlining the most pressing issues that current paradigms have yet to fully address.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This comprehensive survey on cross-domain policy transfer for embodied agents holds significant value and is poised to make a substantial impact on the fields of robot learning and embodied AI. Its significance can be understood through several key aspects:

Firstly, its primary contribution lies in **bringing coherence to a fragmented research landscape**. Before this survey, the vast array of approaches to cross-domain policy transfer existed in isolation, often tailored to specific types of domain gaps or learning paradigms. By unifying notations, providing a nuanced taxonomy of domain gaps, and systematically categorizing existing methodologies, the authors have created a much-needed organizational framework. This clarity will undoubtedly help new researchers quickly grasp the field's complexities and will assist experienced researchers in identifying connections and differentiations across seemingly disparate methods, fostering a more holistic understanding.

Secondly, the survey serves as a **critical resource and roadmap for future research**. By distilling overarching methodological insights, it highlights the fundamental strategies that have proven effective, allowing researchers to build upon established principles rather than reinventing the wheel. More importantly, its dedicated section on "Open Challenges and Future Trends" explicitly outlines the most pressing unsolved problems. These include overcoming limitations with diverse sensor/actuator modalities, effectively leveraging multi-source data, enabling continual adaptation to dynamic target environments, balancing generalization with adaptation, and developing reliable off-domain evaluation metrics. This forward-looking perspective provides clear directions for future scientific inquiry, guiding the allocation of research efforts and accelerating progress in these vital areas.

Thirdly, the work directly addresses a **fundamental bottleneck in the real-world deployment of embodied agents**. The high cost, safety concerns, and logistical complexities of collecting real-world data mean that simulation-to-real transfer is indispensable for scaling up robot learning. By comprehensively reviewing methods that bridge the sim-to-real gap, the survey contributes directly to the development of more robust, versatile, and ultimately deployable robotic systems. This has profound implications for practical applications in areas like autonomous driving, industrial automation, and service robotics, where reliable policy transfer is paramount.

Finally, the authors' initiative to maintain an accompanying GitHub repository ("awesome-cross-domain-policy-transfer-for-embodied-agents") further enhances the survey's impact by promoting **community engagement and knowledge continuity**. This open-source resource demonstrates a commitment to facilitating ongoing research and ensuring that the survey remains a living document, adapting to new developments in the field.

In conclusion, "A Comprehensive Survey of Cross-Domain Policy Transfer for Embodied Agents" is more than just a literature review; it is a foundational piece that structures, synthesizes, and propels a crucial area of AI research. By clarifying the past and present, it illuminates the path forward, thereby significantly accelerating the development and widespread adoption of capable embodied agents in the real world.
