# RoBoSR: Structured Scene Representations for Embodied Robotic Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> RoBoSR introduces a framework that uses structured 3D scene graphs as the core representation for robotic reasoning, enabling embodied agents to perform long-horizon manipulation tasks. This approach explicitly models objects and their spatial relationships to enhance instruction following, support causally constrained planning, and facilitate state-consistency correction in both simulated and real-world environments.

## Problem

- Learning-based embodied agents often struggle with real-world variability and long-horizon tasks due to implicit scene representations and sequential biases.
- Classical symbolic frameworks are rigid, require extensive manual domain knowledge, and are difficult to scale to dynamic, open-ended environments.
- Many existing methods face a 'representation bottleneck' where task-irrelevant visual information is entangled with task-relevant physical relations, limiting robustness and generalization.

## Method

- **RoBoSR** proposes a representation-centric framework utilizing ==structured 3D scene graphs== (Msg=(Ot,Et)M_{sg} = (O_t, E_t)Msg=(Ot,Et)) as the primary state space, explicitly separating high-level reasoning from low-level action execution.
- A dedicated dataset, Manip-Cognition-1.6M, was created to provide joint supervision for scene understanding, instruction interpretation, and sub-task planning, fostering structure-aware reasoning.
- The agent is trained in two stages: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for structured scene interpretation and action reasoning, followed by Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT) to address grounding errors and inconsistent state transitions.

## Results

- RoBoSR-8B demonstrated superior task progress on the GSR-bench simulation across all difficulty levels compared to various LLM baselines, especially in spatial-aware sequencing and goal-conditioned generalization tasks.
- Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT) consistently improved task progress, showing an **11.7%** absolute gain on challenging spatial tasks by reducing hallucinations, common sense reasoning errors, and goal understanding errors.
- Real-world experiments confirmed RoBoSR's capabilities in instruction-state alignment, causally constrained multi-step task execution, and autonomous state-consistency correction when confronted with perturbations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured intermediate representations, such as scene graphs, provide a crucial inductive bias for scalable embodied reasoning by disentangling high-level task logic from raw perceptual data.
> - Explicitly modeling object states and their spatial relationships enables systematic, compositional reasoning, which supports principled generalization across diverse and complex tasks.
> - Formulating decision-making as state transitions within a grounded scene graph allows for causally constrained planning and robust error correction when deviations occur during execution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied learning aims to develop robotic systems capable of integrating perception, reasoning, planning, and execution for interacting with the physical world. A significant challenge within this domain is enabling robust task reasoning under the inherent variability of real-world environments, particularly for open-ended and long-horizon tasks.

Existing approaches to embodied reasoning generally fall into two categories:

1. **Learning-based approaches (e.g., Vision-Language-Action models like RT-2, OpenVLA, $\pi^0$):** These methods often rely on extensive human demonstrations and implicitly encode environmental information within high-dimensional neural features. While effective for end-to-end policy learning and benefiting from large-scale data, they can face limitations. Their sequential inductive biases may restrict flexibility in tasks with multiple valid execution paths or long dependencies. Furthermore, operating in pixel or latent embedding spaces can create a "representation bottleneck" where task-irrelevant visual variability becomes entangled with task-relevant physical relations, potentially reducing robustness and generalization, especially in long-horizon manipulation or under distribution shifts. Hierarchical variants, such as RT-H, introduce planning-control decomposition, but their intermediate representations often remain implicit and lack explicit supervision over object states and spatial relations.

2. **Classical symbolic frameworks (e.g., Task and Motion Planning, PDDL):** These approaches model the world using explicitly defined symbolic states, predicates, and transition rules. This allows for compositional reasoning and formal search over structured state spaces. However, they typically require manual definition of abstractions and domain knowledge, making them rigid, complex to configure, and difficult to scale in dynamic, open-ended environments.

Scene graphs represent an intermediate approach, offering a structured yet learnable representation by explicitly modeling objects, attributes, and their spatial relations. Prior work has used scene graphs for perception, mapping, and as auxiliary signals for language-conditioned planning (e.g., VoxPoser, ReKep, ConceptGraphs, ENACT). However, in many such systems, scene graphs primarily serve as perceptual abstractions, and decision-making often remains prompt-driven or does not explicitly model state transitions within the graph space itself.

RoBoSR positions itself within this landscape by advocating for structured intermediate representations as a critical inductive bias for scalable embodied reasoning. It departs from prior scene graph applications by treating the grounded scene graph as the *primary state space* for decision-making, where reasoning is explicitly modeled as state transitions within this structured space. This design aims to combine the flexibility of data-driven learning with the explicit relational structure necessary for robust reasoning, thereby bridging the gap between implicit neural features and rigid symbolic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enhance the reasoning capabilities of embodied agents for general tasks by leveraging structured intermediate representations.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in current embodied learning paradigms:
- **Challenges with Real-World Variability:** Existing methods, particularly those relying on demonstration-driven sequential biases, often struggle with the inherent variability of real-world environments. Their reliance on consistent procedural sequences limits their flexibility when agents encounter open-ended tasks with multiple valid execution paths or long-horizon tasks requiring reasoning over extended dependencies.
- **Implicit Scene Representations:** Many learning-based approaches encode task scene information implicitly within high-dimensional neural features. While this enables end-to-end policy learning, it does not explicitly model relational structures, which can impede systematic reasoning and generalization across diverse scenes and tasks. A representation must capture object-centric entities, spatial relationships, and task-relevant semantics, as these elements dictate appropriate action sequences.
- **Rigidity of Classical Symbolic Systems:** Traditional Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) frameworks, while offering explicit structured symbolic representations, often impose rigid format constraints and require manually predefined abstractions. This makes them challenging to scale to open-ended, dynamic environments.
- **Need for Causal Reasoning and Long-Horizon Planning:** For complex, multi-step tasks, an embodied agent requires the ability to infer intention-oriented action sequences, interpret the environment, understand task intent, and generate coherent plans. This necessitates causal reasoning capabilities, enabling the agent to enforce subtask dependencies and support coherent long-horizon task planning rather than merely following predefined sequences.

Motivated by these limitations, the study introduces RoBoSR to address the need for structured intermediate representations that can combine the adaptability of neural perception with the explicit relational structure required for robust embodied reasoning.

Specifically, RoBoSR aims to imbue the agent with three key capabilities:
1. **Instruction–State Alignment:** By grounding reasoning in an object-centric representation, the agent should be able to map high-level instructions to scene-consistent actions, thereby supporting instruction following in open-world tasks.
2. **Causally Constrained State-Space Reasoning:** RoBoSR intends to formulate reasoning as explicit state evolution, constraining subtask planning through causal structure rather than fixed demonstration sequences. This is designed to support coherent long-horizon reasoning in open-ended tasks.
3. **State-Consistency Correction:** Through its explicit and comparable state representation, RoBoSR aims to make execution deviations observable, enabling the correction of inconsistencies through structured reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RoBoSR proposes a representation-centric framework that formulates decision-making as reasoning over structured scene states, explicitly separating high-level state reasoning from low-level action execution.

**A. RoBoSR Modeling**
The core of RoBoSR is a structured 3D scene graph, $M_{sg} = (O_t, E_t)$, constructed from RGB-D observations. This graph represents the workspace and object states from multiple affordance perspectives:
- **Object Affordance:** Each object $o_j \in O_t$ is represented as a structured entity. This includes functional components (e.g., a mug's handle), articulated child parts (e.g., cabinet drawers), and their states (e.g., open/closed).
- **Spatial Affordance:** Each edge $e_k \in E_t$ encodes a spatial relationship between two objects. These relationships encompass canonical predicates like "on," "inside," or "adjacent," as well as more nuanced configurations such as "tilted against" or "partially occluded." The combination of these affordances provides a structured, functionally grounded, and relationally rich representation for manipulation tasks.

**B. RoBoSR Data Engine (Manip-Cognition-1.6M)**
To enable learning of structure-aware reasoning, the authors constructed Manip-Cognition-1.6M, a dataset providing joint supervision for scene understanding, instruction interpretation, and sub-task planning.
- **Dataset Composition:** The dataset is derived from Epic-Kitchens-100, EgoPlan, Behavior-1K, and Enact, totaling 6,000 task trajectories related to daily activities. Through step-wise decomposition and augmentation, 1.6 million samples were generated.
- **Scene Abstraction Data (15k samples):** This subset trains the model to construct structured scene graphs (SG) from natural language descriptions of object configurations and spatial relations ($Text_t \leftrightarrow SG_t$). It supervises object grounding (identifying object entities) and spatial relation extraction.
- **Action Planning Data (1.3M samples):** This subset trains the model to capture causal dependencies for sequential decision-making. It includes three objectives:
 1. **Goal-conditioned Planning:** Predicts the next symbolic action $A_t$ given the current scene graph $SG_t$ and a high-level task goal $G$ ($ (SG_t, G) \rightarrow A_t $).
 2. **Forward Scene Prediction:** Predicts the structural state transition ($\Delta SG_{edge}$) induced by an action ($ (SG_t, A_t) \rightarrow \Delta SG_{edge} $).
 3. **Inverse Action Reasoning:** Infers a multi-step action sequence ($ \{A_i\}^{t+n-1}_{i=t} $) required to achieve a target state $SG_{t+n}$ from an initial state $SG_t$ ($ (SG_t, SG_{t+n}) \rightarrow \{A_i\}^{t+n-1}_{i=t} $).
- **Goal Interpretation Data (0.3M samples):** This subset trains the model to infer structured goal states ($SG_{goal}$) from partial world configurations and natural language instructions ($ (SG_t, Instruction) \rightarrow SG_{goal} $). This involves long-horizon task semantics and implicit intermediate steps.

**C. Training Agentic Reasoning over RoBoSR**
RoBoSR uses the Qwen3-8B architecture and is trained in two stages:
1. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** The initial stage focuses on structured scene interpretation, goal grounding, and action reasoning. It uses low-rank adaptation (LoRA) for parameter-efficient fine-tuning on instruction-following trajectories conditioned on RoBoSR representations. The policy generates intermediate reasoning traces followed by executable symbolic actions.
2. **Reinforcement Fine-Tuning (RFT):** To address potential object-grounding errors and inconsistent state transitions from SFT, RFT is applied using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The objective function, $R_{total} = \lambda_1 R_S + \lambda_2 R_G + \lambda_3 R_T$, incorporates three reward components:
 - $R_S$ (Step-wise Constraint Reward): Penalizes multi-action hallucinations, enforcing single atomic action per response.
 - $R_G$ (Scene-Graph Grounding Reward): Penalizes object-grounding errors by ensuring predicted object identifiers are valid nodes in the current scene graph.
 - $R_T$ (Termination Reward): Regulates stopping behavior, rewarding correct task termination and penalizing premature or infinite action generation.

**D. Physical System Deployment**
The system deploys a modular perception–reasoning–action architecture:
- **Perception–Reasoning Module:** An open-world segmentation network (based on SAM3) extracts object-centric regions from RGB-D observations. A geometry-conditioned rule-based algorithm then constructs the RoBoSR graph by inferring object states and spatial relations. This structured representation is fed to RoBoSR for reasoning and action selection.
- **Action Expert (Low-level Control):** Semantic Keypoint Imitation Learning (SKIL) is used as the low-level controller. For each manipulation primitive (e.g., "grasp a mug handle"), a dedicated SKIL policy is trained using 50 demonstrations. Masked observations corresponding to the selected object, as identified by SAM3, are passed to SKIL to generate motion trajectories.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RoBoSR included benchmarks on simulated tasks (GSR-bench) and real-world robot implementations.

**A. Evaluation on GSR-bench**
GSR-bench, comprising 180 tasks with over 10 planning steps on average, assesses long-horizon reasoning under spatial and semantic constraints. Evaluation metrics focused on Task Progress (TP) across three categories: Semantic Object Disambiguation (SOD), Spatial-Aware Sequencing (SAS), and Goal-conditioned Generalization (GCG), each with three difficulty levels.
- **Structured Reasoning Superiority:** RoBoSR-8B (pre-trained only) demonstrated performance over prompting-based Large Language Model (LLM) baselines (Gemini-2.5-pro, GPT-5, Claude-sonnet-4.5, DeepSeek-V3, Qwen3-8B) across all difficulty levels. This difference was particularly noticeable in SAS and GCG tasks. While LLMs experienced performance degradation with increased task complexity, RoBoSR maintained more stable task progress, suggesting that explicit reasoning over object states and spatial relations provides a necessary inductive bias for long-horizon embodied tasks.
- **Zero-Shot Generalization and Task Grounding:** RoBoSR-8B, trained solely on Manip-Cognition-1.6M, exhibited robust zero-shot reasoning capabilities. However, a performance gap between easy and difficult settings indicated that abstract task semantics and termination conditions were not fully resolved by pretraining alone.
- **Data Efficiency of Structured Representations:** RoBoSR-8B-FT, with minimal post-training (36 in-domain demonstration trajectories from GSR-bench), achieved over 60% task completion rates on easy and medium tasks and substantially improved difficult goal-conditioned reasoning. This suggested that RoBoSR provides a sample-efficient foundation for adaptation, requiring limited task-specific supervision for alignment with environment constraints.

**B. Ablation Studies on GSR-bench**
Ablation studies investigated the contribution of RoBoSR's core components, using Task Progress (TP) and categorizing failures into Hallucination Error (HE), Planning Error (PE), Common Sense Reasoning error (CSR), and Goal Understanding Error (GUE).
- **Impact of Data Scale:** Increasing task-specific data from 12 to 36 trajectories improved TP on Object tasks, primarily by reducing HE and GUE, but showed limited gains on SAS and GCG tasks. This indicated that homogeneous data scaling effectively resolves object-level ambiguities but is insufficient for long-horizon causal reasoning and spatial planning.
- **Contribution of Data Compositions:**
 - Removing Action Planning Data, especially goal-conditioned planning trajectories, led to the most significant performance degradation (up to 50% TP reduction) and increased HE and GUE, highlighting its central role in long-horizon reasoning.
 - Removing Scene Abstraction Data disproportionately affected SAS tasks, leading to higher object-level hallucinations (increased HE).
 - Excluding Forward Scene Prediction or Inverse Action Reasoning primarily increased PE, indicating their importance for learning action-state causality.
 - Removing Goal Interpretation Data resulted in a pronounced increase in GUE, confirming its necessity for accurate task termination and goal satisfaction.
- **Effect of Data Augmentation and Noise Injection:**
 - Scene-level augmentation improved spatial reasoning and action inference, reducing PE, particularly on SPA tasks.
 - Goal augmentation enhanced robustness in task completion, suppressing GUE.
 - Training with noisy scene graphs resulted in a slight TP drop under clean evaluation, but substantially improved stability and resistance to perceptual errors when evaluated with corrupted or incomplete scene graphs, demonstrating the efficacy of denoised training.
- **Reinforcement Fine-Tuning vs. Supervised Fine-Tuning:** Applying RFT on top of SFT consistently improved TP, with an 11.7% absolute gain on challenging Spatial tasks. This improvement was attributed to:
 - Reduced HE due to the Scene-Graph Grounding Reward ($R_G$) and Step-wise Constraint Reward ($R_S$).
 - Decreased CSR errors as $R_S$ enforced strict single-step, closed-loop execution.
 - Mitigated GUE (e.g., premature stopping) by the Termination Reward ($R_T$). These results confirmed RFT's role in aligning reasoning trajectories with physical and goal constraints beyond what supervised learning alone could achieve.

**C. Real-World Demonstrations**
RoBoSR was validated in real-world experiments demonstrating its three core capabilities:
- **Instruction–State Alignment:** Demonstrated in language-guided pick-and-place tasks (e.g., "Pick up the red mug and place it on the table"), where RoBoSR mapped high-level instructions to scene-consistent actions.
- **Causally Constrained State-Space Reasoning:** Assessed through multi-step daily-life tasks (e.g., "Object sorting" or "Set the table with plates and cups in order"). RoBoSR maintained a state representation and enforced causal constraints between subtasks.
- **State-Consistency Correction:** Evaluated by introducing perturbations during tasks (e.g., displaced or dropped objects). RoBoSR detected inconsistencies between observed and expected states and autonomously corrected errors.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents RoBoSR as a representation-centric framework for embodied robotic manipulation, distinguished by its explicit modeling of the world through structured scene graphs. The introduction of RoBoSR signifies a shift from direct perception-to-action mappings towards reasoning over explicit intermediate world models.

The primary significance of RoBoSR lies in its ability to disentangle high-level task reasoning from raw perceptual inputs. By explicitly representing object states and their spatial relations, the framework enables structured and compositional reasoning, which is critical for interacting with evolving environments. This structural grounding has been demonstrated to support principled generalization, coherent long-horizon planning, and transferable reasoning capabilities, as evidenced by its performance in both simulated benchmarks and real-world experiments.

The findings suggest that structured intermediate representations serve as a critical inductive bias for scalable embodied reasoning. RoBoSR's approach addresses limitations found in current paradigms: it overcomes the sequential biases inherent in many demonstration-driven methods and the rigidity of classical symbolic Task and Motion Planning. Concurrently, it offers an improvement in systematic reasoning and generalization compared to approaches that rely solely on implicit neural representations.

The potential impact of RoBoSR is on the development of more robust, adaptable, and general-purpose robotic systems. Such systems could perform complex, open-ended tasks in varied real-world settings with greater autonomy, reduced reliance on extensive demonstrations for novel scenarios, and improved error recovery capabilities. Future work, as outlined by the authors, includes extending RoBoSR to represent highly deformable objects and fine-grained continuous states, as well as enhancing the robustness and scalability of the perception front-end for constructing scene graphs, including tighter perception-reasoning integration and uncertainty-aware state estimation.
