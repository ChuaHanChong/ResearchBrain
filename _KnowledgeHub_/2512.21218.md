# Latent Implicit Visual Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Latent Implicit Visual Reasoning (LIVR) enhances Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) by enabling them to implicitly learn and utilize visual abstractions through dedicated latent tokens and a visual bottleneck mechanism. This approach consistently outperforms direct supervised fine-tuning by an average of 3.43% to 6.24% and surpasses methods relying on explicit visual supervision across various perception-heavy tasks, eliminating the need for costly intermediate annotations.

## Problem

- Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) are predominantly text-centric, limiting their ability to perform complex, sophisticated visual reasoning tasks.
- Existing methods to enhance LMM visual reasoning rely on explicit supervision for intermediate visual steps, leading to high annotation costs, human biases, and poor generalization.
- LMMs often struggle to form rich, spatially structured visual abstractions akin to human 'mental imagery' without explicit verbalization.

## Method

- Augment LMMs with learnable, randomly initialized =="latent tokens"== that are appended to the input prompt, creating an additional visual compute space.
- Implement a novel ==visual bottlenecking mechanism== using an attention mask, forcing all visual information to flow exclusively through these latent tokens to reach the answer.
- Utilize a ==two-stage training process==: initially optimize latent tokens with the visual bottleneck, then fine-tune the model to jointly integrate original visual inputs with the enriched latent representations.

## Results

- LIVR consistently improved performance over Direct Supervised Fine-Tuning across three LMM backbones and nine perception-heavy tasks, achieving average gains ranging from **3.43%** to **6.24%**.
- The method demonstrated significant improvements on tasks requiring complex visual abstractions, such as Jigsaw (**+12%** on Qwen2.5-VL) and Functional Correspondence (**+27.40%** on LLaVA-OneVision).
- LIVR significantly outperformed Mirage, an approach using explicit visual supervision, achieving **68.00%** accuracy on Jigsaw versus Mirage's **48.60%** and **66.00%** versus **46.00%** on Visual Spatial Planning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Multimodal Models can be trained to autonomously discover and utilize complex visual abstractions implicitly, without explicit human supervision for intermediate steps.
> - A dedicated visual bottleneck mechanism effectively compels latent tokens to encode task-relevant visual information by restricting direct attention to visual inputs.
> - Latent tokens provide a flexible, task-agnostic mechanism for LMMs to perform sophisticated visual reasoning, outperforming methods reliant on predefined visual intermediates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) have demonstrated capabilities in visual understanding tasks. However, the paper identifies a limitation: these models are predominantly text-centric, with language serving as their primary reasoning modality. This design often leads to struggles in vision-centric tasks that demand fine-grained visual reasoning and complex abstraction. The majority of contemporary LMMs, such such as LLaVA-style architectures, project visual inputs into a language model designed to produce only textual outputs. This creates a language bias, compelling the LMM to process visual information through text, which may lack the expressivity required for sophisticated visual abstractions, akin to how humans use mental imagery for tasks like spatial manipulation or pattern recognition without relying on verbal descriptions. Furthermore, much of the recent LMM advancement has focused on tasks with limited visual reasoning requirements, where textual reasoning primarily occurs after initial visual feature extraction.

To address this, previous research has explored methods to enhance LMMs' visual reasoning capabilities through explicit intermediate supervision. These approaches involve training models to predict intermediate visual steps, such as bounding boxes, image crops, depth maps, or helper images. However, these strategies introduce several challenges: they incur substantial annotation costs, impose human-designed priors on what constitutes "useful" visual abstractions, and often exhibit limited generalization across diverse tasks or when the desired intermediate representation is ambiguous or abstract.

The broader research landscape for visual reasoning in LMMs can be categorized into:
- **Text-Based Visual Reasoning:** Methods like Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting extend explicit textual step generation to multimodal contexts (e.g., LLaVA-CoT, Visual-RFT). While improving performance, these approaches rely solely on text for intermediate reasoning, potentially limiting the formation of rich, spatially structured visual abstractions.
- **Interleaved Multimodal Reasoning:** These methods integrate visual representations into the reasoning process.
 - **Region-Based Visual Recycling:** Approaches that ground intermediate steps in specific image regions by predicting spatial coordinates or bounding boxes (e.g., Argus, ViGoRL). These methods are constrained to localized information and assume useful reasoning can be expressed as one or more spatial regions, which may not apply to global or abstract visual tasks.
 - **Generated Visual Intermediates:** Techniques that generate new visual representations (e.g., MVoT, CoT-VLA, Mirage, LVR, Aurora). While expanding reasoning beyond text, these still require explicit definition of the intermediate visual representation (e.g., future frames, depth maps, task-specific helper images), inheriting the cost and restrictive priors of explicit supervision.
- **Latent Reasoning:** A separate line of work explores allocating additional computation in a model's latent space (e.g., Coconut, Think Before You Speak). This suggests that latent representations offer a flexible internal space for reasoning, decoupled from external tokens. However, previous LMM approaches using latent variables for visual reasoning (e.g., Mirage, LVR) still relied on explicit intermediate supervision.

The presented work, Latent Implicit Visual Reasoning (LIVR), positions itself within this landscape by proposing a novel latent reasoning mechanism that specifically addresses the limitations of explicit supervision. It aims to allow LMMs to discover and use latent visual reasoning tokens without any explicit intermediate supervision, thereby offering a task-agnostic solution that enables implicit learning of visual abstractions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a task-agnostic mechanism that enables Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) to perform sophisticated visual reasoning by discovering and utilizing latent visual reasoning tokens, without requiring explicit intermediate supervision.

The motivation for this objective stems from several critical limitations observed in current LMMs and existing approaches to enhance their visual reasoning:
1. **Language Bias in LMMs:** Most modern LMM architectures are fundamentally text-centric. Visual inputs are typically projected into a language model that is optimized to generate textual outputs. This design inherently forces the LMM to translate visual information into a textual format for reasoning, which may compromise the expressivity required for complex visual abstractions. Analogous to human mental imagery, certain visual tasks might be more naturally solved through direct visual processing rather than linguistic description.
2. **Limitations of Explicit Intermediate Supervision:** Prior attempts to improve LMM visual reasoning have often relied on explicit supervision of intermediate visual steps. These methods involve training models to predict specific visual targets such as bounding boxes, image crops, depth maps, or "helper images."
 - **High Annotation Costs:** Generating task-specific intermediate annotations (e.g., precise bounding boxes, depth maps) is labor-intensive and expensive, hindering scalability across a wide range of tasks.
 - **Restrictive Priors:** Explicitly supervising intermediate steps embeds human assumptions about what constitutes "useful" visual reasoning. These hand-designed targets may not be the most effective representations for the model to learn or generalize from, potentially limiting the model's capacity to discover more optimal, abstract visual strategies.
 - **Poor Generalization:** Approaches tied to specific intermediate representations often struggle to generalize to diverse vision-centric tasks where the optimal intermediate steps might be different or unclear. For tasks requiring abstract visual structures (e.g., visual similarity, artistic style classification), even human experts may find it difficult to define appropriate intermediate representations to supervise.
3. **Need for Implicit Learning:** The authors hypothesize that LMMs could benefit from an approach where models implicitly learn to form and utilize visual abstractions. This would mirror human cognitive processes, where mental imagery allows for complex visual problem-solving without explicit verbalization or predefined intermediate visual steps.

By proposing Latent Implicit Visual Reasoning (LIVR), the authors aim to overcome these limitations, providing LMMs with a flexible, internal visual computation space that can adaptively extract relevant visual information directly from images without imposing human-engineered intermediate steps or incurring high annotation overheads.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Latent Implicit Visual Reasoning (LIVR) method is designed to augment Large Multimodal Models (LMMs) with the ability to implicitly discover and utilize task-relevant visual representations through dedicated latent tokens and a novel visual bottlenecking approach.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries

The work is based on standard LMM architectures, comprising a visual encoder, a language model decoder, and a projector. Given a text prompt (Q) and visual input (I), the visual encoder (v) processes the image, which is then projected (p) into the embedding space of the language model. The language model (M) then processes these visual embeddings along with the text prompt (encoded by l) to generate a textual response (R). Tasks are framed as visual question answering (VQA), where the model predicts an answer (a) given visual input (I) and a text prompt (Q).

#### 3.2. Latent Implicit Visual Reasoning (LIVR)

LIVR addresses the limitation of current LMMs, which typically project visual information once at the input and then primarily reason through text. The core hypothesis is that LMMs can benefit from additional visual computation space, which LIVR provides through latent tokens, trained to form task-relevant visual representations without explicit human-designed supervision.

- **Latent Tokens:**
 - The model's existing vocabulary (V) is augmented with K new special tokens, denoted as L = {l1, l2..., lK}. The vocabulary size becomes |V| + K.
 - During training, these latent tokens are appended to the input prompt, creating a new prompt Q' = Q + L.
 - These tokens are initialized randomly, and their corresponding rows in the embedding table are unfrozen and updated during training.
 - A key aspect is that the model learns how to *use* these latent tokens to represent visual information rather than learning to *generate* them as output.

- **Visual Bottlenecking:**
 - This is a critical mechanism to ensure that the latent tokens actively learn to carry visual information.
 - It involves modifying the attention mask within the LMM architecture.
 - Specifically, during a designated training stage, the attention mask is configured such that:
 - Answer tokens can only attend to the prompt tokens (Q) and the latent tokens (L).
 - Answer tokens are explicitly prevented from attending to the original visual input tokens (I).
 - To prevent indirect visual information leakage, prompt tokens (Q) are also prevented from attending to the visual inputs (I).
 - This setup forces the latent tokens to become the sole pathway for visual information to reach the answer tokens, compelling the model to encode task-relevant visual features within these latents. Without this bottleneck, the model might simply ignore the added latent tokens and continue to rely on direct attention to the original image tokens.

- **Multi-Stage Training:**
 - LIVR employs a two-stage training approach:
 - **Stage 1 (Visual Bottlenecking):** The model is trained with the modified attention mask described above. The objective is the standard negative log likelihood (NLL) computed solely on the answer tokens. This stage explicitly optimizes the latent tokens to capture the most useful visual information necessary for solving the given question.
 - **Stage 2 (Standard Masking):** After the latent tokens have been trained to encode meaningful visual information in Stage 1, the attention mask is reverted to a standard configuration. In this stage, answer tokens are allowed to attend to both the original image tokens and the enriched latent tokens. The loss computation remains the same (NLL on answer tokens). The purpose of Stage 2 is to enable the model to learn how to effectively integrate and jointly use both the raw visual input and the newly formed latent visual representations to generate the final answer.

#### 3.3. Implementation Details

- **Parameter Tuning:** The language model backbone is fine-tuned using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) applied to its attention and MLP blocks. The vision encoder and the projector are kept frozen to maintain their pre-trained capabilities.
- **Trainable Parameters:** In addition to the LoRA parameters, only the embedding rows corresponding to the K new latent tokens are unfrozen and updated during training. All other parts of the embedding table remain fixed.
- **Hyperparameters:** A default of K=16 latent tokens is used. The training schedule typically involves 4 epochs for Stage 1 and 6 epochs for Stage 2. These hyperparameters were determined through ablation studies.
- **Optimization:** AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1x10^-4, weight decay of 0.01, betas (0.9, 0.999), and epsilon 10^-8. An effective batch size of 8 (achieved with a per-device batch size of 1 and 8 gradient accumulation steps) is used. The learning rate schedule incorporates a 5% linear warmup followed by cosine decay, applied separately to each training stage.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of LIVR involved controlled data-matched experiments on single-task and multi-task settings, comparisons with prior reasoning methods, and extensive ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Data-matched Experiments

- **Tasks and Datasets:** LIVR was evaluated on nine perception-heavy tasks adapted from the BLINK benchmark: Counting, Jigsaw, Object Localization, Visual Correspondence, Art Style Classification, Semantic Correspondence, Functional Correspondence, Relative Reflectance, and Visual Similarity. These tasks demand significant visual reasoning and abstraction. Custom training datasets were created from sources like COCO, ArtBench-10, SPair-71k, HPatches, FunKPoint, MID, and DreamSim. All training data consisted of direct question-answer pairs without intermediate supervision.
- **Baselines and Models:** Three open-source LMMs were used as backbones: Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct, Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct, and LLaVA-OneVision-1.5-4B-Instruct. Comparisons were made against Zero-shot performance and Direct Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), which served as the primary data-matched baseline.
- **Single-Task Fine-Tuning:**
 - LIVR consistently outperformed Direct SFT across all tasks and LMM backbones.
 - With Qwen2.5-VL, LIVR improved average accuracy by 6.24% over Direct SFT. Notable gains included 12% on Jigsaw and 13.02% on Functional Correspondence, indicating enhanced ability to form complex visual abstractions. Improvements were also observed on tasks like Art Style, Visual Similarity, and Relative Reflectance, where explicit visual intermediates are difficult to define.
 - On Qwen3-VL and LLaVA-OneVision-1.5, LIVR achieved average improvements of 3.43% and 5.60% respectively, demonstrating generalizability across different models.
- **Multi-Task Fine-Tuning:**
 - LIVR was tested on Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct across a combined dataset of six tasks.
 - It improved over Direct SFT on all six tasks, with an average gain of 2.77%. This suggests that the task-agnostic nature of LIVR, which does not require task-specific intermediate labels, makes it suitable for heterogeneous multi-task fine-tuning.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Prior Reasoning Methods

- **Visual Spatial Planning (VSP):** LIVR (using K=4 latent tokens and no helper images) was compared with Mirage, a latent reasoning approach that uses intermediate helper images. LIVR achieved 66.00% accuracy on Qwen2.5-VL-3B, outperforming Mirage's 46.00%. On Qwen2.5-VL-7B, LIVR achieved 77.50%, surpassing Mirage (76.00%), Mirage with text-CoT (58.00%), and Mirage with RL (60.00).
- **Spatial Reasoning Generalization (SAT, BLINK-3, RoboSpatial):** LIVR-3B, operating without text-CoT, explicit grounding, or reinforcement learning, demonstrated strong results across benchmarks like SAT Val (85.6% vs. ViGoRL's 62.9%), BLINK-3 (59.5% vs. ViGoRL's 48.5%), and RoboSpatial (66.7% vs. ViGoRL's 67.1%), indicating generalization to out-of-distribution tasks.
- **Broader Visual Reasoning Benchmarks (MMVP, V*, BLINK-5):** LIVR-7B was competitive or outperformed methods like LVR, PixelReasoner, Vision-R1, and PAPO on MMVP (75.3% vs. LVR's 71.7%), V* (80.1% vs. LVR's 80.6%), and BLINK-5 (54.28% vs. LVR's 55.37%), despite not using costly intermediate supervision such as image crops, visual intermediates, or text CoT.

#### 4.3. Ablations and Additional Experiments

- **Usefulness of Latent Tokens:** Ablation studies confirmed that LIVR's latent tokens are actively utilized and encode task-relevant visual information. Removing latent tokens at evaluation time for LIVR led to a significant performance drop, whereas a "latents-only" baseline (without bottlenecking) showed no impact upon removal, suggesting its latents were ignored.
- **Design Ablations for LIVR:**
 - Both dedicated latent tokens and the visual bottleneck were found necessary for LIVR's performance. Variants lacking either component (e.g., "mask only" or "latents only") underperformed.
 - Placing latent tokens *after* the prompt yielded higher accuracy than placing them before, suggesting better conditioning on the question.
 - The most effective masking strategy involved blocking both answer-to-vision and prompt-to-vision attention during Stage 1.
 - A balanced multi-stage schedule (4 epochs Stage 1, 6 epochs Stage 2) provided the best trade-off.
 - Using unshared embeddings for each latent token improved expressivity and performance compared to shared embeddings.
 - K=16 latent tokens was found to be optimal, balancing capacity and learnability.
- **Visualizations:** Latent-to-image attention maps demonstrated that latent tokens focused on task-relevant regions, such as object parts for semantic correspondence, bounding box areas for localization, and individual objects for counting. t-SNE visualizations of hidden states showed latent tokens largely occupying the same region as image tokens, with some forming distinct clusters, indicating both general visual feature representation and specialized abstract learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Latent Implicit Visual Reasoning (LIVR) represents a contribution to the field of multimodal AI, particularly in enhancing the visual reasoning capabilities of Large Multimodal Models (LMMs).

The primary significance of LIVR lies in its ability to enable LMMs to discover and leverage useful latent visual representations without requiring explicit intermediate supervision. This addresses a critical limitation of previous approaches, which often relied on costly, task-specific annotations such as helper images, bounding boxes, image crops, depth maps, or Chain-of-Thought textual rationales. By eliminating these requirements, LIVR offers a more scalable and practical solution for training LMMs on a broader spectrum of vision-centric tasks.

The consistent outperformance of LIVR over direct supervised fine-tuning across diverse, perception-heavy tasks and multiple LMM backbones demonstrates its effectiveness. Its gains, particularly on tasks requiring complex visual abstractions or where explicit intermediates are hard to define (e.g., Jigsaw, Functional Correspondence, Art Style, Visual Similarity), suggest that implicit learning mechanisms can form more flexible and effective internal representations.

Furthermore, LIVR's task-agnostic nature is a significant advantage. The same method can be applied directly to multi-task settings, as demonstrated by its performance improvements in joint multi-task training. This contrasts with approaches that tie latent tokens to specific visual targets, which often necessitate different supervision regimes for each task. The competitive or superior performance against prior reasoning methods that do rely on explicit intermediate supervision or text-based reasoning underscores LIVR's efficiency and robustness.

The method’s design, particularly the visual bottlenecking approach, provides a mechanism to ensure that latent tokens actively learn to encode task-relevant visual information. The ablation studies confirm the necessity of both dedicated latent tokens and this bottlenecking mechanism, contributing to the understanding of how such implicit learning can be effectively induced in LMMs. The attention visualizations offer qualitative evidence that these latents are indeed focusing on pertinent visual elements, even if their precise internal encoding remains complex.

Potential impacts of LIVR include:
- **Reduced Annotation Burden:** The elimination of explicit intermediate supervision can significantly lower the cost and effort associated with preparing data for visually complex reasoning tasks, accelerating research and development in this area.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** By allowing models to learn implicit, adaptive visual abstractions, LIVR may lead to LMMs that generalize better across new tasks and out-of-distribution scenarios, as suggested by its performance on spatial reasoning benchmarks.
- **Richer Visual Understanding:** LIVR moves LMMs closer to developing a more nuanced, spatially aware visual understanding that goes beyond text-centric reasoning, potentially fostering new capabilities in areas like visual commonsense reasoning, complex scene understanding, and perception-heavy robotics.
- **Foundation for Future LMM Architectures:** The success of implicit latent visual reasoning could inform the design of future LMMs, promoting architectures that inherently support multi-modal reasoning at a deeper, more integrated level.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
A recognized limitation of LIVR is the reduced interpretability of latent tokens compared to explicit, human-designed intermediates. While attention maps provide some insight, fully understanding the precise information encoded by each latent token remains challenging. Future work could explore scaling LIVR to even larger models, investigating dynamic allocation of latent tokens based on task difficulty, and applying this latent visual reasoning paradigm to more complex multi-step or interactive vision-language tasks.
