# Learning Robust Execution in Robotic Manipulation with Agentic Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> An agentic reinforcement learning framework improves the robustness of robotic manipulation policies by learning high-level decisions for execution management and recovery. This approach, developed by researchers including those from Harbin Institute of Technology Shenzhen, enhanced task success rates by up to 39.2% under kinematic disturbances without requiring modifications to the underlying low-level policies.

## Problem

- Advanced robotic manipulation policies, such as VLA models, are vulnerable to execution-level deviations and compounding errors, leading to task failures, especially in long-horizon scenarios.
- Existing solutions like data scaling are resource-intensive and often fail to address the 'long-tail' of failure cases, while external semantic reasoning components add complexity and latency.
- Current learning-based policies often lack explicit mechanisms to continuously assess execution stability at runtime or to proactively recover from degraded execution states.

## Method

- Developed two complementary ==runtime execution quality metrics==: a ==local metric== for short-term stability (motion effectiveness, smoothness) and a ==global metric== for long-horizon drift (comparison to successful reference trajectories).
- Proposed an ==agentic reinforcement learning== framework where a high-level policy learns to regulate a frozen low-level manipulation policy by selecting one of four discrete execution modes: `EXECUTE`, `RETRY`, `REPAIR`, or `RESET`.
- Implemented three predefined ==recovery mechanisms== (`RETRY`, `REPAIR`, `RESET`) that involve rolling back to prior stable states or restarting the task, guided by the learned agentic policy and execution quality scores.

## Results

- The agentic framework consistently improved task success rates for various low-level policies across the LIBERO benchmark, with average gains ranging from **+5.1%** to **+13.7%** under standard conditions.
- Under random kinematic disturbances, the method achieved substantial robustness, boosting average success rates by **+25.7%** to **+39.2%** compared to baselines (e.g., OpenVLA on LIBERO-Long improved from **33.4%** to **67.6%**).
- Analysis revealed adaptive decision-making: `RETRY` was favored for mild degradations, `REPAIR` for severe issues like collisions, and `RESET` for irrecoverable errors, with recovery actions leading to positive changes in execution quality.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Monitoring execution quality through combined local and global metrics provides effective real-time signals for identifying degradation, enabling the high-level agent to make informed recovery decisions.
> - A lightweight agentic policy can be trained to adaptively select appropriate recovery actions, proportionate to the type and severity of execution degradation, effectively managing complex low-level policies without requiring their modification or retraining.
> - Decoupling execution management from low-level action generation offers a cost-effective and generalizable approach to enhance robustness across various learning-based manipulation policies and diverse tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation remains a challenging domain due to inherent uncertainties, the need for long-horizon task execution, and the accumulation of small errors that can destabilize operations. Recent advancements, particularly in learning-based manipulation policies like Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, have demonstrated significant capabilities in generalization across diverse objects, tasks, and environments through large-scale pretraining. These models often operate as monolithic executors, directly mapping visual observations and language instructions to low-level actions.

However, a noted limitation of current VLA models and similar learning-based policies is their vulnerability to execution-level deviations during deployment, especially in long-horizon tasks. Minor discrepancies in perception or dynamics can lead to execution drift, causing unstable behaviors, loss of progress, or irreversible task failures. Existing approaches to mitigate such failures include:

1. **Data Scaling:** Expanding training datasets and fine-tuning VLA models to cover a broader range of conditions. While effective, this is often resource-intensive due to high data collection costs and substantial computational overhead for retraining large models. The "long-tail" distribution of failure cases also limits its complete effectiveness.
2. **External Reasoning Components:** Introducing additional modules, such as Vision-Language Models (VLMs), to detect failures and facilitate replanning. These methods, while demonstrating strong recovery potential, often increase system complexity, introduce inference latency, and can be sensitive to instruction distribution shifts, impacting scalability and efficiency.
3. **Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL):** Employing structured decision layers above low-level controllers, where a high-level policy selects skills for a lower-level policy. Some HRL approaches model recovery as a high-level problem, but often operate on primitive actions or require specific recovery policies.

This research positions itself within this landscape by addressing the execution robustness problem from an "execution-level" perspective. Instead of retraining or modifying the low-level policies (like VLAs), or adding complex semantic reasoning modules, it proposes a lightweight, agentic framework. This framework aims to provide explicit mechanisms for monitoring execution quality and adaptively managing the execution process when degradation occurs. It seeks to achieve robustness by learning to regulate existing frozen low-level policies, thereby reducing the computational cost and complexity associated with other recovery strategies. The work draws inspiration from hierarchical decision-making but focuses specifically on discrete, high-level execution modes for recovery, informed by runtime quality metrics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this work stems from the observed fragility of advanced robotic manipulation policies, particularly VLA models, when confronted with real-world uncertainties and compounding errors during long-horizon tasks. Even policies that perform well under ideal conditions can easily deviate from nominal trajectories due to subtle errors, leading to degraded execution states and eventual task failure. The paper identifies a gap in current systems: the lack of explicit mechanisms to assess execution stability at runtime and to recover effectively when execution drifts.

The limitations of existing mitigation strategies further underscore this motivation:
- **Cost of Data Scaling:** Expanding training data for every possible failure scenario is economically and computationally prohibitive, especially for foundation models.
- **Complexity of VLM-based Replanning:** Integrating large language or vision-language models for online reasoning introduces latency and system complexity, making real-time, low-latency recovery challenging.
- **Lack of Proactive Management:** Many learning-based policies are reactive, executing learned actions without continuously monitoring their own performance or having inherent recovery capabilities.

Given these challenges, the research sets forth the following key objectives:

1. **Develop Complementary Runtime Execution Quality Metrics:** Design and implement two metrics, local and global execution quality, to continuously assess the stability and adherence to nominal behavior of a robotic task execution at runtime. These metrics are intended to provide real-time signals for identifying execution degradation.
2. **Propose an Agentic Reinforcement Learning Framework:** Introduce a novel framework where a high-level "agentic" policy learns to regulate the execution of a frozen low-level manipulation policy. This framework explicitly separates the concerns of execution management from low-level action generation.
3. **Learn High-Level Decision-Making for Recovery:** Train the agentic policy using reinforcement learning to select among a small, predefined set of execution modes (e.g., `EXECUTE`, `RETRY`, `REPAIR`, `RESET`) based on observed execution history and quality. The objective is for this policy to adaptively trigger appropriate recovery mechanisms when degradation is detected, restoring the robot to states from which nominal execution can proceed.
4. **Enhance Execution Robustness Without Modifying Low-Level Policies:** Demonstrate that this agentic framework can substantially improve task success rates and robustness against disturbances, for various low-level policies, without requiring their modification, retraining, or fine-tuning. This aims for a more efficient and generalizable solution for robustness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves an **agentic reinforcement learning (RL) framework** designed to manage and recover robotic manipulation tasks. This framework operates on top of an existing, frozen low-level policy (e.g., a VLA model or diffusion policy) without altering its learned behaviors.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The system is framed as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The high-level agentic policy, $\pi_{agent}$, makes decisions at fixed time intervals, observing a limited execution history. Its actions are discrete execution modes, not continuous low-level control commands. The overall goal is to maximize expected discounted return through a reward function that encourages task success and efficient execution while penalizing recovery actions.

#### 3.2. Local and Global Execution Quality Metrics

Two complementary metrics are introduced to assess execution quality in real-time:

1. **Local Execution Quality ($q_{local}$):** This metric monitors short-term stability and progress.
 - **Motion Effectiveness (E):** Calculated as the ratio of actual end-effector displacement over a sliding window to the sum of commanded action magnitudes over the same window. A low value suggests stagnation despite control effort. This is then normalized using a saturation transform.
 - **Motion Smoothness (S):** Assessed using the inverse coefficient of variation of instantaneous end-effector speeds within the sliding window. High values indicate consistent motion, while low values penalize erratic fluctuations.
 - **Aggregation:** E_norm and S are combined via a weighted sum, passed through a sigmoid function, and smoothed using an exponential moving average to yield $q_{local}$.

2. **Global Execution Quality ($q_{global}$):** This metric captures long-horizon drift from nominal task progression.
 - **Feature Vector ($z_t$):** At each step, a compact feature vector is created from the recent execution window, including averaged robot state (end-effector pose, gripper width), averaged low-level action, net displacement, and mean action magnitude.
 - **Reference Library:** Before deployment, a library of feature vectors from successful reference trajectories (collected using the frozen low-level policy) is built. These features are binned according to normalized task progress to account for temporal misalignment.
 - **Comparison:** During execution, the current $z_t$ is compared against its k-nearest neighbors in the corresponding progress bin of the reference library.
 - **Score Calculation:** The global quality $q_{global}$ is computed as an exponentially decaying function of the average Euclidean distance to these neighbors, smoothed by an exponential moving average.

These two metrics are then combined into an **aggregated execution quality score ($q_{agg}$)** using a weighted sum, which serves as a primary input for the agentic policy and a criterion for selecting rollback states during recovery.

#### 3.3. Recovery Mechanisms

Three predefined recovery mechanisms are implemented as actions for the agentic policy, each addressing different levels of execution degradation:

1. **RETRY:** For mild degradation, this mechanism drives the robot back to a recent state with the highest $q_{agg}$ within the last $M$ (fixed to 15) low-level steps. An operational space controller (OSC) is used for this rollback, keeping the gripper open, after which nominal execution resumes.
2. **REPAIR:** For more severe degradation (e.g., collisions), this mechanism performs a stronger rollback. It restores the robot to a contact-free state with the highest $q_{agg}$ within the last $N$ (fixed to 30) low-level steps. Contact-free states are identified by thresholding the end-effector contact force. The OSC and open gripper strategy are also used here.
3. **RESET:** For irrecoverable errors, this mechanism terminates the current episode and restarts the entire task from its initial configuration.

#### 3.4. Agentic Reinforcement Learning

The high-level agentic policy $\pi_{\theta}$ (parameterized by $\theta$) learns to select one of four discrete execution modes: `EXECUTE`, `RETRY`, `REPAIR`, or `RESET`.

- **Observation Space:** The agentic policy observes a fixed-length execution history $h_t$ at each decision step. This history includes recent robot proprioception, low-level actions generated by the frozen policy, and the two execution quality scores ($q_{local}$, $q_{global}$). Raw visual observations are not used by the actor policy. The critic, however, has access to additional privileged global state information from the simulator for improved value estimation during training.
- **Action Space:** A discrete set of four integers mapping to the execution modes: `{0: EXECUTE, 1: RETRY, 2: REPAIR, 3: RESET}`. Decisions are made at an interval of $K=5$ low-level steps.
- **Reward Function:** Designed to encourage task completion and efficient execution while penalizing recovery actions.
 - Task Success: +1.0
 - Task Failure: -1.0
 - Time Penalty: -0.02 per step
 - Recovery Costs: `EXECUTE` (0), `RETRY` (-0.1), `REPAIR` (-0.3), `RESET` (-0.5).
- **Optimization:** The policy is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), which is suitable for discrete action spaces and aims to maximize the expected discounted return. Both the policy and critic are implemented as lightweight multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs).

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

The method is evaluated on the LIBERO benchmark, comprising four task suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long), which vary in task diversity and horizon. The agentic policy is trained for each task using up to 1,000,000 high-level decision steps. Baselines include OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$, and Diffusion Policy, all fine-tuned on LIBERO. Performance is measured by task success rate. Robustness is specifically tested by introducing a random kinematic disturbance (noise injection for 5 consecutive steps) at a random timestep in each episode. RESET actions during evaluation are counted as failures for a conservative comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments aimed to assess the framework's ability to improve task success and robustness, its adaptability to various policies and degradation types, and its efficiency.

1. **Improved Task Success and Robustness (Table II):**
 - **Standard Conditions:** The agentic framework consistently improved success rates across all LIBERO subsets and low-level policies. Notable average gains were observed for OpenVLA (+5.1% to +13.7%) and Diffusion Policy (+5.4% to +13.7%), particularly on the challenging LIBERO-Long suite (e.g., OpenVLA improved by 20.5%). For strong baselines like $\pi_0$ and $\pi_{0.5}$, which already achieved high performance, the method maintained their success rates without degradation.
 - **Disturbance Settings:** Under random kinematic disturbances, baseline performance degraded substantially. In contrast, the proposed method showed significant robustness, with average success rate improvements ranging from +25.7% to +39.2% across task suites. For instance, OpenVLA on LIBERO-Long increased from 33.4% to 67.6%. This indicates the agentic policy's capability to recover from unmodeled state deviations.

2. **Training Performance and Stability (Fig. 5):** The PPO training curves demonstrated a steady and consistent increase in both training reward and task success rate over 12,000 steps, with narrow confidence intervals. This suggests that the agentic policy can be trained stably using sparse task-level rewards to improve outcomes without modifying the low-level policy.

3. **Adaptive Decision-Making (Fig. 6):** Analysis of agentic decisions, conditioned on different types of execution degradation, revealed an adaptive preference pattern.
 - `RETRY` was predominantly selected for mild degradations such as transient noise or slight grasp misalignments.
 - `REPAIR` was favored for more severe issues like collisions or obstructions.
 - `RESET` was reserved for irrecoverable states (e.g., object falling outside the workspace).
 This indicates that the agentic policy differentiates between degradation types and selects recovery actions proportional to their perceived severity.

4. **Agentic Decisions and Execution Quality (Fig. 7, Table III):**
 - **Decision Distribution in Quality Space:** Different execution modes occupied distinct regions in the local-global execution quality space. `EXECUTE` decisions were concentrated in high-quality regions, `RETRY` at intermediate levels, and `REPAIR`/`RESET` in low-quality regions. This confirms the policy's conditioning on execution quality.
 - **Impact on Quality:** Recovery actions (RETRY, REPAIR, RESET) generally led to positive changes in both local ($\Delta q_{local}$) and global ($\Delta q_{global}$) execution quality, as well as an increased probability of global quality improvement. Stronger recovery actions (REPAIR, RESET) yielded higher probabilities of positive quality changes compared to RETRY or simply continuing (`EXECUTE`). This supports that the selected modes induce graded and appropriate recovery effects.

5. **Recovery Cost and Efficiency (Table IV):** The agentic policy introduced a moderate increase in the number of non-`EXECUTE` modes and corresponding episode length. The overhead scaled with the fragility of the underlying low-level policy; stronger policies incurred fewer recovery actions and smaller increases in episode length. This suggests that the agentic policy intervenes judiciously rather than indiscriminately. The added execution cost was considered modest relative to the gains in success rate and robustness.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing an efficient and effective method for improving execution robustness. Its significance and potential impact include:

- **Enhanced Reliability of Learning-Based Policies:** The framework directly addresses a critical weakness of state-of-the-art VLA and diffusion policies – their susceptibility to execution-level failures. By providing an explicit mechanism for monitoring and recovery, it can make these powerful policies more reliable for real-world deployment in uncertain and dynamic environments.
- **Cost-Effective Robustness:** Unlike methods that require extensive retraining or fine-tuning of large foundation models, this approach operates on frozen low-level policies. This significantly reduces the computational and data collection costs associated with achieving robustness, making it more practical for adoption.
- **Interpretability and Adaptive Management:** The agentic policy makes high-level, discrete decisions that are interpretable (e.g., "retry this grasp," "repair by repositioning," "reset the task"). Its ability to adapt recovery strategies based on the type and severity of execution degradation offers a more nuanced and intelligent execution management system.
- **Generality Across Low-Level Policies:** The successful application of the framework to various low-level policies (OpenVLA, $\pi_0$, $\pi_{0.5}$, Diffusion Policy) and diverse tasks within the LIBERO benchmark suggests its general applicability, indicating it can serve as a robust augmentation layer for a wide range of existing and future manipulation policies.
- **Facilitating Sim-to-Real Transfer:** By relying only on proprioceptive signals and low-level actions, without requiring privileged simulator information for the actor policy, the framework is designed to be amenable to deployment in real-world robotic systems, potentially bridging the sim-to-real gap for robust manipulation.

A primary limitation noted by the authors is the framework's current capacity to recover from only moderately severe execution degradation and less so from out-of-distribution scenarios. Future work is projected to focus on improving recovery capabilities for more extreme degradation and validating the approach in real-world settings. Despite this, the presented work offers a promising direction for creating more resilient and autonomous robotic systems.
