# What Matters for Latent Actions in Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> First large-scale empirical study of latent action models (LAMs) for robot learning, unifying methods under one autoencoding framework and testing 41 design choices across modeling paradigm, regularization, and integration strategy. Finds fine-tuning VLM backbones with latent actions gives stronger policy-learning initialization, validated on LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, RoboTwin2.0, and real Franka Panda tasks.

## Problem

- Prior LAM methods vary latent-action learning, regularization, and integration choices in isolation under inconsistent settings, obscuring which factors actually drive manipulation performance.
- Downstream robotic evaluation requires the full three-stage pipeline, so prior work leans on proxy metrics like MLP-probe error whose reliability as a stand-in is largely unverified.
- Robot datasets differ in control interfaces, action spaces, and dimensionality, confounding cross-method comparison when physical actions are folded into latent-action learning.

## Method

- Unified ==autoencoding framework== comparing implicit ==IDM-FDM== paradigms (LAPO, LAOF, CoMo) against explicit ==consecutive-frame-difference autoencoding== (CFD-AE) over pixel, DINO-feature, and optical-flow deltas.
- Systematic ==regularization== study across AE, VAE, VQ-VAE, Sparsity, and SIGReg objectives, sweeping regularization strength to isolate its effect from regularizer type.
- Five ==latent-action integration strategies== for the action head (DAP, LAP, JAP, JAP-DAP, JAP-LAP) that vary whether and how latent actions mediate physical-action prediction during VLM fine-tuning.

## Results

- LAPO trained on raw data is the strongest modeling paradigm overall (**0.7327** average), beating optical-flow variants (RAFT 0.6427) and outperforming CoMo and LAOF across LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and RoboTwin2.0.
- Latent dimensionality of **32** gives the best trade-off on 14-DoF RoboTwin2.0 (**0.856** vs. 0.802 at dimension 8), and unnormalized latent actions beat normalized in **28 of 33** method-benchmark combinations (**+0.0115** average).
- Real-world Franka Panda tests show LA-Tuned OpenVLA-OFT reaching **317/400** successful rollouts versus 259/400 for the baseline, lifting success rate from **64.75%** to **79.25%** and converging faster (**85.0%** at 10k steps vs. 76.25% at 40k).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simplicity often beats added machinery: raw-data LAPO and off-the-shelf DINO feature differencing rival or beat optical-flow-augmented latent-action learning, so extra motion-specific inductive bias can hurt.
> - Regularization strength matters far more than regularizer family; picking a well-tuned coefficient dominates the choice between VAE, Sparsity, or SIGReg for downstream policy quality.
> - Reconstruction-based proxy metrics correlate well with downstream performance for coarse model screening but are unreliable for fine-grained ranking, cautioning against proxy-only model selection.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is significantly impacted by the availability of large-scale, high-quality robot action data, which remains costly and difficult to collect. This scarcity limits the development of scalable action representations. While embodied foundation models, such as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) and World Action Models (WAM), have made progress, much of this has been due to advancements in adjacent foundation model paradigms (Vision-Language Models and Video Generation Models) trained on web-scale image-text and video corpora, rather than corresponding breakthroughs in action modeling itself. Current embodied foundation models are typically initialized from these large pre-trained perceptual models and then adapted using comparatively limited robot action data. This imbalance between abundant perceptual data and scarce robot action data forms a fundamental bottleneck, hindering the emergence of scaling behaviors observed in large language models.

Latent Action Models (LAMs) have emerged as a paradigm to address this bottleneck by leveraging large-scale unlabeled videos. LAMs learn compact "latent actions" that serve as surrogates for physical actions, capturing transition dynamics from consecutive frames in a self-supervised manner. While LAMs offer a promising avenue for scalable robot learning, the research landscape for these models is fragmented. Existing methods often evaluate different design choices in isolation and under inconsistent experimental settings, making it challenging to identify the critical factors that influence downstream robotic manipulation performance. Furthermore, there is a lack of reliable protocols for evaluating latent action quality, with prior work relying on unverified proxy metrics.

This paper positions itself as the first comprehensive empirical study to systematically investigate LAM design choices. It aims to unify diverse LAM methods within a common framework, rigorously evaluate various design dimensions, and assess the reliability of proxy metrics. By doing so, it seeks to provide a coherent empirical understanding of LAMs, identify effective design principles, and establish robust evaluation methodologies, thereby contributing to the development of more scalable and effective embodied foundation models for robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to conduct a systematic and comprehensive empirical study of latent action learning specifically for robotic manipulation. This overarching goal is motivated by the fragmented nature of existing LAM research and the lack of a clear understanding regarding which design choices contribute most significantly to downstream performance. The paper outlines several detailed objectives:

1. **Unification of LAM Methods:** To consolidate representative LAM methods within a common autoencoding framework. This unification aims to enable fair comparisons by eliminating architectural differences and allowing a focused investigation of core design choices.
2. **Systematic Investigation of Design Choices:** To rigorously evaluate 41 distinct LAM design choices across three key dimensions:
 - **Latent action modeling paradigms:** Examining different approaches to learning latent action representations from consecutive frames without robot action data.
 - **Learning objectives and regularization methods:** Investigating how various regularization techniques and their strengths shape the learned latent action representations.
 - **Latent action integration strategies:** Analyzing different methods for incorporating latent actions into the prediction of physical actions during downstream policy learning.
3. **Evaluation of Proxy Metrics:** To examine four proxy metrics for assessing latent action quality and determine their reliability in predicting actual downstream robotic manipulation performance. This objective is motivated by the high cost of full three-stage training pipelines for iterative LAM development.
4. **Assessment of Latent Action Dimensionality and Normalization:** To identify the most suitable latent action dimensionality for various robot platforms and determine the necessity of latent action normalization during fine-tuning.
5. **Investigation of Scaling Behavior:** To assess whether fine-tuning Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbones with latent actions exhibits scaling laws in robotic manipulation, particularly concerning the volume of fine-tuning data.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from the fundamental challenge of data scarcity in robot learning. By clarifying the factors that contribute to effective latent action learning, the researchers aim to provide practical guidelines for developing more robust and scalable robot policies that can leverage the abundance of unlabeled video data, thereby accelerating progress in embodied AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research establishes a unified three-stage training pipeline to systematically evaluate LAM design choices:

- **Stage I (Pre-training – Representation Learning):** The goal here is to learn high-quality LAMs from unlabeled video data, comprising only image frames. This stage focuses on producing physically meaningful latent action representations that capture transition dynamics. This stage does not utilize robot action data or task-specific policy demonstrations.
- **Stage II (Mid-training – VLM Backbone Fine-tuning):** The pre-trained inverse dynamics model from Stage I acts as a latent action annotator, generating latent actions for semantically conditioned video data, forming triplets (o_t, z_t, l). These latent actions are then used to fine-tune a Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone.
- **Stage III (Post-training – Downstream Policy Learning):** The fine-tuned VLM backbone from Stage II is further optimized using a limited amount of robot action data (o_t, a_t, l) to learn a downstream policy that predicts physical actions.

The study investigates 41 unique design choices across three main dimensions:

**Design I: Latent Action Modeling Paradigms**
This dimension explores how latent actions are learned from consecutive frames without physical action data. Two broad paradigms are considered:
1. **Implicit IDM-FDM Framework:**
 - **LAPO:** A standard self-supervised autoencoding framework with an Inverse Dynamics Model (IDM) inferring latent action from (o_t, o_t+1) and a Forward Dynamics Model (FDM) predicting o_t+1 from (o_t, z_t). Optimized via future-frame reconstruction.
 - **LAOF:** Extends LAPO by adding optical flow as an auxiliary supervision signal, aiming to improve robustness to distractors. FlowDecoder predicts optical flow from z_t.
 - **CoMo:** Modifies IDM input by replacing the future frame with consecutive-frame differences (specifically ∆DINO) to mitigate shortcut learning.
2. **Explicit Consecutive-Frame Differences Autoencoding (CFD-AE):**
 - This paradigm directly learns latent actions by autoencoding predefined motion signals:
 - **∆RGB:** Pixel-wise differences between consecutive frames.
 - **∆DINO:** Semantic feature differences using DINOv2.
 - **Optical Flow:** Estimated using RAFT or SEA-RAFT.
 All CFD-AE methods share the same autoencoding pipeline, ensuring a unified latent action dimensionality for fair comparison.

**Design II: Learning Objectives and Regularization**
This dimension examines how different learning objectives and regularization methods influence latent action representations. The general objective is L = L_method + λ_reg * L_reg, where L_reg is the regularization term and λ_reg is its strength.
- **AE (Autoencoder):** A baseline with deterministic encoding and no explicit regularization.
- **VQ-VAE (Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder):** Introduces discrete latent actions through quantization to a codebook, encouraging compact and discrete representations.
- **VAE (Variational Autoencoder):** Regularizes the conditional latent distribution towards a standard Gaussian using a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence objective, promoting a smooth and continuous latent space.
- **Sparsity:** Encourages sparse and compact representations by activating a small subset of latent dimensions, combined with Variance-Covariance-Mean (VCM) regularization.
- **SIGReg (Sketched-Isotropic-Gaussian Regularizer):** Promotes distributional isotropy by matching projections of latent actions onto random one-dimensional directions to a standard Gaussian distribution.

**Design III: Latent Action Integration Strategies**
This dimension investigates how latent actions are incorporated into downstream physical action prediction (Stage III), building on a VLM backbone VLM_ψ (Qwen3-VL-4B in this study).
- **DAP (Direct Action Prediction):** Latent actions fine-tune VLM_ψ in Stage II, but are discarded in Stage III. Physical action a_t is predicted directly from VLM_ψ(o_t, l).
- **LAP (Latent-to-Action Prediction):** Latent action z_t serves as an intermediate representation. VLM_ψ(o_t, l) predicts z_t, which is then decoded into a_t.
- **JAP (Joint Latent-Action Prediction):** Latent and physical actions are jointly predicted from VLM_ψ(o_t, l) in a single Stage III training, assuming access to robot action data.
- **JAP-DAP / JAP-LAP:** VLM_ψ is first fine-tuned using JAP-style joint optimization (with latent and physical actions), then a new DAP or LAP head is trained from scratch in Stage III. This isolates the effect of physical action supervision during VLM fine-tuning.

**Proxy Metrics:** Four proxy metrics are used to evaluate latent action quality:
- **−Linear Probe** and **−MLP Probe:** Measure the negative physical action prediction error when using lightweight decoders from latent actions, indicating how much physical action information is preserved.
- **SSIM Gain** and **MSE Gain:** Normalized reconstruction metrics quantifying the improvement in future-frame prediction over simply copying the current frame, reflecting the capture of transition dynamics.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted on three widely used benchmarks: LIBERO (7-DoF single-arm), LIBERO-Plus (7-DoF single-arm with robustness perturbations), and RoboTwin2.0 (14-DoF dual-arm). Real-world validation is performed on a Franka Panda robot with four manipulation tasks. A large, heterogeneous video-only corpus of approximately 59M frames, including subsets of Open X-Embodiment, Robotwin, and Liberoplus datasets, is used for Stages I and II. OpenVLA-OFT with Qwen3-VL-4B serves as the baseline VLM backbone in Stages II and III.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical study yielded several key findings regarding LAM design choices:

1. **Latent Action Modeling Paradigms (Q1):**
 - **LAPO** consistently demonstrated the strongest overall performance across benchmarks. Its simplicity, requiring no additional preprocessing and being trainable directly on raw data, was highlighted.
 - **∆DINO** (semantic feature differences) proved highly competitive, achieving performance close to or exceeding LAPO on certain benchmarks (e.g., LIBERO). This suggests that strong pretrained semantic priors from models like DINOv2 can provide an effective inductive bias for latent action learning.
 - Methods explicitly relying on **optical flow** (RAFT, SEA-RAFT, and LAOF) generally exhibited the lowest performance. This indicates that explicitly modeling pixel-wise displacements might discard crucial visual cues related to transition dynamics (e.g., contact, occlusion) or introduce noise from estimation errors, particularly under distribution shifts (LIBERO-Plus). Optical flow quality did not reliably translate to latent action quality.
 - **CoMo**, which incorporates semantic feature differences into the IDM, underperformed LAPO, suggesting that replacing the future-frame input to mitigate information leakage did not result in a net performance gain.

2. **Learning Objectives and Regularization (Q2):**
 - For **continuous latent actions**, the regularization *strength* was found to be more critical than the specific *type* of regularization (VAE, Sparsity, SIGReg), provided an appropriate strength was chosen. Weak-to-moderate regularization was generally recommended (e.g., λ_reg = 10^-7 for VAE, 10^-5 for Sparsity, 10^-3 for SIGReg).
 - **VQ-VAE** (discrete latent actions) achieved the strongest zero-shot generalization on LIBERO-Plus, suggesting that discrete bottlenecks can learn reusable high-level action primitives that improve robustness to distribution shifts. However, it performed less effectively on other benchmarks, potentially due to reduced continuity and flexibility.
 - **VAE** was recommended as the default choice for continuous regularization due to its simplicity and effectiveness.

3. **Latent Action Integration Strategies (Q3):**
 - **DAP** (latent actions discarded after VLM fine-tuning) showed the weakest performance, indicating that indirect influence from pre-training alone has limited benefits.
 - **LAP** (latent actions as intermediate representations) consistently improved performance over DAP, suggesting that preserving latent actions as a control representation is beneficial.
 - **JAP** (joint prediction of latent and physical actions) achieved the best performance. This implies that latent actions are most effective when integrated as a structured auxiliary objective throughout downstream policy learning, continuously regularizing the backbone towards motion-aware representations.
 - The **JAP-DAP** and **JAP-LAP** variants demonstrated that the primary benefit arises from the joint optimization shaping the VLM backbone representation, rather than the specific action head architecture in the final stage.
 - Recommendation: LAP for video-only VLM fine-tuning and JAP-LAP when robot action annotations are available.

4. **Proxy Metrics (Q4):**
 - Proxy metrics (Linear/MLP probe loss, SSIM/MSE Gain) showed a correlation with downstream performance, making them suitable for *coarse-grained model screening*. However, their lower Spearman correlation indicated they were not reliable for *fine-grained model ranking* or identifying the optimal model.
 - **FDM reconstruction metrics (SSIM/MSE Gain)** exhibited stronger correlations than probe metrics, likely because they directly assess the model's ability to capture transition dynamics.
 - Correlations deteriorated when comparing models with different latent action dimensionalities, suggesting their utility is best within a consistent dimensionality.
 - Probe losses were lower for end-effector delta control (LIBERO) compared to joint position control (RoboTwin), aligning with the nature of inverse dynamics.

5. **Latent Action Dimensionality and Normalization (Q5):**
 - A latent action dimensionality of **d_z = 32** was found to provide the best overall trade-off for both 7-DoF single-arm and 14-DoF bimanual robot control. Smaller dimensions might suffice for simpler tasks, while larger ones may be redundant.
 - Using **unnormalized latent actions** consistently yielded better downstream performance (average gain of +0.0115), especially under distribution shifts, when the latent space was already appropriately regularized. Normalization might suppress magnitude-related information relevant for policy learning.

6. **Scaling Laws (Q6):**
 - Increasing the **scale of Stage II VLM fine-tuning data** consistently improved downstream robotic manipulation performance across all benchmarks. The most significant gains were observed on benchmarks with stronger distribution shifts (LIBERO-Plus, up to 9.0% improvement). This indicates that latent action pre-training enables scalable adaptation of VLM backbones.

7. **Real-World Experiments:**
 - Fine-tuning the VLM backbone with latent actions (**OpenVLA-OFT (LA-Tuned)**) resulted in a substantial improvement in real-world manipulation success rates on a Franka Panda robot (+14.5 percentage points).
 - The LA-Tuned backbone demonstrated faster convergence and enhanced robustness to task-irrelevant distractors, suggesting improved task-conditioned grounding and understanding of physical world interactions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a comprehensive empirical foundation for the understanding and development of Latent Action Models (LAMs) in robot learning. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Clarifying LAM Design Principles:** The study addresses the fragmentation in LAM research by systematically evaluating numerous design choices across modeling paradigms, regularization methods, and integration strategies. This leads to clear, empirically-backed recommendations, such as the effectiveness of LAPO and ∆DINO, the critical role of regularization strength, and the benefits of joint latent-action prediction. These findings can guide future research and development, reducing trial-and-error.
2. **Enhancing Embodied Foundation Models:** By demonstrating that fine-tuning VLM backbones with latent actions provides stronger initialization, faster convergence, and improved generalization for downstream robotic manipulation, the work significantly contributes to the development of more capable embodied foundation models. This is particularly relevant as the field moves towards leveraging large pre-trained models for robotics.
3. **Addressing Data Scarcity in Robot Learning:** The core promise of LAMs is to mitigate the bottleneck of scarce robot action data by exploiting abundant unlabeled video. This research validates and refines this promise, showing how LAMs can effectively adapt perceptual models to robotic tasks using primarily visual information, thereby enabling more scalable robot learning.
4. **Improving Evaluation Protocols:** The analysis of proxy metrics clarifies their utility and limitations. While not a complete replacement for full task evaluation, they are shown to be useful for coarse-grained model screening, particularly reconstruction-based metrics. This can accelerate the iterative development cycle for LAMs by providing quicker feedback.
5. **Practical Guidelines for Practitioners:** The findings offer actionable advice for researchers and engineers, including optimal latent action dimensionality (d_z = 32), the preference for unnormalized latent actions when regularization is present, and recommended integration strategies for different data availability scenarios (LAP for video-only, JAP-LAP with robot action data).
6. **Real-World Validation:** The successful deployment and performance gains on a real Franka Panda robot underscore the practical utility of the proposed methodologies, demonstrating that the benefits observed in simulation translate to real-world scenarios, including improved robustness to distractors and data efficiency.

The study's contributions collectively pave the way for more robust, data-efficient, and generalizable robot learning systems by bridging the gap between abundant visual data and scarce robot action data through effective latent action representation learning. Future work, as suggested by the authors, may involve elevating latent actions to a foundational level representation akin to text embeddings and leveraging even larger, in-the-wild video datasets, further amplifying this impact across diverse robotic platforms.
