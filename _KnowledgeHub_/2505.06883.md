# FACET: Force-Adaptive Control via Impedance Reference Tracking for Legged Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> FACET is a reinforcement learning framework that enables legged robots to achieve force-adaptive control and tunable compliance by learning to imitate a virtual mass-spring-damper system. This approach allows robots to gracefully manage external forces, perform high-force interaction tasks, and demonstrates improved robustness against perturbations, successfully pulling payloads up to 10 kg on a real quadruped robot.

## Problem

- Most existing reinforcement learning (RL) controllers for legged robots are 'force-agnostic', leading to stiff behaviors that can be dangerous during physical interactions and provide poor control for tasks requiring force exertion.
- Applying traditional impedance control directly to legged robots is challenging due to their complex, contact-rich dynamics and difficulties in precisely measuring or tracking ground reaction forces.
- Prior RL efforts for compliant or force-controlled legged robots often lack direct task-space compliance or require explicit mode switching, limiting their versatility and safety in dynamic environments.

## Method

- A reinforcement learning policy is trained to track the integrated position and velocity trajectories of a virtual ==mass-spring-damper system== defined for the robot's Center of Mass (CoM), providing an indirect force commanding interface — the core of the **FACET** framework.
- ==Temporal smoothing== is introduced to the tracking targets, where the reward signal uses a mixture of targets integrated from multiple past time steps, ensuring a stable and robust learning process.
- A ==two-stage teacher-student== training framework is employed; a teacher policy uses privileged simulation information, and a student policy is finetuned with observable information and a learned ==state estimator== for robust sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- FACET-controlled robots demonstrated superior robustness, withstanding large lateral impulses up to **200 Ns** by exhibiting compliant 'following' behavior, significantly outperforming baseline controllers that became unstable.
- The framework enabled controllable compliance, achieving substantially lower collision impulses during interactions with obstacles and allowing for modulation of compliance by tuning the virtual stiffness parameter.
- On a real Unitree Go2 quadruped, the policy enabled effortless compliant following via kinesthetic guidance and successfully pulled payloads up to **10 kg** (approximately **two-thirds** of its own weight), significantly surpassing the capabilities of built-in and robust velocity-tracking baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning to imitate the integrated position and velocity trajectories of a virtual impedance model provides a more stable and effective approach to force-adaptive control for legged robots than tracking raw accelerations.
> - Temporal smoothing of tracking targets is crucial for generating robust and learnable reward signals, especially for student policies operating with imperfect state estimation in real-world scenarios.
> - A two-stage teacher-student reinforcement learning architecture, combined with extensive domain randomization, effectively bridges the sim-to-real gap, allowing complex force-adaptive behaviors to be learned in simulation and deployed on physical robots.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robot control has witnessed remarkable progress due to reinforcement learning (RL), enabling robots to achieve unprecedented agility, robustness, and maneuverability across diverse terrains and in complex loco-manipulation tasks. Existing literature showcases RL-powered robots navigating challenging landscapes, performing parkour, and executing intricate loco-manipulation feats such as pick-and-place, door-opening, and even ball-shooting. This body of work has primarily focused on achieving high-performance kinematic and dynamic tasks through position or velocity tracking objectives.

However, FACET identifies and addresses a significant limitation in this prevailing paradigm: the "force-agnostic" nature of most RL-based controllers. While effective for purely motion-based tasks, these controllers often neglect or oversimplify interactions involving external forces. This oversight leads to several critical issues:
1. **Stiff and potentially dangerous behaviors:** When subjected to human intervention or collisions, robots trained with velocity-tracking objectives tend to exhibit rigid responses, potentially causing damage to themselves, their surroundings, or interacting humans.
2. **Poor control during forceful interactions:** For tasks requiring significant force exertion, such as pushing or pulling heavy objects, current controllers often lack the fine-grained control needed, assuming negligible interaction forces or easily counteracted ones.

Traditional robotics, particularly for manipulators, has long relied on **impedance control** to achieve compliant and force-adaptive behaviors. Impedance control provides an indirect force control interface, allowing manipulation of a virtual mass-spring-damper system to regulate interaction forces. However, applying impedance control directly to legged robots is challenging due to their complex, contact-rich dynamics and the difficulty in precisely measuring or tracking ground reaction forces.

FACET strategically bridges this gap by marrying the strengths of modern RL with the proven principles of impedance control. While some prior works have explored torque-based action spaces to introduce compliance at the joint level or force control objectives for loco-manipulation, these often have limitations. For instance, torque-based methods do not directly translate to task-space compliance, and existing force control approaches for legged manipulation might require explicit mode switching (position vs. force control) or rely on near-static assumptions.

FACET distinguishes itself by learning to imitate the dynamics of a *virtual mass-spring-damper system* defined on the robot's center of mass (CoM) in task space. This approach fundamentally changes the control objective from mere position/velocity tracking to actively shaping the robot's dynamic response to external forces through tunable impedance parameters. By providing a *principled way to control the level of compliance*—a feature lacking in methods like Deep Compliant Control (DMC)—FACET offers a more sophisticated and adaptable solution for human-robot collaboration and robust physical interaction. The work pushes the frontier of RL in legged robotics by enabling a new class of force-aware behaviors essential for safe and effective deployment in complex, dynamic, and human-populated environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind FACET stems from the recognized limitations of existing reinforcement learning (RL) controllers for legged robots, which, despite their advancements in agility and robustness, largely remain force-agnostic. This blind spot regarding external forces leads to undesirable and potentially unsafe behaviors when robots interact physically with their environment or with humans.

The key objectives of the FACET framework are:

1. **Enable Force-Adaptive Control:** To develop a control policy that can intelligently adapt to and manage external forces experienced by the robot, rather than merely resisting them or ignoring their implications. This includes both responding compliantly to gentle interactions and robustly exerting large forces when required.
2. **Achieve Controllable Compliance:** To imbue legged robots with variable compliance in task space, allowing for fine-grained control over how stiff or soft the robot behaves. This objective is crucial for enhancing safety during human-robot interaction, mitigating collision impacts, and enabling intuitive kinesthetic guidance.
3. **Facilitate Force-Centric Tasks:** To open up new possibilities for tasks that inherently involve significant physical interaction, such as pushing heavy payloads, pulling objects, or delicate loco-manipulation requiring precise force regulation, without the need for explicit mode switching.
4. **Enhance Robustness to Perturbations:** To significantly improve the robot's ability to withstand and recover from large, unexpected external impulses, ensuring stable locomotion even under severe disturbances.
5. **Ensure Safe Operation in Complex Environments:** By addressing the issues of stiffness and unpredictable responses to external forces, FACET aims to make legged robots safer and more predictable for deployment in real-world, human-populated, and cluttered environments.
6. **Extend Applicability to Complex Robot Morphologies:** To demonstrate that the proposed impedance-tracking framework is generalizable and can be effectively applied to more complex multi-body systems, such as legged loco-manipulators and humanoids, enabling whole-body compliance control.

In essence, FACET is motivated by the need to evolve legged robot control beyond purely kinematic or dynamic tracking to incorporate a deeper understanding and control over physical interactions, thereby making these robots more versatile, safer, and ultimately more useful in diverse real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FACET proposes a novel reinforcement learning framework for legged robots that enables force-adaptive control and variable compliance by explicitly learning to imitate the dynamics of a virtual mass-spring-damper system. The methodology can be broken down into three core components: Impedance Reference Model Tracking, Temporal Smoothing of Tracking Targets, and a specialized Reinforcement Learning Setup.

#### 3.1. Impedance Reference Model Tracking

The central idea is inspired by classical impedance control, which has proven effective for robotic manipulators. FACET extends this concept to legged robots by defining a virtual mass-spring-damper system for the robot's Center of Mass (CoM). This reference model's dynamics are governed by:

$m\ddot{x}_{\text{ref}} = K_p(x_{\text{des}} - x_{\text{ref}}) + K_d(\dot{x}_{\text{des}} - \dot{x}_{\text{ref}}) + f_{\text{ext}}$

where $m$ is the virtual mass, $K_p$ is the virtual stiffness, $K_d$ is the virtual damping, $(x_{\text{des}}, \dot{x}_{\text{des}})$ are the commanded setpoints, and $f_{\text{ext}}$ represents external forces and torques acting on the robot.

The control policy's input comprises these impedance parameters $(x_{\text{des}}, K_p, K_d, m)$, rather than explicit velocity or force targets. This indirect force commanding interface allows for intuitive control of motion and interaction forces. The challenge lies in directly matching these reference dynamics in the complex, contact-rich environment of a legged robot, where ground reaction forces ($f_{\text{grf}}$) are generated through leg contacts, and external forces ($f_{\text{ext}}$) are often difficult to sense reliably.

To circumvent the complexities of direct force or acceleration tracking, FACET employs an RL approach to track the *integrated position and velocity trajectories* generated by the reference model. The objective function for training minimizes the squared error between the robot's simulated CoM state $(x_{\text{sim}}, \dot{x}_{\text{sim}})$ and the reference model's state $(x_{\text{ref}}, \dot{x}_{\text{ref}})$:

$L_{\text{track}} = ||x_{\text{sim}} - x_{\text{ref}}||^2_2 + ||\dot{x}_{\text{sim}} - \dot{x}_{\text{ref}}||^2_2$

This formulation stabilizes training and allows the policy to learn complex behaviors without explicit force sensing or intricate inverse dynamics calculations.

#### 3.2. Temporal Smoothing of Tracking Targets

To generate robust and informative tracking targets, the method integrates the reference dynamics (Eq. 2) over time. A critical design choice is how to handle the initial state for this integration.
- **Open-loop tracking:** Integrating from the beginning of an episode might create targets that are physically infeasible for the robot due to its inherent limitations (e.g., motor capabilities, contact constraints).
- **Closed-loop tracking:** Integrating from the most recent robot state can make the targets overly adaptive to current errors but potentially noisy and short-sighted, hindering the learning of long-term smooth behaviors.

FACET addresses this by introducing **temporal smoothing**: instead of a single target, the reward function uses a mixture of targets obtained by integrating from multiple past time steps ($t' \in \{t - 8\Delta t, t - 16\Delta t, t - 32\Delta t\}$) along the robot's trajectory. This provides a smoother and more stable reward signal, blending the benefits of adaptability with the long-term consistency of an ideal trajectory.

#### 3.3. Reinforcement Learning for Impedance Tracking

The training architecture employs a **two-stage teacher-student framework**, a common strategy for sim-to-real transfer in robotics where certain "privileged" information (e.g., precise body velocities, accelerations, external forces, contact states) is available in simulation but not on the real robot.

1. **Stage 1 (Teacher Training):** A teacher policy $\pi_{\text{teacher}}$ is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in IsaacLab. This teacher has access to both standard proprioceptive observations (joint positions, velocities, base angular velocity, projected gravity, previous actions) and *privileged information* (base linear velocity and acceleration, joint torques, external forces/torques, feet contact states, and the reference targets). Concurrently, a state estimator $E_{\text{est}}$ (a GRU network) is trained to predict a latent feature extracted by a privileged encoder $E_{\text{priv}}$ from the privileged observations, based only on the observable history.
2. **Stage 2 (Student Finetuning):** The student actor $\pi_{\text{student}}$ is initialized with the learned parameters of the teacher policy. It then undergoes further training using PPO, but crucially, it only has access to the observable information and the estimations from $E_{\text{est}}$. This finetuning allows the student to adapt to operating with imperfect state estimation, finding an optimal policy within the constraints of real-world sensor availability.

The critic in this asymmetric actor-critic paradigm always receives both observable and privileged information to ensure accurate value estimation. Extensive domain randomization is applied during training (e.g., random perturbations to joint parameters, body mass, external forces) to improve robustness and enhance sim-to-real transfer. Impedance parameters and external forces are widely sampled to expose the policy to diverse interaction profiles. Critical damping ($K_d = 2\sqrt{K_p}$) is consistently used to ensure stable reference dynamics.

For multi-body robots like loco-manipulators, the framework is extended by defining separate reference models for different bodies of interest (e.g., base and end-effector), with reward functions defined analogously for each.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of FACET is validated through a comprehensive set of experiments conducted in simulation and on real hardware, showcasing improved robustness, controllable compliance, and adaptability across various tasks and robot morphologies.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiments

Simulations were performed with a quadruped robot (Go2), a legged loco-manipulator (Unitree B1 + Z1 Arm), and a humanoid (G1), with extensive domain randomization during training and evaluation.

- **Robustness to Large Perturbations:** FACET demonstrates significantly superior robustness against large lateral impulses. When commanded to walk forward at 1.5 m/s, FACET-controlled robots successfully withstood impulses up to 200 Ns (400 N peak force), exhibiting graceful, compliant following behavior where the robot adjusted its velocity along the impulse direction to maintain balance. In contrast, baseline policies (vanilla velocity tracking and `robust` policy trained with random impulse perturbations) either failed catastrophically or reacted stiffly, leading to instability. This finding highlights FACET's ability to "follow rather than resist" large forces, a critical attribute for survival under impact.
- **Soft Collision:** FACET policies enable safer physical interactions by reducing collision impulses. When the quadruped walked into a virtual wall, FACET achieved a substantially lower collision impulse compared to the `robust` baseline. Critically, the level of compliance and thus the collision impulse could be *modulated by tuning the virtual stiffness parameter $K_p$*, demonstrating fine-grained control over interaction forces. This property is vital for deployment in cluttered or human-proximate environments.
- **Extension to Legged Loco-manipulator:** The framework successfully extends to a more complex Unitree B1 quadruped with a Z1 arm. By defining separate impedance reference models for both the base and the end-effector, FACET enables whole-body compliant control. This was demonstrated through kinesthetic teaching (pulling the end-effector to guide the robot) and controlled large force pulling tasks. Varying the base stiffness ($K_{\text{base}_p}$) allowed for precise control over the pulling force, showing that the virtual spring forces effectively translated to actual interaction forces.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

An ablation study was conducted to evaluate key design choices: the nature of tracking targets (position/velocity vs. acceleration) and the effect of temporal smoothing.

- **Necessity of Integrated Position and Velocity Targets:** Teachers trained with raw acceleration targets or purely open-loop/closed-loop targets showed significantly higher tracking errors and reduced success rates, indicating they struggled to learn meaningful behaviors. Integrated position and velocity trajectories are crucial for stable reference tracking.
- **Benefits of Temporal Smoothing for Student Learning:** While an open-loop teacher, having a perfectly smooth target, sometimes achieved slightly better performance than the smoothed teacher in ideal conditions, the *smoothed teacher* ultimately led to a much better *student policy* (Smoothed Student) with lower tracking errors compared to the Open-loop Student. This suggests that the smoothed targets, by accounting for the gap between ideal reference dynamics and the robot's physical capabilities, provide a more robust and learnable signal for policies that need to operate with imperfect state estimation.
- **Two-Stage Teacher-Student Superiority:** A concurrent one-stage baseline, which attempts to estimate external forces and reference targets directly, performed significantly worse, reaffirming the benefits of the two-stage teacher-student approach for sim-to-real transfer with imperfect information.

#### 4.3. Real-World Results

The student policy trained in simulation was deployed on a real Unitree Go2 quadruped robot.

- **Compliant Following:** With virtual stiffness $K_p = 0$, the robot demonstrated effortless compliant following. It could be kinesthetically guided with minimal force (e.g., a fingertip push or a gentle pull with a string), starting or stopping smoothly without explicit velocity commands. This behavior is a direct manifestation of the tunable compliance and is difficult to achieve with traditional stiff velocity-tracking controllers.
- **Large Force Pulling:** The FACET policy showcased impressive force-generating capabilities. By setting a large $K_p$, the robot reliably pulled payloads up to 10 kg (approximately 2/3 of its own weight) with stable contact and body orientation. This significantly outperformed baseline controllers; the built-in controller failed beyond 2.5 kg, and a `robust` velocity-tracking policy failed beyond 5 kg, struggling with instability. This confirms FACET's ability to engage with and exert large, controlled forces in physical interaction tasks on real hardware.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that FACET effectively imbues legged robots with controllable compliance and force-adaptive behaviors, addressing critical limitations of prior RL approaches and significantly enhancing their versatility and safety.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FACET represents a significant advancement in the field of legged robotics and reinforcement learning, with broad implications for the design and deployment of robotic systems in complex, interactive environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Bridging the Gap in RL for Legged Robots:** The most substantial impact of FACET is its success in bridging the critical gap between highly agile, RL-driven locomotion and the necessity for nuanced, force-aware interaction. By moving beyond force-agnostic position/velocity tracking, FACET introduces a principled method to control how legged robots react to and exert forces, a capability previously underdeveloped in RL-based locomotion.
2. **Enhanced Safety and Human-Robot Interaction:** Controllable compliance is paramount for robots operating in human-populated environments. FACET's ability to achieve "soft collisions" and be kinesthetically guided with minimal effort directly translates to safer human-robot collaboration. This feature reduces the risk of injury to humans and damage to the robot or its surroundings during unexpected contacts, fostering greater trust and acceptance of autonomous legged robots.
3. **Expanded Task Versatility:** The framework enables legged robots to perform a new class of tasks that rely heavily on physical interaction. Examples include:
 - **Cooperative Manipulation:** Working alongside humans or other robots to move heavy objects.
 - **Environmental Interaction:** Pushing doors, opening cabinets, or traversing deformable terrain with controlled force.
 - **Heavy Payload Handling:** As demonstrated by pulling 10kg payloads, FACET allows robots to engage in high-force tasks critical for logistics, construction, or rescue operations.
 This expanded repertoire makes legged robots more versatile and valuable in a wider range of applications.
4. **Robustness to Real-World Dynamics:** By learning to *comply* with large external impulses rather than rigidly resisting them, FACET significantly improves the robot's robustness in unpredictable environments. This adaptive response enhances stability and survival rates under strong perturbations, making the robots more reliable for deployment in unstructured and dynamic settings.
5. **Generalizability Across Morphologies:** The successful extension of FACET to a loco-manipulator and humanoid robot in simulation, alongside quadruped hardware validation, highlights its generalizability. This suggests that the core principles of impedance reference tracking can be applied to diverse robot platforms, paving the way for whole-body compliance control in more complex multi-degree-of-freedom systems.
6. **Advancement in Reinforcement Learning Methodologies:** The use of temporal smoothing for tracking targets and the robust two-stage teacher-student training framework offer valuable insights for improving sim-to-real transfer and handling unobservable state information in complex RL tasks. This contributes to the broader RL community by demonstrating effective strategies for learning highly dynamic and interactive behaviors.
7. **Foundation for Future Research:** FACET lays crucial groundwork for future research directions, such as addressing mechanical constraints in multi-body impedance control, integrating advanced state estimation techniques (e.g., using accelerometers and foot force sensors), and developing more sophisticated gait control that dynamically adapts to impedance targets.

In conclusion, FACET represents a pivotal step towards developing truly intelligent and adaptable legged robots that can safely and effectively navigate, interact, and operate alongside humans in the physical world. By endowing robots with tunable compliance and force-adaptive control, this work unlocks new possibilities for their deployment in high-impact, real-world applications.
