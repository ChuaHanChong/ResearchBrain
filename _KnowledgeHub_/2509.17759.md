# MotionTrans: Human VR Data Enable Motion-Level Learning for Robotic Manipulation Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> MotionTrans cotrains multi-task VR-collected human demonstrations with robot teleoperation data in a shared robot observation-action space, letting end-to-end policies (Diffusion Policy, pi-0-VLA) directly execute human-only tasks on real hardware. Cotraining 15 human and 15 robot tasks transfers 13 motions, with 9 tasks reaching non-trivial zero-shot success and pretraining boosting finetuning success rate by 40%.

## Problem

- Prior human-to-robot transfer methods rely on intermediate representations like affordances, keypoint flows, or wrist trajectories, which are incompatible with mainstream end-to-end policies.
- Existing human-robot cotraining and pretraining works show gains in visual grounding, robustness, and training efficiency, but never verify whether policies can learn genuinely new motions from human data alone.
- No prior dataset offers sufficient task and motion diversity with non-overlapping human and robot task sets to systematically test explicit motion-level transfer at scale.

## Method

- A ==VR-based human-robot data collection system== extending ARCap and Open-Television, capturing egocentric RGB, wrist poses, and hand keypoints, yielding the 3,213-demonstration **MotionTrans Dataset** across 30 tasks and 10+ scenes.
- A ==human-to-robot format transformation pipeline== using dex-retargeting for hand joints, relative action-chunk wrist poses, unified action normalization, and speed slowdown to align human data with the robot observation-action space.
- A ==weighted multi-task human-robot cotraining strategy== that balances loss contributions between human and robot datasets by their relative sizes, applied to Diffusion Policy and pi-0-VLA architectures.

## Results

- Zero-shot deployment on 13 human-only tasks achieves non-trivial success on **9** tasks with about **20%** average success rate and **0.5** average Motion Progress Score across both policy architectures.
- Pretraining on MotionTrans before few-shot finetuning yields roughly **40%** average success rate improvement over from-scratch baselines in both 5-shot and 20-shot settings.
- Ablations show relative-pose action representation and unified (not embodiment-dependent) normalization are critical, while visual rendering of robots into human video gives negligible additional benefit.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robot data cotraining, not human data alone, is the causal enabler of motion transfer, since human-only pretrained policies produce random motion with 0% success at deployment.
> - Motion-level transfer appears to work through interpolation between robot-demonstrated action ranges guided by task-aware, embodiment-invariant visual attention learned from human data.
> - Design choices proven useful for visual robustness or training efficiency in prior work (embodiment-dependent normalization, absolute pose) can actively hurt motion-level transfer, so evaluation goal should dictate design choice.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation is rapidly advancing, largely driven by imitation learning (IL) from teleoperated demonstrations. However, a significant bottleneck remains the **cost and labor-intensity of collecting large-scale real robot data**. This scarcity of robot-specific data limits the scalability and generalization capabilities of current robotic policies.

To overcome this, researchers have increasingly turned to auxiliary data sources. While rich visual and language understanding can be pre-trained from internet-scale datasets, acquiring analogous **motion knowledge** remains a formidable challenge. Human data, characterized by its abundance, ease of collection, and diverse manipulation behaviors, offers a promising avenue.

Previous works have explored leveraging human data in several ways:
- **Task-aware representation learning:** Early approaches extracted intermediate representations like affordances, object poses, or motion flows from human demonstrations to facilitate knowledge transfer. However, these methods often introduce bottlenecks by requiring explicit representation design, hindering integration with modern end-to-end visuomotor policies.
- **End-to-end policy learning with posed human data:** More recently, with advancements in wearable sensing (e.g., VR devices), direct use of human motion data (hand poses, wrist information) for robot policy cotraining or pretraining has gained traction. These approaches have demonstrated benefits in areas such as visual grounding, robustness, and training efficiency for *known* robot tasks.

However, a critical question has remained largely unanswered: **Can human data enable robot policies to directly acquire *new* motions for task completion in a zero-shot manner?** This paper addresses this specific gap. While existing methods improve aspects like robustness or efficiency, they have not systematically demonstrated the capability for robots to *directly perform novel tasks* by learning their motions exclusively from human demonstrations. MotionTrans aims to realize the "greatest advantage" of human data: explicit, motion-level transfer for previously unseen tasks on a real robot. By focusing on end-to-end policies and systematic evaluation of zero-shot motion transfer, MotionTrans positions itself at the forefront of research aiming to fully unlock the potential of human behavioral data for generalizable robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the MotionTrans project is to **enable end-to-end robotic manipulation policies to directly learn and execute new motions for task completion based primarily on human VR data.** This encompasses:

- **Realizing explicit human-to-robot motion transfer:** The core goal is to demonstrate that motions learned from human demonstrations can be directly deployed on real robot hardware to accomplish tasks without any robot-specific training data for those tasks (zero-shot setting).
- **Enhancing few-shot learning performance:** Beyond zero-shot capabilities, the project aims to show that pretraining on a diverse human-robot dataset can significantly boost the performance of policies when only a small number of robot demonstrations for a specific task become available (few-shot setting).
- **Systematically exploring underlying mechanisms:** The research seeks to identify and understand the critical factors and mechanisms that contribute to successful motion transfer from humans to robots, such as the role of cotraining with robot data and the breadth of task-related motion coverage.

The motivation behind these objectives stems from the pervasive **data scarcity bottleneck in robotic imitation learning**. Collecting large, diverse real robot datasets is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming. Human data, conversely, is abundant, diverse, and easy to acquire. By enabling robots to learn directly from human demonstrations at a motion level, the researchers hope to:
- **Scale robotic capabilities:** Unlock a vast reservoir of human manipulation knowledge to rapidly expand the repertoire of tasks robots can perform.
- **Improve generalization:** Provide robots with a richer, more generalizable understanding of manipulation behaviors, leading to more robust and adaptable policies.
- **Reduce development costs:** Drastically cut down the need for costly robot-specific data collection for every new task.
- **Bridge the embodiment gap:** Develop effective strategies to reconcile the inherent differences between human and robot kinematics and dynamics, allowing human-centric motions to be translated into robot-executable actions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MotionTrans introduces a comprehensive framework designed to facilitate motion-level learning from human VR data for end-to-end robot policies. The framework consists of three main components: data collection, human data transformation, and weighted human-robot cotraining.

**A. Problem Definition:**
The goal is to train a policy $P_{policy}$ on a combined dataset $D = D_{robot} \cup D_{human}$, where human and robot task sets are non-overlapping. Evaluation is conducted in two settings:
- **Zero-shot:** Deploying $P_{policy}$ on real robots to perform tasks from $D_{human}$ without any robot data for these specific tasks.
- **Few-shot:** Finetuning $P_{policy}$ with a small number of robot demonstrations for tasks in $D_{human}$ and evaluating performance.
The policy's observation-action space ($S = (I_t, P_t, A_t)$) includes egocentric RGB images, proprioceptive states (wrist pose and hand joint state history), and action chunk predictions (wrist pose and hand joint state future).

**B. Human-Robot Data Collection System:**
- **Human Data Collection:** A portable, VR-based system (extending ARCap) is used, leveraging a Meta Quest 3 VR headset and a ZED2 RGB camera. It records hand keypoint positions, wrist poses from the VR device, and RGB images. A self-designed calibration method translates VR-space hand information to the RGB camera's coordinate frame. Real-time feedback in VR guides collectors to ensure hand visibility and alignment, and encourages viewpoint changes to enhance hand pose distribution diversity.
- **Robot Data Collection:** A Franka Emika robot arm combined with a 6DoF Inspire Dexterous Hand serves as the hardware platform, chosen to mimic human arm/hand functionality. Teleoperation (based on Open-Television) captures human wrist and hand poses via VR to drive the robot. A ZED2 camera provides egocentric RGB images.
- **MotionTrans Dataset:** A new dataset is collected, comprising 3,213 demonstrations across 15 human tasks and 15 robot tasks in over 10 scenes. Tasks cover a wide range of skills (pick-and-place, pouring, wiping, etc.). Human and robot task sets are non-overlapping. Scene diversity is emphasized for visual robustness (e.g., human tasks in at least 4 scenes, half of robot data in diverse "in-the-wild" settings). GPT-4o is used to enrich language instructions for VLA training.

**C. Human Data Transformation to Robot Format:**
This crucial step converts raw human data into a format compatible with robot policies, effectively treating human data as "supplementary robot data."
- **Image Observation ($I_t$):** Egocentric views are maintained for both human and robot data to ensure consistent spatial relationships.
- **Wrist Poses ($W_t$):** Measured in the egocentric camera coordinate system, unifying spatial definitions.
- **Hand Joint State ($H_t$):** The dex-retargeting library, an optimization-based inverse kinematics solver, maps human hand keypoints ($K_t$) to robot hand joint states.
- **Addressing Embodiment Gap Observations:**
 - **Speed Discrepancy (O1):** Human data is slowed down by a factor of 2.25 through interpolation to match robot manipulation speeds.
 - **Workspace Distribution Mismatch (O2):** Action-chunk-based *relative* poses are used for wrist action representation, reducing distribution differences. Encouraging viewpoint changes during human data collection also helps policies adapt to a larger workspace.
- **Visual Rendering:** The authors experimented with rendering robots into human videos to narrow the visual gap but found no significant improvement, thus not using it by default.

**D. Weighted Multi-Task Human-Robot Cotraining:**
After transformation, human and robot data are jointly trained under a shared end-to-end robot policy.
- **Policy Architectures:** Two widely used architectures are explored:
 - **Diffusion Policy (DP):** Extended for multi-task learning by associating each task with a learnable embedding. DINOv2 is used as a visual encoder for enhanced perception.
 - **Vision-Language-Action (π₀-VLA):** Utilizes large-scale pre-trained Vision-Language Models for multimodal perception and instruction following, using language directly to assign tasks. π₀-droid checkpoints are loaded.
- **Unified Action Normalization:** Unlike prior work, a single Z-score normalization is applied across *both* human and robot data for proprioceptive states and actions. This avoids a normalization mismatch between training and inference that would negatively impact motion-level transfer.
- **Weighted Cotraining Strategy:** To balance potential data imbalances, a weighted loss function $L_D = \alpha L_{D_{robot}} + (1 - \alpha) L_{D_{human}}$ is used, where $\alpha = \frac{|D_{human}|}{|D_{human}| + |D_{robot}|}$. This ensures equal summed weights for human and robot data, promoting balance. Domain adaptation techniques like domain confusion were explored but found to lead to instability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments systematically evaluated MotionTrans across zero-shot and few-shot settings, ablation studies, and analyses of transfer mechanisms.

**A. Zero-shot Experiment:**
- **Direct Motion Transfer Achieved:** MotionTrans enabled policies to achieve non-trivial success rates for 9 out of 13 evaluated human tasks, directly learning motions from human data without any robot demonstrations for these tasks. The average success rate (SR) was approximately 20%.
- **Meaningful Motion Generation:** For all 13 tasks, both Diffusion Policy (DP) and π₀-VLA achieved positive Motion Progress Scores (average ~0.5), indicating that even in unsuccessful cases, policies generated meaningful motions (e.g., reaching target objects, initiating sub-processes).
- **Crucial Role of Robot Data:** Cotraining with robot data was identified as essential; policies trained solely on human data exhibited random motion and 0% SR in zero-shot deployment, highlighting robot data's role in bridging the embodiment gap.
- **Architectural Differences:**
 - **DP:** Demonstrated stronger performance in precise manipulation (e.g., grasping), with an average 65% grasping SR for pick-and-place tasks, compared to π₀-VLA's 20%. It showed superior spatial location capabilities.
 - **π₀-VLA:** Excelled in task adherence and instruction following, performing more complete actions like pouring, suggesting a better balance between visual perception and task semantics.

**B. Few-shot Experiment:**
- **Significant Finetuning Boost:** Pretraining on the MotionTrans Dataset resulted in an average 40% improvement in success rate for few-shot finetuning (5-shot and 20-shot settings) compared to training from scratch. This indicates that human-robot pretraining provides valuable motion priors.
- **Combined Data Importance:** Pretraining with both human and robot data (MotionTrans) significantly outperformed pretraining on human-only or robot-only data. Robot-only pretraining generally outperformed human-only, suggesting that maintaining the same embodiment is more crucial than task similarity for pretraining data.
- **Impact of Data Quantity:** The benefits of human-robot pretraining were more pronounced in the low-data (5-shot) regime, with advantages diminishing or disappearing at 20-shot, as more robot-specific data became available.

**C. Design Ablation:**
- **Relative Poses are Key:** Using action-chunk-based *relative* poses (as in MotionTrans) was critical. Replacing it with *absolute* egocentric poses significantly decreased performance (SR 10.0% vs. 23.1%), due to increased distribution differences.
- **Unified Normalization Essential:** Embodiment-Dependent Normalization (independent for human/robot data) dramatically reduced performance (SR 8.4% vs. 23.1%), confirming that consistent normalization between training and inference is vital for motion-level transfer.
- **Visual Rendering Limitations:** Rendering robots into human videos did not yield significant improvements over direct training on human videos, possibly because neural networks could still discern the rendered domain.

**D. Analysis of Motion Transfer Mechanisms:**
- **Action Interpolation:** A case study on the "Bread-Bucket" task suggested that robot actions for human tasks are generated by *interpolating* actions from diverse robot data (e.g., different placement heights from robot tasks like "Bread-Pad" and "Bread-Platform"). Human task data guides this interpolation to generate task-aware motions within the robot's safe manipulation range.
- **Visual Perception Transfer:** Attention map visualizations (using DINOv2 and Grad-CAM) showed that the visual encoder learned to focus on target manipulation objects in human tasks, even when deployed on a robot (embodiment shift). This embodiment-invariant, task-aware representation helps the policy locate and interact with novel objects.
- **Scaling Trend:** Preliminary evidence suggested a scaling trend, where broader coverage of task-related motions and tasks in the training dataset led to improved motion transfer performance for new tasks.

**E. Supplementary Results:**
- **Robot Tasks Performance:** Cotraining with non-overlapping human tasks did not significantly impact the policy's performance on robot tasks, as sufficient robot data was already available for those tasks.
- **Visual Robustness:** MotionTrans policies exhibited robustness to novel visual backgrounds, with only slight performance drops when evaluated in scenes different from the training environment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MotionTrans represents a significant step forward in leveraging human demonstrations for robotic manipulation. Its key significance lies in systematically demonstrating and enabling **motion-level learning from human data for end-to-end robot policies**, a capability that has remained largely unverified despite the growing interest in human-in-the-loop robotics.

The potential impact of this work is substantial:

- **Addressing the Data Bottleneck:** By proving that robots can acquire new motion skills directly from human VR data, MotionTrans offers a viable path to significantly reduce the dependency on costly and labor-intensive real robot data collection. This could accelerate the development and deployment of more capable and versatile robots.
- **Unlocking the Full Potential of Human Data:** The framework moves beyond using human data merely for visual grounding or robustness, demonstrating its capacity to impart entirely new manipulation behaviors and skills to robots, greatly expanding the operational scope of robotic systems.
- **Providing a Clear Framework and Principles:** MotionTrans offers a comprehensive framework encompassing data collection, transformation, and cotraining strategies. Crucially, it identifies key factors for successful motion transfer: cotraining with robot data to bridge the embodiment gap and broad task-related motion coverage for effective action interpolation. These principles provide valuable guidance for future research and practical implementations.
- **Advancing Generalizable Robotics:** The ability to generalize learned motions to novel objects and tasks, even in zero-shot settings, contributes directly to the long-term goal of developing general-purpose robot agents. The insights into how actions and visual perception transfer, particularly through interpolation and embodiment-invariant attention, provide fundamental understanding for building more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems.
- **Facilitating Research and Development:** The open-sourcing of the MotionTrans dataset, code, and model weights is a major contribution to the robotics community. It provides a valuable benchmark and toolkit for other researchers to build upon, fostering further innovation in human-to-robot transfer learning.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations. The current monocular egocentric perception setup limits the policy's height perception, sometimes causing failures in reaching correct heights, especially in "in-the-wild" scenes. This could be addressed by incorporating wrist cameras for depth perception on both human and robot platforms. Another limitation is the reliance on a self-collected human dataset. Future work aims to extend motion-level learning to larger, internet-scale datasets, which would be the ultimate test for true generalizability and scalability.

Overall, MotionTrans pushes the boundaries of how human expertise can be translated into robot capabilities, laying important groundwork for future developments in scalable, data-efficient, and generalizable robotic manipulation.
