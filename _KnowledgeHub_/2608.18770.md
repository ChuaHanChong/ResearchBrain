# To Go Far, Go Together: Diverse Preferences Induce a Curriculum for Reward Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> CurriPO treats a diverse population of per-user reward models as an automatically discovered curriculum for RLHF policy optimization. It builds a tree-structured traversal that branches across checkpoints and reuses reward models from better initializations, serving all users in one pass. On MuJoCo continuous-control personalization it reaches 1.2-2.1x the population satisfaction of the strongest baseline while cutting training time roughly 20-30x.

## Problem

- Reward transformations can preserve the optimal policy while drastically changing how hard it is to optimize, so held-out reward accuracy alone cannot predict optimization difficulty.
- Prior personalization work builds better per-user reward models (latent utilities, shared preference spaces, preference-group mixtures) but ignores whether the resulting reward is optimizable by a policy.
- Per-user alignment requires a separate policy per user under a user-specific reward landscape, so users with hard-to-optimize rewards risk becoming a new underserved group even with accurate rewards.

## Method

- **CurriPO** ==probe-driven reward traversal==: iteratively optimizes a selected user's reward model from a warm-start checkpoint, retains the result in a growing set called the wake, and re-probes to pick the next model.
- ==Branching mechanism==: lets the next optimization stage start from any checkpoint in the wake rather than only the most recent one, letting the curriculum expand into a tree instead of a single chain.
- ==Reuse mechanism== with ==KL-regularized local stage optimization==: reselects a reward model already used for training when a newer checkpoint gives it a better probe score, re-anchoring a KL penalty to the chosen warm-start policy each stage.

## Results

- For N=12 users under oracle feedback CurriPO reaches **0.514/0.318/0.355/0.328** population satisfaction across four environments, beating every baseline (best baseline RM+RND at 0.388/0.239/0.260/0.157) while training in **1.1h** versus 2.6-5.7h.
- At N=100 CurriPO keeps its edge (e.g. **0.512** vs 0.352 for RM on HC-1D) and scales training cost from 1.1h to only **1.6h**, versus baselines rising from single digits to 22-48h; gains hold under B-Pref noisy labels and are significant by Wilcoxon test (p=0.0078, Holm-adjusted p=0.0391).
- Ablations show removing branching (Chain) causes the largest drop, removing reuse (No reuse) closes most but not all of the gap, and removing the KL penalty (No KL) inflates variance and degrades satisfaction in most settings; randomizing traversal order also sharply hurts performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A population's heterogeneous reward models can itself serve as a curriculum signal, removing the need for a separate curriculum-design or task-generation process.
> - Optimization difficulty, not just reward accuracy, is a distinct failure mode in personalized alignment; scaling training budget alone does not rescue targets that are structurally hard to optimize from a bad initialization.
> - Treating population diversity as a resource rather than an obstacle lets one training pass serve many objectives more cheaply than per-user optimization, an idea that could transfer to other multi-objective RLHF settings like LLM or VLA personalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of AI alignment, specifically focusing on aligning AI systems with individual human values through preference-based reinforcement learning (RL). A common approach in this area involves collecting human feedback, fitting a reward model based on these preferences, and subsequently optimizing a policy against this learned reward model. This paradigm has been applied across various domains, contributing to advancements in AI alignment.

Prior research in personalized alignment has largely focused on improving the accuracy and data efficiency of reward models to capture heterogeneous preferences. This includes methods that infer distributions over latent utilities (Siththaranjan et al., 2024), shared preference-space techniques for few-shot learning (Poddar et al., 2024), and approaches using mixtures of preference groups or shared prototypes (Shenfeld et al., 2025; Kim et al., 2026). These efforts aim to ensure that minority preferences are accurately represented in the reward models, preventing them from being overlooked due to data scarcity or aggregation.

However, this paper identifies a distinct challenge: even when accurate personalized reward models are available for all users, including those with minority preferences, the subsequent policy optimization process itself can introduce new forms of underrepresentation. It highlights that the accuracy of a reward model on held-out feedback does not guarantee that a policy can be effectively optimized against it. Previous work by Ng et al. (1999) and Skalse et al. (2023) has shown that reward transformations can significantly alter optimization difficulty without changing the optimal policy, and Razin et al. (2025b) demonstrated that a reward model can rank pairs correctly while still inducing a flat optimization landscape. This issue is particularly salient in per-user alignment, where a separate policy must be optimized for each user, often encountering diverse and potentially difficult-to-optimize reward landscapes.

This work addresses this specific gap by proposing that the multi-user structure, commonly seen as a challenge for personalized alignment, can be leveraged to improve the policy optimization stage. It introduces a novel perspective by applying curriculum learning principles, traditionally used for organizing training examples or tasks, to the diverse set of user-specific reward models. Unlike traditional curriculum learning, which typically progresses toward a single difficult objective, this method aims to efficiently cover a diverse population of users with different, potentially hard-to-optimize objectives by treating easier-to-optimize reward models as stepping stones. The paper differentiates itself from direct preference optimization methods (e.g., IPL, CPL) by retaining an explicit reward model and focusing on the optimization dynamics rather than bypassing the reward modeling stage.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to address the challenge of optimization difficulty in personalized AI alignment, where individual users' reward models may vary significantly in how easily a policy can be optimized against them. The paper argues that current approaches, which prioritize data-efficient and accurate per-user reward models, are insufficient because users whose reward models are difficult to optimize at the policy level can become a new underserved group, even if their preferences are accurately modeled.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations:
- **Optimization Difficulty Variation:** Reward models learned from different users can present substantially different optimization landscapes, with some being inherently easier to optimize from an initial policy than others. This heterogeneity can lead to disparities in alignment quality across a user population.
- **Limitations of Reward Model Accuracy:** High accuracy on held-out preference feedback for a reward model does not directly translate to ease of optimizing a policy against that model. This implies that improving reward model fidelity alone does not guarantee effective policy alignment for all users.
- **Emergence of a Natural Curriculum:** Given a sufficiently diverse user population, a natural curriculum can emerge between easy-to-optimize and hard-to-optimize reward models. The core intuition is that policies optimized for "easier" users could serve as beneficial starting points or "stepping stones" for optimizing policies for "harder" users.

Based on these motivations, the specific objectives of the paper are:
1. To develop a novel method, CurriPO, that explicitly leverages the multi-user structure in personalized AI alignment to improve the policy optimization process.
2. To construct an automatic curriculum from the diverse set of user reward models, enabling a tree-structured traversal of policy space that covers the entire population efficiently.
3. To demonstrate that this curriculum-based optimization can lead to improved overall population satisfaction, particularly benefiting users who would otherwise remain underserved by conventional per-user policy optimization methods.
4. To achieve these gains while simultaneously reducing the computational training time compared to existing baselines.
5. To validate the contributions of specific mechanisms, such as branching and reusing of checkpoints, within the proposed curriculum structure.

By addressing the optimization phase, this research aims to enhance the fairness and effectiveness of personalized AI systems by ensuring that a broader spectrum of user preferences, including those that are conventionally difficult to align, can be effectively served.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, CurriPO (Probe-Driven Reward Traversal), operates within a reward-free episodic decision process (M) and aims to provide each user (u) in a population (U) with a personalized policy (π_u) derived from an initial policy (π_0). This is achieved by using pairwise human feedback to fit per-user reward models (f_u), which collectively form a "reward model bank."

**Reward Modeling from Pairwise Preferences:**
The methodology begins by constructing the reward model bank. For each user `u`, a reward model `f_u: S × A → R` is fitted using pairwise preference feedback `F_u`. Each feedback instance consists of two trajectory segments (`ξ_0`, `ξ_1`) and a binary label (`y`), indicating which segment is preferred. The preference predictor is based on the Bradley–Terry model, where the probability of preferring `ξ_1` over `ξ_0` is `logistic(f_u(ξ_1) - f_u(ξ_0))`. Each `f_u` is optimized by minimizing binary cross-entropy over its respective feedback data.

**CurriPO: Probe-Driven Reward Traversal:**
CurriPO treats the collected reward model bank as an automatically discovered curriculum for policy optimization. The process is iterative, starting from an initial policy `π_0`.
1. **Traversal Stages:** At each stage `k`, CurriPO selects a user `u_k` and a policy checkpoint `π_w_k` from its "wake" (`W_k`), which is the set of all policies generated so far, including `π_0`. `π_w_k` serves as the warm-start for optimizing `f_u_k`. The newly optimized policy `π_{k+1}` is then added to `W_k` to form `W_{k+1}`.
2. **Probing the Wake:** To guide the traversal, CurriPO evaluates all checkpoints in `W_k`. For each checkpoint `π` and user `u`, a `competence` score `comp(π, u)` is estimated. This score represents the pool percentile that `π` achieves under `u`'s reward model, calculated by averaging `f_u` scores over short probe rollouts from `π` and comparing them against the empirical cumulative distribution of `f_u` scores over a behavior pool `D`. The `coverage` `C_k(u)` for a user `u` is defined as the maximum competence achieved by any checkpoint in `W_k` for that user.
3. **Selecting the Next Traversal Step:** CurriPO implements a curriculum principle by progressively expanding towards reward models that are sufficiently accessible from the current wake.
 - **Admissible Users:** It first identifies `admissible users` `U_k`, which are users whose current coverage `C_k(u)` exceeds a minimum threshold `c_lo`.
 - **Selection Rule:** Among these admissible users, `u_k` is chosen as the least-covered user (`argmin_u∈U_k C_k(u)`). The warm-start checkpoint `π_w_k` is then selected as the policy in `W_k` that currently serves `u_k` best (`argmax_π∈W_k comp(π, u_k)`). Ties are broken towards the most recently added checkpoint.
 - **Branching:** This selection mechanism allows the next optimization step to start from any checkpoint in the `wake`, rather than being restricted to the immediately preceding one. This enables the curriculum to branch into a tree structure, covering a broader range of heterogeneous objectives.
 - **Reusing:** Previously optimized reward models can be selected again for optimization (up to `q` times) if a newly added checkpoint in the wake provides a more promising initialization. This allows for refinement of policies for users who were difficult to serve initially.
4. **Local Stage Optimization:** Once `(π_w_k, u_k)` is selected, a local policy optimization stage is performed. The policy is initialized from `π_w_k` and optimized against the standardized reward model `f_u_k`. A KL divergence penalty (`β D_KL(π || π_w_k)`) is applied to `π`, ensuring that the optimization remains localized to the current checkpoint, preventing abrupt departures from the established curriculum and preserving useful behaviors.
5. **Serving from the Wake:** The traversal continues until a stopping criterion is met (e.g., `min_u C_k(u) ≥ c_stop` or budget exhaustion). The final output is the entire wake `W`. For deployment, each user `u` is assigned the checkpoint `π_u` from `W` that is most preferred by `u`'s own reward model (`argmax_π∈W comp(π, u)`).

**Experimental Setup:**
CurriPO was evaluated on personalized continuous-control tasks in MuJoCo environments (HalfCheetah-1D/2D, Hopper-1D/2D), which involve user preferences over behavioral features like velocity, energy consumption, and z-height. Scripted users were generated with diverse preferences. Experiments were conducted with population sizes of N=12 and N=100, using both oracle (deterministic) and B-Pref noisy preference feedback. Evaluation metrics included utilitarian, egalitarian, and Nash population satisfaction, calculated based on ground-truth user utility functions not observed during training. Baselines included reward model optimization methods (RM, RM+RND, VPL) and direct preference optimization methods (CPL, IPL).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted provided several key findings regarding CurriPO's performance and the effectiveness of its design components:

1. **Superior Population Satisfaction:**
 - CurriPO consistently achieved higher utilitarian population satisfaction compared to all baselines across all four MuJoCo environments (HalfCheetah-1D/2D, Hopper-1D/2D), for both population sizes (N=12, N=100), and under both oracle and B-Pref noisy feedback.
 - Quantitatively, CurriPO demonstrated 1.2–2.1 times the population satisfaction of the strongest baseline. These gains were particularly pronounced in the more complex 2-D settings.
 - Under noisy feedback, CurriPO maintained its advantage, indicating robustness to altered reward model geometries induced by imperfect preferences.

2. **Efficiency and Scalability:**
 - CurriPO significantly reduced training time compared to baselines. For instance, increasing the population from 12 to 100 users only marginally increased CurriPO's training time (from 1.1 h to 1.6 h), whereas baselines experienced substantial increases (from 2.6–5.7 h to 22–48 h).

3. **Improved Service for Underserved Users:**
 - Analysis of satisfaction landscapes revealed that CurriPO maintained high satisfaction across a broader range of preference targets, especially in regions where conventional optimization methods (RM, RM+RND) exhibited low satisfaction.
 - Ablation studies showed that baselines were not merely undertrained for these "hard-to-reach" users; even with significantly increased training budgets, conventional methods failed to improve satisfaction in these challenging regions.
 - CurriPO also achieved higher Nash and egalitarian population satisfaction, indicating that its performance gains were driven by extending effective coverage to users who were poorly served by other methods, rather than solely by improving already well-served users.

4. **Contribution of Key Mechanisms (Branching, Reusing, KL Penalty):**
 - **Branching:** An ablation study comparing CurriPO to a "Chain" variant (without branching or reusing) demonstrated that Chain performed substantially worse. This suggests that the ability to warm-start optimization from any checkpoint in the wake is critical for reaching diverse and hard-to-reach users.
 - **Reusing:** The "No reuse" ablation (with branching but no reusing) showed degradation in some settings compared to full CurriPO. Case studies further illustrated that reusing previously optimized reward models from newly discovered, more favorable initializations in the wake could significantly increase satisfaction for challenging users.
 - **KL Penalty:** Removing the KL penalty in the local optimization stage ("No KL") led to degraded satisfaction and increased variance, supporting its role in ensuring that each stage constitutes a local, progressive transition within the curriculum.

5. **Importance of Curriculum Ordering and Threshold (`c_lo`):**
 - Randomizing the curriculum order for hard-to-reach targets sharply reduced satisfaction, confirming that CurriPO's specific coverage-based ordering is crucial and that simply warm-starting from other users' policies is insufficient.
 - Ablating the `c_lo` threshold (which restricts expansion to admissible users) substantially degraded performance, emphasizing the importance of expanding the curriculum at an appropriate frontier. Performance was found to be relatively stable around the default `c_lo` setting, indicating a robust parameter choice.

6. **Serving from the Wake Effect:**
 - Applying CurriPO's final serving rule (assigning the best checkpoint from the policy pool) to baselines resulted in only minor improvements. This indicated that the primary benefit of CurriPO stems from its curriculum traversal, which generates a more diverse and higher-quality policy pool, rather than solely from the selection mechanism itself.

In summary, the results indicate that CurriPO effectively leverages the diversity in user preferences to construct an adaptive curriculum, leading to more comprehensive and efficient policy alignment across heterogeneous populations, particularly benefiting those users whose preferences are difficult to satisfy using conventional optimization techniques.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of AI alignment and personalized reinforcement learning by identifying and addressing a distinct problem: the optimization difficulty associated with diverse user-specific reward models, independent of their accuracy. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Novel Approach to Multi-User Optimization:** CurriPO introduces a novel paradigm by explicitly exploiting the multi-user structure at the policy *optimization* stage, rather than solely at the reward *modeling* stage. This represents a conceptual shift in how multi-user settings can be leveraged, transforming what is often considered a challenge (heterogeneous preferences) into a resource (a curriculum for optimization). This distinction is important as it demonstrates that even perfectly modeled preferences can fail to be satisfied if the optimization process is inadequate.

2. **Enhanced Fairness and Inclusivity in AI Alignment:** By specifically targeting and improving alignment for users whose reward models are difficult to optimize, CurriPO has the potential to reduce new forms of underrepresentation that arise during policy learning. This can lead to more equitable and inclusive AI systems that better cater to the needs and preferences of a diverse user base, preventing certain groups from being systematically underserved. The strong performance on egalitarian and Nash satisfaction metrics directly supports this impact.

3. **Improved Efficiency and Scalability:** The method's ability to achieve higher population satisfaction with substantially lower training costs is significant. This efficiency makes personalized AI alignment more practical and scalable for real-world applications where resources might be constrained and user populations are large and diverse.

4. **Generality of Curriculum Learning Principles:** The work demonstrates a successful application of curriculum learning in a novel context, where the "tasks" are the diverse user preferences themselves. This could inspire similar approaches in other multi-objective or multi-task reinforcement learning scenarios where a diverse set of objectives could be structured into a curriculum for more effective and efficient learning.

5. **Implications for Future AI Systems:** As AI systems, particularly large language models (LLMs) and vision-language-action models, become increasingly integrated into personal and societal contexts, the demand for personalized alignment will grow. CurriPO offers a foundational step towards addressing the inherent optimization challenges in aligning such complex systems with individual users. Its principles could be extended to these domains, informing the design of future personalized AI.

6. **Guidance for Reward Model Design:** The findings implicitly highlight the need for reward models that are not only accurate but also *optimizable*. Future research on reward modeling might consider metrics beyond held-out accuracy, incorporating aspects related to the "learnability" or "flatness" of the induced policy landscape.

Overall, this research provides a robust framework for improving the practical deployment of personalized AI systems by ensuring effective alignment across diverse user preferences, ultimately contributing to more useful, fair, and scalable AI technologies.
