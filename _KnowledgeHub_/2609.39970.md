# PhasePlan: Ordered Future-Phase Planning for Robot Brain Models

> [!summary] Summary
> PhasePlan adds an ordered future-phase planner to robot brain models like pi0.5. For each of the 16 future positions in an action chunk, it predicts a task-phase distribution and uses that plan feature to condition the matching action token. The planner is trained first, then frozen. On real conveyor-belt manipulation, offline joint-action error drops 22.5% versus the original pi0.5.

## Problem

- Fixed-length action chunks often span several task phases, which hides where transitions such as Wait-to-Act or Act-to-Release occur inside the predicted chunk.
- Demonstrations are phase-imbalanced: long waiting and transport phases dominate, so action optimization favors frequent motions and neglects short, timing-critical release and transition moments.
- Prior progress representations such as GuidedVLA's future-skill histogram capture phase proportions but not order or transition position, so different phase sequences look identical.

## Method

- **Ordered future-phase planner**: 16 independent learnable ==phase queries== read shared multimodal observation tokens, and a per-position ==cross-entropy loss== trains each query to predict the phase at its offset.
- **Per-position plan conditioning**: the contextual planning representation is fused with a ==soft phase embedding==, then drives zero-initialized ==FiLM==-style scale and shift modulation of only the matching action-expert token.
- **Two-stage adaptation**: the planner is trained first with phase labels built from VLX-Seek object boxes, gripper state, and temporal rules, then frozen while the ==flow-matching== action expert adapts.

## Results

- The planner reaches about **92.5%** per-position future-phase accuracy and **89%** transition-type accuracy. Macro-F1 drops from over **91%** to **43%** without RGB, and to **36%** with shuffled RGB.
- Overall joint MAE is **0.034605 rad**, **22.5%** below original pi0.5 and **12.7%** below the future-skill-histogram baseline. Hand-command accuracy reaches **93.162%**, and cross-phase MAE is **0.041318 rad**.
- Freezing the planner improves transition timing and slightly lowers joint error compared with joint updating. Inverse-phase-frequency weighting lifts Release hand accuracy from **82.6%** to **84.3%** but raises joint MAE **1.3%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Position-aligned plans beat aggregate summaries: encoding which phase occurs at each future step, not just phase proportions, measurably improves chunked action prediction.
> - Predicting a plan alone does nothing for actions. The gain appears only when each plan feature is wired into its matching action token.
> - Freezing a learned intermediate representation before downstream adaptation preserves its temporal semantics, which matters most for short, rare transition phases like release.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

On a moving conveyor, a robot waits for a red package, grasps and transports it to a basket, releases it, then returns home. Waiting and transporting take longer than the short release and the phase transitions. A policy that predicts a block of future movements, called an action chunk, can cross one of those transitions. Optimizing common actions can therefore leave the timing of a short but important phase unclear.

PhasePlan predicts the phase separately at each of the next 16 action positions. It then uses each position's prediction to condition the matching action. The authors evaluated it in detail on the conveyor task with pretrained π₀.₅. There, offline joint-action mean absolute error (MAE), the average absolute difference between predicted and recorded joint targets in radians, falls from **0.044653 rad** for the original policy to **0.034605 rad** for PhasePlan. That is a **22.5% reduction**. This is an offline prediction result. It is not evidence of fewer attempts, shorter elapsed time, fewer generated tokens, or higher task success.

Real-robot deployment on the conveyor task. Six keyframes show the green conveyor, blue collection basket, package, and robot arm. The paper describes approach and grasp, transport to the basket, and placement under real visual feedback and robot dynamics. This is a qualitative sequence and reports no success rate.

### 2. Why phase order matters

A phase is a task segment such as Wait, Act, Release, or Return-Home. A summary of phase counts loses their order. "Wait, Wait, Act, Act" and "Act, Act, Wait, Wait" have the same counts but call for different actions at each position. Similar average action errors need not mean similar transition timing or closed-loop behavior.

The closest contrast is [[2605.12369|GuidedVLA]]. The PhasePlan paper describes GuidedVLA as predicting an aggregated distribution of future skills, meaning the proportions of phases in an action chunk. PhasePlan instead predicts a phase distribution for each future action position. It aligns each resulting plan feature to its corresponding action token. The paper tests a future-skill-histogram baseline of this kind in the conveyor setup. The authors report an improvement over it.

### 3. How the plan guides actions

The policy receives head- and wrist-camera RGB, robot state, and the instruction. In the conveyor experiment it predicts a 16-action chunk. Independent queries read the same observation context but represent different future offsets. At each future position, the planner produces a contextual planning representation and a distribution over phases. Together these condition the action representation at the corresponding offset.

Training happens in two stages. First, the planner learns from position-aligned phase labels. The planner is then frozen while the action expert and conditioning path are adapted. At deployment the planner remains inside the policy. Its phase labels are training supervision, not required deployment inputs.

Overview of the PhasePlan framework. Head-view RGB, wrist-view RGB, robot state, and language-instruction inputs feed shared multimodal observation tokens. Distinct future-position queries feed an independent-query cross-attention planner that outputs planning representations and phase distributions. The schematic also depicts planner training followed by action training with the planner frozen.

### 4. The decisive comparison

All methods share the same π₀.₅ initialization, demonstrations, episode split, optimization budget, and action representation. One variant, Ours-P, trains the ordered future-phase planner but leaves the original action path unconditioned. PhasePlan connects each position's plan feature to its corresponding future action token.

In Table 1, Ours-P has the same reported overall and cross-phase joint MAE and hand accuracy as original π₀.₅. PhasePlan improves those metrics. This is the paper's most direct test of whether adding an ordered planner alone suffices. The authors intend this controlled comparison to isolate per-position conditioning. The result is consistent with that mechanism in the tested setup but does not prove it is the only cause.

Offline action prediction on the conveyor validation set, averaged over three seeds. The validation set contains 3,584 windows, including 1,613 cross-phase windows. Rows are Original π₀.₅, Future-skill histogram, Ours-P, and PhasePlan. Columns are overall joint MAE, cross-phase joint MAE, and hand accuracy; lower MAE and higher accuracy are better. Ours-P matches the original policy's reported entries, whereas PhasePlan improves them. This is an offline comparison, not a closed-loop success result.

### 5. Contribution and boundary

Representing where a transition falls in a chunk gives each future action a phase-aligned cue. The Ours-P comparison is consistent with that alignment helping offline action prediction.

The detailed quantitative evaluation covers one dynamic conveyor task using the π₀.₅ implementation. The paper reports qualitative real-robot execution on the conveyor task and qualitative AcrossWAM1.0 execution. It does not report quantitative AcrossWAM1.0 results.
