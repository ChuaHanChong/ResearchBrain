# Imagination Policy: Using Generative Point Cloud Models for Learning Manipulation Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> IMAGINATION POLICY introduces a framework that uses generative point cloud models to estimate desired geometric goal states for robotic manipulation, enabling high-precision tasks and improved sample efficiency. The approach achieves superior performance on challenging RLBench tasks like Plug-Charger and Insert-Knife, and demonstrates high success rates on a physical robot with limited demonstrations.

## Problem

- Existing robotic manipulation policies struggle with tasks requiring high precision and fine motor control, such as inserting or plugging objects.
- Learning effective manipulation policies often demands a substantial number of expert demonstrations, leading to low sample efficiency.
- Directly mapping observations to actions in robot learning can lose valuable information about the desired geometric configuration of objects, hindering precision and generalization.

## Method

- ==The IMAGINATION POLICY== framework factors action inference into two stages: a generative model estimates desired target point clouds, followed by transformation inference via point cloud registration (SVD).
- A novel generative point cloud model is developed to 'imagine' the desired rigid body motion for both pick and place key-frame manipulation.
- The method integrates SE(3) bi-equivariant constraints during training through extensive SO(3) data augmentation, enhancing sample efficiency and generalization by enforcing symmetry properties.

## Results

- Achieved state-of-the-art performance on several challenging RLBench tasks, demonstrating superior success rates on high-precision tasks like Plug-Charger (**26.67%** vs. **0%** for baselines with 10 demos) and Insert-Knife (**42.67%** vs. max **8%** for baselines).
- Exhibited high sample efficiency, outperforming baselines with 5 demonstrations compared to 10 for Stack-Wine (**97.33%** vs. **32%**).
- Successfully validated on a real UR5 robot across multiple tasks (e.g., Mug-Tree **80.0%**, Plug-Flower **93.3%**) without pre-training on simulated data, and showed capabilities for longer-horizon and articulated object tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling and reasoning about the desired geometric configuration of objects in a goal state leads to more precise and effective manipulation policies.
> - Incorporating bi-equivariant symmetries in the generative model significantly improves sample efficiency and enables generalization from fewer demonstrations.
> - Decoupling the action inference process into a geometric generation step and a transformation estimation step simplifies the overall problem and leverages existing robust algorithms like SVD.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation, particularly high-precision pick-and-place tasks, presents significant challenges in robot learning. A common approach in robotic policy learning involves directly mapping observations, often visual data like point clouds, to actions such as SE(3) poses or displacements. While methods like PerAct, RVT, and Diffuser Actor have demonstrated capabilities in multi-task scenarios, they often encounter difficulties with tasks requiring high precision and typically necessitate extensive expert demonstrations for effective learning. This direct mapping strategy can inherently lose valuable information regarding the desired geometric configuration of objects in the scene, potentially leading to reduced sample efficiency and lower precision.

The broader landscape of robotic learning has seen advancements in utilizing point clouds as a flexible, geometric representation for object shapes and poses. Works such as those by Simeonov et al. have explored SE(3)-invariant point features and extensions of diffusion policies to point cloud observations for learning multimodal actions. Other related approaches, like TAX-POSE, focus on generating target point clouds from segmented inputs, although they may not iteratively predict point movements and are often limited to single-task, one-step pick-and-place scenarios.

A parallel development in robot learning involves leveraging symmetries inherent in 3D Euclidean space tasks, which are invariant to rigid transformations (translations and rotations). Equivariant modeling techniques have been applied to enforce pick symmetries for grasp learning and to improve sample efficiency in pick-and-place operations. However, many existing equivariant methods are constrained to single-task pick-and-place settings, limiting their applicability to more general, multi-task manipulation scenarios that involve sequences of actions or complex object interactions beyond simple rearrangement. The integration of such symmetries into general key-frame, multi-task manipulation policies, while maintaining high precision and sample efficiency, remains an area of active research. This work aims to bridge these gaps by introducing a generative model that explicitly reasons about desired geometric states, incorporating bi-equivariant symmetries for enhanced learning and generalization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work is drawn from human cognitive abilities, where individuals can mentally "imagine" a desired goal state for object rearrangement and subsequently execute actions to achieve that imagined state. Current robotic policy learning algorithms often bypass this imaginative step, opting instead for a direct mapping from sensory observations to robot actions. The authors contend that this direct approach is suboptimal because it omits critical information about the desired geometry of the scene, leading to less sample-efficient and less precise manipulation policies.

The research aims to address two key challenges in current multi-task manipulation policy learning:
1. **High-precision manipulation:** Many contemporary methods struggle with tasks demanding fine motor control and accurate geometric alignment, such as plugging a charger or inserting a knife. The authors hypothesize that enabling the model to explicitly reason about detailed geometric interactions—how different parts of an object surface should be displaced for alignment—can facilitate more precise control over tool tip movements and mating surfaces.
2. **Sample-efficient learning:** Learning effective manipulation policies often requires a substantial number of expert demonstrations. The authors propose to improve sample efficiency by incorporating symmetries inherent in two-object systems, known as bi-equivariance. By reasoning about the desired relative configurations of objects, the model can generalize more effectively from fewer demonstrations. While previous work has utilized bi-equivariant structures, this paper seeks to extend their application beyond simple pick-place scenarios to the more general key-frame multi-task setting.

Specifically, the paper outlines three main contributions:
1. **Novel generative point cloud model:** To the best of the authors' knowledge, this work proposes the first generative point cloud model designed to estimate desired rigid body motion within a key-frame manipulation context.
2. **SE(3) bi-equivariant constraints:** The paper demonstrates how to implement SE(3) bi-equivariant constraints effectively in this novel generative manipulation setting, contributing to improved sample efficiency and generalization.
3. **State-of-the-art performance:** The resulting method, IMAGINATION POLICY, is evaluated to achieve state-of-the-art performance on several challenging RLBench tasks against established baselines.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The IMAGINATION POLICY framework factors the action inference process into two sequential stages: point cloud generation and transformation inference. This approach aims to transform the complex task of action inference into a more localized generative problem focused on desirable geometric states.

**Problem Statement:** The method operates on a dataset `D` containing samples `(P_a, P_b, T_a, T_b, P_ab, ℓ)`. Here, `P_a` and `P_b` are segmented point clouds representing two objects (e.g., gripper and object to pick, or placement and object to arrange). `P_ab` represents the combined point cloud of these two objects in their desired configuration as described by a language instruction `ℓ`. `T_a` and `T_b` are the rigid transformations that convert `P_a` and `P_b` into the desired `P_ab`. The objective is to learn a policy function `f : (P_a, P_b, ℓ) → a`, where `a` is the gripper movement in SE(3). The method considers both pre-place and place actions as part of the overall manipulation sequence.

**Imagination Policy Overview:**
The core of IMAGINATION POLICY is a generative model `f_gen : (P_a, P_b, ℓ) → (ˆP_a, ˆP_b)`, which, conditioned on the language instruction `ℓ`, generates new point cloud coordinates `ˆP_a` and `ˆP_b` that approximate `P_ab`. Following this generation, point cloud registration methods, specifically Singular Value Decomposition (SVD), are employed to estimate rigid transformations `ˆT_a` (from `P_a` to `ˆP_a`) and `ˆT_b` (from `P_b` to `ˆP_b`). The final robot actions are then derived from these estimated transformations:
- For a pick action (`a_pick`), where `P_a` is the canonical gripper and `P_b` is the object, the action is `a_pick = ˆT_b^-1`.
- For place actions (`a_place`), where `P_a` is the placement and `P_b` is the object to be arranged, the action is `a_place = (ˆT_a)^-1 ˆT_b`.

#### 3.1. Pair Generation for Place (`f_place`)

The `f_place` model handles scenarios where two objects (`P_a`, `P_b`) are rearranged relative to each other.
- **Encoding Point Features:** Point cloud encoders `ϕ_a` and `ϕ_b` (based on PVCNN architecture) process the XYZ coordinates and RGB color of points in `P_a` and `P_b` to produce pointwise features `{f_i^a}` and `{f_j^b}`.
- **Generating Points:** A modified Point Straight Flow (PSF) model acts as the conditional generator. It iteratively flows an initial noisy point cloud `X_0` (sampled from a scaled Gaussian) towards the target configuration `P_ab` by integrating over a neural network parameterized vector field `v_θ`. The network `v_θ` is conditioned on the current point cloud state `X_t`, the encoded point features `F_a` and `F_b`, the encoded language feature `f_ℓ` (from a CLIP model), and the time step `t`. The model is trained using an MSE loss, minimizing the difference between `v_θ` and the optimal drift `(P_ab - X_0)`.
- **Estimating Action:** After `f_gen` produces `ˆP_a` and `ˆP_b`, SVD is used to find the best-fitting rigid transformations `ˆT_a` and `ˆT_b`. The `a_place` action is then computed as `(ˆT_a)^-1 ˆT_b`.
- **Realizing Bi-equivariance:** The model is designed to be bi-equivariant for place actions. Proposition 1 states that if the initial Gaussian noise `X_0` and the encoded point features `F_a`, `F_b` are rotation-invariant, then `f_place(g_a · P_a, g_b · P_b) = g_a f_place(P_a, P_b) g_b^-1` for any rotations `g_a, g_b ∈ SO(3)`. This is achieved by ensuring that the generated point clouds remain identical despite input rotations, leading to an appropriately transformed action.

#### 3.2. Single Generation for Pick (`f_pick`)

The `f_pick` network is specialized for picking an object (`P_b`) with the gripper (`P_a`).
- In this setting, `P_a` (gripper points) is fixed in a canonical pose. Only `X_P_b_0` (noise for `P_b`) is sampled from a Gaussian. The target `P_ab` is the union of the canonicalized gripper and `P_b` in its grasped pose (`P_a ∪ T_b P_b`).
- The loss function focuses solely on the object points: `min_θ E(||v_θ - (T_b P_b - X_P_b_0)||^2)`.
- After `ˆT_b` is estimated from `(P_b, ˆP_b)`, the pick action is `a_pick = ˆT_b^-1`.
- **Equivariance:** Proposition 2 demonstrates that `f_pick` is equivariant to rotations on the pick target (`P_b`), meaning `f_pick(P_a, g_b · P_b) = g_b f_pick(P_a, P_b)` if `F_a` and `F_b` are rotation-invariant.

**Model Architecture Details:**
Both `f_pick` and `f_place` share the same architecture, utilizing PVCNN for point encoders (outputting 64-dim features) and PSF as the generator backbone. A pre-trained CLIP-ViT32 model encodes language instructions into a 32-dim vector. Time step and binary point masks are also encoded as 32-dim positional embeddings. The generator input per point is a 163-dim vector. Training uses the Adam optimizer with an MSE loss. During inference, `X_0` is sampled randomly, and `v_θ` is integrated over 1000 steps to generate `P_ab` and calculate the action. Point clouds are pre-processed by centering, voxel grid downsampling (4mm), and subsampling/duplicating to 2048 points. Crucially, extensive SO(3) data augmentation (uniform random rotations) is applied to `P_a` and `P_b` during training to enforce rotation-invariant generation and realize the desired symmetry properties.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents evaluation results across simulated benchmarks and real-world robot experiments, along with ablation studies to validate design choices.

**3D Key-frame Pick and Place on RLBench:**
The method was evaluated on six challenging multi-task pick-and-place scenarios from RLBench: Phone-on-Base, Stack-Wine, Put-Plate, Put-Roll, Plug-Charger, and Insert-Knife. It was compared against strong baselines: PerAct, RVT, 3D Diffuser Actor, and RPDiff (adapted for multi-task learning).
- **Performance:** IMAGINATION POLICY demonstrated superior performance on most tasks, particularly those requiring high precision such as Plug-Charger, Insert-Knife, and Put-Roll, where baselines often failed. For instance, in Plug-Charger, IMAGINATION POLICY achieved a 26.67% success rate with 10 demos, while all baselines had 0%. In Insert-Knife, it reached 42.67% compared to a maximum of 8% from baselines.
- **Sample Efficiency:** The method exhibited high sample efficiency. With only 5 demonstrations, IMAGINATION POLICY sometimes outperformed baselines trained with 10 demonstrations. For example, on Stack-Wine, it achieved 97.33% success with 5 demos, significantly surpassing the best baseline's 32% with 10 demos.
- **Limitations:** The method showed lower performance on Put-Plate compared to RVT. The authors hypothesize this might be due to the task object's symmetry, which could make it difficult to encode distinguishable point features and lead to incorrect correspondences during rigid transformation estimation.
- **Key-Frame Expert:** An upper bound for performance using expert poses (Key-Frame Expert) revealed room for improvement, particularly on Put-Plate (74.6% vs. 34.67%), Put-Roll (56% vs. 23.61%), Plug-Charger (72% vs. 26.67%), and Insert-Knife (90.6% vs. 42.67%), indicating challenges in accurate generation for these complex tasks.

**Ablation Study (Multimodal Pick-part Dataset):**
An ablation study using a custom multimodal pick-part dataset (four YCB objects with different grasp labels) provided quantitative insights into the model's components:
- **Equivariance:** IMAGINATION POLICY demonstrated robustness and equivariance, achieving average rotation errors of 2-3 degrees and translation errors of approximately 1 cm, even with randomly rotated and sampled inputs.
- **Component Impact:** Removing downsampling or color information slightly increased rotation errors. A notable performance degradation occurred without SO(3) data augmentation during training, confirming its importance for learning rotation-invariant features and benefiting from symmetry.
- **Encoder Choice:** The PVCNN-based point cloud encoder outperformed both PointNet and a pre-trained equivariant Vector Neuron encoder, suggesting PVCNN's architecture offers stronger expressivity in this context.

**Real Robot Experiment:**
The approach was validated on a UR5 robot with a Robotiq-85 gripper, demonstrating its applicability to physical systems without pre-training on simulated data.
- **Setup:** A multi-task agent was trained from scratch with a total of 30 demonstrations across three tasks: Mug-Tree, Plug-Flower, and Pour-Ball.
- **Performance:** High success rates were achieved: 80.0% for Mug-Tree, 93.3% for Plug-Flower, and 93.3% for Pour-Ball.
- **Failure Analysis:** Failures were primarily attributed to distortions in real-world sensor observations (e.g., disappearing object features due to noise/calibration) and errors in motion planning (e.g., collisions during execution).

**Longer Horizon and Articulated Object Tasks (Appendix):**
- **Stack-three-Chairs:** The method achieved an overall success rate of 48.05% for this multi-step, longer-horizon task, demonstrating its capability to handle sequential pick-and-place operations.
- **Open-Microwave:** For an articulated object task, by segmenting the microwave door (with handle) and frame, IMAGINATION POLICY achieved a 69.33% success rate in opening the microwave door. This indicates potential for extending the method to articulated object manipulation by predicting relative poses between different links.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The IMAGINATION POLICY represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing a novel paradigm for policy learning. Its core strength lies in leveraging generative point cloud models to "imagine" desired goal states, thereby transforming action inference into a geometrically informed process. This approach directly addresses two critical limitations of existing methods: the struggle with **high-precision manipulation** and the demand for **high sample efficiency**.

By explicitly reasoning about the geometric configuration of goal states, IMAGINATION POLICY facilitates more accurate control, as evidenced by its superior performance on challenging, high-precision RLBench tasks like Plug-Charger and Insert-Knife, where many baselines failed. This capability could lead to more robust and reliable robotic systems for intricate assembly, manufacturing, or service robotics applications.

Furthermore, the principled incorporation of **SE(3) bi-equivariant constraints** significantly enhances the model's sample efficiency and generalization capabilities. The ability to learn effective policies from as few as 5 expert demonstrations, outperforming baselines trained with 10 demonstrations, is a notable achievement. This few-shot learning capacity has substantial implications for reducing the cost and effort associated with robot training, making it more feasible to deploy robots in environments with limited expert data or rapidly changing task requirements.

The successful validation on a **physical robot** further underscores the practical relevance and robustness of IMAGINATION POLICY, demonstrating its ability to transfer from simulation to real-world scenarios. The exploration of longer-horizon and articulated object tasks suggests the method's potential for tackling more complex and diverse manipulation challenges beyond rigid pick-and-place, extending its impact to broader domains of robotic interaction.

Acknowledged limitations, such as the reliance on segmented point clouds and current inference speed, indicate clear avenues for future research. However, the foundational conceptual framework of generative imagination for policy learning, coupled with its empirical performance and demonstrated symmetries, positions IMAGINATION POLICY as a promising advancement that could influence future developments in intelligent robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks demanding precision and rapid adaptation.
