# VIRAL: Visual Sim-to-Real at Scale for Humanoid Loco-Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The VIRAL framework enables robust, vision-based humanoid loco-manipulation, achieving zero-shot sim-to-real transfer for continuous tasks using only onboard RGB perception. The system demonstrated a 54 out of 59 task success rate on a Unitree G1 robot, performing near-expert level with an average cycle time of 20.2 seconds and generalizing across diverse real-world environments without fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Autonomous loco-manipulation skills for humanoid robots using only onboard perception are lacking, hindering real-world deployment.
- Collecting large-scale, high-quality real-world data for complex humanoid tasks is prohibitively expensive and logistically challenging.
- Bridging the sim-to-real gap for combined locomotion and manipulation in high-DoF humanoids with visual input remains a significant challenge due to complexity and the 'reality gap'.

## Method

- A two-phase teacher-student learning framework trains a privileged RL teacher in simulation, then distills it into a vision-based student policy using DAgger and behavior cloning.
- Leverages large-scale GPU-accelerated simulation for both teacher (16 GPUs) and student (up to 64 GPUs with tiled rendering) training.
- Implements extensive sim-to-real transfer techniques including system identification for dexterous hands, camera alignment, and comprehensive visual and simulation randomization.

## Results

- Achieved 54 out of 59 successful continuous loco-manipulation cycles on a real Unitree G1 humanoid, closely matching an expert human teleoperator's performance (20.2 vs. 21.4 seconds cycle time).
- Policies demonstrated strong zero-shot generalization to diverse real-world conditions including varied object placements, table types, lighting, and object categories.
- Ablation studies confirmed the criticality of RSI (**95%** success vs. <**10%**), delta action space, state-of-the-art vision backbones, and extensive domain randomization for policy performance and transferability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Compute scale is critical for both teacher and student training, demonstrating that large-scale simulation infrastructure is necessary for robust learning of complex humanoid behaviors.
> - Reference State Initialization (RSI) using teleoperated demonstrations significantly improves exploration and learning for long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks.
> - A delta action space (outputting command increments to a whole-body controller) and a hybrid DAgger-Behavior Cloning distillation approach are crucial for stable training and robust sim-to-real transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous humanoid robots capable of complex loco-manipulation (coordinated locomotion and manipulation) is a grand challenge in robotics, often envisioned as the path to general-purpose physical intelligence. However, current humanoid systems largely demonstrate either blind locomotion, static tabletop manipulation, or rely heavily on human teleoperation or non-onboard sensors. The critical missing piece is the ability to perform long-horizon, autonomous loco-manipulation using only onboard perception in diverse, real-world environments.

Recently, inspired by the success of large language models, there has been a significant push towards "robotic foundation models" trained on large-scale real-world teleoperation datasets. While promising for specific manipulation tasks, this approach faces substantial hurdles for mobile and humanoid manipulation, where environmental variations are far greater, and data collection costs are prohibitively high due to hardware complexity, safety concerns, and teleoperation overhead.

VIRAL addresses this challenge by revisiting and significantly scaling the visual sim-to-real paradigm. Sim-to-real has proven highly effective for legged locomotion, where policies trained in simulation routinely transfer to hardware. However, its success in manipulation has been largely limited to tabletop settings and narrow tasks. Combining locomotion and manipulation, especially for high-DoF humanoids with onboard vision, presents unique difficulties for sim-to-real, largely due to the increased complexity of contact physics, visual diversity, and the "reality gap."

This paper explicitly aims to bridge the gap between isolated locomotion and manipulation research by asking: "Can visual sim-to-real enable useful humanoid loco-manipulation with onboard perception?" It positions itself not as proposing a novel RL or sim-to-real algorithm, but rather as providing a comprehensive "technical recipe" and full-stack solution to make RGB-based humanoid loco-manipulation practical. This positions VIRAL as a significant engineering and system integration achievement that pushes the boundaries of what is achievable with current simulation fidelity, GPU compute, and humanoid hardware, moving beyond theoretical proposals to deliver a deployable, robust system.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the VIRAL project is to overcome the "key barrier to the real-world deployment of humanoid robots: the lack of autonomous loco-manipulation skills." Specifically, the authors aim to develop a visual sim-to-real framework that enables a humanoid robot to learn long-horizon, continuous loco-manipulation tasks entirely in simulation and then deploy these skills zero-shot to real hardware using only onboard RGB perception.

The core motivations driving this objective are:

- **Addressing the "Data Problem" in Robotics:** Collecting large-scale, high-quality real-world data for complex humanoid loco-manipulation is extremely expensive, time-consuming, and logistically challenging. The goal is to leverage the scalability and low marginal cost of modern GPU-accelerated, photorealistic simulators to generate orders of magnitude more data than is feasible with human teleoperation.
- **Achieving General-Purpose Physical Intelligence:** The long-term vision for humanoids is to perform a large fraction of physical work. Autonomous loco-manipulation is a foundational capability for this, requiring tight coordination of movement and interaction with objects in diverse environments.
- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap for Complex Tasks:** While sim-to-real has succeeded in legged locomotion and some tabletop manipulation, combining these for humanoids with visual input remains a significant challenge. The authors are motivated to develop a holistic approach that tackles the intricacies of this transfer for highly dexterous, mobile manipulation.
- **Providing a Practical "Recipe":** Beyond theoretical contributions, the project aims to identify and demonstrate the critical design choices, their interactions, and the necessary computational scale to make RGB-based humanoid loco-manipulation work reliably in practice. This involves dissecting where existing methods often fail and proposing solutions.
- **Enabling Onboard Perception:** The focus on RGB-based policies from onboard sensors is crucial for autonomy, reducing reliance on external infrastructure or privileged information, and making the system truly deployable in unstructured environments.

In essence, VIRAL seeks to demonstrate that large-scale visual sim-to-real can offer a practical, scalable, and robust path toward autonomous, goal-conditioned loco-manipulation for humanoid robots, without requiring extensive real-world data collection or fine-tuning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VIRAL employs a sophisticated teacher-student learning framework combined with extensive sim-to-real transfer techniques, designed to scale up visual simulation training and bridge the reality gap for humanoid loco-manipulation.

**Framework Overview:** The system follows a two-phase teacher-student design:

- **Phase 1: Privileged RL Teacher Training:** A teacher policy ($\pi_{teacher}$) is trained in simulation with full access to a "privileged" state (all relevant ground truth information, including object poses, velocities, etc.). This phase operates without the computational burden of visual rendering, utilizing a distributed setup on 16 GPUs.
- **Phase 2: Vision-based Student Distillation:** The learned teacher policy is then distilled into a student policy ($\pi_{student}$) that receives only onboard RGB images and proprioception (sensor data available on the real robot). This distillation is performed via large-scale visual simulation on up to 64 GPUs with tiled rendering.

**Key Elements of Teacher Training:**

- **Teacher Formulation:** The teacher is a goal-conditioned RL policy, outputting high-level commands (delta linear/angular velocities, delta joint targets for arm/finger motors) for a pretrained whole-body control (WBC) policy (specifically HOMIE). This WBC layer handles low-level motor control, abstracting away complexities and improving deployability.
- **Reward Design:** The loco-manipulation task is segmented into walking, placing, grasping, and turning. Stage-based rewards are carefully designed to guide exploration, including terms for object proximity, grasping success, lifting height, and turning accuracy.
- **Delta Action Space:** Instead of absolute joint targets, the policy outputs increments (deltas) to the WBC commands. This delta representation is found to significantly accelerate and stabilize RL training.
- **Reference State Initialization (RSI):** To mitigate the challenges of long-horizon tasks and sparse rewards, the RL environments are initialized from 200 teleoperated simulation demonstrations. This exposes the policy to diverse, rewarding intermediate states, reducing reliance on brittle reward tuning and improving transferability.
- **Distributed Training:** PPO with a custom TRL implementation is used for distributed training across GPUs.

**Key Elements of Student Training:**

- **DAgger & BC Mixture:** The student policy is distilled from the teacher using a hybrid approach of online DAgger and behavior cloning (BC). This combines the rapid initialization of BC (learning from teacher rollouts) with the error-correction robustness of DAgger (learning from student rollouts and querying the teacher for optimal actions), crucial for handling distribution shifts during deployment.
- **Network Backbone:** A state-of-the-art image encoder (DINOv3) extracts high-quality RGB features, which are then fused with proprioceptive data and fed into the policy head. History-aware architectures (e.g., LSTMs) are explored to incorporate temporal context.
- **Distributed Simulation Learning System:** A customized TRL implementation with Accelerate supports efficient scaling across multiple GPUs and compute nodes, enabling high-throughput visual sim-to-real learning, which is critical given the computational cost of visual rendering.

**Key Elements of Sim-to-Real Transfer:**

- **System Identification (SysID) for Dexterous Hand:** To address significant sim-to-real mismatch in the Unitree G1's high-gear-ratio dexterous hands, SysID is performed to align simulated joint trajectories (armature, stiffness, damping) with real-world measurements.
- **FOV Alignment and Randomization:** Camera intrinsics are matched to manufacturer specifications, and extrinsics (position/orientation) are calibrated by visually matching simulated and real images. Additionally, camera extrinsics randomization is applied during training to ensure robustness to hardware-induced viewpoint variations.
- **Visual and Simulation Randomization:** Extensive domain randomization is applied during student training. This includes varying image quality (brightness, contrast, noise, blur), global illumination (dome-light environments), material/color properties of scene assets (floors, tables, objects, robot components), and sensor delays. These perturbations prevent overfitting and enhance policy transferability.

This integrated approach, leveraging advanced RL techniques, large-scale distributed simulation, and meticulous sim-to-real calibration and randomization, forms the "technical recipe" for enabling robust, vision-based humanoid loco-manipulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The VIRAL framework demonstrates impressive capabilities for humanoid loco-manipulation, validated through both real-world deployment and extensive simulation experiments.

**Real-World Performance:**

- **Robustness:** The RGB-based policy achieved a high success rate, completing 54 out of 59 continuous loco-manipulation cycles on a Unitree G1 humanoid. This included walking between tables, placing objects, grasping new ones, and turning. This indicates strong reliability for extended deployment.
- **Near-Expert Performance:** VIRAL's performance approached that of an expert human teleoperator, achieving a cycle time of 20.2 seconds compared to the expert's 21.4 seconds, and significantly outperforming a non-expert teleoperator (73% success rate, slower execution). This highlights its potential to reduce human workload in assisted teleoperation settings.
- **Generalization:** The policy successfully generalized to diverse real-world environmental variations without any fine-tuning. These variations included different tray and object positions, robot start poses, table heights and types, tablecloth colors, lighting conditions, and object categories. This strong generalization is attributed to the extensive domain randomization used during training.

**Simulation Experiments and Ablations:**

- **Reference State Initialization (RSI):** Ablations showed RSI to be critical for teacher policy training. Without RSI, the teacher policy plateaued below 10% success, while with RSI, it reached nearly 95% success. RSI improves exploration by initializing episodes from diverse intermediate states, making it easier to learn long-horizon tasks.
- **Delta Action Space:** A delta action space, where the policy outputs increments to WBC commands, was found to be crucial for humanoid loco-manipulation. Unlike some legged locomotion works using absolute joint targets, the delta-action teacher reliably solved the task, while the absolute-action variant failed to achieve high success.
- **Vision Backbone:** Using state-of-the-art vision backbones (e.g., DINOv3) resulted in stronger visual representations, faster convergence, and higher task success rates for the student policy, emphasizing the importance of high-quality visual features.
- **DAgger-BC Mixture:** The hybrid DAgger-BC distillation approach with an α=0.5 ratio was optimal. Pure BC (α=1) led to faster loss reduction but produced brittle policies that failed in sim-to-real, whereas the mixture improved error-correction robustness and deployment success by exposing the student to its own rollout states.
- **History Architecture:** History-aware models (e.g., feed-forward history, LSTM) consistently outperformed a single-step baseline, with longer temporal windows providing additional gains, indicating the benefit of incorporating temporal context into the student policy.
- **Visual Randomization:** Comprehensive visual domain randomization (material, dome light, camera extrinsics) was essential for robust sim-to-real transfer. Turning off all randomization caused a significant performance drop (35.1% decrease), and removing any single component also degraded performance, demonstrating their complementary importance.
- **Scaling Compute:** Large-scale GPU compute was identified as critical for both teacher and student training.
 - For the teacher, increasing GPUs from 1 to 16 substantially accelerated learning, improved state-space coverage, and was necessary for achieving high asymptotic success rates (above 90%), as policies with insufficient compute failed to reach desired performance.
 - For the student, scaling GPUs from 1 to 64 significantly accelerated convergence, smoothed optimization dynamics, and yielded slightly higher final success rates, indicating that extensive experience collection improves robustness.
- **Object Generalization:** Training with multiple distinct objects yielded substantially better generalization on the grasping sub-task compared to training with a single object (cylinder-only baseline), showing the importance of object diversity in simulation.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that a combination of a well-structured teacher-student framework, carefully designed RL components (delta actions, RSI, rewards), extensive visual and physical randomization, and massive computational scaling in simulation is effective in enabling robust, generalizable, and near-expert-level humanoid loco-manipulation policies that transfer zero-shot to the real world.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The VIRAL project makes a significant contribution to the field of robotics by demonstrating that visual sim-to-real can effectively enable complex, long-horizon loco-manipulation skills on humanoid robots, a task previously largely confined to carefully engineered demos or heavy teleoperation.

**Key Significance:**

- **Practical Path to Autonomous Humanoid Skills:** VIRAL offers a concrete, validated "technical recipe" for developing and deploying autonomous humanoid loco-manipulation. Its zero-shot transfer success for continuous, long-horizon tasks with onboard RGB perception is a major step towards making humanoids truly productive in unstructured environments.
- **Emphasis on Compute Scale:** The paper rigorously demonstrates that "compute scale is critical." This finding provides crucial guidance for future research and development, indicating that investments in large-scale simulation infrastructure (many GPUs, distributed learning) are not merely for speed but are often *necessary* for reliable learning of complex humanoid behaviors.
- **Holistic System Design:** By integrating a teacher-student framework, delta action spaces, reference state initialization, careful reward design, state-of-the-art vision backbones, and extensive domain randomization, VIRAL showcases the importance of a comprehensive, full-stack approach rather than relying on a single algorithmic silver bullet.
- **Reduced Reliance on Real-World Data:** By enabling high-fidelity learning entirely in simulation, VIRAL significantly reduces the prohibitive cost and safety concerns associated with collecting large-scale real-world datasets for humanoid mobile manipulation, offering an alternative or complementary paradigm to "robotic foundation models" built on real-world data.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated Humanoid Development:** The VIRAL framework provides a robust foundation and a blueprint for researchers and developers working on humanoid robots, potentially accelerating the development of more complex and general-purpose skills.
- **Broader Adoption of Sim-to-Real:** The success of VIRAL in a highly challenging domain could encourage broader adoption and further innovation in visual sim-to-real for other complex robotic systems and tasks.
- **Human-Robot Collaboration:** By achieving near-expert performance and outperforming non-experts, VIRAL policies could enhance human-robot collaboration by providing reliable autonomous capabilities that reduce human cognitive load in teleoperated or supervised settings.

**Acknowledged Limitations and Future Outlook:**

The authors provide a remarkably candid discussion of four critical "coverage gaps" that current sim-to-real paradigms, including VIRAL, still face:

1. **Physics Coverage (Physical Diversity Gap):** While modern simulators can model complex dynamics, scaling the engineering effort to accurately ground these features for the open-ended diversity of real-world physics (e.g., material properties for all objects, fluid-structure interactions) is often prohibitively expensive.
2. **Task Coverage (Long-Tail of Task Generation):** Creating simulation environments for the vast diversity of real-world tasks, including functional affordances and "unknown unknowns," presents a massive content generation bottleneck.
3. **Reward and Policy Coverage (Reward Engineering Bottleneck):** Manually designing robust, RL-friendly reward functions for thousands of distinct tasks is intractable, highlighting a trade-off where human demonstrations might implicitly bypass this "specification problem."
4. **Hardware Coverage (Hardware-Simulation Gap):** Unmodeled friction, backlash, thermal throttling, and sensor noise in real hardware (especially dexterous manipulators) limit the transferability of policies that rely on idealized simulation actuation.

Despite these limitations, the authors emphasize that sim-to-real will retain a critical role. They argue that the field has found a "sweet spot" for locomotion (aggressive randomization of limited parameters, carefully designed rewards), but the equivalent for manipulation remains elusive due to greater complexity. The future, they suggest, lies in **redefining the role of simulation within a broader data ecosystem**, where sim-to-real **complements rather than replaces** real-world learning and integrates with emerging real-world imitation learning and foundation models. This forward-looking perspective positions VIRAL not as the ultimate solution, but as a crucial, high-impact step on a longer journey toward truly general-purpose robotic intelligence.
