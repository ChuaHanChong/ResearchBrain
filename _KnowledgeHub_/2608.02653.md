# Light-Loco-Parkour: Versatile Perceptive Whole-Body Locomotion via Multi-Skill Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> Light Origins developed a unified perceptive whole-body locomotion policy for humanoid robots, Light-Loco-Parkour, which enables autonomous parkour skills and agile traversal of complex terrains using only onboard depth sensing and a velocity command. The policy, deployed zero-shot on Lightbot 0, successfully performs dynamic maneuvers like vaulting and climbing, and navigates diverse environments including outdoor stairs and stepping stones.

## Problem

- Enabling humanoids to perform versatile whole-body locomotion beyond simple walking in complex, human-centric environments remains a challenge.
- Current methods face data scarcity for expressive, dynamically feasible whole-body parkour motions and struggle with generalization to varying terrain geometries.
- Developing autonomous skill transition for long-horizon tasks, where policies must decide which skill to invoke and when, is difficult without explicit references or hand-coded state machines.

## Method

- A **multi-skill distillation** framework was developed, training a single recurrent perceptive policy via ==PPO== for unified locomotion and whole-body parkour.
- An ==iterative self-augmentation== method generates dynamically feasible, terrain-paired whole-body skill references from sparse human motion seeds, addressing data scarcity and enhancing generalization.
- Reward-based transition learning, incorporating ==conditional adversarial motion priors== and ==DAgger== supervision, enables autonomous skill selection and chaining based on onboard perception for long-horizon tasks.

## Results

- The Light-Loco-Parkour policy demonstrated zero-shot transfer on Lightbot 0, performing dynamic whole-body parkour skills like reverse-vault and speed-vault, alongside agile locomotion on complex terrains such as outdoor stairs and plank bridges.
- The iterative self-augmentation method generated high-precision, dynamically feasible references for skills up to **0.83H** (robot standing height) and showed generalization to unseen trapezoidal obstacle shapes.
- The policy achieved autonomous transitions between locomotion and parkour skills, chaining behaviors in cluttered environments with **98%** success, and outperformed or matched state-of-the-art controllers on various benchmarks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Iterative self-augmentation combined with mimic learning can effectively address data scarcity and generalization for whole-body parkour by expanding sparse human motions into dynamically feasible, terrain-paired reference continua.
> - A recurrent neural network (GRU) operating on noisy onboard depth and proprioception is crucial for inferring unmeasured states and maintaining terrain memory, enabling robust perceptive control for complex maneuvers.
> - Autonomous transitions between diverse locomotion and whole-body skills can be learned from reward alone using a multi-expert distillation scheme and conditional adversarial motion priors, eliminating the need for explicit skill labels or hand-coded state machines.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics aims to enable robots to navigate and interact with complex, human-centric environments that are often inaccessible to wheeled or tracked platforms. A significant challenge in this area is endowing humanoids with versatile whole-body locomotion capabilities that extend beyond simple walking on flat or mildly uneven terrain. This work positions itself within this context by addressing limitations in existing control systems for whole-body interaction with cluttered environments.

Current approaches to humanoid control generally fall into two categories:
- **Mimic-based methods** leverage human motion data, often from motion capture or video, to train policies that track reference trajectories. While successful in reproducing dynamic, expressive whole-body skills (e.g., cartwheels), these methods are limited by their reliance on the reference distribution. Policies may struggle with out-of-distribution states, and data for precise object interaction is scarce, often leading to motions that do not adapt to varying scene geometry. Issues like floating contacts, scene penetration, or dynamic infeasibility can arise during motion retargeting.
- **Locomotion methods** utilize reinforcement learning (RL) to optimize policies for tracking commanded planar velocities without explicit reference trajectories. These methods have demonstrated robust gaits for stairs, slopes, and unstructured terrain, with successful transfer to physical robots. However, reward-only learning often results in leg-dominant behaviors on humanoids, as coordinating arms, torso, and knees for contact-rich skills typically requires extensive and complex reward shaping, which does not scale across diverse parkour maneuvers.

Existing whole-body locomotion systems, which aim to combine reference-driven motion with terrain-conditioned perceptive control, tend to inherit the limitations of reference-based learning. These include the need for offline motion generation, large motion-capture datasets that are difficult to scale, and challenges in maintaining dynamic feasibility and generalization across a multitude of skills and terrain variations.

This research specifically addresses three identified challenges in realizing capable, generalizable, and deployable whole-body locomotion:
1. **Data scarcity:** Generating expressive whole-body parkour references that are precisely paired with terrain contacts and dynamically feasible for the robot's embodiment is difficult.
2. **Generalization:** Policies trained on specific environmental configurations often overfit and cannot adapt contact timing, body posture, or force application when terrain features change.
3. **Autonomous skill transition:** Reference-learned skills are typically short-horizon. A long-horizon task requires the policy to autonomously determine which skill to invoke, when, and how to transition back to general locomotion based solely on its observations.

The Light-Loco-Parkour system contrasts with prior work such as PHP by learning perceptive locomotion from reward alone, without an explicit reference in the loop, allowing for more natural and robust motion. Unlike PHP, it selects and executes whole-body skills based on onboard depth and a velocity command, rather than a one-hot command that can draw the robot onto obstacles. It also operates as a single deployable policy, distinguishing it from methods like Zhang et al. that require additional compute units for runtime motion generation. The method also introduces a teacher-student architecture to stabilize convergence during skill generalization, building upon approaches like HIL that can struggle with slow and unstable learning without explicit reference input.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an end-to-end perceptive whole-body locomotion system for humanoid robots that enables complex and agile movement through cluttered and demanding terrains. This system aims to perform parkour skills, such as climbing, vaulting, and precise balancing, by coordinating the entire robot body.

The motivation for this work stems from the observed gap between human capabilities in navigating complex environments and the limitations of current humanoid robot control systems. Human environments often necessitate the coordinated use of a robot's entire body – hands for vaulting, knees for bracing, and torso for leaning – in continuous concert with perceived terrain. Enabling humanoids to sense their surroundings and recruit their whole body for traversal would expand their applicability significantly. Potential applications range from search-and-rescue operations over rubble and inspection of cluttered industrial sites to general operation in human-centric spaces.

To achieve this overarching goal, the research focuses on three specific objectives, articulated as contributions:

1. **Develop a whole-body perceptive-control pipeline:** The aim is to extend an existing RL-trained, velocity-tracking locomotion policy to incorporate parkour skills derived from object-interacting motions. This unified policy should be capable of tracking velocity in open terrain, executing whole-body obstacle traversal, and resuming locomotion subsequently. This entire process is intended to be driven solely by onboard depth sensing and a velocity command, without requiring explicit reference inputs, skill labels, hand-coded gates, or runtime motion graphs.
2. **Acquire terrain-conditioned skills from sparse seeds:** The objective is to overcome data scarcity in whole-body parkour by developing a method that can expand a single seed motion into a continuum of dynamically feasible, terrain-paired references. This process, which covers various obstacle geometries, aims to eliminate the need for large motion corpora and enhance skill generalization across different obstacle configurations.
3. **Learn autonomous skill transitions from reward:** The final objective is to enable the policy to autonomously decide when and which whole-body skill to invoke. This transition mechanism should emerge purely from task reward, based on onboard perception and velocity commands, thereby obviating the need for one-hot skill labels, hand-coded state machines, or runtime motion generators.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Light-Loco-Parkour (LightLP) system is an end-to-end perceptive whole-body locomotion system for humanoid robots. It is trained using reinforcement learning, specifically the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, within the IsaacLab simulation environment. The deployed policy operates on a custom-built 90 cm humanoid robot, Lightbot 0, equipped with onboard depth camera, IMU, and joint encoders, and runs on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano.

The methodology is structured around four main modules:

1. **Perceptive Locomotion Learning:**
 - **Policy Architecture:** A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) architecture serves as the core feature extractor for map and proprioceptive inputs. A teacher-student scheme is employed for training.
 - **Teacher Policy:** Trained with privileged information, including a clean height scan and proprioception. It uses an MLP encoder that processes the last five observation frames to provide temporal context. An asymmetric actor-critic design provides the critic with additional oracle information (e.g., under-foot height scans) for more accurate value estimates, while the actor's observations are limited to quantities inferable by the student to facilitate distillation.
 - **Student Policy:** Designed for onboard deployment, receiving noisy depth images from a single camera and proprioception. It employs an MLP for depth encoding, fuses it with proprioception, and feeds the result into a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) for memory retention before a final MLP outputs the action. The GRU helps infer unmeasured states (e.g., current velocity) and retain terrain information that has moved out of the camera's field of view.
 - **Reward Design:** The reward function encourages velocity command tracking (linear and angular), upright orientation, and a velocity-slack bonus to allow speed modulation during obstacle negotiation. Penalties include undesired contacts, joint limits, heading errors, opposite direction movement, and action rates. Crucially, an *illegal-footstep penalty* is introduced, using foot-mounted height scanners to discourage stepping into holes or off edges, essential for sparse footholds. A *foot-acceleration penalty* with an exponential-decay filter encourages soft landings by accumulating excess foot acceleration over time.
 - **Termination Conditions:** Episodes terminate due to time-out, out-of-bounds movement, excessive joint velocity (treated as time-outs), torso contact, excessive base acceleration, and fall-over (stochastic termination to allow recovery attempts).
 - **Terrain and Curriculum:** Training occurs on a procedurally generated terrain grid with 10 difficulty levels and 32 terrain types (e.g., slopes, stepping stones, plank bridges, stairs). A terrain-level curriculum promotes robots to harder levels upon successful traversal and command tracking, ensuring continuous skill development.
 - **Domain Randomization:** Extensive domain randomization is applied to ground contact properties, inertial parameters, actuation characteristics, depth camera pose, and base pushes. Initial states (base pose, velocity, joint positions) are also randomized to improve sim-to-real transfer and robustness to disturbances.

2. **Whole-Body Skill Acquisition:**
 - **Data Augmentation for References:** To address data scarcity, the method starts with sparse human motion seeds from Internet videos.
 - **Seed Preprocessing:** SMPL body models are extracted using GVHMR and retargeted with GMR. A virtual obstacle is manually aligned with implied contact points, forming a coarse motion-and-terrain seed pair.
 - **Object-Interaction Mimic:** This stage refines the seed motions. Building on BeyondMimic, it extends imitation learning to contact-rich skills by tracking global root positions and adding privileged observations (obstacle distance, size). A global-frame position tracking term for contact points enforces physical interaction with the obstacle. Reference State Initialization (RSI) is used to explore high-momentum states during learning.
 - **Iterative Self-Augmentation:** Once a policy reliably mimics the seed motion on an obstacle, its rollout trajectory in the simulator is adopted as the next-iteration reference. This ensures dynamic and kinematic feasibility. The obstacle and motion are iteratively lifted by 5-10 cm to generate a continuum of harder skill variations, forming a diverse dataset of terrain-paired references.
 - **Skill Learning and Generalization:**
 - **Height-Scan Distillation:** The expert policy for the most demanding iteration of each skill (tallest obstacle, furthest reach) is distilled onto the student's perceptive locomotion observation space (proprioception, local height scan, velocity command). This uses DAgger for expert action supervision combined with a PPO loss on a composite task reward, allowing the policy to recover from imperfect tracking.
 - **Skill Generalization:** The distilled policy is then trained across the *full range* of augmented obstacles without expert imitation. The mimic reward now dynamically tracks the reference paired with the currently sampled obstacle. A task reward term (Equation 9) guides the robot towards a target position beyond the obstacle, and a sharper terminal reward (Equation 10) further encourages crossing. The robot is initialized at the start of the motion, but with joint angles drawn from the perceptive locomotion policy to ease later transitions.

3. **Transition Learning:**
 - **Multi-Expert Distillation:** A combined environment is created, partitioned into groups: a locomotion group and dedicated skill groups for each whole-body maneuver. A single student policy is supervised by the respective expert (locomotion or skill) in each group using DAgger. The student is not given an explicit skill label, forcing it to infer the required behavior from exteroception.
 - **Transition Fine-Tuning:** The distilled policy is fine-tuned with RL in a "transition group" environment featuring cluttered scenes with interleaved skill obstacles and open locomotion terrain. No per-frame reference motion is provided here. The reward function for this group (Equation 11) combines velocity tracking, a sparse position reward for completing crossings, a dense proximity reward, and a *conditional adversarial motion prior (AMP) reward*. The AMP dynamically switches between the locomotion prior and the relevant skill prior when the robot passes a skill-specific "trigger position," actively driving the handoff. To facilitate learning from sparse rewards, episodes are initialized both in front of and behind obstacles.

4. **From Height Scan to Onboard Depth:**
 - A final distillation pass converts the height-scan-conditioned policy into a recurrent depth-conditioned policy suitable for the robot's onboard camera.
 - **Auxiliary Reconstruction Loss:** A small decoder maps the GRU's hidden state back to the privileged height scan observed by the teacher. This auxiliary loss encourages the recurrent state to explicitly encode a height-scan-like representation from partial, self-occluded depth frames. This decoder is only used during training.
 - **Realistic Depth Synthesis:** To bridge the sim-to-real gap, simulated depth images are rendered through a noise and latency model calibrated to the Intel RealSense D435 camera. This includes range-dependent Gaussian noise, global depth-scale jitter, persistent block dropout, and measured processing latency, teaching the policy to operate with noisy and stale depth data.
 - A short fine-tune under this noise-and-latency model produces the final deployable policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded a single deployable policy, Light-Loco-Parkour, which was demonstrated on the Lightbot 0 humanoid robot in both simulation and real-world scenarios.

**Real-World Experiments (Lightbot 0, Zero-Shot Deployment):**
The policy demonstrated autonomous perceptive whole-body locomotion across a range of challenging indoor and outdoor environments using only onboard sensing (depth camera, IMU, joint encoders) and a velocity command. No odometry, motion capture, or external mapping was required.
- **Whole-Body Skills:**
 - **Reverse-vault:** Performed a dynamic maneuver involving a jump, 360° aerial rotation, and pelvis contact for redirection, followed by impact absorption through a second rotation for stable landing.
 - **Speed-vault:** Executed a high-speed maneuver, planting one hand on an obstacle to redirect momentum into a ballistic aerial phase, with coordinated arm support and leg swing.
 - **Climb-and-step:** Utilized bilateral arm support to lift the body over obstacles beyond leg-only reach, transitioning through a kneeling posture and recovering to a standing pose. This skill successfully transferred to an unseen trapezoidal obstacle, indicating generalization to varied obstacle shapes.
- **Perceptive Locomotion:**
 - **Plank bridge:** Successfully traversed narrow plank bridges, requiring precise foot placement and dynamic balance control.
 - **Stepping stones:** Navigated discrete stepping stones with gaps, demonstrating perception-driven foothold selection and memory-based locomotion.
 - **High platform:** Mounted a 30 cm high platform, presenting a significant balance challenge due to asymmetric posture.
 - **Outdoor curbed stairs:** Ascended and descended real outdoor curbed stairs and traversed grass slopes in a zero-shot setting, handling depth noise and terrain uncertainty.
- **Autonomous Transitions:** The policy demonstrated the ability to chain locomotion and whole-body skills into continuous courses, autonomously switching between behaviors (e.g., skill-to-locomotion, locomotion-to-skill) without external cues.

**Simulation and Benchmark:**
- **Reference Generation (Table IV):** The iterative self-augmentation method produced high-precision, dynamically feasible, object-paired references for climb-and-step, speed-vault, and reverse-vault, without issues of penetration or floating contacts that affected other methods like GMR and OmniRetarget. The generated references allowed for training skills up to 0.83H (robot standing height) for climb-and-step, extending beyond the typical ranges reported by prior work.
- **Ablation Study (Table V):**
 - The **final fine-tune** was shown to be critical for robust performance, significantly improving success rates (e.g., from 34.6% to 99.9% on stepping stones) by optimizing task success under the student's deployable observations.
 - The **recurrent GRU architecture** was found to be essential. Removing it resulted in severe performance degradation (e.g., 0% on stepping stones), highlighting its role in inferring missing state information and maintaining a memory of terrain from partial depth observations.
 - **Expert distillation** was crucial for learning high-dynamic whole-body skills; without it, the teacher policy failed to converge from sparse reward alone.
 - **Reference augmentation** was necessary for skill generalization. Training without it resulted in reduced success, even for the teacher, indicating that a single reference overfits.
 - Generalization to unseen obstacle shapes was confirmed by high success rates (93.4% for reverse-vault, 95.3% for speed-vault) on a trapezoidal obstacle never encountered during training (Table VI).
- **Benchmark (Table V):** LightLP achieved comparable or superior success rates across various terrains compared to state-of-the-art humanoid controllers (PHP, MGMT, BeamDojo, CReF), demonstrating broader terrain coverage and the ability to handle higher normalized obstacle heights.
- **Transitions (Table VII, Fig. 11):**
 - The **transition group training** was essential for the autonomous chaining of skills and locomotion, increasing success from 0-33% to 98%.
 - The **adversarial motion prior (AMP)** was necessary for generating natural, physically plausible motions during transitions.
 - The policy demonstrated **command adherence** by successfully retreating from an obstacle when commanded, confirming that its behavior is governed by both velocity commands and depth observations rather than solely by obstacle presence.
 - t-SNE visualization of RNN hidden states (Fig. 11) showed distinct clusters for locomotion and parkour skills with the transition group, indicating that the policy learns to encode and switch between behavioral modes based on perceived terrain and commands.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Light-Loco-Parkour system represents a notable advancement in humanoid whole-body locomotion by integrating diverse capabilities into a single, deployable policy.

- **Unified Control Paradigm:** This work demonstrates the unification of perceptive locomotion and complex whole-body parkour skills within one learned controller. This moves beyond isolated, terrain-specific behaviors towards a more general and versatile humanoid control system, which could simplify development and deployment for varied tasks.
- **Enhanced Autonomy:** The policy's ability to autonomously select and execute appropriate behaviors—be it walking, balancing, climbing, or vaulting—based solely on onboard depth sensing and a velocity command, without explicit skill labels or hand-coded state machines, significantly enhances the robot's autonomy and adaptability in unstructured environments.
- **Addressing Data and Generalization Challenges:** The iterative self-augmentation method for generating dynamically feasible, terrain-paired whole-body references from sparse human motion seeds provides a practical solution to the data scarcity problem in contact-rich whole-body control. This approach enables the training of skills that generalize effectively across different obstacle geometries and scales, as demonstrated by the success on unseen obstacle shapes and varied terrain heights.
- **Robustness and Real-World Applicability:** The comprehensive domain randomization, combined with a teacher-student distillation framework and a final fine-tuning stage under realistic depth noise and latency, contributes to the policy's robustness and its zero-shot transferability from simulation to real hardware. This enhances the practical utility of humanoid robots in real-world, dynamic, and unpredictable outdoor settings.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The deployment of the entire system as a single policy on a low-power onboard compute unit (NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano) highlights its efficiency. This is crucial for practical applications where computational resources are constrained, differentiating it from approaches requiring more powerful, dedicated external processing units.
- **Expanded Operational Envelope:** The demonstrated capabilities, including high-speed vaulting, precise climbing, and stable traversal of narrow or sparse footholds, extend the operational envelope of humanoid robots into more challenging environments. This could enable humanoids to perform complex tasks in search-and-rescue, industrial inspection, logistics, and assistance in human habitats.

The limitations, such as the human-in-the-loop seed collection, degraded behavior with overlapping obstacles, and restricted perceptual coverage from a fixed camera, suggest avenues for future research. Addressing these could lead to even more scalable skill acquisition, richer and more robust transition behaviors, and the integration of active perception or explicit scene memory, further broadening the impact of whole-body humanoid control.
