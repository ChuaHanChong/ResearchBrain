# iTACO: Interactable Digital Twins of Articulated Objects from Casually Captured RGBD Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Simon Fraser University and Shanghai Jiao Tong University developed iTACO, a method for generating interactable digital twins of articulated objects directly from casually captured RGBD videos. The framework robustly estimates joint parameters and part-level geometry, outperforming existing baselines by achieving lower articulation and geometry errors on a new, larger synthetic dataset.

## Problem

- Existing methods for creating digital twins of articulated objects often require highly constrained data capture, such as multi-view setups or robotic interaction, hindering scalability.
- Many dynamic reconstruction techniques produce dynamic meshes or deformation fields, which lack the explicit kinematic structure (joints, links) needed for physics-based simulation or robot control.
- Prior approaches struggle with generalization to new object categories, often relying on external mesh libraries or extensive fine-tuning on limited datasets.

## Method

- iTACO employs a coarse-to-fine framework, starting with rough estimates of moving parts, camera poses, and initial joint parameters from casually captured RGBD videos.
- It leverages pre-trained foundation models like MonST3R for dynamic map estimation and LoFTR for robust feature matching to disentangle camera and object motion.
- A gradient-based optimization refines joint parameters and movable part segmentation, using automatic segment tracking (via SAM2) to improve efficiency and manage newly observed object interiors.

## Results

- iTACO achieved substantially lower joint axis errors (0.32 rad vs. 0.82-1.16 rad for baselines) and type classification errors (15.90% vs. 40.90-88.88%) for articulated objects on a new synthetic dataset 20 times larger than previous benchmarks.
- The method demonstrated superior geometry estimation with significantly lower Chamfer Distance errors for the whole object (0.01 vs. 0.11-3.39) and movable parts (0.13 vs. 0.59-0.60) compared to state-of-the-art baselines.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both the coarse prediction and refinement stages, along with segment-based optimization, are critical for iTACO's performance and robustness to casual capture conditions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A coarse prediction stage, effectively decoupling camera and object motion, is crucial for providing a stable initialization for subsequent gradient-based refinement in complex, casually captured dynamic scenes.
> - Integrating general-purpose pre-trained vision models for scene understanding and segmentation within a structured pipeline allows for robust articulated object analysis without reliance on object-specific libraries or extensive fine-tuning.
> - Optimizing at the segment level for movable parts, rather than pixel-wise, significantly enhances computational efficiency and improves the accuracy of joint parameter and geometry estimation during refinement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The creation of digital twins for articulated objects is a critical task with widespread applications in computer animation, embodied AI, and robotics. However, existing methods often fall short in practical, scalable, and generalizable acquisition of such digital replicas. iTACO directly addresses these limitations by proposing a novel approach that leverages "casually captured RGBD videos" — a significantly less constrained and more accessible data source than previously utilized.

The paper categorizes prior work into three main families and meticulously highlights their shortcomings:

1. **Dynamic Reconstruction of Shape:** Methods like A-SDF or REACTO reconstruct the changing shape of an articulated object over time, resulting in a sequence of meshes. While impressive for visual effects, these outputs lack an explicit kinematic structure (links and joints) necessary for interactive simulation or robot manipulation. iTACO, in contrast, explicitly infers joint parameters and part-level geometry, making the digital twin *interactable*.
2. **Joint Parameter and Part-Level Geometry from Different Object States:** Approaches such as PARIS, Ditto, or Neural Implicit Representation infer articulation from observations of an object in different configurations. A major drawback here is the implicit assumption of precise alignment between these different states, which is either labor-intensive (manual alignment) or error-prone (algorithmic alignment) in practice. iTACO circumvents this by working with a continuous video stream, where relative motion naturally provides the necessary information. Some works also use robots for interaction, but this is costly and not scalable.
3. **Leveraging Large Language and Multimodal Models (LLMs/VLMs):** Recent advancements, exemplified by Articulate-Anything (AA) and URDFormer, attempt to generate articulated object representations from various inputs, including images or videos, often relying on external mesh libraries or fine-tuning foundation models. While promising, these methods currently suffer from scalability issues (retrieving specific objects from a library) and generalization challenges (out-of-distribution problems after fine-tuning on limited data). iTACO differentiates itself by not relying on external libraries or extensive fine-tuning, aiming for inherent generalization.

Furthermore, the paper situates itself within the broader context of **dynamic scene reconstruction** and **motion analysis**. While many dynamic scene reconstruction methods (e.g., DynamicFusion, D-NeRF, 4D Gaussian Splatting) excel at novel view synthesis and shape reconstruction, they typically yield deformation fields rather than explicit rigid transformations required for kinematic models. iTACO draws inspiration from works like MonST3R and CasualSAM for handling dynamic scenes and camera motion but focuses on extracting interpretable kinematic parameters. The paper also discusses **automatic skeletal rigging** in computer animation, noting that their focus on rigid articulated objects (e.g., drawers, microwaves with prismatic and revolute joints) differs from the non-rigid links and ball joints typically modeled for human/animal animation.

By focusing on casually captured RGBD videos, iTACO tackles a more challenging yet far more practical input setting. This move democratizes the acquisition process, making it accessible to anyone with a smartphone, thus significantly broadening the scope and scalability of creating digital twins for robotics and embodied AI research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of iTACO is to create **interactable digital twins of articulated objects from casually captured RGBD videos**. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and aims to address specific desiderata that current methods fail to fully satisfy:

1. **Minimal Input Constraints:** Current methods often require carefully captured data, such as multi-view observations from specific angles or robotic interaction. This prevents practical, scalable, and generalizable acquisition of articulated objects. iTACO aims to drastically reduce these constraints, making the data acquisition process as simple as recording a video with a hand-held smartphone. This ease of data capture is crucial for scaling up the creation of digital twins for diverse real-world objects.
2. **Explicit Representation for Simulation:** Many existing dynamic reconstruction techniques output dynamic meshes or deformation fields, which are not directly usable for physics-based simulation or robot control. The key objective here is to infer explicit **joint parameters** (type, axis, position, state) and **part-level geometry** that form a kinematic structure. This explicit representation allows for direct integration into simulators for training embodied AI agents or robotics research, enabling convenient and efficient virtual experimentation before real-world deployment.
3. **Generalization Across Diverse Object Categories:** The reliance on external libraries or fine-tuning on limited datasets often restricts the generalization capabilities of existing models to new, unseen object categories or out-of-distribution instances. iTACO is motivated to achieve generalization by neither relying on an external object mesh library nor requiring additional data for fine-tuning. Instead, it aims to leverage pre-trained general-purpose vision models within its framework.
4. **Tackling Challenges of Casual Capture:** The motivation also stems from the unique challenges posed by casually captured videos. These include simultaneous object and camera motion (a hand-held camera moving while a human interacts with the object) and significant occlusions caused by the human operator. Decoupling camera motion from object part motion and accurately segmenting movable parts under these conditions are central problems that iTACO is designed to overcome.

In summary, iTACO seeks to bridge the gap between easy, scalable data acquisition and the creation of functionally rich, interactable digital twins that are readily usable for advanced AI and robotics applications, moving beyond the limitations of highly constrained data capture and implicit representations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

iTACO employs a **coarse-to-fine framework** to infer joint parameters and segment movable parts of an articulated object from a dynamic RGBD video. The pipeline is designed to be robust to the challenges of casually captured videos, including simultaneous camera and object motion and occlusions.

The methodology consists of two main stages:

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation and Assumptions

- **Input:** An RGBD video $V = \{I_t\}_{t=0}^T$ (frames with RGB and depth) and an initial object surface point cloud $P_O$ (e.g., obtained from a static scan of the object before interaction).
- **Assumptions:** The video is casually captured with a hand-held device. The first video frame is aligned to the object surface coordinate system. Crucially, it's assumed that only one part of the object is moving at a time (e.g., opening a single door or drawer). For multi-joint objects, multiple interaction videos can be processed sequentially.
- **Output:** Joint parameters $J \in \mathbb{R}^7$ (type, axis position, axis orientation) and a representation of the movable parts of the object. Also, camera poses $p_{cam_t} \in SE(3)$ and joint states $s_t \in \mathbb{R}$ for each frame.

#### 3.2. Coarse Prediction Stage

This stage provides initial, rough estimates of the moving map, camera poses, and joint parameters, effectively decoupling the complex problem.

1. **Moving Map Estimation:** The pipeline first uses **MonST3R** (a powerful foundation model for dynamic scene reconstruction, an extension of DUSt3R) to produce a rough "moving map" $M_t$ for each sampled video frame. This map indicates which pixels are undergoing motion.
2. **Feature Matching and Camera Pose Estimation:**
 - **LoFTR** (Detector-Free Local Feature Matching with Transformers) is used to compute pixel-level feature matches between pairs of video frames.
 - Feature matches within the *static regions* (identified by $M_t$) are used to compute relative camera poses $p_{cam_{t,t-1}}$ between consecutive frames. These relative poses are then used to align all observations to a common coordinate system (typically the first camera's coordinate system).
3. **Initial Joint Parameter Estimation:**
 - Feature matches within the *dynamic regions* are used to estimate the rigid transformation of the movable parts.
 - From these rigid transformations, initial joint parameters (axis, position) are estimated for both revolute and prismatic joint types.
 - **RANSAC** is applied for robustness to outliers in feature matching.
 - Joint parameters are estimated from multiple video frame pairs and then averaged to produce a coarse prediction for the joint type and its parameters. The joint type (revolute or prismatic) is determined by which type receives the most votes across frame pairs.

#### 3.3. Refinement Stage

The coarse predictions are then refined using a gradient-based optimization framework. The core idea is to ensure that when transformed back to the initial state using the estimated camera poses and joint parameters, the observed object points align with the initial object surface point cloud.

1. **Optimization Objective (Chamfer Distance):**
 - The objective function $L$ is a sum of two single-directional Chamfer distances: $L = L_{static} + L_{dynamic}$.
 - $L_{static}$: Measures the alignment of static parts of the observed point cloud $P_t$ (transformed by camera pose $p_{cam_t}$) to the initial object surface $P_O$.
 - $L_{dynamic}$: Measures the alignment of moving parts of $P_t$ (transformed by camera pose $p_{cam_t}$ and joint transformation $k_t$) to $P_O$.
 - The joint transformation $k_t$ is computed from the joint parameters $J$ and the current joint state $s_t$.
 - This loss function is differentiable, allowing gradient-based optimization (e.g., Adam optimizer) to refine $J$, $p_{cam_t}$, and the moving map $M_t$.

2. **Addressing Inefficiency and "Newly Observed Parts":**
 - **Automatic Part Segmentation:** To avoid optimizing millions of pixel-level probabilities for $M_t$, the method employs an automatic part segmentation module. It segments the first video frame into several object parts (similar to Segment-Anything) and then tracks these regions throughout the video using **SAM2**. Instead of pixel-wise probabilities, a vector $v$ representing the probability of each tracked segment being a moving part is optimized, significantly reducing parameter space and improving efficiency.
 - **Handling Newly Observed Parts:** This segmentation module also helps identify parts not present in the initial scan but revealed during interaction (e.g., interior of a drawer). These "newly observed parts" are filtered out during Chamfer distance computation to prevent spurious correspondences and errors, as they would not align with $P_O$.

3. **Final Movable Part Identification and Mesh Reconstruction:**
 - After refinement, pixels in the moving map $M_0$ of the first frame with probabilities above a threshold (e.g., 0.7) are unprojected to 3D.
 - Points on the initial object surface $P_O$ that are close to these unprojected moving pixels are identified as the "movable part" of the object.
 - Finally, **NKSR** is used to reconstruct high-quality meshes for both the static and movable parts of the object.

The coarse-to-fine design, coupled with advanced pre-trained models for segmentation and feature matching, allows iTACO to robustly handle the complexities of casually captured dynamic scenes while delivering explicit, interactable digital twins.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

iTACO demonstrates superior performance compared to existing state-of-the-art methods in building interactable digital twins from casually captured RGBD videos, validated through extensive experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets.

#### 4.1. Dataset Contribution

- The authors constructed a new synthetic dataset, **20x larger than prior work**, comprising 784 videos of 284 synthetic objects across 11 categories from the PartNet-Mobility dataset. This substantial dataset (S-Dataset for a smaller subset, L-Dataset for the full set) enables a more robust evaluation under this new challenging setting.
- Real-world RGBD videos were also collected using an iPhone 12 Pro (LiDAR camera), with Polycam for surface reconstruction and Record3D for video capture, further demonstrating the practical applicability of iTACO.

#### 4.2. Articulation Parameter Estimation

- **Quantitative Results (Table 1, S-Dataset; Table C.2, L-Dataset):** iTACO consistently outperforms baselines (Articulate Anything and Robot-See-Robot-Do) across most metrics for both revolute and prismatic joints.
 - For **revolute joints**, iTACO achieved significantly lower joint axis error (e.g., 0.32 rad vs. AA's 0.82 rad and RSRD's 1.16 rad on S-Dataset), lower joint position error (0.13m vs. AA's 0.81m and RSRD's 1.18m), lower type classification error (15.90% vs. AA's 40.90% and RSRD's 88.88%), and lower joint state error (0.25 rad vs. RSRD's 1.05 rad).
 - For **prismatic joints**, iTACO also showed superior performance in axis error (0.24 rad vs. AA's 0.92 rad and RSRD's 1.27 rad) and joint state error (0.08m vs. RSRD's 0.63m). Its joint type error was remarkably low (10.34%).
- **Qualitative Results (Figure 4, 5):** iTACO produces visually accurate articulation parameters, correctly identifying joint types, axes, and ranges of motion for diverse objects (Stapler, Microwave, USB, Cabinet, Book).
- **Baseline Weaknesses:**
 - **Articulate Anything (AA):** Frequently struggled with retrieving the correct object meshes from its library, leading to high failure rates and incorrect link placement or affordance detection (e.g., retrieving a lamp for a stapler, misplacing a USB stick lid).
 - **Robot-See-Robot-Do (RSRD):** Showed limitations with textureless objects, likely due to its reliance on 3D Gaussian splatting, and often failed to accurately crop and cluster Gaussians into parts. Crucially, RSRD struggled with camera motion, leading to significant deviations in movable part segmentation.

#### 4.3. Geometry Estimation Results

- **Quantitative Results (Table 2, S-Dataset; Table C.3, L-Dataset):** iTACO achieved the lowest Chamfer Distance (CD) errors for the whole object (CD-w), movable part (CD-m), and static part (CD-s).
 - CD-w: 0.01 for iTACO vs. AA's 0.11 and RSRD's 3.39 (S-Dataset).
 - CD-m: 0.13 for iTACO vs. AA's 0.59 and RSRD's 0.60 (S-Dataset).
 - This indicates that iTACO reconstructs the geometry more faithfully, especially for the moving parts, which are critical for interaction.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies confirmed the importance of each component of the iTACO framework:

- **iTACO w/o Refine:** Removing the refinement module drastically increased errors, particularly in joint type prediction (e.g., 68.18% type error for revolute joints on S-Dataset, up from 15.90%). This highlights the coarse prediction's inability to precisely distinguish joint types when frame changes are subtle. Geometry accuracy also significantly degraded.
- **iTACO w/o Coarse:** Randomly initializing refinement parameters without coarse prediction led to poor performance, indicating that a good initial estimate is crucial for the gradient-based refinement to converge effectively.
- **iTACO w/o Segment:** Replacing the automatic part segmentation with pixel-wise optimization degraded joint prediction and segmentation accuracy, demonstrating the efficiency and effectiveness of segment-based optimization. This also highlighted issues with incorrect point correspondences when segments are not properly formed.

#### 4.5. Real-World Qualitative Evaluation

- iTACO successfully processed real-world RGBD videos, producing accurate digital twins for objects like a Cabinet and a Book.
- Baselines continued to struggle: AA failed on objects not in its mesh library (e.g., Book) or predicted incorrect articulation (e.g., a door rotating into a cabinet), while RSRD failed to produce valid results due to clustering and tracking issues with 3D Gaussians on real, often textureless, objects.

#### 4.6. Additional Findings (Supplementary Material)

- **Variance of Joint Estimation (Table C.4):** Experiments with multiple seeds showed that iTACO is stable, and the refinement module significantly reduces the variance of predictions.
- **Moving Map Segmentation (Table C.5):** iTACO's moving map mIOU was comparable to MonST3R, albeit slightly lower, mainly because iTACO filters out newly observed interior parts which MonST3R might include as moving.
- **Camera Pose Estimation (Table C.6):** iTACO's camera pose estimation, particularly rotation, was more accurate than MonST3R and CUT3R, demonstrating its robust handling of camera motion. Using MonST3R's camera poses significantly degraded iTACO's performance (Table C.8), underscoring the importance of iTACO's integrated camera pose refinement.
- **Automatic Video Segmentation Strategy (Figure C.3, C.5):** The strategy of performing part segmentation on reversed videos was found to be more effective for objects that reveal internal structure upon opening. Failure cases highlighted that inaccurate or inconsistent automatic part segmentation remains a primary limitation, affecting newly observed parts, segmentation accuracy, and consistency.

In essence, iTACO establishes a new benchmark for creating interactable digital twins from casual RGBD video, demonstrating robustness and accuracy in a practical setting where prior methods fall short.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "iTACO" carries significant implications across several fields, particularly computer vision, robotics, and embodied AI. Its core contribution lies in tackling the practical challenge of acquiring interactable digital twins of articulated objects from easily obtainable, casually captured RGBD videos.

**Key Significance:**

1. **Democratization of Digital Twin Creation:** By enabling the use of hand-held cameras (like smartphone LiDAR sensors), iTACO significantly lowers the barrier to entry for creating accurate, interactable 3D models of articulated objects. This shift from specialized, constrained data capture (multi-view setups, robotic interaction) to ubiquitous mobile devices makes the technology accessible to a broader user base and facilitates data collection at an unprecedented scale.
2. **Advancement in Robotics and Embodied AI:** Interactable digital twins are fundamental for training and evaluating intelligent agents in simulation before real-world deployment. iTACO provides a scalable pipeline to populate these simulations with diverse, realistic articulated objects. This will accelerate research in robot manipulation, task planning, and human-robot interaction by providing a rich, interactive virtual environment. Robots can learn to open doors, drawers, or operate appliances in simulation with highly accurate kinematic models derived from real objects.
3. **Robustness to Real-World Challenges:** The framework's ability to handle simultaneous camera and object motion, as well as occlusions, marks a significant step towards practical computer vision systems that operate in uncontrolled environments. This robustness is critical for real-world applications where perfect data capture conditions are rarely met.
4. **Novel Dataset Contribution:** The introduction of a new, large-scale synthetic dataset (20x larger than previous benchmarks) is a valuable contribution to the research community. This dataset will serve as a crucial benchmark for future research in articulated object reconstruction and motion analysis under challenging casual capture conditions.
5. **Foundation for Future Work:** iTACO's coarse-to-fine approach, combining modern foundation models (MonST3R, LoFTR, SAM2) with gradient-based optimization, offers a powerful paradigm. Its success highlights the potential of integrating general-purpose pre-trained models for specific, complex 3D reconstruction tasks.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Accelerated Robot Learning:** With easier access to digital twins, researchers can quickly generate vast amounts of simulated data for training reinforcement learning agents, drastically reducing the need for expensive and time-consuming real-world robot experiments.
- **Enhanced Virtual and Augmented Reality:** Interactable digital twins can enrich AR/VR experiences, allowing users to interact with virtual replicas of real-world objects in highly realistic and functional ways.
- **Industrial Applications:** For design, manufacturing, and maintenance, accurate digital twins of articulated machinery or products can aid in prototyping, simulation of assembly/disassembly, and remote diagnostics.
- **Education and Training:** Interactive 3D models can be powerful tools for teaching mechanics, engineering, or even household repairs, allowing hands-on virtual exploration of articulated mechanisms.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**

Despite its significant advancements, the paper acknowledges several limitations and outlines avenues for future work:

- **Dependence on Automatic Part Segmentation:** The refinement module's performance is heavily influenced by the accuracy and consistency of the automatic part segmentation. Failure modes include incorrect identification of newly observed parts, inaccurate segment boundaries, or inconsistent segmentation across frames. Future work could integrate 3D geometry-based segmentation (e.g., SAMPart3D, PartField) to refine or correct image-based segmentation errors.
- **Interior Reconstruction:** The current pipeline does not reconstruct the interior parts of the object that are initially hidden but become visible during interaction. This is an important direction for creating truly complete digital twins.
- **Generalization to Multi-Joint Interactions:** The assumption of manipulating one joint at a time simplifies the problem. Extending iTACO to simultaneously estimate multiple interacting joints would broaden its applicability to more complex objects.
- **Robustness of Dynamic Scene Understanding:** Improvements in underlying dynamic scene understanding and video segmentation models will directly enhance iTACO's performance. As these foundation models continue to evolve, iTACO's robustness and accuracy are likely to improve further.

In conclusion, iTACO represents a substantial leap forward in the practical and scalable acquisition of interactable digital twins. By making advanced 3D modeling accessible through common consumer devices, it unlocks new possibilities for research and applications in robotics, embodied AI, and beyond, positioning itself as a foundational technology for a future where digital and physical worlds are seamlessly intertwined.
