# Learning in the Null Space: Small Singular Values for Continual Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The NESS algorithm introduces a continual learning method that directly utilizes small singular values from previous task inputs to constrain weight updates, thereby mitigating catastrophic forgetting. This approach consistently demonstrates superior backward transfer rates, often achieving positive or near-zero forgetting on standard benchmarks, while maintaining stable performance across a sequence of tasks.

## Problem

- Catastrophic forgetting prevents models from sequentially learning new tasks without losing performance on prior ones.
- Balancing model plasticity (adapting to new information) with stability (retaining old knowledge) remains a core challenge in continual learning.
- Existing orthogonality-based continual learning methods often rely on complex gradient projection techniques which can be optimizer-dependent and may not ensure strict orthogonality in weight space.

## Method

![[2602.21919-method.png]]
*Overview of NESS: continual training updates the network per task via a low-rank decomposition Delta W = U V, where U is a frozen orthogonal basis of small-singular-value vectors from the SVD of previous-input covariance and V is trainable, constraining updates to an approximate null subspace.*

- The NESS algorithm directly parameterizes weight updates by constructing a fixed orthogonal basis from the singular vectors corresponding to small singular values of previous task inputs.
- Weight updates (ΔW) are defined as U_t V_t, where U_t is the non-trainable orthogonal basis (representing the approximate null space of past tasks) and V_t is a compact, trainable matrix.
- This approach embeds the orthogonality constraint directly into the weight space, allowing new task learning in directions minimally affecting prior knowledge, and incorporates a stability bound via norm regularization on V_t.

## Results

- NESS consistently achieved superior Backward Transfer (BWT) rates, indicating significantly reduced catastrophic forgetting across CIFAR-100, 5-datasets, and MiniImageNet.
- On CIFAR-100 (10 tasks), NESS with SGDm obtained a BWT of 0.03%, outperforming orthogonal baselines such as SGP (-1.18%) and competitive with TRGP (0.06%).
- The method demonstrated strong stability, often achieving positive or near-zero BWT (e.g., 0.41% on MiniImageNet), visually confirmed by fewer performance degradations on past tasks compared to other methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directions corresponding to small singular values of previous task inputs are approximately orthogonal to those inputs, providing a stable subspace for new learning without interference.
> - Embedding orthogonality directly into the weight update parameterization offers a more robust and optimizer-agnostic solution for catastrophic forgetting compared to gradient projection methods.
> - Utilizing a Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA-style) for weight updates in this null space intrinsically provides parameter efficiency and explicit stability guarantees.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses one of the most fundamental and persistent challenges in machine learning: **catastrophic forgetting in Continual Learning (CL)**. Continual learning aims to enable a single model to sequentially learn a series of tasks without losing performance on previously learned ones, balancing the need for *plasticity* (adapting to new information) and *stability* (retaining old knowledge). Catastrophic forgetting, where a model's performance on prior tasks degrades significantly after learning new ones, is a major barrier to deploying adaptive AI systems in dynamic, real-world environments.

The existing landscape of CL methods is diverse, broadly categorized into:
- **Memory-based methods:** Employing an episodic memory buffer to replay samples from past tasks (e.g., A-GEM).
- **Architecture-based methods:** Modifying or expanding the network structure (e.g., HAT).
- **Optimization-based methods:** Introducing constraints or regularization (e.g., EWC) or subspace-based approaches.
- **Representation-based methods:** Learning stable, task-invariant representations.
- **Bayesian methods:** Formulating CL from a probabilistic perspective.

This paper specifically situates itself within **orthogonality-based continual learning**, which is a prominent sub-category of optimization-based methods. These approaches are designed to minimize interference between tasks by enforcing constraints that make updates for new tasks "orthogonal" to the knowledge acquired from previous tasks. Historically, these methods have evolved:
- **Early methods:** Such as Orthogonal Gradient Descent (OGD) and Orthogonal Weight Modulation (OWM), focused on restricting gradient directions or reusing feature representations.
- **Pioneering gradient projection:** A significant advancement came with Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) by Saha et al., which introduced the concept of projecting gradients onto the *null space* of previously learned task subspaces. This prevents gradients from interfering with the directions critical for previous tasks.
- **Subsequent extensions and refinements:** Following GPM, numerous methods built upon this foundation, including FS-DGPM (incorporating sharpness-aware flattening), TRGP (trust-region strategy), SGP (scaled updates), and DFGP (integrating mixup and sharpness-aware minimization). More recently, methods like Space Decoupling (SD) and CODE-CL have explored other forms of subspace manipulation or conceptor-based gradient projection.

A key unifying characteristic of these prominent orthogonality-based methods is their reliance on **enforcing orthogonality through gradient projection**. They typically compute the Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) of input representations from previous tasks, identify the dominant subspace (large singular values), and then project gradients for new tasks onto its *orthogonal complement* (the subspace of small singular values). While effective, this approach modifies the optimization process itself, potentially introducing complexities related to optimizer choice and gradient computation.

NESS distinguishes itself by **revisiting this principle from a novel perspective**. Instead of projecting gradients, NESS incorporates the orthogonality constraint *directly into the parameterization of the weight updates (ΔW)* in the *weight space*. It explicitly constructs a *fixed orthogonal basis* from the singular vectors associated with small singular values of previous task inputs. This basis then defines the allowable update space for a new, compact trainable matrix (similar to LoRA). This shift from gradient manipulation to direct weight-space parameterization represents a significant departure from prior work, offering a more direct and potentially more stable way to implement the "null space" idea. It links continual learning with recent advances in parameter-efficient fine-tuning, thereby integrating two important research directions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel Continual Learning (CL) algorithm that effectively alleviates catastrophic forgetting while maintaining the model's ability to learn new tasks efficiently. This involves finding a robust balance between the conflicting demands of stability (retaining past knowledge) and plasticity (adapting to new information).

The specific motivations driving the development of NESS (Null-space Estimated from Small Singular values) are multi-faceted:

- **Exploiting Theoretical Foundations:** The authors are motivated by the strong theoretical properties and efficiency observed in orthogonal-based training methods. They aim to leverage these benefits in a new CL paradigm.
- **Addressing Limitations of Gradient Projection:** While effective, existing orthogonality-based methods primarily rely on gradient projection. This approach can be complex, dependent on the specific optimizer used, and might not always guarantee strict orthogonality in the weight space during the entire optimization process. The authors seek a more direct and robust mechanism.
- **Direct Application of Orthogonality in Weight Space:** The core motivation is the insight that "small singular values correspond to directions that are nearly orthogonal to the input space of previous tasks." Instead of implicitly using this through gradient projection, NESS aims to directly construct and utilize this approximate null space for weight updates.
- **Designing a Stable and Efficient Algorithm:** The goal is to build a CL method that not only achieves low forgetting rates but also maintains stable accuracy across a sequence of tasks. Furthermore, the method should be computationally efficient, particularly in terms of trainable parameters.
- **Structured Parameterization:** By adopting a Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA-style) formulation, where updates are parameterized by a compact trainable matrix within a fixed orthogonal basis, the authors aim to intrinsically satisfy the stability constraint without complex gradient manipulations. This provides an elegant solution to embedding the orthogonality requirement directly into the model's architecture for task adaptation.
- **Providing Theoretical Guarantees:** The motivation includes offering a theoretical analysis to support the efficiency and stability of the proposed formulation, specifically an explicit bound on the perturbation to previous task outputs.

In essence, NESS is motivated by the desire to implement the principles of null-space learning for continual tasks in a more direct, structurally integrated, and optimizer-agnostic manner, thereby advancing the state of the art in catastrophic forgetting mitigation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

NESS (Null-space Estimated from Small Singular values) introduces a novel approach to continual learning by directly constraining weight updates to an approximate null space of previous task inputs, rather than relying on gradient projection. The methodology can be broken down into several key steps:

**Problem Formulation:**
The paper defines CL as learning a sequence of tasks $\{Task_1, \dots, Task_T\}$ where only data from the current task $D_t = (X_t, Y_t)$ is available. The objective is to learn parameters $W_T$ that perform well across all tasks, mitigating catastrophic forgetting.

**Core Principle: Stability and Plasticity Balance:**
For each new task $t$, the method aims to adapt the model ($plasticity$) while limiting interference with previously learned tasks ($stability$). This is formally expressed as a per-input stability constraint for each layer $l$:
$\|x^{(l)\top} \Delta W^{(l)}_t\|_2^2 \le \epsilon$, for all previous inputs $x^{(l)} \in I^{(l)}_t$, where $\Delta W^{(l)}_t$ is the weight update for layer $l$ and $\epsilon$ controls the allowable output perturbation.

**Construction of the Stability Subspace:**
1. **Input Collection:** For each layer $l$ and current task $t$, the model performs a single forward pass over the training data of the *current* task $D_t$ to collect its input features $I^{(l)}$. Importantly, to estimate the null space of *previous* tasks, it concatenates inputs from all preceding tasks $\{Task_1, \dots, Task_{t-1}\}$ that would have passed through this layer. (The paper clarifies this as $I_t = [X_1: X_2: \dots: X_{t-1}]$ at the considered layer).
2. **Singular Value Decomposition (SVD):** The SVD is computed for the aggregated previous inputs $I_t = \tilde{U}_t \Sigma_t \tilde{V}^\top_t$. Eigen-decomposition of the covariance matrix $XX^\top$ is used in practice, which yields the left singular vectors $U$.
3. **Small Singular Value Selection:** Directions corresponding to small singular values are crucial for minimizing interference. A threshold $\epsilon_1 > 0$ is used to define these directions: $j = \min \{i: \sigma_{t,i} \le \epsilon_1 \|I_t\|_F\}$. The singular vectors $\{u_{t,j}, \dots, u_{t,d}\}$ associated with these small singular values form the fixed orthogonal basis $U_t$. These vectors span an approximate null subspace, meaning inputs from previous tasks have minimal energy along these directions.

**Structured Parameterization of Weight Updates (LoRA-style):**
The weight update for a given layer $\Delta W_t$ is parameterized as $\Delta W_t = U_t V_t$.
- $U_t$: This is the **fixed orthogonal basis** (derived from small singular values of previous inputs). It is *not trainable*.
- $V_t$: This is a **trainable matrix**, initialized to zero. It is typically a compact, low-rank matrix, similar to adaptations in LoRA.

**Explicit Stability Bound and Practical Enforcement:**
The structured update directly provides a stability guarantee. For any previous input $x \in I_t$, the output perturbation $\|x^\top \Delta W_t\|_2$ is bounded by $\epsilon_1 \|I_t\|_F \|V_t\|_2$. By enforcing a norm constraint on $V_t$ (e.g., via weight decay), specifically $\|V_t\|_2 \le \sqrt{\epsilon} / (\epsilon_1 \|I_t\|_F)$, the stability constraint on output perturbation is satisfied. This ensures that updates primarily affect directions where previous inputs have low energy, thus limiting interference.

**Training Paradigm (Algorithm 1):**
1. **First Task Training:** The first task is trained using standard gradient descent on all layers, without any subspace constraints.
2. **Subsequent Task Training (Tasks 2 to T):**
 - For each layer $l$ and task $t$:
 - Collect layer inputs $I^{(l)}$ from the *current* task $D_t$ (and conceptually, previous tasks for subspace computation).
 - Construct $U^{(l)}_t$ (the fixed basis from small singular values) using the `GetUV` function, which computes the eigen-decomposition of $XX^\top$ for efficiency, avoiding storage of full input matrices.
 - Initialize the trainable matrix $V^{(l)}_t$ to zero.
 - Train *only* $V^{(l)}_t$ (the backbone weights $W^{(l)}_{t-1}$ and $U^{(l)}_t$ are frozen) using the current task dataset $D_t$ and cross-entropy loss. Norm regularization (weight decay) is applied to $V^{(l)}_t$ to enforce the stability bound.
 - After training $V^{(l)}_t$, merge the update into the backbone: $W^{(l)}_t \leftarrow W^{(l)}_{t-1} + U^{(l)}_t V^{(l)}_t$.
 - A task-specific classification head is trained without subspace constraints to allow full adaptation to the new task's output space.

This methodology ensures that learning for new tasks occurs predominantly in directions minimally used by previous tasks, making the orthogonality constraint an inherent part of the model's weight update mechanism rather than an external optimization-time projection.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of NESS focused on its ability to mitigate catastrophic forgetting and maintain stable performance across a sequence of tasks, comparing it against a suite of strong baselines on standard image classification benchmarks.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Datasets:** CIFAR-100 (10 tasks), 5-datasets (5 tasks), and MiniImageNet (20 tasks).
- **Architectures:** 5-layer AlexNet for CIFAR-100, Reduced ResNet18 for 5-datasets and MiniImageNet.
- **Baselines:** A mix of traditional methods (OWM, EWC, HAT, A-GEM) and, more importantly, strong orthogonal-based methods (GPM, SGP, TRGP, FS-DGPM, DFGP).
- **Metrics:** Average Accuracy (ACC) and Backward Transfer (BWT), with a primary focus on BWT, which quantifies the forgetting of old tasks (higher/less negative BWT is better).
- **NESS Optimizers:** Tested with SAM and SGD with momentum (SGDm).

**Key Findings:**

1. **Competitive Performance:** NESS demonstrates competitive average accuracy (ACC) across all evaluated datasets when compared to the baselines. While not always achieving the absolute highest ACC, its performance is robust and within the range of top-performing methods.

2. **Superior Backward Transfer (Reduced Forgetting):** This is NESS's most significant strength.
 - NESS consistently achieves excellent BWT rates, indicating significantly reduced catastrophic forgetting.
 - **On CIFAR-100 (10 Tasks):** NESS with SGDm achieved a BWT of 0.03% (±0.40), making it the best among all methods, including TRGP (0.06% ±0.17) and significantly outperforming SGP (-1.18% ±0.37) and various DFGP configurations (ranging from -0.95% to -1.11%).
 - **On 5-datasets (5 Tasks):** NESS with SGDm achieved a BWT of -0.58% (±0.15), comparable to TRGP (-0.09% ±0.08) and substantially better than SGP (-1.82% ±0.13) and DFGP variants (ranging from -1.70% to -3.87%).
 - **On MiniImageNet (20 Tasks):** NESS with SGDm achieved a BWT of 0.41% (±0.58), outperforming all baselines, including DFGP (-0.11% to -1.11%) and SGP (-0.53% ±0.68).
 - The paper highlights that NESS consistently achieves a BWT rate greater than -1% or even positive BWT, which is a strong indicator of stability, especially when compared to baselines that often report BWT rates well below -1% (e.g., FS-DGPM at -3.03%, some DFGP settings at -3.87%).

3. **Stability Across Tasks (Visualizations):** The forgetting rate visualizations (Figures 2-5, and additional figures in Appendix A.2) visually confirm NESS's superior stability. The plots show fewer "red cells" for NESS, which represent significant performance degradation on past tasks, indicating that knowledge from earlier tasks is better preserved after subsequent learning. This visual evidence strongly supports the quantitative BWT metric.

4. **Efficiency Analysis:**
 - NESS is designed for efficiency. By parameterizing updates as $\Delta W_t = U_t V_t$ where $U_t$ is fixed and $V_t$ is trainable and compact (due to its construction from dynamically selected small singular value directions), the total number of trainable parameters for task adaptation is significantly smaller than the full network parameters.
 - The efficiency is directly controlled by the threshold $\epsilon_1$; a lower $\epsilon_1$ implies a smaller subspace and thus fewer trainable parameters in $V_t$.
 - The online computation of the covariance matrix ($XX^\top$) for SVD further contributes to computational efficiency by avoiding the storage of full input matrices.

In summary, NESS effectively demonstrates that directly leveraging small singular values in the weight space for parameter-efficient updates leads to a robust continual learning algorithm with notably low forgetting rates and stable performance across tasks, often outperforming existing gradient projection-based orthogonal CL methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of NESS marks a significant contribution to the field of Continual Learning, offering a novel and effective strategy to combat catastrophic forgetting. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Novel Paradigm for Orthogonality in CL:** NESS introduces a distinct approach to enforcing orthogonality by directly parameterizing weight updates in the null space of previous task inputs. This shifts the focus from manipulating gradients during optimization to a structural constraint on the weight space itself. This departure from gradient projection methods (which dominate existing orthogonal CL literature) opens new avenues for research, potentially inspiring a new family of continual learning algorithms that embed stability constraints more deeply into model architectures.

2. **Enhanced Forgetting Mitigation:** The most compelling finding is NESS's consistently superior performance in Backward Transfer (BWT), a critical metric for assessing forgetting. Achieving positive or near-zero BWT across various benchmarks is a strong indicator of its ability to retain knowledge from past tasks. This direct attack on catastrophic forgetting makes NESS a promising candidate for real-world applications where models must continuously adapt without losing previously acquired skills.

3. **Parameter Efficiency and Scalability:** By adopting a Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA-style) approach, NESS significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters for each new task. This parameter efficiency is crucial for deploying large foundation models in dynamic environments, where full fine-tuning for every new task is computationally prohibitive and resource-intensive. NESS's design makes continual learning more scalable and practical.

4. **Theoretical Clarity and Practical Control:** The explicit stability bound derived in the paper provides a clear theoretical understanding of how NESS prevents forgetting. It shows that the magnitude of output perturbation on previous tasks is directly controlled by the chosen threshold $\epsilon_1$ and the norm of the trainable matrix $V_t$. This theoretical clarity offers practitioners a principled way to control the stability-plasticity trade-off, enabling more informed hyperparameter tuning.

5. **Robustness to Optimizer Choice:** Because NESS's orthogonality constraint is embedded directly into the weight update parameterization, it becomes largely independent of the specific optimizer used. This enhances the robustness and generality of the method, contrasting with gradient projection techniques that can be sensitive to optimizer characteristics.

6. **Broader Implications for Neural Network Dynamics:** The paper underscores the importance of small singular values—components often overlooked or considered "low-energy"—in preserving knowledge and enabling stable adaptation. This insight contributes to a deeper understanding of how information is stored and modified within neural networks during sequential learning, potentially fostering new research into the role of different spectral components in model stability and plasticity.

7. **Future Research Directions:** The authors candidly identify limitations and open problems, such as the need for more effective $\epsilon_1$ threshold tuning strategies (especially across different layers), handling biases and BatchNorm layers within the NESS framework, and moving towards fully memory-free operations. These open questions highlight fertile ground for future research that can build upon the NESS foundation, further refining and extending its applicability.

In conclusion, NESS represents a significant advancement in continual learning, particularly for orthogonal-based methods. By demonstrating the efficacy of direct weight-space manipulation using small singular values, it offers a stable, efficient, and theoretically grounded approach to mitigate catastrophic forgetting, with substantial potential to impact the development of more adaptive and robust AI systems.
