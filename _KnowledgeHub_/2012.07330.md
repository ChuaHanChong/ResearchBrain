# Active Hierarchical Imitation and Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework combines active learning with hierarchical imitation and reinforcement learning to enhance robot learning from human demonstrations in continuous state-action spaces. This approach, particularly with a reward-based active learning strategy, achieved improved task success rates and reduced human expert effort in 3D maze navigation.

## Problem

- Obtaining sufficient high-quality human demonstrations for robot learning is labor-intensive and costly.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) often suffers from poor sample efficiency, especially in continuous, sparse-reward environments.
- Prior Hierarchical Imitation and Reinforcement Learning (HIRL) work was primarily limited to simplified 2D discrete environments and did not extensively leverage direct human demonstrations for continuous tasks.

## Method

- An **Active Hierarchical Imitation** and ==Reinforcement Learning== (HIRL) framework is developed for continuous state-action spaces, using a two-level hierarchy with a DAgger-trained high-level meta-controller and a DDPG-pre-trained low-level controller.
- Three active learning methods (noise-based, multi-policy, reward-based) are proposed to select informative states for demonstration or manage data in the experience replay buffer.
- These methods are designed to reduce the physical and mental effort required from human experts during the training process for complex tasks like 3D maze navigation.

## Results

- DAgger+Reward-based Active Learning demonstrated the highest task success rates and significantly reduced expert demonstration costs to achieve higher performance.
- A human user study revealed that DAgger+Reward-based Active Learning led to significantly lower perceived physical demand and effort from participants, alongside higher perceived task success.
- Conversely, DAgger+Multi-Policy Active Learning resulted in lower success rates and increased perceived physical demand and effort for human demonstrators.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Hierarchical structures enable effective learning of complex tasks in continuous state and action spaces when combined with interactive imitation learning.
> - Judicious application of active learning can significantly reduce the expert cost and improve learning efficiency for robot training from human demonstrations.
> - The effectiveness of active learning strategies varies, with reward-based active learning proving superior in both objective performance and human perception of the training process compared to multi-policy or vanilla DAgger.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated at the intersection of several subfields within artificial intelligence and robotics, specifically focusing on Imitation Learning (IL), Reinforcement Learning (RL), Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL), Active Learning (AL), and Human-Robot Interaction (HRI).

Traditional approaches to teaching robots complex tasks often rely on either Imitation Learning, where a robot learns from expert demonstrations, or Reinforcement Learning, where an agent learns through trial and error by maximizing a reward signal. Both methods have shown effectiveness, but also possess limitations. IL can be highly efficient when optimal expert policies are available, but acquiring sufficient, high-quality demonstrations can be labor-intensive, particularly from human experts. RL, while powerful for learning optimal policies, often suffers from poor sample efficiency, especially in environments with sparse rewards or large state-action spaces.

Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL) emerged to address some of the complexities of RL by decomposing tasks into smaller, more manageable sub-tasks, thereby improving exploration and sample efficiency through temporal abstractions. Combining IL and HRL, termed Hierarchical Imitation and Reinforcement Learning (HIRL), has been explored as a promising direction to leverage the strengths of both, reducing expert costs in training agents for complex tasks. However, prior HIRL work primarily focused on relatively simple 2D environments with discrete action spaces and often utilized hard-coded or RL-trained expert policies rather than human demonstrations.

This paper addresses these limitations by extending HIRL to continuous state and action spaces, which are characteristic of many real-world robotic tasks. It further integrates Active Learning techniques to enhance learning efficiency and reduce the burden on human demonstrators in HRI scenarios. Active learning, in general, aims to reduce the amount of labeled data required by intelligently querying an expert for labels on the most informative samples. In the context of learning from demonstration (LfD), this translates to strategically asking for expert demonstrations or feedback. The paper also investigates the human perception of the training process, a crucial aspect for practical HRI applications that is often overlooked in technical evaluations. By focusing on human-provided demonstrations and continuous environments, this work contributes to making robotic learning from human input more practical and efficient.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the efficiency of robot learning from human demonstrations for complex tasks by integrating active learning algorithms within a hierarchical imitation and reinforcement learning framework. This integration aims to reduce the physical and mental effort required from human experts during the training process.

The motivation for this work stems from several observed limitations and opportunities in the existing research landscape:

1. **Leveraging Hierarchical Structures:** Humans naturally decompose complex tasks into sub-tasks. The paper is motivated by the proven efficiency of hierarchical structures in both imitation and reinforcement learning for robots to learn complex tasks, especially those with sparse rewards. The authors specifically draw inspiration from prior HIRL work that demonstrated decreased expert cost in training agents within discrete state-action spaces.
2. **Addressing Limitations of Prior HIRL Work:** Previous research in HIRL predominantly focused on simplified 2D game environments with discrete action spaces. Furthermore, many imitation learning studies improved policies derived from pre-trained or hard-coded "expert" policies, rather than directly from human experts. This work aims to extend the applicability of HIRL to more realistic continuous state and action spaces, such as those found in maze navigation and robot manipulation tasks.
3. **Enhancing Sample Efficiency in Continuous Settings:** Learning policies parameterized by goals in continuous settings (contextual policy learning or multi-task learning) can be computationally intensive, often requiring days of training. The authors are motivated to develop methods that accelerate this learning process, expecting that combining imitation learning with a hierarchical structure will contribute to faster learning.
4. **Optimizing Human-Robot Interaction:** In scenarios where human experts are required to provide demonstrations to teach robots, it is critical to maximize learning efficiency to minimize the expert's effort. Beyond mere efficiency, understanding the human demonstrator's perception of the learning and training process—how physically and mentally demanding it is, and their perception of the robot's success—is crucial for designing user-friendly and effective HRI systems. The research seeks to develop methods that not only achieve better objective performance but also improve the subjective experience for human users.
5. **Integrating Active Learning:** To further improve sample efficiency and reduce human effort, the authors propose and investigate the application of active learning methods specifically designed for their hierarchical framework. This is motivated by the success of active learning in reducing labeling costs in other domains, aiming to translate these benefits to the context of learning from demonstration for robotics.

In summary, the detailed objectives include developing a hierarchical imitation and reinforcement learning framework for continuous state-action spaces, integrating and evaluating various active learning methods (specifically noise-based, multi-policy, and reward-based) within this framework, and conducting a human user study to assess both the objective performance and the subjective human experience regarding physical demand, task success, and effort.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research develops an Active Hierarchical Imitation and Reinforcement Learning (HIRL) framework designed to learn multi-level policies in continuous state and action spaces. The core methodology involves a two-level hierarchy, where a high-level meta-controller selects subtasks and a low-level controller executes them.

**A. Hierarchical Imitation and Reinforcement Learning (HIRL) Framework:**
The framework utilizes a two-level hierarchical structure:
- **High-level Meta-Controller:** Responsible for selecting subgoals to achieve the final task goal. This controller is trained using an interactive imitation learning algorithm.
- **Low-level Controller:** Responsible for executing actions to reach the subgoals provided by the high-level controller. This controller is pre-trained using a reinforcement learning method.

**B. Interactive Imitation Learning for High-Level Policy:**
- The interactive imitation learning algorithm DAgger (Dataset Aggregation) is employed to train the high-level policy ($\pi_{HI}$). DAgger iteratively aggregates expert demonstrations and the learner's policy actions to mitigate covariate shift. In each iteration, the agent executes its current policy, the demonstrator provides optimal actions for the states visited, and these state-action pairs are added to a dataset to retrain the policy.
- The authors initially considered AggreVaTeD but found it unsuitable for their setting due to sparse environmental rewards and limited interactive training time. AggreVaTeD relies on policy gradients and reward information, which was scarce in their maze navigation task, leading to negligible policy improvement within a reasonable number of episodes.
- The high-level policy generates a subgoal ($g_{LO}$) by blending its own output ($\beta\pi_{HI}$) with the expert's demonstration ($(1-\beta)\pi^*$), where $\beta$ is a mixing factor that decays over time.

**C. Low-Level Controller:**
- The low-level policy is pre-trained using a revised Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) method, a model-free, off-policy actor-critic algorithm suitable for continuous action spaces. This controller executes actions for a fixed horizon (H) to reach the given subgoal.
- The low-level policy's pre-training allows the high-level policy to operate on a more semantically meaningful action space (subgoals) rather than primitive actions.

**D. Active Learning Methods:**
To enhance learning performance and efficiency, three active learning methods are proposed and investigated:

1. **Noise-Based Active Learning:**
 - This method aims to select initial states ($s_g^*$) where the current high-level policy exhibits high uncertainty.
 - It operates by taking candidate sub-states (e.g., Cartesian position) and adding random uniform noise to other dimensions of the agent's state.
 - The method then calculates the variance of the policy's output across these noisy states for each candidate sub-state. The sub-state that results in the highest variance of policy outputs is selected as the starting point for the next episode, under the assumption that high variance indicates areas where the policy is less confident and thus more informative to learn from.

2. **Multi-Policy Active Learning:**
 - Inspired by ensemble learning (bagging), this method trains multiple instances (n policies) of the high-level policy on the same training dataset.
 - For a given candidate state, it computes the variance of the outputs from these multiple policies.
 - Similar to the noise-based method, states where the policies disagree the most (highest variance) are considered most informative and selected as the starting point for a new episode.

3. **Reward-Based Active Learning:**
 - Unlike the previous two, this method focuses on selecting useful data for the experience replay buffer rather than actively initializing the agent's position.
 - In goal-achieving tasks with sparse rewards (where rewards are only given upon reaching the final goal), data from unsuccessful episodes is often discarded or given low value.
 - This method proposes to selectively remove data from unsuccessful episodes from the replay buffer with a probability $p = e^{-t_d}$, where $t_d$ is the time step distance from the end of the episode. This means that data collected earlier in an unsuccessful episode is more likely to be discarded, while data closer to the terminal state of the unsuccessful episode has a higher chance of being retained. The rationale is that states and actions near the end of an episode, even if unsuccessful, might still contain valuable information about "what not to do" or relevant local dynamics.

**E. Human User Study:**
A within-subjects user study was conducted with five participants to evaluate the HIRL framework with DAgger (vanilla), DAgger + Multi-Policy AL, and DAgger + Reward-Based AL.

- **Task Environment:** A 3D maze navigation task in MuJoCo, where an Ant agent navigates from a random starting corner to a random target. The maze layout is randomly generated, and the agent receives a bird's-eye view pixel representation of the environment. The time limit per episode is 500 steps.
- **Participants:** 5 able-bodied individuals (3 female, 2 male), aged 23-25.
- **Procedure:** Each participant underwent 3 trials (one for each algorithm) in a randomized order, with each trial consisting of 100 episodes. Participants provided demonstrations by moving a mouse to indicate subgoal positions. A 30-episode practice trial preceded the study.
- **Objective Measures:**
 - **Success Rate:** Percentage of tasks completed successfully.
 - **Expert Cost:** Average number of demonstrations provided per episode.
 - **Attempts:** Average number of subgoals generated by the high-level policy per episode.
- **Subjective Measures:** A 5-point Likert scale questionnaire, based on NASA-TLX, was administered to capture participants' perceptions regarding physical demand, task success, and human effort.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results from both objective and subjective measures provide insights into the performance and user experience of the developed active learning-based HIRL algorithms compared to the vanilla DAgger approach.

**A. Objective Measures:**

1. **Success Rate:**
 - **DAgger+Reward-based AL** demonstrated the highest and most consistent success rate among the three algorithms, surpassing 60% within 100 episodes. Its performance steadily improved over episodes.
 - **DAgger** (vanilla) exhibited moderate success rates, generally lower than DAgger+Reward-based AL.
 - **DAgger+Multi-Policy AL** performed the worst, showing lower success rates and exhibiting the largest variation in performance across different executions, suggesting less stable learning.

2. **Expert Cost:**
 - To achieve a success rate exceeding 50%, **DAgger+Reward-based AL** resulted in the most significant savings in expert demonstrations (expert cost). This indicates its efficiency in learning with less human input at higher performance levels.
 - **DAgger** (vanilla) required fewer expert demonstrations when the success rate was below 50%.
 - **DAgger+Multi-Policy AL** consistently demanded the most expert demonstrations within the 100 episodes, suggesting it was the least efficient in utilizing human input.

3. **Attempts (Subgoals Generated):**
 - All three algorithms showed a similar trend: a sharp decrease in the number of attempts within the first 20 episodes, followed by a slower decline.
 - There was no notable or obvious difference in the average number of subgoals generated among DAgger, DAgger+Multi-Policy AL, and DAgger+Reward-based AL. This suggests that while active learning methods influence efficiency and success, they do not significantly alter the fundamental hierarchical decomposition of the task in terms of subgoals generated per episode.

Based on the objective measures, DAgger+Reward-based AL demonstrated superior performance by achieving higher success rates while simultaneously reducing the demand for expert demonstrations, particularly for higher performance levels. Conversely, DAgger+Multi-Policy AL performed less effectively than the vanilla DAgger.

**B. Subjective Measures:**

A one-way ANOVA test was used to determine the statistical significance of differences in questionnaire responses averaged across all participants.

1. **DAgger and DAgger+Multi-Policy AL Comparison:**
 - Participants reported higher physical demand and greater effort when providing demonstrations for the robot using **DAgger+Multi-Policy AL** compared to DAgger. A statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) was observed for physical demand, and an even more significant difference (p < 0.001) for effort.
 - Conversely, participants perceived lower success when the robot employed DAgger+Multi-Policy AL.

2. **DAgger and DAgger+Reward-based AL Comparison:**
 - Participants reported lower physical demand and reduced effort when interacting with the robot using **DAgger+Reward-based AL** compared to DAgger. These differences were statistically significant (p < 0.001 for physical demand, p < 0.05 for effort).
 - Participants also perceived higher task success when the robot learned with DAgger+Reward-based AL, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001).

These subjective results indicate that DAgger+Reward-based AL was perceived by human participants as leading to better agent performance and an easier, less demanding training experience. In contrast, DAgger+Multi-Policy AL was associated with a perceived decrease in agent performance and a more demanding training process.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the fields of robot learning and human-robot interaction by advancing hierarchical imitation and reinforcement learning with active learning strategies. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Learning Efficiency in Continuous Spaces:** The work successfully extends Hierarchical Imitation and Reinforcement Learning (HIRL) to continuous state and action spaces, which is a crucial step towards applying these methods to more complex, real-world robotic tasks beyond simplified 2D domains. This generalization makes HIRL more relevant for practical applications such as robot manipulation and complex navigation.

2. **Reduction of Expert Effort:** The introduction and evaluation of active learning methods within the HIRL framework directly address the challenge of reducing the high cost associated with obtaining expert demonstrations. The finding that DAgger+Reward-based AL significantly saves expert cost for higher success rates is important for making human-in-the-loop training more feasible and sustainable.

3. **Improved Human-Robot Interaction Experience:** Beyond objective performance, the study provides valuable insights into the human perspective of robot training. The subjective results, indicating that DAgger+Reward-based AL leads to a perceived reduction in physical demand and mental effort, alongside higher perceived task success, are critical. This suggests that the chosen active learning strategy can directly impact user satisfaction and willingness to engage in prolonged robot training. Such insights are essential for designing intuitive and effective HRI systems.

4. **Identification of Effective Active Learning Strategies:** The research demonstrates that not all active learning strategies are equally effective in this context. While reward-based active learning proved beneficial, multi-policy active learning did not show an improvement and, in fact, increased expert cost and perceived effort. This differentiation provides guidance for future research and practical implementations, highlighting the importance of tailoring active learning approaches to the specific learning paradigm and environment.

5. **Foundation for Future Work:** The findings suggest that active reward learning is a promising direction for speeding up learning from demonstration feedback. This opens avenues for future research into more sophisticated reward-based active learning mechanisms, exploring their robustness across various continuous control tasks, and potentially integrating them with more advanced deep reinforcement learning architectures. The framework established could also serve as a basis for investigating other active learning queries, such as requesting demonstrations for specific challenging scenarios or clarifying ambiguities.

In conclusion, this research presents a method for making robot learning from human demonstration more efficient and user-friendly in complex continuous environments. By successfully integrating reward-based active learning into a hierarchical imitation and reinforcement learning framework, the authors have provided a foundation for the development of robots that can learn effectively from human input with reduced burden on human experts, thereby potentially accelerating the deployment of intelligent robotic systems in diverse applications.
