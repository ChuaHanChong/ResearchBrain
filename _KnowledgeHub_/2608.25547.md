# A Tendon-Driven Five-Fingered Hand with Distributed Tactile Perception for Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A tendon-driven five-fingered robotic hand with a soft-rigid-hybrid structure, combining compliance with usable grasp force. Strain-gauge and PVDF sensing elements sit on the distal and middle phalanges of every finger, giving dual-modality static and dynamic tactile perception. An integrated actuation-perception pipeline runs motion primitives while logging multi-channel tactile signals. Counting gestures, four thumb pinches, three grasps, and bottle-grasp signal recording validate the design.

## Problem

- Single-DOF grippers handle only regular-shaped objects in simple tasks, failing at in-hand manipulation, tool use, and fine adjustment of object pose.
- Rigid hands such as Shadow and DexHand deliver dexterity but suffer high control complexity and poor compliance when grasping flexible or delicate objects.
- Soft hands gain flexibility yet cannot supply sufficient manipulation force, and most existing hands place tactile sensing only at limited fingertip contact regions.

## Method

- ==Soft-rigid-hybrid== five-fingered hand with ==tendon-driven== finger flexion, finger swing, and wrist motion; ==underactuated coupling== between adjacent fingers, actuators and controller relocated into the forearm.
- Distributed dual-modality tactile skin: a ==strain-gauge== (slow-adapting, piezoresistive) plus ==PVDF== (fast-adapting, piezoelectric) pair on both distal and middle phalanges, four elements per finger.
- Integrated actuation-perception architecture with a state-based ==motion-primitive== strategy grouping low-level actuator targets and transition times into grasp, hold, and release phases while acquiring tactile frames in parallel.

## Results

- Dexterity demonstrations succeeded across **five** counting gestures (numbers one to five), **four** thumb-to-finger pinches, and **three** grasps: bottle grasp, two-finger squeeze, and pinch-and-hold of a card.
- **Four-channel** thumb tactile recording during bottle grasp resolved **five** distinct phases: grasp, lifting up, holding, lifting down, and release, with PVDF sampled by ADC at **1000 Hz**.
- PVDF channels spiked only at contact-state transitions while strain gauges tracked slow static force changes; a consistent time lag appeared between middle and distal segments as contact formed earlier proximally.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pairing piezoresistive and piezoelectric elements mimics slow- and fast-adapting mechanoreceptors, so one skin covers both sustained contact force and transient events like slip or tap.
> - Spreading sensors beyond fingertips onto middle phalanges yields proximal-to-distal timing lags that carry extra information about how contact forms during wrapping.
> - Caution: these sensors give magnitude only, not force direction, and normal grasping and tangential lifting forces superpose indistinguishably in the recorded signal.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

The development of dexterous robotic hands is a critical area within robotics, particularly for advancing humanoid robots and embodied artificial intelligence towards complex manipulation tasks. Traditional robotic grippers, often characterized by a single Degree-of-Freedom (DOF), are widely adopted in industrial settings but are primarily limited to simple operations involving regular-shaped objects in structured environments. This limitation restricts their application in scenarios requiring in-hand manipulation, tool use, or fine adjustments of object pose in unstructured or complex environments.

The design of robotic hands involves considerations such as material selection, mechanical structure, driving mechanisms, and sensory perception. Existing robotic hands can broadly be categorized into rigid, soft, and hybrid structures. Rigid hands, such as the Shadow hand or open-source projects like DexHand, offer high dexterity for tasks like object reorientation. However, they often exhibit high control complexity and low compliance, making them less suitable for grasping deformable or irregularly shaped objects. Conversely, soft hands provide greater flexibility and compliance but typically lack the force generation capability required for certain manipulation tasks. The challenge lies in integrating the advantages of both rigid and soft structures to achieve a balance of compliance, force, and dexterity.

A significant gap in many existing robotic hands is the absence or limited scope of tactile sensing. While some grippers incorporate sensing elements in specific contact regions, the human hand's ability to perform complex manipulations heavily relies on its sophisticated and distributed tactile perception. The integration of comprehensive tactile feedback, allowing robots to perceive both static contact forces and dynamic changes, is considered essential for achieving human-like manipulation capabilities, including adaptive grasping, slip detection, and surface exploration. This research aims to address these challenges by presenting an integrated approach that combines a hybrid mechanical design with distributed, dual-modality tactile sensing.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of current robotic hands in performing complex manipulations, which are crucial for advancing embodied artificial intelligence in humanoid robots. Specifically, the authors identify two main restrictions: the inherent dexterity of robotic hands and their tactile perception capabilities. Achieving human-level dexterity and manipulation competence requires robotic hands that can adapt to various object properties and environmental conditions, a feat that is often hindered by designs lacking sufficient compliance, force, and comprehensive sensory feedback.

To address these identified challenges, the key objectives of this work are articulated through the development of a novel robotic hand design and its integrated perception system. The central objective is to propose a tendon-driven five-fingered hand that incorporates distributed dual-modality tactile perception. This design aims to combine the advantages of both soft and rigid materials through a soft-rigid-hybrid structure, thereby endowing the hand with both compliance for delicate operations and sufficient operational force for robust grasping.

More specifically, the paper outlines three main contributions:
1. **Development of a soft-rigid-hybrid five-fingered hand**: This involves designing a mechanical structure that utilizes tendon-driven mechanisms for finger flexion, finger swing, and wrist motion. The hybrid material approach is intended to provide a balance between adaptability and force.
2. **Integration of dual-modality tactile sensing elements**: The objective is to embed both piezoresistive (strain gauge) and piezoelectric (PVDF) sensing elements onto multiple phalanges of the fingers. This distribution and dual-modity approach are designed to enable the perception of both static contact forces and dynamic force variations simultaneously.
3. **Establishment of an integrated actuation-perception architecture**: The goal is to create a system where motion commands can be synchronously executed and multi-channel tactile signals can be recorded in parallel during manipulation tasks. This architecture is crucial for closed-loop control and for analyzing the interplay between mechanical motion and contact response.

Overall, the research is motivated by the need to bridge the gap between simple robotic grippers and the complex manipulation capabilities of human hands, providing a foundation for more advanced robotic intelligence and autonomy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research presents an integrated approach to designing a dexterous robotic hand, encompassing mechanical architecture, actuation, and distributed tactile perception, all unified within a coordinated system.

#### 3.1. Overall Hand Architecture and Mechanical Design

The hand design is inspired by the open-source DexHand project, adopting a tendon-driven mechanism with underactuated coupling between adjacent fingers. The complete system comprises five fingers, a palm, a wrist module, and a forearm. The multi-joint fingers are arranged anthropomorphically. To minimize the mass and structural complexity of the distal hand part while maintaining accessibility for tendon pretensioning, wiring inspection, and hardware adjustments, the wrist-forearm transition module, servo motor housing, and a controller board are integrated into the forearm. Specific attention is given to cable routing, as both actuation and perception cables need to be managed. A key feature of the mechanical design is the employment of a soft-rigid-hybrid structure for the fingers, intended to provide compliance for delicate operations while retaining the capability to exert necessary manipulation forces.

#### 3.2. Actuation and Control System

The actuation system relies on a tendon-driven mechanism. Each finger is treated as a coupled joint chain, where its posture is determined by tendon displacement, routing geometry, pretension, and contact conditions, rather than by independent joint control. The thumb is configured for opposing motions, while the other four fingers are designed for coordinated closure. The transmission system includes crossed-tendon paths, dedicated finger driving/swing tendons, and wrist-driving tendons. This configuration allows for adaptive wrapping behaviors: in free-space, fingers follow nominal coupled trajectories, but upon contact, tendons drive joints towards less-constrained directions to conform to the object's shape. The routing geometry and pretension are crucial for repeatable and consistent contact formation.

The control and acquisition architecture integrates forearm-mounted actuator units, embedded control hardware, interface electronics, and a multi-channel tactile acquisition unit into a unified actuation-perception pipeline. Host-side commands are processed by the embedded control unit, mapped to actuator targets, and transmitted via interface electronics. A state-based motion-primitive strategy organizes tendon-driven manipulation, defining low-level commands for actuator targets and transition times, grouped into grasp, hold, and release phases. This structure facilitates repeatable analysis of the relationships among tendon motions, contact formation, and tactile responses.

#### 3.3. Tactile Sensing Module Design and Principles

To emulate human cutaneous perception, the hand incorporates a distributed dual-modality tactile sensing system embedded within its soft skin.
- **Sensing Elements (SEs)**:
 - **Piezoresistive SEs**: Strain gauges (SGs) are utilized to mimic slow-adapting (SA) mechanoreceptors. These elements respond to static or quasi-static stimuli by changing their resistance upon deformation. The resulting voltage change is captured. SGs are primarily used for low-frequency signals, applicable in scenarios such as grasp and release, button pressing, object lifting, collision detection, and continuous contact.
 - **Piezoelectric SEs**: Polyvinylidene fluorides (PVDFs) are leveraged to simulate fast-adapting (FA) mechanoreceptors. As self-powered elements, PVDFs generate varying magnitudes of voltage in response to dynamic forces. After filtering and amplification, these voltages are acquired by an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) module at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. PVDFs are designed for high-frequency components and dynamic stimuli, suitable for applications like sliding, tapping, and slip detection.
- **Distribution**: Unlike conventional tactile fingertips with limited sensing areas, this design distributes tactile SEs across the entire hand. Specifically, a pair of SG and PVDF sensors is attached to the distal phalanx, and another pair to the middle phalanx of *each* of the five fingers. This configuration results in four tactile SEs per finger, enabling both segments to perceive static and dynamic contact forces. The fabricated soft-rigid-hybrid fingers integrate these sensors directly into their structure.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed robotic hand through a series of manipulation tasks and analyzes its tactile perception capabilities.

#### 4.1. Dexterity Demonstration Through Manipulation Tasks

Three sets of manipulation tasks were conducted to demonstrate the dexterity and functional range of the developed robotic hand:
- **Counting Gestures**: The hand was successfully controlled to form five distinct counting gestures, representing numbers 1 through 5. This involved driving specific fingers to execute flexion or extension movements while others remained straight. The results indicated that both flexion and extension movements could be accurately implemented, producing gestures resembling those of a human hand.
- **Pinch Actions**: Critical for fine manipulations, the ability of the thumb to perform pinch operations with each of the other four fingers was tested. The hand successfully executed four different pinch operations, demonstrating its dexterity through the synergistic action of finger driving tendons and swing tendons. This capability is essential for tasks like picking up small objects.
- **Grasp Operations**: Three typical grasp operations were evaluated:
 - **Bottle Grasp**: All five fingers executed flexion movements to grasp a plastic bottle placed near the palm, demonstrating a stable power grasp.
 - **Squeeze Action**: This task, useful for gripping thin objects like cards, involved dominantly driving the index and middle fingers with swing tendons while the other three fingers remained bent. The hand successfully performed this selective manipulation.
 - **Pinch and Holding**: The thumb and index finger were controlled to pinch a card and maintain the holding gesture, while the other three fingers remained unbent. This demonstrated precise two-finger manipulation and sustained holding.

The successful implementation of these diverse manipulation tasks indicated that the designed hand is competent in forming a wide array of gestures, which are fundamental for constructing various action primitives required for complex robotic operations.

#### 4.2. Tactile Perception in Grasp Manipulations

To assess the performance of the integrated tactile module, tactile signals from all channels were collected during a bottle grasp task. The four-channel tactile signals from the thumb finger were analyzed across five primary phases: grasp, lifting up, holding, lifting down, and release. The analysis revealed several observations:

- **Dual-Modality Response**:
 - The two PVDF channels consistently responded primarily during **transition stages** between contact states. This included shifts from non-contact to contact (e.g., at the beginning of the grasp phase), from a static state to sudden movement (e.g., during lifting up and lifting down), and from grasp to release. This behavior aligns with their design as fast-adapting sensors for dynamic stimuli.
 - The two SG channels exhibited relatively **slow variations** throughout the manipulation process. They showed apparent responses in each phase when the applied static force changed, indicating their effectiveness in sensing static or quasi-static contact.
- **Time Lag in Contact Sequence**: A discernible time lag was observed between the tactile signals from the middle and distal segments for both PVDF and SG channels. This lag was attributed to the sequential nature of contact formation, where the middle segment typically made contact with the grasped bottle earlier than the distal segment.
- **Force Superposition**: During the lifting up and lifting down phases, the signals indicated a superposition of forces. While the exact direction of the applied force could not be determined from the sensor outputs alone, the grasping force (normal to the finger surface) and the lifting force (tangential to the finger surface) appeared to combine in their magnitude, influencing the sensor readings.

These results collectively demonstrated the feasibility and functionality of the integrated actuation-perception system. The distributed dual-modality tactile perception system provides distinct feedback regarding both static contact and dynamic force changes across multiple points on the fingers during manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant step towards developing robotic hands capable of dexterous manipulation by addressing critical limitations in current designs. The proposed tendon-driven five-fingered hand, integrating a soft-rigid-hybrid structure and distributed dual-modality tactile perception, offers several areas of potential impact.

Firstly, the **soft-rigid-hybrid structure** contributes to enhanced adaptability and force capabilities. By combining the compliance of soft materials with the structural integrity of rigid components, the hand can perform delicate operations that require flexibility while also exerting sufficient force for robust grasping. This design approach mitigates the individual drawbacks of purely soft or purely rigid hands, expanding the range of objects and tasks a robot can handle effectively.

Secondly, the implementation of **distributed dual-modality tactile perception** is a key advancement. By embedding both piezoresistive (static) and piezoelectric (dynamic) sensors across the distal and middle phalanges of all five fingers, the hand gains a more comprehensive understanding of its interaction with the environment. This distributed sensing capability, mimicking human mechanoreceptors, enables the simultaneous detection of sustained contact forces and transient events like initial contact, slip, or changes in dynamic interaction. This rich tactile feedback is crucial for intelligent grasping strategies, adaptive manipulation, and potential applications such as object identification through tactile exploration or fine-tuning grip force to prevent object damage or slippage.

Thirdly, the **integrated actuation-perception architecture** provides a foundation for more sophisticated control strategies in robotic manipulation. The ability to synchronously execute motion commands and acquire multi-channel tactile data in parallel allows for real-time feedback loops. This facilitates the development of control algorithms that can adapt motions based on tactile feedback, leading to more robust and dexterous manipulation, especially in unstructured or dynamic environments where visual information alone may be insufficient.

The successful validation of dexterity through various manipulation tasks (counting gestures, pinch actions, grasp operations) and the clear demonstration of tactile perception capabilities (distinguishing static vs. dynamic contact, identifying contact sequences) suggest the hand's potential for complex applications in robotics. This work contributes to the broader goal of embodied artificial intelligence, where robots can interact with their environment in a more human-like, intuitive, and adaptable manner. Future applications could include intricate assembly tasks, human-robot collaboration, surgical procedures, or domestic assistance, where fine motor control and tactile sensitivity are paramount. The methodology and design principles presented could serve as a basis for further research in haptic feedback systems, learning from demonstration, and reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation, ultimately improving the operational performance and dexterity of future robotic systems.
