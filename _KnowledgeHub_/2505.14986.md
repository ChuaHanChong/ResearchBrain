# AnyBody: A Benchmark Suite for Cross-Embodiment Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Princeton University researchers developed "AnyBody," a benchmark suite for cross-embodiment manipulation, revealing that while multi-embodiment policies can achieve competitive performance on seen robots and interpolation tasks, they largely fail at zero-shot generalization to entirely novel or compositionally different robot structures. This highlights the limitations of current methods in achieving broad morphological adaptability for manipulation.

## Problem

- A lack of standardized, comprehensive benchmarks for systematically evaluating cross-embodiment learning in robotic manipulation tasks.
- The inability of current robotic control policies to generalize effectively to novel physical embodiments, requiring extensive retraining for each new robot.
- Existing large-scale datasets often confound morphological generalization with complex perception challenges, while simulation benchmarks typically feature limited robot morphological variation and focus on locomotion over manipulation.

## Method

- Introduced **AnyBody**, a benchmark suite within ==NVIDIA Isaac-Sim== featuring **18 diverse robot variations** (procedural and real-world) and two fundamental manipulation tasks (==reach== and ==push==).
- Defined clear generalization axes: ==interpolation== (geometric variations within a category), ==extrapolation== (fundamentally different link structures), and ==composition== (novel component combinations).
- Evaluated ==multi-embodiment (ME)== policies, including Transformer-based architectures, against single-embodiment (SE) baselines, incorporating stable RL techniques like ==symlog returns== and discrete action spaces.

## Results

- ME-Transformer agents achieved comparable or superior multi-task scores on training morphologies and demonstrated strong zero-shot generalization for interpolation tasks, often matching or surpassing SE baselines.
- For extrapolation and composition, ME agents showed low success rates (e.g., **0%** for `Arms-push` in extrapolation), lagging far behind SE baselines — a critical failure to generalize to truly novel morphologies.
- Adoption of `symlog` returns and discrete action spaces boosted ME-Transformer performance by **4×** multi-task and **2×** zero-shot for `Arm3 reach`, proving crucial for stable multi-embodiment learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Multi-embodiment (ME) training can achieve comparable or even superior multi-task performance on *seen* robots compared to single-embodiment policies, benefiting from a regularizing effect leading to smoother learning.
> - Zero-shot generalization is feasible and strong for interpolation tasks, where agents adapt to geometric variations within known morphological categories.
> - However, zero-shot generalization remains a significant challenge for extrapolation and composition tasks, indicating that current methods struggle to reason about entirely novel link structures or novel combinations of components.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The ability to generalize control policies across a wide array of robot embodiments is a cornerstone challenge in robotics, crucial for achieving scalability, transferability, and ultimately, more general artificial intelligence. Humans inherently possess this capability, effortlessly adapting to operate tools and machines of varying shapes and functionalities. Mimicking this capacity in robots would enable agents to learn from heterogeneous datasets and deploy policies across different robotic platforms without exhaustive retraining.

However, the authors identify a significant gap in the current research landscape: the lack of standardized, comprehensive benchmarks for systematically evaluating cross-embodiment learning, particularly in manipulation tasks.

Existing work in related areas includes:
- **Large-scale cross-embodiment datasets:** Initiatives like Open-X and DROID have amassed vast amounts of data from diverse robots. While impressive few-shot or zero-shot performance on unseen embodiments has been demonstrated using models trained on these datasets, the efficacy of *true* zero-shot generalization remains difficult to assess, as evaluation robots are often part of the larger pretraining data. Furthermore, these datasets frequently introduce complex perception challenges (e.g., visual inputs), which can confound the study of morphological generalization in isolation.
- **Simulation benchmarks:** The robotics community has developed numerous simulation benchmarks (e.g., Meta-World, ManiSkill2, RoboSuite) for various manipulation skills and complex tasks. However, these typically focus on a limited set of robots with minimal morphological variation, primarily aiming to study general skill learning rather than morphological adaptability. While some benchmarks, such as those used by Gupta et al. for Metamorph, have explored morphology-aware learning, they are predominantly designed for locomotion tasks, leaving a void for manipulation.
- **Morphology-aware learning approaches:** Several research efforts have explored incorporating explicit morphology information into policy learning, often within a multi-task learning framework to train universal policies. Works like "Hardware Conditioned Policies," "Know Thyself," "Body Transformer," and "NerveNet" have shown promise. Yet, their evaluations often suffer from using a restricted number of robot variations (e.g., 4-7 robots) or being highly tailored to the specific methods proposed, making cross-method comparisons and systematic progress difficult.

"AnyBody" directly addresses these limitations by introducing a benchmark specifically designed to:
1. **Encompass a broad range of robot morphologies:** Moving beyond a few similar robotic arms to include abstract and procedurally generated robots alongside real-world counterparts.
2. **Define clear generalization axes:** Systematically testing interpolation (within a category), extrapolation (across categories with different link structures), and composition (recombining known components in novel ways).
3. **Focus on fundamental manipulation tasks:** Providing a controlled environment to study the impact of morphology without being overwhelmed by visual complexity.

By providing this standardized and diverse benchmark, "AnyBody" aims to enable more rigorous evaluation of cross-embodiment learning methods, allowing researchers to measure progress, compare approaches effectively, and pinpoint the specific challenges in generalizing manipulation skills across vastly different robot bodies. This work is a crucial step toward isolating and understanding the "morphology axis" of generalization in embodied AI.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to tackle the fundamental challenge of enabling robotic control policies to generalize effectively to novel physical embodiments. The authors are driven by both practical and scientific motivations:

**Practical Motivations:**
- **Scalability and Transferability:** The current paradigm often requires extensive retraining for each new robot or environment. Cross-embodiment learning promises to scale robot training by allowing policies to leverage large, heterogeneous datasets and deploy across diverse platforms (e.g., different lab setups or commercial robots) with minimal or zero retraining. This significantly reduces development costs and deployment time.
- **Exploiting Morphology Information:** Many large-scale training setups overlook explicit robot morphology information in their inputs. The authors are motivated to investigate whether incorporating this crucial data can unlock capabilities like zero-shot generalization, offering an efficient alternative to common reliance on fine-tuning for new robots.

**Scientific/Intelligence Motivations:**
- **General Intelligence:** The ability to understand and operate a wide variety of tools and machines, irrespective of their specific physical form or action space, is a hallmark of human general intelligence. By enabling robots to reason over varied action spaces and control diverse embodiments, this research contributes to the broader goal of building more generally intelligent artificial agents.

**Specific Objectives of the AnyBody Benchmark and Evaluation:**
To achieve these motivations, the authors design and leverage "AnyBody" to answer several key questions:
1. **Benchmark Introduction:** To introduce `AnyBody`, a comprehensive suite of simulated environments featuring a diverse range of robot morphologies and two foundational manipulation tasks (reach and push). The benchmark is designed to systematically evaluate generalization along three axes: interpolation, extrapolation, and composition.
2. **Performance Comparison:** To determine whether morphology-aware multi-embodiment (ME) training can outperform traditional single-embodiment (SE) baselines, particularly on in-distribution tasks.
3. **Zero-Shot Generalization Feasibility:** To assess the feasibility and limits of zero-shot generalization to *unseen* robot morphologies across different generalization regimes (interpolation, extrapolation, and composition).
4. **Influence of Design Choices:** To investigate how different architectural choices (e.g., MLP vs. Transformer) and training design decisions (e.g., symlog returns, discrete actions, task re-weighting) impact policy generalization capabilities.
5. **Provision of Open-Source Tools:** To provide an open-source codebase that extends IsaacSim with multi-task training capabilities, thereby facilitating further research in this area.

Through this systematic study, the authors aim to highlight the current limitations of multi-embodiment learning and provide concrete insights into effective strategies for designing policies that can adapt to new robot bodies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces `AnyBody`, a benchmark suite implemented within the NVIDIA Isaac-Sim simulator, along with a custom codebase that enables multi-task training not natively supported by IsaacLab. This foundation allows for a systematic study of cross-embodiment manipulation.

#### 3.1. Benchmark Design (AnyBody)

- **Tasks:** Two core manipulation tasks are selected:
 - **Reach:** The robot's end-effector must move to a randomly specified 3D target position. This tests spatial understanding and precise joint control.
 - **Push:** The robot must push a block from one side of the workspace to another. This involves physical interaction and understanding object manipulation.
- **Scene Complexity:** Tasks can optionally include obstacles to increase difficulty and test obstacle avoidance capabilities.
- **Input Types:** The benchmark supports both state-based and point cloud-based representations for environmental objects, though experiments in the paper primarily use state-based inputs for clarity.
- **Robot Diversity:** `AnyBody` features 18 distinct robot variations, including:
 - 8 procedurally generated categories (e.g., simple `Arm3`, `NLink`, `Chain`, `Prims` from basic shapes) to control complexity.
 - 10 real-world robot arms (e.g., Franka Panda, UR5, Kinova, Jaco, XArm, WidowX) to provide realistic challenges. This diversity spans a wide range of degrees of freedom, link structures, and affordances.
- **Generalization Axes:** The benchmark defines clear train/test splits for three generalization categories:
 - **Interpolation:** Agents are trained and tested on robots from the same morphological category, but with geometric variations (e.g., `Arm3` with unseen link lengths, `Panda` with scaled dimensions). This tests generalization within a known structural family.
 - **Extrapolation:** Agents are trained on multiple robot categories and tested on a robot with a *fundamentally different link structure* (e.g., training on primitive robots and testing on a `Chain`; training on several real arms and testing on an entirely novel one like `WidowX`). This assesses generalization to entirely new morphologies.
 - **Composition:** The test morphology is constructed from *components* seen during training but in a *novel combination or task context*. This includes `EE-Arms` (train on arms + various end-effectors, test on new arm-end-effector combo) and `EE-Task Transfer` (train one robot for push, another for reach, then test a combined robot for a combined task). This specifically probes compositional reasoning about robot parts.

#### 3.2. Modeling and Policy Design

- **Problem Formulation:** The problem is framed as a multi-task learning challenge where a single policy `π` must operate across a set of different Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), each corresponding to a different robot.
- **State Space (`s_t`):** Comprises the robot's state (`s_r`), environment observation (`o_e`), and a goal token (`g_r`) for reach tasks.
 - **Robot State (`s_r`):** Each robot is represented as a sequence of links from base to end-effector. Each link's state is a 48-dimensional vector encoding link index, parent index, end-effector flag, geometry type (primitive fit), link origin, joint axis, joint origin, joint type (prismatic/revolute/fixed), and sinusoidal encoding of joint value (`q`).
- **Action Space:** Joint-space control, where the policy outputs changes in joint positions (`∆q`). An action mask is applied to ensure only movable joints are controlled. The authors argue against end-effector control due to collision issues and the potential for other links to interact with objects.
- **Baselines:** Two main categories of RL agents are evaluated:
 - **Single-Embodiment (SE) agents:** Trained with a dedicated actor-critic network for each robot morphology. These serve as a performance upper bound for that specific robot, but cannot generalize.
 - **Multi-Embodiment (ME) agents:** Use a single actor-critic network trained across all training morphologies, designed for generalization.
 - **ME-MLP:** A simple multi-layer perceptron that flattens projected features of links and environment objects.
 - **ME-Transformer (ME-Tf):** Uses a Transformer encoder, taking d-dimensional tokens for each link and object, along with an observation mask. The encoder outputs feature vectors for each link, which are then projected to action values. This architecture is inspired by prior work on morphology-aware learning.
- **RL Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used, chosen for its stability and robustness to hyperparameter tuning compared to other algorithms like SAC or TRPO.

#### 3.3. Training Considerations

- **Simulation Scale:** Training involves 1280 parallel environments in Isaac-Sim, distributed equally among the training morphologies to ensure consistent interaction counts.
- **Reward Stabilization:** To counter varying reward distributions and sharp reward spikes (especially in reach tasks), the authors adopt:
 - **Symlog Transformation:** Predicting the symlog of returns (from DreamerV3) and using its inverse (symexp) for target critic values.
 - **Slow Critic Updates:** Using an exponential moving average of critic weights to further stabilize learning.
- **Action Space Discretization:** For the `reach` task, discrete actions (logits over exponentially spaced bins around zero) for `∆q` are used. This simplifies learning zero outputs and reduces jitter, leading to faster convergence for precise tasks. Continuous actions are used for `push`.
- **Curriculum Learning:** For the `push` task, a curriculum is applied by gradually increasing the target y-value for the block, mitigating the sparse reward problem.
- **Computational Resources:** Policies are trained on single GPUs (NVIDIA 3090s or L40s), with training times ranging from 1 day (MLP) to 4 days (Transformer) for 1 million steps.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics

- **Multi-task Score (MT):** Average score across all *training* morphologies, indicating how well the agent optimizes its learning objective for seen robots.
- **Zero-shot Score (ZS):** Average score across *unseen testing* morphologies, directly measuring generalization capability.
- **Reach Task:** Average end-effector-to-goal (EE-goal) reward over 10k evaluation steps, relative to a random agent.
- **Push Task:** Success rate (proportion of episodes where the block is successfully pushed) over 10k evaluation steps.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provide crucial insights into the current state of cross-embodiment generalization in manipulation, addressing the key questions posed by the authors:

**Q1. Can morphology-aware ME training outperform single-embodiment baselines?**
- **Multi-task Performance (MT Score):** ME agents, particularly the Transformer-based variants (`ME-Tf`), generally achieve comparable or even superior multi-task scores compared to SE agents across most training morphologies. This suggests that multi-embodiment training can effectively optimize for performance across a diverse set of *seen* robots.
- **Learning Stability:** ME training appears to provide a regularizing effect, leading to smoother and more stable learning curves, and potentially reducing the chance of converging to poor local minima, as observed in the Panda-push task.
- **Architectural Advantage:** The Transformer-based architecture consistently outperforms MLP-based ME agents, indicating that its ability to process sequential, tokenized link information is beneficial for learning across diverse morphologies.

**Q2. Is zero-shot generalization to unseen morphologies feasible?**
- **Interpolation:** Zero-shot generalization is *feasible and often strong* for interpolation tasks (e.g., `Arm3`). Here, ME agents can match or even surpass the performance of SE agents, demonstrating an ability to generalize to geometrically varied robots within a known structural category.
- **Extrapolation and Composition:** Zero-shot generalization *remains a significant challenge* for extrapolation and composition tasks. ME agents show low success rates and often lag far behind SE baselines. For instance, in the `Arms-push` task (extrapolation), ME agents essentially fail with a 0% success rate. This highlights that current multi-embodiment training methods struggle to reason about entirely novel link structures or novel combinations of components.
- **Fine-tuning:** While fine-tuning on test morphologies significantly improves performance compared to training from scratch, even 30,000 steps of fine-tuning often falls short of the performance achieved by SE agents. This suggests that while multi-embodiment training provides a better initialization or more general control understanding, there's still a substantial gap in fully adaptable policies.

**Q3. How does agent performance compare across different tasks and benchmark categories?**
- **Task Difficulty:**
 - `Arm3` (interpolation) is generally easy, with high MT and ZS scores.
 - `Panda` (interpolation) is more challenging for both reach and push due to its complex link structure.
 - `Composition` tasks are harder than `interpolation`, with noticeable performance gaps between SE and ME agents. `EE-Arm` is more challenging than `EE-Task` due to inter-task complexity.
 - `Extrapolation` tasks are the most challenging. `Prims` is easier than `Arms`, though still difficult for `push`. `Arms` is consistently the hardest for both `reach` and `push`.
- **Task-Specific Sensitivity:** `Reach` tasks become significantly harder with increased morphological complexity (making precise joint control for goal reaching difficult). `Push` tasks, surprisingly, seem less affected by robot complexity, although they can be challenging in `extrapolation` scenarios. There are also task-specific performance patterns (e.g., `EE-Arm` struggles with `reach` but performs well in `push`, while `Prims` shows the opposite).

**Q4. How significantly do policy architectures and learning choices affect these outcomes?**
- **Reward Normalization and Stability:** The use of `symlog` returns and slow critic updates significantly boosts `ME-Tf` performance (e.g., 4x MT, 2x ZS for `Arm3 reach`), indicating their criticality for stable learning across diverse reward scales.
- **Action Space:** A discrete action space proves highly advantageous for the `reach` task, promoting faster convergence and more precise, stable goal reaching by mitigating jitter.
- **Combined Impact:** The combination of `symlog` and discrete actions is crucial for the `ME-Tf` agent to outperform SE baselines even in simpler tasks like `Arm3`, highlighting the importance of these design choices.
- **Task Re-weighting:** While not strictly necessary for simpler tasks, it is retained for handling substantial reward variations across more diverse tasks.

In summary, the results demonstrate that while multi-embodiment training can achieve competitive in-distribution performance and shows promise for generalizing to minor morphological variations, it critically fails to generalize zero-shot to entirely novel or compositionally different robot structures. This suggests that current methods, even Transformer-based ones, are not yet learning a robust, compositional understanding of robot morphology that enables broad, zero-shot generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "AnyBody" benchmark represents a significant contribution to the field of robotics and embodied AI, addressing a critical unmet need for standardized evaluation of cross-embodiment manipulation. Its primary impact stems from its ability to systematically probe policy generalization across diverse robot morphologies and distinct generalization axes.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**
1. **Standardized Benchmark:** "AnyBody" fills a crucial void by offering the first comprehensive, multi-robot manipulation benchmark specifically designed for morphology generalization. Unlike previous works that used limited robot sets or focused on locomotion, "AnyBody" provides a diverse collection of 18 robot variations and clear train/test splits for interpolation, extrapolation, and compositional generalization. This standardization will enable rigorous comparison of future morphology-aware learning methods.
2. **Open-Source Codebase:** The provision of an open-source codebase that extends IsaacSim with multi-task training capabilities is invaluable for the research community, lowering the barrier to entry for researchers exploring this challenging domain.
3. **Systematic Evaluation and Insights:** The systematic evaluation of current RL agents on "AnyBody" provides a clear baseline and highlights the strengths and weaknesses of existing multi-embodiment learning approaches. The findings unequivocally demonstrate that while multi-task training can improve in-distribution performance and enable generalization to minor morphological variations (interpolation), it *critically struggles with zero-shot generalization to novel link structures (extrapolation) and new combinations of components (composition)*.
4. **Implications for Foundation Models:** This work casts a critical light on the claims of "generalist" robotic policies and foundation models. If even sophisticated Transformer-based agents struggle with zero-shot generalization to truly novel morphologies, it suggests that these models might not yet possess the fundamental ability to *reason* about morphology and adapt without extensive fine-tuning or prior exposure to similar embodiments. This finding is vital for guiding future development of scalable and truly transferable robotic intelligence.
5. **Guidance for Future Research:** The benchmark and its results directly illuminate several promising avenues for future research:
 - **Novel Policy Architectures:** The limitations of generic MLPs and Transformers suggest a need for specialized architectures that can process morphology in more semantically meaningful chunks, rather than treating it as a flat sequence of tokens.
 - **Improved Morphology Representations:** Current methods rely on approximating link geometries with primitive shapes. More accurate and richer representations of robot bodies could unlock better generalization.
 - **Efficient Morphology-Aware Training:** Developing training strategies that can efficiently identify and leverage informative morphologies or intelligently weigh learning objectives across different embodiments is crucial to improve learning efficiency and performance.
 - **Integration with Multi-Task Learning:** Exploring how advanced multi-task learning techniques can be directly applied or adapted to the multi-embodiment problem.
 - **Expanding Task Complexity:** The authors acknowledge that current tasks are relatively simple and plan to extend the benchmark with more complex articulated object manipulation tasks, which will further push the boundaries of generalization.

**Limitations Acknowledged by the Authors:**
- **Focus on Morphology:** The benchmark primarily isolates morphological generalization by largely omitting visual variations and using state-based inputs. While this allows for a focused study, integrating visual inputs and aligning them with morphological understanding remains a critical challenge for real-world deployment.
- **Task Simplicity:** While foundational, the current manipulation tasks (reach, push) are still relatively basic. The authors aim to add more complex tasks (e.g., articulated object manipulation) in future work.
- **Computational Cost:** Training RL policies for such diverse variations and tasks is computationally intensive, requiring significant GPU resources. This highlights the need for more efficient adaptation methods.

In conclusion, "AnyBody" provides a much-needed robust framework for evaluating a core challenge in robotics. Its findings underscore that while multi-embodiment learning is making progress, achieving robust zero-shot generalization to *arbitrary* novel robot bodies, a prerequisite for truly general robotic intelligence, remains an open and difficult problem. This work serves as a critical stepping stone and a powerful diagnostic tool for guiding future advancements toward robots that can truly "control any body."
