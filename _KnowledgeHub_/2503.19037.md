# Evolutionary Policy Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University's Robotics Institute developed Evolutionary Policy Optimization (EPO), a hybrid algorithm integrating genetic algorithms with policy optimization to enhance scalability and sample efficiency in reinforcement learning. EPO consistently achieves superior performance and robustness across diverse manipulation and locomotion tasks, effectively scaling with increased computational resources without performance saturation.

## Problem

- On-policy reinforcement learning algorithms exhibit limited scalability and data diversity in parallel environments, leading to redundant data and diminishing returns from increased computational resources.
- Existing Evolutionary RL methods often suffer from sample inefficiency (e.g., gradient-free evolutionary strategies) or degraded asymptotic performance due to distribution shift (e.g., CEM-RL).
- Population-Based Training (PBT) trains policies in isolation, missing opportunities to amortize experience across the entire population and leading to inefficient data utilization.

## Method

- **EPO** employs a population of K agents, each with a unique ==latent 'gene' embedding==, that share a common actor-critic network, preventing parameter bloat while allowing diverse behaviors.
- A ==genetic algorithm== manages the latent embeddings of non-master agents, using evaluation, selection, crossover, and mutation to generate diverse yet effective policies.
- A ==hybrid policy optimization== strategy updates the master agent using its own on-policy data combined with off-policy data from other agents, while non-master agents are updated purely with their individual on-policy data for stability.

## Results

- EPO achieved higher success rates and improved sample efficiency on complex manipulation tasks (e.g., **35.8** episode successes in two-arm reorientation after **1x10^10** steps, surpassing SAPG's **28.58** successes after **5x10^10** steps), where most baselines failed to acquire meaningful behaviors.
- The genetic algorithm component was crucial for stable training and superior performance, particularly in challenging tasks, by filtering underperforming agents and preventing performance degradation seen in methods without it.
- EPO demonstrated lower performance variance across different random seeds and exhibited strong scaling, continuously improving performance as the total number of environments increased from **6,144** to **49,152**, overcoming saturation observed in other on-policy methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating a genetic algorithm with policy optimization enables stable and effective training in massively parallel environments by systematically exploring diverse behaviors and pruning underperforming agents, leveraging higher-quality data.
> - The use of a shared actor-critic network conditioned on latent embeddings allows for controlled diversity within a population without unbounded policy divergence or significant parameter bloat.
> - A hybrid policy update mechanism, combining a master agent learning from both on-policy and off-policy aggregated data with non-master agents learning individually on-policy, enhances sample efficiency while preserving asymptotic performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated effectiveness in various domains, including games, robotics, and large language model alignment. However, RL methods, particularly on-policy algorithms, typically exhibit sample inefficiency due to the practice of discarding past experience after each policy update. On-policy model-free methods, such as policy gradients, are valued for their asymptotic performance and training stability, leading to their adoption in data-rich simulated environments. Nevertheless, these algorithms encounter scalability challenges with larger batch sizes; increasing parallel environments does not consistently lead to greater data diversity. The data distribution generated by a single policy quickly converges, resulting in correlated trajectories rather than varied experiences, which limits the benefit of additional computational resources.

Evolutionary Algorithms (EAs) offer a complementary approach by naturally scaling and fostering exploration through population-based randomized search. The integration of EAs with RL, termed Evolutionary RL (EvoRL), involves maintaining a population of policies and refining them using evolutionary operators. Historically, EvoRL has faced limitations. Classic gradient-free evolutionary strategies (ES) are highly parallelizable but often sample-inefficient. Methods like CEM-RL attempt to improve efficiency with off-policy updates but can experience degraded asymptotic performance due to distribution shift. Population-Based Training (PBT) ensembles multiple on-policy agents, but each agent operates in isolation, leading to an inefficient use of collective experience.

This research addresses the limitations of both on-policy RL and existing EvoRL methods. It aims to develop an algorithm that can scale with parallel environments, efficiently learn from shared experience, and maintain the high asymptotic returns and stability characteristic of on-policy approaches, thereby bridging a gap in current scalable RL methodologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work arises from identified challenges within existing reinforcement learning paradigms, specifically regarding the scalability and efficiency of on-policy methods and the sample efficiency and stability issues of evolutionary algorithms.

The authors articulate two core problems motivating their research:
1. **Limited Scalability and Diversity in On-Policy RL:** On-policy reinforcement learning algorithms, while offering strong asymptotic performance and training stability, demonstrate suboptimal scaling with increased batch sizes and parallel environments. This is attributed to a lack of diversity in data collection; policies tend to generate highly correlated trajectories, rendering additional parallel environments redundant rather than beneficial for broader exploration or faster learning. This inefficiency restricts the potential of on-policy methods in large-scale simulation settings where vast computational resources are available.
2. **Sample Inefficiency and Distribution Shift in Evolutionary RL:** Evolutionary Algorithms are inherently scalable and promote exploration through their population-based search mechanisms. However, existing EvoRL approaches often suffer from sample inefficiency. Gradient-free evolutionary strategies can be as simple as random search, while methods attempting to improve efficiency with off-policy updates, like CEM-RL, may degrade asymptotic performance due to distribution shift. Furthermore, approaches like Population-Based Training (PBT) train policies in isolation, leading to a missed opportunity to amortize experience across the entire population, thereby wasting valuable data.

In response to these challenges, the authors propose the development of an algorithm that can synthesize the strengths of both evolutionary algorithms and policy gradients. The key objectives of this proposed algorithm, Evolutionary Policy Optimization (EPO), are:
- To **scale effectively** with an increasing number of parallel environments, overcoming the data redundancy issue prevalent in traditional on-policy RL.
- To **learn efficiently from shared experience**, ensuring that data collected by diverse agents within a population is leveraged effectively to accelerate the learning process, addressing the experience amortization problem in some EvoRL methods.
- To **preserve the high asymptotic returns and stability** that are hallmarks of on-policy reinforcement learning, avoiding the performance degradation seen in some off-policy EvoRL variants.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to introduce a robust, efficient, and scalable RL algorithm suitable for complex, data-rich environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Evolutionary Policy Optimization (EPO) is a hybrid algorithm designed to integrate the population-based exploration capabilities of Genetic Algorithms (GAs) with the performance and stability of policy gradient methods, specifically Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). While the framework is instantiated with PPO, the authors state its potential applicability to other on-policy actor-critic RL algorithms.

The EPO approach involves several key components:

#### 3.1. Population-Based Agent Structure

EPO initializes a population of `K` agents. Each agent is characterized by a unique latent embedding, referred to as its "gene" (ϕ_k ∈ R^N). A central design principle is that all agents share a common actor-critic network, parameterized by θ (actor) and ψ (critic). This shared network acts as the "gene-expression rules," which aims to prevent parameter bloat as the population size increases, allowing for efficient training and storage of large neural networks. The actor network is conditioned on both the current state and the agent's unique latent embedding to generate actions, while the value function is similarly conditioned. One agent within this population is designated as the "master" agent (π_1).

#### 3.2. Genetic Algorithm for Agent Selection

A genetic algorithm is employed to manage and evolve the latent embeddings of the non-master agents. This process aims to generate diverse yet effective behaviors within the population.
- **Evaluation:** Each agent interacts with its assigned environment for a complete episode, after which its performance is quantified by a fitness value (e.g., cumulative reward or success rate).
- **Selection:** Based on these fitness scores, a selection operator determines which agents proceed to the next generation. The top `x` performing non-master agents are designated as "elites" and are carried over unchanged to the next generation.
- **Adaptive Genetic Updates:** Genetic operations (selection, crossover, and mutation) are not applied uniformly at every iteration. For complex tasks where initial performance differences may be minimal (e.g., all rewards near zero), these updates are only executed when the difference between the maximum and minimum fitness scores among non-master agents exceeds a certain proportion of the median fitness score (Equation 3). This condition ensures that evolutionary pressure is applied only when meaningful behavioral differentiation has emerged, though fixed-interval updates are also mentioned as an alternative.
- **Crossover:** New latent embeddings are generated from selected elite agents. Given two elite latent embeddings, ϕ_i and ϕ_j, a new embedding ϕ' is produced by averaging: ϕ' = 1/2 × (ϕ_i + ϕ_j). The authors note that other crossover methods were tested, showing robustness to the specific choice.
- **Mutation:** Following crossover, a mutation step introduces Gaussian noise to the new embedding: ϕ'' = ϕ' + ϵ, where ϵ is sampled from a normal distribution N(0, σ^2).
The new agent with the mutated latent embedding is then added to the population for the subsequent generation. This GA process focuses solely on modifying the latent embeddings while the shared network parameters remain fixed during this phase.

#### 3.3. Hybrid-Policy Optimization

All parameters (θ, ψ, and the latent embeddings {ϕ_k}) are updated through a hybrid policy optimization strategy inspired by the Split and Aggregate Policy Gradient (SAPG) formulation. This strategy allows the master agent to learn from diverse data while non-master agents maintain stable learning.
- **Master Agent (π_1) Update:** The master agent's parameters are updated using a combination of its own on-policy data and off-policy data aggregated from the trajectories of the non-master agents.
 - **Off-Policy Loss (L_off):** A set of transitions (S'_1) is sampled from the combined replay buffers of all non-master agents (∪ K_k=2 S_k). To correct for distribution shift when using this off-policy data, importance sampling ratios (r_π1) and an off-policy correction term (μ) are applied, along with PPO's clipping mechanism (Equation 4).
 - **Total Policy Loss:** The master agent's total policy loss combines its standard on-policy PPO loss (L_on, Equation 2) with the aggregated off-policy loss, weighted by a hyperparameter λ (L(π_1) = L_on(π_1) + λ · L_off(π_1, S'_1)).
 - **Critic Update:** The master agent's critic is also updated using a hybrid approach. On-policy data uses n-step returns, while off-policy data utilizes a 1-step return approximation. The final critic loss is a weighted sum of these on-policy and off-policy components.
- **Non-Master Agent Updates (π_k, k ≥ 2):** To ensure stability and preserve diversity, non-master agents are updated exclusively using their individual on-policy losses, derived from their own collected trajectories (Equations 2 and 6). This prevents potential destabilization from direct off-policy learning across highly divergent policies.

This methodology aims to leverage the exploration capabilities of a diverse population while maintaining the learning stability and performance benefits of on-policy gradient methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of EPO encompassed eight diverse and challenging environments, including dexterous manipulation, legged locomotion, and classic control tasks. The algorithm was benchmarked against several state-of-the-art RL methods: SAC, Parallel Q-Learning (PQL), PPO, SAPG (with 64 and 6 agents), PBT (with 64 and 8 agents), and CEM-RL. All comparative experiments used 24,576 environments and identical network architectures, with population-based methods standardized to 64 agents where applicable. Each experiment was conducted with 5 different random seeds.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparison

EPO demonstrated consistent and notable performance advantages over all baselines across the tested environments.
- **Manipulation Tasks:** On simpler manipulation tasks (e.g., single-arm regrasping and throwing), EPO achieved higher success rates than PPO, SAPG, and PBT. For more complex tasks requiring two-arm coordination (e.g., Two-Arm Reorientation, Two-Arm Reorientation with Obstacle), EPO exhibited significant learning progress, while most baselines failed to acquire meaningful behaviors, even after extensive training steps. For instance, EPO achieved 35.8 episode successes in two-arm reorientation with 1 × 10^10 steps, surpassing SAPG's reported 28.58 successes after 5 × 10^10 steps, indicating improved sample efficiency.
- **Locomotion Tasks:** In locomotion, EPO matched baselines on simpler tasks like flat-ground ANYmal walking but substantially outperformed them on complex parkour tasks requiring more extensive exploration (e.g., Unitree A1 Parkour).
- **Classic Control:** In the DeepMind Control Suite Humanoid task, while all methods showed reasonable performance, EPO yielded marginally higher returns.

#### 4.2. Role of the Genetic Algorithm

The inclusion of the genetic algorithm (GA) was identified as a critical component. A comparison between SAPG 64 and EPO showed that SAPG, which uses a hybrid-policy update without the GA, did not consistently benefit from large-scale simulation and sometimes experienced performance degradation with an increased number of agents. This was attributed to the introduction of a higher proportion of poorly performing policies. In contrast, EPO's GA component, by filtering underperforming agents and leveraging higher-quality data, contributed to stable and effective training, particularly in challenging tasks like two-arm manipulation.

#### 4.3. Robustness

EPO exhibited lower performance variance across different random seeds compared to baseline methods. This indicates a degree of robustness to hyperparameter variations and initialization, which is a common challenge in RL algorithm deployment.

#### 4.4. Comparison with Evolutionary RL Baselines

- **PBT:** PBT's efficiency decreased on more complex tasks. Each agent learns from its own experience, and data from eliminated agents is discarded. This limitation meant PBT did not significantly benefit from larger population sizes (PBT 64 and PBT 8 showed similar performance).
- **CEM-RL:** This method's reliance on off-policy updates, combined with poor initial performance from many agents, led to an accumulation of low-quality data and degraded asymptotic performance across a range of tasks.
EPO addressed these limitations by combining its hybrid-policy update mechanism with the GA's selective pressure.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

- **Population Size:** Experiments on a single-arm reorientation task, with a fixed total number of environments (N = 24,576), showed that increasing the population size from K=8 to K=64 generally improved EPO's performance. The authors attribute this to the GA's ability to prune underperforming agents, ensuring that increased diversity does not lead to noisier data. The shared actor-critic network also contributed to controlled diversity and stability.
- **Crossover Strategy:** EPO demonstrated robustness to different crossover strategies (simple averaging, uniform crossover, fitness-weighted averaging), with comparable success rates across these methods.

#### 4.6. Scaling Law

EPO demonstrated effective scaling with increased computational resources. By fixing the number of environments per agent (384) and scaling the number of agents from K=16 to K=128 (resulting in total environments scaling from 6,144 to 49,152), EPO continued to improve performance. This observation indicates that EPO can effectively leverage large training batches, overcoming the saturation typically observed in other on-policy RL methods beyond a certain batch size.

#### 4.7. Computational Cost

The computational cost analysis (Appendix A) indicated that EPO had a slightly higher frames-per-second (fps) cost compared to PBT and SAPG due to the overhead of calculating fitness scores and determining genetic update iterations. However, its GPU memory usage was comparable to SAPG (~24GB for 64 agents) and significantly less than PBT (~120GB for 64 agents). Increasing the number of agents in EPO did not substantially impact memory usage or inference time, as the additional cost primarily involved a small number of latent embeddings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of Evolutionary Policy Optimization (EPO) introduces a hybrid reinforcement learning algorithm that addresses critical limitations in existing methods. By integrating genetic algorithms with policy optimization, EPO demonstrates a capability to scale effectively with computational resources while maintaining high asymptotic performance and stability.

The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to overcome the sample inefficiency of traditional on-policy RL algorithms when deployed in massively parallel environments, where redundant data often limits the benefits of increased computational power. Simultaneously, EPO mitigates the sample inefficiency and potential performance degradation associated with distribution shift in various evolutionary RL approaches. This combination allows for a more efficient utilization of simulation data, which is particularly relevant in data-rich domains such as robotics simulations and game engines.

The empirical results across a diverse suite of challenging tasks—ranging from dexterous manipulation to complex locomotion—indicate that EPO can achieve superior performance and sample efficiency compared to several state-of-the-art baselines. Its demonstrated robustness to hyperparameter variations and its ability to scale with increasing data without performance saturation suggest a reliable and scalable approach for large-scale RL training. The shared actor-critic architecture, combined with the genetic algorithm's management of latent embeddings, offers a mechanism for controlled diversity within a population, preventing unbounded policy divergence while promoting exploration.

The authors suggest that EPO's focus on scaling with training data is complementary to efforts aimed at scaling performance through larger neural network architectures, implying potential synergy with future advancements in network design and regularization techniques.

From a broader perspective, advancements in scalable RL, such as EPO, could have implications for autonomous systems, decision-making AI, and robotics. This could influence labor markets, automation policies, and safety regulations. The authors emphasize the importance of ensuring that future applications of such methods adhere to ethical AI principles, particularly concerning fairness, transparency, and robustness in high-stakes decision-making contexts. The increased scalability could also accelerate progress in AI alignment and human-AI interaction, especially as RL is applied to large language models. However, the potential for misuse in generating persuasive or manipulative AI systems is acknowledged, underscoring the need for responsible application and ethical safeguards.
