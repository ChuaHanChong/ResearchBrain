# Subspace Inference Enables Efficient Active Reward Learning from Preferences

> [!summary] Summary
> PreferenceEKF casts active preference-based reward learning as sequential Bayesian filtering: an extended Kalman filter updates a Gaussian posterior inside a low-dimensional neural-network subspace built by SVD of SGD iterates, letting the method sample arbitrarily many reward models to score information-gain queries. Across 12 D4RL tasks it matches or beats four Bayesian deep learning baselines while training roughly 5-40x faster.

## Problem

- RLHF preference feedback carries at most one bit per query, so learning neural reward models needs thousands of comparisons, which does not scale to human annotators.
- Information-theoretic acquisition functions require sampling reward models from a parameter posterior, so prior active reward learning was restricted to linear or Gaussian-process models.
- Ensembles must train several independent networks and retrain on all past data, while dropout avoids that cost but is known to approximate the posterior poorly.

## Method

- **PreferenceEKF** treats network weights as hidden states with identity dynamics and a ==Bradley-Terry measurement model==, giving a closed-form ==extended Kalman filter== update per incoming preference query.
- ==Subspace inference== keeps the covariance tractable: an affine map from a 200-dimensional subspace to full weights, with the projection basis from ==SVD== of warm-up SGD iterates or random projection.
- Sampling from the subspace Gaussian and projecting back to full parameters yields arbitrarily many reward models, scaling the ==InfoGain acquisition function== to neural reward models.

## Results

- Bootstrap tests over **12 tasks** and **12 seeds** show active PreferenceEKF beats active DeepEnsemble, Dropout and Laplace on normalized log-likelihood AUC (**p<0.001**, Cohen's d **2.26-5.23**), matching LLMCMC.
- Training took **12.1 minutes** versus **97.7** for DeepEnsemble, **354.6** for Laplace and **780.2** for LLMCMC, with runtime scaling better as sample count and network size grow.
- It attained the lowest expected calibration error and second-lowest Brier score, and at matched **M=5** samples reached **-0.220** final likelihood against **-0.518** and **-0.774** for baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Overparameterized networks solve tasks in a small subspace, so classical filtering methods like the EKF become viable trainers once inference happens in that subspace.
> - Sequential Bayesian updates on only the newest query avoid the replay-all-data retraining that ensembles and Laplace need, which is where most of the speedup originates.
> - The unimodal Gaussian posterior fits one annotator only; the active variant also failed to beat random querying significantly, so uncertainty quality does not guarantee acquisition gains.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a prominent method for aligning decision-making agents with human intentions. Its popularity stems from the relative ease with which humans can provide preference feedback compared to direct reward signals or expert demonstrations. However, a primary challenge in RLHF is its sample inefficiency; each binary preference query provides limited information, often necessitating thousands of queries to learn an effective reward model (RM).

To address this sample inefficiency, active learning approaches are employed. These methods strategically select the most informative queries to ask, aiming to accelerate the reward learning process. A critical component for many active learning strategies is the accurate quantification of model uncertainty, which guides the selection of informative data points.

In the context of deep learning, effective uncertainty quantification (UQ) for large neural network (NN) reward models remains a challenge. Traditional Bayesian methods offer principled UQ but are computationally intensive and difficult to scale to high-dimensional neural networks. Alternative approaches include ensemble methods, which train multiple independent models and average their predictions, and dropout, which approximates Bayesian inference by sampling different network architectures during inference. While simpler, ensemble methods are computationally costly for large NNs, and the effectiveness of dropout for UQ has been a subject of debate.

Recent advancements in approximate Bayesian inference methods have enabled more scalable Bayesian deep learning techniques. This research contributes to this landscape by developing a method that aims to overcome the scalability limitations of principled Bayesian UQ for large neural networks in the context of active preference learning. The paper introduces an approach that leverages subspace inference within a sequential Bayesian filtering framework, specifically an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF), to maintain and update uncertainty over reward model parameters efficiently. This positions the work as an effort to integrate the theoretical advantages of Bayesian UQ with the practical demands of scaling to modern neural network architectures for sample-efficient RLHF. The authors state that this is the first work to apply subspace filtering for training neural network reward models from preference feedback.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the acknowledged limitations of current Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) paradigms, particularly concerning the efficiency of reward model learning from human preferences.

**Motivation:**
1. **Sample Inefficiency in RLHF:** RLHF is effective for aligning agents with human intentions but is notably sample-inefficient. Human preferences, often provided as binary comparisons, offer limited information per query. This necessitates a large number of queries (potentially thousands) to learn an accurate reward model, making the process time-consuming and expensive.
2. **Importance of Active Learning:** To mitigate sample inefficiency, active learning is crucial. It enables an agent to intelligently select queries that yield the most information about human preferences, thereby accelerating the learning process.
3. **Challenges in Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) for Large Neural Networks:** Effective active learning typically relies on probabilistic modeling of uncertainty to compute data acquisition functions. For large neural network (NN) reward models, accurately quantifying this uncertainty is a significant challenge.
4. **Limitations of Existing UQ Methods:**
 - **Bayesian Methods:** While principled, traditional Bayesian inference methods (e.g., posterior inference over the full NN parameter space) are computationally prohibitive for large NNs.
 - **Ensemble Methods:** Training multiple independent models for ensembles is computationally intensive, especially as NN reward models grow in size.
 - **Dropout:** Although computationally lighter than ensembles, the effectiveness of dropout for robust uncertainty quantification remains debated in the literature.
 - This landscape highlights a gap: a need for a method that offers both the principled UQ of Bayesian approaches and the scalability required for modern deep learning architectures.

**Key Objectives:**
1. **Develop PreferenceEKF:** Introduce a sample-efficient approach, termed PreferenceEKF, that tracks reward model uncertainty by formulating active preference learning as a sequential Bayesian filtering problem.
2. **Enable Subspace Inference:** Instead of performing computationally expensive posterior inference over the full neural network parameter space, the method aims to conduct sequential inference via an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) within a low-dimensional parameter subspace. This approach is intended to continuously update the reward model posterior as new preference queries arrive.
3. **Facilitate Scalable Parameter Sampling:** The low-dimensional subspace representation should enable scalable sampling of neural network parameters. This capability is crucial for efficiently computing common uncertainty-based acquisition functions, such as expected information gain (InfoGain), which are used to select informative preference queries.
4. **Empirical Validation:**
 - **Sample Efficiency and Calibration:** Evaluate whether PreferenceEKF achieves better sample efficiency and model calibration in preference modeling tasks compared to other Bayesian deep learning approaches.
 - **Runtime and Scalability:** Assess the computational advantages of PreferenceEKF, specifically its runtime and how it scales with model size and the number of posterior samples, relative to baselines.
 - **Downstream Policy Performance:** Determine if reward models learned using PreferenceEKF result in competitive policy performance when used for offline reinforcement learning.
 - **Robustness to Subspace Construction:** Investigate the impact of different subspace construction methods on the performance of PreferenceEKF.
 - **Generalizability:** Explore the applicability of the method to more complex scenarios, such as learning from image-based trajectories and sparse preference feedback.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces PreferenceEKF, a method for active preference-based reward learning that leverages subspace inference and an Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) within a sequential Bayesian framework.

#### 3.1. Preference-Based Reward Modeling

The foundational task involves learning a reward function, $r: S \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, from human preference feedback. Given a dataset of trajectories $D_{traj}$, human annotators provide binary preference labels $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ for queried pairs of trajectories, $Q_i = (\tau_a^i, \tau_b^i)$, indicating which trajectory is preferred. The goal is to approximate this underlying reward function with a parameterized neural network reward model $r_\theta$. The likelihood of a preference is typically modeled using the Bradley-Terry (BT) model:
$p_\theta(y | \tau_a, \tau_b) = \frac{\exp(\beta \cdot R_\theta(\tau_a))}{\exp(\beta \cdot R_\theta(\tau_a)) + \exp(\beta \cdot R_\theta(\tau_b))}$, where $\beta$ is a temperature parameter and $R_\theta(\tau_i) = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} r_\theta(s_{i,t})$ is the trajectory return.

#### 3.2. Information-Theoretic Active Learning

The method employs the InfoGain acquisition function for active preference-based reward learning. This function selects the query $Q_i$ that maximizes the mutual information between the query's preference label $y_i$ and the model parameter $\theta$, conditioned on previously observed data:
$Q_i^* = \arg \max_{Q_i} I(\theta; y_i | Q_i, b_{i-1})$, where $b_{i-1} = p(\theta | D_{1:i-1})$ is the posterior distribution over reward model parameters. This objective can be rewritten and approximated via sampling:
$Q_i^* \approx \arg \max_{Q_i} \frac{1}{M} \sum_{y_i \in \{0,1\}} \sum_{\theta \in \Theta} P(y_i | Q_i, \theta) \log_2 \left( \frac{M \cdot P(y_i | Q_i, \theta)}{\sum_{\theta' \in \Theta} P(y_i | Q_i, \theta')} \right)$, where $\Theta$ is a set of $M$ models sampled from the posterior $b_{i-1}$. This requires efficient sampling from the model posterior.

#### 3.3. PreferenceEKF: Subspace Inference and Extended Kalman Filter

The core of PreferenceEKF is to perform sequential Bayesian inference within a low-dimensional subspace of the neural network's parameter space.

- **Sequential Bayesian Inference:** Active preference learning is framed as a sequential Bayesian filtering problem. As each new preference query-response pair $(Q_i, y_i)$ arrives, the posterior belief over parameters $p(\theta_i | D_{1:i})$ is updated from the previous posterior $p(\theta_{i-1} | D_{1:i-1})$. This involves a dynamics model $p(\theta_i | \theta_{i-1})$ and a measurement model $p(D_i | \theta_i)$.
- **EKF for Neural Networks:** To make inference tractable, the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) is utilized. This assumes Gaussian noise for both dynamics and measurement models and linearizes the non-linear functions (dynamics $g(\theta)$ and measurement $h(\theta, Q)$) around the current mean estimate.
 - **Dynamics Model:** The parameter dynamics $g(\theta_{i-1})$ is modeled as an identity function, $g(x) = x$, with additive Gaussian noise $N(0, U)$, where $U$ is a prespecified covariance matrix. This implies parameters slowly drift over time.
 - **Measurement Model:** The measurement function $h(\theta_i, Q_i)$ is derived from the Bradley-Terry likelihood (Eq. 1), which predicts the probability of a preference given the reward model parameters. This is also assumed to have additive Gaussian noise $N(0, V)$, where $V$ is a prespecified covariance matrix. The BT likelihood is linearized around the current mean estimate to fit the EKF framework.
 - **Posterior Form:** Under these assumptions, the EKF provides a closed-form update, resulting in a Gaussian posterior $b_i = N(\mu_i, \Sigma_i)$.
- **Subspace Inference:** To overcome the $O(|\theta|^2)$ scaling of the covariance matrix $\Sigma_i$ in the full parameter space, inference is performed in a low-dimensional subspace.
 - A full-space parameter $\theta$ is mapped from a low-dimensional subspace parameter $z$ (where $|z| \ll |\theta|$) using an affine transformation: $\theta(z) = Az + \theta^*$. Here, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{|\theta| \times |z|}$ is a fixed projection matrix, and $\theta^* \in \mathbb{R}^{|\theta|}$ is an offset.
 - The posterior $b_i$ is maintained over $z$, denoted as $N(\mu_i', \Sigma_i')$, where $\mu_i' \in \mathbb{R}^{|z|}$ and $\Sigma_i' \in \mathbb{R}^{|z| \times |z|}$.
 - Models sampled from this subspace posterior $b_i'$ are then projected back to the full parameter space using $\theta(z)$ for neural network forward passes and computing acquisition functions.
- **Subspace Construction:** The projection matrix $A$ and offset $\theta^*$ are obtained by training the neural network using Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) on a small "warm-up" dataset in the full parameter space. $A$ is derived by applying Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to the collected SGD iterates. Alternatively, $A$ can be constructed via random projections. The final SGD iterate is used as $\theta^*$.

#### 3.4. Active Learning Algorithm (Algorithm 1 Summary)

1. **Subspace Construction:**
 - Run SGD on an initial preference dataset ($D_{init}$) to obtain a sequence of full-space parameters $\theta_{1:w}$.
 - Set $\theta^*$ as the final parameter $\theta_w$.
 - Apply SVD to the SGD iterates to construct the projection matrix $A \in \mathbb{R}^{|\theta| \times |z|}$.
2. **Subspace Inference (Active Learning Loop):**
 - Initialize the belief $b_0(z)$ as a zero-mean isotropic Gaussian in the subspace of dimension $|z|$.
 - For $t=1$ to query budget $B$:
 - **Query Selection:** `ComputeQuery` uses InfoGain (Eq. 3) to select the most informative query $Q_t$ from the pool $P$. This involves sampling models from $b_{t-1}(z)$, projecting them to full space, and computing their preferences.
 - **Label Acquisition:** `GetLabel` obtains the label $y_t$ for $Q_t$ from an annotator (human or simulated oracle).
 - **Belief Update:** `EKF` updates the subspace posterior $b_{t-1}(z)$ to $b_t(z)$ using the new query-response pair $(Q_t, y_t)$.

#### 3.5. Baselines and Evaluation

PreferenceEKF is compared against four Bayesian deep learning baselines: DeepEnsemble, Dropout, Laplace Approximation, and Last-Layer Markov Chain Monte Carlo (LLMCMC).
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Reward Learning:** Bradley-Terry log-likelihood on a held-out test set, sample efficiency (log-likelihood vs. number of queries), and runtime.
 - **Uncertainty Quality:** Expected Calibration Error (ECE) and Brier score.
 - **Policy Optimization:** Empirical rollout returns of policies trained using Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) on datasets labeled with learned reward models.
- **Tasks:** Experiments are conducted on D4RL benchmarks (MuJoCo locomotion, Adroit Shadow Hand, Maze2D navigation) and V-D4RL (pixel-based). Synthetic preference labels with noise are primarily used, with additional experiments on human-annotated data and sparse feedback.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across various evaluation criteria, demonstrating the performance and characteristics of PreferenceEKF.

1. **Sample Efficiency in Active Reward Learning:**
 - PreferenceEKF, in both its active and random querying variants, achieved log-likelihood performance on par with or superior to all other Bayesian deep learning baselines (DeepEnsemble, Dropout, Laplace, LLMCMC) across aggregated D4RL tasks. Active PreferenceEKF demonstrated competitive sample efficiency and a higher final log-likelihood.
 - Statistical tests indicated that active PreferenceEKF statistically significantly outperformed active DeepEnsemble, Dropout, and Laplace, and performed on par with active LLMCMC in terms of normalized area under the curve (AUC) for log-likelihood.
 - InfoGain was identified as an effective acquisition function, enabling active variants to surpass their random counterparts, unlike disagreement or entropy.
 - PreferenceEKF's performance advantage was not solely due to a larger number of posterior samples; it maintained its lead even when all methods used the same, smaller number of samples (M=5), suggesting the learned posterior effectively captures annotator preferences.

2. **Training Runtime and Scalability:**
 - PreferenceEKF exhibited significantly faster training runtime compared to baselines, achieving approximately 5x speedup over DeepEnsemble and over 40x speedup compared to LLMCMC. This efficiency is attributed to its sequential EKF updates, which process only the most recent query, unlike baselines that often retrain on all accumulated data.
 - The method demonstrated superior scalability with respect to both the number of model samples (M) and the neural network architecture size. Its runtime scaled more gracefully than DeepEnsemble and Dropout, while maintaining competitive test log-likelihood, particularly when a large number of model samples or larger neural networks were required.

3. **Model Calibration:**
 - In terms of uncertainty quantification quality, PreferenceEKF achieved the lowest Expected Calibration Error (ECE) among all methods and the second-lowest Brier score (behind active DeepEnsemble). This indicates that the subspace inference approach yields higher quality posterior approximations and better calibrated model predictions.

4. **Ablation Study on Subspace Construction:**
 - An ablation analysis on subspace construction techniques revealed that while SVD-based construction (using SGD iterates from a warm-up dataset) performed well for smaller subspace dimensions, random projection for basis generation could achieve comparable or even superior performance as the subspace dimensionality increased.
 - Furthermore, PreferenceEKF demonstrated effectiveness even without an initial warm-up dataset by relying solely on random projection for subspace construction, decoupling its reliance on initial SGD-based training.

5. **Policy Optimization with Learned Reward Models:**
 - Reward models learned using PreferenceEKF resulted in policies with performance competitive with those optimized using reward models from other methods in offline RL tasks (IQL). These policies generally performed on par with or slightly worse than policies trained with ground-truth environment rewards. This indicates that PreferenceEKF produces reward models suitable for downstream policy optimization.

6. **Extension to Image Data and Sparse Preference Feedback:**
 - The method demonstrated applicability to real-world robotics data with sparse comparative feedback (SOAR dataset), achieving performance comparable to LLMCMC. This highlights its potential for settings where dense reward signals are unavailable.
 - PreferenceEKF was also successfully applied to pixel-based control tasks (V-D4RL benchmark) by utilizing pretrained image embeddings, performing on par with DeepEnsemble. This suggests its viability for high-dimensional visual inputs when combined with appropriate embedding strategies.

7. **Limitations:**
 - The unimodal Gaussian assumption of the Extended Kalman Filter limits its ability to effectively model multimodal preference distributions, such as those arising from multiple human annotators with differing preferences.
 - Scalability to foundation model-scale reward models remains an open question.
 - The work primarily focuses on parameter uncertainty, and further evaluation with metrics of joint predictive uncertainty, such as those from epistemic neural networks, could be explored.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents PreferenceEKF as a significant advancement in the field of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), particularly concerning the efficiency and scalability of reward model learning.

**Significance:**
The primary significance lies in providing a method that addresses the long-standing challenge of sample inefficiency in RLHF by enabling efficient and principled uncertainty quantification (UQ) for large neural network reward models. By framing active preference learning as a sequential Bayesian filtering problem and employing an Extended Kalman Filter within a low-dimensional parameter subspace, PreferenceEKF offers a novel approach to Bayesian Deep Learning that scales beyond what traditional Bayesian methods or ensemble-based approaches can achieve for reward modeling. This method allows for robust approximation of the reward model posterior and scalable sampling of neural network parameters, which is crucial for information-theoretic active learning strategies.

**Potential Impact:**
1. **Reduced Cost and Increased Practicality of RLHF:** By significantly improving the sample efficiency of reward learning, PreferenceEKF has the potential to substantially reduce the amount of human feedback required. This directly translates to lower annotation costs and faster iteration cycles, making RLHF more practical and accessible for real-world applications across various domains, including natural language processing, personalized recommendation systems, and human-robot interaction.
2. **Enhanced Model Quality and Calibration:** The demonstrated improvements in model calibration (lower ECE and Brier scores) indicate that PreferenceEKF yields more reliable uncertainty estimates. This can lead to more trustworthy models that better reflect their own confidence, which is critical in safety-sensitive applications or where decision-making relies on the model's self-assessment.
3. **Scalability of Bayesian Methods for Deep Learning:** The work highlights the viability of subspace filtering as a scalable approach for applying principled Bayesian inference to deep neural networks. This could foster further research and development in scalable Bayesian Deep Learning, extending its benefits beyond reward modeling to other sequential learning tasks or large model fine-tuning in robotics.
4. **Broader Applicability to Diverse Data Modalities:** The successful application of PreferenceEKF to challenging scenarios, such as learning from sparse preference feedback in real robotics data and from pixel-based observations (via embeddings), suggests its robustness and adaptability. This expands the range of problems where efficient preference learning can be applied.
5. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future investigations into several critical areas. These include extending the framework to handle multimodal human preferences (e.g., from diverse annotator populations), exploring its applicability to even larger, foundation model-scale reward models, and integrating the learned reward model posterior into reinforcement learning policies for improved exploration or mitigation of issues like reward hacking.

**Broader Impacts (as acknowledged by authors):**
The authors note potential societal implications, including the risk of suboptimalities like bias amplification or neglect of minority preferences due to the reduction of the parameter space. They suggest future research should investigate the robustness of PreferenceEKF and potential information loss caused by subspace reduction to mitigate these risks.
