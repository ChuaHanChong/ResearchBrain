# RAPT: Model-Predictive Out-of-Distribution Detection and Failure Diagnosis for Sim-to-Real Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Queensland and CSIRO Robotics developed RAPT, a model-predictive framework for out-of-distribution detection and failure diagnosis in sim-to-real humanoid robots. It achieved an aggregate AUROC of 0.92 in simulation and 75% recall on physical hardware, while also providing automated root-cause analysis with 75% Top-1 accuracy for real-world failures.

## Problem

- Learned control policies, particularly from Deep Reinforcement Learning, often fail silently in real-world humanoid robots when encountering Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) states due to the 'Sim-to-Real gap', leading to potential hardware damage or safety risks.
- Existing anomaly detection methods for high-frequency robotic control are often computationally intensive, lack reliable calibration for low false-positive rates, and provide little to no interpretability or diagnostic information about failure causes.
- Generic time-series anomaly detection methods often fail to account for the complex kinematic constraints and rigid-body dynamics inherent in legged systems like humanoids, limiting their effectiveness in identifying physically inconsistent behaviors.

## Method

![[2602.01515-method.png]]
*The RAPT pipeline. (A) The neural architecture. (B) The hierarchical detection logic using three gates. (C) The post-hoc diagnostic process utilizing saliency heatmaps and multi-modal Large Language Models.*

- **RAPT**, a ==self-supervised recurrent probabilistic model==, learns the spatio-temporal structure of nominal robot proprioceptive data in simulation and detects anomalies as deviations (elevated ==reconstruction error==) from this learned distribution.
- A hybrid detection strategy combines RAPT's contextual ==Negative Log-Likelihood== (NLL) anomaly scoring with a deterministic ==range-based detector==, using hardware-calibrated thresholds to adapt to the Sim-to-Real gap while maintaining sensitivity.
- A two-stage diagnostic pipeline integrates ==temporal saliency== (via ==Integrated Gradients== with Backpropagation Through Time, weighted by NLL inverse variance) with a ==multi-modal LLM== for zero-shot semantic classification of root causes.

## Results

- RAPT achieved an aggregate AUROC of **0.92** across simulation tasks, significantly outperforming baselines (e.g., LSTM-VAE at **0.77**), and demonstrated an absolute improvement of approximately **+0.34** in Safety Score (TPR at 0.5% FPR).
- On a physical Unitree G1 humanoid, RAPT achieved **75%** recall for detecting real-world anomalies while maintaining zero false positives during nominal operation and a low inference latency of **1.63 ms**, well within real-time constraints.
- The automated diagnostic pipeline attained **75%** Top-1 accuracy (improving to **87.5%** with visual input) for zero-shot semantic root-cause classification of real-world failures, proving useful for debugging subtle Sim-to-Real discrepancies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reconstruction likelihood, when properly calibrated and interpreted as a continuous signal, can effectively quantify the Sim-to-Real mismatch during robot deployment, moving beyond binary success/failure metrics to enable fine-grained monitoring.
> - Localized anomaly signals, identified by maximizing per-dimension anomaly scores (Max Mean) rather than averaging them, significantly improve detection performance, indicating that OOD events often manifest in specific, critical observation channels.
> - Combining spatio-temporal attribution maps from a probabilistic model with the reasoning capabilities of a multi-modal Large Language Model allows for effective zero-shot semantic diagnosis of robot failures, enhancing interpretability and debugging.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of learned control policies, particularly those derived from Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL), into physical robotic systems is a challenging area. While DRL agents can exhibit high performance in simulated environments, their reliability often diminishes drastically when faced with the inherent stochasticity and unmodeled complexities of the real world. This discrepancy is commonly referred to as the "Sim-to-Real gap." When robots encounter states that are "Out-Of-Distribution" (OOD) relative to their training data—due to factors such as unmodeled dynamics, sensor noise, or environmental variations—they frequently fail silently. Such silent failures can lead to undesirable behaviors, potential hardware damage, or risks to human operators, especially in safety-critical applications like humanoid robotics.

Existing anomaly detection methods, which aim to identify these OOD states, often present limitations when applied to high-frequency, high-dimensional robotic control:
- **Computational Incompatibility:** Many methods are too computationally intensive for real-time control rates (e.g., 50 Hz for humanoid robots), hindering their practical deployment.
- **Calibration Challenges:** Achieving the extremely low false-positive rates required for robust real-world deployment is difficult, as many methods struggle with precise calibration and distinguishing between nominal variations and true anomalies.
- **Lack of Interpretability:** A significant portion of current safety mechanisms operate as "black boxes," providing only a binary "stop" signal without offering insights into why the robot deviated from its nominal behavior. This absence of diagnostic information impedes debugging efforts and the iterative refinement necessary to bridge the Sim-to-Real gap.
- **Generative Model Limitations:** Approaches based on deep generative models, such as Variational Autoencoders (VAEs), can suffer from "input complexity bias," where they sometimes assign higher likelihoods to OOD inputs than to complex in-distribution data, undermining their effectiveness for anomaly detection.
- **Ignoring Kinematic Constraints:** Many generic time-series analysis methods treat individual sensor channels independently, failing to account for the rigid-body constraints, mechanical couplings, and non-linear dependencies inherent in legged systems like humanoids. This can lead to missed anomalies or less effective detection.
- **Uncertainty and Dynamics Modeling Issues:** Uncertainty estimation methods (e.g., MC Dropout, ensembles) often incur high inference costs and may conflate different types of uncertainty. Dynamics-based models can be computationally heavy and might not detect all types of OOD events, particularly "silent failures" where transitions remain superficially consistent but observations are corrupted.
- **Control-Theoretic Challenges:** While control-theoretic safety methods offer formal guarantees, their practical application is limited in high-dimensional, unstructured observation spaces typical of deep reinforcement learning, due to their reliance on accurate explicit dynamics models which are often unavailable.

RAPT (Recurrent Anomaly Probabilistic Trajectory Model) aims to address these identified gaps by introducing a lightweight, self-supervised, and interpretable monitoring system specifically designed for high-frequency humanoid control. It re-frames the Sim-to-Real mismatch not as a binary outcome but as a continuously observable signal, allowing for both reliable online detection and post-hoc diagnosis. This approach differentiates itself by providing calibrated, per-dimension anomaly signals and integrating a diagnostic pipeline to explain the causes of failures, thus facilitating the debugging process and improving the overall safety and reliability of humanoid robot deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the safety and reliability of deploying learned control policies on humanoid robots in real-world environments. The authors are motivated by the critical challenges arising from the "Sim-to-Real gap," which causes policies robust in simulation to encounter Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) states during real-world execution, leading to silent failures and potential hardware damage.

The specific motivations and objectives can be detailed as follows:

- **Addressing Silent Failures in Safety-Critical Domains:** Learned policies, when confronted with OOD states (e.g., unmodeled dynamics, sensor noise, environmental changes), tend to fail silently by executing high-confidence, yet inappropriate, actions. This is particularly hazardous in domains like humanoid robotics, autonomous driving, or healthcare, where such failures can result in significant hardware damage or risk to human safety. The need for a robust mechanism to detect when a robot has drifted from its training distribution, and critically, *why* it drifted, is paramount. An illustrative example from the authors' lab, involving a humanoid policy executing a high-velocity maneuver with an unmodeled payload, underscores this urgency.

- **Overcoming Limitations of Existing Anomaly Detection Methods:**
 - **High-Rate Control Compatibility:** Many existing anomaly detection techniques are not computationally efficient enough to operate at the high control frequencies (e.g., 50 Hz) required for dynamic humanoid locomotion and manipulation.
 - **Reliable Calibration:** Achieving extremely low false-positive rates, which are essential for practical, long-term deployment of robots, is a persistent challenge for current methods. Poor calibration can lead to frequent false alarms, reducing operator trust and utility.
 - **Interpretability and Diagnosis:** A significant motivation is the "black box" nature of most current safety shields, which provide only a binary stop signal without explaining the root cause of an anomaly. For debugging the Sim-to-Real gap and improving policies, understanding *why* a failure occurred (e.g., terrain irregularity, sensor noise, actuator degradation) is as important as detecting its presence.
 - **Handling Input Complexity Bias and Kinematic Constraints:** The paper explicitly notes that deep generative models can suffer from input complexity bias, incorrectly assigning high likelihoods to OOD data. Furthermore, generic time-series methods often fail to model the inherent rigid-body constraints and complex kinematic couplings present in legged systems, treating joint sensors in isolation rather than as a holistic chain. This limits their effectiveness in detecting physically inconsistent behaviors.

- **Primary Contributions/Objectives of RAPT:**
 1. **Deployment-Time Measurement of Sim-to-Real Gap:** To re-conceptualize reconstruction likelihood as a calibrated, continuous signal quantifying Sim-to-Real mismatch during deployment. This enables fine-grained monitoring, verification, and debugging, moving beyond simple success/failure metrics.
 2. **Online, Lightweight OOD Detection for Humanoids:** To develop RAPT as a recurrent probabilistic model capable of high-frequency (50 Hz) detection of OOD behavior. The system is designed to be lightweight, fast to train, and deployable, offering superior performance compared to existing methods across diverse humanoid tasks.
 3. **Saliency-Based Failure Diagnosis:** To establish a post-hoc diagnostic pipeline that leverages gradient-based temporal saliency derived from RAPT's reconstruction objective. By mapping these saliency patterns to semantic failure descriptions, combined with a multi-modal language model, the system aims to provide zero-shot classification of failure modes, thereby facilitating rapid interpretation of hardware and software faults.

In essence, the motivation is to create a comprehensive safety and diagnostic tool that not only prevents hazardous silent failures but also provides actionable intelligence to engineers for continuous improvement of robot policies and hardware in the real world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The RAPT (Recurrent Anomaly Probabilistic Trajectory Model) methodology comprises a four-stage pipeline: data collection, self-supervised model training, hybrid real-time anomaly detection, and post-hoc root-cause diagnosis. The entire pipeline is designed for computational efficiency, enabling rapid iteration in a Sim-to-Real context.

#### 3.1. A. Experimental Tasks & Data Collection

The evaluation focuses on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot (29 Degrees of Freedom) across four control tasks: velocity-tracking locomotion, two motion mimicry tasks (Dance102, Gangnam), and ballistic throwing. For each task, nominal training data is gathered by executing a pre-trained expert policy within NVIDIA Isaac Lab, a large-scale simulation environment. This process yields a dataset of proprioceptive observations (o_t ∈ R^d_obs) that represent the robot's expected behavior during normal task execution. All observations are normalized to zero mean and unit variance prior to model training.

#### 3.2. B. RAPT Architecture and Training

RAPT is a self-supervised reconstruction model designed to learn the characteristic spatio-temporal structure of nominal robot behavior. Anomalies are then detected as deviations from this learned structure, manifesting as elevated reconstruction error during deployment.

1. **Residual State Encoder:** An initial Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), denoted f_ϕ, maps the raw observation o_t to a higher-dimensional latent embedding e_t (R^256). This expanded dimension (d_model = 256 > d_obs ≈ 140) provides the capacity to capture non-linear kinematic couplings across the robot's degrees of freedom. The encoder incorporates L=4 residual blocks, each applying a non-linear transformation with a skip connection to improve optimization stability. Each block consists of a linear layer, Layer Normalization, and a ReLU activation function.

2. **Recurrent Latent Bridge:** To model temporal dependencies and detect anomalies that manifest through inconsistent temporal evolution rather than just individual state deviations, a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) operates in the latent space. This GRU processes the latent embeddings e_t, updating a hidden state h_t (R^256) and outputting a temporal feature vector g_t. The vector g_t summarizes recent spatio-temporal context. During training, sequences are unrolled for T=50 steps; during inference, the hidden state is propagated online.

3. **Latent Bottleneck & Probabilistic Decoder:** To prevent the model from learning a trivial identity mapping (a known issue with over-parameterized autoencoders), a denoising strategy and a latent information bottleneck are employed. During training, nominal observations are corrupted with additive Gaussian noise (σ_noise = 0.01), forcing the network to learn robust feature extraction. The recurrent features g_t are then projected to a lower-dimensional latent vector z_t (R^192) using a sigmoid activation and Layer Normalization. Although d_latent is moderately reduced from the recurrent feature space, it exceeds the raw observation space and was empirically selected.
 A probabilistic decoder, g_θ, then parameterizes a diagonal Gaussian distribution over the reconstruction ([μ_t, log σ^2_t] = g_θ(z_t)). This design allows reconstruction errors to be adaptively weighted by the predicted uncertainty, thereby down-weighting dimensions with higher intrinsic variability.
 The model is trained end-to-end by maximizing the Gaussian likelihood of the nominal observations, using the AdamW optimizer (α = 10^-3, B = 256) with a OneCycleLR schedule over 100 epochs.

#### 3.3. C. Hybrid Inference & Anomaly Detection

During deployment, a hybrid detection strategy combines RAPT's contextual anomaly scoring with a deterministic range-based baseline, providing comprehensive coverage for both learned contextual deviations and explicit state-space constraint violations.

1. **NLL Calibration & Sim-to-Real Adaptation:** To account for the inevitable Sim-to-Real gap (e.g., sensor noise, friction, actuation latency) and prevent persistent false positives, the anomaly signal is normalized through a brief calibration phase on the physical hardware. The per-dimension Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL), ℓ_t,i, serves as the anomaly signal. During calibration, the expert policy is executed for T_cal seconds under nominal conditions, and the maximum observed per-dimension residuals (τ^max_i) and global mean residual (τ_global) are recorded.
 Thresholds are defined using a "max-plus-kσ" rule:
 - Gate 1,2: (∃i : ℓ_t,i > τ^max_i + 5σ_i) ∨ (¯ℓ_t > τ_global + 3σ_global).
 - A tighter bound (k=3) is applied to the global mean NLL, which is empirically Gaussian-distributed. A looser bound (k=5) is applied to individual dimensions, treated as a conservative safeguard in a multiple-comparisons regime. This calibration procedure removes static domain bias while preserving sensitivity to dynamic anomalies.

2. **Bounding-Box Range Detector (Gate 3):** This parallel detector flags observations that fall outside a calibrated hyper-rectangle defined by the minimum and maximum values observed during nominal data collection, expanded by a margin δ (0.05 in simulation, 0.2 on hardware).
 The final anomaly decision is the logical OR of all three gates (per-dimension NLL, global mean NLL, and range detector).

#### 3.4. D. Automated Root-Cause Analysis

Upon anomaly detection, a two-stage diagnostic pipeline is triggered to identify the underlying cause of the failure.

1. **Temporal Saliency via Backpropagation Through Time (BPTT):** To localize the fault, Integrated Gradients (IG) are extended using BPTT. The input is a trajectory tensor X_t (R^H×d_obs) representing a history window (H=200 timesteps) leading up to the failure. Gradients are computed with respect to the reconstruction NLL at the final timestep, conditioned on the hidden state preceding the window. By using NLL, attribution scores are weighted by inverse variance (σ^-2_t,i), focusing saliency on violations of high-confidence dynamic constraints. This method identifies when a sensor or actuator begins to drift and distinguishes between sudden shocks and gradual degradation by producing a spatio-temporal attribution heatmap.

2. **LLM-Based Root Cause Analysis:** A large multi-modal language model (LLM) is used to map structured diagnostic evidence to semantic failure categories in a zero-shot setting. The LLM receives a composite prompt containing: (i) the temporal saliency matrix, (ii) task context (e.g., commanded velocities), (iii) kinematic state trajectories over the pre-failure window, and (iv) an optional time-synchronized external camera key-frame (when available). The LLM classifies failures into one of 21 predefined categories, providing a semantic hypothesis to aid human operators in diagnosis.

#### 3.5. E. Safety Responses

Upon anomaly detection, RAPT triggers predefined safety responses specified by the operator (e.g., switching to a high-damping controlled fall, halting motion to a fixed standing posture, or commanding a backward step in collision scenarios). RAPT acts as a reliable and timely trigger for these human-specified safety behaviors, separating detection from control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of RAPT focused on its detection accuracy in simulation, generalization to real-world hardware, and the efficacy of its diagnostic pipeline.

#### 4.1. A. Simulation Benchmarks

RAPT was evaluated across four humanoid control tasks (throwing, velocity tracking, Dance102 mimicry, Gangnam mimicry) in NVIDIA Isaac Lab, comparing against baselines including PatchAD, LSTM-VAE, Deep SVDD, and Isolation Forest.

- **Detection Accuracy:** RAPT achieved the highest Safety Score (True Positive Rate (TPR) at a fixed episode-level False Positive Rate (FPR) of 0.5%) and AUROC across all evaluated tasks.
 - RAPT's aggregate AUROC was 0.92, surpassing the strongest baseline, LSTM-VAE (0.77).
 - In Safety Score, RAPT achieved values ranging from 0.67 to 0.75 across tasks. This represented an absolute improvement of approximately +0.34 over the strongest baseline (LSTM-VAE, which scored around 0.30-0.44). This indicates RAPT's superior ability to maintain sensitivity under stringent false-positive constraints, a critical requirement for real-world safety.
- **Inference Latency:** RAPT maintained a low inference latency of 1.63 ms, which is well within the 20 ms control budget for 50 Hz humanoid control (less than 10% of the budget). This performance was comparable to LSTM-VAE (1.77 ms) and significantly faster than some baselines like PatchAD (11.45 ms).
- **Hybrid vs. Model-Only Detection:** When comparing RAPT's performance with and without the auxiliary Range Detector, the "Model Only" RAPT still exhibited robust performance (e.g., 0.72 Safety Score on "Mimic (Gangnam)" vs. 0.75 with Hybrid). This suggests that RAPT effectively internalizes a substantial portion of the valid state manifold structure. Shallower baselines, however, showed a more pronounced reliance on the explicit range checks.

#### 4.2. B. Ablation Studies

A Taguchi L12 orthogonal array ablation study was performed over 48 models to quantify the contribution of RAPT's design choices.

- **Inference Logic:** This was identified as the dominant factor (35.4% importance). Using the maximum per-dimension anomaly score for aggregation (Max Mean) improved the Safety Score by +14.3% over mean aggregation, indicating that OOD signals are often localized to specific observation dimensions.
- **Uncertainty Modeling:** Probabilistic modeling (using NLL) improved robustness under heteroscedastic noise by +10.3% compared to deterministic reconstruction error.
- **Dynamics (Recurrence):** The inclusion of temporal recurrence (GRU) contributed an 8.5% improvement in Safety Score.
- **Other Factors:** Residual connections (+4.5%) and low latent compression (+7.8%) also showed positive contributions. The training target (dynamics vs. reconstruction objective) exhibited task dependence, with manipulation and mimicry favoring direct reconstruction.

#### 4.3. C. Real-World Deployment

RAPT was deployed on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid at 50Hz. After a brief nominal calibration phase resulting in zero false positives during nominal operation, the system was evaluated on 24 anomalous trials spanning various physical perturbations.

- **Anomaly Detection Performance:** The Hybrid RAPT configuration (RAPT + Range Detector) achieved the highest recall of 75%, detecting 18 out of 24 anomalous runs. RAPT (Model Only) achieved 67% recall, outperforming the Range Detector alone (62%) and the baseline LSTM-VAE (50%).
- **Practical Utility:** Beyond quantitative detection, RAPT proved valuable as a deployment verification tool. It successfully flagged subtle, silent configuration errors (e.g., under-tuned wrist PD gains) that would not have triggered simple range-based checks, demonstrating its utility in identifying non-catastrophic Sim-to-Real inconsistencies.

#### 4.4. D. Automated Root-Cause Diagnosis

The diagnostic pipeline was evaluated on 16 real-world failure logs where RAPT (Hybrid) had successfully detected an anomaly. The system classified failures into one of 21 predefined categories.

- **Proprioceptive-Only Diagnosis:** Using only proprioceptive saliency data, the system achieved 75% Top-1 accuracy and 87.5% Top-3 accuracy.
- **Multi-Modal Diagnosis:** The addition of a single time-synchronized visual key-frame (Prop. + Visual) improved accuracy to 87.5% Top-1 and 100% Top-3. This indicates that visual context can assist in disambiguating between external disturbances and internal system faults.
- **Operational Impact:** From an operational perspective, the diagnostic pipeline was found to be useful for reducing post-incident debugging time by localizing failures to specific subsystems (e.g., leg actuation vs. perception) and identifying silent Sim-to-Real discrepancies like mismatched PD gains.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RAPT framework presents contributions to the field of safe and reliable robot learning, with several potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Safety and Reliability of Robotic Systems:** RAPT provides a mechanism for online, high-frequency detection of Out-Of-Distribution (OOD) behavior in humanoid robots. Its ability to achieve high true positive rates at very low false-positive rates is critical for practical, safety-critical deployments, mitigating the risk of silent policy failures that could lead to hardware damage or operational hazards.
- **Improved Sim-to-Real Transfer and Debugging:** By treating the Sim-to-Real gap as a continuous, observable signal, RAPT offers fine-grained monitoring and verification capabilities. The interpretable, per-dimension anomaly signals, coupled with the automated root-cause diagnosis pipeline, provide developers with actionable insights into *why* a robot is failing. This can significantly reduce debugging time, accelerate the identification of modeling discrepancies, and streamline the iterative process of deploying learned policies from simulation to physical hardware.
- **Advancement in Anomaly Detection for Complex Robotics:** RAPT addresses several key limitations of prior anomaly detection methods, including computational speed for real-time control, robust calibration under strict safety constraints, and the integration of kinematic awareness into the detection process. Its performance on complex humanoid tasks sets a benchmark for anomaly detection in high-dimensional, dynamic robotic systems.
- **Foundation for Adaptive and Autonomous Systems:** The continuous measurement of Sim-to-Real mismatch provided by RAPT can serve as a crucial input for more advanced adaptive robotic behaviors. This signal could inform downstream decision-making, such as triggering policy switching to a more robust or conservative behavior, initiating online adaptation, or prompting human intervention when the robot operates in unfamiliar distributional regimes.
- **Facilitating Community Research and Development:** The authors commit to releasing their training pipeline, real-world labeled datasets, and C++ deployment code. This open-sourcing effort can accelerate further research and development in safe robot learning and anomaly detection within the robotics community, fostering reproducibility and building upon the presented work.
- **Bridging the Detection-Diagnosis Gap:** The integration of gradient-based saliency with Large Language Models for semantic root-cause analysis represents a step towards moving beyond simple anomaly flags to providing meaningful explanations. This capability is essential for practical robotic systems that require not only awareness of failure but also an understanding of its underlying cause to facilitate effective recovery or human-led corrective actions.
