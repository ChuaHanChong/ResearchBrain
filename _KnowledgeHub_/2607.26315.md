# MoMo: Dial Motion Mode in Robot Manipulation with Spatiotemporal Action Tokenization

> [!summary] Summary
> MoMo, a two-stage imitation learning framework developed at Apple, disentangles spatial and temporal action components to enable continuous control over "motion mode" in robot manipulation. The framework allows compositional generalization, successfully applying learned motion styles to tasks never demonstrated in those specific modes while maintaining task completion and generating distinct, interpretable variations in robot dynamics and end-effector orientation.

## Problem

- Traditional imitation learning policies often entangle task-specific behaviors with execution variations, requiring extensive new demonstrations for each task or style variation.
- Robots need to adapt *how* they perform tasks (e.g., pacing, smoothness, hesitation) to suit various contexts, but learning these "motion modes" as reusable, task-agnostic factors remains challenging.
- Achieving compositional generalization in robot control, where unseen task-mode combinations can be executed without specific demonstrations for every pair, is a fundamental hurdle in imitation learning.

## Method

- The authors introduce **MoMo**, a two-stage imitation learning framework that decomposes robot actions into complementary ==spatial== and ==temporal code streams==.
- In Stage 1, a ==spatiotemporal autoencoder== tokenizes 2-second action chunks into discrete ==spatial codes== (encoding task-dependent geometry from raw joint positions) and ==temporal codes== (encoding motion characteristics from ==Discrete Cosine Transform== features of joint deltas).
- In Stage 2, a ==behavior-cloning transformer== is trained to predict these discrete code indices based on visual observations, proprioception, a task identifier, and a user-controlled continuous ==motion-mode scaler==.

## Results

- MoMo consistently produced distinguishable motion dynamics, with "steady" Mode A trajectories showing lower joint speed, lower joint acceleration, and a **15-25 degree** lower end-effector approach pitch compared to "dynamic" Mode B.
- The framework demonstrated compositional generalization, successfully transferring unseen motion modes to new tasks (e.g., `push` in Mode A, `highfive` in Mode B), with objective metrics shifting towards the requested mode and largely preserving task completion.
- Latent space analysis confirmed the disentanglement, showing spatial codes clustered primarily by task, while temporal codes formed a clear continuous band separating motion modes across all tasks, and human raters consistently perceived these style variations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly decomposing robot actions into separate spatial and temporal components facilitates the disentanglement of task-specific structure from reusable behavioral motion characteristics.
> - Temporal action codes primarily encode motion mode qualities (e.g., speed, acceleration patterns) and are largely task-agnostic, enabling their transfer across different manipulation tasks.
> - Spatial action codes predominantly encode task-dependent geometric structures and remain relatively insensitive to variations in motion mode.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research integrates into the fields of robot imitation learning, action tokenization, and motion-mode disentanglement for character animation and robotics. Traditional imitation learning policies typically learn task-specific behaviors, often requiring new demonstrations for each task or for each variation of a behavior within a task. This approach limits the reusability and generalizability of learned behaviors.

The paper builds upon a growing body of work that treats robot actions as discrete tokens, which can be predicted by transformer architectures. Examples include Behavior Transformers (BeT) which cluster actions, VQ-BeT which uses a residual Vector Quantized Variational Autoencoder (RVQ-VAE) for action tokenization, and FAST which tokenizes actions in the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) domain. This work extends these concepts by proposing a novel dual-stream action tokenization that explicitly separates spatial and temporal components of robot actions.

Furthermore, it draws inspiration from literature on disentangled motion representations, particularly in animated characters, where style and content are separated. In robotics, prior work has explored neural policy style transfer and constrained mode learning. This paper adapts the concept of disentangling "style" or "motion characteristics" to robot manipulation, focusing on a "motion mode" that modulates how a task is executed rather than what task is performed. This differentiates it from existing methods that often entangle style with task-specific learning. The study also connects to research on robot motion characteristics like legibility and predictability, which emphasize the communicative and adaptive aspects of how robots move. By aiming to control motion mode as a reusable factor, this work seeks to enhance robot adaptability and interaction quality beyond mere task success.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to investigate whether execution-level variations in robot actions, termed "motion modes," can be learned as a reusable behavioral factor shared across diverse manipulation tasks. This addresses a fundamental challenge in imitation learning: current policies often learn behaviors jointly with each specific task, necessitating new demonstrations when extending a behavior to a novel task or applying a new behavioral style to an existing task.

The motivation stems from the observation that for robots to operate effectively, especially in human-centric environments, they must not only perform tasks accurately but also adapt *how* their actions unfold to suit the task, object, or interaction context. This "how" aspect, encompassing factors like pacing, smoothness, hesitation, acceleration, and end-effector orientation, is conceptualized as a "motion mode." Unlike skill primitives (e.g., reaching, grasping), motion mode is intended to be task-agnostic, modulating an underlying task policy without altering its functional goal.

The authors propose that explicit separation of task-relevant action structure from reusable behavioral variation could be achieved through action tokenization. By representing motion mode as a controllable, continuous input, the aim is to enable compositional generalization, allowing the robot to execute unseen task-mode combinations without requiring demonstrations for every possible pairing. Specifically, the paper aims to demonstrate that:
1. A continuous motion-mode condition can produce distinguishable, interpretable variations (e.g., steady, dynamic, intermediate behaviors) across different manipulation tasks.
2. This learned motion mode can be transferred to tasks never demonstrated in that particular mode, while largely preserving task success.
3. The proposed spatiotemporal decomposition within an action tokenizer facilitates this disentanglement and reuse, with spatial codes primarily encoding task-dependent structure and temporal codes primarily encoding motion-mode qualities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce MoMo, a two-stage imitation-learning framework designed to decompose action chunks into complementary spatial and temporal code streams, enabling control over motion mode.

**A. System Setup**
The experimental setup uses an Interbotix X-Series hardware in an ALOHA leader-follower configuration, comprising a WidowX-250 leader arm for teleoperation and a ViperX-300 6-DOF follower for policy execution and data collection. The system operates at 50 Hz, with action chunks of length 100 spanning 2 seconds of motion. Two cameras (downward-facing fixed and wrist-mounted) capture 480x640 resolution images. The testbed is a tabletop environment with designated preparation and target zones.

**B. Data Collection**
Six real-robot manipulation tasks involving tabletop objects (e.g., fake food, plates, pans) were used:
- **Multi-mode tasks (demonstrated in both modes):** `pick`, `pour`, `look`, `avoid`. For each, 60 demonstrations were collected (30 per mode).
- **Single-mode tasks (demonstrated in one mode for transfer testing):** `highfive` (Mode A only) and `push` (Mode B only).
Two distinct motion mode prototypes were defined:
- **Mode A (steady):** Characterized by low velocity variance, uniform acceleration, slow adjustments, pauses before grasping, and a level gripper.
- **Mode B (dynamic):** Characterized by higher speed peaks, more acceleration, small bounce-back motions, and interaction-driven orientation changes (e.g., facing a user).
A continuous motion-mode scaler `s` (low for Mode B, high for Mode A) was assigned to each demonstration. Initial object and goal positions were fixed within small tolerances (±5 cm), and trajectories started from a fixed default pose. Demonstrations were converted to HDF5 format, and subsampled with a stride of 10 during training.

**C. Stage 1 Training: Spatiotemporal Action Tokenizer**
Stage 1 is an autoencoder-like structure that quantizes 2-second action chunks into discrete spatial and temporal codes. It consists of two parallel Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ) stacks and a shared decoder.
1. **Architectural Overview:** An action chunk `a` enters two encoders simultaneously. The spatial encoder processes the raw joint-space trajectory, while the temporal encoder processes Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) features of joint deltas. Each encoder feeds an independent RVQ stack. The quantized outputs from both stacks are concatenated, fused via an MLP, and then decoded back into a reconstructed action chunk `â`. An auxiliary DCT-prediction head supervises the temporal stream to ensure it retains frequency-domain information.
2. **Spatial Encoder (E_sp):** This is a 1D convolutional stack that maps the raw joint-space waveform (`a ∈ R^(100x7)`) to a latent stream (`z_sp ∈ R^(20x256)`). Operating on absolute joint positions, it captures path geometry, contact, and end-effector targets.
3. **Temporal Encoder (E_te):** This encoder first computes joint deltas (`∆q_t`), then applies a type-II DCT to retain the first 16 low-frequency coefficients per joint. The resulting feature vector (`∆q ∈ R^(C·7)`) is projected by a 2-layer MLP into a latent stream (`z_te ∈ R^(20x256)`). By focusing on time-frequency content of joint velocities, it captures pacing, smoothness, and rhythmic structure, which are key dimensions of the motion modes.
4. **Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ):** Both `z_sp` and `z_te` are quantized by independent RVQ stacks. The spatial stack uses `L_sp = 4` levels with codebook sizes ``, using a small first codebook as a content bottleneck. The temporal stack uses `L_te = 4` levels of size 256 each. Codebook updates are deferred to allow contrastive gradients to flow.
5. **Decoder Fusion:** The quantized spatial (`z_q_sp`) and temporal (`z_q_te`) streams are concatenated, passed through a LayerNorm, GELU, and fusion MLP (`W_f`) to produce a single `D`-dimensional latent, which a 1D upsampling decoder (`ψ`) then reconstructs into `â`.
6. **Training Objective:** The Stage 1 loss function combines seven terms:
 - `L_recon`: L1 reconstruction loss.
 - `λ_DCT * L_DCT`: Ensures temporal codes retain DCT structure.
 - `λ_swap-task * L_swap-task` and `λ_swap-mode * L_swap-mode`: Swap losses designed to disentangle by decoding a base chunk's spatial codes with a matched donor's temporal codes (same task, opposite mode), pushing the result towards the base path and donor frequency content.
 - `λ_con * L_con` and `λ_MI * L_MI`: Disentanglement losses (contrastive on temporal codes, adversarial mutual information penalty on spatial latent) to separate task from motion mode.
 - `L_commit`: Standard per-quantizer commitment loss.

**D. Stage 2 Training: Learning Behavior Transformer over Stage 1 Codes**
Stage 2 trains a behavior-cloning transformer that predicts the discrete code indices from Stage 1.
1. **Architecture:** The transformer encoder takes visual features (ResNet-34 from front and wrist cameras), current proprioception, a task-identifier embedding, and an embedding of the continuous motion-mode scaler `s` as input. The decoder has classification heads for each codebook level (across both stacks) and a continuous offset head to correct quantization error.
2. **Training Objective:** The loss combines cross-entropy (CE) on the predicted codebook indices and an L1 loss on the continuous offset, with equal weights. Stage 1 weights (encoders, codebooks, fusion, decoder) are frozen to preserve the learned spatiotemporal split and code vocabulary.

**E. Inference**
At inference, the transformer receives observations, a task identifier, and a user-controlled motion-mode scaler `s`. It predicts code indices and an offset. The indices are looked up in the frozen Stage 1 codebooks, summed within each stack, fused, and decoded into an action chunk. The offset is added. Temporal aggregation is used to stabilize execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation protocol involved rolling out policies on six tasks (four trained in both modes, two in single modes) at three scaler values (s=0.1 for Mode B, s=0.5 for Interpolated, s=0.9 for Mode A), with 20 rollouts per setting. Metrics included joint speed, joint acceleration, and end-effector approach pitch, supplemented by blinded human evaluation of style adhesion and task completion.

**A. Objective Results**
- **In-distribution Mode Conditioning:** On the four tasks trained with both Mode A and Mode B, all three motion dynamics metrics consistently separated the two modes. Mode A trajectories were characterized by lower joint speed, lower joint acceleration, and a 15-25 degree lower approach pitch (gripper pointing further downward) compared to Mode B. The interpolated `s=0.5` condition reliably fell between these two extremes across all metrics. Qualitatively, Mode A paths were tighter and uniformly slow, while Mode B paths were more spatially varied with pronounced fast segments.
- **Intermediate Mode Condition:** Spearman rank correlation analysis confirmed that commanded-action dynamics (speed mean, acceleration mean) changed monotonically with the requested scaler `s` for all four trained tasks. Strong, statistically significant negative correlations (p < 10^-6 for all 8 tests) indicated that higher `s` (Mode A) consistently led to slower and steadier movements, demonstrating effective interpolation.
- **Mode Transfer:** For the `push` (trained only in Mode B) and `highfive` (trained only in Mode A) tasks, MoMo demonstrated transfer of the unseen mode.
 - For `push` at `s=0.9` (requested Mode A), speed and acceleration shifted towards the Mode A range observed in other tasks, and approach pitch tilted further downward.
 - For `highfive` at `s=0.1` (requested Mode B), dynamics shifted towards Mode B, although speed and acceleration remained between the endpoint ranges, indicating a less pronounced shift than in `push`.
 - Statistically significant monotonic correlations between `s` and dynamics (p < 5 × 10^-3) were also observed for transfer tasks, albeit weaker than for in-distribution tasks. These results indicate that MoMo can generate trajectories for unseen task-mode pairs where quantitative metrics shift towards values associated with the requested mode learned on other tasks.

**B. Latent Space Visualization and Insights**
PCA projections of the quantized latents revealed distinct structures:
- **Spatial Codes (`z_q_sp`):** Primarily separated by task, forming distinct clusters along principal axes. Different `s` values were interleaved within each task cluster, indicating that spatial codes are largely insensitive to motion mode and primarily encode task-dependent geometric structure.
- **Temporal Codes (`z_q_te`):** Formed a clear Mode B – Mode A band along PC1, with all six tasks overlapping. This suggests that temporal codes primarily encode motion-mode qualities and are largely task-agnostic.
This latent space organization was maintained during deployment, confirming that the Stage 1 decomposition persists. For transfer tasks (e.g., `highfive` at `s=0.1`, Mode B), the spatial code remained confined to the `highfive` cluster, while the temporal code shifted towards the Mode B region of the training distribution, even though `highfive` was never demonstrated in Mode B. This suggests that transfer occurs primarily by shifting the temporal latent while keeping the spatial latent task-constrained, consistent with reusing behavioral variation across task-specific structure.

**C. Human Subject Evaluation**
Blinded human raters (three experts) evaluated rollouts:
- **Style Adhesion:** Ratings were monotonic with the requested `s` for every task, including transfer conditions. Mode B was rated as dynamic, Mode A as steady, and the interpolated condition consistently fell between. This indicates that human raters consistently perceived the motion mode variations, even in cases where objective speed/acceleration metrics showed less pronounced separation (e.g., `highfive` transfer), suggesting raters identified differences in smoothness, hesitation, or gripper choreography.
- **Task Completion:** Task completion remained near ceiling across most cells, including the mode-transfer tasks. The only exception was `push` at the held-out Mode A, where task completion was slightly reduced due to less reliable contact, despite the trajectory being correctly slowed.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents MoMo, a framework demonstrating that motion mode can function as a reusable, task-agnostic behavioral modulation factor in robot manipulation. The compositional generalization observed—where MoMo successfully interpolates between prototype dynamics and transfers requested modes to tasks never demonstrated in those modes—represents a meaningful step towards more adaptive and flexible robot control policies.

The primary significance lies in the architectural and objective-driven factorization of actions into spatial and temporal streams. By treating the temporal stream as a modulation channel for motion mode, the framework allows for independent control over *what* a task achieves (task-specific spatial structure) and *how* it is executed (behavioral motion mode). This is supported by objective metrics, latent space analyses, and human ratings, which collectively indicate that the framework achieves a form of compositional task-mode generalization.

This approach has several potential impacts:
- **Enhanced Robot Adaptability:** Robots could adapt their execution style based on context (e.g., delicate vs. fast, human-aware vs. efficient) without extensive re-demonstration or retraining for each scenario.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** The ability to modulate motion style can enhance legibility and predictability of robot intent, leading to more natural and intuitive human-robot collaboration. An operator or a higher-level policy could dynamically adjust the motion mode scaler at runtime, facilitating responsive interaction.
- **Advancements in Imitation Learning:** The concept of learning compact, reusable behavioral factors that compose with task-specific action structures offers a paradigm for developing more generalizable robot policies, especially as pre-training on large skill distributions becomes more prevalent. This could enable lightweight adaptation where only behavioral factors are tuned, rather than requiring full task retraining.
- **Foundation for Richer Behavioral Control:** While the current study uses two prototype modes and a 1D scaler, the framework lays the groundwork for exploring higher-dimensional and more nuanced behavioral factors, potentially controlled by higher-level policies (e.g., LLMs) that interpret interaction context.

The authors acknowledge limitations, including the partial nature of the factorization (motion mode still has geometric correlates), the embodiment, task set, number of rollouts, and the limited dimensionality of the motion mode. Despite these, the work provides initial evidence for a robust method of task-mode factorization, opening avenues for future research into richer behavioral factors and larger, more diverse task distributions.
