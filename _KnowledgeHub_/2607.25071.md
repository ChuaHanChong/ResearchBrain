# Input Shaping for Point-to-Point Motion with a Continuum Robot Arm

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Louisiana State University experimentally validated input shaping using robust time-delay filters (TDFs) to reduce residual vibrations in a cable-driven continuum robot during point-to-point movements. This approach led to significant reductions in overshoot and settling time for cable tension, even when the robot underwent large, geometrically nonlinear deflections.

## Problem

- Continuum robots, due to their inherent flexibility and underactuated nature, are prone to significant residual vibration during point-to-point movements.
- These vibrations degrade end-effector positioning accuracy and prolong the settling time, limiting precision and efficiency in applications like teleoperation and medical interventions.
- Conventional open-loop command inputs can inadvertently excite the robot's flexible-body vibration modes, exacerbating oscillations.

## Method

- ==Input shaping==, specifically ==time-delay filters (TDFs)==, was employed as a feedforward control strategy to mitigate residual vibrations.
- Both non-robust and **robust TDFs** were designed based on a ==linearized dynamic model== of the continuum robot arm, derived from ==system identification== to determine dominant vibrational characteristics.
- The input shapers convolve the original command signal with a sequence of timed impulses, causing vibrations that destructively interfere with the system's natural oscillations.

## Results

- The robust TDF consistently reduced overshoot in cable tension, for example, cutting "Up" motion overshoot from **153.1%** to **51.1%** and "Down" motion overshoot from **75.9%** to **44.3%** in one scenario (10 mm displacement at 200 mm/s).
- Settling times were significantly shortened by the robust TDF; for instance, "Down" motion settling time for a 20 mm displacement at 200 mm/s decreased from **1899 ms** to **878 ms**.
- The input shaping strategy effectively damped oscillations and stabilized cable tension across various commanded displacements and actuation velocities, demonstrating its robustness and applicability to large robot deflections.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robust time-delay filters consistently provide superior vibration suppression compared to uncompensated inputs and non-robust shapers, improving overall motion quality.
> - Input shaping designed using a linear system model remains effective even when the continuum robot undergoes large, geometrically nonlinear deflections, suggesting consistent dominant vibrational characteristics.
> - Input shaping offers a practical and implementable feedforward solution for enhancing motion precision in compliant robots without requiring complex feedback loops.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a prevalent challenge in the control of continuum robots: residual vibration during motion. Continuum robots, characterized by their slender, flexible, and often snake-like bodies, are increasingly utilized in applications demanding access to confined or complex environments, such as medical procedures, inspection tasks, and underwater operations. Unlike conventional rigid-link robots, continuum robots are typically underactuated and inherently compliant, making them susceptible to significant deformation under external forces and exhibiting vibratory modes when subjected to high-bandwidth control inputs.

The broader research landscape for continuum robots encompasses various design paradigms, including those utilizing pneumatic artificial muscles, electromechanical actuators, concentric pre-curved tubes, and flexible cables (tendons). Control strategies have evolved from static and kinematic models to more complex linear and nonlinear dynamic models. Existing control approaches often involve kinematic-level control with resolved-rate methods, model-based dynamic feedback such as PD-plus-feedforward, feedback linearization, and learning-based controllers.

However, in many open-loop, rate-controlled scenarios, such as teleoperated systems or pre-programmed motions, arbitrary input commands can inadvertently excite the robot's flexible-body vibration modes. This excitation leads to undesirable oscillations, degrading the precision of position or velocity tracking at the end effector. This particular research investigates input shaping, specifically time-delay filters (TDFs), as a feedforward control strategy to mitigate these vibrations. Input shaping is a technique known for its ability to reduce residual vibrations in flexible systems by appropriately timing and scaling command signals. By applying this method to a cable-driven continuum robot, the authors aim to enhance the precision and robustness of its point-to-point movements, thereby contributing to the practical deployment of these robots in critical applications where motion fidelity is paramount. This positions the work as an important step towards bridging the gap between theoretical modeling and robust experimental control for flexible robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a fundamental characteristic of cable-driven continuum robots: their inherent flexibility and underactuated nature. These traits, while enabling access to complex environments, render them prone to significant residual vibration at the completion of point-to-point movements. Such vibrations degrade the robot's end-effector positioning accuracy and prolong the settling time, thereby limiting the precision and efficiency of tasks in various applications, particularly those involving teleoperation, medical interventions, or industrial inspection where precise and stable positioning is critical. In open-loop control architectures, where direct feedback on shape or tip position might be limited or absent, conventional input commands can inadvertently excite these vibratory modes, further exacerbating the problem.

Against this background, the key objectives of this study were to:

1. **Evaluate Input Shaping Effectiveness:** Experimentally apply a time-delay filter (TDF) as an input shaping strategy to a cable-driven continuum robot arm to eliminate or significantly reduce residual vibrations following a rest-to-rest maneuver.
2. **Design and Implement TDFs:** Design both a non-robust and a robust TDF based on a linearized dynamic model of the continuum robot arm. This involves performing system identification to determine the dominant vibrational characteristics (damping ratio and natural frequency).
3. **Comparative Performance Analysis:** Conduct a comprehensive experimental comparison of the continuum robot's response under three conditions: an uncompensated velocity-driven pulse input, the non-robust TDF, and the robust TDF. The evaluation metrics focused on overshoot and settling time in the cable tension response.
4. **Assess Robustness:** Specifically investigate whether the robust TDF design, which accounts for potential uncertainties in system dynamics, provides superior performance compared to the non-robust version across varying operational parameters (e.g., commanded displacement and actuation velocity).
5. **Evaluate Model Applicability:** Determine the practical effectiveness of input shapers designed using a linear system model, particularly when the actual robot undergoes large, geometrically nonlinear deflections during operation. The objective was to ascertain if the linear model-derived shaper parameters remain sufficiently accurate for real-world, large-amplitude motions.

These objectives collectively aim to demonstrate a practical and implementable solution for enhancing the motion quality of compliant continuum robots, thereby contributing to their broader adoption in precision-demanding tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this study involved the design, implementation, and experimental validation of input shaping techniques on a custom-built cable-driven continuum robot arm.

#### 3.1. Experimental Setup

The researchers developed a single-degree-of-freedom cable-driven continuum robot for the experiments. The setup included:
- **Robot Body:** An elastic backbone (A228 music wire, 0.82 mm diameter, 0.24 m length) with 12 cable-support discs for routing the actuation cable.
- **Actuation System:** A stepper motor (MOTOU 17HS4401S, NEMA 17) with a TB6600 driver controlled by an Arduino Uno. The motor turned a cable spool (6.5 cm diameter) to pull or release the cable.
- **Sensing:** A load cell sensor (LSP-5, 0–5N) connected via an LCA-RTC amplifier measured the cable tension. The amplifier's current-loop output was converted to a measurable voltage using a 150Ω resistor and read by a 10-bit Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC) on an Arduino Mega 2560.
- **Cable Routing:** One end of the cable was affixed to the spool, the other to the load cell. A second cable originating from the load cell was routed through ball-bearing v-groove pulleys into the continuum arm, through its support discs, and terminated at the final disc.
- **Power Supply:** An adjustable benchtop power supply (Keysight E3630A, 11.8-26 V) provided power.
The entire mechanical system was mounted on a fixed ground plate.

#### 3.2. System Identification

For designing the input shapers, a linear dynamic model of the continuum arm was assumed, focusing on its first N independent modes of vibration. The cable velocity, $v_{in}(t)$, was considered the causal input, and cable tension, $\lambda(t)$, was chosen as the output of interest. This choice was based on the premise that vibrations of flexible modes strongly coupled to the actuator would be reflected in the tension and are most effectively controlled by the actuator.

The theoretical model structure (Equations 1-12) described the system as a collection of uncoupled second-order modes forced by cable tension, with a kinematic compatibility equation relating modal coordinates, cable tension, and cable displacement. This framework allowed for the derivation of transfer functions relating tension and tip displacement to cable velocity input.

To identify the dominant poles of the real system, a series of velocity pulse inputs were applied. Each pulse involved pulling the robot up, holding it, and then releasing it back to the down-position. The transient response, particularly during the release phase, was analyzed using a log decrement method and a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT). From the data presented in Figure 5, a lightly damped mode with a damping ratio ($\zeta$) of 0.044 and a damped natural frequency ($\omega_d$) of 25.085 rad/s was identified.

A range of experimental scenarios were defined to systematically evaluate the input shapers, varying the commanded cable displacement ($x_f$: 10, 20, 30 mm) and actuation speed ($v_{in}$: 200, 300 mm/s), which in turn defined the pulse time ($t_p = x_f / v_{in}$).

#### 3.3. Input Shaping

Input shaping operates by convolving the original command signal with a sequence of timed impulses. These impulses are designed to generate vibrations that destructively interfere with the natural oscillations of the system, thereby canceling out residual vibration. The TDF approach was adopted for its seamless integration into open-loop control architectures.

1. **Non-Robust Time-Delay Filter (TDF):** This shaper (Equation 14) involves two impulses, $A_0$ and $A_1$, applied at the start and after a time delay $T$, respectively. The amplitudes $A_0$ and $A_1$ and the delay $T$ are calculated based on the identified damping ratio ($\zeta$) and damped natural frequency ($\omega_d$) of the dominant mode:
 - $A_0 = \frac{\exp(\frac{\zeta\pi}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}})}{1 + \exp(\frac{\zeta\pi}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}})}$
 - $A_1 = 1 - A_0$
 - $T = \frac{\pi}{\omega_d}$
 This design places a single pair of zeros at the nominal locations of the system's underdamped poles.

2. **Robust Time-Delay Filter (TDF):** To enhance robustness against uncertainties or variations in the system's natural frequency, a robust TDF was designed by squaring the non-robust filter (Equation 15): $G_{robust}(s) = (A_0 + A_1 e^{-sT})^2$. This effectively places two identical pairs of zeros at the nominal pole locations, broadening the frequency range over which vibration suppression is effective.

A block diagram (Figure 6) illustrated the integration of these shapers into the control loop. The application of input shaping extends the total maneuver time due to the introduction of time delays.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study compared the experimental performance of the continuum robot under three control strategies: an uncompensated pulse input, a non-robust time-delay filter (TDF), and a robust TDF. The evaluation focused on the cable tension response during up-down point-to-point motions, using peak force, overshoot, and settling time as key metrics. Four different scenarios involving varied cable displacements (10, 20, 30 mm) and actuation velocities (200, 300 mm/s) were tested.

The main findings are summarized as follows:

1. **Overall Performance Improvement with Input Shaping:** Both the non-robust and robust TDFs demonstrated an improved response compared to the uncompensated pulse input, characterized by a reduction in residual oscillation.

2. **Superiority of Robust TDF:** The robust TDF consistently delivered superior performance across all tested scenarios when compared to both the uncompensated pulse and the non-robust TDF. This was evident in its ability to further reduce overshoot and shorten settling times.

3. **Reduction in Overshoot:**
 - For the 10 mm displacement at 200 mm/s, the robust TDF reduced the "Up" motion overshoot from 153.1% (pulse) to 51.1%, and the "Down" motion overshoot from 75.9% to 44.3%. The non-robust shaper also reduced overshoot, but to a lesser degree (94.2% and 60.1% respectively).
 - For the 20 mm displacement at 200 mm/s, the robust TDF reduced "Up" overshoot from 131.7% to 12.5%, and "Down" overshoot from 63.2% to 6.1%.
 - Similar significant reductions were observed for 30 mm displacement at 200 mm/s (from 92.9% to 16.5% for "Up," and 25.9% to 5.9% for "Down") and 20 mm displacement at 300 mm/s (from 151.6% to 43.8% for "Up," and 96.4% to 31.5% for "Down").

4. **Reduction in Settling Time:**
 - The robust TDF consistently shortened settling times. For the 10 mm displacement at 200 mm/s, settling time for "Up" motion decreased from 1223 ms (pulse) to 1002 ms (robust), and for "Down" motion from 1608 ms to 1348 ms.
 - The most notable improvements in settling time were seen in scenarios such as 20 mm displacement at 200 mm/s, where "Up" settling time reduced from 1253 ms to 898 ms, and "Down" settling time drastically reduced from 1899 ms to 878 ms.
 - Even for the higher velocity (300 mm/s, 20 mm displacement), the robust TDF reduced "Up" settling time from 1328 ms to 1139 ms, and "Down" settling time from 2475 ms to 1567 ms.

5. **Effectiveness Despite Nonlinearity:** A significant observation was that the input shaping strategy, which was designed based on a linearized system model, remained effective even when the robot underwent large, geometrically nonlinear deflections. For instance, a 30 mm cable displacement led to a predicted tip displacement of approximately 0.5 m, far beyond the typical range for linear model validity. The authors attribute this sustained effectiveness to the real vibration period remaining sufficiently consistent with the period predicted by the linearized model.

The visual representations in Figures 9 through 11 further support these quantitative findings, showing a noticeable damping of oscillations and faster stabilization of the cable tension for input-shaped commands, with the robust TDF exhibiting the least residual vibration. While the implementation of input shaping inherently extends the maneuver time, the report indicates this increase was modest in comparison to the performance benefits.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research hold several significant implications for the development and application of continuum robots:

1. **Enhanced Precision and Performance:** The consistent reduction in overshoot and settling time achieved through input shaping directly translates to improved precision and faster operational capabilities for continuum robots. This is critical for applications demanding high accuracy and rapid task completion, such as delicate surgical procedures, precise object manipulation, or rapid inspection in dynamic environments.

2. **Robustness in Real-World Scenarios:** The demonstrated superiority of the robust time-delay filter underscores its value in practical applications. Real-world continuum robots often experience variations in payload, environmental interactions, or inherent system uncertainties that can alter their dynamic properties. The robust shaper's ability to maintain effective vibration suppression under such conditions enhances the reliability and predictability of robot performance.

3. **Practical Control Strategy for Open-Loop Systems:** Input shaping is a feedforward control technique that can be seamlessly integrated into existing open-loop or partially closed-loop control architectures without requiring complex feedback loops or computationally intensive model-predictive controllers. This ease of implementation makes it an attractive and accessible solution for many current continuum robot systems, particularly in teleoperated settings where real-time model updates might be challenging.

4. **Validation of Linear Model for Vibration Control:** The observation that linear model-based input shaping remains effective even for large, geometrically nonlinear deformations of the continuum robot is a key practical insight. It suggests that, for the purpose of vibration suppression, the dominant vibrational characteristics (like period and damping) may remain sufficiently consistent over a wider range of robot configurations than typically assumed for full kinematic or dynamic modeling. This can simplify the control design process, as it may not always necessitate highly complex nonlinear models for vibration mitigation.

5. **Foundation for Advanced Motion Planning:** By effectively addressing residual vibrations in single-degree-of-freedom point-to-point motions, this work lays a foundational groundwork for developing more complex and precise motion planning strategies. Future research can build upon these findings to extend input shaping to multi-degree-of-freedom systems and arbitrary end-effector trajectories, paving the way for more sophisticated and autonomous operations with continuum robots.

In summary, this research provides experimental validation for a practical and robust method of vibration suppression in cable-driven continuum robots, significantly contributing to their potential for precise and reliable implementation in diverse and demanding applications.
