# X-Morph: Human Motion Priors for Scalable Robot Learning Across Morphologies

> [!summary] Summary
> X-Morph from the National University of Singapore presents an end-to-end pipeline to transfer human motion into physically consistent and trackable behaviors for diverse non-humanoid legged robots like quadrupeds and hexapods. It achieves this by combining cross-morphology retargeting, physics-aware correction, and reinforcement learning, enabling interactive teleoperation, text-conditioned control, and providing structured priors for subsequent robot learning.

## Problem

- Non-humanoid legged robots (quadrupeds, hexapods, etc.) lack extensive behavior datasets comparable to those available for humanoid robots.
- Skill acquisition for non-humanoid robots typically relies on labor-intensive, robot-specific demonstrations or manual reward engineering.
- Direct retargeting of human motion to morphologically distinct robots often results in visually plausible but physically inconsistent, dynamically untrackable motions.

## Method

- **X-Morph** is an end-to-end pipeline that combines an offline ==cross-morphology retargeting network==, a ==physics-aware reference corrector==, and a ==reinforcement learning-based tracking policy==.
- The retargeting network is augmented with ==physics-based losses== to penalize artifacts like foot skating, ground penetration, and joint-limit violations, generating more trackable robot references.
- A ==privileged reinforcement learning teacher== trains a ==causal student policy== that observes deployable proprioception and compact reference features, enabling real-time interactive control from video or text inputs.

## Results

- The system demonstrated live video-driven teleoperation and text-conditioned skill execution across quadruped, hexapod, and quadruped manipulator robots for various locomotion and loco-manipulation tasks.
- A physics-aware corrector reduced near-ground foot slip by **27.2%**, ground penetration by **46.9%**, and contact-height error by **44.2%** in generated references, leading to improved tracking performance.
- Retargeted motions served as effective behavior priors, providing structured initializations that enabled a hexapod to learn a door-opening behavior, accelerating downstream task learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human motion inherently contains transferable features (e.g., contact timing, body coordination) that can be preserved while allowing morphology-specific deviations for non-humanoid robots.
> - Physics-aware correction of retargeted motion is crucial to transform abstract human-derived references into physically consistent and dynamically executable robot behaviors.
> - Distilling a causal, deployable policy from a privileged teacher enables robust, real-time tracking of complex, physics-corrected references for interactive robot control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic control, particularly for humanoid robots, has experienced rapid advancements. This progress is attributed, in part, to the availability of extensive human motion datasets and the development of motion-prior learning methods. These resources facilitate the acquisition of whole-body behaviors without requiring extensive, task-specific engineering.

However, a disparity exists when considering non-humanoid legged robots, such as quadrupeds, hexapods, and quadruped manipulators. For these morphologies, comparable rich behavior datasets are largely absent. Consequently, skill acquisition for non-humanoid systems often relies on robot-specific demonstrations, manual reward engineering, or the creation of small, curated skill libraries. This approach typically necessitates rebuilding behavior data for each new robot morphology.

Prior research has explored the concept of learning reusable motion priors and skill spaces from large, unstructured datasets, exemplified by systems such as DeepMimic and subsequent methods that scale general motion tracking, privileged imitation, and generative motion priors. These developments have enabled a diverse range of whole-body behaviors for humanoid or near-humanoid robots, where source and target body structures are similar. Similarly, animal-motion imitation has been applied to quadrupeds to generate agile real-robot behaviors from reference motions. This body of work underscores the utility of motion data as a reusable behavior prior, though predominantly within morphologically similar contexts.

In computer graphics and animation, motion retargeting across characters with different skeletal topologies has been a subject of ongoing study. The objective in these applications is typically to preserve semantic content, style, or visual plausibility during transfer. Recent advancements have addressed more significant topology changes through techniques like encoder-decoder models, part-based representations, textual conditioning, or spatial correspondences.

Closer to the robotics domain, several works have investigated the transfer of human motion to non-human or non-humanoid embodiments, including legged robots. Examples include HumanConQuad, which retargets human motion to quadrupeds and trains imitation policies; ACE, which learns adversarial correspondence embeddings for retargeting human motion to diverse nonhuman characters; Cross-Loco, which jointly learns human-robot correspondences and legged skills; STMR, which combines spatial retargeting, temporal refinement, and imitation learning for quadruped motions; and MoReFlow, which frames cross-morphology motion retargeting as flow matching. These works collectively demonstrate the feasibility of learning meaningful human-to-robot or cross-species correspondences and incorporating aspects of physical feasibility and deployment into cross-morphology motion transfer.

The X-Morph work positions itself within this landscape by addressing a specific gap: while cross-morphology retargeting can produce visually plausible motions, these are often physically inconsistent, difficult to track under robot dynamics, or not directly deployable. X-Morph distinguishes itself by treating the retargeted motion not as a final output, but as an intermediate representation that undergoes subsequent physics-aware correction, reinforcement learning-based tracking, and distillation into a causal, deployable policy. This approach aims to bridge the gap between abstract human motion data and physically executable, interactive robot behaviors across a range of distinct non-humanoid morphologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind X-Morph stems from the disparity in available behavior data between humanoid and non-humanoid legged robots. While humanoid robot control has benefited significantly from large-scale human motion datasets, non-humanoid platforms (quadrupeds, hexapods, quadruped manipulators) lack such rich resources. This scarcity necessitates labor-intensive, robot-specific demonstrations or manual reward shaping for skill acquisition, impeding the scalability of behavior learning across diverse robot designs.

A central question motivating this research is whether the abundant human motion data can be leveraged as a reusable substrate for generating behaviors in these morphologically distinct non-humanoid robots. While direct, literal retargeting of human motion to a quadruped or hexapod often results in motions that appear visually plausible but are physically inconsistent, dynamically untrackable, or prone to artifacts like foot skating, ground penetration, or joint-limit violations, human motions inherently contain transferable features. These include elements such as contact timing, body coordination, limb sequencing, task intent, and expressive whole-body patterns. The objective is not to achieve precise human imitation but rather to preserve these behaviorally relevant structures while allowing for morphology-specific deviations necessary for physical execution on a robot.

The key objectives of the X-Morph project are multi-faceted:

1. **Develop an End-to-End Behavior Transfer System:** The primary objective is to create a complete pipeline that systematically converts human motion into deployable locomotion and loco-manipulation policies for a variety of non-humanoid legged robot morphologies. This system is designed to integrate cross-morphology retargeting with physics-aware reference correction, privileged reinforcement learning (RL) for robust tracking, and causal policy distillation for real-time deployment.
2. **Ensure Physical Consistency and Trackability:** A critical goal is to overcome the limitations of direct retargeting by generating robot reference motions that are not only kinematically plausible but also physically consistent and trackable by closed-loop controllers. This involves explicitly correcting artifacts and biases the learning towards references that are useful for downstream tracking and interaction under real-world robot dynamics and actuation limits.
3. **Demonstrate Generalization Across Diverse Morphologies:** The research aims to evaluate the pipeline's effectiveness across morphologically distinct legged platforms. Specifically, it seeks to ascertain whether the system can generate viable behaviors for a quadruped, a hexapod, and a quadruped equipped with a manipulator, thereby demonstrating the approach's scalability beyond humanoid systems.
4. **Enable Interactive and Real-Time Control:** A practical objective is to enable the use of transferred human motion as an interactive behavior prior. This includes supporting live video-based teleoperation, where human actions are translated into robot movements in real-time, and live text-to-skill execution, where language commands trigger corresponding robot behaviors by leveraging existing human motion generation or retrieval systems.
5. **Facilitate Downstream Task Learning:** Beyond direct execution, the research aims to show that the learned motion library can provide valuable behavioral structure for subsequent robot learning tasks. This means that the transferred motions can serve as initial behaviors or priors to accelerate the acquisition of more complex skills through reinforcement learning, analogous to how large human motion datasets aid humanoid control.

In summary, X-Morph is motivated by the need to efficiently endow non-humanoid legged robots with a broad range of behaviors by intelligently leveraging existing human motion data, thereby reducing manual engineering and accelerating the development of capable robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

X-Morph is structured as an end-to-end pipeline designed to convert source human motion into executable policies for diverse non-humanoid legged robots. The framework consists of three primary learned modules: an offline retargeting network, a reference-conditioned tracking policy, and a causal retargeting network for interactive deployment.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The system's goal is to transform a source human motion sequence (x_h) into an executable behavior for a target robot morphology (M_r), while preserving the motion's task intent, root movement, and task-relevant body/end-effector coordination. A morphology is defined by its kinematic tree, joint limits, nominal base height, and contact points or end-effectors. Each source-target pair is associated with a retargeting specification (C_h→r) that defines semantic body-part correspondences. The problem is decomposed into two stages:

1. **Reference Generation:** An offline cross-morphology retargeting model (f_θ) maps the human motion to a target robot reference trajectory (ˆs_r), which includes joint positions and root motion features. This reference is then refined by a physics-aware corrector (c_ψ) to produce a cleaned trajectory (˜s_r), reducing artifacts.
2. **Reference Tracking:** A closed-loop robot policy (π_ϕ) observes the robot state (o_t) and a short horizon of reference states (˜s_r_t:t+H) to output an action (a_t).

#### 3.2. Morphology-Aware Reference Generation

The retargeting network is built upon the body-part retargeting framework proposed by Hu et al.. This approach encodes motion via semantically corresponding body parts rather than requiring identical source and target kinematic trees.
Human motions from datasets like AMASS and LAFAN1 are initially represented in a Unitree G1 humanoid embodiment. This intermediate representation, based on a known robot description, provides a structured format with defined joint order, limits, root pose, and forward kinematics, simplifying cross-morphology learning.
Each motion frame is represented as m_r_t = [q_r_t, v_r_t, ω_r_z,t], including joint angles, local root linear velocity, and root yaw rate. The retargeting network predicts this normalized motion representation for the target morphology, and the target root trajectory is reconstructed by integrating predicted local velocities.

The model employs morphology-specific motion encoders and decoders linked through a shared body-part latent space. A pose-aware attention module aggregates joint-level features into body-part tokens, which are then decoded using the target morphology's properties. Training incorporates standard reconstruction, cycle-consistency, adversarial, and FK-based kinematic losses, similar to PAN.
Crucially, X-Morph augments these objectives with physics-based losses to enhance trackability. These penalize foot skating, floating contacts, ground penetration, and joint-limit violations, utilizing target-robot forward kinematics and nominal ground height. Manipulation losses are also included to align task-relevant end-effectors based on C_h→r, biasing the network towards physically useful references for downstream tracking.

#### 3.3. Physics-Aware Reference Correction

The output of the retargeting network, while morphology-consistent, may still contain physical inconsistencies unsuitable for robot tracking. An offline physics-aware corrector addresses these artifacts. This component is a temporal residual model that processes full motion clips, editing joint angles and root trajectory components. Its function is to reduce contact artifacts (foot skating, floating feet, ground penetration, discontinuous root motion, end-effector drift) while preserving the retargeted motion's local root characteristics. The resulting "cleaned" trajectories (˜s_r) are then used for training the tracker, student distillation, causal retargeting, and deployment.

#### 3.4. Reference-Conditioned Policy Tracking

A reference-conditioned tracker is trained to convert the cleaned robot references into closed-loop control policies. At each control step, the policy observes the robot's current state (o_t) and a short preview (horizon H) of the reference features (˜s_r_t:t+H), including target joint positions, base pose/velocity, and foot/end-effector states. The policy outputs actions (e.g., target joint positions for a low-level PD controller).

- **Privileged Teacher:** Initially, a privileged teacher policy is trained. This teacher receives extensive, clean full-state observations, including deployable proprioception augmented with privileged information like extended reference context, body poses, base-height error, root velocities, joint torques, contact state, and motion identity. Training utilizes APEX-style action priors and DeepMimic-style tracking rewards.
- **Student Distillation:** The deployable policy is a causal student distilled from the privileged teacher. This student observes only deployable proprioception and a compact reference stream (current and one-step-future reference features). It is trained using supervised action distillation, minimizing the L2 distance between its predicted actions and those of the privileged teacher.

#### 3.5. Causal Retargeting for Interactive Deployment

To enable real-time and interactive applications, the computationally intensive and non-causal offline retargeting-and-correction stack is replaced by a causal retargeting student. This student network is trained to predict cleaned target references autoregressively from a short causal history of source G1 motion features and its own previous target predictions. It imitates the outputs of the offline retargeting network and physics corrector.

- **Video Input:** For live teleoperation, human motion is first estimated from a monocular RGB stream using FastSAM3D Body to recover an SMPL representation. This is then converted online to the G1 humanoid representation using GMR. The causal retargeting student processes this G1 motion stream to generate target robot references, which are then executed by the deployed tracker.
- **Text-Conditioned Skill Execution:** Language commands can be used to generate or retrieve a corresponding human G1 motion sequence (e.g., using Kimodo). This G1 motion is then fed through the same causal retargeting and tracking stack, enabling language-driven robot behaviors without needing a separate language-conditioned controller for each morphology.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

X-Morph was evaluated across five main axes: cross-morphology motion execution, physical tracking performance, interactive control via teleoperation, text-conditioned skill execution, and application as downstream behavior priors. The evaluation included three distinct robot platforms: a quadruped (Go2), a hexapod (Yuna), and a quadruped equipped with a manipulator (B2-Z1).

#### 4.1. Cross-Morphology Motion Execution

**Video-Driven Teleoperation:** The system demonstrated the ability to transfer various human motions—including walking, turning, squatting, expressive upper-body movements, and object-interaction intent—to the quadruped, hexapod, and quadruped manipulator. The pipeline operates online, publishing retargeted robot references at up to 28.9 Hz from a 30 Hz camera stream. For object interaction (e.g., box picking), the system generated structured robot trials, which, while not always leading to high-success open-loop manipulation policies due to accumulated errors, provided valuable task-relevant initialization data for training closed-loop policies. The system also transferred diverse whole-body motions like dancing to non-humanoid robots.

**Text-Conditioned Motion Execution:** X-Morph successfully utilized language commands to drive robot behaviors. By converting a text prompt into a corresponding human motion (via generation or retrieval) and then retargeting it to the target morphology, the system enabled text-to-skill execution. This illustrates the reuse of existing human-motion interfaces for non-humanoid robot control.

**Retargeted Motions as Downstream Behavior Priors:** Qualitatively, the research showed that X-Morph's generated loco-manipulation priors could serve as effective initializations for downstream robot learning. A hexapod door-opening behavior, initialized using a prior from a Kimodo-generated human interaction, learned a coordinated reaching and body-positioning strategy in simulation. This suggests that human motion can provide structured, morphology-specific initial behaviors for complex tasks, without claiming superior sample efficiency over learning from scratch.

#### 4.2. Reference Quality and Generalization to Unseen Human Clips

**Impact of Physics-Aware Corrector:** Ablation studies on 33 matched locomotion clips for the Go2 quadruped demonstrated the necessity and effectiveness of the physics-aware corrector. While the raw retargeting network produced plausible motions, they contained physical inaccuracies. The corrector significantly improved reference quality, reducing near-ground foot slip by 27.2%, ground penetration by 46.9%, contact-height error by 44.2%, and low-speed floating by 39.3%. These improvements were achieved with only a moderate reduction in the root-speed scale (Table 1).

**Improved Tracking with Corrected References:** Training the reference-conditioned tracker with corrected references yielded improved tracking performance. For the Yuna hexapod in sim2sim tracking, using corrected references led to a 17.4% reduction in joint Mean Absolute Error (MAE), a 27.5% reduction in yaw-rate Root Mean Square Error (RMSE), and a 17.5% decrease in mean foot slip compared to using uncorrected references (Table 2). This indicates that the corrected references lead to more stable and accurate closed-loop control.

**Generalization to Unseen Motions:** The policies demonstrated generalization capabilities on held-out human motions and motion variants not used during retargeter or tracker training. Qualitatively, the robots maintained stability even during larger limb excursions and side-specific interaction motions. This suggests that the learned representation captures fundamental, reusable body-part structures and task intents rather than merely memorizing specific target joint trajectories (e.g., Go2 performing right-paw manipulation despite being trained only on left-paw manipulation, and Yuna performing front-leg manipulation with greater diversity than its training data, as shown in Appendix Figure 7).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The X-Morph framework presents a method for transforming human motion into executable behaviors for non-humanoid legged robots. This work addresses the challenge of scarce behavior data for these robot morphologies by offering a practical pathway to repurpose abundant human motion.

The significance of X-Morph lies in several key areas:

1. **Scalable Behavior Acquisition:** It proposes a method for learning a wide array of behaviors for diverse legged robots (quadrupeds, hexapods, quadruped manipulators) by leveraging existing large-scale human motion datasets. This could substantially reduce the manual effort and time traditionally required for skill acquisition in non-humanoid robots.
2. **Bridging the Reality Gap:** By integrating physics-aware correction and robust reinforcement learning-based tracking, X-Morph aims to produce physically consistent and trackable robot behaviors. This directly addresses a critical limitation of previous retargeting approaches, which often yield visually plausible but dynamically impractical motions.
3. **Interactive Control Paradigms:** The system enables interactive control through modalities such as live video teleoperation and text-conditioned motion generation. This capability could enhance human-robot interaction by allowing intuitive, natural language or visual commands to direct complex robot behaviors.
4. **Reusable Behavior Priors:** X-Morph demonstrates that retargeted human motions can serve as valuable behavior priors for downstream reinforcement learning tasks. This provides structured initializations for robots, potentially accelerating the learning of more complex tasks and mirroring the successful use of human motion priors in humanoid control.
5. **Broadening the Scope of Motion Datasets:** The work suggests that large human motion datasets, traditionally focused on humanoid applications, can be utilized as reusable behavioral scaffolds for a wider class of legged robots. This expands the utility and impact of such datasets across the robotics domain.

**Potential Impact:**
The potential impact of X-Morph includes accelerating the development of versatile non-humanoid legged robots capable of performing complex locomotion and loco-manipulation tasks in diverse environments. By providing a principled way to transfer and adapt human-like behaviors, it could facilitate the deployment of these robots in applications such as exploration, inspection, logistics, and assistance, where adaptable and dexterous locomotion is crucial. Furthermore, it could foster advancements in human-robot collaboration by enabling more natural and intuitive interfaces for robot control.

**Limitations:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations. X-Morph does not guarantee that every human motion can be meaningfully transferred to every morphology, highlighting that the system relies on manually specified semantic correspondences between source and target body parts. Poor correspondence definitions can lead to references that are difficult to correct or track. The physics-aware corrector, while effective, is a residual model and not a full trajectory optimizer, thus not guaranteeing dynamic feasibility. Current experiments primarily focus on flat or moderately structured terrain, implying that more complex terrains may necessitate terrain-aware correction and tracking. Finally, the performance of video-driven deployment is dependent on the quality and latency of monocular human pose estimation, which can be affected by factors like occlusion, rapid motion, or unusual camera viewpoints.
