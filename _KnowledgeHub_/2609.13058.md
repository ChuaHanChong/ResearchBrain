# Expert-Space Exploration in MoE Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> ESRL (Expert-Space Exploration Reinforcement Learning) treats Mixture-of-Experts routing as an extra exploration dimension in LLM RL post-training. During rollout, it keeps high-confidence anchor experts. It perturbs router logits only within a candidate pool, using entropy-adaptive Gaussian noise. GRPO updates then replay the recorded expert paths. On Qwen3-30B-A3B, it lifts average math Pass@1/Pass@8 over GRPO by 3.2/4.5 points with no extra sampling cost.

## Problem

- RL work on Mixture-of-Experts LLMs mostly targets optimization stability and training efficiency, treating expert selection as a fixed component rather than a source of exploration.
- Deterministic top-K routing activates the same expert path for a given prefix, so rollout exploration is confined to token sampling within one fixed sparse subnetwork.
- Group-relative methods like GRPO lose advantage signal as policies concentrate and rollouts homogenize, while naive router noise activates unsuitable experts and degrades rollout quality.

## Method

- ==Anchored expert sampling==: keeps the top anchored experts from the original logits. The remaining exploratory experts are picked by adding ==Gaussian noise== only within a ==top-M candidate pool==, with gating weights from unperturbed logits.
- ==Entropy-adaptive noise==: the per-token, per-layer noise scale interpolates between a minimum and maximum by one minus normalized ==router entropy==, perturbing sharp routers more and uncertain ones less.
- ==Rollout Routing Replay== integration: records each trajectory's activated expert sets and forces them during ==GRPO== optimization, so explored experts receive gradients and rollout-training routing mismatch shrinks.

## Results

- On Qwen3-30B-A3B math, average Pass@1/Pass@8 reaches **42.1/64.2** versus GRPO's **38.9/59.7**, beating GSPO, RO-GRPO and GRPO-R3. Pass@8 also rises on top-1 Sigma (**+2.7**) and shared-expert Moonlight (**+3.5**).
- On science and code, average Pass@8 rises from **64.9** to **77.0** over GRPO, including **+23.3** on GPQA and **+15.1** on MMLU-Pro. On the Instruct model, Pass@1 improves from **63.4** to **68.9**.
- In ablations, removing anchors collapses Pass@1 to about **32%** at noise 0.8. Fixed noise (**38.7**) underperforms GRPO-R3 (**39.7**), adaptive noise reaches **42.1**, and ESRL with **64** samples beats GRPO-R3 with **128**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Model internals can serve as an exploration axis: in sparse models, perturbing which computation path runs gives temperature-like diversity that complements token sampling.
> - Structured perturbation beats raw noise: anchoring confident components and limiting alternatives to a plausible pool preserves quality, much like top-k or top-p truncation in decoding.
> - Any stochastic discrete choice made at rollout should be replayed at update time; otherwise explored paths receive no gradient and importance ratios drift.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning has become an important method for post-training large language models, particularly for tasks with automatically verifiable outcomes such as mathematical reasoning, programming, and scientific question answering. Methods such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) reduce the memory and computational requirements of PPO-style training by estimating advantages from groups of responses to the same prompt rather than using a separate value model. In these methods, the diversity of sampled trajectories is important because the learning signal depends on reward differences within each group.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models introduce conditional computation. Instead of activating all model parameters for every token, a router selects a small subset of experts according to the current hidden state. Existing research on reinforcement learning for MoE models has generally concentrated on optimization stability, routing collapse, expert-load balancing, and the mismatch between the routing decisions used during rollout and those recomputed during policy optimization. Examples discussed in the paper include GSPO, which uses sequence-level policy ratios; Rollout Routing Replay (R3), which aligns rollout and training routes; auxiliary load-balancing losses; and reward-based routing methods such as RO-GRPO.

The paper identifies a different and relatively underexplored role for the MoE router. Standard top-\(K\) routing is deterministic: for a given prefix and model state, the same experts are selected. Token-level sampling can produce different prefixes and therefore different later routing decisions, but it does not directly explore alternative expert paths under the same context. The authors therefore frame expert routing as an additional exploration space, alongside the conventional token space. Their contribution is to make this exploration stochastic but controlled, with the aim of increasing trajectory diversity without activating unsuitable experts or creating substantial rollout–training inconsistency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The work has two primary objectives.

First, it investigates whether perturbing expert-selection decisions produces meaningful changes in language-model generation. The authors examine this at several levels: expert utilization, next-token distributions, sequence-level diversity, and response quality. The central hypothesis is that changing the sparse computation path can alter the output distribution even when the input prefix and model parameters remain fixed.

Second, it introduces a practical reinforcement-learning framework, called **Expert-Space Exploration Reinforcement Learning (ESRL)**, that uses routing perturbation to improve the quality and informativeness of RL rollouts. The motivation follows from a limitation of group-relative RL. As training progresses, the policy can become concentrated, causing multiple responses within a group to become similar. If all responses are correct or all are incorrect, the group provides little or no relative-advantage signal. Increasing token sampling temperature can restore diversity, but excessive temperature may reduce response quality. The authors seek an additional diversity mechanism that is specific to the architecture of MoE models.

Their preliminary experiments show that unrestricted routing noise has a temperature-like effect: it increases output diversity and changes the next-token distribution. However, it can also select experts that are poorly matched to the current hidden state. ESRL is designed to address this quality–diversity trade-off by preserving reliable experts, limiting alternatives to a plausible candidate pool, adapting perturbation to router confidence, and replaying the selected expert paths during policy optimization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Preliminary Analysis of Routing Perturbation

The authors first add Gaussian noise to router logits in a Qwen3-30B-A3B model containing 48 MoE layers, 128 routed experts, and eight active experts per token. They measure:

- The rate at which all-token and top-1 expert assignments change.
- The Jaccard similarity between standard and perturbed top-\(K\) expert sets.
- Expert-load coefficient of variation across layers.
- Changes in the rank of the original top-1 token.
- Jaccard similarity between the original and perturbed top-20 token sets.
- PCA projections of first-token logits.
- Sequence-level diversity using Self-BLEU, where lower values indicate more diverse responses.

Increasing noise causes more expert assignments to change, lower top-\(K\) overlap, and more balanced expert utilization. It also shifts and disperses the next-token distribution. At the sequence level, routing perturbation lowers Self-BLEU in a manner comparable to increasing decoding temperature. However, direct perturbation generally gives lower accuracy than temperature sampling at similar diversity levels, confirming that unconstrained routing can produce unsuitable computation paths.

#### 3.2. Entropy-Adaptive Noise

For each token and MoE layer, the router logits are converted into a probability distribution over all experts. The normalized entropy is computed as

\[
H=-\frac{1}{\log E}\sum_e p_e\log p_e,
\]

where \(H\) ranges from zero to one. ESRL sets the noise scale according to

\[
\sigma=\sigma_{\min}+(\sigma_{\max}-\sigma_{\min})(1-H).
\]

Thus, sharply concentrated, low-entropy router distributions receive stronger perturbations because their standard routing decisions are more repetitive and confident. High-entropy distributions receive weaker perturbations because they are already uncertain and may be more sensitive to disruption.

#### 3.3. Anchored Expert Sampling

ESRL divides the \(K\) active expert slots into anchored and exploratory slots. The anchored experts are selected using ordinary top-\(K\) routing from the original router logits. They preserve a subset of high-confidence computation paths.

For the remaining slots, ESRL constructs a candidate pool from the \(M\) highest-scoring experts not already selected as anchors. Noise is added only within this candidate pool. The exploratory experts are then chosen by ranking the perturbed logits. The final activated set is the union of the anchored and exploratory experts.

Importantly, the perturbed logits determine only which experts are activated. The aggregation weights are computed from the original router logits. This preserves the router’s original relative confidence among the selected experts and avoids the larger policy deviation observed when noisy logits are also used for weighting.

#### 3.4. Rollout Routing Replay and GRPO Optimization

During rollout, ESRL records the selected expert set for every generated token and every MoE layer. During optimization, the standard top-\(K\) decision is bypassed, and the recorded expert set is replayed. The current router recomputes the mixture weights over that fixed set, allowing the explored experts to receive gradients from the trajectories they helped generate.

The policy is optimized with GRPO. The token-level importance ratio compares the current policy evaluated under the replayed route with the rollout policy probability. Group-relative advantages are calculated from the rewards of responses generated for the same prompt, and the standard clipped GRPO objective is applied. ESRL therefore changes rollout exploration while leaving the reward function and the underlying policy-optimization objective unchanged.

For top-1 MoE models, the candidate-pool restriction is retained but anchoring is disabled because there is only one routed slot. In models with shared experts, shared experts remain permanently active and are excluded from perturbation.

#### 3.5. Experimental Design

The main experiments use the Math training dataset, batches of 256 prompts, eight responses per prompt, and 300 training iterations. The default rollout settings are temperature 0.8, top-\(p\) 0.95, and a maximum response length of 8,192 tokens. The evaluated MoE backbones are:

- Qwen3-30B-A3B, activating eight of 128 routed experts.
- Sigma-20B-A0.5B, using top-1 routing.
- Moonlight-16B-A3B, activating six of 64 routed experts, including two shared experts.

Baselines include GRPO, GRPO with R3, GSPO, auxiliary load-balancing loss, N-Sampling, and RO-GRPO. Additional experiments use science and code data, a stronger Qwen3 instruct model, different temperatures and group sizes, alternative noise distributions, and several routing ablations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Performance Across MoE Architectures

On mathematical benchmarks, ESRL improves the aggregate results of GRPO across all three tested MoE architectures.

For Qwen3-30B-A3B, ESRL achieves an average **Pass@1 of 42.1** and **Pass@8 of 64.2**, compared with 38.9 and 59.7 for GRPO. These correspond to improvements of **3.2 and 4.5 percentage points**, respectively. ESRL also exceeds GRPO-R3, GSPO, and RO-GRPO on the aggregate metrics.

For Sigma-20B-A0.5B, ESRL reaches 21.1 Pass@1 and 41.3 Pass@8, compared with 20.4 and 38.6 for GRPO. For Moonlight-16B-A3B, it reaches 28.8 and 51.0, compared with 27.5 and 47.4. The improvements are therefore present in top-1, multi-expert, and shared-expert routing configurations, although their magnitude varies by model and benchmark.

#### 4.2. Generalization to Science and Code

Using Qwen3-30B-A3B, ESRL improves the average science-and-code results from **53.2 to 55.9 Pass@1** and from **64.9 to 77.0 Pass@8** relative to GRPO. The largest changes occur in Pass@8: GPQA increases by 23.3 points, MMLU-Pro by 15.1 points, and MMLU-Redux by 8.2 points. LiveCodeBench improves by 1.9 points in Pass@1 and 2.1 points in Pass@8.

The larger Pass@8 gains suggest that ESRL expands the coverage of successful reasoning trajectories rather than only improving the most likely single response.

#### 4.3. Robustness and Training Dynamics

ESRL outperforms GRPO-R3 over the tested rollout temperatures, with particularly clear gains at lower temperatures where token-level sampling is more conservative. It also performs better with smaller rollout groups. According to the paper, ESRL with 64 responses can outperform GRPO-R3 with 128 responses, and ESRL with 128 can exceed GRPO-R3 with 256. ESRL maintains a higher proportion of informative groups containing both successful and unsuccessful responses, especially later in training.

Ablations support all three design components. Fixed noise performs below GRPO-R3, whereas entropy-adaptive noise yields the reported 42.1 Pass@1 and 64.2 Pass@8. Removing anchors makes performance highly sensitive to noise and causes near-collapse at larger scales. Moderate candidate pools work better than unrestricted or very broad pools. Using original, rather than perturbed, routing logits for mixture weights is also important.

Routing perturbation reduces expert-load concentration during training, as measured by both coefficient of variation and load-imbalance factor. Finally, a two-stage experiment shows that routing-only exploration can support training without token sampling. Joint routing and token exploration produces the strongest aggregate Pass@8, while decoupled training obtains slightly higher average Pass@1 and stronger high-budget Pass@N behavior.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is conceptual and methodological. It treats the internal sparse computation path of an MoE model as an explicit exploration dimension for reinforcement learning. This extends the usual view that exploration occurs only through sampled output tokens. Because ESRL operates at rollout time and does not alter the reward function or GRPO objective, it is compatible in principle with optimization-level, reward-level, and routing-replay improvements.

Potential applications include improving reasoning coverage under limited sampling budgets, reducing dependence on high decoding temperatures, and mitigating expert concentration during RL training. The approach may be particularly relevant for large MoE systems where many experts are available but deterministic routing repeatedly uses a narrow subset.

The evidence is currently limited to the architectures, datasets, and training configurations reported in the manuscript. The method introduces additional route-recording requirements, and the paper’s claim of no additional computational cost does not eliminate possible memory, communication, or systems overhead from storing and replaying token-level expert paths. Further evaluation would be needed on larger task distributions, non-verifiable rewards, longer training runs, and production-scale deployments. Nevertheless, the results indicate that controlled expert-path exploration is a distinct and potentially complementary mechanism for improving reinforcement learning in sparse language models.
