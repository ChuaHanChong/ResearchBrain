# Vision-based Manipulation from Single Human Video with Open-World Object Graphs

> [!summary] Summary
> An algorithm called ORION enables robots to learn vision-based manipulation skills from a single, actionless human video by constructing Open-world Object Graphs. Leveraging recent vision foundation models, the system demonstrates an average success rate of 74.4% across diverse tasks and generalizes to novel objects and environments.

## Problem

- Traditional robot skill acquisition methods are costly, data-intensive, and often require extensive manual engineering or specialized simulators, limiting scalability.
- Existing imitation learning approaches struggle to generalize manipulation skills to novel objects, varied visual backgrounds, or different spatial layouts in unstructured, 'open-world' environments.
- Learning from casual human videos is challenging due to the lack of ground-truth actions, object models, and often depth information, which many prior methods require.

## Method

- **ORION** extracts an ==object-centric manipulation plan== from a single human video, using ==vision foundation models== (VLMs, ==Grounded-SAM==, ==Co-Tracker==, ==FoundationPose==) for identifying, segmenting, and tracking task-relevant objects.
- It constructs ==Open-world Object Graphs (OOGs)== at keyframes, representing object geometries (3D point clouds or 6-DoF poses) and interaction cues, with ==scene alignment== to a common plane.
- A robot policy ==warps== the demonstration's object-centric trajectories to the robot's current environment and optimizes ==end-effector actions== based on these warped trajectories and grip information.

## Results

- ORION achieved an average success rate of **74.4%** across short-horizon and long-horizon manipulation tasks, demonstrating its capability to generalize from single human videos in open-world settings.
- The object-centric approach significantly outperformed a human hand-motion imitation baseline, which largely failed to achieve target object configurations.
- The method proved robust to diverse video conditions and performed strongly even with RGB-only demonstration videos, achieving an **85.3%** average success rate on such tasks by leveraging monocular depth and 6-DoF pose estimation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An object-centric spatiotemporal abstraction, focusing on object motions and contact relations, is critical for generalizing manipulation skills from human demonstrators to robots, outperforming direct human hand-motion imitation.
> - Robust tracking of object keypoints (Co-Tracker) or 6-DoF poses from monocular RGB video is essential for accurate keyframe discovery and motion modeling, especially in diverse, unstructured scenes.
> - The effective integration of open-world vision foundation models provides a powerful perceptual backend for robots, allowing them to interpret complex human videos and adapt to unseen objects and environments without explicit pre-programming.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses a critical and long-standing challenge in robotics: enabling robots to acquire complex manipulation skills efficiently and robustly in unstructured, real-world environments. Traditionally, robot skill acquisition has relied on costly and time-consuming methods such as manual engineering, extensive teleoperation data collection, or sophisticated simulation environments. The authors highlight these limitations, noting that conventional approaches often require separate data for each new object, location, or even minor changes in visual backgrounds.

This work positions itself within the burgeoning field of **imitation learning from human videos**, which seeks to leverage the vast, readily available reservoir of human demonstration videos from the internet or mobile devices. Prior research in this area has primarily focused on pre-training visual representations or value functions, or learning generative models for human motions. However, the authors argue that these methods often fall short in open-world settings because they do not explicitly capture object interactions and states in 3D space, leading to poor generalization.

The paper introduces the concept of **"open-world imitation from observation,"** a particularly challenging sub-problem. In this setting, a robot must learn from a *single, actionless human video* without prior knowledge of object models, ground-truth actions, or behaviors shown. The goal is to generalize to novel objects and vastly different environments (visual backgrounds, camera angles, spatial layouts). This contrasts with much of prior imitation learning from observation (IfO) work that often assumes known physical states or simulators of demonstrated tasks.

A key enabler for this work is the recent advent of **vision foundation models** (e.g., GPT-4o, Grounded-SAM, Co-Tracker, FoundationPose, InstantMesh). These models, pre-trained on internet-scale data, excel at understanding open-vocabulary visual concepts, allowing robots to recognize and localize objects in natural videos without requiring explicit programming or extensive domain-specific training. This capability is crucial for moving beyond constrained lab settings to truly "open-world" scenarios.

The paper differentiates itself from related work in several key ways:
- **One-shot imitation learning:** While prior one-shot methods exist, they often require extensive meta-training data or real-world self-play, which can be costly or limited to reset-free tasks. ORION, in contrast, learns from a *single video* without such prerequisites.
- **Other open-world imitation approaches:** Some recent works also tackle open-world settings but often rely on stronger geometric inputs (e.g., calibrated RGB-D, multi-view captures, pre-scanned object meshes) that are not typically available in casual single-view videos. ORION requires only RGB or RGB-D video.
- **Demonstrator-centric vs. Object-centric:** The paper explicitly contrasts its **object-centric** approach with demonstrator-centric methods like OKAMI, which focus on reconstructing human body/hand motions. ORION prioritizes replicating *object trajectories*, offering independence from the specific human demonstrator's pose and potentially broader applicability.

By addressing the gaps in these areas, ORION represents a significant step towards scalable, generalizable robot manipulation in complex, uncontrolled environments, pushing the boundaries of what robots can learn from readily available human demonstrations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a robot to learn **vision-based manipulation skills from a single human video demonstration in an "open-world" setting**. This encompasses several specific goals:

1. **Open-World Generalization:** To develop an algorithm that allows a robot to manipulate *novel objects* and perform tasks robustly even when deployment environments differ significantly from the demonstration video in terms of visual backgrounds, camera angles, spatial layouts of objects, and instances of objects from the same categories. This is a core challenge, as prior methods often struggle with such variations.
2. **Single-Video Learning:** To learn a manipulation policy from *just one* human video demonstration, which is actionless (state-only) and requires no prior robot-specific data, models, or ground-truth annotations for the objects or scenes involved. This drastically reduces the data collection burden compared to conventional approaches.
3. **Leveraging Foundation Models:** To effectively integrate recent advances in open-world vision foundation models to interpret complex visual scenes, identify task-relevant objects, and understand their interactions without explicit pre-programming for specific object categories.
4. **Object-Centric Representation:** To design a robust spatiotemporal abstraction that focuses on the task-relevant objects and their interactions, rather than on human-specific movements, to facilitate generalization across different embodiments (human demonstrator to robot executor).
5. **Diverse Demonstration Sources:** To demonstrate the feasibility of learning from videos captured by common mobile devices (e.g., iPad), internet videos (YouTube), or even synthetic videos (e.g., Veo 2), highlighting the method's flexibility and potential for broad adoption.
6. **Scalability to Long-Horizon Tasks:** To show that the proposed method can effectively handle both short-horizon (single-step) and long-horizon (multi-step) manipulation tasks without significant performance degradation.

The overarching motivation stems from the desire to overcome the limitations of current robot learning paradigms, which are often costly, data-intensive, and lack generalization capabilities in unstructured environments. By tapping into the vast and growing pool of human demonstration videos, the authors aim to pave the way for a future where robots can rapidly acquire new skills by simply observing humans in everyday scenarios, thereby scaling up robot autonomy significantly.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **ORION (Open-woRld video ImitatiON)**, an algorithm designed to extract object-centric manipulation plans from a single human video and derive generalizable policies. ORION's methodology can be broken down into two main phases: manipulation plan generation from the video and robot policy for action synthesis.

#### 3.1. Manipulation Plan Generation From Video (Algorithm 1, Figure 2)

This phase aims to create a spatiotemporal abstraction of the human demonstration video, focusing on object states and motions.

1. **Tracking Task-Relevant Objects:**
 - **Object Identification:** ORION first leverages a Vision-Language Model (VLM, e.g., GPT-4o) to process video frames and generate a list of strings describing task-relevant objects, indicating whether they are manipulated or serve as references.
 - **Segmentation:** Grounded-SAM is then used with these descriptions to generate segmentation masks for the task-relevant objects in the first video frame.
 - **Tracking Over Time:** For the remaining frames, object segmentation is propagated using a Video Object Segmentation Model (Cutie) to ensure object permanence (RGB-D case).
 - **RGB-only Specifics:** If only RGB video is available, monocular depth estimation is used, and object meshes are generated (InstantMesh). Then, FoundationPose is employed to track the 6-DoF pose trajectories of objects across the video, rather than point cloud tracking.

2. **Discovering Keyframes:**
 - ORION identifies "keyframes" where significant changes in object contact relations or motion patterns occur. This is based on the insight that manipulation subgoals often coincide with velocity changes.
 - **Keypoint Tracking:** In the RGB-D case, a track-any-point (TAP) model, Co-Tracker, is used to track a subset of 3D points within object segmentations across the video, handling occlusions.
 - **Changepoint Detection:** An unsupervised changepoint detection algorithm (kernel-based) analyzes the velocity statistics of these keypoint trajectories (or 6-DoF poses in RGB-only) to automatically discover keyframes. For RGB-only, K=2 (start and end frames) is often sufficient.

3. **Generating Open-world Object Graphs (OOGs):**
 - For each discovered keyframe, an OOG (denoted G) is constructed. An OOG is a graph-based, object-centric representation that models the scene.
 - **High-level nodes:**
 - **Object Nodes:** Each task-relevant object gets a node, storing colored 3D point clouds (from back-projected segmentation and depth) or estimated object meshes (for RGB-only).
 - **Grasp Node:** A specialized node stores interaction cues like contact points and grip status (open/closed). In RGB-D, HaMeR (hand reconstruction model) helps pinpoint hand locations. In RGB-only, grasp heuristics based on object geometry and 6-DoF pose are used.
 - **Low-level nodes:**
 - **Point Nodes:** Each corresponds to an object keypoint. It stores an object-centric feature trajectory (3D points or 6-DoF poses) describing the object's motion between keyframes.
 - **Edges:** Object and hand nodes are fully connected, with binary attributes indicating contact. Point nodes are connected to their respective object nodes.
 - **Scene Alignment:** To resolve spatial ambiguity, the table plane is estimated, and all OOG features are transformed to align with the world's xy-plane, allowing for robust determination of contact relations.

The output of this phase is a sequence of OOGs, {G₀..., Gₗ}, representing the manipulation plan.

#### 3.2. Robot Policy to Synthesize Actions (Algorithm 2, Figure 3)

This phase translates the extracted OOG sequence into robot-executable actions, emphasizing object motion similarities.

1. **Retrieving OOGs from the Plan:**
 - At test time, the robot's current observation is used to generate an OOG (Gᴿᵒ) via the same vision pipeline.
 - ORION matches Gᴿᵒ with an OOG from the plan (Gₗ) based on contact relations, identifying the current state and the next target state (Gₗ₊₁).
 - The "target object" (object to be manipulated) and "reference object" (spatial anchor) are identified using motion features (RGB-D) or VLM classification (RGB-only).
 - The corresponding point clouds/poses of these objects in the demonstration (ˆo) and robot rollout (o) scenes are extracted.

2. **Predicting Object Motions (Trajectory Warping):**
 - ORION warps the object-centric feature trajectory (τᵛ_target) from the demonstration to fit the robot's current environment.
 - **RGB-D (Translation Warping - Algorithm 3):** Global registration aligns demonstration and robot point clouds to determine new start/end positions for keypoints. The original trajectory is then normalized, scaled, and rotated to fit these new conditions, preserving directional and curvature patterns.
 - **RGB-only (Rotation Warping - Algorithm 4):** For 6-DoF pose trajectories, both translation and rotation warping are performed. An alignment rotation is computed to match the reference-to-target object direction. Spherical linear interpolation (SLERP) is used to smoothly transition between the normalized and boundary-constrained rotations.

3. **Optimizing Robot Actions:**
 - Given the warped trajectory (τᴿᵒ_target), ORION optimizes a sequence of SE(3) transformations for the robot's end-effector. The objective is to minimize the squared difference between consecutive points on the predicted trajectory and the end-effector's corresponding motion. This ensures actions are relative to the objects' current locations.
 - In the RGB-only case, the warped 6-DoF pose trajectory of the object directly provides the desired end-effector poses.
 - **Grip Information:** The optimized SE(3) sequence is augmented with grip status (open/closed) and interaction points from the grasp node.
 - **Robot Control:** Inverse kinematics converts the SE(3) sequence into joint commands, which are executed using a joint impedance controller, ensuring smooth and compliant motion.

This iterative process continues until the task is completed or fails, allowing the robot to adapt to varying conditions throughout the task execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments systematically evaluate ORION's efficacy across various tasks and conditions, yielding several key findings:

1. **Overall Efficacy in Open-World Setting:** ORION successfully constructs manipulation policies from single human videos in the open-world. Across varied short-horizon and long-horizon tasks, using both RGB-D and RGB-only demonstration videos, ORION achieved an average success rate of **74.4%**. This demonstrates its capability to learn from minimal human input and generalize to unseen environments and objects.

2. **Superiority of Object-Centric Abstraction:** Comparing ORION against the **HAND-MOTION-IMITATION** baseline (which predicts robot actions based on human hand trajectories), ORION showed significantly better performance. The baseline yielded low success rates, primarily failing to achieve target object configurations (e.g., misplacing an object). This result strongly supports ORION's design choice of focusing on *object-centric information* rather than direct imitation of human hand motions, which is crucial for generalization across different robot embodiments and spatial layouts.

3. **Importance of TAP Keypoints for Motion Modeling:** The ablation study comparing ORION (using Co-Tracker for TAP keypoints) with the **DENSE-CORRESPONDENCE** baseline (using optical flows) highlighted the critical role of robust motion tracking. The optical flow baseline performed drastically worse, especially on more precise assembly tasks like "Simple-boat-assembly." This was attributed to optical flow misidentifying keyframes in smooth transitions rather than at significant changes in object contact relations, leading to incorrect manipulation plans. This validates ORION's choice of using TAP keypoints for accurate keyframe discovery and motion modeling.

4. **Robustness to Diverse Video Conditions:** Experiments on the "Mug-on-coaster" task using demonstration videos recorded in very different visual conditions (kitchen, office, outdoor) showed **no statistically significant difference** in policy performance. This indicates that ORION is robust to variations in visual backgrounds, camera perspectives, and general recording environments, further supporting its "open-world" capabilities.

5. **Scalability to Long-Horizon Tasks:** ORION demonstrated effectiveness in scaling to multi-step, long-horizon tasks. Comparisons between short-horizon tasks (e.g., "Mug-on-coaster", "Simple-boat-assembly") and their long-horizon counterparts ("Rearrange-mug-box", "Complex-boat-assembly") revealed no significant performance drop. This suggests that ORION's sequential OOG plan structure effectively decomposes complex tasks into manageable subgoals, allowing it to tackle longer manipulation sequences.

6. **Efficacy with RGB-Only Demonstration Videos:** A significant finding is ORION's strong performance even when demonstration videos lack depth information (RGB-only). With an average success rate of **85.3%** on RGB-only tasks, the method proved capable of leveraging modern monocular depth estimation and 6-DoF pose tracking models to compensate for the absence of depth. The ability to learn from diverse RGB-only sources (YouTube, Veo 2, and author's own recordings with depth removed) further underscores its broad applicability. Interestingly, for "Succulents-in-llama-vase," the RGB-only pipeline performed comparably, or even slightly better, than the RGB-D pipeline, suggesting that the trade-off in noise from depth-based global registration versus pose estimation can sometimes favor the RGB-only approach due to strong world knowledge embedded in foundation models.

Overall, the results strongly support ORION as a novel and effective method for learning generalizable robot manipulation skills from single human video demonstrations in open-world, unstructured environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a significant advancement in robot manipulation and imitation learning, with substantial potential impact across several fronts:

1. **Democratizing Robot Skill Acquisition:** By enabling robots to learn complex manipulation tasks from a single, actionless human video, ORION dramatically lowers the barrier to entry for teaching robots new skills. This moves beyond the need for costly manual engineering, extensive teleoperation, or elaborate simulators, making skill acquisition more accessible to non-experts and researchers without specialized robotics labs.

2. **Scalability through Human Data:** The ability to learn from readily available "in-the-wild" human videos (captured by mobile devices, from the internet, or even synthetically generated) unlocks a vast, virtually unlimited data source for robot learning. This is crucial for scaling up the repertoire of robot skills and enabling robots to operate in diverse, unstructured environments where pre-programmed solutions are impractical.

3. **Enhanced Generalization:** ORION's core contribution lies in its ability to construct policies that generalize across four critical dimensions: varying visual backgrounds, different camera angles, novel object instances (from the same categories), and varied spatial layouts. This level of generalization is a key challenge in robotics and allows robots to perform tasks robustly even when deployment conditions differ significantly from the demonstration.

4. **Leveraging Foundation Models for Robotics:** The paper showcases a powerful integration of state-of-the-art vision foundation models (VLMs, segmentation, tracking, 3D reconstruction) as a robust perceptual backend for robot learning. This demonstrates a promising paradigm where general-purpose AI models, trained on massive internet data, can imbue robots with broad common sense and open-world understanding capabilities, reducing the need for domain-specific robot perception training.

5. **Novel Object-Centric Representation:** The introduction of Open-world Object Graphs (OOGs) is a notable methodological contribution. This graph-based, object-centric representation effectively abstracts away irrelevant scene details and focuses on task-relevant objects and their interactions, facilitating the transfer of skills from humans to robots. Its two-level hierarchy and explicit modeling of contact relations provide a robust framework for planning and execution.

6. **Robustness to Sensory Input:** The demonstrated efficacy with both RGB-D and RGB-only videos significantly expands the applicability of ORION. The competitive performance with RGB-only input, by strategically leveraging advanced monocular depth estimation and 6-DoF pose models, means that even casual videos without depth sensors can be utilized for robot learning.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Goal Definition:** It primarily considers task goals defined by contact states, thereby avoiding the ambiguities of spatial relations (e.g., "placing items next to an object"). Inferring broader human intentions from videos is a complex future direction.
- **Stationary Camera Assumption:** The current method assumes demonstrations are captured with a stationary camera. Most real-world videos, however, involve moving cameras. Future work could explore reconstructing dynamic scenes from moving cameras, which would require estimating geometry of both static and dynamic objects while preserving real-world scale.
- **Perceptual Backend Dependency:** While leveraging foundation models is a strength, the performance is inherently tied to their capabilities. Continuous improvement in vision models will directly boost ORION's success rates and expand the range of tasks it can handle.

In conclusion, ORION represents a significant stride towards practical and scalable robot manipulation through human observation. Its object-centric approach, coupled with the power of modern vision foundation models, offers a compelling vision for how robots can learn new skills autonomously from the rich, diverse visual data available in the human world.
