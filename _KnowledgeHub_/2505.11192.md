# FALCON: False-Negative Aware Learning of Contrastive Negatives in Vision-Language Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Korea University developed FALCON, a learning-based approach to dynamically schedule negative samples during Vision-Language Pretraining (VLP) by balancing hard and false negatives. This method consistently achieves superior performance across image-text retrieval, VQA, and NLVR2 tasks, for example, attaining R@1 of 62.28 for Text Retrieval on MSCOCO, and proves robust across various VLP frameworks and noisy datasets.

## Problem

- Hard negative sampling, crucial for robust representations in Vision-Language Pretraining (VLP), significantly increases the risk of encountering "false negatives."
- False negatives (semantically related pairs mistakenly treated as negatives) degrade representation quality by forcing the model to separate embeddings that should be close.
- Existing solutions rely on static heuristics, fixed pretrained models, or predefined thresholds, which fail to adapt to dynamic, anchor-specific negative hardness and evolving embedding spaces during training.

## Method

- FALCON introduces a trainable =="negative mining scheduler" (πϕ)== implemented as a lightweight residual MLP, which dynamically determines an appropriate negative hardness level (quantile value) for each anchor.
- The scheduler is trained to maximize the reduction in ==masked language modeling (MLM) loss== after a VLP model update, serving as a proxy signal for improved cross-modal alignment.
- Mini-batches are constructed by iteratively selecting samples based on the scheduler's predicted quantile, enabling ==adaptive balancing of hard and false negatives== during VLP.

## Results

- FALCON consistently achieved the best performance on image-text retrieval, VQA, and NLVR2 tasks, e.g., R@1 of **62.28** for Text Retrieval on MSCOCO, outperforming heuristic baselines.
- The method demonstrates adaptive scheduling behavior, initially sampling harder negatives and then lowering the target quantile as training progresses, leading to better performance across all training stages and improved wall-clock time efficiency.
- FALCON proved robust across various VLP architectures (ALBEF, BLIP-2, SigLIP-2) and datasets, including noisy web-crawled data, with performance gains being more pronounced on cleaner datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The optimal balance between hard and false negatives is not static but is anchor-specific and dynamically evolves throughout the VLP training process.
> - Using the reduction in masked language modeling (MLM) loss serves as an effective, robust, and non-exploitable proxy signal for guiding a negative mining scheduler, outperforming contrastive losses.
> - An instance-level, adaptive negative sampling strategy, guided by a learned scheduler, is crucial for mitigating false negatives and improving cross-modal representation quality in diverse VLP datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language Pretraining (VLP) aims to learn joint representations from large-scale image-text pairs, enabling models to perform well on various downstream tasks such as image-text retrieval (IRTR), visual question answering (VQA), and natural language for visual reasoning (NLVR). Contemporary VLP models commonly leverage self-supervised objectives, including masked language modeling (MLM), image-text contrastive (ITC), and image-text matching (ITM) losses.

The effectiveness of contrastive and matching objectives is significantly influenced by the quality and hardness of negative samples used during training. Hard negative sampling, which involves selecting negative examples that are semantically similar to the anchor, has been identified as crucial for improving the learned embedding space. Consequently, substantial research has focused on developing strategies to identify and sample such informative negatives.

However, a challenge associated with hard negative sampling is the increased likelihood of encountering "false negatives." False negatives occur when a semantically related image-text pair is mistakenly classified as a negative, particularly prevalent in large-scale, web-crawled datasets characterized by many-to-many correspondences between images and texts. Incorporating these false negatives into contrastive learning can introduce conflicting supervision signals, potentially degrading the quality of the learned representations by forcing the model to separate embeddings that should ideally remain close.

Prior efforts to mitigate false negatives have typically involved two main approaches: refining the loss function to reduce the impact of false negatives, or relabeling identified false negatives as positives, often relying on strong pretrained models or fixed heuristic thresholds. These methods, however, frequently depend on the reliability of the pretrained models and the generality of the heuristics, which may limit their adaptability across diverse datasets and training conditions.

FALCON addresses this challenge by proposing a learning-based approach to adaptively manage the trade-off between hard and false negatives. Rather than relying on static heuristics or external pretrained models for false negative detection, FALCON integrates a dynamic negative mining scheduler into the mini-batch construction process. This positions FALCON within the broader landscape of VLP research as an attempt to enhance representation learning by refining the core data sampling mechanism in a more adaptive and data-driven manner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop FALCON (False-negative Aware Learning of Contrastive Negatives), a learning-based mini-batch construction strategy for Vision-Language Pretraining (VLP). This strategy is designed to adaptively balance the trade-off between selecting informative hard negatives and avoiding misleading false negatives during the training process. By optimizing this balance, FALCON aims to improve the quality of learned cross-modal representations.

The motivation for developing such a strategy stems from two core limitations observed in existing VLP negative sampling methods:

1. **Optimal Similarity is Anchor-Specific and Evolves Over Training:** Current VLP methods often rely on fixed heuristics, such as selecting samples with high cosine similarity to the anchor, to identify hard negatives. While effective to some extent, the optimal range of similarity for negative mining is neither static nor universal. It varies depending on the specific anchor instance (e.g., semantically complex anchors are harder to align and learn more slowly than simple ones) and changes throughout the training process as the embedding space matures.
 - Early in training or for complex anchors, selecting negatives with lower similarity is necessary to minimize the risk of false negatives.
 - Later in training or for simple anchors, the model can safely incorporate more similar (harder) negatives as the embedding space becomes more refined.
 - Fixed similarity thresholds or simple progressive schedules fail to capture this dynamic, leading to either an excess of easy negatives (inefficient learning) or a high incidence of false negatives (conflicting supervision). Empirical data presented in the paper indicates that the false negative rate substantially increases when highly similar samples are selected, especially as training progresses and false negatives associated with simple anchors cluster at higher similarity quantiles.

2. **Adoption of Pretrained Models is Not a Complete Solution:** Some prior works attempt to address false negatives by using strong pretrained models (e.g., BLIP-129M) to detect and filter potential false negatives. These models typically assign an Image-Text Matching (ITM) score, and pairs exceeding a certain threshold are relabeled as positives. While this approach can mitigate nonstationarity associated with learned similarity metrics, its effectiveness remains limited by several factors:
 - **Anchor-Specific Reliability:** Pretrained models can accurately identify positives for simple descriptions or images, but they often struggle with more complex or out-of-distribution scenarios, assigning low ITM scores even to genuinely aligned pairs. This inconsistency means a fixed ITM threshold will be either too conservative for simple pairs or insufficient for complex ones.
 - **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** The conventional two-stage approach (select similar negatives, then filter with a pretrained model) is highly sensitive to the chosen filtering threshold. If the threshold is misconfigured, the training can be severely hampered by an excessively high false negative rate.

FALCON aims to overcome these limitations by introducing an adaptive, learning-based mechanism that dynamically adjusts the negative sampling strategy. This avoids the reliance on fixed heuristics or static pretrained model outputs, allowing the model to make more informed decisions about negative sample hardness on a per-instance basis and throughout the training curriculum.

The paper highlights three main contributions:
- FALCON is presented as the first learning-based approach to explicitly schedule the trade-off between hard and false negatives in contrastive learning within the VLP domain.
- The work proposes a novel architecture for a negative mining scheduler and an associated training scheme, designed for computational efficiency.
- Extensive experimental validation is conducted across three distinct vision-language frameworks (ALBEF, BLIP-2, SigLIP-2) and various downstream tasks and settings, demonstrating the method's effectiveness and robustness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FALCON proposes a learning-based mini-batch construction strategy that dynamically adjusts the hardness of negative samples during Vision-Language Pretraining (VLP). The core idea is to train a scheduler, denoted as $\pi_\phi$, to predict an appropriate hardness quantile for each anchor instance, thereby balancing informative hard negatives with misleading false negatives.

#### 3.1. VLP Loss Formulation

The work is situated within the standard VLP training paradigm, which optimizes a combination of contrastive and generative objectives. The overall VLP loss, $L_{VLP}$, is typically expressed as:
$L_{VLP}(\theta):= E_{(V,T) \sim V,T} [ L_{ITC}(V, T; \theta) + L_{ITM}(V, T; \theta) + L_{MLM}(V, T; \theta) ]$
where $L_{ITC}$ is the Image-Text Contrastive loss, $L_{ITM}$ is the Image-Text Matching loss, and $L_{MLM}$ is the Masked Language Modeling loss (or other generative objectives like ITG for BLIP models). The quality of the learned embedding space, particularly through ITC and ITM, is highly dependent on the selection of negative samples.

#### 3.2. Construction of Mini-batch Using Negative Mining Scheduler

FALCON extends the mini-batch grouping strategy proposed by GRIT-VLP. GRIT-VLP constructs mini-batches by iteratively selecting the most semantically similar sample to the most recently chosen one within a localized search space, thereby promoting hard negative mining. FALCON refines this by not strictly selecting the *most* similar sample, but instead selecting a sample based on a *specified level of similarity* (a hardness quantile $q$) determined by the scheduler $\pi_\phi$.

The mini-batch construction process unfolds as follows:
1. **Initialization:** A local search space $M$ is defined, and an initial anchor sample is uniformly selected from this space.
2. **Scheduler Prediction:** For each anchor, the scheduler $\pi_\phi$ predicts a hardness quantile value $q \in$. A higher $q$ indicates a desire for harder (more similar) negatives, while a lower $q$ favors easier (less similar) negatives.
3. **Iterative Sampling:** Subsequent samples are chosen from the remaining unselected candidates within $M$ according to the predicted hardness quantile $q$ relative to the most recently added sample. This process is repeated until a mini-batch of size $B$ is formed.
4. **VLP Model Update:** The constructed mini-batch is then used to update the parameters $\theta$ of the VLP model via gradient descent.
5. **Search Space Iteration:** This mini-batch construction and VLP update procedure is iteratively applied until all candidates within the search space $M$ have been utilized.

#### 3.3. Design of Negative Mining Scheduler ($\pi_\phi$)

To enable $\pi_\phi$ to adaptively select appropriate negative sample hardness, it is provided with information about the similarity distributions among candidate samples in the search space.
1. **Similarity Matrix Construction:** A unified similarity matrix $S$ is computed by summing the image-to-text (I2T) and text-to-image (T2I) pairwise cosine similarity matrices. These similarities are calculated using the [CLS] embeddings of image and text representations, which are efficiently maintained in a queue to avoid redundant forward passes.
2. **Input to Scheduler:** Instead of raw similarity values, a compact representation $\tilde{S}$ is generated by subsampling $m$ evenly spaced quantiles from each row of $S$. This $\tilde{S}$ is then row-wise normalized using softmax to mitigate the impact of changing similarity scales over training. This normalized matrix $\tilde{S}$ serves as the input to the scheduler.
3. **Scheduler Architecture:** The scheduler $\pi_\phi$ is implemented as a lightweight 4-layer residual MLP. To ensure permutation equivariance over the rows of $\tilde{S}$ without resorting to heavier architectures, the rows are sorted before being fed into the MLP. The MLP outputs parameters ($\alpha, \beta$) for Beta distributions, which model the desired hardness (similarity quantile $q$) for negative samples.

#### 3.4. Training Negative Mining Scheduler

The scheduler $\pi_\phi$ is trained to encourage mini-batch constructions that enhance cross-modal alignment within the learned representation space.
1. **Training Signal:** The objective for $\pi_\phi$ is to maximize the reduction in the masked language modeling loss ($L_{MLM}$) measured before and after updating the VLP model with a mini-batch constructed by $\pi_\phi$. This change, $\Delta_{V,T}^k:= L_{MLM}(V, T; \theta^k) - L_{MLM}(V, T; \theta^{k+1})$, serves as a proxy for improvements in the VLP model's ability to integrate visual context into language understanding via cross-attention, thus reflecting refined semantic alignment. The choice of $L_{MLM}$ is motivated by its robustness to the hardness of negatives, unlike $L_{ITC}$ or $L_{ITM}$ which can incentivize the selection of trivial negatives.
2. **Optimization:** The scheduler parameters $\phi$ are updated via gradient ascent using the log-derivative trick:
 $\phi^{k+1} = \phi^k + \gamma \cdot E_{\pi_{\phi^k}} [\Delta_{V,T}^k \cdot \nabla_{\phi^k} \log \pi_{\phi^k}(V, T | \tilde{S})]$
 where $\gamma$ is the step size.
3. **Implementation Details:**
 - **Warm-Start:** During the first epoch, when the cached embeddings are insufficient, a standard uniform mini-batch sampling procedure is used, and $\pi_\phi$ is not applied or trained. From the second epoch onward, cached embeddings from the previous epoch are used to compute similarity matrices.
 - **Dynamic Updates:** Cached embeddings are updated epoch-by-epoch, ensuring the scheduler's decisions reflect the evolving state of the VLP model.
 - **Generalization:** Instance-level scheduling and shuffling of the search space at the end of each epoch are applied to prevent overfitting and expose the scheduler to a diverse set of instances.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of FALCON involved extensive experimentation across various vision-language tasks and VLP frameworks, demonstrating its effectiveness in mitigating false negatives and improving representation learning.

#### 4.1. Comparison to Heuristic Negative Mining Methods

Experiments conducted on the MSCOCO dataset, a human-curated dataset with minimal noise, showed that FALCON achieved improved performance across image-text retrieval (IRTR), visual question answering (VQA), and natural language for visual reasoning (NLVR2) tasks compared to heuristic negative mining methods (Table 1).
- Methods that aggressively sample hard negatives without explicitly addressing false negatives (e.g., GRIT-VLP, DiHT) exhibited degraded performance, indicating the detrimental effect of conflicting supervision signals from false negatives.
- Methods that attempt to mitigate false negatives using pretrained models (e.g., MAFA, SRCL) also showed limitations, which the authors attribute to their reliance on fixed similarity thresholds or static filters that fail to adapt to the dynamic nature of optimal similarity during training.
- FALCON's adaptive strategy, which dynamically adjusts the target quantile for negative sampling (Figure 6), consistently outperformed these baselines. It was observed that FALCON initially samples harder negatives to accelerate learning and then adaptively lowers its target quantile as training progresses and the risk of false negatives increases.
- In terms of computational efficiency (Figure 7), FALCON achieved better performance relative to wall-clock time compared to baselines like GRIT-VLP, MAFA, and ALBEF, despite requiring additional forward passes for its scheduler network.

#### 4.2. Robustness on Standard 4M Datasets

To assess robustness in more challenging, noisy environments, FALCON was evaluated on large-scale web-crawled datasets (Conceptual Captions, SBU Captions, Visual Genome, and MSCOCO, totaling 4M image-text pairs). FALCON achieved the best overall performance in zero-shot and fine-tuned image-text retrieval (Table 2). However, the performance gap between FALCON and baselines was less pronounced than on MSCOCO, a phenomenon the authors attribute to the significant noise and semantic misalignment inherent in web-crawled captions. Further experiments with BLIP-captioned versions of these datasets (Table 9) and the DataComp benchmark (Tables 10, 11) showed increased performance gaps, supporting the hypothesis that caption quality influences the scheduler's ability to estimate the hard/false negative trade-off.

#### 4.3. Compatibility with BLIP-2

FALCON was integrated into the BLIP-2 framework by replacing the Masked Language Modeling (MLM) loss with the Image-Grounded Text Generation (ITG) loss as the proxy for cross-modal alignment. Experiments on MSCOCO demonstrated that FALCON yielded significant performance gains across most tasks, including retrieval, VQA, OKVQA, and GQA (Table 3, 12). This indicates that FALCON's benefits can extend to VLP models employing different generative objectives that leverage joint text and image encoder information.

#### 4.4. Compatibility with SigLIP-2

When applied to SigLIP-2, using its generative objectives (image captioning, grounded captioning, and automatic referring expression prediction) as proxy signals, FALCON showed significant gains in image-to-text retrieval but only marginal gains in text-to-image retrieval (Table 4). This asymmetry was attributed to the nature of the auxiliary losses in SigLIP-2, which are primarily computed through the vision encoder and transformer decoder, with limited participation from the text encoder. This suggests that for optimal effectiveness, FALCON's proxy signal should integrate information from both vision and text encoders.

#### 4.5. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies provided further insights into FALCON's design choices (Table 5):
- **Search Space Size (|M|):** Increasing the search space size initially improved performance by allowing the scheduler more options for negative sample selection. Beyond a certain size, performance remained comparable, indicating robustness to varying distributions of hard and false negatives within larger pools. This contrasts with baseline methods that often degrade with larger search spaces due to increased false negative risk.
- **Training Objectives of $\pi_\phi$:** Using $L_{MLM}$ as the proxy for cross-modal alignment improvement proved most effective. Incorporating contrastive objectives ($L_{ITC}$ and $L_{ITM}$) for training $\pi_\phi$ degraded VLP performance, likely because these objectives can encourage the exploitation of trivial negatives, undermining the goal of finding appropriately hard samples.
- **Instance-level vs. Batch-level Scheduling:** Dynamically assigning a distinct similarity level for each anchor instance (Instance-level) significantly outperformed selecting a single similarity level shared across all anchors in a mini-batch (Batch-level). This highlights the importance of anchor-dependent similarity selection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces FALCON, a learning-based approach to a fundamental challenge in Vision-Language Pretraining (VLP): the trade-off between selecting informative hard negative samples and avoiding detrimental false negatives. This work addresses a critical limitation of existing heuristic or pretrained model-dependent negative sampling strategies.

The primary significance lies in the development of a dynamic, adaptive mechanism for mini-batch construction, which moves beyond fixed thresholds or static filters. By employing a negative mining scheduler that learns to balance the hardness of negatives on an instance-specific basis and throughout the training process, FALCON contributes to more robust and effective cross-modal representation learning.

The demonstrated improvements across various VLP frameworks (ALBEF, BLIP-2, SigLIP-2) and a diverse set of downstream tasks (IRTR, VQA, NLVR2) indicate the generalizability and robustness of FALCON. Its ability to perform effectively even on large-scale, noisy web-crawled datasets suggests its practical utility in real-world VLP scenarios where data quality is often a concern. Furthermore, FALCON achieves these benefits without incurring substantial additional computational overhead, making it a viable enhancement for VLP training pipelines.

This work highlights that optimal negative sampling is not a fixed parameter but a dynamic quantity that depends on the specific anchor and the evolving state of the embedding space. By providing a learning-based solution to this problem, FALCON offers a methodological advance in contrastive learning within the VLP domain.

The findings also provide insights for future research. The observed asymmetry in performance improvements when integrated with SigLIP-2 suggests that the choice of proxy signal for cross-modal alignment is crucial and should ideally incorporate information from both vision and text encoders for maximum impact. This opens avenues for developing more comprehensive, learning-based scheduling strategies that are independent of auxiliary objectives, potentially broadening their applicability across various contrastive learning paradigms.

Moreover, as VLP backbones form the foundation for emerging Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs), improvements in these foundational models, such as those offered by false-negative-aware strategies like FALCON, are likely to contribute to the continued development and enhanced effectiveness of LVLMs in understanding and generating multimodal content.
