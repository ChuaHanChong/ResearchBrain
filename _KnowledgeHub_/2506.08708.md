# PhyBlock: A Progressive Benchmark for Physical Understanding and Planning via 3D Block Assembly

> [!summary] Summary
> PhyBlock introduces a progressive benchmark to evaluate Vision-Language Models' physical understanding and multi-step planning capabilities through 3D block assembly tasks, revealing that state-of-the-art models exhibit a steep performance drop with increasing task complexity and struggle with spatial orientation and dependency reasoning. It comprehensively evaluates 23 leading VLMs and establishes a significant gap between current AI and human performance.

## Problem

- Existing benchmarks for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) primarily focus on perception or single-step reasoning, failing to assess multi-step, long-horizon planning crucial for complex real-world tasks.
- Many current benchmarks neglect realistic physical interactions (e.g., gravity, stability), leading to an idealized evaluation environment that limits practical applicability.
- State-of-the-art VLMs lack robust understanding of physical interactions, spatial relationships, and object stability necessary for grounded planning in 3D environments.

## Method

- **PhyBlock** introduces a two-branch benchmark built within a high-fidelity physics simulator (Genesis) using interactive 3D blocks to evaluate physical understanding and planning.
- The benchmark features 400 hierarchical 3D block assembly tasks categorized into four ascending difficulty levels, assessing capabilities from basic perception to advanced spatial planning.
- It includes 2,200 rigorously curated Visual Question Answering (VQA) samples across 16 semantic categories, with a focus on dynamic reasoning like counterfactual and predictive questions.

## Results

- All evaluated VLMs showed a steep decline in assembly planning F1 scores, dropping over 50% from Level-1 to Level-4 tasks, with Claude 3.7 Sonnet falling from 76.78% to 41.82%.
- Models demonstrated a pronounced perception-reasoning gap, performing well on basic object VQA but dramatically collapsing on dynamic reasoning queries like counterfactual and causal inference.
- Error analysis revealed pervasive issues across architectures, primarily incorrect spatial orientation and violations of fundamental physical dependencies, accounting for the majority of planning mistakes.
- Human experts achieved approximately 94.7% accuracy, underscoring a considerable gap between current VLM capabilities and human-level physical understanding and planning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLMs exhibit a steep performance drop, often by more than half, from simple to complex assembly tasks, indicating limitations in long-horizon planning and dependency reasoning.
> - A significant perception-reasoning gap exists; models excel at identifying object properties but struggle dramatically with VQA questions requiring dynamic reasoning or causal inference.
> - Universal error modes across all VLM architectures include incorrect spatial orientation and failure to meet fundamental support dependencies during assembly.
> - Enabling 'thinking mode' or chain-of-thought prompting provides minimal to no performance improvement for physical understanding and planning tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

PhyBlock directly addresses critical limitations within the existing landscape of benchmarks for Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and embodied AI. Current benchmarks, while making significant strides, often fall short in two key areas:
1. **Perception Dominance without Long-Horizon Planning:** Many benchmarks primarily focus on evaluating perceptual understanding or single-step reasoning, such as object detection, semantic segmentation, or simple interaction skills (e.g., RLBench, LIBERO for manipulation; navigation-oriented benchmarks like Habitat). They fail to rigorously assess VLMs' capabilities for multi-step, long-horizon planning that is essential for complex real-world tasks. Models might excel at identifying objects but struggle to sequence actions coherently over an extended period to achieve a goal.
2. **Unrealistic Physical Assumptions:** A prevalent issue is the assumption of idealized object states, often neglecting real-world physical interactions like gravity, friction, stability, and structural dependencies. This detachment from physical reality limits the practical applicability of models evaluated on such benchmarks. Recent efforts like Reflective Planning and VLABench advance multi-stage reasoning but still lack explicit physical grounding.

PhyBlock bridges these gaps by introducing the first benchmark specifically designed for 3D block assembly, a task that inherently embodies fundamental physical concepts (stability, support, spatial relationships) and demands progressive, multi-step planning. By leveraging a physics-based simulator, PhyBlock creates realistic 3D scenes that dynamically respond to interactions, enabling systematic evaluations of increasingly complex tasks. This work is crucial for advancing embodied agents, especially those functioning as high-level planners in hierarchical systems (like GR00T-N1 or Helix), which require profound physical awareness to generate actionable, physically plausible plans for low-level controllers. It shifts the focus from mere perception to grounded reasoning and proactive problem-solving in a physically constrained environment, pushing VLMs beyond descriptive capabilities towards predictive and planning intelligence.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind PhyBlock stems from the observation that despite the impressive advancements of modern Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like GPT-4o, Claude-3.7, and Gemini 2.0 in multimodal reasoning, their ability to comprehend physical phenomena, especially within structured 3D environments, remains significantly limited. For embodied agents operating in the real world, understanding physical interactions, spatial relationships, object stability, and structural dependencies is paramount. Without this understanding, VLMs cannot reliably serve as high-level planners that generate robust and physically-grounded action sequences.

The key objectives of this research are:
1. **Introduce a Progressive Benchmark:** To create PhyBlock, a novel and comprehensive benchmark that systematically assesses VLMs' physical understanding and planning capabilities through robotic 3D block assembly tasks.
2. **Evaluate Progressive Spatial Reasoning and Physical Comprehension:** To design a benchmark that integrates a four-level cognitive hierarchy assembly task and targeted Visual Question Answering (VQA) samples. This dual approach aims to evaluate models across a spectrum of difficulty, from basic object properties and spatial relationships to complex scene understanding and dynamic reasoning.
3. **Benchmark State-of-the-Art VLMs:** To conduct a comprehensive evaluation of 23 leading open-source and closed-source VLMs to identify their strengths, limitations, and common failure modes in physically grounded, multi-step planning scenarios.
4. **Diagnose Performance Gaps:** To precisely diagnose why current VLMs struggle with physical understanding and planning, particularly focusing on errors in spatial orientation, dependency reasoning, and long-horizon planning.
5. **Advance Embodied Reasoning:** To position PhyBlock as a unified testbed that facilitates progress in embodied reasoning, helping to bridge the gap between abstract vision-language understanding and practical real-world physical problem-solving.

Ultimately, the authors are motivated to develop a rigorous yardstick that can measure whether VLMs truly internalize how objects interact in three dimensions, a critical step towards building truly capable and intelligent embodied agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PhyBlock is designed as a comprehensive two-branch benchmark built on interactive 3D blocks within a high-fidelity physics simulator, Genesis.

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Capability Levels (Assembly Planning)

Inspired by cognitive-developmental research on structured block play, the benchmark features 400 systematically constructed 3D block assembly tasks categorized into four ascending difficulty tiers:
- **Level-1 Basic Perception:** Focuses on identifying and selecting correct blocks based on visual features (up to 4 blocks).
- **Level-2 Simple Combinations:** Evaluates elementary structural reasoning and valid assembly sequences with basic support relations (fewer than 6 blocks, up to 3 layers).
- **Level-3 Complex Structures:** Requires planning optimal, stable assembly sequences with significantly more complex dependency relationships (up to 12 blocks, max 8 layers).
- **Level-4 Advanced Spatial Planning:** Challenges models with systematic planning for large-scale structures under intricate spatial constraints (up to 22 components, demanding global understanding and long-horizon reasoning).

#### 3.2. AOV-Based Assembly Evaluation

To rigorously evaluate assembly plans, the authors introduce an Activity-on-Vertex (AOV) network. This graph-based representation models blocks as vertices and their assembly dependencies as directed edges. This allows for:
- **Fine-grained assessment:** Computing intermediate metrics for partial completion.
- **Diagnostic analysis:** Identifying failure modes via systematic analysis of dependency violations.
- **Planning robustness evaluation:** Accounting for variations in valid assembly sequences.
The evaluation metrics used are precision, recall, and F1-score, calculated based on step-wise correctness against AOV constraints, where correct steps are True Positives (TP), incorrect are False Positives (FP), and missing steps are False Negatives (FN). Micro-F1 is used for overall performance.

Two evaluation settings are considered for assembly planning:
- **Pose-Constrained Evaluation (Setting A):** Requires exact matches in shape, color, and *orientation* for a step to be correct, emphasizing precise geometric reasoning.
- **Topology-Oriented Evaluation (Setting B):** Relaxes the orientation constraint, deeming a step correct if shape and color match, focusing on structural planning and task decomposition.

#### 3.3. Physics Understanding VQA

The second branch consists of 2,200 rigorously curated Visual Question Answering (VQA) samples, spanning 16 semantic categories grouped into four key dimensions:
- **Object Property:** Shape, Color, Size, Number (basic attributes).
- **Object Relationship:** Relative Positions, Absolute Positions, Relative Dependency, Relative Rotations (spatial and logical relationships).
- **Scene Understanding:** Object Counting, Layer Counting, Type Counting, Viewpoint (holistic perception).
- **Dynamic Reasoning:** Counterfactual, Predictive, Ordering, Affordance (physical dynamics, stability, action consequences).
VQA performance is measured by accuracy.

#### 3.4. Dataset Construction

- **Simulated Block Assets:** Eight LEGO-like block geometries in five distinct colors are modeled in Blender and simulated in Genesis with realistic rigid-body dynamics (friction, density).
- **Block Assembly Scenes:** 400 scenes were constructed (150 manually, augmented to 400) within Genesis, with precise JSON annotations for block pose, type, color, layer, and topological dependencies. This ensures physical validity and uniqueness.
- **VQA Construction:**
 - **LLM-based Static VQA:** Utilized GPT-4o with prompt engineering to generate questions for basic perception tasks (e.g., color, shape).
 - **Simulation-driven Dynamic VQA:** Developed a data engine based on Genesis to generate questions for complex dynamic reasoning (e.g., Predictive, Counterfactual) by applying perturbations and rendering scene evolutions as videos/images.
- **Human Validation:** All generated VQA tasks underwent a robust multi-stage human validation process to ensure quality and rigor.

#### 3.5. Evaluated Models and Baselines

The benchmark evaluated 23 state-of-the-art Vision-Language Models, including 14 proprietary models (GPT-O1, GPT-4o, GPT-4o-mini, Gemini-1.5 series, Gemini-2.0 series, Qwen-VL-Max, Claude 3.5 series, Claude 3.7 series) and 11 open-source models (LLaVA-OneVision series, Qwen 2.5 series, InternVL 2.5 series). A random baseline and a human expert upper bound (~94.7% accuracy) were also established for comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The comprehensive evaluation of 23 state-of-the-art VLMs on PhyBlock revealed consistent and critical limitations:

1. **Steep Performance Cliff with Increasing Task Complexity:** All evaluated VLMs demonstrated a significant decline in assembly planning performance as task complexity increased. The mean planning F1 scores dropped by more than half from the simplest (Level-1) to the most challenging (Level-4) assembly tiers. For instance, Claude 3.7 Sonnet, the best performer, saw its F1 score drop from 76.78% at Level-1 to 41.82% at Level-4. No model maintained high recall when tasks demanded long-horizon sequencing or hidden-support reasoning, indicating a fundamental struggle with complex, multi-step planning.

2. **Pronounced Perception–Reasoning Gap:** Models generally excelled at low-level VQA questions related to object properties (e.g., color, shape, object counting), achieving high accuracy. However, their accuracy dramatically collapsed on reasoning-heavy VQA queries, particularly those involving counterfactual reasoning, causal inference, and affordance prediction. This gap mirrors the failures observed in assembly tasks, suggesting that models perceive objects well but lack the explicit understanding of physical dynamics required for grounded reasoning. GPT-o3 achieved the highest overall VQA accuracy (70.0%), performing strongly on object properties and relationships, but still showing limitations in dynamic reasoning tasks.

3. **Universal Error Modes: Spatial Orientation and Dependency Reasoning:** Error analysis of assembly steps revealed two pervasive error modes across all architectures:
 - **(i) Incorrect Orientation Chosen:** Models frequently mis-estimated the precise orientation of blocks, leading to systematically incorrect poses during assembly. This was a dominant error type, even for larger, more capable models.
 - **(ii) Dependency Not Met:** Models consistently ignored fundamental support dependencies, violating basic stability rules crucial for physical assembly. This led to structural collapses and propagating errors throughout the planning process. These two error types accounted for the majority of mistakes.

4. **Negligible Benefit from "Thinking Mode" Prompting:** Notably, enabling "thinking mode" or chain-of-thought prompting in larger models (e.g., Claude 3.7 Sonnet-Thinking) offered little to no significant performance improvement on the assembly tasks. The error distribution, particularly concerning orientation and dependency issues, remained virtually unchanged. This suggests that simply generating more text tokens or explicit reasoning chains does not compensate for missing intrinsic physical priors or robust spatial understanding within the models.

5. **Impact of Model Scale and Pose Constraints:** Robust multi-step reasoning capabilities only emerged in models with over 100 billion parameters. Smaller models (e.g., InternVL2.5-1B) performed poorly. Furthermore, an ablation study showed a significant performance boost when strict pose alignment constraints (Euler Pose Consideration) were relaxed, highlighting that models are particularly challenged by precise 3D spatial reasoning, especially regarding orientation.

6. **Significant Gap with Human Performance:** Human experts achieved an average accuracy of approximately 94.7% on sampled tasks, providing an empirical upper bound and underscoring the considerable gap between current VLM capabilities and human-level physical understanding and planning.

Collectively, these findings expose a fundamental shortfall in current multimodal pre-training paradigms: while VLMs excel at perceiving static objects and generating text, they still lack the deep physical insight and sequential reasoning necessary for reliable embodied planning in dynamic, physically grounded environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PhyBlock represents a significant contribution to the fields of embodied AI, robotics, and Vision-Language Model research, with several important implications and potential impacts:

1. **Unified Testbed for Embodied Reasoning:** PhyBlock establishes a much-needed unified testbed that rigorously evaluates both physical understanding and multi-step planning within a 3D environment. By combining hierarchical assembly tasks with fine-grained VQA, it provides a comprehensive diagnostic tool to pinpoint specific strengths and weaknesses of VLMs, going beyond mere perceptual tasks. This is crucial for systematically advancing the capabilities of embodied agents.

2. **Exposing Fundamental VLM Limitations:** The benchmark's empirical findings clearly expose critical shortcomings in current state-of-the-art VLMs. The "steep performance cliff" with increasing complexity and the "perception–reasoning gap" highlight that current multimodal pre-training paradigms do not sufficiently equip models with the physical priors and sequential reasoning needed for real-world interactions. This provides a clear roadmap for future architectural innovations and training regimes.

3. **Guiding Future VLM Development:** By identifying universal error modes, particularly persistent difficulties in spatial orientation and dependency reasoning, PhyBlock offers actionable insights for VLM developers. Future research can focus on integrating explicit physics reasoning, more robust 3D spatial representations, and interactive feedback mechanisms into VLM architectures. This could involve novel ways to encode physical laws, learn affordances, or leverage simulation environments for more targeted training.

4. **Advancing Robust Robotic Planning:** For the robotics community, PhyBlock provides a challenging benchmark that simulates complex assembly tasks, moving beyond simplified manipulation scenarios. The AOV-based evaluation, with its ability to diagnose failure modes, can help in developing more robust and fault-tolerant high-level planners for robotic systems. Bridging the gap between abstract VLM reasoning and real-world robotic execution is a critical step towards general-purpose robots.

5. **Cognitively-Inspired Design:** The benchmark's inspiration from human cognitive development (structured block play) suggests a path towards designing AI systems that learn and reason in ways more analogous to humans. This cognitive grounding could lead to more intuitive and generalizable AI.

6. **Extensibility and Scalability:** The modular design of block assets, the parametric scene construction in Genesis, and the LLM/simulation-driven VQA generation pipelines make PhyBlock highly extensible. Researchers can easily scale the dataset to millions of scenes and incorporate low-level motor actions, further enriching the benchmark and bringing it closer to real-world deployment scenarios.

In conclusion, PhyBlock not only provides a robust framework for evaluating physical intelligence but also serves as a critical diagnostic tool, charting a clear path toward developing truly capable and physically aware embodied agents that can effectively reason about and interact with the complex 3D world.
