# Multi-Stage Reinforcement Learning for Non-Prehensile Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces a Multi-Stage Reinforcement Learning (MRLM) framework for non-prehensile manipulation, which effectively learns diverse manipulation skills for objects with complex geometries. The approach achieves high success rates for occluded grasping tasks in simulation and demonstrates zero-shot transfer to real robots.

## Problem

- Existing non-prehensile manipulation methods often focus on single skills, restricting applicability to complex, multi-stage tasks and struggling with objects of intricate geometries.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) for long-horizon manipulation suffers from low sample efficiency due to sparse rewards and inconsistent feature spaces when using separate networks for point cloud states and goals.
- Inconsistent contact dynamics between robots and objects in non-prehensile tasks make robust scene description, planning, and control challenging.

## Method

- A multi-stage RL approach (**MRLM**) decomposes complex tasks into four sequential stages, guided by intermediate object poses and candidate contact points, to improve sample efficiency and learn diverse skills.
- A ==point cloud-based state-goal fusion== representation integrates observation states and task goals into a single input for the policy network (**P2ManNet**), handling complex object shapes and ensuring consistent feature spaces.
- The learning process utilizes an off-policy ==SAC== algorithm with a novel ==spatially-reachable distance metric== in the reward function, and automatic replay buffer compaction, to guide optimal contact and accelerate learning.

## Results

- MRLM achieved **100% success** for 'far grasp' and **95%** for 'close grasp' in simulation, demonstrating that key components like point cloud representation, the novel distance metric, and buffer compaction are critical for performance.
- The policy exhibited complex emergent behaviors, including precise object flipping and tilting, and demonstrated recovery capabilities for certain manipulation mistakes.
- Achieved zero-shot sim-to-real transfer with **95% success** for 'close grasp' and **67.5%** for 'far grasp' on a real UR5e robot, showing generalization to unseen object shapes and varying environmental parameters.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing long-horizon, complex manipulation tasks into smaller, more manageable stages with structured rewards significantly improves sample efficiency and enables the learning of intricate skill sequences.
> - Fusing observation and goal information within a unified point cloud input creates a consistent feature space, crucial for robust policies that understand object geometry and facilitate precise manipulation.
> - A specialized spatially-reachable distance metric, rather than Euclidean distance, is vital for guiding the gripper's approach to contact points, preventing local optima and promoting stable object interaction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Non-prehensile manipulation, which involves interacting with objects without directly grasping them, represents a complex domain within robotics. This field aims to enhance robot dexterity, enabling robots to perform intricate tasks that extend beyond simple pick-and-place operations. A significant challenge in non-prehensile manipulation is the inconsistent contact dynamics between the robot and the object, which necessitates robust scene description, planning, and control mechanisms.

Existing approaches to non-prehensile manipulation largely fall into two categories: planning-based methods and learning-based methods. Planning-based methods, which include techniques like random-sampling, search algorithms, and optimization, typically involve pre-calculating the entire manipulation process. While these methods can achieve complex tasks, they are often computationally intensive and may struggle with objects of intricate geometries or dynamic, unpredictable environments.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as an alternative to improve planning efficiency for manipulation tasks. However, current RL-based methods also face limitations. Many prior works focus on learning a single manipulation skill, such as reaching or pushing, which restricts their applicability to more flexible, multi-stage tasks. Other RL approaches simplify object representations, for instance, by representing objects as minimum circumscribed boxes, which limits their effectiveness for complex shapes. Furthermore, training end-to-end long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards in RL often leads to low sample efficiency, making the learning process time-consuming. Some RL approaches utilize point clouds for state estimation or skill learning; however, these methods frequently employ separate networks to encode point cloud states and task goals, potentially leading to inconsistent feature spaces that hinder policy convergence.

This research by Wang et al. positions itself to address these limitations by proposing a multi-stage reinforcement learning framework that can learn diverse manipulation skills. Specifically, it aims to:
- Enable flexible, multi-skill manipulation beyond single primitives.
- Handle complex object geometries using a point cloud-based representation.
- Improve sample efficiency for long-horizon tasks by decomposing them into more manageable stages with structured rewards.
- Maintain consistent feature spaces by fusing state and goal representations within the point cloud input.
- Develop robust policies capable of zero-shot transfer from simulation to the real world.

By tackling these challenges, the work contributes to advancing the state of the art in non-prehensile manipulation, particularly for tasks requiring sequences of dynamic interactions with various objects and environmental constraints.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a Multi-Stage Reinforcement Learning approach for non-prehensile Manipulation (MRLM) that can execute flexible object manipulation tasks. This overarching goal is motivated by the inherent limitations observed in prior research within the field of non-prehensile manipulation.

Specifically, the key objectives and their underlying motivations include:

1. **Enabling Flexible and Multi-Skill Manipulation:** Previous non-prehensile methods often focused on learning single manipulation skills (e.g., reach, push). This limitation restricts robots to simple tasks and prevents them from adapting to varying environmental conditions or achieving complex reorientation maneuvers. MRLM aims to overcome this by allowing the robot to explore and acquire arbitrary skills to accomplish a task defined by a desired 6-DOF object pose, thereby fostering more flexible object manipulation.

2. **Handling Complex Object Shapes:** Many existing RL approaches represent objects using simplified geometric primitives, such as minimum circumscribed boxes. This simplification renders them unsuitable for manipulating objects with complex or irregular geometries, which are common in real-world scenarios. The motivation for MRLM is to utilize point clouds for object representation, enabling the system to perceive and interact with objects of diverse and complex shapes more effectively.

3. **Improving Sample Efficiency for Long-Horizon Tasks:** End-to-end reinforcement learning for long-horizon manipulation tasks often suffers from sparse rewards, leading to inefficient learning and prolonged training times. MRLM addresses this by decomposing the overall task into multiple, shorter stages. Each stage has more immediate and easily explorable goals, which is intended to significantly improve the sample efficiency of the learning process.

4. **Addressing Inconsistent Contact Dynamics and Changing Environmental Conditions:** Non-prehensile manipulation inherently involves dynamic and often unpredictable contact points between the robot, object, and environment. These inconsistent contacts lead to continuously changing dynamic models, making planning and control challenging. MRLM is designed to operate by dynamically calculating intermediate object poses and contact points based on static equilibrium principles, allowing the robot to adapt to these changes and maintain stable manipulation.

5. **Ensuring Consistent Feature Spaces in Point Cloud-based RL:** When using point clouds for manipulation, some prior works utilized separate networks for encoding observation states and task goals. This separation can result in inconsistent feature spaces, impeding the convergence of the learning policy. A key objective for MRLM is to propose a state-goal fusion representation that simultaneously encodes both observations and goals within a unified point cloud input, thereby maintaining feature space consistency and facilitating more stable policy learning.

6. **Developing Robustness and Generalization Capabilities:** The ultimate motivation is to develop a manipulation system that not only performs effectively in simulated environments but also exhibits strong generalization to unseen objects and zero-shot transferability to the real world. This requires integrating techniques like Automatic Domain Randomization (ADR) and carefully designed reward functions to ensure policies are robust against variations in physical properties and environmental parameters. The "Occluded Grasping" task is chosen as a specific application and motivation, as it embodies the challenges of initially blocked grasp configurations, requiring complex non-prehensile reorientation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MRLM (Multi-Stage Reinforcement Learning for Non-Prehensile Manipulation) is an approach designed to address complex non-prehensile manipulation tasks by dividing them into multiple, more manageable stages.

#### 3.1. Task Decomposition

The core of MRLM involves decomposing a complex manipulation task into four distinct stages based on the switching of object poses and contact points between the gripper and the object. This decomposition strategy is applied to tasks like occluded grasping.

1. **Intermediate Object Pose (O_m) Calculation:** For a given grasp pose, an intermediate object pose O_m is calculated based on static equilibrium principles.
 - If the target grasp pose `g` is close to a wall, `O_m` is obtained by rotating the object 90 degrees around the world x-axis. This transformation is defined by a matrix (Equation 1) incorporating object dimensions.
 - If `g` is away from the wall, `O_m` involves translating the object along the y-axis and rotating it -45 degrees around the world x-axis, with the object leaning against the wall. This pose is also defined by a transformation matrix (Equation 2) referencing wall and table coordinates.

2. **Candidate Contact Point Calculation:** To facilitate transitions between object poses, candidate contact points are identified on the object surface.
 - The process involves uniformly sampling points on the object surface.
 - Normal vectors (pointing inwards) and motion vectors (difference between current and O_m coordinates, Equation 3) are computed for each sampled point.
 - Points where the angle between the normal and motion vector exceeds a threshold are filtered.
 - Finally, candidate contact points are selected if they are opposite to the normal, are at a distance equal to the gripper finger radius from the object surface, and do not collide with the scene (illustrated in Fig. 3).
 - During training, RL explores these candidate points, while during testing, the point with the highest Q-value is selected.

3. **Four Operational Stages:** The task is explicitly divided into four sequential stages:
 - **Stage 1:** The gripper moves to a selected contact point `p`, while the object maintains its initial pose `O_i`.
 - **Stage 2:** The gripper remains in contact at `p`, and the object is manipulated to reach the intermediate pose `O_m`.
 - **Stage 3:** The gripper moves to the contact points `p_g` corresponding to the final grasp pose `g`, with the object maintaining `O_m`.
 - **Stage 4:** The gripper closes and lifts the object to the final goal pose. The first three stages are learned by the policy, while the fourth is executed by a predefined program.
 - A stage is deemed successful if the object's position and orientation differences from the goal, and the gripper's position difference from its target contact points, are below predefined thresholds.

#### 3.2. State-Goal Representation

MRLM employs a point cloud-based state-goal fusion representation as input for its policy network. This representation integrates observation states and task goals into a single data structure.
- **Point Cloud Components:** The point cloud comprises points from four entities: the object, the gripper fingers (designed as spherical for consistent force application), the tabletop, and the wall.
- **Point Information:** Each point in the cloud includes its 3D location (x, y, z), its target motion (dx, dy, dz, representing the difference to the goal coordinates), and a 2-bit index (id1, id2) to identify its source (e.g., background (0,0), gripper fingers (0,1) and (1,0), object (1,1)).
- **Scales:** Specific numbers of points are used for each component (e.g., 30 for background, 228 for gripper fingers, 100 for object).

#### 3.3. RL Algorithm

The learning process utilizes an off-policy reinforcement learning algorithm, specifically SAC, to train a Point cloud Motion based Manipulation Network (P2ManNet).

1. **Observation and Action Space:**
 - **Observation:** In addition to the point cloud state-goal representation, the policy receives the gripper's current pose, opening width, and target width.
 - **Action Space:** The policy outputs a 5-dimensional continuous action `a = (t, e, w)`, where `t` is a 3D desired change in gripper position, `e` is a desired change in x-axis rotation, and `w` is a desired change in gripper opening width. These actions are executed via a lower-level Operation Space Controller (OSC).

2. **Network (P2ManNet):**
 - P2ManNet consists of an actor network and a critic network.
 - Both networks share an encoder structure, which uses an improved PointNet architecture combined with MLPs to extract and fuse features from the observation and goal point clouds. Alignment networks from the original PointNet are removed for efficiency.
 - The actor network decodes these features to predict a Gaussian action distribution (mean `μ` and standard deviation `σ`).
 - The critic network fuses observation features and action features to predict the Q-value.

3. **Reward Function:** A reward shaping mechanism is employed for all stages using the function:
 `r = −D(O, O_g) − βΔT(E, E_g) − P(E_g)` (Equation 5)
 - `D(O, O_g)`: Object pose difference (translational and rotational, Equation 6), scaled by weights `α_1` and `α_2`, to incentivize moving the object to its target pose.
 - `ΔT(E, E_g)`: Translational distance between the target and current gripper finger positions, scaled by `β`, to optimize gripper-object contact.
 - `P(E_g)`: An occlusion penalty applied if the target gripper ends are blocked by the environment.
 - All non-penalty terms are scaled to using the tanh function.

4. **Spatially-Reachable Distance Metric:** A novel metric is introduced for `ΔT(E, E_g)` to overcome limitations of Euclidean distance, which can lead to local optima.
 - This metric calculates the shortest reachable path between two points, approximating it by summing the shortest path in the yz-plane (`d_yz`) and the difference in x-coordinates (`d_x`) (Fig. 5).
 - This approach is intended to prevent the gripper from incorrectly approaching contact points (e.g., from the side) and instead guide it to move above the object before descending, particularly important for "close grasp" scenarios.

5. **Replay Buffer:** To improve learning efficiency across multiple stages, a separate replay buffer is maintained for each stage.
 - An "automatic compaction" mechanism is implemented: once a stage's test success rate exceeds a threshold (90%), a floor (50%) and ceiling (70%) for its buffer data volume are set. If the buffer size exceeds the ceiling, data is uniformly sampled and deleted to reduce it to the floor, preventing an accumulation of already learned stage trajectories that would otherwise reduce the current stage's learning efficiency.

#### 3.4. Improving Generalization

To ensure the policy generalizes across variations in the environment, MRLM incorporates Automatic Domain Randomization (ADR).
- The policy initially trains in an environment with fixed parameters.
- The sampling boundaries for environment parameters (e.g., object size, density, friction coefficient) are gradually expanded based on the policy's test performance.
- This approach is intended to reduce manual parameter tuning and accelerate convergence compared to training with the maximum parameter range from the outset. Table I provides details on the varied parameters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated MRLM through a series of simulation experiments and real-world zero-shot transfer tests, focusing on an occluded grasping task.

#### 4.1. Simulation Experiment Setup

The simulation environment was built using Robosuite, based on the Mujoco simulator, featuring a UR5e robot with a parallel gripper manipulating an object on a table. The P2ManNet policy was trained using the SAC algorithm, with hyperparameters detailed in Table II. Evaluations involved comparing policies across 5 random seeds and two grasp types (close and far grasp), averaging performance over 10 episodes.

#### 4.2. Ablation Experiment

Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of MRLM's key components (Fig. 6):
- **Overall Performance:** MRLM, with its complete system, achieved a 100% success rate for far grasp (under 600,000 exploration steps) and 95% for close grasp (under 700,000 exploration steps). Close grasp exhibited slightly lower sample efficiency due to exploring more candidate contact points.
- **Point Cloud-based State-Goal Representation:** When this representation was replaced with an array-based observation (gripper pose, object pose, etc.), the policy achieved 100% success on the second stage but 0% on final goals for far grasp, with even worse performance for close grasp. This suggests that the point cloud representation is crucial for precise manipulation requiring geometric understanding, such as flipping and tilting.
- **Spatially-Reachable Distance Metric:** Replacing the proposed metric with Euclidean distance resulted in a 40% reduction in success rate and a higher tendency to fall into local optima, where the gripper would approach the object from the side, causing unintended object movement. This validates the effectiveness of the novel distance metric in guiding optimal contact.
- **Automatic Buffer Compaction:** Without automatic buffer compaction, the success rate decreased by 60%, indicating that efficiently managing the replay buffer to prioritize data from the current learning stage significantly improves learning efficiency.
- **Single-Stage RL Comparison:** A comparison to a single-stage RL method (Zhou et al.) on a similar task yielded a 0% success rate for the single-stage approach under MRLM's sparser reward setup. This supports the benefit of task decomposition for complex, long-horizon tasks.
- **Reward Weight Sensitivity:** Experiments with varying `α1`, `α2`, and `β` weights (Fig. 7) demonstrated that MRLM's performance is sensitive to these reward terms.

#### 4.3. Emergent Behaviors

MRLM exhibited complex emergent behaviors for successful policies (Fig. 2):
- **Close Grasp:** The gripper first pressed a contact point on the object's upper surface (opposite the grasp) to flip it, then moved above the object before precisely approaching the grasp contact points.
- **Far Grasp:** The gripper pushed the object against a wall to rotate it. After reaching the intermediate pose, it used one finger to hold the object, positioned another finger underneath, and allowed the object to drop onto the bottom finger before approaching the final grasp pose.
- **Mistake Recovery:** MRLM demonstrated recovery capabilities; for instance, if the object fell during rotation with far grasp, the gripper would re-tilt it (Fig. 8a).
- **Local Optima:** Some failures involved falling into local optima, such as the policy with close grasp pushing the object from the side without successfully flipping it (Fig. 8b).

#### 4.4. Policy Generalization to Environments

The generalization of MRLM policies to varying environmental parameters was tested (Fig. 9):
- **Open Loop Trajectories:** Policies generated from open-loop trajectories showed low success rates (<30%).
- **Fixed Environment Policies:** Policies trained in a fixed environment performed better in the default setting but experienced sharp performance degradation as environment parameters deviated from the training conditions.
- **Policies with ADR:** MRLM trained with Automatic Domain Randomization (ADR) showed improved generalization across a range of environmental parameters. However, with close grasp, performance was lower near the lower bounds of density and friction due to objects bouncing. When object density and friction were set to default values, the success rate significantly improved, confirming the impact of these physical properties.

#### 4.5. Policy Generalization to Object Shape

MRLM's generalization to unseen object shapes (outside the training distribution of simple boxes) was evaluated (Fig. 10, Fig. 11).
- Objects with shapes closely resembling boxes (e.g., Object ③ and ④) achieved the highest success rates with minimal oscillation.
- Objects with curved contact surfaces (e.g., Object ⑤, ⑥, ②) showed lower success rates, ranging from 0% to 50%.
- For Object ⑤ (hollow structure), the default friction coefficient hindered flipping. Increasing the friction coefficient to 0.7 significantly improved the close grasp success rate by an average of 60%, indicating sensitivity to object properties beyond just shape.

#### 4.6. Real-Robot Experiments

Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer experiments were conducted on a UR5e robot with a Robotiq 2f-140 gripper, using a binocular camera for point cloud acquisition and object pose estimation (PCA). Four physical objects with varying shapes, densities, and frictions were tested (Table III, Fig. 11).
- **Quantitative Results:** MRLM achieved a 95% success rate with close grasp and 67.5% with far grasp across the tested objects.
- **Generalization to Shapes:** Notably, MRLM achieved 9/10 successes on a round metal box with close grasp, demonstrating some generalization to shapes not explicitly trained in simulation.
- **Qualitative Observations:** The robot exhibited strategies similar to those observed in simulation. Recovery from failed flipping transferred well to the real world. However, recovery from failed tilting in far grasp scenarios transferred less effectively, potentially due to discrepancies between real and simulated scenes.
- **Failure Analysis:** Failures with close grasp often resulted from the object being pushed away during flipping. Far grasp failures typically occurred when the object deviated from the intermediate pose and fell during tilting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing MRLM, a multi-stage reinforcement learning framework for non-prehensile tasks. The work addresses several limitations of existing methods, indicating notable advancements and potential for broader impact.

**Key areas of significance include:**

1. **Enhanced Task Complexity and Flexibility:** By decomposing long-horizon non-prehensile tasks into multiple stages, MRLM enables robots to perform more complex, sequential manipulations that involve changes in object pose and contact points. This allows for a wider range of emergent behaviors, such as precise flipping and tilting, which go beyond the capabilities of single-skill or primitive-based manipulation systems. This flexibility has the potential to expand the types of tasks robots can perform in unstructured environments.

2. **Robustness to Object Geometry and Environmental Variations:** The use of a point cloud-based state-goal fusion representation allows MRLM to effectively handle objects with complex and diverse shapes, a significant improvement over methods relying on simplified object models. Furthermore, the integration of Automatic Domain Randomization (ADR) enhances the policy's generalization capabilities across varying environmental parameters (e.g., object size, density, friction), contributing to more robust real-world deployment.

3. **Improved Learning Efficiency:** The multi-stage decomposition strategy, combined with per-stage replay buffers and automatic compaction, addresses the challenge of low sample efficiency often encountered in end-to-end RL for long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards. By structuring the learning process, MRLM facilitates faster convergence and reduces training resource requirements for complex manipulation sequences.

4. **Demonstrated Sim-to-Real Transferability:** The successful zero-shot transfer of policies from simulation to a physical robot, achieving high success rates for close grasp, highlights the practical applicability of MRLM. This capability is crucial for reducing the cost and complexity of deploying robotic solutions in real-world settings, as extensive real-world training can be avoided.

**Potential impacts include:**

- **Increased Robotic Dexterity:** MRLM can lead to robots that are more adaptable and skillful in interacting with objects, especially in scenarios where grasping is initially impossible or inefficient. This could be particularly impactful in domains like manufacturing, logistics, and assistive robotics, where delicate reorientation or handling of diverse items is often required.
- **Automation in Constrained Environments:** The ability to manipulate objects in occluded or constrained spaces, as demonstrated by the occluded grasping task, suggests potential for applications in tasks like organizing cluttered shelves, manipulating components in tightly packed assemblies, or retrieving items from confined areas.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The multi-stage RL framework, novel state-goal representation, and spatially-reachable distance metric provide a foundation for further research into more advanced manipulation skills, multi-object interactions, and even human-robot collaboration scenarios where flexible object handling is essential.

While the work acknowledges limitations such as the manual design of intermediate object poses and the training time, the proposed solutions and empirical results indicate a significant step towards developing more intelligent and dexterous robotic manipulators capable of tackling real-world non-prehensile tasks.
