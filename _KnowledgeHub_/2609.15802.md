# The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> This economics paper models recursive self-improvement as a directed graph in which nodes are production-function outputs and edge strengths are elasticities, so net acceleration in AI capabilities equals the summed product of elasticities around each feedback loop. It separates narrow AI-R&D capability from broad economic capability, lists measurable parameters labs could publish, and calibrates the condition: current loops fall short of self-sustaining acceleration but are strengthening.

## Problem

- Prior definitions of recursive self-improvement conflate feedback loops, full R&D automation, and intelligence explosion, so claims that automation guarantees explosive growth go unexamined.
- Earlier takeoff models parametrize how capabilities and compute produce algorithmic progress, yet that function depends on poorly understood returns to intelligence and human-AI differences.
- Review treatments largely set aside the possibility that AI improves narrowly at optimizing R&D benchmarks without gaining broadly economically valuable capability, and lack feasible public data asks.

## Method

- A ==graphical elasticity framework==: edges carry partial elasticities, loop strength is their product along a path, and acceleration requires ==total elasticity above one==, generalizing to a ==feedback-matrix dominant eigenvalue above one== when economic loops accumulate compute and data.
- Model variants that split ==narrow capability== feeding algorithmic progress from ==broad capability== feeding output, adding a ==superelasticity== term, plus a two-sub-algorithm version explaining temporary growth spurts from ceilings and parallel bottlenecks.
- An ==R&D effort aggregator== over human labor, experimental compute, and inference compute, rearranging the condition into ==return-to-research== times ==research-effort elasticity== times ==capability elasticity==, each mapped to cost shares, growth rates, and a public data wish list.

## Results

- Calibration sets return to research near **one**, since algorithmic efficiency and R&D effort both grow about **threefold** yearly, and capability elasticity near **6.5** from regressing the Epoch Capabilities Index on log compute.
- Self-sustaining acceleration then needs research effort to rise over **15%** per capability-index unit; a reported **fourfold** engineer uplift since coding agents launched implies only about **9%**, below threshold.
- Supporting evidence shows strengthening loops: algorithmic efficiency improved **700-fold** over 2019-2026, models beat humans on research decisions **64%** versus **50%** earlier, and internal coding-inference compute share grew **100-fold** in six months.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Loop strength multiplies along a path, so a single weak or saturating edge, such as a human, data, or compute bottleneck, cancels acceleration regardless of other strong edges.
> - Fully automating research does not imply an intelligence explosion; diminishing returns and falling passthrough elasticities can produce only a temporary growth spurt.
> - Tracking how much research effort each capability unit buys, plus separating narrow from broad capability gains, is the highest-value measurement for forecasting takeoff.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

The paper contributes to the growing literature on the economics of artificial intelligence, technological progress, and AI-driven automation. Its central subject is recursive self-improvement (RSI): the possibility that AI systems improve the methods, algorithms, or infrastructure used to develop subsequent AI systems, thereby creating a feedback loop in technological progress.

The paper builds on several strands of prior research. Classical endogenous-growth models, particularly Jones’s R&D-based growth framework, describe how research labor and the existing stock of knowledge generate new technological improvements. Aghion, Jones, and Jones applied related ideas to AI-driven automation and economic growth. More recent work by Davidson and collaborators has modeled AI takeoff dynamics through compute accumulation and networks of innovation feedback loops.

The paper’s main conceptual contribution is to represent these mechanisms as directed graphs in which nodes are economic or technological variables and edges represent elasticities. This framework allows the authors to analyze multiple feedback loops without reducing RSI to the stronger and less precise claim that an “intelligence explosion” must occur once AI can automate its own research. The paper distinguishes among general feedback loops, R&D automatability, self-sustaining acceleration, and an intelligence explosion. In particular, it defines self-sustaining acceleration as a situation in which AI capabilities can continue accelerating without increases in exogenous inputs such as human labor or training compute.

The paper also extends the literature by distinguishing “narrow” capabilities that assist with AI research from “broad” capabilities that generate wider economic value. This distinction addresses a limitation in models that treat AI capability as a single scalar quantity. An AI system could become increasingly effective at optimizing algorithms or software while making slower progress in areas such as physical-world reasoning, scientific experimentation, or other economically important tasks.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The paper has four principal objectives.

First, it seeks to clarify the mechanisms underlying RSI. Public discussion often treats AI-assisted AI development as sufficient for runaway or explosive growth. The authors instead ask a more specific question: under what conditions does the feedback loop become self-sustaining? Their answer depends on the strength of the relationships connecting existing algorithmic efficiency, AI capabilities, research effort, and new algorithmic improvements.

Second, the authors aim to identify potential bottlenecks. AI-assisted research may depend on human supervision, experimental compute, inference compute, training compute, and high-quality data. Even if AI systems become more capable, these complementary inputs may limit the rate at which AI can improve itself.

Third, the paper develops an empirical agenda. It identifies parameters that would be useful for evaluating RSI and lists data that AI companies could plausibly measure and disclose. The authors emphasize three especially important unknowns: the growth rate of algorithmic efficiency, the allocation of AI laboratory R&D expenditure across labor, experimental compute, and inference compute, and direct measures of how much AI contributes to technical progress.

Fourth, the paper provides a tentative calibration using existing data. The goal is not to estimate a precise probability of RSI, but to determine whether currently available evidence is consistent with a self-sustaining acceleration.

### 3. Methodology and Analytical Approach

#### 3.1. Graph-Based Feedback Framework

The analysis begins with a basic endogenous-growth model. Algorithmic efficiency, denoted \(A\), is defined as the inverse of the training compute required to reach a given capability level. Improvements in algorithmic efficiency, \(\dot A\), depend on the existing level of algorithmic efficiency and on R&D labor.

In this basic model, self-sustaining acceleration occurs when the elasticity of new algorithmic improvements with respect to the existing stock of algorithmic efficiency exceeds one:

\[
\varepsilon_{\dot A,A} > 1.
\]

If this elasticity is below one, the growth rate of algorithmic efficiency declines as the technology stock increases. If it equals one, growth is exponential but not accelerating. If it exceeds one, the growth rate itself increases over time.

The authors then add AI capabilities, \(C\), which depend on algorithmic efficiency and training compute. AI capabilities also contribute to the production of new algorithmic improvements. This creates the core RSI loop:

\[
A \rightarrow C \rightarrow \dot A.
\]

The condition for self-sustaining acceleration becomes the sum of the direct self-feedback loop and the core AI-capability loop:

\[
E_{\dot A,A}
=
\varepsilon_{\dot A,A}
+
\varepsilon_{\dot A,C}\varepsilon_{C,A}
>1.
\]

The product of elasticities is important because a feedback loop may contain several stages. A strong effect of algorithms on capabilities is insufficient if capabilities have little effect on research productivity, and vice versa.

#### 3.2. Bottlenecks and Complementary Inputs

The authors extend the baseline model by introducing human R&D labor, experimental compute, inference compute, training compute, and data. These inputs influence either algorithmic improvements or AI capabilities.

The framework does not require a particular functional form for production. Instead, it focuses on local elasticities, which measure percentage responsiveness. Bottlenecks arise when the elasticity of one link in a feedback loop declines. For example, capable AI agents may not increase research output substantially if they lack sufficient experimental compute, inference compute, human oversight, or data.

#### 3.3. Narrow and Broad Capabilities

The model separates narrow capabilities, \(C_1\), from broad capabilities, \(C_2\). Narrow capabilities contribute to AI R&D, while broad capabilities influence economic output. The RSI condition depends on the narrow capability loop, not automatically on broad capability growth.

The authors introduce a “superelasticity” term to capture whether the passthrough from algorithmic efficiency to broad capabilities changes as algorithmic efficiency rises. Consequently, algorithmic progress can accelerate while broad capabilities or economic output do not. This could occur if algorithm optimization is relatively easy to verify and automate, while broader tasks remain constrained by data, physical interaction, or other factors.

#### 3.4. Multiple Algorithmic Sectors

The paper also models cases in which AI improves only one component of a larger algorithmic system. Progress in an AI-assisted subalgorithm can produce a temporary growth spurt, but overall efficiency may eventually be constrained by another component that AI is not improving. The corresponding elasticity between the improved subalgorithm and overall efficiency falls as the improved component becomes less of a bottleneck.

#### 3.5. Economic Feedback Loops

The authors add economic feedback channels in which higher AI capabilities raise economic output, which then finances additional training compute, experimental compute, inference compute, or data collection. With multiple accumulating stocks, the condition for self-sustaining acceleration is expressed in terms of the dominant eigenvalue of a feedback matrix. Acceleration occurs when this eigenvalue exceeds one.

This extension captures the possibility that economic growth itself indirectly supports RSI, even if the direct AI-to-research loop is not sufficient on its own.

#### 3.6. Empirical Calibration

For calibration, the authors introduce an aggregate R&D-effort measure combining human researchers, AI researchers operating through inference compute, and experimental compute. They rearrange the RSI condition into three components:

1. The return to R&D effort.
2. The elasticity of effective research effort with respect to AI capabilities.
3. The elasticity of AI capabilities with respect to algorithmic efficiency.

Under a balanced-growth approximation, the return to R&D is estimated as the growth rate of algorithmic efficiency divided by the growth rate of R&D effort. Input expenditure shares are used as proxies for production elasticities under cost-minimization and constant returns to scale.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper’s theoretical results show that RSI is not equivalent to AI systems being able to perform some AI research tasks autonomously. Self-sustaining acceleration requires the combined strength of all relevant feedback paths to exceed a threshold. Diminishing returns, input bottlenecks, and declining passthrough between narrow and broad capabilities can prevent acceleration.

The calibration uses an estimated annual tripling of algorithmic efficiency and an approximate tripling of R&D effort. It also uses an estimated capability elasticity with respect to algorithmic efficiency of approximately 6.5 under the paper’s chosen Epoch Capabilities Index normalization. These assumptions imply that the elasticity of research effort with respect to AI capabilities must exceed approximately 0.15 for self-sustaining acceleration.

The authors interpret this threshold as requiring roughly a 15 percent increase in AI R&D productivity for each additional unit of the selected capability index. A rough calculation based on reported AI engineer productivity improvements suggests an elasticity of about 0.09 since the introduction of coding agents. This estimate is below the model-implied threshold, leading the authors to conclude that current feedback loops do not yet appear strong enough to generate self-sustaining acceleration.

However, the authors stress that the estimated return to AI capabilities is increasing. Reported productivity improvements from newer systems are larger than those associated with earlier generations. They therefore do not rule out the possibility that the threshold could be crossed in the near future.

The evidence concerning autonomous AI research is mixed. AI systems have reportedly performed well on some optimization and algorithmic tasks, and their use in internal coding and research has increased. At the same time, system-card evaluations show weaker performance on complex debugging, execution, and tasks requiring research judgment or “taste.” Results from inference-compute experiments also suggest that AI agents may find inexpensive improvements quickly, after which returns diminish.

The paper identifies several reasons why acceleration could remain limited. Algorithmic progress may depend on continued growth in training and experimental compute. Data could become a binding constraint, particularly for post-training and reinforcement-learning systems. Economic growth may be insufficient to finance the required expansion of compute and infrastructure. Political opposition to data centers, regulation, and deployment constraints could also slow the feedback process.

The paper further argues that narrow acceleration may precede broad acceleration. Progress may concentrate in software, mathematics, games, and other domains with clear verification criteria, while more open-ended or physically embedded tasks advance more slowly.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper provides a structured framework for evaluating RSI claims without assuming that AI research automation necessarily produces an intelligence explosion. Its emphasis on measurable elasticities offers a way to connect theoretical debates with empirical data from AI laboratories.

The distinction between narrow and broad capabilities is particularly relevant for interpreting economic effects. Rapid progress in algorithm optimization could generate major advances in selected technical domains without immediately producing equivalent growth in aggregate productivity or GDP. Conversely, economic feedback loops could reinforce AI progress if increased output or investment expands access to compute and data.

If self-sustaining acceleration eventually occurs, the paper identifies several potential consequences. Rapid technological change could increase labor-market transition costs and create institutional delays in regulation, infrastructure, and governance. Progress could be uneven across industries and countries, potentially increasing concentration of economic and political power. The authors also note that rapid capability gains could reduce the time available for alignment, safety, and governance measures.

The paper’s conclusions remain provisional because many key parameters are poorly measured and several simplifying assumptions may not hold. Its main practical contribution is therefore an empirical research agenda: measure AI contributions to real R&D, track laboratory spending and input shares, estimate capability-productivity relationships, and separate algorithmic progress that transfers broadly from progress confined to narrow optimization tasks.
