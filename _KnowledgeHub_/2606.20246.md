# Finetuning Vision-Language-Action Models Requires Fewer Layers Than You Think

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers introduced CKA-guided Layer Pruning (CLP), a method that identifies and removes redundant transformer layers in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models like π0 and GR00T-N1.5. This training-free approach compresses model depth by up to 50%, reduces training time by up to 1.94x, and often enhances real-world task success rates without compromising policy performance.

## Problem

- Large-scale Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, with billions of parameters, incur prohibitive computational costs for downstream fine-tuning and real-time inference.
- Existing VLA optimization methods often have limited scope, fail to address the fine-tuning bottleneck, or introduce architectural complexity.
- Modern continuous-control VLA policies exhibit "severe layer-wise representational redundancy," with many consecutive transformer layers performing highly correlated operations.

## Method

- Developed CKA-guided Layer Pruning (**CLP**), a training-free structural compression pipeline that statically removes redundant transformer layers from pre-trained VLA models.
- Utilized ==Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA)== to quantify representational similarity between consecutive layers, identifying "stagnant zones" where information transfer is minimal.
- Selected and permanently removed the most redundant layers based on CKA scores, producing a smaller, more efficient model ready for downstream fine-tuning without auxiliary modules or dynamic execution.

## Results

- Achieved up to **50%** depth reduction for π0 and GR00T-N1.5, resulting in **1.39**-**1.42x** fewer FLOPs and up to **1.94x** faster training and inference, while maintaining or improving policy success rates.
- Outperformed various training-free and training-adaptive compression methods in efficiency-performance trade-offs across three simulation benchmarks (LIBERO, RoboCasa, SimplerEnv).
- Validated across 10 real-world manipulation tasks on four robotic embodiments, demonstrating an average success rate increase from **73.5%** to **75.9%** for GR00TN1.5, with specific task improvements up to **+20%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - State-of-the-art VLA models, despite their depth, contain substantial structural redundancy, with up to 50% of transformer layers being removable without performance loss.
> - CKA is an effective metric for pinpointing these redundant layers, and post-pruning fine-tuning enables the remaining layers to reorganize and restore the original representation manifold.
> - Removing redundant layers acts as an implicit regularization mechanism, which can improve policy performance, especially in data-scarce real-world settings by reducing overfitting.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has experienced a shift with the advent of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. These models, exemplified by early architectures like RT-2, OpenVLA, and CogACT, translate multimodal inputs (vision and language) into physical control actions, demonstrating generalization capabilities across language-conditioned tasks. More recent advancements, such as π0 and the GR00T family, have moved towards continuous action generation using diffusion or flow-matching objectives, leading to enhanced trajectory generation and smoother control.

However, these architectural developments are accompanied by a substantial increase in model scale, often spanning billions of parameters. This scale introduces significant computational challenges, including high hardware and cluster time costs for downstream fine-tuning, as well as operational latency and memory-bandwidth overhead that restrict real-time inference on edge devices.

To address these computational bottlenecks, the embodied AI community has pursued several optimization strategies:

1. **Training-free methods:** These approaches accelerate action generation or visual processing primarily during inference by optimizing computational density. Techniques include parallel decoding, speculative execution, token pruning, and temporal caching. Examples include OpenVLA-OFT, Knowledge Insulating, EfficientVLA, SpecPrune-VLA, VLA-Cache, and ADP. While effective for inference acceleration, these methods generally do not reduce the computational cost associated with the initial, expensive downstream fine-tuning phase.
2. **Lightweight VLA architectures from scratch:** This direction focuses on designing inherently smaller VLA models with high capabilities. Examples include RoboMamba, Flower-VLA, SmolVLA, and NORA. While these models aim to improve accessibility by reducing the parameter footprint, they may not fully retain the broad capabilities inherited from large-scale, broadly pre-trained VLA backbones.
3. **Training-adaptive computation:** This category involves modifying pre-trained models to incorporate dynamic computation mechanisms. Methods like DeeR-VLA and MoLe-VLA learn to exit early or route inputs through a subset of layers based on task demands. While capable of accelerating both training and inference, these approaches often introduce auxiliary routing modules, additional training objectives, or complex architectural modifications, which can increase overall system complexity and potentially create friction with standard downstream learning algorithms.

The current work identifies three limitations in existing VLA optimization frameworks:
- **Narrow scope:** Many techniques focus on older, discrete text-token autoregressive baselines (e.g., OpenVLA) rather than modern continuous-control models (π0, GR00T-N1.5), and real-world validation is often limited.
- **Fine-tuning bottleneck:** Training-free methods do not alleviate the substantial computational cost of downstream fine-tuning, a critical phase in robot learning.
- **Architectural complexity:** Training-adaptive methods introduce significant architectural changes, complicating deployment and integration.

This research addresses these limitations by proposing a structural compression method that specifically targets modern continuous-control foundation models, aims to accelerate the fine-tuning phase, and avoids introducing additional architectural complexity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to alleviate the prohibitive computational burdens associated with multi-billion parameter VLA models during both downstream fine-tuning and real-time inference. The authors aim to achieve this by developing an efficient and structurally clean framework that reduces the necessary model depth without compromising policy performance.

The motivation for this work stems from a specific architectural characteristic observed in modern continuous control VLA foundation policies, such as π0 and GR00T-N1.5. Despite being trained on extensive and diverse physical trajectories, these models exhibit a "severe layer-wise representational redundancy." This implies that consecutive transformer blocks within these deep architectures often produce highly correlated token representations, suggesting that many layers perform minimal unique transformations.

The core research question guiding this work is: "Can redundant layers be removed before fine-tuning without sacrificing policy performance?" Answering this question positively would address the critical bottlenecks identified in the broader research landscape:

1. **Applicability to Modern VLAs:** The focus is specifically on modern continuous-control models, moving beyond older autoregressive baselines.
2. **Acceleration of Fine-tuning:** By statically compressing the model *before* fine-tuning, the method directly reduces the computational cost and time required for downstream adaptation, which is a major bottleneck for robot learning.
3. **Architectural Simplicity:** The proposed method aims to achieve efficiency without introducing auxiliary routing modules, additional training objectives, or complex task-dependent runtime decisions, thereby maintaining the original model's structural integrity.

By exploiting this identified representational redundancy, the authors seek to develop a method that results in a statically smaller model, leading to reduced active parameters, lower memory usage, decreased training costs, and faster inference latency.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **CKA-guided Layer Pruning (CLP)**, a training-free structural compression pipeline designed to identify and remove redundant transformer layers in VLA models prior to fine-tuning.

**A. VLA Model Architecture:**
The work focuses on state-of-the-art continuous-control VLA policies (e.g., π0, GR00T, SmolVLA), which generally employ a decoupled architecture:
- **VLM Backbone:** A stack of Nv transformer layers that extract environmental context from language and visual observations (x_lang, x_img). Hidden token representations H_vlm_ℓ evolve through successive transformer blocks F_vlm_ℓ.
- **Action-Generation Head:** A flow- or diffusion-based model with Na transformer layers that parameterizes a velocity field to predict action chunks (a) conditioned on multimodal inputs. Action-token representations H_act_m evolve, cross-conditioned by the VLM context Z. This shared transformer-based formulation makes both VLM and action modules compatible for layer-wise similarity analysis.

**B. Diagnosing Layer Redundancy with Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA):**
The methodology begins with an empirical exploration of how information evolves across network depth in deep VLA architectures.
- **CKA Metric:** Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) is employed to quantify representation similarity between layers. CKA is invariant to orthogonal transformations and isotropic scaling, making it suitable for identifying structural redundancies. A CKA score closer to 1 indicates higher representational similarity between two hidden states (H_i, H_j).
- **Representational Plateaus:** By tracing the hidden state trajectories using CKA, the authors observed that feature representations in π0 and GR00T-N1.5 exhibit "distinct zones of stagnation," characterized by large, contiguous blocks of high inter-layer similarity (Figure 2). These "plateaus" suggest that successive layers perform highly redundant operations, with major feature transformations concentrated in fewer, distinct transitions. This observation forms the basis for the hypothesis that these high-similarity regions contain layers that can be pruned.

**C. CKA-Guided Pruning Process (Algorithm 1):**
The CLP framework executes the following steps:
1. **Targeted Module Selection:** The process targets specific prunable modules, such as the VLM backbone or the action head.
2. **Calibration Set (D_cal):** A small calibration set, sampled from training episodes, is used to perform a single forward pass through the pre-trained policy. This pass extracts and concatenates token representations, forming a unified layer activation matrix (H_M_ℓ) for each layer ℓ in the target module M.
3. **Sequential Redundancy Quantification:** CKA is computed between consecutive layers (s_M_ℓ = CKA(H_M_ℓ-1, H_M_ℓ)) to measure representational similarity. A high s_M_ℓ score indicates minimal representational change.
4. **Contiguous Block Aggregation:** To mitigate local sampling noise and ensure structural coherence, adjacent redundant layers are grouped into contiguous blocks (B_M). Layers are clustered into the same block if their sequential CKA score (s_M_ℓ) is above a designated similarity threshold (τ).
5. **Candidate Pruning Set (P_M):** For each identified block, the initial layer (r(B)) is retained as a functional anchor, and all subsequent layers within that block are pooled into a candidate pruning set.
6. **Final Layer Selection (R_M):** From the candidate pool, the k_M (pre-defined pruning budget) most redundant layers, based on their s_M_ℓ scores, are selected for removal.
7. **Static Structural Truncation:** The selected layers in R_M are permanently removed from the network. Because all transformer blocks within a module share identical hidden dimensions, removal simply involves reconnecting the remaining predecessor and successor blocks. This static truncation reduces network depth prior to any downstream adaptation.

**D. Key Advantages of CLP:**
- **Training-free:** The pruning process requires only a single forward pass on a small calibration set, eliminating the need for additional training or auxiliary objectives for compression.
- **Statically Smaller Model:** CLP produces a permanently compressed model, directly reducing active parameters, memory usage, training cost, and inference latency, without introducing runtime computational overhead from dynamic routing or early-exit mechanisms.
- **Compatibility:** The approach is compatible with native training objectives for fine-tuning, as it does not alter the fundamental architectural structure beyond depth reduction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents extensive experimental validation across three simulation benchmarks (LIBERO, RoboCasa, SimplerEnv) and 10 real-world manipulation tasks spanning four distinct robotic embodiments (Aloha Single and Bimanual arms, UR10, UR5).

**RQ1: Compression Trade-Offs**
- **Depth Reduction and FLOPs:** Both π0 and GR00T-N1.5 demonstrated stable performance even with up to a 50% pruning ratio (Figure 3a, 3b). This level of pruning resulted in a reduction of total FLOPs by approximately 1.39× for π0 and 1.42× for GR00T-N1.5, with negligible degradation in policy success rate.
- **Generalizability Across VLAs:** CLP was applied to π0, GR00T-N1.5, and SmolVLA, compressing their total model size by 21.3% to 25.9%, reducing trainable parameters by 25.8% to 37.0%, and cutting GFLOPs by up to 50%, all while maintaining equivalent success rates (Table 1, Table 2). For instance, SmolVLA-CLP achieved a 64.3% reduction in training time.

**RQ2: Latent Behavior & Ablation**
- **Manifold Restoration:** Analysis of hidden-state geometry using PCA revealed that structural pruning initially contracts the latent space significantly. However, after downstream fine-tuning, the remaining layers reorganize their feature pathways, restoring a representation manifold closely aligned with the original unpruned network (Figure 1, Figure 3f). This "manifold restoration" mechanism explains how the compressed policy regains expressive capacity.
- **CKA Effectiveness:** A comparison of pruning strategies (Figure 3d) showed that CKA consistently yielded the most stable performance across benchmarks, closely matching the unpruned baseline and maintaining the highest average success rate. Alternative methods, such as Mean-Squared Error, Cosine similarity, random pruning, or simply removing the last *k* layers (KEEP-FIRST), led to more unstable performance degradation. Hidden-state analysis (Figure 3f) suggested that CKA preserves the global topological structure necessary for effective post-pruning adaptation, whereas other methods distorted representations into isolated subspaces.

**RQ3: State-of-the-art Compression Comparison**
- **Against Training-Free Methods:** On the LIBERO benchmark (Table 2), CLP achieved superior efficiency-performance trade-offs compared to various training-free token-pruning methods (e.g., OpenVLA-OFT, FastV, EfficientVLA, ADP, SpecPrune-VLA). CLP delivered 1.39–1.47× speedups while maintaining competitive success rates. Crucially, CLP accelerates the expensive downstream fine-tuning phase, a benefit not offered by inference-only training-free methods.
- **Against Training-Adaptive Methods:** In challenging few-shot settings with limited data (e.g., 10% of LIBERO training data or 30-demonstration subsets of RoboCasa), CLP demonstrated robust performance. On 10% LIBERO data (Table 6, Figure 3c), CLP achieved an 84.6% average success rate, surpassing the full π0 baseline (77.7%) and the training-adaptive π0-MoLe (79.7%), while reducing training time by 1.38×. On SimplerEnv with GR00TN1.5 (Table 3), CLP improved the average success rate from 16.6% to 20.0% and reduced training time from 22.9 to 15.7 hours. These results indicate that structure-aware compression with lightweight fine-tuning is more effective and scalable, especially in low-data regimes, compared to both inference-only pruning and parameter-expanding adaptive architectures.

**RQ4: Real-World Deployment**
- **Robustness and Performance Gains:** CLP was validated across 10 real-world manipulation tasks using four different robotic embodiments (UR10, UR5, single-arm ALOHA, bimanual ALOHA) (Table 4, Figure 1, Figure 4). The method consistently maintained or improved overall task performance. For GR00TN1.5, the average success rate increased from 73.5% to 75.9%. Performance improvements were noted on several tasks, including napkin serving (+20%), screwdriver placement (+15%), banana-to-pot transfer (+10%), and bimanual cloth folding (+5%).
- **Training Speedups:** These performance gains were accompanied by substantial reductions in fine-tuning time, achieving up to 1.94× faster training in real-world deployments (Figure 3e, Table 8).
- **Regularization Effect:** The authors hypothesize that the performance gains, particularly in data-scarce real-world settings (e.g., 100 demonstrations), are due to an implicit regularization effect. Removing redundant layers reduces model capacity, which can discourage overfitting to task-specific noise. The consistency of these improvements across diverse tasks, objects, and robot embodiments suggests that CLP enhances efficiency without compromising real-world control capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research demonstrates that state-of-the-art VLA models, including complex continuous-control foundations like π0 and GR00T-N1.5, contain significant structural redundancy. This finding challenges the assumption that ever-increasing architectural depth is necessary for advanced VLA capabilities.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:

- **Rethinking VLA Model Scale:** The core finding that 30–50% of transformer layers can be removed without performance degradation suggests a fundamental re-evaluation of the optimal depth for VLA architectures. This could influence future model design and pre-training strategies.
- **Enhanced Computational Efficiency:** CLP offers a directly applicable, compute-efficient paradigm for scalable robot learning. By reducing memory footprint, training time, and inference latency, it lowers the hardware and cluster time requirements for developing and deploying VLA-based robotic systems.
- **Increased Accessibility:** The substantial reduction in computational resources makes advanced VLA models more accessible to researchers and practitioners with limited hardware budgets, democratizing access to powerful robot learning capabilities.
- **Improved Adaptation in Low-Data Regimes:** The demonstrated performance improvements and regularization effects in data-scarce settings are particularly impactful for real-world robotics, where collecting large-scale datasets is often challenging and expensive. CLP enables more effective fine-tuning with limited demonstrations.
- **Simplified Integration and Deployment:** As a training-free, static compression method, CLP avoids introducing auxiliary routing modules, dynamic inference decisions, or complex distillation pipelines. This simplicity facilitates easier integration with existing VLA adaptation techniques and streamlines real-world deployment on various robotic platforms.
- **Broad Generalizability:** The comprehensive validation across multiple simulation benchmarks, diverse real-world tasks, and four distinct robotic embodiments indicates that CLP is a robust and platform-agnostic solution, applicable to a wide range of manipulation scenarios.

By enabling more efficient, scalable, and sustainable foundation policies, this work contributes to addressing a critical barrier in the widespread adoption and advancement of VLA models in robotics.
