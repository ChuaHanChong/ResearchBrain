# Can vision language models learn intuitive physics from interaction?

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Helmholtz Munich investigated whether Vision Language Models (VLMs) acquire generalizable intuitive physics understanding through active interaction. The study found that neither one-step reinforcement learning nor supervised fine-tuning consistently led to robust generalization across intuitive physics tasks, suggesting models tend to develop task-specific shortcuts rather than deep transferable knowledge.

## Problem

- Pre-trained Vision Language Models (VLMs) often demonstrate limited understanding of the physical world and its causal structures, performing inadequately on intuitive physics tasks.
- Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) improves VLM performance on specific tasks but often leads to "shortcut learning" without acquiring generalizable physical intuitions.
- The role of active interaction, analogous to human cognitive development, in fostering robust and generalizable intuitive physics in VLMs remained an underexplored area.

## Method

- The study compared an interactive learning approach, using one-step Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), with a non-interactive Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) method for ==fine-tuning== Qwen3-VL models.
- Synthetic datasets of block towers were created in the ThreeDWorld environment for tasks such as binary stability judgment and precise block displacement, complemented by a real-world dataset for evaluation.
- Task-specific reward functions were designed to guide interactive training, enabling the ==VLM== to generate action sequences aimed at stabilizing towers or moving blocks to optimal positions.

## Results

- Both GRPO and SFT models achieved high performance on their training tasks, with mean test accuracies of **0.969** for binary stability and mean test rewards of approximately **19.99** (out of 20) for x-only tasks.
- Neither training method consistently generalized to novel synthetic tasks or real-world images, with all models performing below the average human performance (**0.75**-**0.85** accuracy) on judging real block tower stability (GRPO: **0.6**, SFT: **0.59**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that the limited generalization was consistent across different model sizes (**7B**, **8B**, **32B**), alternative RL algorithms, and extended training durations, which often led to increased overfitting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Both interactive (GRPO) and non-interactive (SFT) fine-tuning methods effectively improve VLM performance on their specific training tasks but fail to consistently generalize to novel or related intuitive physics tasks.
> - Crucial physical quantities (e.g., tower stability, block offset) were highly decodable from base model activations, suggesting models possess latent competence that does not reliably translate to out-of-distribution performance.
> - The findings indicate that current VLM fine-tuning paradigms, even with interactive elements, primarily facilitate the acquisition of task-specific shortcuts rather than a robust, transferable understanding of physical principles.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of machine learning models that exhibit human-like reasoning about their environment, including its physical, social, and causal structures (often referred to as intuitive theories), constitutes a central objective in machine learning research. This work specifically addresses intuitive physics, which is the implicit capacity to comprehend and predict object properties and interactions. Prior research has indicated that current Vision Language Models (VLMs), despite advancements in architecture and training, exhibit limitations in understanding the physical world and its causal structures. VLMs have shown insufficient performance on standard visual cognition tasks, including those testing intuitive physics, and do not consistently align with human behavior. While supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can enhance VLM performance on specific tasks, this improvement often does not translate into the acquisition of robust, generalizable physical rules.

Cognitive science posits that humans develop a profound understanding of their world through active interaction, rather than solely through passive observation. This perspective suggests that direct experimentation with environmental dynamics facilitates the learning of robust and generalizable concepts. The importance of interaction is being acknowledged in the training of generative models, but its application to fostering visual cognitive abilities like intuitive physics in VLMs had not been extensively investigated prior to this study. This research contributes to this gap by empirically evaluating the impact of interaction, operationalized through reinforcement learning (RL), on the development of intuitive physics in VLMs, comparing it against non-interactive supervised fine-tuning. It builds upon previous findings on VLM limitations in physical reasoning and extends the inquiry beyond task-specific performance to examine generalization capabilities across related but distinct physical tasks and real-world scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to evaluate whether Vision Language Models (VLMs) can acquire generalizable intuitive physics knowledge through active interaction with a simulated environment. This objective is motivated by several observations regarding the current capabilities of VLMs and established theories in cognitive science.

Firstly, existing VLMs exhibit deficiencies in understanding the physical world. They perform inadequately on fundamental visual cognition tasks related to intuitive physics, such as reasoning about object permanence, continuity, solidity, and support. While supervised fine-tuning (SFT) can enhance performance on specific tasks, it has been observed that models fine-tuned in this manner often fail to learn robust physical rules that generalize to novel contexts. This suggests that current training paradigms may lead to task-specific "shortcuts" rather than a fundamental understanding of physical principles.

Secondly, a prominent body of research in cognitive science emphasizes the crucial role of interaction in human learning, particularly for developing a robust understanding of the world’s physical dynamics. Unlike passive observation, active experimentation allows individuals, such as children, to test hypotheses about their environment and learn from the outcomes of their own actions. The hypothesis is that a similar interactive learning process might be necessary for VLMs to transcend superficial task performance and acquire truly generalizable intuitive physics.

To investigate this, the study formulated three specific hypotheses:
1. **Generalization to new towers (within-task):** Models trained through interaction (interactive condition) would exhibit superior generalization to novel tower configurations not encountered during their training, compared to models trained non-interactively (non-interactive condition). This evaluates if interaction aids in robustly learning the underlying rules of a specific task.
2. **Generalization to new tasks (cross-task):** Models in the interactive condition would generalize more effectively to entirely new, but related, tasks, such as judging the stability of a pre-built tower, as opposed to models in the non-interactive condition. This addresses the transferability of learned physical intuitions.
3. **Decodability of physical quantities:** Key physical quantities, such as tower stability, would be more decodable from the activations of later model layers in interactively trained models compared to non-interactively trained models. This explores whether interaction fosters a deeper internal representation of physical concepts, distinguishing between model "competence" (what it implicitly knows) and "performance" (what it explicitly outputs).

The overall motivation was to explore whether incorporating an interactive learning paradigm, drawing inspiration from cognitive development, could address the observed limitations of VLMs in acquiring robust and generalizable intuitive physics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research investigated the acquisition of intuitive physics in Vision Language Models (VLMs) by comparing an interactive, reinforcement learning (RL) approach against a non-interactive, supervised fine-tuning (SFT) baseline.

**Model Architecture and Fine-Tuning:**
The primary model employed was the 8B parameter, 4-bit quantized version of the Qwen3-VL model. Ablation studies also included Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Qwen3-VL-32B, Llama-3.2-11B, and Gemma-3-12B. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning (PEFT) with QLoRA was utilized, where low-rank adapters (r = α = 16) were injected into all layers. All models were trained for 10,000 steps (with some ablations up to 48,000 steps) on single 80GB A100 GPUs using the Adam optimizer.

**Datasets:**
Two synthetic datasets of block towers were constructed using the ThreeDWorld environment, each consisting of 2-4 randomly colored cubes rendered as 256x256 pixel RGB images from a fixed camera angle:
- **Top Block:** Towers with the topmost block displaced horizontally.
- **Side Block:** Towers with a block on the ground positioned next to the tower, displaced horizontally.
An additional evaluation dataset consisted of real images of wooden block towers from Lerer et al. (2016), comprising 2-4 blocks that were either stable or unstable.

**Tasks:**
Four tasks were defined based on the synthetic datasets:
1. **Binary Stability:** (Top Block dataset, and Lerer dataset for generalization evaluation) Models provided a binary "Yes/No" judgment on whether a given tower was stable.
2. **X-only (Top Block):** Models returned a single integer to move the top block along the x-axis to improve tower stability.
3. **X-only (Side Block):** Models returned a single integer to move a displaced block on the floor along the x-axis to the center of the image.
4. **X-Y (Side Block):** Models returned two integers to move a displaced block on the floor along both x- and y-axes to an optimal, stable position on top of the tower.
Prompts for each task were standardized, instructing the model on the required response format (e.g., integer range, `tag`).

**Fine-Tuning Methods:**
- **Interactive Condition (Reinforcement Learning):** Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was used. The VLM served as the policy, generating 16 token sequences (actions) for each image-prompt input. Rewards were calculated based on a defined reward function, and adapter weights were updated via gradient ascent using a clipped advantage term.
- **Non-Interactive Condition (Supervised Fine-Tuning):** Models were trained using batch gradient descent to minimize the token-level cross-entropy loss against optimal action sequences. This method served as a baseline, analogous to behavioral cloning or offline RL.
- **Group Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO):** An alternative RL algorithm was also tested in ablations, using sequence-level optimization instead of token-level.

**Reward Functions:**
- **Binary Stability:** Rewards were -1 for non-parseable answers, 0 for incorrect legal answers, and 1 for correct legal answers.
- **X-only tasks:** Rewards were -5 for non-parseable completions. For parseable answers, Gaussian functions based on the distance from the optimal central position were used, with higher rewards for stable towers (20 * e^(-d^2)) and weaker rewards for unstable ones (2 * e^(-d^2) - 2).
- **X-Y task:** Rewards included -5 for non-parseable, -4 for moving below the floor. Gaussian functions based on Euclidean distance to the optimal position were used, with differentiated rewards for stable, within-tower, and above-ground/unstable scenarios.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Performance Improvement:** Accuracy for binary stability tasks and mean test reward for continuous action tasks on held-out data from the training distribution.
- **Generalization to Related Tasks:** Cross-evaluation where models trained on one task were tested on all other synthetic tasks.
- **Generalization to Real Images:** Performance on the Lerer et al. (2016) dataset, with human average performance as a reference.
- **Decodability Analysis:** Linear probes were trained on VLM activations (image tokens only) at each layer to predict ground-truth binary stability (logistic regression) and x-offset (linear regression), assessing "competence" regardless of output performance.
- **Attention Map Analysis:** Qualitative comparison of attention maps between base and fine-tuned models to identify shifts in visual focus.

**Ablation Studies:**
A series of ablations were conducted to test the robustness of the findings:
- **Model Variants:** Testing other Qwen and Llama/Gemma models of varying sizes.
- **Training Duration:** Extending GRPO training up to 48,000 steps.
- **Sequential SFT:** Fine-tuning pre-trained models on related tasks (e.g., X-only then Binary Stability) to check for transferable features.
- **Multi-Task Training:** Training models on two tasks sequentially (blocked) or simultaneously (interleaved).
- **Hyperparameters:** Varying adapter rank (1, 8 vs. 16), vision encoder fine-tuning, and generation length (with explicit reasoning prompts).
- **Multi-Step Interaction:** A triplet version of the x-y side block task was introduced, allowing for sequential actions and intermediate states to simulate more complex interaction.
- **Prompt Sensitivity:** Testing models with variations of prompts to assess robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several key findings concerning the ability of Vision Language Models (VLMs) to learn intuitive physics from interaction.

**Post-Training Performance Improvement:**
Both the interactive (GRPO) and non-interactive (SFT) fine-tuning methods enabled models to achieve high performance on their respective training tasks. For instance, on the "binary stability top block" task, GRPO achieved 0.943 accuracy, and SFT reached 0.969. Similarly, for "x-only top block," both GRPO and SFT models attained mean test rewards near the ceiling (19.999 and 20, respectively, out of 20). This indicates that both methods are effective in improving within-task performance but shows no direct benefit of interactive training in this context.

**Generalization to Related Tasks:**
Contrary to the initial hypothesis, neither GRPO nor SFT consistently produced models with generalizable physical intuitions across different tasks.
- **Limited Intra-Dataset Generalization:** A slight degree of generalization was observed for Qwen3-VL-8B between tasks that shared the same underlying data distribution, such as from "x-only top block" to "binary-stability top block." The GRPO model trained on "x-only top block" achieved an accuracy of 0.788 on "binary-stability top block," and the SFT model achieved 0.624 (random baseline 0.5). However, this generalization was limited and not robust.
- **Poor Inter-Dataset Generalization:** Generalization to tasks involving different datasets, even with similar physical principles (e.g., from "x-only top block" to "x-only side block" with larger block displacements), was largely absent. Models often failed to generate legal answers or performed at baseline levels.
- **Impact of Longer Training:** Ablations showed that training Qwen2.5-VL-7B models with GRPO for an extended duration (up to 48,000 steps) tended to cause overfitting to the specific training task, further diminishing any generalization to other tasks.

**Generalization to Real Images:**
Models were evaluated on real images of block towers from Lerer et al. (2016) to assess generalization to naturalistic data. All models, regardless of training method or specific synthetic training task, performed below the human average. For instance, the Qwen3-VL-8B GRPO model trained on "binary-stability top block" achieved 0.6 accuracy, while human performance was higher. This suggests a lack of robust transfer of learned intuitions from simulated environments to real-world visual data. No discernible benefit of interactive training over SFT was observed for this external generalization task.

**Decodability Analysis (Competence vs. Performance):**
An analysis of model activations revealed that key physical quantities, such as the binary stability of a tower and the x-offset of the top block, were highly decodable from the base model's intermediate layers. This decodability did not substantially change after either GRPO or SFT fine-tuning. This finding suggests that the models possessed the "competence" to represent the necessary physical information internally, but this competence did not consistently translate into improved "performance" or generalization on out-of-distribution tasks. This implies the models might be relying on superficial pixel-level shortcuts rather than learning underlying physical rules.

**Attention Maps:**
Qualitative inspection of attention maps did not reveal clear or consistent differences in how base models and fine-tuned models focused their attention on image regions, providing limited insight into the models' task-solving strategies.

**Ablation Studies Findings:**
- **Other Models and RL Algorithms:** Experiments with Qwen2.5-VL-7B, Qwen3-VL-32B, Llama-3.2-11B, Gemma-3-12B, and the GSPO RL algorithm generally corroborated the main findings. Generalization remained limited, with the 32B SFT model showing only a slight improvement in generalization over its RL counterparts for one related task.
- **Additional Supervised Fine-Tuning:** When Qwen2.5-VL-7B models pre-trained on "x-only top block" were subsequently fine-tuned for a limited number of steps on "binary-stability top block," they learned the new task faster than the base model. This suggests the initial training imparted some transferable features, enabling quicker adaptation rather than direct generalization.
- **Multi-Step Task:** A multi-step version of the "x-y side block" task, which involved sequential interactions, also failed to produce models that generalized, even to the single-step version of the same task. This indicates that even more complex interaction structures within the current framework did not facilitate robust generalization.
- **Multi-Task Training:** Sequential multi-task training with GRPO allowed models to retain performance on both tasks they were trained on, which was not observed for blocked SFT. However, this did not lead to generalization to an unseen related task. Interleaved SFT performed reasonably well on both post-training tasks.
- **Prompt Sensitivity:** Fine-tuned models demonstrated robustness to variations in prompt wording, indicating that the lack of generalization was not merely due to specific prompt phrasing.

In summary, the research found that while both interactive reinforcement learning and supervised fine-tuning effectively improved VLM performance on specific tasks, neither method reliably fostered generalizable intuitive physics knowledge that could transfer to novel, related tasks or real-world images. The consistent presence of decodable physical information in model activations, despite poor generalization, points towards models leveraging superficial cues rather than acquiring deep physical understanding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of how Vision Language Models (VLMs) acquire and generalize knowledge about intuitive physics, challenging a widely held hypothesis from cognitive science in the context of contemporary AI models. The findings suggest that the integration of interactive learning, as operationalized in this study through one-step reinforcement learning, does not confer a significant advantage over supervised fine-tuning for developing robust and generalizable physical intuitions in VLMs. This implies that merely interacting with a simulated environment under these conditions may not be sufficient for VLMs to transcend task-specific "shortcut learning" and develop a deeper, human-like understanding of physical dynamics.

The observation that key physical quantities are highly decodable from VLM activations, even in the base model and prior to any performance improvements on new tasks, highlights a crucial distinction between model *competence* (the internal representation of information) and *performance* (the ability to utilize that information to generate correct outputs, especially out-of-distribution). This gap suggests that current post-training methods may not effectively encourage models to leverage their internal representations for robust generalization, instead leading them to exploit surface-level correlations within the training data.

The study's implications extend to the design of future VLM training paradigms. The limited generalization observed across different tasks and to real-world images indicates that current parameter-efficient adaptation techniques, whether supervised or reinforcement-based, might be insufficient for instilling a comprehensive intuitive physics. This suggests that achieving human-like reasoning abilities in machines may necessitate a re-evaluation of fundamental pre-training strategies, data diversity, curriculum learning approaches, and potentially more complex, multi-step interaction paradigms that allow for observation of action outcomes and long state-action sequences. Furthermore, the findings motivate future work on auxiliary objectives that explicitly encourage models to ground their predictions in underlying physical variables, rather than relying on readily available but non-generalizable shortcuts.

While the immediate societal impact of this particular study is primarily within the domain of scientific advancement in machine learning, the broader goal of endowing AI with intuitive physics has potential long-term implications for fields such as robotics (for safer and more adaptable autonomous agents), education (for intelligent tutoring systems), and human-computer interaction (for more intuitive and predictable AI partners). The current findings serve as a benchmark for the limitations of existing approaches and guide future research towards more effective strategies for building physically intelligent AI.
