# Multi-Robot Open Adaptive Teaming Across Unseen Environments, Partners, and Scales

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces the Hypergraphic Open-ended Learning Algorithm (HOLA) framework, based on a novel hypergraphic-form game, to address the challenge of open adaptive multi-robot teaming. The framework enables multi-robot systems to simultaneously generalize across unseen environments, unknown partners, and varying team sizes, achieving robust sim-to-real transfer on multi-drone and multi-quadruped platforms without fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Current multi-robot coordination mechanisms rely on "closed-world assumptions," limiting deployment in complex real-world environments with unknown factors.
- Existing multi-agent learning (MAL) approaches address unseen partners, dynamic team composition, or unseen environments in isolation, often within simplified simulations, failing to generalize across all three simultaneously.
- Direct transfer of simulation-trained policies to physical multi-robot systems is difficult due to continuous dynamics, sensor noise, partial observability, and non-stationary teammate behaviors.

## Method

- A ==hypergraphic-form game== formulation (**OH-Game**) is introduced to model dynamic, team-level cooperative relationships and infer coordination structures beyond pairwise interactions.
- The **Hypergraphic Open-ended Learning Algorithm (HOLA)** is developed, featuring a ==pre-training phase== for diverse agents and an iterative ==open-ended learning phase==.
- HOLA's ==Oracle module==, with a ==ϕ Solver==, trains new policies to become "best-preferred" partners by sampling low-capability partners, diverse environments, and dynamic team compositions.

## Results

- HOLA achieved an **82%** success rate in fixed-team scenarios with unseen teammates and environments, outperforming the next best baseline (FCP) by **10** percentage points, while maintaining lower collision rates.
- In multi-drone open team coordination, HOLA demonstrated capture rates of approximately **73%** in seen environments and **85%** in unseen environments, along with low collision rates (**~14%**), significantly outperforming baselines.
- The learned policies transferred directly to physical multi-drone and multi-quadruped platforms without fine-tuning, demonstrating robust generalization across unseen environments, partners, and dynamic team sizes in real-world deployments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modeling multi-agent cooperation using hypergraphs provides a principled game-theoretic foundation for representing higher-order dependencies essential for complex, dynamic coordination tasks.
> - An open-ended learning approach, specifically targeting training with challenging partners and diverse environmental conditions, enables agents to learn versatile and robust coordination strategies.
> - Quantifying cooperative ability through Hyper-preference Centrality and inverse-weighting partner sampling allows for systematically developing agents that can adapt to a wide range of teammates and scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of multi-robot systems is progressing towards deployment in complex, dynamic real-world environments such as disaster zones, warehouses, and surveillance networks. However, current multi-robot coordination mechanisms often rely on "closed-world assumptions," assuming fixed team compositions, known teammates, and familiar environments. This paradigm, exemplified by Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE), has achieved results in controlled settings but faces fundamental limitations when these assumptions are violated.

Existing multi-agent learning (MAL) research in machine learning has explored challenges related to unseen partners, such as "zero-shot coordination" and "ad hoc teamwork," and "dynamic team composition" for variable team sizes. However, these efforts frequently address these dimensions in isolation and often operate within simulated environments with discrete action spaces and stable communication, which do not fully reflect the complexities of physical multi-robot systems. Real-world robotics introduces continuous dynamics, sensor noise, partial observability, and potential communication loss, making direct transfer of simulation-trained policies difficult. While domain randomization has aided single-robot sim-to-real transfer, its extension to multi-robot coordination is complicated by the non-stationary and unpredictable nature of teammate behaviors.

This paper positions itself by formalizing the challenge of "open adaptive multi-robot teaming," which necessitates simultaneous adaptation to unseen environments, unknown partners, and varying team sizes in real time, without retraining. It aims to bridge the gap between theoretical advances in multi-agent coordination and practical deployment on physical multi-robot platforms, addressing the combined uncertainties of both environment and social interaction.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this work stems from the limitations of current multi-robot systems in real-world deployment. As multi-robot applications move beyond controlled laboratory settings, robots must contend with dynamic and unpredictable conditions. Specifically, the authors identify three simultaneous challenges that existing approaches generally fail to address collectively:
1. **Unseen Environments:** Robots must operate effectively in novel physical layouts and obstacle configurations that were not part of their training data.
2. **Unknown Partners:** Robots must coordinate with teammates whose behaviors or policies they have never encountered during training, without pre-established coordination conventions.
3. **Varying Team Sizes:** Robot teams must be able to adapt to dynamic changes in composition, where agents join or depart during task execution, requiring continuous re-coordination.

The overarching objective of this research is to formalize this combined challenge as the "open adaptive multi-robot teaming problem" and to develop a unified framework that enables multi-robot systems to simultaneously generalize across these three dimensions. This involves creating a principled foundation for coordination structure inference that can adapt dynamically to team changes and facilitate robust sim-to-real transfer without fine-tuning.

To achieve this, the paper sets out to:
- Introduce a **hypergraphic-form game formulation** to model dynamic, team-level cooperative relationships, moving beyond traditional pairwise interaction models to capture higher-order dependencies essential for complex coordination tasks.
- Develop the **Hypergraphic Open-ended Learning Algorithm (HOLA)**, an adaptive training framework that progressively expands the diversity of training partners and environments. This framework aims to enable agents to learn robust coordination strategies that are not optimized for fixed team configurations.
- Validate the proposed approach through **extensive simulations and real-world deployments** on diverse multi-robot platforms (multi-drones and multi-quadrupeds), demonstrating simultaneous generalization across all three adaptability dimensions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research centers on the Hypergraphic Open-ended Learning Algorithm (HOLA), which is built upon a novel game-theoretic formulation.

**A. Problem Formalization: Open Adaptive Teaming**
The problem is formalized as an "Open Adaptive Teaming problem for multi-robot systems," defined by a tuple ⟨L, U, S, A, Θ, E, Φ, R, P, Penv⟩. This includes learnable robot agents (L), uncontrolled partner agents (U), state space (S), action space (A), partner behavior type space (Θ), environment space (E), robot constraint set (Φ), reward function (R), transition function (P), and environment distribution (Penv). Key aspects of this formalization include:
- **Variable Team Size:** A joint agent-action space (AC) and joint agent-type space (ΘC) are defined to explicitly account for dynamic team compositions.
- **Reward Function:** The reward R is decomposed into Rtask (task performance) and Rconstraint (penalizing constraint violations), ensuring learned policies generate robot-feasible behaviors.
- **Dynamic Transitions:** The transition function P allows for changes in both state and the set of existing agents (C_t) with their types, reflecting agents joining or departing mid-episode.
The objective is to learn policies for learnable agents that maximize expected discounted return while respecting robot constraints.

**B. Open Preference Hypergraph (OPG) and Hypergraphic-Form Game**
To model complex multi-agent interactions, the paper proposes a "hypergraphic-form game formulation."
- **Open Hypergraphic-Form Game (OH-Game):** Defined as G = (V, E, w, L), where V represents agent policies, E is a set of hyperedges connecting arbitrary numbers of vertices (agents), w assigns utilities (expected returns) to hyperedges, and L specifies the range of hyperedge cardinalities (team sizes). This formulation represents higher-order cooperative relationships that cannot be captured by pairwise interactions, explicitly as a game-theoretic construct, not a graph neural network.
- **Open Preference Hypergraph (OPG):** Derived from the OH-Game, OPG is an unweighted directed hypergraph (V, E_e) that captures each agent's preferred coalition partners for each possible team size by selecting the hyperedge with maximal utility.
- **Hyper-preference Centrality (η):** Introduced to quantify the cooperative ability of each node (agent). It aggregates the importance of a node across different cardinalities (team sizes), measuring how many agents prefer to coordinate with it. A best-preferred agent is defined as one with η = 1.

**C. Hypergraphic Open-ended Learning Algorithm (HOLA)**
HOLA integrates the hypergraphic game formulation into an open-ended learning framework, consisting of two main phases:
1. **Pre-training Phase:** An initial population of diverse drone agents is pre-trained using maximum entropy reinforcement learning, extended with a maximum entropy population-based training (MEP) objective. This encourages the development of distinct yet cooperatively effective strategies.
2. **Open-ended Learning Phase:** This iterative phase continuously develops "best-preferred" agents within the evolving OH-Game. It comprises two modules:
 - **Grapher Module:** Constructs the latest OH-Game (G_j) by integrating newly generated learner agents (Π_new_j) with the existing population (V_j-1). It samples partner agents to form hyperedges for various cardinalities and evaluates coordination performance across diverse environments (ε ∼ P_env) to compute hyperedge weights (w_j(e)). This formulation considers both task performance and physical constraint satisfaction.
 - **Oracle Module:** This module trains new policies (π_j+1) to become "best-preferred" partners. It defines an objective function (J_j) that includes three expectation terms:
 - **Partner Sampling (π_-j ∼ ϕ_j(V_j)):** Targets coordination with low-capability partners, ensuring agents learn to adapt to challenging teammates.
 - **Environment Distribution (e ∼ P_env):** Promotes generalization across diverse environmental scenarios.
 - **Dynamic Team Composition (C_t ∼ P_team):** Models within-episode changes in team membership.
 - **ϕ Solver Submodule:** Within the Oracle, this submodule computes the partner sampling distribution (ϕ_j). It inversely weights agents by their cooperative capacity, ensuring that agents with lower cooperative ability receive higher sampling probability. This distribution is derived using the inverse of the generalized Myerson value, adapted for hypergraphs with varying cardinalities (which is shown to be equivalent to summing Shapley values across coalition sizes). The training objective aims for the new policy to achieve a high rank (e.g., top m) in hyper-preference centrality, making it an "approximate best-preferred agent."

**D. Experimental Setting**
- **Task:** Cooperative multi-robot pursuit, where pursuers must capture evaders in an arena with obstacles.
- **Platforms:** Multi-drone system (Crazyflie 2.1 quadrotors) and multi-quadruped system (GEMBODY AI L1 robots) were used for real-world deployments. Both utilize motion capture for global pose and centralized planners.
- **Evaluation Protocols:**
 - **Open Team Protocol:** Tests all three adaptability dimensions simultaneously (unseen teammates, unseen environments, dynamic team size changes within episodes).
 - **Fixed Team Protocol:** A controlled ablation study where team size remains constant within episodes, while unseen teammates and environments are still present.
- **Evader Policy:** An escape policy based on repulsive forces from pursuers/obstacles and wall-following rules.
- **Unseen Teammate Pools:** Heterogeneous pools constructed from policies trained with different algorithms (Greedy, VICSEK, D3QN-G) to span a range of cooperative capabilities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of HOLA was conducted through extensive simulations and real-world deployments, assessing both fundamental coordination capabilities and comprehensive open adaptive teaming performance.

**A. Fixed Team Coordination**
1. **Coordination with Unseen Teammates and Environments:**
 - HOLA was compared against six baseline methods (MAPPO, DACOOP-A, self-play, PBT, FCP, MEP) in a 3-pursuer-2-evader scenario with randomly sampled heterogeneous teammates.
 - HOLA achieved an 82% success rate, outperforming the second-best FCP (72% success rate). It also exhibited a lower collision rate (13.67%) compared to baselines (PBT had the highest at 22.67%) and a shorter mean episode length (663.72 timesteps) than FCP (801.19). This indicates superior zero-shot coordination with unknown partners.
 - In unseen, randomly generated environments, HOLA maintained the highest success rate (44.00%) and matched the lowest collision rate (54.67%) among all methods. It ranked second in average success timesteps (1294.65), closely trailing PBT. This confirmed effective simultaneous adaptation to both partner and environmental uncertainties in a fixed team setting.
2. **Ablation Study: Effectiveness of ϕ Solver Module:**
 - A variant, HOLA_R (without the ϕ solver, using inverse mean reward), showed consistent performance degradation across all metrics. It exhibited lower success rates, higher collision rates, and longer episode lengths compared to the full HOLA. This highlights the importance of explicitly modeling team-level cooperative dependencies and targeted partner sampling using the hypergraphic formulation.

**B. Open Team Coordination**
This protocol introduced dynamic team composition changes *within* episodes, along with unseen environments, representing a more challenging scenario.

1. **Multi-Drone Pursuit:**
 - HOLA consistently achieved the highest capture rates: approximately 73% in seen environments and 85% in unseen environments.
 - A notable observation was that most methods showed higher capture rates in unseen (three-obstacle) environments compared to seen (one-obstacle) environments, attributed to richer obstacle topology creating more strategic cornering opportunities for evaders. HOLA demonstrated robust performance across both, with a smooth transition.
 - HOLA completed tasks efficiently (around 320 timesteps in both conditions) and maintained low collision rates (approximately 14% in unseen environments), significantly outperforming baselines like self-play (24% collision) and PBT (44% collision). This indicated that HOLA found coordinated strategies that enhanced both efficiency and safety.

2. **Multi-Quadruped Pursuit:**
 - On quadruped platforms, overall performance was lower across all methods due to the increased complexity of legged locomotion and tighter spatial constraints.
 - HOLA maintained a lead with 57.2% and 62.6% capture rates in seen and unseen environments, respectively, consistently outperforming the closest baseline (MAPPO) by 6-10 percentage points.
 - HOLA also demonstrated superior efficiency, completing tasks 60-80 timesteps faster than MAPPO, self-play, and PBT.
 - Collision rates were higher on quadrupeds generally. HOLA recorded 39.6% (seen) and 33.0% (unseen), generally the lowest. While MAPPO had a slightly lower collision rate (31.8%) in unseen environments, it came at the cost of a 6-percentage-point deficit in capture rate, suggesting a more conservative strategy compared to HOLA's aggressive but coordinated approach.

**Summary of Results:**
The results across both robotic platforms and environmental conditions consistently demonstrate that:
- The hypergraphic formulation in HOLA enables robust open team coordination that generalizes across different robotic morphologies, environmental layouts, and dynamic team compositions.
- HOLA's performance advantage is substantial, achieving simultaneous optimization of capture efficiency and collision avoidance.
- The consistency of HOLA’s performance across seen and unseen conditions suggests that it learns transferable relational patterns rather than environment-specific strategies, a critical property for real-world application.
- Learned policies transferred directly to physical hardware without fine-tuning, validating the sim-to-real transferability and robustness of the approach in novel real-world scenarios with unseen teammates.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in multi-robot collaboration by formally defining and providing a solution for the "open adaptive multi-robot teaming problem." Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

**Theoretical Contributions:**
- **Formalization of a Unified Challenge:** The paper formally defines a critical and previously under-addressed problem in multi-robot systems, encompassing simultaneous adaptation to unseen environments, unknown partners, and dynamic team scales. This provides a clear framework for future research.
- **Novel Game-Theoretic Formulation:** The introduction of a hypergraphic-form game to model team-level cooperative relationships offers a principled foundation for understanding and inferring coordination structures beyond pairwise interactions. This is a significant theoretical departure from existing graph-based neural architectures, providing a more robust mechanism for credit assignment in dynamically changing teams.
- **Adaptive Learning Framework:** HOLA's open-ended learning approach, specifically its Oracle module with the ϕ Solver, systematically trains agents to become versatile and "best-preferred" partners by targeting collaboration with low-capability agents and diverse environments.

**Practical Implications and Impact:**
- **Enhanced Real-World Deployability:** By demonstrating robust sim-to-real transfer of learned policies to physical multi-drone and multi-quadruped platforms without fine-tuning, the research significantly reduces a major barrier to deploying multi-robot systems in unstructured, unpredictable environments. This implies that robots can be deployed without extensive prior knowledge of their operational context or specific teammates.
- **Flexible and Resilient Robot Teams:** The ability of HOLA-trained agents to adapt to dynamic team compositions (agents joining or leaving mid-task) and coordinate with unseen partners leads to more flexible and resilient multi-robot systems. This is crucial for applications where team integrity or composition cannot be guaranteed, such as search-and-rescue, disaster response, and dynamic logistics.
- **Broadened Application Scope:** The findings suggest potential for deploying multi-robot teams in a wider array of real-world scenarios, including:
 - **Search and Rescue:** Teams can dynamically reconfigure and coordinate with unfamiliar units in complex, changing environments.
 - **Warehouse Automation:** Robots can seamlessly integrate new agents or adapt to unexpected robot failures.
 - **Surveillance and Exploration:** Teams can maintain cooperative coverage despite varying team sizes or unforeseen environmental changes.
 - **Defense and Security:** Operations requiring robust collaboration in dynamic and adversarial settings.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as improving safety in dense interactions, scaling to larger teams, and extending to broader task families, provide clear directions for future research, indicating the foundational nature of this work.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the capabilities of multi-robot systems, moving them closer to reliable operation in complex, open-world scenarios by addressing the combined challenges of adaptability and generalization.
