# The Last AI Built by Humans: Toward Genuine Recursive Self-Improvement

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey and roadmap for recursive self-improvement (RSI): AI systems that turn experience into persistent self-changes which in turn reshape how later improvements are generated, validated, and inherited. It introduces the Headroom-Closed Index to normalize cross-benchmark progress, organizes work into five autonomy levels from improvement execution to meta-improvement around the improvement loop, and maps four application regimes plus industrial practice.

## Problem

- Frontier model development stays human-coordinated and resource-heavy: engineers still decide what to improve, build the data and environments, and verify every candidate change.
- Prior self-evolution surveys organize work by update object, stage, or mechanism, so systems changing the same component but delegating very different decisions look alike.
- Reported gains conflate higher task scores with genuine recursion; fixed archive rules, evaluator exploitation, and unrollbackable persistence go unmeasured in existing evaluations.

## Method

- ==Headroom-Closed Index== normalizes each benchmark against its entry-year 90th-percentile frontier, aggregating families per domain-year with square-root model-count weighting and credibility weights on multi-source consensus scores.
- An ==improvement-loop anatomy== (system state, experience, target, improver, strategy, verifier, successor) grounds a five-level ==autonomy hierarchy== graded by which improvement decisions the AI internalizes, not by capability.
- The same loop questions are applied across four feedback regimes (science, embodied intelligence, software engineering, healthcare) and eight industrial pipelines, separating ==structural== from ==effective recursion==.

## Results

- Across 393 model-benchmark observations, 2026 headroom closure reaches **86.4** for advanced mathematics and **85.8** for graduate science, but only **52.6** for software engineering and **39.9** for tool agents.
- Persistence failures recur: **14%** of Godel Agent's 100 MGSM trials ended below the starting policy, while Darwin Godel Machine lifts a SWE-bench subset **20%** to **50%** with selection rules left outside self-modification.
- A-Evolve-Training raised an external 30B Nemotron score from **0.80** to **0.86** over four autonomous rounds versus **0.87** for the top human submission; an evolved outer improver showed no significant efficiency advantage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Higher autonomy is not better improvement: delegated authority coexists with inefficient search, regression, and evaluator gaming, so score responsibility scope separately from gain quality.
> - Distinguish structural recursion, where a revised mechanism is inherited and invoked, from effective recursion, where it yields stronger successors under matched budgets; most claimed RSI shows only the former.
> - Verification, not candidate generation, is the binding constraint: freeze evaluators within epochs, anchor to independent ground truth, and match compute before crediting self-improvement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position within the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is a survey and conceptual framework for recursive self-improvement (RSI), rather than a report of one narrowly defined algorithm or benchmark. It sits at the intersection of several research traditions: self-modifying programs and the Gödel Machine, automated machine learning, neural architecture search, continual and lifelong learning, meta-learning, agentic systems, automated scientific discovery, reinforcement learning, and software-agent evolution.

The authors argue that these areas have often been discussed separately even though they address related parts of an improvement process. Continual learning concerns the retention of knowledge across tasks, AutoML automates model-development decisions, and agentic AI enables multistep interaction and tool use. However, the paper maintains that none of these properties alone establishes RSI. The defining issue is whether a persistent system can use experience to modify itself and whether that modification subsequently changes how later improvements are generated, evaluated, selected, or retained.

The paper’s central contribution is therefore organizational. It treats the complete improvement loop as the unit of analysis and classifies systems according to which decisions have been transferred from human designers or fixed infrastructure to the AI system. This distinguishes the paper from surveys organized mainly by algorithms, model components, or application stages.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. Development bottlenecks

The paper begins from the observation that modern AI development is becoming increasingly expensive and operationally complex. Foundation-model development requires large-scale data preparation, architecture design, distributed training, evaluation, and infrastructure debugging. Post-training and reinforcement learning also require extensive generation, filtering, verification, and curation of learning experience.

The authors identify three related burdens. First, training and evaluating large models require substantial compute and expert labor. Second, increasingly capable systems require scalable feedback environments, synthetic data pipelines, task generators, and reliable evaluators. Third, deployed agents require continuing adaptation as documents, tools, software repositories, users, and workflows change. These adaptations are usually performed through repeated human-led cycles of failure diagnosis, system revision, testing, and release.

RSI is presented as a response to this coordination burden. The paper defines it as an autonomous, closed-loop process in which an AI system identifies limitations, proposes and validates improvements, retains accepted changes, and uses the resulting capabilities to improve the process of future improvement. The intended benefits are not limited to higher task scores. RSI may also improve resource efficiency or enable discovery of strategies not explicitly prescribed by human developers.

#### 2.2. Conceptual distinctions

The authors emphasize three conditions for credible RSI. The first is safe inheritance: improvements must persist without causing regression or propagating erroneous knowledge. The second is autonomy attribution: researchers must establish which decisions are controlled by the AI rather than by fixed search procedures, human acceptance criteria, or external evaluators. The third is reliable verification: repeated access to an evaluator must not allow the system to exploit weaknesses in the measurement process.

The paper also distinguishes ordinary task refinement from system improvement. Its baseline, B0, covers methods such as self-critique, reflection, and tree search when changes remain confined to the current task. RSI begins only when a validated change becomes persistent system state.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Autonomy hierarchy

The framework contains five levels:

- **L1: Improvement-execution autonomy.** The AI executes a human-defined improvement procedure and retains its outputs.
- **L2: Improvement-strategy autonomy.** The AI diagnoses weaknesses and chooses which intervention to attempt, while the objective and evaluation criteria remain externally specified.
- **L3: Experience-acquisition autonomy.** The AI determines which tasks, examples, or interactions it should acquire next based on its evolving capabilities and failures.
- **L4: Environment-adaptation autonomy.** The AI uses feedback from deployment to revise persistent memories, skills, tools, code, harnesses, or other operational components.
- **L5: Recursive-inheritance autonomy.** The AI modifies and reuses the mechanism that governs subsequent improvement, such as an improver, evaluator, research policy, or successor-generation procedure.

For every system, the authors ask where the loop closes, what state is inherited, and which decisions remain external. They distinguish **structural recursion**, where a revised mechanism is reused in a later round, from **effective recursion**, where the revised mechanism demonstrably produces stronger successors under comparable budgets and independent evaluation.

#### 3.2. Empirical landscape analysis

The paper introduces the Headroom-Closed Index (HCI) to compare progress across heterogeneous benchmarks. Scores are normalized relative to the 90th-percentile performance observed when each benchmark first enters the dataset, with zero representing that entry-year frontier and 100 representing a perfect score. The authors then aggregate benchmark-family frontiers into domain trajectories using a square-root weighting based on the number of contributing models.

The audit includes 393 model–benchmark observations across ten capability domains for models released between 2023 and September 2026. To reduce protocol inconsistency, the authors link results only when benchmark versions and evaluation harnesses are stable or can be bridged through overlapping model evaluations. They also apply weighted consensus rules that give different weights to benchmark-owner results, independent evaluations, combined reports, and model-author reports.

#### 3.3. Literature and industry review

The review covers 491 papers and systems, supplemented by technical reports, engineering blogs, open-source repositories, product documentation, and company disclosures. The authors organize examples by autonomy level, improvement target, application domain, and industrial role.

The application analysis covers scientific discovery, embodied intelligence, software engineering, and healthcare. These domains were selected because they provide different feedback regimes: uncertain experimental evidence in science, costly physical interaction in robotics, executable tests in software, and delayed, safety-critical outcomes in healthcare.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Uneven capability progress

The HCI analysis indicates that capability growth is uneven across domains. By 2026, advanced mathematics and graduate-level science reach HCI values of 86.4 and 85.8, respectively, while broad knowledge reaches 77.2. Interactive capabilities remain further from their benchmark ceilings: software engineering reaches 52.6, search and terminal agents 56.8, and tool-use agents 39.9. The paper interprets this gap as evidence that success on bounded reasoning tasks has not transferred uniformly to long-horizon workflows requiring planning, state tracking, tool selection, and adaptation.

The authors use these differences to motivate RSI, particularly for interactive tasks where persistent experience, targeted practice, and deployment feedback could address recurring failures. However, the post-2026 projections in the figure are explicitly illustrative rather than measured results.

#### 4.2. Distribution of RSI research

The survey finds that research is concentrated at lower autonomy levels. Of the 491 surveyed papers, 215 are classified as L1, 155 as L2, 64 as L3, 28 as L4, and 29 as L5. Thus, most work concerns execution automation or strategy search, while experience autonomy, deployment adaptation, and recursive meta-improvement are less common.

At L1, examples include data curation, synthetic-data generation, procedural debugging, deployment adaptation, and harness engineering. At L2, systems search over prompts, agent architectures, workflows, training programs, kernels, and model configurations. At L3, systems such as adaptive self-play, Voyager, SIMA 2, and SEAgent use learner state or prior interaction history to shape future practice. L4 systems retain skills, memories, executable tools, or harness changes based on deployment evidence. L5 examples include STOP, Gödel Agent, A-Evolve-Training, and the Red Queen Gödel Machine, but the paper characterizes their evidence as bounded and incomplete.

#### 4.3. Domain-specific conclusions

Software engineering provides the clearest experimental setting because code, tests, versions, and rollback mechanisms are directly available. Nevertheless, passing repository tests does not guarantee security, maintainability, or fidelity to user intent.

Scientific RSI benefits from executable experiments and reusable research artifacts, but causal attribution is difficult because failed outcomes may result from hypotheses, protocols, instruments, or models. Embodied intelligence can use environmental interaction as feedback, but physical trials are expensive, difficult to reproduce, and potentially unsafe. Healthcare requires stronger oversight because clinical outcomes are delayed, confounded, population-specific, and consequential.

Industrial examples show practical forms of persistent adaptation. Humanlaya reports that four feedback-driven quality-system updates reduced key defects from 9.0% to 3.7% on held-out task packages. Xiaohongshu reports recommendation-score improvements from its dual-timescale memory and post-training loop. Other systems, including Theseus, ModelBest, Tencent Hunyuan, Agent-Native Research Lab, and Frontis.AI, illustrate environment reconstruction, reusable engineering knowledge, executable experience banks, verifiable research artifacts, and cross-task meta-improvement. The paper repeatedly notes that many of these results are company-reported and have not been independently replicated.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s significance lies primarily in establishing a vocabulary for separating ordinary automation from genuine recursive improvement. Its autonomy hierarchy can help researchers specify whether a system merely executes a fixed update, selects improvement strategies, controls its learning agenda, adapts during deployment, or modifies the improvement mechanism itself.

If developed safely, RSI could reduce the labor required for model engineering, improve long-horizon agents through accumulated experience, support more adaptive scientific research, and enable software systems that diagnose and repair recurring weaknesses. It could also shift AI development from isolated training runs toward persistent, versioned, and evidence-based improvement pipelines.

The paper does not conclude that fully autonomous RSI has been achieved. Instead, it identifies persistent-state management, evaluator independence, cross-component diagnosis, safe transfer, long-horizon testing, cost accounting, and human governance as unresolved requirements. Its central empirical claim is limited: current systems demonstrate several components of RSI, especially L1 and L2, with emerging evidence for L3, L4, and bounded L5. Genuine RSI would require repeated, attributable, transferable improvements in the system’s capacity to improve itself, measured under explicit resource constraints and protected evaluation.
