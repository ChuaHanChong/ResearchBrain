# Intelligent Switching for Reset-Free RL

> [!summary] Summary
> RISC is a reset-free RL algorithm that alternates forward and backward (reset) controllers, switching early based on a learned success-critic competency score rather than fixed time limits, combined with timeout-nonterminal value bootstrapping. It sets or matches state-of-the-art on 3 of 4 EARL benchmark tasks with faster sample efficiency.

## Problem

- Reverse-curriculum reset-free methods spend most environment interactions revisiting state-goal regions the agent has already mastered near the goal, wasting sample budget.
- Prior forward-backward RL work lacks a principled, consistent bootstrapping strategy for the terminal state when handing control between forward and reset controllers.
- Existing switching mechanisms trigger only on fixed time limits or reaching the current goal, ignoring the agent's actual confidence in that region of state space.

## Method

- ==Success critic Q_F==, trained via TD like a value function on the same batch as the policy, estimates competency F(s,g) as discounted expected steps to reach goal g from state s.
- **Intelligent early-switching rule** sets switch probability to F(s,g) times (1 minus beta^t), so switching likelihood grows with competency and trajectory length rather than being fixed, damped by three modulation mechanisms (switching-portion fraction zeta, minimum trajectory length, conservative factor beta) to prevent oscillatory back-and-forth thrashing.
- ==Timeout-nonterminal bootstrapping== always bootstraps the successor state's value at controller-switch points, keeping TD targets consistent across the alternating forward and reset roles.

## Results

- **RISC** achieves best-in-class reset-free returns on 3 of 4 EARL tasks: Tabletop Manipulation **1.0±0.0**, Sawyer Door **1.0±0.0**, Sawyer Peg **0.42±0.07**, beating FBRL, MEDAL, VapRL, and R3L baselines.
- Ablation shows removing timeout-aware bootstrapping causes the largest performance drop and removing early switching causes a moderate drop, confirming both components are necessary.
- RISC attains the highest interquartile-mean area-under-curve across environments among all compared methods, and on a 4-rooms gridworld its visitation stays more evenly spread with sustained high Optimal-Value-Percent-Difference, while a reverse-curriculum baseline concentrates experience in already-learned states.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Consistent value-target bootstrapping across controller switches matters as much as, or more than, the exact switching heuristic for stable non-episodic training.
> - Gating exploration on a learned competency estimate turns dual-controller curricula outside-in, a pattern that could generalize to any multi-controller exploration setup beyond reset-free RL.
> - Naively using a raw competency score as switch probability causes pathological thrashing between controllers, so any confidence-based exploration signal needs explicit rate-damping mechanisms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has achieved significant successes, predominantly in simulated environments, where agents benefit from frequent and automatic environment resets. These resets allow agents to practice tasks from consistent starting states, aiding exploration and recovery from undesirable states. However, the requirement for such strong episodic resetting mechanisms poses a fundamental limitation for applying RL to real-world scenarios, particularly in robotics, where resets often necessitate manual intervention, specialized engineering, or are impractical to implement.

The challenge of learning in environments without automatic resets has led to the emergence of the "reset-free" or "autonomous RL" setting. Prior research in this area commonly employs a dual-controller approach: a "forward controller" trained to achieve the task goal, and a "reset controller" designed to return the agent to a favorable initial or learning-conducive state distribution. Examples of such approaches include Forward Backward RL (FBRL), R3L, VapRL, MEDAL, and IBC. While these methods explore different strategies for the reset controller (e.g., novelty-seeking, demonstration-based, curriculum-building), the mechanisms governing the *switching* between the forward and reset controllers have received less comprehensive investigation.

Specifically, the paper identifies two underexplored aspects within existing reset-free RL methods:
1. **Bootstrapping Strategies:** The method of bootstrapping the value of the last state in a trajectory, particularly when a controller switches rather than reaching a natural terminal state, has not been consistently addressed. Previous work (Pardo et al., 2022) indicated that different bootstrapping strategies could lead to different policies, a finding this paper aims to extend and analyze within the reset-free context.
2. **Timing of Switching:** Most existing reset-free RL algorithms utilize fixed time limits for switching between controllers. This can lead to inefficiencies, as agents may spend valuable interaction time in state-space regions where they are already proficient. The potential for agents to learn *when* to switch, based on their current competence, represents an avenue for improving sample efficiency.

This work contributes to the reset-free RL landscape by addressing these two aspects. It proposes an algorithm that integrates a principled bootstrapping strategy for inter-controller transitions and introduces an intelligent, confidence-based switching mechanism. This approach aims to enhance the performance and sample efficiency of agents operating in real-world-like continuous environments lacking frequent manual resets.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the disparity between the training conditions of most successful RL algorithms and the constraints of real-world deployment. The frequent and automatic environment resets common in simulated training environments are typically unavailable or prohibitively expensive in physical systems such as robots. These resets are crucial in episodic RL, enabling agents to practice tasks from a consistent initial state, recover from failures, and efficiently explore the state space. Without them, agents must learn not only the task but also how to return to advantageous learning positions, often requiring additional handcrafted mechanisms or human intervention.

The paper identifies specific challenges and unaddressed opportunities within the existing framework of reset-free RL, leading to the following key objectives:

- **Address Limitations of Episodic RL in Reset-Free Settings:** The primary objective is to develop RL algorithms that can effectively learn complex tasks in environments where automatic, frequent resets are not provided during training. This directly targets the scalability and practical applicability of RL to real-world robotics and other physical systems.
- **Analyze and Optimize Bootstrapping Strategies:** To investigate the impact of different bootstrapping methods on policy learning in reset-free RL. The motivation is to determine a strategy that ensures consistent learning targets for the value function when an agent's control policy switches mid-trajectory between a forward (task-oriented) and a reset (initial-state-oriented) controller. The hypothesis is that improper bootstrapping can introduce inconsistencies that hinder learning efficiency and policy quality.
- **Develop an Intelligent Controller Switching Mechanism:** To move beyond fixed-time switching policies, the paper aims to develop a mechanism that allows the agent to intelligently decide *when* to switch between its forward and reset controllers. The motivation is to improve sample efficiency by enabling the agent to spend less time in state-space regions it has already mastered and more time exploring or practicing in areas where its competence is lower. This is driven by the observation that traditional curriculum learning approaches, such as reverse curriculums, might lead to redundant exploration in known areas.
- **Formulate a Novel Algorithm (RISC):** To integrate the optimized bootstrapping strategy and the intelligent switching mechanism into a cohesive reset-free RL algorithm named Reset Free RL with Intelligently Switching Controller (RISC). The objective is to demonstrate that combining these two elements can lead to performance improvements over existing reset-free methods.
- **Empirical Validation on Challenging Benchmarks:** To rigorously evaluate RISC's performance against existing state-of-the-art reset-free RL algorithms on challenging robot manipulation and navigation tasks, specifically using the EARL benchmark. This aims to empirically confirm the benefits of the proposed approach in terms of learning speed and final task achievement.
- **Component Contribution Assessment:** To conduct an ablation study to quantify the individual contributions of the proper bootstrapping and intelligent switching components to RISC's overall performance, thereby clarifying their necessity and impact.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between simulation-trained RL and real-world application by enhancing the core mechanisms of reset-free learning, specifically focusing on how agents manage transitions and allocate learning effort across different sub-goals.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Reset Free RL with Intelligently Switching Controller (RISC), addresses the challenges of reset-free learning through two primary mechanisms: a principled approach to value function bootstrapping during controller switching and an intelligent, confidence-based strategy for deciding when to switch between forward and reset policies.

#### 3.1. Bootstrapping for Reset-Free RL

The paper first examines the implications of bootstrapping strategies for value function estimation, particularly when trajectories are terminated by external factors or controller switches rather than natural terminal states.
- **Problem Identification:** In traditional episodic RL, trajectories often end due to time limits. Two common strategies exist: `timeout-nonterminal`, where the value of the last state is bootstrapped using its successor's estimated value (as per the Bellman equation), and `timeout-terminal`, where the last state's value is treated as zero. The paper references prior work showing that `timeout-nonterminal` can lead to better policies.
- **Analysis in Reset-Free Context:** The analysis extends to reset-free RL, where the agent constantly switches between a forward policy ($\pi_f$) targeting task goals and a reset policy ($\pi_r$) targeting initial states. In this setting, the `timeout-terminal` strategy (Equation 5) causes the value function's learning target for one policy to become dependent on the actions and timeout rules of the *other* policy. This interdependency, where switching rules are agent-dependent, can lead to inconsistent learning targets and hinder the development of stable policies.
- **RISC's Solution:** RISC adopts the `timeout-nonterminal` bootstrapping strategy (Equation 4). This approach ensures that the value function for each policy maintains consistent learning targets, as it always bootstraps from the successor state's estimated value, irrespective of a controller switch. This is argued to be crucial for robust learning in environments with dynamically changing effective episode boundaries.

#### 3.2. Intelligent Switching Mechanism

RISC's second core component is a mechanism for intelligently deciding when to switch between the forward and reset controllers, moving beyond fixed time limits.
- **Motivation for Intelligent Switching:** Traditional reverse curriculums, while useful, can lead to agents spending excessive interaction time in state-space regions where they have already achieved mastery. RISC aims to optimize experience generation by encouraging exploration in less familiar areas.
- **Competency Estimation (Success Critic):**
 - To enable intelligent switching, RISC requires a measure of the agent's competence in achieving its current goal. This is formalized as `F_π(s, g) = γ_sc^t`, where `t` is the minimum number of steps taken by policy `π` to reach goal `g` from state `s`, and `γ_sc` is a success critic discount factor. This metric decreases with the time required to reach the goal, effectively assigning lower competence to states further from the goal or requiring longer trajectories.
 - A neural network, termed the "success critic" (`Q_F`), is trained to estimate `F_π(s, g)`. Its structure is analogous to a Q-function, updated using temporal difference learning. The "reward" for `Q_F` is 1 if the current state `s_t+1` matches the goal `g` (based on a binary success function `f(s, g)`) and `γ_sc * Q_F(s_t+1, a_t+1, g)` otherwise.
 - The `Q_F` is trained concurrently with the agent's main policy and value critic, using the same experience buffer and discount factor `γ_sc = γ`. At inference, `F_π(s, g)` is derived by averaging `Q_F` over actions sampled from the current policy.
- **Modulating Switching Behavior:** To prevent excessive or unstable switching based solely on `F_π(s, g)`:
 - **1. Switching on Portion of Trajectories (`ζ`):** At the beginning of each trajectory, intelligent switching is only enabled with a probability `ζ`. If not enabled, the trajectory proceeds for a fixed maximum duration.
 - **2. Minimum Trajectory Length (`m`):** Even when intelligent switching is enabled, decisions to switch are deferred until the current trajectory has reached a minimum length `m` timesteps. This ensures sufficient experience is gathered before a switch.
 - **3. Conservative Factor (`β`):** The probability of switching is defined as `P(switch) = F_π(s, g) × (1 − β^t)`, where `t` is the current trajectory length and `β ∈` is a conservative factor. This formulation ensures that `P(switch)` increases with trajectory length `t` (as `β^t` decreases), making early switching less likely and allowing the agent to gather more experience before committing to a switch.

#### 3.3. Algorithm Summary

RISC (Algorithms 1 & 2 in the paper) functions by continuously interacting with the environment, generating experience, and updating its policies. It alternates between forward and reset goals. At the start of each trajectory, it probabilistically determines if intelligent switching is active (using `ζ`). If active, it then checks for switching only after a minimum trajectory length `m` is reached, or if the current goal is achieved, or if a maximum trajectory length `M` is reached. The switching decision, when active, is made based on the success critic's output `F_π(s, g)` and the current trajectory length `t`, modulated by the conservative factor `β`. All value and success critics are trained using the `timeout-nonterminal` bootstrapping strategy. The base RL algorithm used for experiments is SAC for robot tasks and DQN for gridworld.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents empirical evaluations of RISC across several environments, comparing it against established reset-free RL baselines and ablations of its own components.

#### 4.1. Curricula in Reset-Free RL (4-Rooms Gridworld)

- An initial experiment in a 4-rooms gridworld environment compared RISC to a simple Reverse Curriculum (RC) agent.
- **Experience Generation:** RISC demonstrated a tendency to generate experience in state-space regions where its Q-values were lower and its estimates were less accurate (higher Optimal Value Percent Difference - OVPD). This indicates that RISC actively seeks out and learns from areas it has not yet mastered.
- **Comparison to Reverse Curriculum:** The RC agent, in contrast, was observed to spend a significant portion of its interaction time in regions where it had already achieved high competence, leading to a rapid drop in OVPD even before convergence. This suggests that reverse curriculums, which focus on expanding outwards from goal states, can be less sample-efficient by over-exploring familiar areas.

#### 4.2. Evaluating RISC on the EARL Benchmark

- RISC was evaluated on four challenging environments from the EARL benchmark: Tabletop Manipulation, Sawyer Door, Sawyer Peg (sparse reward, with demonstrations for seeding replay buffer), and Minitaur (dense reward, no demonstrations).
- **Overall Performance:** RISC demonstrated superior or comparable performance compared to other reset-free RL baselines (FBRL, R3L, VapRL, MEDAL) in terms of aggregate normalized scores (both interquartile mean and mean) across the benchmark environments.
- **Learning Speed:** RISC exhibited faster learning rates, achieving higher returns earlier in training compared to other reset-free methods. The area under the curve (AUC) analysis of learning trajectories showed RISC to be significantly more sample-efficient.
- **Task-Specific Results:**
 - On Tabletop Manipulation, Sawyer Door, and Sawyer Peg, RISC matched or exceeded the performance of the best existing reset-free methods, including those that leverage demonstrations (VapRL, MEDAL).
 - Notably, on Sawyer Door and Sawyer Peg, RISC even achieved performance levels comparable to or surpassing the "Episodic RL" baseline, which represents an unrealistic oracle with frequent automatic resets.
 - On Minitaur, where demonstration-reliant methods (VapRL, MEDAL) could not be applied, RISC significantly outperformed other methods like FBRL and R3L.
- **Method Complexity and Requirements:** The paper notes that VapRL and MEDAL require demonstration data and, for VapRL, access to a reward function for arbitrary states, limiting their applicability. RISC uses demonstrations only to seed the replay buffer and does not require them for its core mechanisms, allowing it to function in a broader range of settings.

#### 4.3. Ablating the Components of RISC

- An ablation study was conducted to ascertain the individual contributions of RISC's two main components: `timeout-nonterminal` bootstrapping and intelligent early switching.
- **Impact of Bootstrapping:** Removing `timeout-nonterminal` bootstrapping (i.e., using a `timeout-terminal` strategy) resulted in a substantial decrease in the agent's performance. This finding supports the theoretical analysis that consistent learning targets, provided by `timeout-nonterminal` bootstrapping, are critical for reset-free RL.
- **Impact of Early Switching:** Removing the intelligent early switching mechanism also led to a measurable, albeit more modest, drop in performance. This indicates that the intelligent switching component contributes to overall efficiency and policy quality.
- **Combined Effect:** When both components were removed, the performance degraded to that of a basic FBRL agent, confirming that the observed gains in RISC are attributable to the synergistic effect of both principled bootstrapping and intelligent switching.
- **Modulating Mechanisms:** Further analysis in Appendix E showed that removing the modulations for the switching behavior (conservative factor, minimum trajectory length, switching proportion) significantly reduced average trajectory lengths and degraded performance across environments, emphasizing their role in stabilizing and optimizing the intelligent switching process.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of reinforcement learning by addressing fundamental limitations that hinder the deployment of RL agents in real-world scenarios, particularly those lacking frequent environmental resets. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

- **Advancement in Reset-Free Reinforcement Learning:** By rigorously analyzing and providing solutions for previously underexplored aspects—principled bootstrapping during controller switching and intelligent decision-making for these switches—the paper enhances the foundational understanding and practical capabilities of reset-free RL. This development is relevant for applications where manual resets are impractical or impossible, such as long-duration robotic tasks or autonomous agents operating in complex, continuous environments.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency:** The intelligent switching mechanism, which directs learning effort towards less mastered regions of the state-goal space, leads to demonstrably faster learning and higher sample efficiency. In real-world applications, where data collection is expensive and time-consuming, improved sample efficiency directly translates to reduced training costs and faster deployment cycles.
- **Robustness and Stability in Learning:** The analysis and adoption of `timeout-nonterminal` bootstrapping provide a mechanism for maintaining consistent learning targets for value functions even when episode boundaries are dynamically determined by agent-controlled switching. This contributes to more stable and robust policy learning in challenging non-episodic settings.
- **Challenging Conventional Curriculum Approaches:** The findings suggest that a dynamic, confidence-based switching strategy (RISC's "outside-in" approach) can be more effective than rigid "reverse curriculum" methods that might over-explore mastered regions. This insight could influence the design of future curriculum learning strategies in RL.
- **Broader Applicability:** RISC's core mechanism does not strictly require demonstrations, beyond potentially seeding a replay buffer. This broadens its applicability compared to some contemporary reset-free methods that are reliant on extensive expert data, making it suitable for environments where demonstrations are scarce or difficult to obtain.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The paper identifies areas for future exploration, such as designing new benchmark environments as current ones approach performance saturation, and addressing the method's limitations in environments with irreversible states or where safety is a paramount concern. The potential for integrating intelligent switching with demonstration-guided learning is also highlighted as a promising avenue. The theoretical analysis of bootstrapping provides a basis for more principled algorithm design in complex RL contexts.

In conclusion, RISC represents a step towards more practical and efficient real-world reinforcement learning by offering a comprehensive approach to managing the complexities of learning without frequent environmental resets.
