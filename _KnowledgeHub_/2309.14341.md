# Extreme Parkour with Legged Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed an end-to-end reinforcement learning framework that enables a low-cost legged robot to perform extreme parkour maneuvers, including jumps twice its height and length, by autonomously learning optimal heading and interpreting raw depth images. This approach achieved robust, diverse, and precise athletic feats on a Unitree A1 robot in real-world environments.

## Problem

- Classical robotic methods and existing learning approaches often struggle with dynamic, novel obstacles, and require precise environment models or task-specific controllers.
- Low-cost legged robots like the Unitree A1 present challenges with noisy, low-frequency sensor data and imprecise actuation, hindering complex and precise dynamic behaviors.
- Developing a unified framework to teach a robot a wide range of diverse, high-precision parkour skills without extensive hand-engineering or reliance on external guidance.

## Method

- An ==end-to-end reinforcement learning== policy is trained in simulation using ==model-free RL (PPO)==, mapping raw depth images and proprioception directly to motor commands for a Unitree A1 robot.
- A novel ==dual distillation== method replaces privileged information (==scandots== and oracle heading) with a learned vision backbone for exteroception and a self-generated heading predictor, using a ==Mixture of Teacher and Student (MTS)== approach to bridge the sim-to-real gap.
- A ==unified reward function==, combining ==waypoint-based velocity tracking==, feet clearance penalties, and an ==inner-product term== for stylized locomotion (e.g., handstand), encourages diverse and extreme parkour skills.

## Results

- The robot successfully performed high jumps over obstacles **0.5 meters** high (**twice its hip joint height**) and long jumps across **0.8 meter** gaps (**twice its body length**).
- The policy learned to seamlessly transition into and maintain a handstand pose, performing bipedal walking on its front legs with robustness across varied real-world terrains and even descending stairs.
- The proposed method achieved **20-80% higher success rates** on difficult real-world parkour tasks compared to baselines and significantly improved safety metrics in simulation by avoiding unsafe foot placements near edges.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - End-to-end learning from raw, noisy depth camera input is effective for achieving highly dynamic and precise real-world robotic behaviors, even on low-cost hardware.
> - The ability for a robot to autonomously infer its optimal heading is critical for robustly performing complex parkour maneuvers in unstructured and diverse environments.
> - A simple, unified reward function can encourage the emergence of a wide variety of advanced, high-precision athletic skills, reducing the need for task-specific reward engineering.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research represents a significant step forward in the fields of robotics, reinforcement learning, and embodied AI, building upon and extending several key areas:

- **Legged Locomotion:** The ability of legged robots to traverse complex terrain has seen rapid progress, largely driven by advances in reinforcement learning. Early methods relied heavily on model-based control and detailed elevation maps derived from sophisticated sensors. However, these classical approaches often struggled with generalization to diverse or dynamic environments. More recently, learned controllers have shown remarkable adaptability to varying dynamics (e.g., different surfaces) and perceptive locomotion strategies that either leverage elevation maps or, more innovatively, operate directly from raw sensor data (e.g., depth images). This paper strongly aligns with the latter trend, pushing the boundary of "perceptive locomotion" by foregoing explicit terrain mapping in favor of end-to-end learning from a single depth camera.

- **Robotic Parkour and Dexterous Locomotion:** While basic walking and running are increasingly achievable for robots, performing "parkour"—dynamic, athletic maneuvers involving precise coordination and high-force interactions—remains a grand challenge. Most previous work on robotic parkour either involved highly engineered, pre-programmed solutions for specific obstacles or simpler tasks. The few existing learning-based approaches often relied on high-fidelity sensors or simplified terrain representations. This paper distinguishes itself by tackling "extreme parkour" tasks (jumps twice the robot's height/length, handstands) on a low-cost robot with inherently imprecise sensors and actuators, directly addressing the gap between laboratory-controlled demonstrations and real-world athletic prowess. It showcases a shift from merely navigating obstacles to actively exploiting and manipulating the environment through highly dynamic movements.

- **Sim-to-Real Transfer and Robustness:** A persistent challenge in applying RL to real robots is the "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in simulation fail to perform well in the real world due to discrepancies in dynamics, sensing, and actuation. The authors leverage techniques like Regularized Online Adaptation (ROA) and innovative distillation methods (dual distillation) to bridge this gap. Their focus on overcoming the imprecisions of a low-cost robot hardware further underscores the paper's contribution to building robust, real-world deployable robotic systems.

- **End-to-End Learning from Vision:** The trend towards end-to-end learning, where raw sensor data (like camera images) directly maps to motor commands, has gained significant traction. This approach minimizes the need for hand-engineered perception pipelines and allows the learning algorithm to discover optimal feature representations. This paper provides compelling evidence for the efficacy of this paradigm in highly dynamic and precise tasks, contrasting it with modular approaches that build explicit elevation maps, especially when dealing with noisy sensor data.

- **Comparison to Concurrent Work:** The authors explicitly position their work against two concurrent papers (Hoeller et al. 2023 and Zhuang et al. 2023), highlighting their advantages. While these concurrent works also demonstrate agile behaviors, they either rely on elevation maps, train task-specific policies, or use simplified obstacle abstractions. In contrast, this paper proposes a conceptually simpler framework that achieves more extreme parkour behaviors by: (i) using "scandots" as generalizable privileged information instead of abstract obstacle parameters, (ii) allowing the policy to infer its own heading, crucial for tasks like tilted ramp jumps, and (iii) employing a unified, general-purpose reward principle. This direct comparison showcases the unique strengths and superior performance of their proposed method in terms of both skill complexity and generalization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a low-cost, legged robot with imprecise sensing and actuation to perform a wide range of highly dynamic and precise parkour maneuvers directly from raw visual input. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

- **Mimicking Human Learning and Adaptability:** Humans learn complex athletic skills like parkour through extensive practice, adapting their eye-muscle coordination and movements to inherent sensory imprecision and motor variability. The authors hypothesize that robots can similarly learn to master such tasks using a data-driven approach, rather than relying on perfectly engineered hardware and pre-programmed solutions. This contrasts with classical robotics, which often demands systems engineered to very low tolerances.

- **Overcoming Limitations of Classical and Existing Learning Approaches:**
 - **Classical Methods:** Traditional approaches typically decouple perception, planning, and control, requiring precise measurement of the environment and pre-optimized trajectories. This limits them to tightly controlled, predetermined obstacle courses and renders them ineffective in novel or dynamic settings where obstacles might change or move.
 - **Low-Cost Hardware Challenges:** While recent learned locomotion methods have achieved impressive results, they often operate on high-fidelity robots or in less extreme scenarios. Low-cost robots like the Unitree A1 possess inherent challenges such as noisy, low-frequency, and jittery depth camera output, coupled with laggy and imprecise motor actuation. These imperfections make it extremely difficult to build accurate terrain maps or execute precise control actions using conventional methods.

- **Enabling Autonomous and Adaptive Navigation:** For robots to operate effectively in complex real-world environments, they need the ability to perceive and react to diverse obstacles on the fly, without human intervention or prior knowledge of the terrain. This includes automatically adjusting their heading direction, a critical capability for dynamic maneuvers that cannot be optimally pre-specified by a human.

- **Developing a Unified Framework for Diverse Skills:** Parkour involves a variety of distinct behaviors (e.g., high jumps, long jumps, handstands, ramp traversal). A significant challenge is to train a single, end-to-end neural network policy that can learn and execute these diverse, high-precision behaviors, rather than relying on a collection of task-specific controllers.

- **Pushing the Performance Envelope:** The paper aims to demonstrate capabilities that significantly surpass existing benchmarks for legged robots, particularly on low-cost hardware, achieving feats such as jumping obstacles twice the robot's height and length.

In essence, the motivation is to demonstrate that robust, extreme athletic performance in legged robots can be achieved not through perfect hardware and precise models, but through an adaptive, learning-based approach that mimics biological learning, enabling robots to "practice" and master complex skills in diverse, challenging environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs an end-to-end data-driven reinforcement learning (RL) framework to train a single neural network policy that directly translates raw depth images and proprioceptive feedback into joint angle commands. The approach is primarily simulation-based, followed by real-world deployment, and incorporates several novel elements:

#### 3.1. Core Training Philosophy: End-to-End RL with Sim-to-Real Transfer

- **Policy Architecture:** A single neural network serves as the policy, taking depth images from a single front-facing camera and proprioceptive states (e.g., joint angles, velocities, robot orientation) as input, and outputting motor commands.
- **Simulation-based Learning:** Policies are trained in a high-fidelity physics simulator using model-free RL (specifically, Proximal Policy Optimization or PPO). This allows for extensive parallel data collection and exploration that would be unsafe or impractical in the real world.
- **Hardware:** The target hardware is a **Unitree A1 quadruped robot**, known for its low cost and agility, equipped with an **Intel RealSense D435 depth camera** and an **NVIDIA Jetson NX** for onboard processing.

#### 3.2. Dual Distillation for Self-Generated Heading and Exteroception (Two-Phase Training)

To bridge the sim-to-real gap and enable the robot to infer necessary information (terrain and optimal heading) autonomously, a novel two-phase distillation method is proposed:

- **Phase 1: Reinforcement Learning from Scandots (Teacher Policy)**
 - **Privileged Information:** An "oracle" or "teacher" policy is trained in simulation with access to "privileged information" not available on the real robot, including:
 - **Scandots:** A highly detailed, noise-free representation of the local terrain geometry.
 - **Oracle Heading:** Precise target heading directions derived from dynamically placed waypoints, which optimally guide the robot through complex obstacles.
 - **Environment Parameters:** Information about the physical properties of the terrain (e.g., friction).
 - **Regularized Online Adaptation (ROA):** This technique is used to train an adaptation module within the teacher policy to estimate environment properties from a history of observations, enhancing robustness to varying dynamics.
 - **Automatic Terrain Curriculum:** Robots are trained on a curriculum of progressively difficult terrains (tilted ramps, gaps, hurdles, high steps). Successful traversal promotes the robot to harder levels, while failures demote it, aiding efficient exploration.

- **Phase 2: Distilling Direction and Exteroception (Student Policy)**
 - **Vision Backbone for Exteroception:** The scandot input to the teacher policy is replaced with a convolutional neural network (ConvNet) combined with a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) that processes raw depth images from the simulated front-facing camera. This network is trained using DAgger (Dataset Aggregation), where the student's actions are used to step the environment, and the teacher provides expert actions for supervision, minimizing distribution shift.
 - **Self-Generated Heading:** Crucially, the oracle heading input from Phase 1 is replaced by a learned predictor. A network is trained to predict the optimal yaw angle (heading) directly from the depth image, eliminating the need for human-specified waypoints at deployment. To prevent catastrophic distribution shift during this training, a **Mixture of Teacher and Student (MTS)** approach is used: the student observes its own predicted yaw angle if it's close to the oracle, otherwise, it observes the oracle yaw angle. This guides the student's learning while allowing it to develop its own heading inference capabilities.
 - **Policy Initialization:** The actor network from Phase 1 is deep-copied to initialize the student policy, minimizing drift when the student directly controls the environment.

#### 3.3. Unified Reward for Extreme Parkour

A simple yet effective reward function is designed to encourage diverse parkour behaviors without task-specific engineering:

- **Waypoint-based Velocity Tracking (`r_tracking`):** Instead of tracking velocity in the robot's base frame (which might lead to suboptimal movements like turning around obstacles), the reward tracks the robot's world-frame velocity towards dynamically generated waypoints (`ˆd_w`). This encourages the robot to always move towards the objective while allowing it freedom to choose its path.
- **Feet Clearance Penalty (`r_clearance`):** To promote stable and safe foot placement, a penalty is applied if a foot lands within 5cm of a terrain edge. This mitigates risky behaviors seen in preliminary experiments where the robot would step precariously close to edges to minimize energy.
- **Stylized Locomotion Reward (`r_stylized`):** An inner-product based reward term is introduced to encourage diverse, aesthetically pleasing gaits (e.g., handstand). By controlling a unit vector `ˆc` (e.g., `[0,0,-1]^T` for handstand), the operator can activate specific stylized movements at test time. This reward allows the single policy to learn multiple distinct locomotion styles.
- **Regularization:** Additional regularization terms from prior work (e.g.,) are included to ensure stable and robust learning.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- The Unitree A1 has 12 joints; hip height is 26cm, body length is 40cm.
- The RealSense D435 camera captures at 10 ± 2Hz.
- Depth backbone runs at 10Hz, base policy at 50Hz, synchronized via UDP on the Jetson NX.
- Constant depth and proprioception latencies are enforced (0.08s and 0.016s respectively) to manage noise and jitter.
- Training time for the deployable policy is under 20 hours on a single 3090 GPU.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates groundbreaking capabilities for a low-cost legged robot performing extreme parkour, supported by both qualitative observations of emergent behaviors and quantitative comparisons against baselines in simulation and the real world.

#### 4.1. Emergent Extreme Parkour Abilities

The unified reward function and end-to-end learning paradigm enabled the robot to learn highly optimized, complex behaviors:

- **High Jump:** The robot successfully jumps over obstacles 0.5m high, which is **twice its hip joint height**. This involves a sophisticated sequence: reducing stride length, precise alignment of front and rear feet, powerful thrust from hind legs, extending front feet to clear the obstacle, using front feet to pull up, tucking rear legs, and transitioning to a stable pose.
- **Long Jump:** The robot jumps across gaps 0.8m wide, **twice its body length (separation between front and rear feet)**. This requires similar coordination: aligning with the edge, moving rear feet close to maximize jump distance, powerful hind leg kick-back, extending front legs to reach, maintaining mid-air posture by extending hind legs, and a coordinated landing.
- **Handstand and Bipedal Walking:** The robot seamlessly transitions from quadrupedal walking to walking on its two front legs (handstand). This intrinsically unstable bipedal locomotion requires constant active adjustments for balance. The policy even shows robustness to soft, deformable grass and is capable of descending stairs in a handstand pose without vision, relying solely on proprioception and learned balance.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Comparison to Baselines

The authors performed comprehensive comparisons to validate their reward design, overall pipeline, and distillation setup.

- **Simulation Results (Reward Design and Pipeline - Table 2):**
 - **Our Method** consistently achieved higher Mean X-Displacement (MXD, indicating progress) and lower Mean Edge Violation (MEV, indicating safer foot placement) across all terrain types (Hurdle, Step, Gap, Ramps).
 - **NoInner (no inner-product reward):** This baseline, tracking velocity in the base frame, struggled significantly, especially on "Step" terrain where it couldn't bypass obstacles and often resorted to colliding and retrying. On "Hurdle," it would walk around the obstacle rather than over it, failing the intended task.
 - **NoClear (no feet clearance penalty):** While achieving decent MXD, this baseline exhibited higher MEV, indicating unsafe foot placement near edges, which proved problematic in the real world.
 - **Noisy (elevation map-based with noise):** This baseline showed lower MXD and high variance, struggling with noise in the simulated elevation maps and sometimes relying on physical collisions for terrain sensing.
- **Simulation Results (Distillation Setup - Table 3):**
 - **Our Method** achieved MXD very close to the "Oracle" baseline (which receives ground truth yaw angles), demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed dual distillation and MTS for heading prediction.
 - **Both (always predicted yaw) and Mask (masked yaw):** These baselines performed poorly with significantly lower MXD, failing to converge due to large distribution drift caused by noisy yaw angle predictions, highlighting the importance of the MTS approach.
- **Real-World Results (Figure 7):**
 - **Our Method** exhibited **20-80% higher success rates** on the most difficult instances of each terrain compared to baselines.
 - **NoDir (human-joystick-controlled direction):** This baseline failed frequently on dynamic tasks like jumps and tilted ramps. Human operators struggled to provide precise, rapid yaw adjustments needed to keep the robot perpendicular to obstacles, causing "out-of-distribution" commands that the policy couldn't handle.
 - **NoClear (no feet edge penalty):** This baseline often failed by placing feet too close to gap or cliff edges, leading to falls, confirming the simulation's MEV findings.
 - The handstand policy, trained without exteroception, demonstrated strong robustness on various real-world terrains and could even descend stairs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work represents a significant leap forward in robotic agility and autonomy, with profound implications across several domains:

- **Elevating Legged Robot Capabilities:** The demonstration of "extreme parkour" on a low-cost, off-the-shelf robot pushes the boundaries of what is considered achievable for legged systems. Performing high jumps (2x robot height), long jumps (2x robot length), and complex handstands showcases unprecedented levels of agility, precision, and dynamic control, bringing robot performance closer to biological capabilities.
- **Validation of End-to-End Learning:** The success of a single neural network policy operating directly from raw, noisy depth images (rather than relying on meticulously engineered perception pipelines or explicit terrain maps) provides strong evidence for the power and scalability of end-to-end, learning-based approaches in highly dynamic and precise real-world robotic tasks. This paradigm can lead to more robust, adaptable, and less brittle robotic systems.
- **Democratizing High-Performance Robotics:** By achieving such advanced capabilities on a low-cost platform like the Unitree A1, the research challenges the notion that extreme robotic performance requires prohibitively expensive, custom-built hardware. This has the potential to make cutting-edge robotics research more accessible to a wider community and accelerate innovation.
- **Novel Methodological Contributions:** The dual distillation method for self-generated heading estimation, combined with the unified inner-product reward design, offers generalizable tools for teaching robots complex, diverse, and context-dependent skills. The ability for the robot to autonomously infer its optimal heading is critical for truly adaptive behaviors in unstructured environments.
- **Robustness in Real-World Deployment:** The explicit focus on overcoming imprecise sensing and actuation on real hardware (Unitree A1, RealSense D435) demonstrates practical robustness. The policies trained with this framework are not just effective in simulation but translate reliably to the complexities and uncertainties of the physical world.
- **Foundational Step Towards General-Purpose Mobile Robots:** Enabling robots to perform complex dynamic maneuvers across varied terrains autonomously is a crucial step towards creating truly general-purpose mobile robots that can operate in human environments, assist in search and rescue, logistics, or exploration in challenging terrains.
- **Future Research Directions:** The authors themselves highlight the potential to extend this approach to mobile manipulators, combining agile locomotion with dexterous manipulation. This opens up exciting avenues for robots that can not only traverse complex environments but also interact with objects within them, potentially leading to advanced applications in fields like construction, disaster response, and assistance for the elderly.

In summary, this paper not only achieves a remarkable technical feat in robot agility but also reinforces the efficacy of an adaptive, learning-centric paradigm, paving the way for more capable, autonomous, and accessible robotic systems in the future.
