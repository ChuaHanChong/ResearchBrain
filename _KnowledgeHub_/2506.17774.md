# PhysiX: A Foundation Model for Physics Simulations

> [!summary] Summary
> PhysiX is a 4.5B-parameter autoregressive foundation model for physics simulation. A universal discrete tokenizer maps heterogeneous simulation fields into a shared token space, a transformer initialized from a natural-video model predicts future tokens by next-token prediction, and a convolutional refinement module removes quantization error. One checkpoint trained across eight tasks of The Well benchmark beats task-specific baselines, most strongly on long-horizon rollouts.

## Problem

- Physics simulation datasets hold only tens of thousands of samples, so large models overfit and the field falls back on small task-specific surrogates trained from scratch.
- Existing neural surrogates such as FNO and U-Net compound errors over autoregressive rollouts and lose fine structure, which limits stable long-horizon prediction.
- Physics datasets differ widely in resolution, channel sets, and governing equations, which blocks unified multitask training and leaves each physical system modeled in isolation.

## Method

- **Universal tokenizer**: a temporally causal ==Cosmos==-derived encoder with ==FSQ quantization==, a shared encoder taking the union of channels with learnable padding for missing ones, and per-dataset decoders.
- **Autoregressive transformer**: a 4.5B ==decoder-only== model initialized from the Cosmos video checkpoint, trained with ==next-token prediction== and ==3D RoPE== truncated to handle mixed spatial resolutions without resizing.
- **Refinement module**: a per-dataset ==ConvNeXt-U-Net== trained on the AR model's decoded pixel-space rollouts to remove quantization artifacts and sharpen the coarse predictions against ground truth.

## Results

- Next-frame prediction: a single checkpoint beats specialized baselines on **5 of 8** datasets with average rank **1.62** versus 2.38, cutting VRMSE by **91%** on shear_flow and **78%** on rayleigh_benard.
- Long-horizon prediction: state of the art on **18 of 21** evaluation points, with over **97%** lower VRMSE on shear_flow and over **90%** lower on rayleigh_benard across all rollout windows.
- Ablations: refinement brings 8-frame VRMSE to **0.07**, below the best baseline's 1-frame **0.14**; video-pretrained initialization and multitask training both beat scratch and single-task variants.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Spatiotemporal priors learned from natural video transfer to physical fields, so video pretraining is one practical way around data scarcity in scientific domains.
> - Discretizing continuous physical fields lets you reuse the LLM scaling toolkit, but a continuous pixel-space correction stage is needed to recover the precision lost to quantization.
> - Joint training on diverse physical systems outperforms per-task training, so related dynamics share structure a single model can exploit, even with heterogeneous input channels.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Traditional physics simulations, which are foundational to scientific and engineering disciplines, predominantly rely on numerical solvers for partial differential equations (PDEs). These methods, while highly accurate, are computationally intensive and often necessitate specialized hardware and expert-tuned software. The high computational cost has motivated the development of machine learning (ML)-based surrogates, which aim to approximate simulation outputs more efficiently.

Early ML-based approaches include Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), which integrate knowledge of governing equations into the loss function, and data-driven surrogate models. The latter category has seen evolution from Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) like U-Nets to neural operator frameworks, such as Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs) and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs). These neural operators aim to learn mappings between infinite-dimensional function spaces, offering generalization capabilities to varying initial conditions, boundary conditions, and spatial resolutions.

Despite these advancements, existing ML-based physics surrogates largely remain task-specific. They are often designed for particular physical systems, trained from scratch on individual datasets, and struggle to adapt to changes in simulation parameters or generalize across distinct physical domains without significant retraining or architectural modifications. Furthermore, many current neural network approaches demonstrate limitations in achieving stable and accurate long-range predictions.

In parallel, the field of artificial intelligence has witnessed the emergence and success of "foundation models" in domains such as natural language processing, image generation, and video generation. These models, characterized by their large scale, extensive pretraining on diverse datasets, and subsequent fine-tuning for specific tasks, have shown an ability to acquire generalizable knowledge and often surpass task-specific approaches. Notable examples include large language models, vision-language models like CLIP, and video generation models such as Emu3 and Cosmos.

The current work introduces PhysiX, an initiative to extend the foundation model paradigm to physics simulations. This endeavor aims to address the limitations of task-specific physics surrogates by developing a unified model capable of learning shared inductive biases across diverse physical systems. This presents unique challenges, including the scarcity of high-quality physics simulation data compared to internet-scale text or video corpora, and the inherent heterogeneity of physics datasets which vary significantly in resolution, dimensionality, underlying equations, and physical semantics. PhysiX seeks to bridge this gap by developing a large-scale model that can leverage knowledge from broader domains like natural video, generalize across diverse physics tasks, and perform robustly in long-horizon predictions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The development of PhysiX is motivated by several identified limitations and opportunities within the field of physics simulation and machine learning.

**Motivation for PhysiX:**

1. **Computational Burden of Traditional Simulations:** Numerical solvers for Partial Differential Equations (PDEs) are highly accurate but computationally expensive, requiring substantial resources and specialized expertise. This limits their scalability and real-time applicability in many scientific and engineering contexts.
2. **Limitations of Existing ML-based Surrogates:** Current machine learning approaches for physics simulation are predominantly task-specific. They are typically optimized for a single physical system or a narrow range of parameters, often requiring extensive retraining or architectural modifications when simulation conditions, geometries, or physical constants change. These models generally exhibit limited generalization capabilities across distinct physical domains and frequently struggle with long-range predictions due to compounding errors.
3. **Data Scarcity in Physics:** Unlike domains like language or natural images, where vast datasets are readily available, physics simulation datasets are comparatively limited in volume, often containing only tens of thousands of samples. This scarcity acts as a significant bottleneck, hindering the training of large, high-capacity models that could potentially learn more generalizable representations, as overfitting becomes a major concern.
4. **Heterogeneity of Physics Data:** Physics datasets present unique challenges due to their inherent diversity. They can vary drastically in spatial resolution, temporal granularity, channel dimensionality (e.g., different physical properties like pressure, velocity, temperature), and the underlying governing equations. This heterogeneity complicates the development of unified models and multitask training strategies.
5. **Success of Foundation Models in Other Domains:** The observed success of foundation models in vision, language, and video domains, where large models pretrained on diverse data exhibit robust generalization and transfer learning capabilities, suggests a similar potential for physics simulations. This motivates the investigation into whether a comparable paradigm can be applied to complex physical systems.

**Key Objectives of PhysiX:**

1. **Develop a Large-Scale Foundation Model for Physics Simulation:** Introduce PhysiX as the first large-scale autoregressive foundation model (4.5 billion parameters) specifically designed for general-purpose physics simulations across a diverse range of systems.
2. **Address Data Scarcity and Heterogeneity:** Create a framework that can effectively utilize limited and heterogeneous physics data by employing a universal discrete tokenizer, enabling data fusion and a unified token space for training.
3. **Improve Long-Range Prediction Fidelity:** Enhance the stability and accuracy of multi-step, long-horizon predictions, which is a known challenge for many existing ML-based physics surrogates.
4. **Leverage Knowledge Transfer from Natural Videos:** Investigate and demonstrate the successful transfer of spatiotemporal priors learned from large-scale natural video datasets (through pretraining) to improve performance in physics simulation tasks.
5. **Enable Synergistic Learning through Joint Training:** Show that joint training of a single model across multiple diverse simulation tasks allows for the capture of shared inductive biases and leads to improved performance on individual tasks.
6. **Outperform Task-Specific Baselines:** Demonstrate that PhysiX, as a universally trained model, can significantly outperform traditional task-specific baselines and previous state-of-the-art approaches on established benchmarks like The Well.
7. **Contribute Towards General-Purpose Scientific Computing Tools:** Advance the field by providing evidence for the viability of foundation models as scalable and efficient tools for modeling complex physical phenomena, potentially accelerating scientific discovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PhysiX integrates a multi-component architecture designed to process and simulate diverse physical systems. It consists of a universal discrete tokenizer, a large-scale autoregressive generative model, and a refinement module.

**Overall Design:**
The process begins by converting `k` historical input frames ($x_1, \dots, x_k$) into sequences of discrete tokens by the tokenizer. These tokens are concatenated into a single 1D sequence, which serves as input to the autoregressive model. The autoregressive model then predicts future tokens corresponding to the next `T` frames. These predicted tokens are subsequently decoded back into pixel space to yield coarse predictions ($\hat{x}_{k+1}, \dots, \hat{x}_{k+T}$). Finally, a refinement module processes these coarse predictions, leveraging the original pixel-space historical context, to produce sharpened and more detailed final outputs ($\hat{y}_{k+1}, \dots, \hat{y}_{k+T}$). This modular design aims to mitigate the information loss introduced by the initial discretization.

#### 3.1. Universal Tokenizer

PhysiX employs a discrete tokenizer architecture adapted from the Cosmos framework, designed to compress continuous spatiotemporal fields into compact discrete token sequences while preserving essential spatiotemporal structures.
- **Architecture:** It follows an encoder-decoder design. The encoder processes an input sequence of video frames using temporally causal convolution and attention layers to generate a compressed latent representation. This representation is then quantized into discrete token sequences using Finite-Scalar Quantization (FSQ). The decoder is responsible for reconstructing the original video frames from these tokens.
- **Temporal Causality:** A key feature is the tokenizer's temporal causality, where each stage of the encoder observes only past and present frames, making it suitable for subsequent autoregressive modeling.
- **Compression:** The tokenizer is configured for 8x spatial and 4x temporal compression. For an input sequence of `M` pixel frames (each $H \times W$), it produces $\hat{M} = \frac{M}{4}$ latent frames, each containing $L = (\frac{HW}{8^2})$ discrete tokens.
- **Cross-Task Generalization:** To enable generalization across diverse simulation datasets, which vary in channel dimensionality, spatial resolution, and physical semantics:
 - The first embedding layer of the encoder is modified to accept a fixed union of all possible channels observed across datasets. Missing channels for any specific data point are padded with learnable 2D tensors unique to each channel type. This allows flexible processing within a unified architecture.
 - While the encoder is shared across datasets to enforce a common embedding space, separate decoders are trained for each dataset to optimize reconstruction quality and accommodate dataset-specific output distributions.
- **Training:** The tokenizer is trained jointly on all available simulation datasets. To ensure balanced representation, samples from smaller datasets are replicated to match the size of the largest dataset. The tokenizer is initialized from a pre-trained Cosmos checkpoint, which was found to accelerate convergence and improve reconstruction performance by leveraging priors from natural video data.

#### 3.2. Autoregressive Generative Models

Following tokenizer training, a large-scale autoregressive model, a decoder-only transformer adapted from the 4.5 billion parameter Cosmos framework, is trained to predict physics in the discrete latent space.
- **Objective:** The model uses a next-token prediction objective, minimizing the negative log-likelihood of the correct token at each position, conditioned on all preceding tokens. Given tokens from `k` historical frames, it predicts tokens for the next `T` frames autoregressively.
- **Positional Embeddings:** It incorporates 3D Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) to capture spatiotemporal relationships within the token sequence.
- **Variable Resolution Handling:** To accommodate datasets with varying spatial resolutions, the positional encodings are dynamically adjusted. Instead of resizing inputs, the 3D RoPE frequencies are truncated along the height and width dimensions to match the current input size, allowing seamless handling of mixed-resolution data.
- **Initialization:** The autoregressive model is initialized from the 4.5 billion parameter Cosmos checkpoint, which provides strong spatiotemporal priors learned from extensive natural video datasets.
- **Training:** Similar to tokenizer training, smaller datasets are oversampled to ensure uniform contribution during training.

#### 3.3. Refinement Module

A convolutional neural network serves as a post-processing refinement module. Its primary role is to mitigate artifacts and quantization errors introduced by the discrete tokenization process, which can be significant in physics simulations requiring high precision.
- **Purpose:** The module refines the coarse predictions from the autoregressive model ($\hat{x}_j$) to produce a sharper, more detailed final output ($\hat{y}_j$), addressing issues like blocky or pixelated outputs.
- **Architecture:** It adopts the architecture of the ConvNeXt-U-Net baseline from The Well benchmark.
- **Training:** The refinement module is trained after the autoregressive model. Training data for the refinement module is generated by autoregressively predicting frames from the training split using the trained AR model. The ground truth frames serve as the refinement target. Importantly, the AR model's outputs are decoded to pixel space before being fed into the refinement model, allowing it to learn improvements in the pixel domain. Separate refinement modules are trained for each dataset, akin to the tokenizer decoders.

#### 3.4. Experimental Settings

PhysiX was trained and evaluated on eight diverse simulation tasks from The Well benchmark. Performance was quantified using Variance-Weighted Root Mean Squared Error (VRMSE) averaged across physical channels. Comparisons were made against four baselines (FNO, TFNO, U-Net, C-U-Net) in both next-frame and long-horizon rollout settings. Ablation studies were conducted to assess the impact of universal vs. single-task training, the refinement module, and pretraining vs. scratch initialization. The model's adaptability to unseen simulations was also evaluated through finetuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on PhysiX demonstrate its performance across various physics simulation tasks and evaluation settings, particularly when compared to existing baselines.

#### 4.1. Next-frame Prediction

On the next-frame prediction benchmark (Table 1), PhysiX exhibited competitive performance, outperforming the baselines on 5 out of 8 datasets. It achieved the lowest average rank (1.62) across all 8 tasks, compared to 2.38 for the best-performing baseline. This performance was achieved using a single model checkpoint trained across all tasks, in contrast to baselines which utilized separate models specialized for each dataset. Significant performance gains were observed on the `shear_flow` and `rayleigh_benard` datasets, with VRMSE reductions of 91% and 78% respectively, relative to the best baseline.

#### 4.2. Long-horizon Prediction

PhysiX demonstrated its primary strength in long-horizon simulation tasks (Table 2 and Figure 3). It achieved state-of-the-art performance on 18 out of 21 evaluation points across different forecasting windows (2-8, 9-26, 27-56 frames). The improvements were consistent and substantial across tasks. For instance, on `shear_flow`, PhysiX reduced VRMSE by over 97% at the 6-12 horizon. For `rayleigh_benard`, the model achieved more than 90% lower error across all rollout windows. On `active_matter`, PhysiX consistently outperformed baselines at every forecast horizon. Qualitatively, Figure 3 illustrates that while baseline models degrade rapidly with increasing lead time due to compounding errors, PhysiX maintains low VRMSE and greater stability over extended rollouts, which is attributed to its autoregressive nature learning from full simulation sequences.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **General Model vs. Task-Specific Models (Table 3):** A comparison between the multi-task (universal) PhysiX model and single-task versions (trained on individual datasets with the same architecture and hyperparameters) indicated that the universal model generally outperforms task-specific models. It achieved lower VRMSE on the majority of datasets across different lead times, suggesting that joint multi-task training facilitates synergistic learning by enabling the model to identify common patterns and mechanisms across diverse physical processes.
- **Effectiveness of Refinement Module (Figure 4):** The integration of the refinement module was shown to consistently reduce both Mean Squared Error (MSE) and VRMSE metrics. On the `gray_scott_reaction_diffusion` dataset, the refinement module improved prediction errors for both multi-task and single-task AR models across all prediction windows. Notably, with refinement, the 8-frame prediction error of the multi-task model (0.07 VRMSE) was lower than the 1-frame prediction error of the best-performing baseline on The Well benchmark (0.14 VRMSE). This highlights the module's success in mitigating quantization errors and improving output precision.
- **Pretrained vs. Scratch Initialization (Appendix G.1, Figure 6, Table 6):** Initializing PhysiX with weights from a Cosmos model pre-trained on natural videos significantly outperformed training the model from scratch with random initial weights across nearly all tasks and evaluation settings. This finding indicates the effective transfer of spatiotemporal prior knowledge from natural video data to the domain of physical simulations.
- **Scaling Results (Appendix G.2, Table 7):** An investigation into model scaling, using randomly initialized models of 700M, 2B, and 4B parameters, showed that the 4B model (in this scratch-trained comparison) generally performed best. The 2B model performed worst, with observations suggesting that both 4B and 2B models exhibited overfitting, and the 2B model converged to a suboptimal point compared to the 700M and 4B models.

#### 4.4. Adaptation to Unseen Simulations (Table 4)

PhysiX's adaptability was evaluated on two novel simulation tasks (`euler_multi_quadrants` and `acoustic_scattering`), which were not part of its pretraining datasets and involved new input channels and physical dynamics. After fine-tuning the tokenizer for each task, two variants of the autoregressive model were tested: `PhysiX_f` (finetuned from the pretrained model) and `PhysiX_s` (trained from scratch using the Cosmos checkpoint as initialization). `PhysiX_f` consistently achieved superior performance across nearly all tasks and prediction horizons, only being surpassed by a baseline for a single-step prediction in one task, with its advantage growing significantly for longer horizons. The consistent outperformance of `PhysiX_f` over `PhysiX_s` underscores its ability to effectively transfer and leverage learned knowledge when adapting to previously unseen simulation types.

#### 4.5. Qualitative Comparison (Figure 5, Appendix G.3)

Qualitative comparisons on `shear_flow` and `rayleigh_benard` tasks visually confirmed PhysiX's superior performance over baseline models. At extended rollout horizons (e.g., 24 steps for `shear_flow` and 15 steps for `rayleigh_benard`), PhysiX's predictions maintained visual consistency with the ground truth across various physical fields (tracer, pressure, buoyancy, velocity components). In contrast, baseline models exhibited noticeable distortions, blurring, and a loss of fine-grained structures, such as vortex patterns and convective plumes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of PhysiX represents a notable step in applying the foundation model paradigm to physics simulations, with several potential implications for scientific computing and discovery.

**General-Purpose Simulation:** PhysiX demonstrates the feasibility of developing a unified, general-purpose surrogate model for diverse physical systems. By successfully operating across a variety of domains without requiring task-specific architectural modifications, it moves beyond the limitations of highly specialized ML surrogates. This capability suggests a shift towards more versatile and adaptable simulation tools.

**Addressing Data Bottlenecks:** The methodology employed by PhysiX, including a universal tokenizer, large-scale autoregressive modeling, and strategic pretraining, offers a pathway to mitigate the data scarcity issue inherent in physics simulations. The ability to effectively learn from limited and heterogeneous datasets through shared representations and knowledge transfer is crucial for advancing ML in scientific domains.

**Enhanced Long-Horizon Prediction:** A critical challenge in ML-based physics surrogates has been their struggle with maintaining accuracy and stability over extended simulation rollouts. PhysiX's superior performance in long-horizon predictions, coupled with its demonstrated stability, addresses this limitation. This capability is vital for applications requiring predictions over prolonged temporal scales, such as climate modeling, material aging, or complex fluid dynamics.

**Effective Knowledge Transfer:** The successful transfer of spatiotemporal priors from pretraining on natural video data to physics simulations is a significant finding. It indicates that high-capacity models can learn generalizable patterns of motion and interaction from broad visual data, which can then be leveraged to improve performance in physics-specific contexts, even when the underlying visual semantics differ.

**Synergistic Learning:** The evidence for synergistic learning through joint training across diverse simulation tasks highlights the potential for foundation models to identify and exploit common physical principles and dynamics. This cross-task generalization can lead to more robust and accurate models that implicitly capture shared inductive biases, rather than relearning them for each new system.

**Acceleration of Scientific Discovery:** By providing generalizable, scalable, and efficient tools for modeling complex physical phenomena, PhysiX has the potential to accelerate research in various scientific and engineering disciplines. Faster, more accurate simulations can facilitate quicker hypothesis testing, design optimization, and deeper understanding of natural processes, thereby reducing the computational burden associated with traditional numerical methods.

**Future Research Directions:** While demonstrating significant advancements, the study also highlights areas for further exploration, such as improving zero-shot generalization to entirely novel physical processes, further reducing discretization errors introduced by tokenization, and extending the framework to handle 3D physical systems or datasets beyond The Well benchmark. These areas represent avenues for continued development towards even more robust and universal scientific foundation models.
