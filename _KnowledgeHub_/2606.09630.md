# ReCoVLA: VLM-Guided Reward Compilation for Failure Recovery in Vision-Language-Action Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> ReCoVLA is a framework that improves the robustness of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies by using a Vision-Language Model (VLM) to generate stage-gated rewards for training residual reinforcement learning policies. This approach enables robots to recover from manipulation failures in off-nominal states, increasing success rates in both simulation and physical robot experiments.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies are brittle and fail to recover robustly when encountering off-nominal states, such as misplacing objects or losing grasp.
- Traditional recovery methods, like fine-tuning VLAs, are costly due to data collection and risk catastrophic forgetting of nominal skills.
- Reinforcement learning for robot recovery is hampered by difficulties in designing precise, non-conflicting rewards, as generic task-level rewards are often too sparse.

## Method

- **ReCoVLA** employs a frozen base Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy and trains an additive ==residual== policy in its latent space to provide corrective actions.
- An external Vision-Language Model (VLM) analyzes visual rollouts to semantically identify specific failure categories and current recovery stages.
- A deterministic reward compiler leverages the VLM's semantic descriptor to compile stage-gated rewards, activating specific primitive reward components only when their preconditions are met.

## Results

- ReCoVLA (M4) increased the average success rate from **36.7%** to **66.7%** in simulation and achieved a **61.7%** average success rate in zero-shot physical robot experiments, surpassing baselines.
- The stage-gated reward compilation (M4) consistently outperformed simpler reward structures (M2, M3) across diverse manipulation tasks, demonstrating its critical role in effective recovery.
- The framework generalized to different VLA backbones, improving OpenVLA's average success from **23.3%** to **45.0%** in simulation, and exhibited robustness to visual and geometric out-of-distribution variations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling high-level semantic failure understanding using a VLM from low-level corrective control through a residual policy improves recovery robustness.
> - Utilizing VLMs to guide structured reward compilation for reinforcement learning is more stable and reliable than direct action generation.
> - Stage-gated reward activation, which ensures reward components are active only under specific recovery preconditions, is critical for achieving reliable and efficient recovery.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated within the field of robotic manipulation, specifically focusing on the robustness and recovery capabilities of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. Current VLA policies, such as those employing flow matching, have demonstrated proficiency in mapping language instructions and visual observations to robot actions across a range of manipulation tasks. Models like VIMA, PaLM-E, RT-1/2, RoboCat, RT-X, and OpenVLA have established foundations for scaling VLA capabilities through broad pretraining and efficient adaptation. These large-scale models possess considerable semantic knowledge and can perform nominal manipulation skills.

However, a practical limitation of imitation-trained VLA policies is their brittleness when encountering off-nominal states. These states, which can arise from action-induced errors like misplacing an object or losing a grasp, often differ from the training data distribution. In such scenarios, a policy effective for the nominal task may lack the specific corrective behaviors required for recovery.

Existing approaches to address this problem include fine-tuning VLA policies with additional recovery data. This method is often costly due to the expense of collecting failure-recovery demonstrations and can lead to catastrophic forgetting, where the fine-tuning process compromises previously acquired capabilities. Reinforcement learning (RL) offers an alternative but presents challenges related to reward design. Generic task-level rewards are frequently too sparse for effective learning, while activating all possible hand-designed reward components can introduce conflicting objectives or alter the intended optimization problem. Furthermore, applying standard policy-gradient RL to flow-matching VLA models is complicated by the lack of direct action likelihood availability, although recent work like π0.6 has begun to address some of these issues, it still necessitates training separate value functions and does not directly resolve the reward-design problem for diverse failure recovery.

This paper builds upon the concept of residual reinforcement learning (RRL), where a learned residual policy provides corrections to an existing controller. Prior research has shown that RRL can efficiently refine imperfect controllers. The proposed ReCoVLA framework extends these ideas by learning the residual policy over the latent space of a frozen VLA and, critically, by using an external vision-language model (VLM) to condition the recovery reward structure based on the specific failure mode. This differentiates it from other VLM-guided recovery approaches that might rely on demonstrations, direct language guidance, or executable actions generated by the VLM. ReCoVLA leverages the VLM for semantic failure identification and reward compilation rather than direct action generation, which positions it as a novel approach to combining VLM understanding with structured RL for robust robot recovery.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are outlined as follows:

- **Enhance Robustness of VLA Policies in Off-Nominal States:** The core objective is to improve the resilience of existing VLA policies when they encounter deviations from their training distribution, such as misplacements or dropped objects. While VLA policies are effective in nominal scenarios, their practical deployment is hindered by their inability to robustly recover from errors.
- **Address Limitations of Traditional Recovery Methods:**
 - **Avoid Catastrophic Forgetting and High Data Costs:** The motivation stems from the recognition that fine-tuning large VLA policies on failure recovery data is expensive in terms of data collection and risks degrading the policy's pre-existing nominal skills. The work aims to develop a recovery mechanism that bypasses these issues by keeping the base VLA frozen.
 - **Overcome Challenges in Reward Design for RL-based Recovery:** Existing RL approaches for failure recovery face difficulties with sparse or conflicting reward signals. A generic task-level reward is often insufficient, while an exhaustive set of hand-designed rewards can be problematic. The goal is to develop a method that provides precise, context-specific rewards without requiring extensive manual engineering.
- **Decouple High-Level Failure Understanding from Low-Level Corrective Control:** The research aims to create a modular system where a high-level component, specifically a VLM, is responsible for comprehending the failure mode and the current stage of recovery. This understanding then informs a separate, low-level corrective control mechanism (a residual policy) through a structured reward. This decoupling intends to make the recovery framework more adaptable and applicable to different VLA backbones.
- **Leverage VLMs for Semantic Reward Selection, Not Direct Action Generation:** A central motivation is to utilize the semantic understanding capabilities of VLMs in a stable and reliable manner for robotics. Instead of using VLMs to directly generate robot actions, which can be unstable or lack fine-grained control, the paper proposes using them to produce a structured "recovery descriptor." This descriptor guides a deterministic reward compiler to select and gate appropriate reward components, thereby providing a robust signal for residual reinforcement learning. This approach aims to capitalize on the VLM's ability to interpret complex visual and linguistic cues about failures while mitigating the risks associated with VLM-generated control.
- **Enable Training of Targeted Recovery Policies:** By compiling failure-specific and stage-gated rewards, the system is motivated to train a library of specialized residual policies. Each policy would be optimized for a particular failure category, allowing for precise corrective actions that are tightly coupled to the current error state, leading to more efficient and effective recovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ReCoVLA is conceptualized as a failure-conditioned residual recovery framework, structured into three principal stages: failure catalog construction, residual policy training, and zero-shot deployment.

**Base VLA and Residual Control:**
The framework operates by keeping a base Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy (e.g., fine-tuned π0.5 or OpenVLA) frozen during recovery training and deployment. This VLA policy, denoted as F_VLA, processes robot observations (o_t) and language instructions (ℓ) to generate a nominal action (a_b_t) and an internal latent feature (h_t). A separate residual policy, π_r_θ, learns to output an additive corrective action (a_r_t) based solely on this latent feature (h_t). When residual recovery is active (controlled by a binary variable β_t), the executed action (a_t) is a clipped sum of the nominal and residual actions: a_t = Π_A (a_b_t + β_t a_r_t). This composition strategy aims to preserve the base VLA's nominal behavior while allowing the residual policy to provide targeted corrections.

**Failure Catalog Construction:**
The process begins by generating simulator rollouts using the no-recovery base VLA policy (M1). For each failed rollout, an external Vision-Language Model (VLM), specifically Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct, acts as a failure analyzer (D_ϕ). It takes the full visual rollout (including observations, robot states, and base actions), the language instruction, and a recovery-analysis prompt as input. The VLM outputs a structured recovery descriptor (ξ_i), which comprises:
- A failure category (c_i).
- A recovery stage (z_i).
- A set of active entities (E_i), such as "broccoli" or "red_dish."
- A confidence score (ρ_i) for the failure state.
- A binary mask (m_i) over a predefined reward-component library.
High-confidence categories (where ρ_i ≥ τ_cat) are used to populate a simulator-trained failure catalog (C_train), providing specific active entities (E_c) and failure-relevant reward masks (m_c) for each recoverable failure.

**Reward Variants and Stage-Gated Compilation:**
A central component is the deterministic reward compiler, which generates rewards for residual RL training from a library of K object-state reward potentials R = {φ_k}_(k=1)^K. These potentials measure progress for primitive recovery concepts (e.g., reaching, grasping, placing, articulation) using potential-difference terms (Δφ_k). All reward terms are derived from simulator object states. The residual regularization term (Ω_t) penalizes the magnitude and change of residual actions.

The paper defines several reward variants for evaluation:
- **M2 (Task-level residual baseline):** Activates all task-relevant reward components and entities for the given instruction, using a task-level mask (m_ℓ) and entity set (E_ℓ).
- **M3 (VLM-detected failure-relevant components):** Uses the VLM-detected failure category to activate only failure-relevant components specified by m_c, with all selected terms active simultaneously.
- **M4 (Full ReCoVLA - Stage-gated reward compiler):** Employs the same failure mask (m_c) as M3 but introduces a binary stage-aware gate (g_c,k) for each selected component. These gates encode recovery-stage preconditions (e.g., a placing component is inactive until the object is grasped), preventing premature reward activation.
- **M6:** Applies the M4 stage-gated reward design to an OpenVLA base policy.

The reward compiler acts as an interpreter for the VLM descriptor. It verifies the descriptor, normalizes labels, resolves active entities to simulator object IDs, assigns semantic roles, and then binds these roles to the argument signatures of the selected reward components. Critically, it attaches fixed stage-gate templates based on the failure type and recovery stage, ensuring that reward terms are only active when their preconditions are met. This design prevents the VLM from generating unstable free-form rewards, instead channeling its semantic understanding into a structured, safe reward compilation process.

**RL Residual Policy Library Training:**
Residual policies are trained exclusively in simulation using PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization). For each recoverable failure category, training rollouts are initialized from simulator states representing the specific failure. The actor policy receives the frozen VLA latent feature (h_t), while the critic has access to simulator state for reward computation. Upon training, the learned residual policies are frozen and stored in a library, indexed by their corresponding VLM-detected failure category.

**Deployment:**
For real-world deployment, the trained mixture of residual policies is used zero-shot. The robot defaults to executing the frozen base VLA. In parallel, the VLM continuously monitors a sparse history of RGB observations. If the VLM detects a known failure category (c_t ∈ C_train) with confidence exceeding a deployment threshold (ρ_t ≥ τ_deploy), the corresponding trained residual policy is activated (β_t = 1). If the failure is unknown or confidence is low, the base VLA continues execution without residual intervention.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of ReCoVLA involved six evaluation variants (M1-M6) across three Fetch manipulation tasks: toolbox organization (contact-rich), vegetable sorting (long-horizon), and soda-can disposal (short-horizon). Experiments were conducted in both simulation and on a physical Fetch robot, with 20 trials per method per task. Performance was measured by binary success rate and a normalized goal-fulfillment quality score (Q-score).

**Overall Performance:**
- ReCoVLA (M4), utilizing the full stage-gated reward compiler with a π0.5 base, consistently achieved the highest performance across all tested conditions.
- In simulation, M4 improved the average success rate from 36.7% (M1, no-recovery π0.5 baseline) to 66.7%, representing a 30.0 percentage point increase. The average Q-score rose from 0.56 to 0.83.
- In physical zero-shot sim-to-real experiments, M4 achieved an average success rate of 61.7% and an average Q-score of 0.75, outperforming other baselines by an average of 18.3 percentage points in success and 0.21 in Q-score.

**Impact of Reward Structure (Ablation Studies):**
- **M2 (Task-level residual reward):** This variant, which activated all task-relevant reward components, showed some improvements but did not match M4. For example, in simulation, M2 achieved an average success rate of 40.0% and a Q-score of 0.55.
- **M3 (VLM-detected failure-relevant reward without stage gates):** M3, which activated VLM-selected failure-relevant reward components but lacked stage-aware gates, reached 48.3% average success in simulation, 18.4 percentage points lower than M4. This indicates that merely selecting relevant reward terms is insufficient, and the stage-aware gates are a critical component for performance gains. The Q-scores for M3 were also consistently lower than M4 across tasks.
- The comparison between M3 and M4 directly demonstrated that the stage-gated reward compilation, which activates reward terms only when their preconditions are met, significantly contributes to reliable recovery by reducing premature reward activation and potential reward hacking.

**Task-Specific Results:**
- M4 achieved the best results on all three tasks in simulation. For toolbox organization, success increased from 25% to 60%; for vegetable sorting, it improved from 30% to 65%. These gains were most pronounced in tasks requiring staged recovery behaviors (e.g., re-grasping, object placement, lid closing).
- In physical experiments, M4 again led across tasks, achieving 50% success for toolbox organization, 60% for vegetable sorting, and 75% for soda-can disposal. Even for the soda-can task, where M2 achieved 55% success, M4 still yielded higher success and Q-score.

**Generalizability to Different VLA Backbones:**
- The framework's design was also evaluated with OpenVLA as the base policy. M6 (OpenVLA with ReCoVLA's stage-gated recovery) improved OpenVLA's average success from 23.3% (M5, no-recovery OpenVLA) to 45.0% in simulation, supporting the claim that the recovery mechanism is applicable to different VLA backbones.

**Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Stress Test:**
- Under object substitutions that altered visual appearance or geometry but preserved task structure (e.g., pumpkin to tomato, cable to tape), the no-recovery base policy (M1) saw its average success rate drop to 10.0% and Q-score to 0.22.
- In contrast, M4 achieved 53.3% average success and 0.65 Q-score under the same OOD conditions, representing a 43.3 percentage-point success gain over M1. This indicates a degree of robustness to visual and geometric variations.

**Additional Comparisons (Appendix):**
- ReCoVLA outperformed other recent recovery and VLA+RL baselines, RLinf and RACER, on the simulation tasks. Averaged across three tasks, ReCoVLA achieved 0.67 success and 0.83 Q-score, compared to 0.12/0.19 for RLinf and 0.43/0.60 for RACER.
- On the Behavior-1K Challenge, using a different robot platform (Galaxea R1 Pro), ReCoVLA showed an advantage over OpenPI Comet, with average success/Q-score of 0.37/0.60 compared to 0.22/0.40.

**VLM Failure Detector Accuracy (Appendix):**
- The VLM failure detector demonstrated a macro accuracy of 85.0% across ten recoverable failure modes in physical rollouts, with most errors being local confusions within the same task. This indicates its reliability for semantic failure identification.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ReCoVLA framework addresses a critical limitation in the deployment of contemporary Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies: their brittleness in off-nominal states. This work offers several points of significance and potential impact:

- **Enhanced Robot Autonomy and Reliability:** By enabling robots to recover from a range of common manipulation failures, ReCoVLA contributes to more robust and reliable autonomous systems. This directly impacts the practical applicability of VLA policies in real-world scenarios where errors are inevitable. The ability to automatically correct mistakes reduces the need for human intervention, potentially leading to increased operational uptime and efficiency.

- **Preservation of Pre-trained VLA Capabilities:** A key aspect of ReCoVLA is its design choice to keep the base VLA policy frozen. This prevents catastrophic forgetting, a common issue when fine-tuning large models on new data, and ensures that the robot retains its nominal manipulation skills. The framework introduces a targeted corrective layer rather than modifying the fundamental competence of the base policy.

- **Structured and Efficient Reward Design for RL:** The method proposes a systematic solution to the challenging problem of reward engineering in reinforcement learning for recovery tasks. By using a VLM to infer failure modes and recovery stages, and then compiling structured, stage-gated rewards from a library of primitive components, ReCoVLA provides precise and context-aware feedback. This approach is more efficient and reliable than manually crafting task-specific rewards or relying on sparse, generic rewards, which often hinder learning progress.

- **Modular and Generalizable Architecture:** The decoupling of high-level semantic failure understanding (via the VLM) from low-level corrective control (via residual RL policies) creates a modular and potentially generalizable framework. The demonstrated effectiveness across different manipulation tasks (short-horizon, long-horizon, contact-rich) and different VLA backbones (π0.5 and OpenVLA) supports its broader applicability beyond specific robot platforms or task domains. The OOD stress tests further indicate a degree of robustness to visual and geometric variations.

- **Advancement in Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The methodology involves training residual policies entirely in simulation and deploying them zero-shot on a physical robot. This capability is significant for scalable robot learning, as it reduces the reliance on costly and time-consuming real-world data collection for training recovery behaviors.

- **New Paradigm for VLM Utilization in Robotics:** ReCoVLA posits a novel way to leverage the advanced reasoning capabilities of VLMs in robotics. Instead of directly generating uncertain actions or free-form text commands, the VLM is used as a "semantic reward selector" that provides structured information to guide a deterministic reward compiler. This approach capitalizes on the VLM's strengths while avoiding its potential weaknesses in precise control, opening new avenues for integrating large perception models into robust robotic control systems.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified limitations, such as the requirement for failure categories to be reproducible in simulation and the current lack of online recovery policy synthesis, suggest clear directions for future research. This includes automating failure catalog expansion, learning or generating reward gates dynamically, and exploring broader physical baselines, contributing to the ongoing development of more adaptive and intelligent robot systems.
