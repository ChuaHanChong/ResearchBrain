# HRO: Hierarchical Room-to-Object Framework for Zero-Shot Object Goal Navigation with Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A hierarchical "room-to-object" framework, HRO, enhances zero-shot object goal navigation by integrating Large Language Models for human-like spatial reasoning, leading to more efficient exploration and accurate semantic associations. The system achieved a 53.0% success rate on HM3D and 84.0% on Gibson datasets, surpassing prior LLM-based and trained navigation methods.

## Problem

- Existing LLM-based zero-shot navigation methods use "flat" reasoning, directly associating objects or regions without explicitly modeling hierarchical spatial relationships like room types.
- This flat reasoning leads to inefficient and "blind" exploration, as agents may visit unlikely areas, and can result in insufficient accuracy in semantic association.
- Current approaches often underutilize the extensive common-sense reasoning potential of LLMs regarding object co-occurrence within specific room types.

## Method

- A hierarchical "room-to-object" framework, **HRO**, is proposed, comprising high-level reasoning for ==room type inference==, mid-level decision for goal-oriented ==semantic affinity scoring==, and low-level ==path planning== and execution.
- Large Language Models (LLMs) are used as ==zero-shot room classifiers==, inferring room types for candidate ==frontiers== by prompting with observed objects and scoring based on ==negative language modeling loss==.
- A ==commonsense knowledge base== maps target objects to likely room types, enabling a ==semantic affinity score== for frontiers that prioritizes exploration in contextually relevant areas.

## Results

- HRO achieved a Success Rate (**SR**) of **53.0%** on HM3D and **84.0%** on Gibson, outperforming all baseline methods, including SemExp, PONI, SemUtil, VoroNav, ESC, and L3MVN.
- The framework, using GPT-2, surpassed GPT-3.5-based methods like VoroNav (**42.0%** SR on HM3D) and ESC (**39.2%** SR on HM3D) by over **11%** SR, demonstrating the power of its hierarchical architecture.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both LLM-driven room type inference (increasing SR by **1.3%**) and goal-oriented semantic affinity scoring (further increasing SR to **53.0%**) contribute to HRO's performance gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adopting a hierarchical "room-to-object" reasoning paradigm, mirroring human spatial cognition, significantly improves exploration pertinence and semantic association accuracy in zero-shot navigation.
> - Structured integration of LLM common-sense knowledge within a hierarchical framework can enable smaller LLMs (GPT-2) to outperform larger LLMs (GPT-3.5) used in flatter reasoning architectures for embodied AI tasks.
> - The accuracy of upstream perception modules, specifically semantic segmentation, is a critical bottleneck, indicating that improvements in this area can substantially boost LLM-driven navigation performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the challenge of zero-shot object-goal navigation (ZS-OGN) for intelligent agents. ZS-OGN is a subfield of embodied AI focused on enabling robots to locate novel objects in unfamiliar environments without prior training specific to those objects or scenes. This capability is relevant for applications such as home service robots and autonomous exploration.

Traditionally, object navigation methods have relied on extensive annotated data and task-specific training. However, the emergence of large pre-trained models, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), has facilitated the development of zero-shot learning approaches. These models leverage their vast prior knowledge to guide agents in novel scenarios, offering enhanced generalization capabilities.

Existing LLM-based zero-shot navigation methods, such as L3MVN, ESC, L-ZSON, and PixNav, typically employ LLMs as "flat" reasoning tools. This means they directly associate objects or regions without explicitly modeling hierarchical spatial relationships, such as the common human cognitive process of first understanding the room type and then locating an object within it. This flat reasoning can lead to inefficient exploration, reduced accuracy in semantic association, and an incomplete utilization of LLMs' common-sense reasoning potential.

Another related development is LROGNav, a concurrent work that also explores room-object relationships using LLMs for navigation. However, LROGNav relies on supervised learning and labeled room segmentation maps, contrasting with the zero-shot, training-free paradigm that HRO aims to achieve. The present work distinguishes itself by proposing a training-free, hierarchical "room-to-object" framework that aligns with human spatial cognition, thereby addressing the limitations of flat reasoning and supervised approaches in ZS-OGN.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a zero-shot object-goal navigation framework that enhances exploration efficiency and semantic reasoning accuracy by incorporating hierarchical spatial cognition. The framework aims to guide an intelligent agent to an unknown target object in an unfamiliar environment using a coarse-to-fine reasoning approach, mimicking human spatial understanding.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several identified limitations in existing LLM-based zero-shot navigation methods:

- **Lack of Hierarchical Spatial Cognition:** Current methods predominantly employ LLMs for "flat" reasoning, directly associating objects or regions. They do not model the human-like hierarchical understanding where one first infers the type of a room from observed objects and then uses this room context to infer potential target locations. This omission can lead to suboptimal navigation strategies.
- **Blindness in Exploration:** Without a structured understanding of environmental semantics, agents may explore areas that are unlikely to contain the target object, resulting in inefficient and "blind" exploration. A hierarchical approach, leveraging room semantics, could make exploration more pertinent.
- **Insufficient Accuracy in Semantic Association:** Direct, flat associations between objects or regions may lack the contextual richness provided by intermediate semantic representations like room types. Incorporating room semantics can refine these associations and improve the accuracy of goal localization.
- **Underutilization of LLM Common-Sense Reasoning:** LLMs possess extensive common-sense knowledge about the typical co-occurrence of objects within specific room types. Existing flat reasoning methods may not fully leverage this potential, limiting the LLMs' guiding capabilities in navigation tasks.
- **Need for Training-Free and Generalizable Solutions:** The goal is to provide a solution that can adapt to unseen objects and unknown scenes without requiring additional object or scene annotation data or task-specific fine-tuning. This enhances the versatility and applicability of the navigation system to truly novel scenarios.

To address these motivations, the proposed HRO framework aims to use "room type" as a semantic bridge, enabling LLMs to provide more structured and contextually relevant guidance. This design intends to align agent exploration with human logic, where scenario understanding precedes targeted object search, thereby improving semantic guidance and overall navigation performance in zero-shot settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HRO framework is an LLM-driven hierarchical "room-to-object" system for zero-shot object-goal navigation, structured into three interconnected modules: high-level reasoning, mid-level decision, and low-level execution. This design facilitates a coarse-to-fine navigation strategy.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The task is defined as zero-shot object-goal navigation, where an agent, starting at an initial pose (P₀) in an unknown indoor environment (Sᵢ), must find a target object category (Cᵢ). The agent operates without prior training, fine-tuning, or exposure to Sᵢ and Cᵢ. At each step, the agent receives RGB-D observations, its current pose, and selects an action from a predefined space (move forward, turn left/right, look up/down, stop). Success is achieved if the agent stops within 1 meter of the target within a maximum of 500 steps.

#### 3.2. Map Representation

The agent constructs a 2D semantic map, drawing inspiration from SemExp. This map is represented as a K × M × M matrix, where M × M is the map size and K is the number of channels. The first two channels store obstacle distribution and explored region information, respectively. The remaining C channels correspond to different semantic categories, representing object distributions.

At each time step, RGB-D observations are converted into a 3D point cloud. Mask R-CNN, a semantic segmentation model, is used to assign semantic labels to points in the cloud. This semantic point cloud is then projected into a 3D voxel space and vertically fused to create the 2D semantic map, including obstacle, explored region, and semantic category maps. Dilation is applied to obstacle map edges to prevent collisions.

#### 3.3. LLM-Driven Room Type Inference (High-Level Reasoning)

This module infers the semantic categories of rooms potentially associated with candidate frontiers, utilizing LLMs as a zero-shot room classifier.

1. **Frontier Identification:** Initial frontier cells are derived by subtracting the dilated obstacle map from the explored map. Connectivity analysis and clustering are applied, followed by removal of small clusters. A Mixed Criteria Scoring method, incorporating area and shortest path distance, is used to select *n* (set to 4) candidate frontiers.
2. **Candidate Room Types:** A predefined set of seven common indoor room types (R = {living room, bedroom, bathroom, kitchen, dining room, office, hallway}) is used.
3. **LLM Prompting and Scoring:** For each candidate frontier, detected objects within a local window around the frontier are aggregated. A prompt template is constructed: `"A room containing " + concat(O) + " is likely a " + r + "."`, where O is the set of observed objects and r is a candidate room type. An LLM (GPT-2 in this study) calculates a compatibility score for each candidate room type based on the negative language modeling loss of the prompt sequence. A lower loss indicates higher compatibility.
4. **Probability Distribution:** The compatibility scores are normalized using a softmax function to obtain a probability distribution P(rᵢ | O) over the candidate room types for each frontier.

#### 3.4. Goal-Oriented Semantic Affinity Scoring (Mid-Level Decision)

This module evaluates the potential value of candidate frontiers based on the target object and inferred room types.

1. **Commonsense Knowledge Base:** A knowledge base (M) is constructed, mapping target objects (G) to sets of room types where they are most likely to appear (e.g., M(bed) = {bedroom}, M(tv) = {living room, bedroom}).
2. **Affinity Score Calculation:** For a given target object (g) and a frontier's room probability distribution (P(r|O)), the semantic affinity score (S_Affinity) is calculated. This score is the maximum probability among the room types that are likely to contain the target object according to the commonsense knowledge base: S_Affinity *fᵢ*(g, O) = max*r*∈M(g) P(r | O). This ensures that strong evidence for any relevant room type results in a high score for the frontier.

#### 3.5. Path Planning and Execution (Low-Level Execution)

This module selects the optimal frontier and executes navigation actions.

1. **Frontier Prioritization:** The final priority score (S*fᵢ*) for each candidate frontier is a weighted sum of its semantic affinity score and its geometric Mixed Criteria Score: S*fᵢ* = α * (S_Affinity *fᵢ*(g, O) / max*f* S_Affinity *fᵢ*(g, O)) + (1 - α)S_MC *fᵢ*. This combines semantic relevance with geometric factors like distance and area.
2. **Path Planning and Action Execution:** The agent selects the frontier with the highest priority score. The Fast Marching Method is then used to plan the shortest path from the agent's current position to the center of the chosen frontier. The agent executes low-level motion commands (e.g., move forward, turn) along this path, continuously updating its environmental map and performing obstacle avoidance. This loop continues until the target is found or the maximum steps are reached.

The overall approach emphasizes integrating LLM common-sense reasoning within a structured, hierarchical framework to enhance the purposeful exploration and navigation in zero-shot scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The HRO framework was evaluated on two 3D scanned real-world datasets, HM3D and Gibson, using the Habitat platform. The performance was measured by Success Rate (SR, %) and Success weighted by Path Length (SPL, %). GPT-2 was used as the LLM for semantic reasoning.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparison with Baselines

Table I presents a comparison of HRO with several baseline methods: SemExp, PONI, SemUtil, VoroNav, ESC, and L3MVN.

- **Overall Performance:** HRO achieved a Success Rate (SR) of 53.0% on HM3D and 84.0% on Gibson. These values represent an improvement over all baseline methods on both datasets. In terms of Success weighted by Path Length (SPL), HRO obtained 24.5% on HM3D and 46.5% on Gibson, maintaining competitive efficiency metrics while achieving higher success rates.

- **Comparison with Non-LLM Baselines (SemExp, PONI):** SemExp (RL-trained) and PONI (potential function networks) require specialized training. HRO, a zero-shot method, achieved SRs of 53.0% (HM3D) and 84.0% (Gibson), surpassing PONI's Gibson SR of 73.6% and SemExp's SRs of 37.9% (HM3D) and 65.2% (Gibson). This indicates that zero-shot methods can achieve comparable or superior performance with better generalization than trained methods.

- **Comparison with LLM-based Baselines (SemUtil, VoroNav, ESC, L3MVN):**
 - **SemUtil (BERT embeddings):** HRO outperformed SemUtil on Gibson by 14.7% in SR (84.0% vs 69.3%) and 6.0% in SPL (46.5% vs 40.5%). This suggests that HRO's targeted room-object affinity modeling is more effective than general semantic utility learning.
 - **VoroNav and ESC (GPT-3.5):** Despite using GPT-2, a smaller LLM than GPT-3.5, HRO surpassed VoroNav and ESC on HM3D. HRO achieved 53.0% SR, compared to VoroNav's 42.0% (an 11.0% improvement) and ESC's 39.2% (a 13.8% improvement). This indicates that the hierarchical reasoning structure of HRO is a critical factor, enabling a smaller LLM to achieve better performance than larger LLMs within a flat reasoning architecture.
 - **L3MVN (GPT-2):** Both HRO and L3MVN used GPT-2 for semantic reasoning. HRO achieved 53.0% SR on HM3D compared to L3MVN's 50.4% (a 2.6% improvement), and 84.0% SR on Gibson compared to L3MVN's 76.1% (a 7.9% improvement). HRO also showed improvements in SPL. This specific comparison highlights the benefit of HRO's hierarchical "room-to-object" reasoning over L3MVN's direct "object-to-object" association.

- **Category-wise Comparison (Figure 4):** A detailed comparison with L3MVN on HM3D across different target categories (plant, toilet, bed, chair, TV, sofa) showed that HRO generally achieved higher success rates for most categories, further supporting the efficacy of its hierarchical approach.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

An ablation study on the HM3D dataset (Table II) analyzed the contribution of HRO's core modules:

- **Object-Object Association (Baseline):** A baseline strategy similar to L3MVN, using direct object semantic relevance, yielded a SR of 50.2% and SPL of 23.6%. This validates the utility of LLM semantic reasoning even in a flat structure.
- **Room Type Inference (RT Inf):** Introducing the high-level LLM-driven Room Type Inference module improved the SR to 51.5% (a 1.3% increase). This indicates that moving from flat "object-with-object" reasoning to hierarchical "object-to-room" reasoning enhances the utilization of spatial semantic priors, guiding the agent towards potentially relevant functional regions.
- **Semantic Affinity Scoring (SA Sco):** Integrating the mid-level Goal-Oriented Semantic Affinity Scoring module into the framework (the complete HRO model) further increased the SR to 53.0% and SPL to 24.5%. This demonstrates the importance of explicitly modeling the affinity between the target object and inferred room types, converting generalized room understanding into target-specific navigation decisions.
- **Ground Truth Segmentation (GT Seg):** When ground truth semantic segmentation results were used instead of Mask R-CNN's predictions, the SR increased substantially to 64.9% and SPL to 35.8%. This finding indicates that the accuracy of upstream object detection and semantic map construction is a significant bottleneck for the current system, and improvements in this area could lead to further performance gains.

In summary, the results demonstrate that the hierarchical "room-to-object" reasoning framework of HRO significantly improves zero-shot object goal navigation, primarily by enhancing exploration pertinence and semantic association accuracy through its structured use of LLM common-sense knowledge.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HRO framework contributes to the field of embodied AI and zero-shot navigation in several ways, with potential implications for future research and applications.

Firstly, the introduction of a **hierarchical "room-to-object" reasoning paradigm** for zero-shot object goal navigation represents a departure from existing "flat" reasoning LLM-based approaches. By explicitly modeling an intermediate semantic representation of "room type," HRO more closely mimics human spatial cognition, where an understanding of the environment's functional areas precedes the localization of specific objects. This hierarchical structure has been shown to enhance exploration pertinence and semantic association accuracy.

Secondly, the framework offers a **training-free and annotation-free solution** for zero-shot object navigation. Unlike methods that require supervised training or task-specific fine-tuning, HRO leverages the inherent prior knowledge within Large Language Models to adapt directly to unseen objects and unknown scenes. This characteristic is significant as it reduces the dependency on extensive labeled datasets, which are often costly and time-consuming to acquire for diverse environments and object categories. This inherent generalizability makes the system more robust and practical for deployment in dynamic, real-world settings where pre-training on all possible scenarios is not feasible.

Thirdly, the experimental results demonstrate that the proposed architecture enables even a moderately sized LLM (GPT-2) to **outperform systems utilizing larger LLMs (GPT-3.5) with flatter reasoning structures**. This suggests that the architectural design and the structured integration of common-sense knowledge are as critical as, or potentially more critical than, the scale of the language model itself for certain embodied AI tasks. This finding could guide future research towards developing more efficient and effective LLM integration strategies for robotic control, rather than solely focusing on larger models.

Finally, the ablation study highlights the **critical impact of accurate semantic segmentation** on overall navigation performance. The substantial performance jump observed when using ground truth segmentation indicates that advancements in perception modules (e.g., object detection and semantic mapping) could unlock further potential in LLM-driven navigation systems. This points to a valuable direction for interdisciplinary research, integrating robust computer vision with advanced language-based reasoning.

The potential impact of this work includes the development of more intelligent and autonomous home service robots capable of executing diverse commands in novel environments without explicit programming for each task. It also contributes to the broader understanding of how human-like cognitive architectures, especially hierarchical reasoning, can be effectively translated into AI systems for complex real-world interaction. Future work may focus on deeper language-environment interaction mechanisms to further unlock the full potential of LLMs in navigation.
