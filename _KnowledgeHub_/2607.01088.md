# ROSA: A Robotics Foundation Model Serving System for Robot Factories

> [!summary] Summary
> ROSA, a new architecture from NVIDIA Research and Stanford, serves Robotics Foundation Models in robot factories by using shared, server-scale GPU resources and factory-objective-driven scheduling. This system achieves up to 12.06 times higher factory productivity compared to dedicated serving baselines, significantly improving efficiency for large-scale robot deployments.

## Problem

- Existing RFM serving systems are inefficient and not scalable for "robot factories" due to reliance on dedicated edge computing per robot.
- Current optimization objectives focus narrowly on minimizing single action model latency, overlooking the need for complex multi-model pipelines and overall factory-level productivity.
- Dedicated per-robot computing leads to high costs, limited performance, and under-utilization of GPU resources in industrial settings.

## Method

- Implements a ==centralized, server-scale architecture== where a fleet of robots shares a pool of powerful data-center GPUs to offload intensive inference.
- Provides a ==declarative programming abstraction== for users to specify complex multi-model robot tasks, heterogeneous performance requirements (==SLOs==), and comprehensive safety/failure handling policies.
- Utilizes a specialized ==scheduler== to optimize for a ==factory-level objective==, maximizing ==weighted robot action throughput== across the fleet while adhering to all specified service-level objectives.

## Results

- Demonstrated up to **12.06x** higher SLO-qualified factory productivity compared to dedicated serving systems and **2.44x** higher than unoptimized shared-server baselines.
- Maintained high service-level objective (SLO) compliance, achieving up to **99.9%** SLO meet rates for complex tasks even with a large robot fleet, preventing performance degradation under heavy load.
- Achieved significant resource efficiency, increasing qualified actions per second by **3.18x** at high loads and requiring up to **8.6x** fewer GPUs than baselines for comparable performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Shifting from dedicated edge computing to a centralized, shared GPU architecture for RFM inference dramatically increases efficiency and scalability in robot factories.
> - Optimizing for overall factory productivity, including weighted robot action throughput and adherence to diverse service-level objectives, is more effective than solely minimizing individual model latency.
> - Robust, robotics-aware scheduling that incorporates dynamic rate control, adaptive resource allocation, and profiling-guided batching is critical for meeting performance SLOs and maximizing throughput across heterogeneous tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the emerging challenge of deploying Robotics Foundation Models (RFMs) in highly automated industrial environments, referred to as "robot factories." RFMs, including Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, World Action Models (WAMs), and associated reasoning and control models, are considered central to the next phase of embodied AI. These models have demonstrated enhanced generalization capabilities, making general-purpose robots practical for diverse tasks such as manipulation and navigation. Industry leaders are actively exploring and deploying such robots in manufacturing and warehousing facilities.

Current research in RFM inference has largely focused on optimizing individual action models (often termed "System 1" models) from algorithmic and system perspectives. These optimizations include using smaller models, quantization, selective layer skipping, and system-level improvements like CUDA graphs. However, the authors identify two pervasive misconceptions and associated research gaps that limit the efficiency and scalability of existing RFM serving systems in a factory context:

1. **Strictly Edge Computing Problem:** Existing paradigms often couple each robot with an onboard System-on-Chip (SoC) or a dedicated GPU server. This approach is costly, offers limited performance due to power/thermal constraints on robots, and leads to under-utilization of GPUs because it prevents inter-robot request batching and leaves GPUs idle during robot action execution.
2. **Minimizing Single Action Model Latency:** The optimization objective typically centers on minimizing the inference latency for a single action model. This neglects the necessity of multi-model pipelines (e.g., high-level planning, safety checking, task progression monitoring) required for reliable robotic task execution and overlooks broader factory-level objectives such as maximizing overall productivity while meeting service-level objectives (SLOs).

ROSA aims to bridge these gaps by proposing a new architectural and scheduling paradigm for RFM serving in robot factories. It shifts from a single-robot, single-model, latency-centric approach to a shared, server-scale, multi-model, factory-objective-driven system. This work positions itself as an early effort to define and solve the RFM serving problem for large-scale, general-purpose robot deployments in industrial settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for ROSA stems from the limitations and misconceptions observed in existing RFM serving systems when scaled to robot factory environments. The authors aim to enable the efficient and reliable deployment of general-purpose robot fleets by rethinking the underlying serving infrastructure and scheduling mechanisms.

The key objectives are structured around three core principles:

1. **To establish a server-scale, shared RFM serving infrastructure:**
 - **Motivation:** Overcome the performance and efficiency limitations of per-robot dedicated compute. On-robot accelerators are less powerful than data-center GPUs, leading to higher inference latency for increasingly complex RFMs. Dedicated edge servers suffer from low GPU utilization due to interleaved inference and execution, and lack of inter-robot batching. Battery-powered robots also face reduced operational duration when performing intensive local inference.
 - **Objective:** Design a system where multiple robots share a centralized pool of powerful server-class GPUs over the network. This aims to:
 - Improve inference performance by providing access to data-center-class hardware.
 - Extend operational duration for battery-powered robots by offloading compute-intensive inference.
 - Enhance hardware utilization and cost efficiency through inter-robot batching and reduced per-robot hardware provisioning.

2. **To provide a robotics-aware programming abstraction and system design:**
 - **Motivation:** Reliable robot task execution often requires more than just action prediction. Complex tasks involve multi-model pipelines for planning, safety, and monitoring. Different robots, tasks, or environments impose diverse performance requirements. Furthermore, robots must gracefully handle failures, such as safety warnings or missed latency SLOs. Existing systems do not adequately support these robotics-specific needs.
 - **Objective:** Develop a system that exposes a programming abstraction and includes system design elements to support:
 - Orchestration across heterogeneous model components (e.g., System 1 action, System 2 planning, safety, monitor models).
 - Specification and satisfaction of heterogeneous performance requirements (e.g., varying P99 latency and invocation rate SLOs).
 - Robust safety and task-failure handling mechanisms, including configurable fallback actions (e.g., stop execution, resend request, retry task, human intervention).

3. **To implement factory-objective-driven system scheduling:**
 - **Motivation:** Minimizing individual inference latency for a single robot is insufficient for a robot factory. The overarching goal is typically to maximize overall factory output or productivity while respecting individual task constraints. Existing schedulers are not designed with this holistic factory-level objective in mind.
 - **Objective:** Design a serving scheduler that optimizes a factory-level objective, specifically maximizing weighted robot action throughput across the robot fleet. This scheduler must also satisfy the latency and invocation-rate SLOs for all required model components. The scheduling decisions should encompass resource allocation, batching configurations, and request routing.

In summary, the key objectives are to build an efficient, reliable, and model-agnostic RFM serving system capable of scaling to robot fleets in industrial settings, moving beyond the limitations of single-robot, single-model deployments by leveraging shared resources and optimizing for factory-level productivity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ROSA (Robotics Oriented Serving Architecture) is designed as a server-scale, GPU-pool-based RFM serving system for robot factories. Its methodology integrates a centralized architecture, a declarative programming interface, and a specialized scheduling mechanism.

**Centralized, Shared RFM Serving Architecture:**
ROSA advocates for a centralized cluster of data-center-class GPUs shared by a fleet of robots. On-robot compute is minimized, focusing on high-frequency local control (e.g., joint torques) and enforcing local safety/failure handling. This approach manages network and queuing overheads by relying on stable industrial networks and calibrated scheduling.

**ROSA Programming Abstraction:**
A declarative interface (e.g., YAML) is provided for users to specify the factory serving workload, including:
- **Server cluster and robot fleet:** Definitions of available GPUs and the number of robots assigned to different tasks.
- **Task definitions:** For each task, detailed attributes are specified:
 - **Model Composition:** The set of RFM components required (e.g., System 1 action, System 2 planning, safety, task monitor models) and their dependencies (e.g., System 2 invocation ratio relative to System 1 actions, action execution time).
 - **Performance Requirements:** P99 latency SLOs and invocation frequencies for each model component.
 - **Safety and SLO Violation Handling:** Policies for responding to warnings from safety models or missed latency SLOs. These include component-level fallbacks (e.g., stop and resend, use last plan, stop and replan) and task-level escalation (e.g., stop and call human after consecutive violations).
 - **Task Retry Policy:** Mechanisms for retrying a failed task (detected by the monitor model) and escalating to human intervention after a defined number of retries.

**ROSA Scheduler:**
The scheduler is central to ROSA, translating the declarative workload specification into an optimal serving schedule. It makes decisions regarding model placement, request routing, batching configurations, and per-robot inference request rates.

- **Assumptions:** The scheduler operates under assumptions suitable for factory workloads:
 - Workload is known in advance (number of robots, task specs, GPUs, SLOs, invocation ratios).
 - Leverages profiling data for each model's latency and throughput characteristics.
 - Assumes synchronous execution where System 1 inference is interleaved with robot action execution.
 - Each GPU worker is dedicated to a single model to avoid performance interference.
 - Employs continuous batching for autoregressive VLM components (System 2, safety, monitor) and discrete batching for System 1 action models.
- **Objective Function:** Maximize the total weighted robot action rate across the fleet, subject to all specified latency and invocation-rate SLOs.
- **Scheduling Process:**
 - **Obligation Model Provisioning:** First, the scheduler determines the minimum server footprint required for "obligation models" (safety, monitor) based on aggregate load and measured serving profiles to meet their SLOs.
 - **Goal-Coupled Model Optimization:** The remaining servers are allocated to "goal-coupled models" (System 1, System 2) which directly influence the action rate.
 - **Homogeneous Task Scheduling (single task type):**
 - A binary search is performed on a single shared action rate (`f`).
 - For each candidate `f`, feasible per-server configurations (model, batch size, number of robot streams) are enumerated, ensuring memory fit and latency SLOs.
 - An Integer Linear Program (ILP) packs these configurations onto the available servers to cover all robot streams, minimizing a tie-breaking cost.
 - The candidate `f` is validated against a closed-loop upper bound determined by action execution time and induced mean latencies of System 1 and System 2.
 - **Heterogeneous Task Scheduling (multiple task types):**
 - An adaptive frontier search navigates the multi-dimensional space of action rate vectors (`f_c`) for different task classes.
 - A two-stage ILP-heuristic packing primitive is used:
 - **Isolated packing:** An ILP is used to select configurations where each server serves one model component for one task class.
 - **Compaction:** Servers hosting the same model component for different classes are greedily merged if doing so remains SLO-feasible, recovering cross-class sharing opportunities.
- **Runtime Adaptation:**
 - **Placement-preserving admission control:** New robots are admitted by routing to existing servers, updating batching/rates without full rescheduling, if SLOs are maintained.
 - **Global rescheduling:** If placement-preserving admission fails or leads to poor efficiency, a full schedule recomputation occurs.
 - **Fault tolerance:** Hot-standby GPU nodes are reserved with loaded model weights; on active GPU failure, affected robot streams are redirected to standbys.

**Implementation:**
ROSA is implemented using Ray Serve for distributed orchestration, handling request routing and managing model replicas. Model-specific inference runtimes are used: vLLM for autoregressive VLM components (System 2, safety, monitor) and PyTorch or JAX for System 1 action models. The scheduler logic is implemented in Python, utilizing OR-Tools for ILP solving.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ROSA was conducted using both real robot experiments with a Franka Panda arm and large-scale synthetic workloads simulating robot factory deployments on an 8-GPU NVIDIA H200 server.

**End-to-End Performance (Factory Productivity):**
- **Overall Improvement:** ROSA demonstrated substantial improvements in SLO-qualified factory productivity:
 - Up to **12.06× higher** than conventional dedicated serving baselines (one GPU per robot or one GPU per model per robot).
 - Up to **2.44× higher** than shared-server baselines that use the same infrastructure but lack ROSA's specialized scheduler.
- **Single-Task Workloads (P1-P4, increasing complexity):**
 - **Vs. Shared-Server Baselines:** As the number of robots increased (e.g., to 32), shared-server baselines (equal or weighted partitioning without rate control) exhibited a significant drop in qualified action throughput, often collapsing to zero due to SLO violations from uncapped request injection. ROSA consistently maintained high qualified throughput (e.g., 89.4 qualified actions/s for P4 with 32 robots at 99.9% SLO meet rate) by managing request rates and resource allocation.
 - **Vs. Dedicated Serving Baselines:** Dedicated systems were severely limited in the number of robots they could support (e.g., max 8 robots for 1 GPU/robot, max 2 robots for P4 with 1 GPU/model). ROSA, by contrast, leveraged its shared GPU pool to serve many more robots efficiently while meeting SLOs (e.g., 89.4 qualified actions/s with 32 robots for P4, compared to 7.4 qualified actions/s with 2 robots for the best dedicated baseline).
- **Heterogeneous Workloads (Mix2, Mix4):**
 - ROSA improved peak qualified throughput by **2.21× on Mix2** and **2.30× on Mix4** over the best shared-server baseline. Baselines struggled in heterogeneous scenarios because their static allocation and uncapped requests failed to adapt to the varied demands of different task classes. ROSA's calculated scheduling maintained high SLO compliance and efficient capacity allocation across diverse tasks.

**Scheduling Decision Ablation:**
- **SLO-Qualified Action Prediction:** Without ROSA's rate control, shared-server baselines experienced significantly higher median latencies (e.g., 534.5 ms for P4 vs. ROSA's 83.8 ms at 32 robots) and 0% System 1 SLO meet rates at high loads. ROSA maintained high SLO meet rates (e.g., 99.96% at 32 robots) by controlling the request frequency.
- **Request-Rate Control:** The study confirmed that capping robot request rates prevents server overload and significantly improves qualified throughput at high loads. For P4 with 64 robots, uncapped sending yielded high raw actions but only 24.8 qualified actions/s (17% SLO meet rate), whereas ROSA's capped schedule produced 78.8 qualified actions/s (97.2% SLO meet rate), a **3.18× improvement**.
- **Resource Allocation:** Suboptimal static resource allocation, even with request-rate control, led to baselines requiring up to **8.6× more GPUs** to match ROSA's performance. ROSA's dynamic allocation across model components (System 1, System 2, safety, monitor) provided significant speedups (e.g., 2.20× over the best static allocation for P4 with 32 robots).
- **System 1 Batching:** The profiling-guided, dynamic selection of batch sizes for System 1 action models was shown to be critical. Larger batch sizes (e.g., 16) improved inference throughput by 3-4× but introduced latency. The scheduler adaptively selected optimal batch sizes based on load, achieving up to a **2.46× speedup** over a fixed batch size of one under high request pressure.

**Real Robot Experiments:**
- ROSA's full serving path was validated on a Franka Panda robot arm for a pick-and-place task, demonstrating the functionality of the action model, task-progression monitor, and safety model in a real-world setting. This confirmed the system's operational viability beyond synthetic simulations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ROSA represents a significant contribution to the field of embodied AI and robotics by addressing critical challenges in deploying Robotics Foundation Models (RFMs) in industrial environments. The work's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Scalable Robot Factories:** ROSA's core proposal of a shared, server-scale RFM serving infrastructure directly addresses the scalability and efficiency limitations of current edge-centric or dedicated serving paradigms. This approach has the potential to make large-scale deployments of general-purpose robots in factories more economically viable and performant, facilitating the broader adoption of advanced robotics in manufacturing, logistics, and other industries.

2. **Increased Productivity and Efficiency:** The demonstrated improvements in factory productivity—up to 12.06× over dedicated serving and 2.44× over unoptimized shared serving—indicate a substantial leap in operational efficiency. This translates to higher throughput of robotic tasks, optimized utilization of expensive GPU resources, and reduced operational costs for robot fleets. For battery-powered mobile robots, offloading inference extends battery life, increasing robot availability.

3. **Enhanced Reliability and Safety:** By providing a robotics-aware programming abstraction that supports multi-model pipelines (including planning, safety, and monitoring models), heterogeneous performance requirements, and robust failure handling mechanisms, ROSA contributes to more reliable and safer robot operations. This is crucial for industrial environments where human-robot interaction and task-critical performance are paramount. The ability to specify SLOs, fallbacks, and retry policies directly contributes to predictable and resilient system behavior.

4. **Forward-Looking and Model-Agnostic Design:** The authors emphasize that ROSA's three design principles (shared server-scale infrastructure, robotics-aware abstraction, and factory-objective-driven scheduling) are expected to remain relevant despite the rapid evolution of RFM architectures. This model-agnostic approach provides a durable foundation for future RFM serving systems, reducing the need for complete architectural overhauls as new models emerge.

5. **Addressing Key Research Gaps:** ROSA directly tackles the identified misconceptions that RFM serving is solely an edge computing problem and that minimizing single-model latency is the sole objective. By shifting to a centralized, shared resource model and optimizing for factory-level productivity, the work redefines the problem statement and offers a comprehensive solution.

In conclusion, ROSA offers a foundational framework for the next generation of RFM serving systems in industrial settings. Its approach promises to unlock greater efficiency, reliability, and scalability for general-purpose robot deployments, thereby accelerating the adoption and impact of embodied AI in real-world factory environments.
