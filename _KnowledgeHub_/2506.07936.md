# Mimicking or Reasoning: Rethinking Multi-Modal In-Context Learning in Vision-Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Institute of Technology challenge the assumption that Vision-Language Models (VLMs) genuinely learn from in-context examples, finding that they often mimic superficial patterns rather than acquire deeper reasoning capabilities. Their work demonstrates that current VLMs generally do not achieve consistent performance gains over zero-shot baselines, even when provided with explicit reasoning demonstrations and under controlled distribution shifts.

## Problem

- A prevailing assumption exists that Vision-Language Models (VLMs) possess multi-modal in-context learning (MM-ICL) capabilities akin to large language models, but the depth of this learning (mimicking vs. reasoning) is unclear.
- VLMs often resort to shallow heuristics like copying answers or majority voting when learning from demonstrations, especially in out-of-distribution (OOD) settings where performance can plateau or degrade.
- Existing MM-ICL benchmarks may not rigorously distinguish between superficial pattern matching and genuine task understanding, leading to ambiguous conclusions about VLM capabilities.

## Method

- The study rigorously evaluates ==MM-ICL capabilities under distribution shifts== by comparing VLM performance in In-Distribution (ID) vs. Out-of-Distribution (OOD) settings for support examples.
- A novel ==MM-ICL with Reasoning pipeline== is introduced that provides information-enriched demonstrations, including explicit step-by-step rationales alongside answers, to provide a stronger learning signal.
- Comprehensive experiments are conducted on a diverse range of modern open-source and proprietary VLMs, including their 'reasoner' variants, assessing sensitivity to factors like ==shot count, retrieval methods, rationale quality==, and support set distribution.

## Results

- Current VLMs and VLM reasoners generally do not derive consistent or substantial benefit from MM-ICL over zero-shot performance across a majority of perception and reasoning datasets, often failing to significantly exceed or sometimes falling below the zero-shot baseline.
- The performance of VLM reasoners significantly improved (e.g., up to **+8.71%** on M3CoT) when demonstrations included both rationales and answers, ensuring format consistency with the model's expected output.
- Improving the quality of rationales (via filtering incorrect samples or using ground-truth rationales) within demonstrations did not consistently lead to improved performance, and multimodal retrievers often underperformed random selection for reasoning models, indicating insensitivity to reasoning quality and complex interactions with retrieval.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current Vision-Language Models (VLMs) frequently rely on superficial pattern matching or 'mimicking' rather than genuine in-context reasoning from demonstrations.
> - Format consistency between demonstration structure (e.g., including rationales) and the VLM's expected output format is crucial for effective in-context learning by reasoner models.
> - The quality of rationales within demonstrations, even if ground-truth or filtered for correctness, often does not consistently improve VLM performance, indicating insensitivity to deeper informational content.
> - Multimodal retrievers can be counterproductive for VLM reasoners by selecting examples with similar inputs but potentially inconsistent reasoning paths, hindering learning from diverse logical structures.
> - Modern VLMs are more robust to distribution shifts (Out-of-Distribution vs. In-Distribution support sets) in demonstrations, but this robustness does not equate to effective in-context learning gains over zero-shot performance itself, rather it suggests less susceptibility to superficial patterns based on data distribution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language models (LLMs) has witnessed remarkable progress, largely attributed to their emergent in-context learning (ICL) capabilities. ICL allows LLMs to learn from a few examples provided in the prompt without explicit parameter updates, facilitating rapid adaptation to new tasks. Inspired by this success, Vision-Language Models (VLMs), which integrate visual and textual understanding, have been widely assumed to possess similar multimodal in-context learning (MM-ICL) abilities. Numerous recent works (e.g., Awadalla et al., Bai et al., Baldassini et al.) have indeed demonstrated that VLMs can perform MM-ICL across various tasks.

However, a critical debate has emerged within the research community regarding the *nature* of this observed ICL. Several studies (e.g., Baldassini et al., Chen et al., Zong et al.) have raised doubts, suggesting that current VLMs might often rely on shallow heuristics, such as direct copying of responses from demonstrations or defaulting to majority voting patterns, rather than acquiring a deeper, transferable understanding of the underlying task logic. This distinction between "mimicking" and "reasoning" forms the core of the current paper's investigation.

This work directly challenges the prevailing assumption of genuine MM-ICL in VLMs by rigorously evaluating their performance under conditions designed to expose superficial learning. It advances the discussion by:
- **Introducing Distribution Shifts:** Unlike many ICL benchmarks that use in-distribution (ID) support examples, this paper specifically tests VLMs under out-of-distribution (OOD) settings, where support examples come from a different dataset than the query. This setup is crucial for distinguishing true understanding from mere memorization or pattern matching tied to specific data distributions.
- **Focusing on Reasoning:** The paper integrates the burgeoning field of VLM reasoning (e.g., Shen et al., Wang et al.) by augmenting demonstrations with explicit step-by-step rationales, akin to Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting in LLMs. This is a novel approach for MM-ICL, aiming to provide a stronger learning signal and a better chance for models to internalize task methodologies. By assessing how models utilize these enriched demonstrations, the study aims to determine if they can move beyond surface-level cues towards deeper reasoning.
- **Benchmarking Modern VLMs:** It conducts extensive experiments on a diverse range of modern open-source VLMs (from 3B to 72B parameters) and a proprietary model (Gemini 2.0 Flash), including their "reasoner" variants. This broad evaluation provides a more current and comprehensive picture of MM-ICL capabilities compared to studies relying on older or a limited set of models.

In essence, this paper serves as a critical re-evaluation of MM-ICL, shifting the focus from merely demonstrating its existence to questioning its depth and robustness. It positions itself as a foundational study that probes the true learning mechanisms of VLMs in a multimodal, in-context setting, aiming to provide a more "solid evidence" for conclusions about their capabilities.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central overarching question that motivates this research is: **Do Vision-Language Models truly learn from in-context demonstrations, or are they just matching superficial patterns?**

The authors observe several critical issues that fuel this inquiry:
- **Counterintuitive Performance in OOD Settings:** When support examples originate from a different dataset (Out-of-Distribution or OOD) than the query, VLM performance often paradoxically plateaus or even degrades as the number of demonstrations increases. This contradicts the expectation that more information should lead to better performance, as seen in In-Distribution (ID) settings.
- **Reliance on Shallow Heuristics:** Prior work and initial observations by the authors suggest that VLMs frequently resort to shallow strategies, such as directly copying answers from recent similar responses or applying majority voting patterns across demonstrations, rather than genuinely understanding the task.
- **Ambiguity in Current MM-ICL Benchmarking:** Existing MM-ICL benchmarks are questioned for assuming ICL capability without thoroughly investigating whether models truly learn from demonstrations. They might inadvertently reward superficial pattern matching over deep task comprehension.

Given these motivations, the paper sets forth the following key objectives:
1. **To rigorously evaluate MM-ICL capabilities under distribution shifts:** Specifically, to compare VLM performance when support examples are In-Distribution (ID) versus Out-of-Distribution (OOD) relative to the query dataset, assessing if models truly master the methodology or merely memorize patterns.
2. **To probe beyond surface-level pattern matching:** By providing richer, information-enhanced demonstrations that include explicit reasoning processes (rationales) alongside answers, the authors aim to offer models a stronger signal for learning and assess if this enables deeper understanding and generalization.
3. **To introduce and validate a new MM-ICL with Reasoning pipeline:** This pipeline aims to resolve a key format inconsistency in prior work where reasoning models were expected to generate rationales but received only answers in demonstrations. By providing demonstrations with both pseudo-reasoning and answers, the study aims to create a more consistent and effective learning environment for reasoning-capable VLMs.
4. **To conduct comprehensive and controlled experimental studies:** The research aims to assess the sensitivity of modern VLMs (both base and reasoner variants, open-source and proprietary) to various factors, including shot count, retrieval methods, rationale quality (pseudo, gold, filtered), and support set distribution. This extensive evaluation seeks to provide robust evidence for the conclusions drawn.

Ultimately, the paper aims to provide a definitive answer to whether current VLMs possess genuine in-context learning abilities for complex multimodal tasks that require reasoning, or if their performance gains in few-shot settings are largely a result of "mimicking" superficial patterns.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors design a systematic framework to dissect the MM-ICL capabilities of VLMs, particularly focusing on their ability to learn from demonstrations that include reasoning steps.

#### 3.1. MM-ICL Framework and Retriever Design

The study formalizes MM-ICL by defining a query `q = (I_q, T_q)` (image `I_q`, question/instruction `T_q`) and a context prompt `C_q` consisting of `N` demonstration examples from a support set `S`. The core idea is to generate a response `R_q = VLM([p_sys, Order(C_q), p_instruct, q])`, where `p_sys` is a system prompt, `Order` determines demonstration order, and `p_instruct` is an optional instruction prompt (e.g., for Chain-of-Thoughts).

For selecting demonstrations, the authors consider:
- **Random Selection:** Uniformly sampling examples from `S`, avoiding bias but potentially suboptimal.
- **Similarity-Based Retrievers:** Employing multimodal encoders to compute representations `h_i = Encoder(I_i, T_i, R_i)` for each example, then selecting the most relevant ones. This is preferred over unimodal encoders to capture crucial modality interactions.

#### 3.2. Task Classification and Distribution Shift

The paper categorizes MM-ICL tasks into two types:
- **Case I: Well-defined tasks without demos:** Tasks with solutions uniquely determined by the query (e.g., VQA, Image Captioning). These are solvable in zero-shot to some degree.
- **Case II: Ill-defined tasks without demos:** Tasks that are only well-defined when demonstrations are provided (e.g., Operator Induction, Open-Ended Object Recognition with synthetic categories).
The study primarily focuses on **Case I tasks** (Visual Question Answering (VQA) using TextVQA and OK-VQA, and reasoning-required VQA using ScienceQA, A-OKVQA, M3CoT). This choice is deliberate to assess whether ICL truly *enhances* a model's ability to solve a pre-defined task, rather than merely helping define an ambiguous one.

To evaluate genuine learning versus superficial memorization, the authors introduce a crucial experimental design:
- **In-Distribution (ID) Setting:** Support examples are drawn from the training split of the *same* dataset as the query.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Setting:** Support examples are drawn from a *different* dataset but share the *same task type* as the query. This setup aims to test whether the VLM understands the underlying methodology of the task or simply relies on patterns specific to the support dataset.

#### 3.3. MM-ICL with Reasoning Pipeline

The core innovation is the proposal of an **information-enriched demonstration format** that includes a detailed step-by-step reasoning process (rationale) in addition to the answer. This is designed to provide a stronger learning signal. To implement this, the authors leverage **VLM Reasoners** (e.g., VLM-R1, VL-Rethinker, LLaVA-CoT) which are models prompted to generate both rationales and answers.

The study defines four key MM-ICL protocols:
1. **Base model without reasoning demos:** Standard MM-ICL where demos include `(Image, Question, Answer)`.
2. **Reasoning model without reasoning demos (Inconsistent):** Demos are `(Image, Question, Answer)`, but the model is expected to output `(Rationale, Answer)`. This highlights a format inconsistency.
3. **Base model with reasoning demos:** Demos are `(Image, Question, Rationale, Answer)`, evaluating if explicit rationales help base models.
4. **Reasoning model with reasoning demos (Consistent):** This is the proposed best practice. Demos are `(Image, Question, Rationale, Answer)`, mirroring the model's expected output format. This involves a two-stage process:
 - **Pseudo Reasoning Generation:** For each support sample, the reasoning model first generates a rationale and answer, ensuring consistency with its training.
 - **Context Formation:** These generated reasoning-augmented demonstrations are then used as context for the query.
To enhance rationale quality within this protocol, two strategies are explored:
 - **Gold Reasoning:** Using ground truth rationales (if available) to reformat into the desired structure.
 - **Correctness-Based Filtering:** Filtering out support samples where the model's generated answer for the demonstration was incorrect, hypothesizing that incorrect rationales might be misleading.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Models Evaluated:** A comprehensive set of open-source VLMs and their reasoning variants: Qwen2.5-VL (3B, 7B, 72B), VLM-R1 (3B), VL-Rethinker (7B, 72B), InternVL 2.5 (4B, 8B), InternVL 2.5-MPO (4B, 8B), Llama-3.2V (11B), and LLaVA-CoT (11B). A closed-source model, Gemini 2.0 Flash (with "non-thinking" and "thinking" prompts), is also included for comparison.
- **Datasets:**
 - **Perception:** TextVQA (text in images), OK-VQA (commonsense VQA).
 - **Reasoning:** ScienceQA (science questions with expert explanations), A-OKVQA (commonsense reasoning with natural language rationales), M3CoT (multi-hop reasoning with CoT rationales).
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Accuracy (exact match for perception datasets), and LLM-judge (GPT-4o mini) for reasoning datasets to handle varied answer formats and assess rationale quality.
- **Controlled Variables:** Shot count (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32), retrieval method (random, multimodal), reasoning quality (pseudo, gold, filtered), and support set distribution (ID, OOD).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experimental evaluation yielded several counter-intuitive and significant findings that challenge the prevailing understanding of MM-ICL in VLMs.

#### 4.1. Initial Observations on OOD vs. ID (Older Models)

The study initially observed that for models like OpenFlamingo (an older VLM), performance on VQA tasks (TextVQA, OK-VQA) in OOD settings often plateaued or degraded with an increased number of shots, contrasting sharply with the monotonic improvement seen in ID settings. This suggested that models were not learning general methodologies but rather relying on subtle patterns or response formats specific to the in-distribution demonstrations. Additionally, multimodal retrievers, which typically boost performance in ID settings, often *underperformed* random selection in OOD scenarios, further indicating a lack of true learning beyond surface-level cues.

#### 4.2. The Crucial Role of Format Consistency in Reasoning MM-ICL

A critical finding for VLM reasoners was the importance of format consistency. When demonstrations explicitly included *both* rationale and answer (consistent format, Protocol 4) mirroring the model's expected output, performance consistently surpassed setups where only the final answer was provided in demonstrations (inconsistent format, Protocol 2). This was particularly evident in higher-shot settings and across reasoning datasets (e.g., up to +8.71% on M3CoT with LLaVA-CoT). This result underscores that even for advanced VLMs, the structural alignment between input demonstrations and expected output format is crucial for effective ICL, highlighting a sensitivity that can hinder true learning if overlooked.

#### 4.3. Limited Benefit of MM-ICL with Reasoning (Core Finding)

Despite introducing information-enriched demonstrations with reasoning and ensuring consistent formatting, the most striking finding is that **current VLMs and VLM reasoners generally do not derive consistent or substantial benefit from MM-ICL over zero-shot performance.** In a majority of cases across both perception and reasoning datasets, the few-shot accuracy (even with optimal shot counts) failed to significantly exceed, or sometimes even fell below, the zero-shot baseline. While some minimal gains were occasionally observed, they were not indicative of robust in-context learning. This suggests that even with explicit rationales, VLMs struggle to effectively learn from or generalize the task methodology presented in demonstrations.

#### 4.4. Insensitivity to Rationale Quality

Further investigation into the quality of rationales within demonstrations revealed that improving rationale quality – either by filtering out incorrect support samples or by injecting ground-truth rationales – did *not* consistently lead to improved performance. In several instances, filtering actually resulted in a slight performance *decline*. The authors hypothesize that this might be due to a reduction in support sample diversity and coverage, suggesting that for current VLMs, sufficient diversity might sometimes be more critical than the informational quality of individual rationales. This finding strongly reinforces the conclusion that VLMs are largely insensitive to the detailed information content (i.e., the *quality of reasoning*) in the demonstrations, further weakening the claim that they perform true MM-ICL.

#### 4.5. Complex Role of Retriever Methods

The effectiveness of multimodal retrievers varied significantly between base models and reasoning models:
- For **base models** (without explicit reasoning in prompts), multimodal retrievers often improved performance on certain datasets (M3CoT, ScienceQA, OK-VQA). This is attributed to the retriever selecting input-similar examples, which might facilitate shallow pattern matching or "copying" of highly similar answers, leading to a performance boost.
- For **reasoning models**, surprisingly, multimodal retrievers consistently *underperformed* random selection, especially on reasoning-intensive datasets. This is explained by the diversity of reasoning paths. Even if inputs are similar, the corresponding rationales (generated or ground truth) can differ in logic. Simple input similarity doesn't guarantee consistency in reasoning trajectories, making it harder for the model to extract a coherent learning signal. Random sampling, by introducing more diverse rationales, might incidentally prevent overfitting to a potentially inconsistent or irrelevant reasoning pattern.

#### 4.6. ID vs. OOD with Modern VLMs Revisited

For modern VLMs, the stark performance gap between ID and OOD observed with older models was significantly narrowed, and sometimes OOD even slightly outperformed ID. This suggests that modern VLMs, particularly when format inconsistency issues are addressed (e.g., using LLM judges), are more robust to distribution shifts in the support set. However, this robustness doesn't equate to effective MM-ICL; the overarching finding remains that few-shot performance often does not surpass zero-shot. This implies that while newer VLMs are less misled by answer formats, they still struggle to genuinely leverage demonstrations for learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research delivers a significant blow to the uncritical assumption that Vision-Language Models genuinely perform in-context learning by leveraging provided examples for deep understanding and reasoning. Its findings have profound implications for both the fundamental understanding of VLM capabilities and the future direction of multimodal AI research and development.

#### 5.1. Challenges a Core Assumption in VLM Research

The most critical contribution of this paper is its direct challenge to the widely held belief that VLMs exhibit ICL in a manner analogous to LLMs, particularly when it comes to complex tasks requiring reasoning. By demonstrating that current VLMs predominantly resort to "mimicking" superficial patterns rather than "reasoning" from demonstrations, the study calls for a re-evaluation of how VLM performance is interpreted in few-shot settings. It suggests that observed performance gains might not be due to genuine in-context learning of methodologies but rather a sophisticated form of pattern matching or format replication.

#### 5.2. Highlights Fundamental Limitations of Current VLMs

The consistent failure of diverse modern VLMs (both open-source and proprietary, and their reasoning variants) to consistently benefit from information-enriched demonstrations – even when rationales are provided and format inconsistencies are addressed – points to a fundamental weakness in their current architectures or training paradigms. Their insensitivity to rationale quality and the counterproductive effect of multimodal retrieval for reasoning models underscore that these models are not effectively processing or integrating the rich, structured information provided in demonstrations for deeper task understanding and generalization.

#### 5.3. Guides Future Research Directions

The limitations identified by this study offer clear avenues for future research:
- **Architectural Innovations:** There is a pressing need for novel VLM architectures that enable tighter cross-modal fusion, better integration of sequential information, and more sophisticated compositional reasoning within context. This might involve designing models that can explicitly learn and apply abstract rules or reasoning chains from examples.
- **Reasoning-Aware Retrieval:** Current retrieval methods, often based on input similarity, prove insufficient for reasoning-intensive MM-ICL. Future research should explore "reasoning-aware" retrieval strategies that prioritize retrieving examples with similar *reasoning paths* or logical structures, rather than just similar inputs or outputs.
- **Advanced Training Paradigms:** New training methods are required to instill genuine in-context learning capabilities. This could involve multi-modal interleaved Chain-of-Thought (CoT) training, where models are explicitly taught to integrate and generalize from multi-modal reasoning steps, rather than just generating them.
- **Rethinking Benchmarking:** Future MM-ICL benchmarks should adopt more rigorous methodologies, including widespread use of OOD settings and robust evaluation metrics (like LLM judges) that are less susceptible to superficial pattern matching and answer format nuances. The focus should shift from merely achieving high accuracy to demonstrating genuine transfer of understanding.

#### 5.4. Implications for Practical Applications

For practical VLM applications relying on few-shot prompting, the findings suggest caution. If VLMs do not truly learn in-context, then deploying them for complex, novel tasks based on a few examples might lead to unreliable or suboptimal performance. Developers might need to temper expectations about rapid, ICL-driven adaptation for intricate reasoning tasks. In certain scenarios, zero-shot prompting might be as effective, or even more reliable, than few-shot prompting if the models are not genuinely leveraging the demonstrations.

In conclusion, "Mimicking or Reasoning" provides a critical and timely analysis that reframes the discussion around MM-ICL. By exposing the shallow nature of current VLM in-context learning, it sets a clear agenda for the next generation of multimodal AI research, urging the community to move beyond surface-level performance metrics towards building truly intelligent and adaptable vision-language models.
