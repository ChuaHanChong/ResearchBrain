# Don't Waste Mistakes: Leveraging Negative RL-Groups via Confidence Reweighting

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces Likelihood Estimation with Negative Samples (LENS), a method that enhances Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) by deriving a confidence-weighted penalty from a maximum likelihood objective, allowing large language models to learn from previously discarded incorrect responses. LENS consistently improves performance on the MATH benchmark, particularly for harder problems, yielding gains of up to 2.54 Pass@1 and 2.64 Pass@16 percentage points for a Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct model.

## Problem

- Existing RLVR methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) discard 'negative groups' (batches of responses where none are correct), leading to wasted computation and reduced learning signals.
- Negative groups constitute a substantial portion of samples (up to 45% early in training), representing a major inefficiency, especially when models struggle with challenging problems.
- Traditional GRPO provides zero advantage for entirely incorrect groups, failing to offer fine-grained feedback or encourage exploration on difficult items.

## Method

- A theoretical framework is developed that reformulates policy learning as a ==Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)== problem for a reward model, connecting it to policy gradients.
- ==LENS== introduces a =='calibrated reward'== that assigns a non-zero, confidence-dependent penalty to incorrect responses, even within negative groups, turning wasted computation into a learning signal.
- The method integrates as a plug-and-play modification into GRPO, using ==length-normalized probabilities== for confidence and estimating a question difficulty factor to generate confidence-weighted advantages.

## Results

- LENS consistently outperforms the GRPO baseline across all evaluation metrics and models (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen-2.5-3B-Base) on the MATH benchmark.
- Performance gains are most pronounced on harder subsets of the MATH benchmark (Levels 4-5), demonstrating LENS's ability to foster exploration and improve on problems where the baseline saturates.
- LENS achieved Pass@1 improvements from **54.09%** to **56.63%** and Pass@16 from **72.70%** to **75.34%** for Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, indicating both single-shot accuracy and broader quality improvements in generated responses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Policy learning can be expressed as a statistical task of estimating a reward probability function, whose MLE gradient naturally provides a confidence-weighted learning signal for both correct and incorrect answers.
> - A model's confidence in its incorrect predictions provides a valuable signal; highly confident wrong answers should incur a larger penalty to encourage exploration and better policy calibration.
> - Leveraging even purely negative groups significantly contributes to performance gains, particularly on challenging reasoning tasks where initial correct answers are scarce.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is situated within the rapidly evolving field of fine-tuning large language models (LLMs) for complex reasoning tasks, particularly through Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). RLVR has emerged as a powerful paradigm, enabling LLMs to achieve significant performance gains on tasks like mathematical problem-solving and coding, where the correctness of a generated solution can be algorithmically verified.

A foundational algorithm in this landscape is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which is widely adopted for its efficiency and effectiveness in RLVR settings. GRPO works by sampling multiple responses for a given question (a "group") and using their relative rewards to compute advantage estimates, thereby avoiding the need for a separate value network (critic) as in traditional PPO.

However, a critical inefficiency inherent to GRPO and its variants is the treatment of "negative groups"—those groups of sampled responses where *none* of the generations are correct. In GRPO, such groups yield zero advantage and thus contribute no gradient signal to the policy update. This represents a substantial waste of computational resources, especially early in training or when tackling particularly challenging problems where correct answers are scarce. The paper highlights that negative groups can constitute up to 45% of samples early in training and remain around 35% even later, emphasizing the scale of this inefficiency.

The broader research landscape has seen various attempts to address the limitations of learning from negative samples in LLM reasoning. Some works explore asymmetric treatment of positives and negatives in REINFORCE-style training, such as reducing the signal from negative samples when data is off-policy or reweighting samples at the token level. Another direction focuses on mining sub-signals from entirely wrong completions, such as identifying correct intermediate steps in a chain of thought to assign fractional rewards. These approaches, however, often introduce complexities like labor-intensive evaluation of intermediate steps or rely on auxiliary reward models.

This paper distinguishes itself by offering a principled, theoretically-grounded approach derived from a maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) objective for reward modeling. It reinterprets the problem not as discarding negative samples, but as extracting valuable, confidence-weighted signals from them. By explicitly connecting reward modeling to policy optimization, the authors propose a modification to GRPO that leverages these overlooked negative groups without requiring additional supervision or complex sub-step verification. This positions LENS as an elegant and computationally efficient solution to a well-recognized problem in advanced LLM training, aiming to improve both performance and sample efficiency by "rescuing" previously wasted data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to address a significant inefficiency in current Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) methods, particularly Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), when applied to training large language models (LLMs) for reasoning tasks.

The core motivation stems from the observation that GRPO discards "negative groups"—batches of generated responses where no sample is correct. Such groups, which are prevalent at the start of training and for harder problems, contribute zero advantage and thus no gradient signal to the model. This leads to:

- **Wasted Computation:** Significant computational resources are expended on generating these incorrect responses, only for them to be discarded from the learning process. The paper quantifies this, showing that up to 45% of groups can be negative.
- **Reduced Learning Signal:** The model misses out on valuable information that could be gleaned from its mistakes, particularly about *how* it is making mistakes. Simply labeling an answer as "wrong" without further differentiation limits the granularity of the learning signal.
- **Suboptimal Exploration:** On challenging problems, the model might struggle to generate any correct answers initially. If all these attempts are simply discarded, the model receives no guidance on how to explore the solution space more effectively, potentially hindering its ability to escape local optima and improve on difficult items.

Given these inefficiencies, the paper poses a critical question: **Can we learn from negative groups in a principled way without requiring additional supervision?**

The key objectives are thus:
1. **Develop a theoretical framework** that formally connects reward modeling (discriminating correct from incorrect answers) with policy optimization, explicitly showing how information from negative samples can be integrated.
2. **Derive a modified reward function or value function** from this framework that assigns non-zero, confidence-dependent penalties to incorrect responses, especially within negative groups.
3. **Propose a practical, plug-and-play variant of GRPO**, termed Likelihood Estimation with Negative Samples (LENS), that leverages these confidence-weighted signals to make negative groups informative.
4. **Empirically demonstrate** that LENS consistently outperforms the standard GRPO baseline, particularly on harder reasoning tasks, thereby proving its effectiveness in improving efficiency and performance.
5. **Increase exploration** on hard negatives by discouraging overconfident wrong answers, thereby fostering a more robust and better-calibrated policy.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to transform previously wasted computational effort into a valuable learning signal, leading to more efficient training and ultimately, more capable LLMs in reasoning domains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this paper is characterized by a theoretically grounded approach that bridges reward modeling with policy optimization to leverage negative samples in Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). The core idea is to reframe the problem of policy learning through the lens of Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) of a reward model.

#### 3.1. From Policy Learning to Reward Modeling via MLE

The authors start by defining the optimal policy $\pi^*$ as one that maximizes the expected reward based on a ground-truth probability function $p^*(q, o)$, which indicates the correctness of an answer $o$ for a question $q$. They then establish a fundamental relationship: $p^*(q, o) = \frac{1}{D(q)} \pi^*(o | q)$, where $D(q)$ is a normalizing factor related to the "difficulty" of the question. This allows policy learning to be re-expressed as a statistical task of estimating $p^*(q, o)$.

To learn $p^*(q, o)$, they propose using a maximum likelihood objective (or cross-entropy minimization) for a parametric model $p_\theta(q, o)$. By plugging in the reparameterization $p_\theta(q, o) = \frac{1}{D(q)} \pi_\theta(o | q)$, they derive an equivalent MLE loss function:
$L(\theta) = - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ r_i \cdot \log \pi_\theta(o_i | q_i) + (1 - r_i) \cdot \log \left(1 - \frac{\pi_\theta(o_i | q_i)}{D(q_i)}\right) \right]$.

#### 3.2. Calibrating Policy Gradient via MLE

The crucial insight comes from analyzing the gradient of this MLE loss. The authors demonstrate that the gradient of $L(\theta)$ is equivalent to a policy gradient expression:
$\nabla_\theta L(\theta) = - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left[ r_i - (1 - r_i) \frac{\pi_\theta(o_i | q_i)}{D(q_i) - \pi_\theta(o_i | q_i)} \right] \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o_i | q_i)$.

This gradient naturally splits into two terms:
1. **Positive Samples (r=1):** $r_i \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o_i | q_i)$, which is the standard policy gradient component, encouraging the model to generate correct answers.
2. **Negative Samples (r=0):** $-(1 - r_i) \frac{\pi_\theta(o_i | q_i)}{D(q_i) - \pi_\theta(o_i | q_i)} \cdot \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(o_i | q_i)$. This term is non-zero even when $r_i=0$, providing a learning signal for incorrect answers. It acts as a "confidence-weighted penalty."

This revelation leads to the definition of a **calibrated reward** $\tilde{r}$:
$\tilde{r} = r - (1 - r) \frac{\pi_\theta(o | q)}{D(q) - \pi_\theta(o | q)}$.
When $r=1$, $\tilde{r}=1$. When $r=0$ (i.e., an incorrect response), the reward is a negative value proportional to the model's confidence $\pi_\theta(o|q)$ and inversely proportional to $(D(q) - \pi_\theta(o|q))$. A higher confidence in a wrong answer results in a larger negative reward, implicitly discouraging overconfident mistakes and promoting exploration. This mechanism is termed Likelihood Estimation with Negative Samples (LENS).

#### 3.3. Confidence-Weighted Value Function

The authors further formalize this by showing that the MLE gradient is identical to the gradient of a modified value function $J_{MLE}(\pi_\theta) = J^+(\pi_\theta) - J^-(\pi_\theta)$:
- $J^+(\pi_\theta)$ is the standard expected reward.
- $J^-(\pi_\theta)$ is a penalty term for incorrect answers, weighted by a function $w(z) = \frac{1}{z} \log \frac{1}{1-z} - 1$, where $z = \pi_\theta(o|q)/D(q)$. This weight function ensures that the penalty is most severe when the policy is highly confident but wrong (as $\pi_\theta \to D(q)$, $w \to \infty$) and negligible when uncertain and wrong (as $\pi_\theta \to 0$, $w \to 0$).

#### 3.4. Proposed Modification to GRPO (LENS Implementation)

LENS is proposed as a direct, drop-in modification to GRPO. Instead of using the binary $r_i$ reward, LENS replaces it with the calibrated reward $\tilde{r}_i$ from Equation (10). These calibrated rewards are then used to compute advantages in GRPO.

**Practical Considerations:**
- **$\pi_\theta$ Term:** For long LLM generations, raw sequence probabilities decay rapidly. To mitigate this, length-normalized (geometric-mean) probability $\bar{\pi}_\theta(o|q) = \pi_\theta(o|q)^{1/|o|}$ is used.
- **Estimating $D(q)$:** The difficulty factor $D(q)$ is crucial. It's estimated via importance sampling as $D_{imp}(q) = \left( \frac{1}{G} \sum_{i=1}^G \frac{r_i}{\pi_{\theta_{old}}(o_i|q)} \right)^{-1}$ for mixed groups. For numerical stability, $D(q)$ is conservatively set to $\max(D_{imp}(q), 2 \cdot \max_{j} \bar{\pi}_{\theta_{old}}(o_j|q))$. For purely negative groups (where $D_{imp}(q)$ is undefined), it falls back to $2 \cdot \max_{j} \bar{\pi}_{\theta_{old}}(o_j|q)$. This ensures a positive denominator and bounds calibrated rewards in $[-1, 1]$.
- **Handling Invariance:** To maintain GRPO's sign invariance property, negative calibrated rewards are scaled by $1/G$.
- **Auxiliary Signal for Negative Groups:** Since the signal from negative groups is based solely on confidence differences, it's treated as a weaker, auxiliary signal. An additional hyperparameter $\alpha$ is introduced to down-weight their contribution to the total objective: $J_{ours} = J_{GRPO}[\text{mixed groups}] + \alpha \cdot J_{GRPO}[\text{negative groups}]$.

This principled derivation and practical implementation allow LENS to leverage negative groups effectively, converting previously wasted compute into valuable learning signals that encourage better-calibrated policies and exploration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of LENS demonstrates consistent and significant improvements over the GRPO baseline, particularly on challenging reasoning tasks. The experiments were conducted on the MATH benchmark using two distinct LLMs: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen-2.5-3B-Base, and two different training datasets (MATH+DAPO and MATH+Numina 1.5) to ensure robustness.

**Key Findings:**

1. **Consistent Performance Gains:** LENS consistently outperforms the GRPO baseline across all evaluation metrics and both models tested. This is evident in the training curves (Figure 5 and Figure 6 in Appendix E), where LENS achieves higher accuracy throughout the training process for both Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen-2.5-3B-Base.

2. **Significant Gains on Harder Items:** A crucial finding is that the improvements brought by LENS are concentrated on the more difficult subsets of the MATH benchmark, specifically "Levels 4-5." On these harder questions, where initially generating a correct answer is rare (leading to many negative groups), LENS shows substantial additional gains compared to GRPO. This supports the hypothesis that leveraging negative groups fosters increased exploration, which is critical for solving problems where the initial policy struggles. As the GRPO curve often saturates, LENS continues to improve on these hard instances.

3. **Improved Pass@k Metrics:** LENS consistently achieves higher Pass@k scores for all $k \in \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16\}$ (Table 1 and Table 3 in Appendix E). This indicates that the algorithm not only improves the likelihood of generating a single correct answer (Pass@1) but also enhances the diversity and quality of the model's top $k$ generations. This is a direct reflection of the confidence-based design promoting broader exploration rather than over-exploiting narrow, potentially flawed, high-confidence paths. For instance, on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with MATH+DAPO, Pass@1 improved from 54.09 to 56.63, and Pass@16 from 72.70 to 75.34.

4. **Training Stability:** The training process with LENS remains stable for over 1,000 steps, indicating that the confidence-reweighting mechanism does not introduce instability or collapse, even without additional ad hoc tricks.

5. **Ablation Study Insights:** An ablation study (Table 2 in Appendix D.2) provides further insights into the contribution of different components:
 - Modifying rewards only in **mixed groups** (groups with at least one correct answer) also yields improvements, primarily at Pass@1. This suggests that even within mixed groups, differentiating between incorrect answers based on confidence helps.
 - Modifying rewards only in **negative groups** shows strong improvements, in some cases nearly matching the full LENS performance. This highlights that a substantial portion of the overall gain indeed comes from making previously discarded negative groups informative. The full LENS, combining both, generally achieves the best performance.

In summary, the results convincingly demonstrate that LENS is a principled and practical method to "rescue" negative groups, converting wasted computational effort into meaningful learning signals, leading to stronger, more robust LLMs for reasoning tasks, particularly those requiring extensive exploration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Don’t Waste Mistakes: Leveraging Negative RL-Groups via Confidence Reweighting" carries significant implications and potential impact across several dimensions of large language model (LLM) development and reinforcement learning.

1. **Enhanced Efficiency in LLM Training:** The most direct impact is on the efficiency of RLVR training for LLMs. By effectively leveraging "negative groups" that would otherwise be discarded, LENS converts wasted computational cycles into valuable learning signals. This is critical as LLM training is notoriously resource-intensive. Reducing the "waste" in data generation and processing translates to faster convergence, lower training costs, and potentially the ability to train more complex models or on larger datasets within existing computational budgets. This is particularly relevant for industrial labs like Meta Superintelligence Labs, where resource optimization is paramount.

2. **Improved Performance on Challenging Reasoning Tasks:** The empirical results consistently show that LENS delivers significant performance gains, especially on harder problems (MATH Levels 4-5). This suggests that the method effectively addresses a crucial bottleneck in LLM reasoning: the ability to learn from initial failures and explore complex solution spaces. By penalizing overconfident incorrect answers, LENS encourages a more nuanced and exploratory policy, which is essential for tackling real-world problems that often involve intricate, multi-step reasoning.

3. **Principled Exploration:** LENS offers a theoretically grounded mechanism for principled exploration. Unlike heuristic approaches, its derivation from a Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) objective provides a clear understanding of *why* and *how* confidence-weighted penalties guide the policy. This intrinsic exploration mechanism, driven by the model's own confidence in its incorrect predictions, is a powerful alternative or complement to explicit exploration bonuses (e.g., entropy-based or RND-based) and can lead to more robust and well-calibrated models.

4. **Generality and Adaptability:** The method is demonstrated to be effective across different LLM architectures (Llama and Qwen) and datasets, indicating its generality. Furthermore, its "plug-and-play" nature with negligible computational overhead makes it easy to integrate into existing GRPO-based RLVR pipelines. This low barrier to adoption means that the benefits of LENS can be readily realized by a wide range of practitioners and researchers.

5. **New Research Directions:** The paper opens several avenues for future research:
 - **Preference-Aware Variants:** The framework can be extended to incorporate preferences over correct generations (e.g., shorter answers). Further exploration could lead to models that not only solve problems but do so in preferred or more human-like ways.
 - **Hyperparameter-Free Objectives:** The current method introduces a hyperparameter $\alpha$ for down-weighting negative groups. Future work could aim to develop a theoretically derived, hyperparameter-free objective that optimally balances signals from mixed and negative groups.
 - **Nonbinary Reward Signals:** The framework theoretically covers nonbinary reward signals. Investigating this could extend LENS to tasks where partial credit or graded feedback is available, broadening its applicability beyond simple pass/fail verification.
 - **Deeper Understanding of Confidence:** The confidence-weighting aspect might spur further research into how LLM confidence can be reliably estimated and leveraged for various training and inference time objectives.

In essence, "Don't Waste Mistakes" provides a practical, efficient, and theoretically sound method that not only improves the state-of-the-art in LLM reasoning but also deepens our understanding of how negative feedback can be effectively harnessed in complex learning environments. Its impact is likely to be felt in both the fundamental research into AI capabilities and the engineering practices for deploying highly capable LLMs.
