# Visibility-Aware Mobile Grasping in Dynamic Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> A hierarchical mobile grasping system from the National University of Singapore enables robots to perform grasping tasks safely in dynamic, unknown, and cluttered environments by tightly coupling real-time perception and whole-body motion planning. The system achieved 68.8% success in simulated static environments and 58.0% in dynamic ones, outperforming decoupled baselines by over 18% in dynamic settings.

## Problem

- Mobile manipulators struggle to perform grasping tasks safely and effectively in dynamic, cluttered, and unknown real-world environments due to severe partial observability.
- Ensuring robot safety is challenging when operating with a limited field-of-view, as systems must both perceive the target and proactively monitor for unseen or dynamic obstacles.
- Existing approaches often decouple navigation from manipulation or assume static, known environments, leading to poor performance and high collision risks in unpredictable settings with underspecified goals.

## Method

- A unified, ==hierarchical== system operating within a ==receding-horizon framework== that tightly integrates a subgoal policy, an active perception policy, and a whole-body motion policy.
- The adaptive ==subgoal policy== (πg) generates and refines grasp, pre-grasp, and observation subgoals to guide the robot and recover from runtime failures in partially known environments.
- An ==active perception policy== (πv) optimizes gaze direction by maximizing visibility of an ==importance field==, shifting focus from target observation (during planning) to velocity-weighted future swept-volume monitoring (during execution) for safety.

## Results

- The integrated system achieved success rates of **68.8%** in simulated static and **58.0%** in dynamic environments, with comparable real-world performance (**65.0%** static, **55.0%** dynamic).
- It demonstrated an **18.0%** higher success rate and significantly lower collision rates (**6.3%** vs. **17.5%**) in dynamic simulations compared to a decoupled navigation-and-manipulation baseline.
- Key bottlenecks identified include inaccuracies in grasp detection (**13-16%** failures), perception noise leading to planning failures (**8-14%**), and blind spots in active perception causing collisions with low-lying or thin dynamic obstacles (**6-10%** failures).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Tightly coupling real-time perception and whole-body motion planning within a unified, hierarchical framework is crucial for safe and successful mobile grasping in dynamic, partially observed environments.
> - Velocity-aware active perception, which prioritizes observing collision-critical regions of the robot's future swept volume, significantly enhances safety by improving the detection of dynamic obstacles.
> - An adaptive hierarchical subgoal generation policy is critical for handling underspecified grasping tasks and recovering from runtime failures, enabling task progress with limited environmental information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses the challenges of mobile grasping in complex, real-world environments characterized by clutter, uncertainty, and dynamism. Traditional mobile manipulation systems often assume complete knowledge of the environment or static conditions, relying on pre-built maps and decoupling navigation from manipulation. Such approaches struggle with severe partial observability and the presence of unknown, dynamic obstacles, which can compromise safety and task completion.

Previous work in mobile manipulation has made progress in controlled settings, often using pre-built 3D maps and handling dynamic elements through simplified 2D detection. However, these systems are limited in environments with rich 3D structures and frequent changes, and they typically require well-defined goal configurations. Approaches focusing on partial observability and dynamic environments often operate in simplified simulations or lower-dimensional configuration spaces, thus not fully addressing real-time whole-body motion safety in cluttered 3D scenes. The prevailing strategy of decomposing the problem into base placement optimization, while computationally efficient, relies on extensive environment knowledge and struggles when goals must be refined incrementally.

The integration of perception and motion planning has been explored through visibility-aware planning and active perception. Early visibility-aware planners enforced motion constraints in observed free space, but often assumed static environments. More recent efforts have jointly optimized gaze and motion for continuous target visibility, but usually for fixed manipulation goals. Active perception methods select viewpoints to maximize information gain for exploration or task-specific grasping. However, many of these approaches either separate viewpoint selection from motion execution or do not explicitly consider swept-volume safety during dynamic replanning.

Learning-based mobile manipulation methods offer impressive capabilities, leveraging modular systems, reinforcement learning, and vision-language-action models. While showing promise, these data-driven techniques often operate without explicit geometric reasoning for collision risk and can exhibit sensitivity to distributional shifts, limiting their generalization to novel layouts or objects.

This work contributes to the field by proposing a unified, hierarchical system that tightly couples perception and planning, explicitly addressing the trade-off between "seeing" (reducing environmental uncertainty) and "moving" (achieving task progress) under limited field-of-view and dynamic conditions. It seeks to overcome the limitations of decoupled, static-environment, or purely reactive/learning-based approaches by integrating real-time whole-body motion planning, velocity-aware active perception, and adaptive subgoal generation within a receding-horizon framework, thereby prioritizing safety and robustness in unknown, dynamic 3D environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a mobile manipulator to perform grasping tasks effectively and safely in dynamic, unknown, and cluttered environments, such as a typical household setting. The robot is tasked with picking up an object (e.g., a can from a shelf) given minimal prior information about its surroundings.

The motivation stems from several critical challenges:
1. **Partial Observability**: The robot operates with a limited field-of-view sensor (e.g., a movable head camera) and lacks a complete, pre-built map of the environment. Most obstacles are initially unknown.
2. **Dynamic Environment**: The environment contains unobserved dynamic obstacles (e.g., a person walking by, objects appearing suddenly) whose trajectories are unknown. These dynamic elements can intersect the robot's planned path during manipulation.
3. **Underspecified Goals**: Grasp configurations for the target object are not fully specified upfront and must be refined as more environmental information becomes available.
4. **Safety under Uncertainty**: The fundamental challenge is to ensure the robot's safety by minimizing the risk of collision between its swept volume and unseen or dynamic obstacles, even those in its blind spots, while simultaneously making progress towards the grasping task.
5. **Conflicting Objectives (Visibility Allocation Problem)**: The robot faces an inherent trade-off. To perceive and refine the underspecified grasping goal, it must direct its sensors towards the target. Concurrently, to approach the target safely, it must scan the broader environment to detect potential dynamic obstacles that might block its path. These "seeing" (reducing uncertainty) and "moving" (task progress) objectives are often in direct conflict.
6. **Real-time Adaptation**: After acquiring new observations, the robot must update its internal representation of the environment and replan its whole-body trajectory in real-time to respond to newly modeled obstacles or refined target configurations. Existing systems often struggle with this real-time coupling of perception and planning in high-dimensional configuration spaces.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a unified, hierarchical mobile grasping system designed to integrate perception and planning within a closed-loop framework, allowing for iterative optimization of whole-body trajectories and gaze directions at runtime.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system operates within a receding-horizon framework, iteratively refining the robot's belief state, which includes its configuration ($q_t$), a partial estimation of the environment map ($M_t$), and the estimated grasp configuration ($g_t$). This framework comprises three core policies: a subgoal policy ($\pi_g$), an active perception policy ($\pi_v$), and a whole-body motion policy ($\pi_r$), supported by localization and control modules.

**A. Subgoal Policy ($\pi_g$)**
This hierarchical policy adaptively generates and refines subgoals, guiding the robot through exploration and recovering from runtime failures. It decomposes the task into three steps:
1. **Grasp Goal Sampling**: The system first attempts to achieve a direct grasp without base motion. It uses a learning-based grasp network (Contact-GraspNet) to generate candidate end-effector poses from the observed point cloud. These candidates are then filtered based on a pre-computed capability map (Appendix C) to ensure kinematic reachability, inverse kinematics (IK) feasibility, and collision-freeness against the current map. Diverse poses are selected using farthest point sampling and ranked by a proximity-weighted score.
2. **Pre-grasp Goal Sampling**: If direct grasping is not feasible, the policy samples whole-body configurations (base pose $q_b$, torso height $h$, end-effector pose $T_{ee}$) to enable base repositioning.
 - **Base Pose ($p_{geo}$)**: Samples collision-free base poses from a 2D signed distance field derived from the 3D belief point cloud, biased towards low-cost regions within the manipulation radius of the target.
 - **Torso Height ($p_{torso}$)**: Queries a pre-built torso height map (Appendix C) to select the optimal height that maximizes end-effector reachability for the sampled base pose.
 - **End-Effector Pose ($p_{ee}$)**: Samples end-effector poses on a sphere around the target, filtered by the capability map to ensure kinematic feasibility.
 - Each sampled configuration is validated for IK feasibility, collision-freeness, and self-occlusion.
3. **Observe Goal Sampling**: When manipulation is blocked by occlusion or unknown geometry, the policy generates subgoals for gathering additional observations. It samples base poses at a fixed standoff distance from the target to maintain visibility, paired with a robust arm configuration, to improve the belief state ($M_t$).

**B. Active Perception Policy ($\pi_v$)**
This policy governs the robot's gaze to balance the needs for target observation (for grasping) and swept-volume monitoring (for safety).
1. **State-Dependent Gaze Optimization**: Gaze direction (sensor orientation) is optimized by maximizing visibility of an importance field $\Phi(x)$ which adapts based on the robot's current phase.
 - **During planning (no trajectory executed)**: $\Phi(x)$ focuses on the target region to facilitate grasp pose generation.
 - **During execution (trajectory $\xi_t$ is executed)**: $\Phi(x)$ shifts to the robot's future swept volume $V(\xi_t)$, prioritizing collision-critical regions. This prioritization is weighted by temporal proximity (near-future areas) and velocity (high-velocity areas) along the planned trajectory (Equation 3).
2. **Dynamic Environment Mapping**: The environment map ($M_t$) is maintained as a point cloud, continuously updated through ray casting using new RGB-D observations. This process adds new perceived surface points as obstacles and removes previously occupied space along each ray, allowing the robot to detect moving obstacles. The update pipeline includes ground plane removal and robot self-filtering (Appendix E).

**C. Whole-Body Motion Policy ($\pi_r$)**
This policy is responsible for generating fast, smooth, and collision-free whole-body trajectories for reactive execution in dynamic environments.
1. **Real-time Planning**: It combines hierarchical planning (decomposing base and arm motions) with vectorized collision checking. Base placements are sampled using a non-holonomic steering kernel (Hybrid A* for global guidance, Reeds-Shepp curves for local connections). Arm configurations are connected using RRT-Connect. Synchronized whole-body configurations are then validated in parallel using SIMD operations for acceleration.
2. **Path Refinement**: Raw paths from RRT-Connect undergo randomized shortcutting to remove redundant waypoints, B-spline fitting for smoothing, and dense re-interpolation to ensure collision-free execution. This typically achieves planning times of 50-80 ms, enabling responsive replanning.

**D. Localization and Control**
- **Localization**: In simulation, ground truth poses are used. For the real robot, Adaptive Monte Carlo Localization (AMCL) tracks the robot's base pose against a pre-built 2D occupancy map, separating static localization from dynamic obstacle handling.
- **Trajectory Tracking**: A Model Predictive Control (MPC) scheme (Appendix F) converts planned whole-body trajectories into 10-dimensional velocity commands (base linear/angular, torso, 7 arm joints) at 20 Hz. This controller handles whole-body coordination, generating smooth velocity profiles and seamlessly accepting updated trajectories for reactive replanning.

Replanning is triggered whenever map updates invalidate the current trajectory or subgoals are reached, ensuring continuous adaptation to the evolving environment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The system was evaluated in 400 randomized simulation scenarios and 40 real-world settings across diverse indoor locations.

**A. Simulation Results**
- **Overall Success Rate**: The full system achieved a success rate of 68.8% in unknown static environments and 58.0% in dynamic environments.
- **Comparison with Navigation-and-Manipulation Baseline**: The proposed integrated approach significantly outperformed the decoupled navigation-and-manipulation baseline. It achieved 22.8% higher success in unknown static environments and 18.0% higher in dynamic environments.
 - The decoupled baseline suffered from higher planning failures (20.5% vs. 8.8%) because it committed to base positions without sufficient environmental knowledge.
 - Collision rates were also notably higher for the baseline (17.5% vs. 6.3%) due to restricted arm-only motions from a fixed base in cluttered spaces.
- **Impact of High-Level Policy ($\pi_g$)**: Ablation of the high-level subgoal policy (Direct Grasping baseline) resulted in the largest performance drop, with success rates falling by 40.5% (unknown static) and 32.3% (dynamic). Planning failures increased by 4.5 to 6 times, indicating the crucial role of adaptive goal generation and failure recovery.
- **Impact of Velocity-Aware Active Perception**: The velocity-agnostic baseline, which omitted velocity-awareness from gaze optimization, showed an increase in collision failures (from 9.8% to 13.8%) in dynamic environments. This suggests that prioritizing observation of faster-moving robot parts contributes to improved safety.
- **Failure Analysis (Simulation)**:
 - **Grasp Detection** (13-16% failures) was identified as a primary bottleneck. The grasp network often generated unreachable poses, especially for out-of-distribution objects or in constrained spaces, due to a lack of kinematic awareness and degradation in accuracy with distance. "Infeasible" grasps accounted for 43.5% of grasp failures.
 - **Planning Failures** (8-14%) arose from perception noise creating false obstacles, the planner failing to find existing paths within time limits, insufficient path smoothness leading to controller tracking errors, or paths being planned too close to obstacles.
 - **Collision Failures** (6-10%) were attributed to blind spots in the active perception system (missing thin or non-convex objects), conservative ground plane removal filtering low-lying obstacles, and execution uncertainty (e.g., translational drift during in-place rotations). The gap between static and dynamic environments showed an increase in collisions (6.3% to 9.8%) and planning failures (8.8% to 14.0%) in dynamic settings.
- **Path Efficiency (SPL)**: The full system achieved the highest Success weighted by Path Length (SPL) scores (62.2% static, 48.0% dynamic), indicating both higher success rates and more efficient paths relative to optimal collision-free paths.
- **Obstacle Distance Analysis**: Success rate increased sharply from 44.2% at 0.5m obstacle appearance distance to 60.8% at 1.5m, then plateaued. This suggests that the system requires a minimum reaction distance of approximately 1.5m to achieve near-optimal collision avoidance performance against suddenly appearing obstacles.

**B. Real-World Results**
- The system achieved comparable success rates to simulation in real-world environments: 65.0% in static and 55.0% in dynamic settings.
- **Real-world Failure Sources**:
 - **Planning failures**: Primarily attributed to noisy observations (e.g., floating points in point clouds) caused by data synchronization issues between localization and depth sensors.
 - **Collision failures**: Resulted from ground plane filtering inadvertently removing parts of low-lying objects (e.g., chair legs) and higher execution noise (e.g., robot getting stuck and then suddenly accelerating, disrupting pose estimation).
 - **Perception failures**: Occurred due to segmentation or detection errors leading to incorrect grasping.

**C. System-Level Comparisons (Appendix K)**
- **CapMap Placement**: This sequential navigation-then-manipulation baseline, which uses manipulation-aware base placement, achieved 44.25% (static) and 40.00% (dynamic). It suffered from high collision rates (23.25%/23.50%) and planning failures, indicating that over-constraining base placement without integrated optimization hinders safety and reachability.
- **Closed-Loop Replanning**: This integrated approach, lacking structured task management, achieved 61.25% (static) and 56.75% (dynamic). While better than sequential designs, it fell short of the full system due to higher planning failures (15.25%/18.50%), suggesting that continuous replanning without high-level task coordination can lead to oscillations and non-convergence.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates a significant advancement in mobile grasping capabilities for robots operating in complex, unstructured, and dynamic human environments. By proposing a unified, hierarchical framework that tightly couples perception and planning, the work addresses fundamental limitations of previous decoupled or static-environment approaches.

The core contributions and potential impacts are:
- **Enhanced Safety and Robustness**: The system's ability to reduce collision rates effectively, particularly in dynamic environments, is a key outcome. The integration of velocity-aware active perception and real-time whole-body replanning directly contributes to safer robot operation in unpredictable surroundings, which is critical for real-world deployment in human-shared spaces.
- **Improved Task Success in Unstructured Environments**: The achieved success rates, both in simulation and real-world, in environments with unknown layouts and dynamic obstacles, indicate a notable step towards more robust and autonomous mobile manipulators. The hierarchical subgoal generation policy is shown to be crucial for navigating task complexity and recovering from runtime failures, enabling progress even with partial information and underspecified goals.
- **Addressing the "Seeing vs. Moving" Trade-off**: The work provides a practical solution to the inherent conflict between acquiring necessary task-specific information (e.g., grasp pose refinement) and ensuring environmental awareness for safe navigation. The state-dependent gaze optimization demonstrates an effective strategy for managing sensor resources.
- **Foundation for Future Complex Robotic Tasks**: The modular architecture of the system suggests its extensibility to more complex scenarios, such as multi-robot coordination, where shared environmental awareness and coordinated actions are vital, or contact-rich manipulation tasks that demand precise interaction and continuous environmental feedback.
- **Guiding Future Research Directions**: The identified bottlenecks, particularly in grasp detection and robustness to perception noise and execution uncertainty, provide clear avenues for future work. Integrating predictive models for human and object motion, exploring advanced grasping mechanisms (e.g., multi-fingered hands), and developing probabilistic completeness guarantees for visibility-constrained planning are logical next steps that could further enhance the system's capabilities and reliability in highly dynamic and uncertain settings.

Overall, this research contributes to the development of more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems, pushing the boundaries of mobile manipulation towards practical and safe deployment in diverse real-world applications.
