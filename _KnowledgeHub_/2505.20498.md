# ControlTac: Scaling Tactile Data with Physically Controlled Tactile Image Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> ControlTac is a two-stage controllable diffusion framework that synthesizes realistic vision-based tactile images from a single reference image, conditioned on a target 3D contact force and a 2D contact-pose mask. A force-conditioned DiT first reshapes elastomer deformation, then a ControlNet-style adapter relocates contact. The augmented data improves force and pose estimation, reaches up to 90% tight-tolerance insertion success, and boosts imitation learning.

## Problem

- Collecting large-scale vision-based tactile data is costly because it requires physical contact across many poses and forces, and gel and lighting differences hinder reuse across sensor instances.
- Tactile simulators such as Taxim suffer a large sim-to-real gap from imperfect soft-body modeling and optical inconsistencies, producing unrealistic images that transfer poorly to dynamic real tasks.
- Text-to-tactile and vision-to-tactile generators lack physical controllability: they produce one low-fidelity image per contact location, unable to vary force or pose for downstream tasks.

## Method

- **Force-Control Generation** stage: a ==SANA autoencoder== encodes the background-subtracted reference image, and a ==DiT diffusion model== conditioned on the relative 3D force vector synthesizes force-adjusted elastomer deformation and shading.
- **Pose-Guided Adapter**: a ==ControlNet-style adapter== on the frozen force backbone takes a fixed object-specific contact mask, moved by 2D rigid transforms, placing contact at target poses without entangling force deformation.
- ==Trajectory-level tactile augmentation== for imitation learning: one sampled force offset shared across each teleoperated trajectory, plus per-frame jitter, regenerates tactile frames while keeping recorded actions unchanged.

## Results

- Image quality: MSE **23** and SSIM **0.83** on seen objects versus **1054**/**0.68** for Taxim simulation, **239**/**0.74** for Sim2Real, and **31**/**0.81** for the joint Hybrid ablation; **29**/**0.81** on unseen AnyTouch2 objects.
- Augmenting **one-third** of real FeelAnyForce data matches full-dataset force MAE; pose estimators trained only on generated data beat real-data training (cylinder X/Y/angle **4/5/3** vs **8/8/4**); classification reaches **0.99** versus simulator **0.92**.
- Real robot: **90%**, **85%**, and **75%** insertion success at 3 mm tolerance for cylinder, cross/T-shape, and Type-C connector; ACT peg-in-hole success rises from **48%** to **72%** with force augmentation at 50 demonstrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Anchoring generation on one real sensor reading and editing only physical factors preserves texture and illumination fidelity far better than rendering tactile signals from scratch or from other modalities.
> - Decoupling physical factors, training force first and adding pose via an adapter on the frozen backbone, beats joint conditioning, because jointly learned controls entangle and force changes distort contact geometry.
> - Observation-space augmentation cannot substitute for spatial coverage: tactile augmentation helped imitation learning only once real demonstrations spanned object and hole locations, and added little once real data saturated.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

In a Pick-and-Peg-in-Hole task, a robot must reach and grasp a cylinder, then insert it into a target receptacle. Teaching it with touch is expensive because collecting tactile images across many real contacts is costly. CONTROLTAC's approach is to start from a real touch image and ask a generator to change the force and the contact location. In a real-robot imitation-learning comparison with 50 real demonstrations, the nine-augmentation-copy setting achieved 72% success versus 48% without augmentation. Each policy was evaluated in 25 real-robot trials. This result describes that task and setting. It is not a claim of statistical significance, and the paper says augmentation is less effective in very limited-data settings.

The authors' real-world Pick-and-Peg-in-Hole imitation-learning demonstration. In this task, the robot reaches and grasps a cylinder, then inserts it into a target receptacle.

### 2. Why tactile data runs short

Vision-based tactile sensors use cameras to capture how a soft gel deforms. Covering many poses and forces therefore requires physical interactions. Gel and lighting differences also make data harder to reuse across sensors. Existing approaches have trade-offs. Simulation can have a sim-to-real gap, and free-form generation may lack physical control.

The paper's proposal goes beyond producing more synthetic images. It uses a real reference image as an anchor, preserving useful real-image details while explicitly varying physical conditions. "Single reference" describes inference after pretraining. Each request supplies one reference image, a target force, and, when pose control is used, a target mask. The generator is not trained from one image.

### 3. Separate force from pose

The generator works in two stages. First, it subtracts the sensor background to isolate contact information. It then encodes the reference into a latent representation, and a diffusion generator conditioned on a relative 3D force change produces a force-adjusted image. Second, an object-specific binary contact mask is transformed with 2D rigid motions to indicate the target contact location and orientation. A pose-guided adapter then steers the force-conditioned generator to that pose. The mask's shape and scale stay fixed. Its alignment is prepared manually, and the reported training alignment precision is ±3 pixels and ±1°.

(a) A reference tactile image passes through background subtraction, an autoencoder, a force-conditional generator, and a decoder to produce a force-adjusted image. (b) A contact mask and pose-guided adapter steer the force-conditioned model to an output image.

A plausible alternative to this ordering is the paper's HYBRID ablation, which jointly conditions on force and pose. Two metrics are used: pixel-wise MSE measures pixel-level image error (lower is better), and SSIM measures structural similarity (higher is better). On unseen FeelAnyForce objects, CONTROLTAC reports an MSE of 26 ± 3 and an SSIM of 0.79 ± 0.04. HYBRID reports 37 ± 6 and 0.75 ± 0.04. This supports the full sequential method over joint conditioning in that image-generation setting. It does not isolate decoupling as the only cause of the improvement.

### 4. What augmentation can and cannot do

In the robot experiment, policies receive visual and tactile observations. Force offsets augment the tactile observations along existing trajectories, while the actions are retained. The policy experiment uses only force-conditioned augmentation because real-time contact masks are unavailable.

Results vary non-monotonically with the amount of augmentation, so more generated data does not always help. When demonstrations are very sparse, spatial-coverage problems remain. Augmentation changes tactile observations along existing trajectories; it does not invent new robot actions or spatial trajectories.

CONTROLTAC offers a way to create more varied vision-based tactile training data without physically collecting a new touch for every force and contact location. Controlling force and pose can enrich tactile observations along demonstrated robot behavior. The approach does not supply missing trajectories, pose control still relies on manually prepared masks, and the method is for vision-based tactile sensors.
