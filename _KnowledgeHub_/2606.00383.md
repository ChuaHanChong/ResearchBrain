# Behavior Cloning of MPC for 3-DOF Robotic Manipulators

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Waterloo developed a behavior cloning approach to approximate Model Predictive Control (MPC) policies for a 3-degree-of-freedom robotic manipulator. Their method achieved a three-fold reduction in inference latency, reaching approximately 1 millisecond per control step, and a 25% decrease in CPU utilization compared to the original MPC, with an average final tracking error of 2.9 centimeters.

## Problem

- Model Predictive Control (MPC) faces significant computational burden, making it challenging for real-time control in resource-constrained robotic systems.
- Robotic manipulators require fast, consistent control loop execution for dynamic interaction, which traditional MPC's fluctuating latency often hinders.
- The need for computationally efficient and real-time capable control policies for complex robotic tasks.

## Method

- A ==behavior cloning== framework was employed to train neural networks to mimic an "expert" hierarchical controller, which combined Inverse Kinematics (IK) and Model Predictive Control (**MPC**).
- A dataset of expert demonstrations, including current robot state, target position, and MPC-generated torques, was collected from a MuJoCo simulation.
- Various neural network architectures, including Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) like GRUs, were systematically evaluated for their ability to predict MPC control torques.

## Results

- The optimal `MLP_Deep` architecture achieved a mean inference latency of approximately **1.1** ms per control step, a three-fold reduction compared to the MPC expert.
- It also demonstrated a **25%** decrease in CPU utilization while achieving an **84.98%** success rate under relaxed tolerances (5 cm) and a mean final tracking error of **2.9** cm.
- Offline evaluations showed that deep Multi-Layer Perceptrons (e.g., `MLP_Deep` with MSE around **0.05**) delivered high accuracy and explained variance, significantly outperforming linear models and GRU architectures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Deep Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) consistently outperformed Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and MLPs augmented with sliding windows, indicating that the immediate robot state provides sufficient information for the control decision.
> - For MLP architectures, increasing network depth was found to be more beneficial than increasing width for accurately capturing the non-linear inverse dynamics of the robot.
> - A precision gap under strict error tolerances (2.9 cm average final error) suggests that while behavior cloning captures the expert's global trajectory, it lacks MPC's continuous gradient-based error correction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Model Predictive Control (MPC) is a control strategy widely recognized for its robustness and stability in various applications, including robotic manipulation. However, its primary limitation for real-time systems and resource-constrained devices is the significant computational burden associated with repeatedly solving complex optimization problems within each control loop. This challenge has driven research into alternative methods for approximating MPC policies.

The broader research landscape has increasingly focused on leveraging machine learning, particularly neural networks, to address the computational demands of advanced control strategies. Neural networks offer a promising avenue for creating computationally efficient surrogate policies due to their ability to learn complex input-output mappings and perform rapid inference. This specific work positions itself within the domain of "behavior cloning" or "imitation learning," where a neural network is trained to mimic the actions of an "expert" controller—in this case, an MPC system.

Previous work has explored using neural networks for fast optimization in MPC, as highlighted by reviews on the topic, and imitation learning has been applied to complex controls, such as humanoid robot walking. This paper contributes to this field by empirically evaluating the effectiveness of various neural network architectures, ranging from traditional regression algorithms to deep learning models, for cloning the behavior of a hierarchical Inverse Kinematics (IK) and MPC controller applied to a 3-degree-of-freedom (3-DOF) robotic manipulator. By focusing on a common robotic manipulation task—end-effector target reaching—the study aims to demonstrate a practical method for reducing the computational overhead of MPC while retaining a degree of its performance characteristics. The investigation into generalization capabilities, stability, and computational trade-offs of different architectures is central to understanding the viability of such approaches for deployment in real-world robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate the feasibility and effectiveness of applying Behavior Cloning to approximate Model Predictive Control (MPC) policies for the real-time control of a 3-degree-of-freedom (3-DOF) robotic manipulator. The overarching goal is to develop computationally efficient surrogate policies that can replicate the performance of MPC while alleviating its significant computational demands.

The motivation for this work stems directly from the inherent trade-offs associated with Model Predictive Control:
- **Computational Burden of MPC:** While MPC offers strong stability and robustness and an optimal control strategy, it requires solving complex optimization problems at each time step. This process is computationally intensive, limiting its practical application in systems requiring high control frequencies, such as real-time robotic manipulators, or on devices with limited computational resources.
- **Need for Real-Time Performance:** For robotic manipulators to operate dynamically and interact with their environment, real-time control with low latency is crucial. The fluctuating and often high inference latency of traditional MPC can hinder its deployment in such scenarios.
- **Potential of Neural Networks:** Neural networks are recognized for their ability to learn complex non-linear mappings and perform inference rapidly once trained. This characteristic makes them a promising candidate for approximating the control policies of computationally expensive expert controllers like MPC, thereby offering a pathway to achieve real-time performance.

To address these motivations, the specific objectives of the paper include:
- **Development of a Baseline Controller:** Establish a robust expert controller by combining Inverse Kinematics (IK) for end-effector position tracking with MPC for joint velocity optimization, serving as the ground truth for behavior cloning.
- **Evaluation of Neural Network Architectures:** Systematically assess a range of neural network architectures, from classical regression algorithms (e.g., Random Forest, Gradient Boosting) to deep learning models (e.g., Deep Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) like GRUs), to identify models capable of effectively approximating the MPC policy.
- **Analysis of Generalization and Stability:** Investigate how well the learned neural network policies generalize to new, unseen target positions within the robot's workspace and evaluate their closed-loop stability in the simulated environment.
- **Assessment of Computational Efficiency:** Quantify the reduction in inference latency and computational cost (e.g., CPU utilization) achieved by the neural network surrogates compared to the baseline MPC.
- **Characterization of Performance Trade-offs:** Identify the trade-offs between accuracy, computational efficiency, and architectural complexity, specifically examining the impact of model depth, width, and the inclusion of temporal memory (e.g., sliding window, RNNs).
- **Fidelity to Expert Policy:** Evaluate how closely the learned policies can replicate the actions and trajectory characteristics of the original MPC controller, both in terms of offline metrics (e.g., Mean Squared Error, Explained Variance) and online performance (e.g., success rate, average position error).

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to demonstrate that behavior cloning can effectively transform a computationally intensive optimal controller into a lightweight, real-time capable policy for robotic manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves a multi-stage process encompassing system description, baseline controller design, data generation, neural network architecture selection, and a comprehensive evaluation framework.

**A. System Description**
The study utilized a 3-degree-of-freedom (3-DOF) robotic manipulator operating within a MuJoCo simulation environment. The manipulator's state at any discrete time step `k` is defined by generalized coordinates `q = [q1, q2, q3]^T` (joint angles) and their time derivatives `q_dot = [q_dot1, q_dot2, q_dot3]^T` (joint velocities), forming a 6-dimensional observable state `x_k = [q_k^T, q_dot_k^T]^T`. The simulation environment handles gravity compensation and joint friction, simplifying the dynamics model required for control design. The primary control objective is to drive the end-effector to reach randomly sampled 3D Cartesian target positions `p_des` within the robot’s reachable workspace.

**B. Baseline Controller: IK with MPC**
A hierarchical baseline controller served as the "expert" for generating demonstration data.
- **Inverse Kinematics (IK) Formulation:** This module translates desired end-effector Cartesian positions `p_des` into feasible joint-space configurations `q_des`. It calculates the Cartesian error `e = p_des - p(q)`, where `p(q)` is the forward kinematics mapping. The IK problem is solved iteratively using the Jacobian transpose method with Damped Least Squares (DLS) for numerical stability near singularities, with an update rule `Δq = J^T (J J^T + λ^2 I)^-1 e`. Joint updates are clamped to prevent overshooting, and joint angles are wrapped to maintain numerical stability.
- **Model Predictive Control (MPC) Formulation:** Given the desired joint angles `q_des` from the IK module, the MPC computes optimal control torques `τ_MPC` over a specified prediction horizon. The system dynamics are simplified to a double-integrator model (`q_double_dot = τ_MPC`), assuming unit inertia and neglecting Coriolis effects due to MuJoCo's compensation of complex dynamics. The MPC solves a finite-horizon optimal control problem, minimizing a quadratic cost function of state error (`x_k - x_ref`, where `x_ref = [q_des, 0^T]^T`) and control effort (`τ_k`). This optimization is performed using CasADi with the OSQP solver, subject to state dynamics and torque constraints.

**C. Data Generation Pipeline**
To train the behavior cloning models, a dataset of expert demonstrations was generated in a closed-loop MuJoCo simulation.
- **Collection Process:**
 1. A reachable 3D Cartesian target `p_des` is sampled within the workspace (using cylindrical coordinates).
 2. The IK solver computes the corresponding joint-space reference `q_des`.
 3. The MPC controller generates torque commands `τ_MPC` based on the current state `x_k` and `q_des`.
 4. At each time step `k`, the current state `x_k` (6D), the target `p_des` (3D), and the predicted torque `τ_MPC` (3D) are recorded.
 5. The simulation steps forward using `τ = τ_MPC + τ_env`, where `τ_env` accounts for MuJoCo's bias forces (Coriolis, centrifugal, gravity).
 Data samples are discarded if either the MPC or IK solver fails to converge, ensuring high-quality demonstrations.
- **Dataset Structure:** Data is stored in HDF5 format, maintaining temporal integrity by grouping sequences within episodes. The input feature vector `X` for the learning models is formed by concatenating the current state (`ℝ^6`) and the target (`ℝ^3`), resulting in a 9-dimensional input. The output `Y` is the 3-dimensional `τ_MPC`.
 - **Flat Formatting:** For non-sequential algorithms (e.g., MLPs, Random Forests), each time step is treated as an independent sample (`X_flat ∈ ℝ^(N×9), Y_flat ∈ ℝ^(N×3)`).
 - **Sequential Formatting:** For time-series algorithms (e.g., GRU), each episode is treated as a sequence of time steps (`X_seq ∈ ℝ^(E×T×9), Y_seq ∈ ℝ^(E×T×3)`).
 - **Sliding Window Formatting:** To inject short-term memory into MLPs, each time step's input is augmented with `W` (set to 5) previous states (`X_seq ∈ ℝ^(N×(W⋅6+3))`).
- **Data Preprocessing:** Gaussian noise is added to both input states and output actions to simulate real-world uncertainties (sensor/actuator noise, environmental disturbances) and enhance robustness. Dataset size was empirically determined to ensure convergence, with 35,000 samples used for regression algorithms (MLPs), while RNNs utilized the full dataset to capture temporal dependencies.

**D. Neural Network Architectures**
The learning problem was formulated as a regression task, aiming to predict `τ_MPC` from `X`. Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE) were used as loss functions, with MSE adopted as the primary metric.
- **Regression Baselines:** Standard scikit-learn algorithms were evaluated on the "flat" dataset: Ridge, Random Forest, MLP Regressor (shallow), Gradient Boosting, and KNN Regressor.
- **Custom Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP):** Feedforward networks with ReLU activations. An architectural search varied depth (number of hidden layers) and width (neurons per layer). These models process the "flat" input.
- **Time Series Models:**
 - **Sliding Window MLP:** MLP architectures augmented with a concatenated history of the past `W=5` state observations.
 - **Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs):** Gated Recurrent Units (GRUs) were evaluated. These models maintain a hidden state summarizing historical context and process "sequential" data. Hyperparameters for GRUs (hidden dimensions, number of layers) were also varied.

**E. Evaluation Methodology**
Performance was assessed using both offline and online metrics.
- **Offline Metrics:**
 - Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) for torque prediction accuracy.
 - Direction Accuracy (DA): Percentage of correctly predicted torque signs (`sign(τ_MPC) == sign(π_θ(X))`).
 - Explained Variance: Proportion of variance in expert's actions explained by the model (`1 - Var(τ_MPC - π_θ(X)) / Var(τ_MPC)`).
- **Online Metrics (Closed-Loop MuJoCo):**
 - **Success Rate:** Percentage of episodes where the end-effector's final position converges within a specified Euclidean distance `ε` of the target. Three tolerance tiers: Strict (`ε = 0.02m`), Moderate (`ε = 0.03m`), Relaxed (`ε = 0.05m`).
 - **Average Position Error:** Mean Euclidean distance between the end-effector and target throughout the trajectory.
 - **Computational Time Efficiency:** Average inference latency per control step, compared against MPC solve time.
 - **Computational Cost:** Average CPU utilization during operation.

Experiments involved multiple independent runs (typically 5) for each model configuration to ensure statistical significance, with data split into training, validation, and test sets.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the efficacy of behavior cloning for MPC approximation, architectural choices, and performance metrics.

**A. Regression Baseline Performance**
- Non-linear regression algorithms significantly outperformed linear methods. Ridge Regression, a linear model, exhibited high MSE (8.729 ± 0.098) and low explained variance (0.221), indicating its inability to capture the underlying non-linear relationship of the inverse dynamics.
- Among the non-linear baselines, the MLP Regressor (a shallow scikit-learn model) achieved the lowest overall MSE (0.053 ± 0.013) and high explained variance (0.994). This indicated its superior capability in minimizing large control deviations.
- The KNN Regressor achieved the highest Directional Accuracy (0.999) and lowest MAE (0.091 ± 0.002), but its higher MSE (0.237 ± 0.038) suggested susceptibility to occasional large prediction errors. Random Forest and Gradient Boosting also performed well, with high explained variance and directional accuracy.

**B. Loss Function Comparison**
- When comparing MSE and MAE loss functions for training PyTorch-based MLP and GRU models, models trained with MSE consistently demonstrated lower test error and lower variance across runs.
- For instance, `MLP_mse` (MSE: 0.1460 ± 0.0522) outperformed `MLP_mae` (MSE: 0.2017 ± 0.0455) in terms of MSE. Similarly, `GRU_mse` (MSE: 1.1442 ± 0.1441) had a lower MSE than `GRU_mae` (MSE: 2.1821 ± 0.1001). This led to the adoption of MSE as the primary loss function for subsequent experiments.

**C. Hyperparameter Tuning of MLP and GRU Architectures**
- **MLP Superiority:** Deep MLP architectures consistently outperformed GRU variants in offline evaluation. The `MLP_Deep` configuration (256, 128, 64, 32 neurons per layer) achieved the lowest Mean MSE overall (approximately 0.05).
- **Depth vs. Width:** For MLPs, increasing network depth provided more significant performance gains than increasing width. `MLP_Deep` outperformed `MLP_Medium` and `MLP_Small`. `MLP_Deep_Scaled` (512, 256, 128, 64 neurons per layer), with increased width, did not achieve a significant reduction in MSE over `MLP_Deep`, indicating that for this task, adding more layers was more beneficial.
- **Impact of Sliding Window:** The inclusion of a sliding window (W=5) to inject short-term memory into MLPs did not improve prediction accuracy; it resulted in a slight increase in MSE for medium and deep models. This suggests that the current state provides sufficient information for the control decision, implying the dynamics are largely Markovian.
- **GRU Performance:** GRU models generally struggled to match the precision of MLPs. `GRU_Shallow` performed the worst (MSE ≈ 2.7). While increasing complexity in GRUs (e.g., `GRU_Deep`, `GRU_Wide`) improved performance compared to shallower GRUs, they still lagged behind the MLP models.

**D. Online Evaluation in MuJoCo**
- **Success Rate:** Scikit-learn baselines largely failed to control the robot, exhibiting success rates below 35%. PyTorch-based architectures, particularly `MLP_Deep`, showed robust online performance. `MLP_Deep` achieved an 84.98% success rate under relaxed tolerances (ε = 0.05m) but dropped to 64.16% under strict tolerances (ε = 0.02m). The mean final tracking error for `MLP_Deep` was 2.9 cm, indicating that most "failures" were near-misses rather than complete deviations.
- **Temporal Architectures Online:** The online evaluation reinforced offline findings: static MLPs outperformed temporal architectures (Sliding Window MLPs, GRUs), further confirming that explicit temporal history was not necessary for this control task.
- **Computational Efficiency:** `MLP_Deep` achieved a mean inference latency of 1.102 ± 0.614 ms per control step, representing approximately a 3x reduction compared to the MPC expert's solve time. The neural network also exhibited a more deterministic inference time distribution, in contrast to the MPC's fluctuating solve times. Furthermore, `MLP_Deep` showed a 25% decrease in CPU utilization compared to the MPC.

**E. Discussion and Conclusion Summary**
The results demonstrate a strong correlation between offline regression metrics and closed-loop control performance. The low offline MSE of MLPs translated into a robust online policy. The observed precision gap under strict tolerances (average final error of 2.9cm) is attributed to the inherent resolution limit of the offline training data, where the neural network successfully learned the expert's global trajectory but lacked the gradient-based feedback mechanism of MPC to eliminate the terminal steady-state error. Crucially, the non-superiority of Sliding Window and GRU models indicated that the system dynamics for this 3-DOF manipulator are effectively Markovian, with the current state providing sufficient information for control. The study concluded that for this task, increasing network depth was more effective than increasing width for capturing non-linear inverse dynamics. A standard Feedforward Neural Network (specifically `MLP_Deep`) was identified as the optimal architecture, approximating the MPC policy with approximately 1ms inference latency and a 2.9cm average error, achieving significant speed and resource utilization improvements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic control by demonstrating the viability and benefits of behavior cloning for Model Predictive Control (MPC) policies on 3-degree-of-freedom (3-DOF) robotic manipulators. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Real-Time Control:** The most direct impact is the significant reduction in computational burden for MPC-like control. By achieving a 3x reduction in inference latency and more deterministic control loop times (approximately 1 ms per control step) compared to the original MPC, the learned neural network policy makes real-time control feasible on platforms where traditional MPC would be too slow or computationally expensive. This opens avenues for deploying advanced, stable control strategies on embedded systems or in applications requiring high control frequencies.

- **Reduced Resource Consumption:** The observed 25% decrease in CPU utilization indicates that the neural network surrogate policies are lightweight. This is beneficial for battery-powered robots or systems with limited processing capabilities, extending operational time and reducing hardware requirements.

- **Foundation for Scalability:** This work establishes a foundation for extending behavior cloning to robotic manipulators with higher degrees of freedom (e.g., 6-DOF). The findings regarding the importance of network depth over width, and the sufficiency of Markovian state representation, provide initial hypotheses for architectural choices in more complex systems.

- **Insights into System Dynamics and Architecture Selection:** The empirical finding that static Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) outperformed recurrent neural networks (RNNs) and MLPs with sliding windows for this specific 3-DOF task is significant. It suggests that the instantaneous state (joint angles and velocities combined with target position) contains sufficient information to determine the optimal control action, implying the underlying dynamics are predominantly Markovian. This insight can guide future architectural design choices, potentially simplifying model development for similar control problems where temporal memory is not a critical factor.

- **Addressing the Precision Gap:** The identification of a precision gap under strict tolerances, where the learned policy achieves good global trajectory tracking but struggles with terminal steady-state error (average 2.9 cm), is a crucial finding. This highlights a limitation of direct behavior cloning from a finite, noisy dataset and points to a key area for future research. Solutions like Data Aggregation (DAgger) or integrating a local, high-precision controller could be explored to bridge this gap, potentially leading to more accurate and robust learned policies for sensitive tasks.

- **Democratization of Optimal Control:** By providing a method to approximate optimal controllers like MPC with computationally inexpensive neural networks, this research contributes to democratizing access to high-performance control strategies. It lowers the barrier for researchers and engineers to implement advanced control in resource-constrained environments, potentially accelerating innovation in various robotic applications.

In summary, this research effectively demonstrates that behavior cloning can yield computationally efficient and robust approximations of MPC for 3-DOF manipulators, providing a practical solution for real-time control while offering valuable architectural and dynamic insights for future work.
