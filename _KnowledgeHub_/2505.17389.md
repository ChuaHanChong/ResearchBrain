# Bootstrapping Imitation Learning for Long-horizon Manipulation via Hierarchical Data Collection Space

> [!summary] Summary
> HD-Space is a data-collection scheme for imitation learning that splits a long-horizon manipulation task into key atomic subtasks (open box, pick cup, place, close) and, for each, records demonstrations starting from end-effector poses evenly sampled around the target point, with overlapping subtask boundaries. Trained with a DINOv2 + MAE-augmented ACT policy, it yields higher success with fewer episodes and fewer frames.

## Problem

- Naive teleoperated demonstrations record only one narrow optimal trajectory per task, so policies drift into unseen states and accumulate compounding errors on long-horizon manipulation.
- Human-in-the-loop correction is passive: experts must wait for the policy to fail before intervening, and real-time correction demands complex intervention hardware and a second training round.
- Real2Sim2Real data generation needs precise object modeling and scene correspondence, and still leaves sim-to-real gaps in scene authenticity and physical dynamics.

## Method

- **HD-Space**'s ==atomic task segmentation==: cut a long-horizon task at nodes where the arm must first locate a pose then execute fine operation, giving semantically meaningful subtasks with overlapping start/end boundaries.
- ==Atomic data collection space==: place the end-effector uniformly around each subtask's target point and demonstrate from each such start state, densely covering the error-prone region rather than one trajectory.
- Stronger policy backbone: ==ACT== with the conditional VAE removed, ==DINOv2-small== replacing ResNet-18 as image encoder, plus ==MAE-style random square masking augmentation== (p=0.7, 50% area, chunk size 32).

## Results

- The stronger backbone alone lifts simulated success from **50%** to **73%** on Transfer Cube and **20%** to **36%** on Peg Insertion over vanilla ACT across **3** seeds, **50** episodes each.
- At matched episode counts HD-Space adds **8%** success on teacup-in-box, **18-32%** on mobile bowl grasping, and **20-44%** on mobile spoon grasping; **25** naive + **25** HD-Space episodes beat **100** naive episodes.
- Average completed subtasks rise from **3.23** to **4.45** (5-pen task) and **3.24** to **3.78** (teacup task) at **100** episodes, while frames per episode drop from **1300** to **120** and **450** to **120**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Where demonstrations start matters more than how many you collect: seeding from a spread of poses around each contact point suppresses compounding error better than extra full-trajectory episodes.
> - Proactive coverage of error-prone state regions can substitute for reactive human-in-the-loop correction, removing the intervention hardware and the wait-for-failure bottleneck.
> - Gains scale with task precision demands (angle, height, moving objects); the scheme is policy-agnostic but demonstrated only single-task, leaving multi-task and VLA settings untested.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Imitation learning (IL) has emerged as a promising approach for teaching robots complex manipulation tasks by learning from human demonstrations. However, one of the primary challenges in deploying IL for real-world robotic applications is the substantial amount of high-quality demonstration data required to train effective policies. This paper introduces a method called Hierarchical Data Collection Space (HD-Space) that addresses this fundamental bottleneck by improving both the efficiency and quality of data collection for long-horizon manipulation tasks.

The research, conducted collaboratively between CVTE (a major Chinese technology company), Sun Yat-sen University, and other institutions, tackles the critical issue of data efficiency in robotics. Traditional data collection methods often result in suboptimal policies due to limited exploration of the state-action space and the presence of redundant or low-quality demonstrations. The HD-Space approach represents a systematic solution to these challenges by reimagining how demonstration data is collected and structured.

### 2. Problem Statement

Current imitation learning approaches face several fundamental limitations that hinder their practical deployment:

**Data Collection Inefficiency**: Traditional methods collect demonstrations by having human operators perform complete task sequences from start to finish. This approach often results in trajectories that follow similar paths, providing limited coverage of the possible state-action space that the robot might encounter during execution.

**Limited Exploration**: When humans demonstrate tasks naturally, they tend to perform them in predictable ways, following the most intuitive or efficient paths. While this makes sense for human execution, it leaves gaps in the robot's understanding of how to handle variations or recover from suboptimal states.

**Scalability Challenges**: Existing methods like Human-in-the-Loop (HIL) learning require complex hardware systems that can intervene during robot execution, making them expensive and difficult to scale. Similarly, sim-to-real approaches face the challenge of accurately modeling real-world physics and dynamics.

**Long-horizon Task Complexity**: As manipulation tasks become longer and more complex, involving multiple sequential sub-tasks, the exponential growth in possible state combinations makes comprehensive data collection increasingly challenging.

The key insight driving this research is that the quality and diversity of training data is often more important than the quantity, particularly for complex manipulation tasks where precise control is required at specific moments.

### 3. The HD-Space Methodology

The Hierarchical Data Collection Space methodology introduces a structured approach to data collection that systematically addresses the limitations of existing methods. The core innovation lies in decomposing complex manipulation tasks into atomic sub-tasks and designing specific data collection strategies for each.

#### 3.1. Atomic Task Segmentation

The first step in HD-Space involves breaking down complex manipulation sequences into what the authors term "atomic sub-tasks." These are fundamental manipulation operations that cannot be meaningfully subdivided further. For example, a task like "put a cup in a box" might be segmented into:

1. **Open box** - Manipulating the box lid or container
2. **Pick up cup** - Grasping and lifting the target object
3. **Put in box** - Placing the object inside the container
4. **Close box** - Securing the container

Each atomic sub-task represents a critical manipulation moment where precise control is essential for task success.

#### 3.2. Atomic State/Action Space Design

For each atomic sub-task, HD-Space defines a specific state/action space that focuses on the most critical aspects of that particular manipulation. Rather than collecting data uniformly across the entire task sequence, the method concentrates on exploring variations around key manipulation points.

The key principle is to explore the initial end-effector poses and approaches that lead into each atomic manipulation. For instance, when collecting data for a "pick up cup" sub-task, instead of always starting from the same position, the human demonstrator begins from various positions around the target object, exploring different approach angles, heights, and orientations.

#### 3.3. Active Exploration Strategy

HD-Space implements an active exploration strategy where human demonstrators are explicitly instructed to:

- **Vary initial conditions**: Start each atomic sub-task from different positions within the defined space
- **Explore approach diversity**: Use different trajectories to achieve the same manipulation goal
- **Include challenging scenarios**: Deliberately create situations that might be difficult but still achievable

This active exploration ensures that the collected data covers a broader range of state transitions and provides the learning algorithm with examples of how to handle variations and recover from suboptimal starting conditions.

#### 3.4. Overlapping Boundaries

To ensure smooth execution of extended manipulation sequences, HD-Space designs overlapping boundaries between atomic state/action spaces. This means that the ending states of one atomic sub-task overlap with the possible starting states of the next sub-task, creating continuity in the overall manipulation sequence.

### 4. Experimental Design and Results

The researchers conducted comprehensive experiments to validate the effectiveness of HD-Space across multiple robotic manipulation tasks, using different robot platforms and task complexities.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

The experiments were conducted on two primary robotic platforms:
- **Cobot Mobile ALOHA Robot**: A mobile manipulation platform with dual arms
- **PiPER Robot with Teleoperation System**: A fixed-base manipulator with teleoperation capabilities

#### 4.2. Task Categories

The evaluation included several categories of manipulation tasks:

1. **Single-object manipulation**: Basic pick-and-place operations
2. **Container manipulation**: Tasks involving opening/closing containers and placing objects inside
3. **Mobile object tracking**: Manipulation of objects that move during the task
4. **Multi-step sequential tasks**: Complex sequences requiring multiple atomic operations

#### 4.3. Performance Metrics

The researchers evaluated HD-Space using three primary metrics:

- **Success Rate (SR)**: The percentage of tasks completed successfully
- **Data Efficiency**: The amount of demonstration data required to achieve target performance levels
- **Long-horizon Performance**: The ability to complete extended sequences of atomic sub-tasks

#### 4.4. Key Findings

**Improved Success Rates**: HD-Space consistently achieved higher success rates across all tested tasks. For example, in the "grabbing tea cup into box" task, HD-Space achieved 88% success rate with 100 demonstrations compared to 64% for naive data collection.

**Enhanced Data Efficiency**: The method demonstrated the ability to achieve better performance with fewer demonstrations. In several cases, HD-Space with 75 demonstrations outperformed naive methods with 100 demonstrations.

**Better Long-horizon Performance**: HD-Space showed particular strength in longer manipulation sequences, maintaining higher success rates as task complexity increased.

**Robustness to Variations**: Qualitative analysis revealed that HD-Space helped robots overcome common failure modes such as picking wrong objects, predicting incorrect angles or heights, and failing to track mobile objects.

### 5. Technical Implementation

#### 5.1. Model Architecture

The researchers implemented HD-Space using the Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) model as their base architecture, with several modifications:

- **Encoder-Decoder Architecture**: Utilizing transformer-based encoder and decoder following established practices
- **Feature Extraction**: Employing DINOv2-small as the image feature extractor instead of ResNet-18 for improved visual understanding
- **Data Augmentation**: Implementing MAE-based data augmentation for input images during training
- **Simplified Design**: Removing the conditional VAE component from the original ACT model

#### 5.2. Data Collection Protocol

The HD-Space data collection follows a structured protocol:

1. **Task Analysis**: Decompose the target task into atomic sub-tasks
2. **Space Definition**: Define the state/action space for each atomic sub-task
3. **Demonstration Collection**: Have human operators perform demonstrations starting from various positions within each atomic space
4. **Quality Control**: Ensure demonstrations cover the intended range of variations
5. **Data Integration**: Combine demonstrations from all atomic spaces into a comprehensive training dataset

#### 5.3. Training Process

The training process incorporates the diverse demonstration data collected through HD-Space:

- **Data Preprocessing**: Standardize and augment the collected demonstrations
- **Atomic Space Integration**: Ensure proper coverage of all atomic manipulation spaces
- **Policy Learning**: Train the transformer-based policy to handle the increased diversity in starting conditions
- **Evaluation**: Test on held-out scenarios that require transitions between atomic spaces

### 6. Implications and Future Directions

The HD-Space methodology represents a significant advancement in data collection strategies for imitation learning, with several important implications for the field of robotics.

#### 6.1. Practical Impact

**Reduced Data Collection Costs**: By improving data efficiency, HD-Space makes imitation learning more practical for real-world applications where demonstration data is expensive to collect.

**Enhanced Robot Robustness**: The systematic exploration of atomic manipulation spaces leads to more robust policies that can handle variations and unexpected situations.

**Scalability**: The hierarchical approach provides a framework for scaling to more complex, longer-horizon manipulation tasks.

#### 6.2. Theoretical Contributions

**Data Quality Focus**: The work emphasizes that data quality and diversity can be more important than quantity, challenging the common assumption that more data always leads to better performance.

**Hierarchical Task Understanding**: The atomic sub-task decomposition provides insights into how complex manipulation tasks can be systematically understood and learned.

**Active Exploration in IL**: The research demonstrates the value of active exploration strategies in imitation learning, moving beyond passive demonstration collection.

#### 6.3. Future Research Directions

The authors identify several promising avenues for future work:

**Multi-task Learning**: Extending HD-Space to scenarios where robots must learn multiple related tasks simultaneously, potentially sharing atomic manipulation skills across tasks.

**Vision-Language-Action Integration**: Combining HD-Space with vision-language-action (VLA) models to enable more flexible and generalizable manipulation policies.

**Automated Space Definition**: Developing methods to automatically identify and define atomic sub-tasks and their associated state/action spaces, reducing the need for manual task analysis.

**Sim-to-Real Integration**: Exploring how HD-Space principles can be applied in simulation environments to improve sim-to-real transfer.

The HD-Space methodology provides a systematic framework for improving imitation learning data collection that addresses fundamental challenges in the field. By focusing on the quality and diversity of demonstration data rather than simply increasing quantity, this approach offers a practical path toward more effective and efficient robot learning systems. The consistent performance improvements across diverse manipulation tasks suggest that these principles can be broadly applied to advance the state of robotic manipulation capabilities.
