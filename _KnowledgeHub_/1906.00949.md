# Stabilizing Off-Policy Q-Learning via Bootstrapping Error Reduction

> [!summary] Summary
> Addresses instability in off-policy Q-learning when learning from fixed datasets by identifying and mitigating bootstrapping error caused by out-of-distribution actions. The proposed BEAR-QL algorithm demonstrates robust performance across diverse data sources, including random, medium-quality, and expert policies, particularly outperforming prior methods on suboptimal datasets in continuous control tasks.

## Problem

- Existing off-policy Q-learning algorithms exhibit instability and poor performance when trained exclusively on fixed, static datasets without further interaction with the environment.
- The high cost and safety concerns of active data collection limit the application of traditional RL in real-world scenarios like robotics and autonomous driving.
- Pathological Q-value estimates arise from the Bellman backup operator evaluating Q-functions on out-of-distribution actions, leading to error accumulation and training divergence.

## Method

- Proposes the **Bootstrapping Error Accumulation Reduction (BEAR)** algorithm, an ==actor-critic== method leveraging a ==distribution-constrained Bellman operator==.
- Utilizes an ==ensemble of Q-functions== for conservative estimation and constrains the policy to keep its actions within the ==support== of the training data's action distribution.
- Employs ==Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)== to measure and enforce the ==support-set constraint== on the learned policy's action distribution during updates.

## Results

- BEAR-QL consistently achieved robust and superior performance compared to BCQ and Naïve RL across static datasets collected from random, medium-quality, and optimal policies.
- On medium-quality datasets, BEAR-QL significantly outperformed BCQ and Behavioral Cloning, demonstrating its ability to improve upon suboptimal data rather than merely imitating it.
- Ablation studies confirmed that using the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) constraint for support matching leads to more stable training and better performance than a KL-divergence constraint.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Instability in off-policy reinforcement learning from static datasets primarily arises from bootstrapping error caused by evaluating Q-functions on out-of-distribution (OOD) actions.
> - A crucial trade-off exists between allowing policy flexibility (low suboptimality bias) and maintaining Q-value accuracy by restricting to data support (low concentrability coefficient).
> - Constraining the learned policy's action *support* rather than its exact density is essential for robust improvement over suboptimal behavior policies when learning offline.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has achieved remarkable successes in simulated environments and structured domains where active data collection is straightforward. However, scaling RL to complex, unstructured real-world settings (such as autonomous driving, robotics, or recommender systems) remains a significant challenge. A primary bottleneck is the prohibitive cost and safety concerns associated with active data collection in real-world scenarios. This has spurred immense interest in **off-policy RL**, which aims to reuse experience collected from various prior policies, enhancing sample efficiency and enabling learning from pre-existing datasets.

Despite its theoretical appeal, practical off-policy RL often struggles when exclusively trained on static, fixed datasets (a setting commonly referred to as **batch RL** or **offline RL**). Traditional off-policy algorithms, including popular Q-learning and actor-critic methods, exhibit high sensitivity to the data distribution. They often fail to learn effectively from arbitrary off-policy data without the opportunity for additional, targeted on-policy exploration. This limitation persists even when the static data is collected by an expert policy, which in principle should simplify exploration.

This paper identifies a critical source of this instability: **bootstrapping error** arising from evaluating Q-functions on **out-of-distribution (OOD) actions**. When a Q-function is learned from a specific data distribution, it may extrapolate poorly to actions not present or rarely observed in the training data. If the Bellman backup operator, which relies on maximizing the Q-function over *all possible* next actions, selects such OOD actions, the resulting Q-value estimates can be pathologically high or low, leading to error accumulation across iterations and ultimately unstable training or divergence.

Previous work has attempted to address related issues:
- **Error Propagation in Approximate Dynamic Programming (ADP):** Studies how Bellman errors accumulate, often focusing on issues like inadequate sampling and distributional shift.
- **Conservative Policy Iteration:** Works like Kakade and Langford (2002) proposed constraining policy updates to be small for provable convergence in online settings.
- **Batch-Constrained Q-learning (BCQ):** A highly relevant concurrent work (Fujimoto et al., 2018) that also identified instability from unseen actions and proposed constraining action selection. However, BCQ often implicitly constrains the *distribution* of the learned policy to be close to the behavior policy, which can be overly restrictive (e.g., reducing the learned policy to behavioral cloning).
- **SPIBB (Safe Policy Improvement with Baseline Bootstrapping) and REM (Random Ensemble Mixture):** Other concurrent works exploring similar themes of stability and conservative updates in offline RL.

This work differentiates itself by providing a deeper theoretical analysis of bootstrapping error from OOD actions and proposing a principled solution that avoids the over-conservatism of some prior methods. It builds on the understanding that it's crucial to evaluate Q-functions only on actions within the *support* of the training data distribution, rather than strictly matching its density. By formally analyzing this concept and proposing a practical algorithm, the paper significantly advances the state-of-the-art in robust off-policy learning from static datasets, bridging the gap towards more practical real-world RL applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop **more robust off-policy reinforcement learning algorithms that can effectively learn from large, static datasets without any further interaction with the environment.**

The authors are motivated by the following key insights and challenges:

1. **High Cost of On-Policy Data Collection:** In many real-world domains (e.g., autonomous driving, robotics), collecting active, on-policy data is expensive, time-consuming, and potentially dangerous. This limits the scalability of traditional RL.
2. **Abundance of Off-Policy Data:** Conversely, many domains already possess vast archives of previously collected interaction data (e.g., logs from human operators, historical system interactions). Leveraging this "offline" data efficiently is crucial for making RL practical.
3. **Instability of Existing Off-Policy Methods:** Current off-policy Q-learning and actor-critic algorithms, while theoretically capable of utilizing off-policy data, exhibit severe practical instability when trained exclusively on fixed datasets. They are highly sensitive to the distribution of the training data and often fail to learn meaningful policies.
4. **Identifying the Root Cause: Bootstrapping Error from OOD Actions:** The paper pinpoints "bootstrapping error" as a primary source of this instability. This error occurs because the Bellman backup operator requires maximizing the Q-function over actions in the next state. If the Q-function, approximated by deep neural networks, is evaluated on actions that are "out-of-distribution" (i.e., not well-represented in the training data), it can produce unreliable and pathologically high Q-values. These errors then accumulate through subsequent Bellman updates, leading to divergence or highly suboptimal policies.
5. **Need for Robust Algorithms:** The authors aim to overcome this sensitivity, enabling off-policy algorithms to learn reliable policies from diverse static datasets (e.g., random data, suboptimal demonstrations, expert trajectories) *before* deployment.

In essence, the motivation is to make RL truly data-driven, similar to the success of deep learning in computer vision and natural language processing, by enabling effective learning from passively collected, large-scale datasets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper's methodology involves both theoretical analysis to understand the problem and the development of a practical algorithm to address it.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Analysis: Distribution-Constrained Backups

The core theoretical contribution is the formalization and analysis of instability due to out-of-distribution (OOD) actions.
- **Problem Formulation:** The standard Bellman optimality operator `T` can select actions `a'` at state `s'` that are not present in the training data distribution, leading to unreliable Q-value estimates.
- **Distribution-Constrained Operator (T_Π):** To mitigate this, the authors propose a modified Bellman operator, `T_Π`, which restricts the set of policies (`Π`) considered during the maximization step. This means `max_a' Q(s', a')` is replaced by `max_{π ∈ Π} E_{a'~π}[Q(s', a')]`.
- **Error Quantification:** They introduce two key concepts to analyze the performance of `T_Π`:
 - **Suboptimality Constant (α(Π)):** Measures how far the optimal policy `π*` is from the constrained policy set `Π`. A more restrictive `Π` increases `α(Π)`.
 - **Concentrability Coefficient (C(Π)):** Quantifies the mismatch between the state-action visitation distribution of policies within `Π` and the training data distribution. A larger `C(Π)` implies greater OOD-action exposure and potential error.
- **Performance Bounds (Theorem 4.1):** The theoretical analysis shows a trade-off: The total error in the learned Q-function is bounded by terms involving both `C(Π)` (reflecting OOD exposure) and `α(Π)` (reflecting suboptimality bias). Restricting `Π` too much reduces `C(Π)` but increases `α(Π)`, leading to suboptimal policies. Conversely, a too-broad `Π` increases `C(Π)`, causing error propagation. The goal is to find an optimal balance.
- **Support-Set Matching (Theorem 4.2):** The analysis motivates constructing `Π` as the set of policies whose actions lie within the *support* of the behavior policy `β` (i.e., `π(a|s) = 0` whenever `β(a|s) < ε`). This is crucial because it allows control over `C(Π)` while allowing flexibility in `π(a|s)` within the support, thereby minimizing `α(Π)`. The intuition is that deep Q-networks can learn well within the data support, but poorly outside it.

#### 3.2. Practical Algorithm: Bootstrapping Error Accumulation Reduction (BEAR)

Based on the theoretical insights, the authors propose BEAR-QL, an actor-critic algorithm (building on TD3 or SAC) designed to reduce bootstrapping error.

- **Conservative Q-estimation:** Similar to BCQ, BEAR uses an ensemble of `K` Q-functions and takes the minimum Q-value for policy improvement (`min_{j=1..K} Q_j(s,a)`). This helps to prevent overestimation errors.
- **Support-Constrained Policy Update:** The key novelty lies in how the actor (`π_φ`) is updated. It maximizes the conservative Q-estimate while being constrained to keep its actions within the support of the behavior policy (`β`).
 - **MMD Constraint:** Since `β` is unknown in practice, the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) is used to measure the "distance" between the learned policy's action distribution (`π_φ(·|s)`) and the training data's action distribution (`D(s)`). MMD is chosen because it can be estimated from samples and is empirically shown to be more effective at *support matching* (rather than exact density matching like KL-divergence), especially with limited samples.
 - **Optimization Objective:** The policy update becomes:
 `π_φ := max_{π∈∆|S|} E_{s~D} E_{a~π(·|s)} [min_{j=1..K} Q_j(s, a)] s.t. E_{s~D} [MMD(D(s), π(·|s))] ≤ ε`
 This is solved using dual gradient descent with a Lagrange multiplier `α` to enforce the MMD constraint.
- **Q-function Update:** The Q-functions are updated using standard Bellman backups, but the target `y(s,a)` for the next state `s'` is computed by sampling actions from the *target policy* (`π_φ'`) and taking a weighted average of the minimum and maximum Q-values (a modification that combines conservative and optimistic elements to improve stability). The maximization is over actions `a_i` sampled from the target policy, which itself is constrained to the data support.
- **Evaluation:** At test time, multiple actions are sampled from `π_φ(s)`, and the one with the maximum Q-value is chosen for execution, effectively using the Q-function as the actor.

The methodology carefully balances theoretical guarantees with practical approximations, leveraging recent advances in deep RL (ensembles, MMD) to address the fundamental problem of OOD action bootstrapping.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments on a range of continuous control MuJoCo tasks (HalfCheetah, Walker2d, Hopper, Ant, Humanoid) using static datasets generated under three conditions:
1. **Random Policy:** Data collected by a uniform random policy.
2. **Medium-Quality Policy:** Data collected by a partially trained, suboptimal policy. This is highly relevant for practical applications.
3. **Optimal Policy:** Data collected by a near-optimal expert policy.

BEAR-QL was compared against several baselines:
- **BCQ (Batch-Constrained Q-learning):** A direct competitor addressing similar issues.
- **Naïve RL (TD3):** A standard off-policy actor-critic algorithm without specific offline robustness mechanisms.
- **BC (Behavioral Cloning):** A simple baseline that just imitates the data distribution, indicating if an algorithm is performing effective RL or merely copying.
- **DQfD (Deep Q-learning from Demonstrations - static version):** Incorporates a constraint to upweight observed state-action pairs.
- **KL-control:** A KL-penalized RL algorithm.

**Key Findings:**

- **Robustness Across Data Distributions:** BEAR-QL consistently demonstrates robust performance across all three data generation scenarios (random, medium-quality, optimal).
 - **Medium-Quality Data:** This is the most crucial scenario for real-world utility. BEAR-QL significantly outperforms both BCQ and Naïve RL. BCQ often performs similarly to Behavioral Cloning, suggesting it struggles to improve upon suboptimal data, whereas BEAR-QL effectively learns higher-performing policies. DQfD and KL-control show instability and high variance.
 - **Random Data:** Both BEAR-QL and Naïve RL perform well. This supports the hypothesis that OOD actions are a primary issue; with random data, most actions are "in-distribution," mitigating the problem for Naïve RL. BCQ performs poorly, as its constraint is too strict for widely distributed random data.
 - **Optimal Data:** BEAR-QL effectively matches the performance of the optimal policy. BCQ also performs well here. Naïve RL often struggles with optimal data because highly focused expert data inherently presents more OOD actions for non-expert policies.
- **Superiority over BCQ:** Empirically, BEAR-QL achieves stronger and more consistent results than BCQ. The paper explains this by noting that BCQ's implicit constraint on policy distribution is often too aggressive, preventing it from substantially improving over the behavior policy. BEAR-QL's support-set constraint is more flexible, allowing for significant policy improvement even when the behavior policy is suboptimal.
- **Q-value Stability:** Additional analysis (Appendices E) shows that BEAR-QL's learned Q-values are generally more stable and less prone to overestimation or divergence compared to BCQ, further corroborating its robustness.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **MMD vs. KL-Divergence:** Using the MMD constraint for support matching leads to more stable training and better performance than a KL-divergence constraint, especially on medium-quality data. This supports the authors' choice of MMD for its ability to constrain support without overly restricting density.
 - **Number of MMD Samples:** Smaller numbers of samples (`n ≈ 4-5`) for MMD computation often yield better performance, suggesting that a less aggressive support constraint is beneficial.

In summary, BEAR-QL demonstrates superior and consistent learning capabilities from diverse static datasets, effectively addressing the instability issue of off-policy RL by carefully managing bootstrapping error from out-of-distribution actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research holds significant theoretical and practical implications for the field of reinforcement learning:

- **Theoretical Advancement in Off-Policy RL:** The paper provides a crucial theoretical analysis that formalizes how bootstrapping from out-of-distribution actions leads to instability and error accumulation in off-policy Q-learning. By defining the distribution-constrained operator and quantifying the trade-offs between suboptimality bias and concentrability, it offers a deeper understanding of the challenges in offline RL. This theoretical grounding provides a strong foundation for future algorithm design.
- **Development of a Robust Offline RL Algorithm:** BEAR-QL is a demonstrably robust algorithm that can learn effectively from various types of static datasets (random, suboptimal, and expert). This addresses a major practical limitation of prior off-policy methods, which often fail when not allowed to interact with the environment.
- **Enabling Real-World RL Applications:** The ability to learn from large, pre-collected datasets is transformative for real-world RL. Domains like autonomous driving (where millions of hours of driving data exist), recommender systems (with vast user interaction logs), and robotics (where physical interaction is slow and expensive) can now potentially leverage RL much more effectively without continuous costly data collection. This moves RL closer to becoming a "truly data-driven discipline," similar to the success seen in supervised learning with large datasets.
- **Guidance for Future Research:** The paper identifies key areas for future exploration:
 - **Early Stopping Conditions for RL:** Performance degradation over long training runs suggests a need for validation metrics specific to RL for determining optimal training duration.
 - **State-Distribution Constraints:** Investigating methods that directly constrain state distributions rather than just action distributions, particularly for datasets collected from mixtures of policies.
 - **Robust Support Matching:** Developing theoretically sound and computationally efficient methods for support matching in high-dimensional continuous action spaces, potentially using f-divergences from domain adaptation.
 - **Large-Scale Applications:** Testing BEAR-QL and similar methods on even larger-scale, more complex off-policy learning problems to further validate their scalability and practical utility.

Overall, "Stabilizing Off-Policy Q-Learning via Bootstrapping Error Reduction" is a pivotal contribution that advances both the theoretical understanding and practical applicability of off-policy reinforcement learning, paving the way for wider adoption of RL in real-world scenarios.
