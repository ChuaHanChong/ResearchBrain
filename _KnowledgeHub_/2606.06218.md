# TAM: Torque Adaptation Module for Robust Motion Transfer in Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The Torque Adaptation Module (TAM) is a learned module that adapts nominal torque commands to bridge the sim-to-real gap, enabling robust motion transfer for various robotic manipulation policies. Trained purely in simulation, TAM improved success rates on real-world tasks like vision-based pushing (76.2% vs 47.6% direct) and behavior-cloned flipping (72.0% vs 50.0% direct) for a Franka Panda robot.

## Problem

- Policies developed in simulation often fail or perform suboptimally on physical robots due to unmodeled dynamics, differing physical parameters, and unknown payloads (the 'sim-to-real gap').
- Existing policy-side adaptation methods typically require costly retraining or distillation of policies for each new adaptation scenario, limiting reusability and modularity.
- Current methods like system identification and domain randomization have limitations, such as requiring continuous data collection or potentially leading to overly conservative policies.

## Method

- Introduction of the **Torque Adaptation Module (TAM)**, a learned, modular layer inserted at the ==torque interface== that corrects nominal torque commands issued by any policy or controller.
- TAM is trained entirely in simulation using a two-stage process: ==multi-robot pretraining== for generalizability, followed by fine-tuning on randomized rollouts specific to a target robot.
- A split architecture with an asynchronous ==History Encoder== (GPU) infers a latent state from proprioceptive history, which conditions a lightweight, synchronous ==Torque Adaptor== (CPU) to predict ==residual torques== at 1 kHz.

## Results

- TAM robustly improved real-world task success for a Franka Panda robot, achieving **76.2%** success in vision-based pushing (vs **47.6%** direct) and **72.0%** in behavior-cloned flipping (vs **50.0%** direct).
- The module demonstrated superior motion alignment, reducing end-effector position RMSE by over **60%** compared to direct execution on a real Panda with hidden parameters and a 1kg payload.
- Multi-robot pretrained TAM weights generalized to distinct manipulator embodiments, including unseen robots (e.g., Google Robot with **1.05** degrees RMSE vs **4.69** degrees direct), with further improvements from robot-specific simulation fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Operating at the low-level torque interface enables a policy-agnostic and modular adaptation solution that is independent of the policy's internal architecture, observation space, or training method.
> - Learning a compact latent state from proprioceptive history that summarizes robot-side mismatch for direct torque correction is more effective than explicitly inferring physical parameters.
> - Augmenting the history encoder's input with a 'physics residual' feature, derived from the discrepancy between applied and ideal-model-predicted torques, provides a direct error signal for adaptation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics extensively investigates the "sim-to-real gap," where policies or controllers developed in simulation perform suboptimally or fail when deployed on physical robots. This discrepancy arises from various factors, including unmodeled dynamics, differing physical parameters between simulation and reality, unknown payloads, and even variations between instances of the same robot model. In manipulation tasks, particularly those involving contact and dynamic motion, even minor motion deviations can lead to task failure by disrupting contact timing and modes.

Existing approaches to address this challenge typically fall into several categories, each with limitations:

- **System Identification (SysID) and Neural Actuator Models:** These methods aim to improve the fidelity of robot models by estimating physical parameters or learning actuator behaviors from real-robot data. While capable of producing accurate models, they often require continuous data collection and re-identification for each new robot instance, payload, or changing condition. Furthermore, they depend on a predefined parameterization, which may not adequately capture complex, unmodeled nonlinearities or hidden internal states.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** This technique involves training policies in simulations where physical parameters are extensively varied. The goal is to make the learned policy robust to a wide range of dynamics. However, DR can lead to overly conservative policies that are less effective in specific conditions, and it can increase the difficulty of policy optimization, especially for complex, contact-rich tasks.
- **Policy-Side Adaptation (e.g., UPOSI, RMA):** These methods infer latent representations of physical properties from observation history and condition the policy on this inferred state. While effective when the policy is trained jointly with the adaptation mechanism and privileged information, they suffer from limited reusability. Integrating adaptation into new or existing policies (e.g., Reinforcement Learning, Behavior Cloning, Model Predictive Control) often requires costly retraining or distillation of policy weights, which is not always feasible for closed-source or third-party policies.
- **Adaptive Control:** Classical adaptive control schemes estimate uncertain dynamics online and integrate them into structured feedback laws, often providing theoretical guarantees. However, these generally require designing a compatible control structure or explicit estimator, making them less modular for integrating with arbitrary, independently developed policies.
- **Action Transformation and Residual Learning:** This category involves modifying the actions produced by a fixed policy or learning an additional residual action to improve performance. While similar in principle to applying a correction, these methods often tie the correction to a specific policy's action space, task objective, or require new real-world data collection for each policy.

The Torque Adaptation Module (TAM) positions itself to address the limitations of these existing methods by introducing a *trajectory-conditioned torque adaptation layer* that operates at the *torque interface*, below both the policy and the low-level controller. This modular placement allows for adaptation to occur without modifying or retraining the policy itself, supporting a wide range of policy types and action spaces while providing high-frequency, physics-informed corrections based on a learned latent representation of the robot's current dynamics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robust motion transfer for robotic manipulation policies by mitigating the impact of dynamic discrepancies between an ideal reference robot (for which a policy is trained or designed) and a physical target robot. These discrepancies can stem from the sim-to-real gap, added payloads, or inherent variations among robot instances.

The specific key objectives and motivations are:

- **Develop a universally applicable adaptation module:** The core objective is to create a learned module, TAM, that can adapt the nominal torque commands issued by any policy or controller to align the target robot's motion with that of an ideal robot. This module should operate independently of the policy's internal architecture, observation space, rewards, or action space.
- **Enhance policy reusability and modularity:** A significant motivation is to overcome the limitations of policy-side adaptation methods, which often necessitate retraining or distilling policies for each new adaptation scenario. By placing TAM at the torque interface, the same adaptation module can be "plug-and-play" with independently authored policies (e.g., those trained via RL or BC, or designed via MPC) and various low-level controllers (e.g., joint impedance, operational-space control). This promotes modularity and reduces the cost associated with deploying policies on new hardware or with new payloads.
- **Avoid policy-side domain randomization:** The authors aim to offload the burden of handling dynamics variations from the policy training process. Instead of training policies with extensive domain randomization, which can lead to conservative behaviors, TAM is designed to absorb these discrepancies by being trained entirely in a randomized simulation environment.
- **Enable high-frequency, dynamic adaptation:** Manipulation tasks, especially contact-rich or dynamic ones, require fast and precise control. TAM is designed to adapt nominal torques at the hardware control rate (e.g., 1 kHz), facilitating dynamic motion without introducing significant latency.
- **Leverage proprioceptive history for robust inference:** A key motivation is to infer the current robot-side mismatch using only available proprioceptive history (joint positions, velocities, applied torques). This approach avoids relying on explicit physical parameter estimation, which can be ill-posed and depends on specific parameterizations. Instead, TAM learns a compact latent state that summarizes the necessary information for torque correction.
- **Validate performance on diverse tasks:** The research seeks to demonstrate TAM's effectiveness across a range of real-world dynamic manipulation tasks, including vision-based RL pushing, behavior-cloned flipping, and MPC ball-on-plate balancing, using a single set of trained TAM weights. This empirical validation against relevant baselines is crucial to establish the module's practical utility.

In essence, the motivation is to simplify the deployment of advanced robotic skills by providing a robust, reusable, and efficient adaptation layer that transparently handles physical discrepancies, allowing policy developers to focus on task performance in an idealized setting.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the **Torque Adaptation Module (TAM)**, a learned module inserted at the torque interface between a low-level controller and the robot plant. TAM aims to correct nominal torque commands ($\tau^0_t$) to produce a corrected torque ($\tau^c_t$) such that the target robot's motion response matches that of an ideal model under the same nominal command.

**Problem Formulation:**
The system assumes an ideal robot model with known dynamics $F_0$ and a target robot with unknown, potentially stochastic dynamics $F_\xi$ (parameterized by $\xi$). A policy $\pi$ (e.g., RL, BC, MPC) generates an action $a_t$, which a low-level controller $C$ converts into a nominal torque $\tau^0_t$. TAM learns a residual policy $\pi_\Delta$ to compute a residual torque $\Delta\tau_t$ from proprioceptive history $h_t = \{q, \dot{q}, \tau^a\}_{t-K:t}$, such that $\tau^c_t = \text{clip}(\tau^0_t + \Delta\tau_t, \tau_{min}, \tau_{max})$ drives the target robot to match the ideal robot's response: $F_\xi(q_t, \dot{q}_t, \tau^c_t, w_t) \approx F_0(q_t, \dot{q}_t, \tau^0_t, w_t)$, where $w_t$ represents external generalized torques. The inverse torque-command maps $\Phi_0$ and $\Phi_\xi$ (from desired acceleration to command torque) are used only for generating training labels.

**Data Generation and Training Objective:**
TAM is trained entirely in simulation using a two-stage process:
1. **Task-Agnostic Rollout Generation:** Training data consists of free-space joint-motion rollouts in randomized MuJoCo/MJX simulations. For each rollout, physical perturbation parameters $\xi$ are sampled from a distribution $p_\Xi$. These perturbations include variations in link mass, inertia, center-of-mass offsets, joint armature, damping, nonlinear sigmoidal joint friction, end-effector payload, and actuator effects (dead zone, asymmetric torque bias, torque scale). Randomized joint-space waypoint trajectories are tracked by a joint impedance controller, and smooth external generalized torques $w_t$ are injected to mimic object interaction. The data generator records state history, applied torques, nominal torques, and external torques.
2. **Teacher Signal for Local Torque Matching:** For each recorded state $(q_t, \dot{q}_t)$ and a sampled nominal torque $\tau^0_t$, the ideal dynamics $F_0$ predict the desired acceleration $\ddot{q}^0_t = F_0(q_t, \dot{q}_t, \tau^0_t, w_t)$. The teacher's corrected torque, $\tau^\star_{\xi,t}$, is then computed by applying the inverse torque-command map $\Phi_\xi$ of the *perturbed* model to achieve this ideal acceleration.
3. **Loss Function:** The training objective minimizes the Huber penalty $L_\tau = \rho_\delta(\hat{\tau}^c_{\theta,t} - \tau^\star_{\xi,t})$ between TAM's predicted corrected torque ($\hat{\tau}^c_{\theta,t}$) and the teacher's corrected torque ($\tau^\star_{\xi,t}$). This focuses on local transition alignment.
4. **Training Protocol:**
 - **Multi-robot pretraining:** The full TAM module (history encoder and torque adaptor) is initially pretrained on randomized rollouts pooled from five diverse robot models (Panda, Piper, RBY-1, iiwa14, Flexiv Rizon4). This learns a generalizable torque-correction initialization.
 - **Robot-specific fine-tuning:** For a specific target robot (e.g., Franka Panda), the pretrained TAM weights are fine-tuned on randomized rollouts specific to that robot's simulation model. Critically, *no real-robot data* or *policy-side domain randomization* is used in either stage.

**Encoder–Torque Adaptor Architecture:**
TAM comprises two runtime modules operating at different frequencies:

1. **History Encoder (Asynchronous, 5 Hz, Workstation GPU):**
 - **Purpose:** Summarizes the robot's long-horizon (12 seconds) proprioceptive and applied-torque history to infer a compact latent state $z_t$ representing the current robot-side mismatch.
 - **Input:** Raw rolling buffer of measured joint positions, velocities, and applied torques ($\{q_{t-i}, \dot{q}_{t-i}, \tau^a_{t-i}\}_{i=1}^K$).
 - **Physics Residual Feature ($e_\tau$):** The history is augmented with a "physics residual" feature $e_\tau_t = \tau^a_t - \Phi_0(q_t, \dot{q}_t, \ddot{\hat{q}}_t, 0)$. This feature quantifies the discrepancy between the actually applied torque and the torque the *ideal model* would have needed to produce the *observed motion*, providing a direct error signal in torque units.
 - **Architecture:** Utilizes patch-wise time-series tokenization. Each joint's history is split into overlapping temporal patches, embedded into tokens, and processed by a causal Transformer architecture. This reduces sequence length while preserving local temporal structure. The Transformer outputs a latent state $z_t$.
2. **Torque Adaptor (Synchronous, 1 kHz, Controller CPU):**
 - **Purpose:** Predicts the residual torque $\Delta\tau_t$ at the high control rate.
 - **Input:** The most recent latent state $z_t$ from the history encoder, a short local window of current state history (q, $\dot{q}$, $\tau^a$), and the current nominal torque $\tau^0_t$ from the low-level controller.
 - **Architecture:** Multiple MLPs embed local features. The per-joint latent state $z_{t,j}$ conditions the hidden layers through adaptive layer normalization, allowing the same network weights to represent different correction laws based on inferred dynamics.
 - **Output:** An additive residual torque $\Delta\tau_t$ for each joint, which is then added to $\tau^0_t$ to form $\tau^c_t$.

**Scope and Limits:**
TAM's design has certain prerequisites and limitations:
- Requires access to the robot's torque interface.
- Assumes a usable inverse torque-command map $\Phi_\xi$ in simulation for label generation.
- Requires matched kinematic structure and actuator ordering between ideal and target robots.
- Corrections are bounded by the robot's joint-wise torque limits; tasks requiring torques beyond hardware authority are out of scope.
- The training target is local motion alignment, not a formal guarantee of long-horizon stability or task success. Complex contact dynamics not adequately represented by randomized external torques during training might confuse TAM.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TAM spanned real-world robotic tasks and simulated cross-robot generalization experiments, demonstrating its efficacy compared to direct execution, online system identification (Online SysID), and policy-side rapid motor adaptation (RMA) baselines.

**Real-World Task Performance on Franka Panda:**
Across three distinct manipulation tasks, TAM consistently improved zero-shot real-robot execution:

- **Vision-Based Box Pushing (RL Policy):** TAM achieved a success rate of 76.2% across 21 trials, a notable improvement over direct transfer (47.6%), Online SysID (57.1%), and even a policy-side RMA baseline (66.7%). The ideal simulation performance was 84.0%.
- **Behavior-Cloned Flip Policy (BC Policy):** For a contact-rich flip task, TAM yielded a 72.0% success rate (50 trials), closely matching the ideal simulation baseline of 73.5%. This significantly surpassed direct transfer (50.0%) and Online SysID (34.0%), highlighting TAM's effectiveness in dynamic, contact-sensitive scenarios. Online SysID's poor performance suggested insufficient data for identification during the short, contact-rich execution.
- **Ball-on-Plate Balancing (MPC Controller):** In this model-based control task, TAM enabled the robot to complete 13.6 ± 2.9 goals per 15-second run (20 runs), outperforming direct execution (8.8 ± 2.4 goals) and Online SysID (13.1 ± 2.3 goals). The ideal simulation reference was 15.9 ± 2.3 goals.

**Motion Alignment Under Unknown Parameters and Low-Level Controller Switches (Tracking):**
Experiments on a real Franka Panda evaluated end-effector target-position RMSE (in cm) during an 8-second operational-space control (OSC) segment, following an initial joint-impedance control phase. Two physical conditions were tested: the nominal robot with hidden parameters and the robot with an added 1 kg end-effector payload.

- **Real Panda (hidden params):** TAM achieved the lowest RMSE, with 1.51 ± 0.30 cm when adaptation state was reset at the OSC switch and 1.04 ± 0.24 cm when the state was carried over from the joint-impedance phase. This compares favorably to Direct (4.09 ± 0.39 cm), Online SysID (2.67 ± 0.63 cm), and PhysPred (1.74 ± 0.50 cm).
- **Real Panda + 1 kg payload:** Similarly, TAM demonstrated superior performance with 2.35 ± 0.39 cm (reset) and 1.29 ± 0.35 cm (carried), significantly reducing error compared to Direct (6.04 ± 0.51 cm), Online SysID (4.69 ± 0.42 cm), and PhysPred (4.15 ± 0.41 cm).
- Carrying the adaptation state consistently led to better performance for TAM, indicating that history from previous control modes is beneficial.

**Simulation Cross-Robot Generalization:**
TAM's ability to generalize to different robot morphologies was tested in simulation using joint-position RMSE (in degrees).

- **Multi-robot pretrained weights:** TAM's weights, trained on a diverse set of 5 robots, showed reduced tracking error on all tested robots, including two never seen during training (Google Robot, Unitree Z1). For instance, on the Google Robot, TAM achieved 1.05 ± 0.40 degrees RMSE, compared to 4.69 ± 1.13 degrees for Direct.
- **Robot-wise simulation fine-tuning:** Fine-tuning the multi-robot pretrained weights on data specific to the target robot in simulation further improved accuracy, yielding the lowest mean error across robots (0.62 degrees RMSE). This suggests practical applicability for developers with target robot simulators.

**Architectural Ablations (Simulation, Box Pushing):**
Ablation studies on the box-pushing task highlighted the importance of key TAM design choices:

- Removing the long-horizon history encoder resulted in a significant drop in success rate (from 84% for Full TAM to 42%).
- Omitting the physics residual feature ($e_\tau$) from the history encoder reduced success to 67%.
- Baselines that explicitly predicted physical parameters from history (59%) or supervised the latent state towards oracle physical parameters (70%) performed worse than Full TAM's direct residual-torque decoding (84%). This indicates that learning a latent state specifically for torque correction, rather than for explicit parameterization, is more effective.

**Latent-State Bottleneck Analysis:**
Analysis of the latent state revealed that a 64-dimensional bottleneck offered a functional tradeoff, enabling effective torque correction while encouraging the history encoder to group states by physical condition rather than by specific trajectory identity (evidenced by a low Rz ratio).

In summary, the results indicate that TAM robustly transfers manipulation policies across various dynamics and robot embodiments, outperforming established baselines. Its modular design and simulation-only training for adaptation contribute to its practical utility.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Torque Adaptation Module (TAM) represents a notable contribution to robotic manipulation by offering a modular and robust solution to the sim-to-real transfer problem. Its significance and potential impact span several key areas:

- **Enhanced Modularity and Policy Agnosticism:** TAM's most significant impact lies in its design as a plug-and-play module operating at the low-level torque interface. This decouples the adaptation mechanism from the policy itself. Researchers and developers can train or design policies (whether RL, BC, or MPC) for an idealized robot model without the need for extensive domain randomization, specialized adaptation branches, or policy-specific retraining. This dramatically improves the reusability of learned policies across different robot instances, payloads, and even low-level controllers, accelerating the deployment pipeline for advanced robotic skills.
- **Robustness in Dynamic Manipulation:** The paper demonstrates that TAM enables robust performance in contact-rich and dynamic manipulation tasks, areas where discrepancies between models and reality often lead to failure. Its ability to adapt to unknown physical parameters and payloads, even when these change mid-task or with controller switches, contributes to the reliability of robot operations in unstructured or variable environments.
- **Efficient Training and Deployment:** The entire TAM module is trained in simulation, initially with multi-robot pretraining and then fine-tuned for a specific target robot, without requiring any real-world data collection for adaptation. This significantly reduces the cost and time associated with deploying learned policies. Furthermore, the split architecture, with a GPU-based history encoder running asynchronously and a lightweight torque adaptor running at 1 kHz on the controller CPU, ensures real-time performance for dynamic tasks without imposing heavy computational demands on the hard real-time control loop.
- **Generalization Across Robot Morphologies:** The experimental results show that a single set of TAM weights can generalize across distinct manipulator embodiments, including robots not seen during the initial multi-robot training. This suggests that TAM learns fundamental principles of torque correction that are not overly specific to a single robot's kinematics or dynamics, opening possibilities for more generalizable adaptation solutions.
- **Alternative to Existing Limitations:** TAM offers a compelling alternative to existing sim-to-real strategies. It bypasses the need for continual, costly system identification, avoids the performance compromises of overly conservative domain-randomized policies, and overcomes the reusability limitations of policy-side adaptation methods.
- **Advancement in Robotics Research:** By demonstrating that a learned latent representation of dynamics, optimized for torque correction, can be more effective than explicit parameter prediction, TAM contributes to the understanding of how machine learning can be leveraged for adaptive control in robotics. This may inspire future research into hybrid model-based and learning-based control architectures.

While the paper acknowledges limitations regarding access to torque interfaces, kinematic matching, and the local nature of its training objective, TAM's demonstrated capabilities underscore its potential to significantly accelerate the transition of advanced robotic manipulation research from simulation to real-world applications.
