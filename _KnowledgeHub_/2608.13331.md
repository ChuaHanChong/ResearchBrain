# Training AI Scientists to Replicate Research

> [!summary] Summary
> Replica is an auto-generated task space of 310 figure-replication tasks from 100 ML and AI-for-science papers; agents must reproduce a redacted results figure within 60 minutes on a fraction of one GPU. A per-task rubric judge, run by a coding agent, supplies reward. Post-training a 27B model with turn-weighted GRPO yields Faraday, which beats Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 while using Codex as a tool.

## Problem

- Replication is inherently underspecified: a paper lossily compresses the research behind it, so agents must infer missing experimental details rather than follow a fully specified recipe.
- Existing agents are trained for closed-ended verifiable problems, and search harnesses need a definite reward to hill-climb, which general paper replication does not provide.
- Prior replication benchmarks hand agents reference code or unit tests, are hand-built and small, while peer-review-style LLM judges stay noisy with weak human agreement.

## Method

- ==Replica task space==: a vision-language pipeline scans, localises and irreversibly redacts one results figure per task across 100 papers, giving 310 tasks under a 60-minute one-seventh-H200 budget.
- ==Rubric-based judge==: an auto-generated per-task rubric spanning five dimensions scores rollouts via a coding-agent judge with full container access, sampled three times, also emitting turn-level credit weights.
- ==Coding-agent-as-tool (CAT)== harness plus ==turn-weighted GRPO== ==LoRA== post-training of Qwen3.6-27B: five simple function tools, Codex CLI invoked through shell, per-token advantages scaled by judge turn weights.

## Results

- Faraday scores **0.856** mean on in-distribution ML tasks and **0.791** on held-out AI-for-science tasks, versus Claude **0.828/0.748** and Codex **0.796/0.729**, winning **73%** and **60%** of tasks.
- Rubric judge self-agreement reaches Kendall tau **0.66** against **0.46** for the baseline judge and **0.30** human-human; three rubric samples match eight baseline samples on noise share.
- 24 generations of prompt optimisation barely moved Codex (**0.796** to **0.802**); humans preferred Faraday in **29 of 41** rollouts; Faraday led **19 of 20** counterfactual task variants.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A small post-trained model can steer a far larger coding agent: scientific judgment sits in weights, engineering execution in the tool, and gains compound with better tools.
> - Hindsight rubric judging is a moving target that resists reward hacking, unlike foresight-specified verifiable rewards, and per-task rubrics cut judge noise enough for stable long-horizon RL.
> - Replication scores fall with publication year for every agent, so recency plus forced scale-down is a usable proxy for contamination-resistant difficulty in scientific benchmarks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the critical issue of research replicability, a cornerstone of scientific knowledge, which is currently facing a "replication crisis" across various scientific fields, including machine learning. Traditional scientific replication is labor-intensive and often underspecified in published papers, making it a challenge for human researchers.

The broader landscape of AI research aims to develop "AI Scientist" agents capable of automating aspects of scientific discovery. Existing approaches often fall into two categories: those that derive verifiable reward functions from existing benchmarks (e.g., FunSearch, AlphaEvolve) and those that employ Large Language Models (LLMs) for qualitative judgment of agent behavior. The former approach has shown success in hill-climbing on specific benchmarks but often struggles with generalizability and requires significant human effort to construct tasks. The latter, while more flexible, has been applied to highly underspecified settings like peer review, where ground truth and noise levels are significant.

This paper positions itself by developing an AI Scientist agent that tackles paper replication—a task that is underspecified by definition, requires open-ended exploration, and lacks a definitive, easily hill-climbable reward signal. Unlike existing systems, which often rely on complex, hand-designed evolutionary or tree-search harnesses, this work focuses on post-training a smaller LLM to direct a larger, off-the-shelf coding agent, thereby emphasizing learned scientific intelligence over hard-coded mechanisms. The authors highlight that frontier agents, despite their engineering proficiency, often struggle with the scientific aspects of replication. The presented work aims to bridge this gap by creating a scalable task space and a robust reward mechanism for training AI agents in this complex, non-verifiable domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to train an "AI Scientist" agent, named Faraday, capable of replicating research papers, specifically by reproducing experimental figures. This objective is motivated by several challenges inherent in the task of scientific replication, particularly when approached using existing AI agents:

- **Underspecification of Research Papers:** Research papers lossily compress the experimental details, leaving implicit information that must be inferred and explored. Existing AI agents, often trained for well-specified, closed-ended problems, struggle with this inherent underspecification.
- **Need for Open-Ended Exploration:** Replicating a paper requires a degree of hypothesis-driven, open-ended exploration to infer missing details, a capability not naturally exhibited by current AI agents optimized for well-defined problem-solving.
- **Absence of a Definite Reward Signal:** For general replication tasks, there is no straightforward, verifiable reward function suitable for gradient-based optimization or hill-climbing. This makes it difficult to train AI agents using traditional reinforcement learning paradigms.
- **Scalability and Transferability:** The "replication crisis" necessitates a scalable solution, ideally one that can generalize across diverse scientific domains and types of experiments, rather than being limited to specific benchmarks.
- **Hierarchical Intelligence:** The authors are motivated to explore a "Coding Agent as a Tool" (CAT) paradigm, where a smaller, scientifically-focused agent (Faraday, 27B parameters) directs a more powerful, general-purpose coding agent (Codex GPT-5.5, estimated 5T parameters). This aims to train a layer of "scientific intelligence" that leverages existing engineering capabilities, rather than attempting to imbue a single large model with both. This also has implications for model oversight and safety.

To achieve these objectives, the paper outlines three main contributions:
1. **Introduction of Replica:** A scalable, automatically generated task space of 310 figure-replication tasks derived from 100 machine learning and AI-for-science papers (1990–2026).
2. **Development of a Stable Post-training Recipe:** This includes a per-task rubric-based judge, multi-sample judge aggregation, and turn-level credit assignment, designed to provide a low-noise, consistent reward signal for long-horizon, non-verifiable tasks.
3. **Training of Faraday:** An AI Scientist agent that uses coding agents as tools, demonstrating quantitative and qualitative improvements in scientific rigor compared to frontier models like Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves constructing a novel task space, developing a robust reward mechanism, and post-training a language model using a specialized reinforcement learning approach to create the "AI Scientist" agent.

#### 3.1. Replica Task Space

- **Task Generation:** Replica consists of 310 tasks derived from 100 well-known ML and AI-for-science papers published between 1990 and 2026. Each task requires the agent to replicate a single results figure from a paper.
- **Process:** For each paper, Gemini 2.5 Pro is used in a three-stage vision-language pipeline to:
 1. Scan and identify main-text results plots and their captions.
 2. Localize the bounding box of each figure.
 3. Irreversibly redact the figure from the paper's PDF.
- **Task Components:** A task comprises the figure's caption, the extracted "gold plot" (for judging), and the redacted paper PDF.
- **Constraints:** Agents are given a 60-minute time limit and a single one-seventh MIG slice of an H200 GPU. They operate within a containerd container with pre-installed research libraries and internet access.
- **Scaled-down Experiments:** When full-scale replication is infeasible due to time/compute limits, the prompt instructs agents to produce the most faithful scaled-down version of the experiment.
- **Data Splits:** The training set contains 242 tasks from ML papers (1990–2026), and the test set has 68 tasks from AI-for-science papers (2012–2026).

#### 3.2. Reward Function (Rubric-based Judge)

- **Challenge:** Paper replication is non-verifiable, especially with scaling, requiring a robust reward signal with low variance and consistency.
- **Rubric Generation:** Claude Opus 4.7, prompted with a meta-rubric, auto-generates task-specific grading rubrics. The "gold plot" is hidden during rubric generation to prevent over-indexing on specific figure details. The rubric is also hidden from the agent during training.
- **Rubric Dimensions:** The judge assesses rollouts across five continuous score dimensions (0-1):
 1. Visual fidelity (figure-to-paper match).
 2. Claim reproduction (support for scientific claim).
 3. Implementation fidelity (underlying experiment matches paper description).
 4. Experimental depth (good use of compute budget, faithful scaled-down version).
 5. Scientific integrity (adherence to instructions, no cheating).
- **Judge Implementation:** Codex GPT-5.5 acts as the judge, with access to the agent's workspace, tools, replication codebase, git history, generated plot, agent rollout trace, and the "gold plot." It has 10 minutes to review and score.
- **Aggregation for Training:** During training, three independent judge evaluations are sampled per rollout to reduce variance. These scores are averaged for the overall rollout reward.

#### 3.3. Human Studies

Two types of human studies were conducted:
- **Judge Comparison Study:** Assessed how well the rubric-based judge aligned with human judgment. Experts ranked six rollouts (four training, one Claude, one Codex) for ten tasks selected for high disagreement between the rubric judge and a simpler baseline judge. This aimed to determine if the rubric judge's ordering of rollouts was preferred by humans when it differed from a baseline.
- **Agent Comparison Study:** Evaluated human preference for Faraday over baselines when the rubric judge indicated a strong advantage for Faraday. Experts ranked three rollouts (one Faraday, one Claude, one Codex) for 29 tasks where Faraday scored at least 0.2 higher than baselines.

#### 3.4. Simple Harness

- **Design Principles:** Simple, interpretable, maximally permissive, minimal and legible tool set. Faraday's capabilities are derived from policy weights, not harness complexity.
- **Affordances:** Faraday interacts with the environment using five function-calling tools: `apply_patch` (for file writing), `read_file`, `list_dir`, `grep_files`, and `shell` (for executing commands, including background processes).
- **Context Management:** Conversations are linear, append-only. Tool calls execute concurrently. Context overflow or inference errors terminate a rollout.

#### 3.5. Coding Agent as a Tool (CAT)

- **Mechanism:** Faraday (the "outer agent") uses Codex GPT-5.5 (the "inner agent" or "tool") through a wrapper script (`coding_agent.py`) via its `shell` tool.
- **Interaction:** The wrapper runs Codex non-interactively, resuming previous sessions by default or starting fresh. Faraday passes detailed prompts, including GPU resources, time guidance, and API keys, as the inner agent doesn't inherit this context.
- **Efficiency:** The design encourages small, focused calls to the coding agent for iterative progress.
- **Model Switching:** Faraday was initially trained with GPT-5.4 mini as the coding tool, then switched to GPT-5.5 in later stages and for evaluation.

#### 3.6. Post-training Recipe

- **Base Model:** Faraday is Qwen3.6-27B.
- **Reinforcement Learning:** Modified GRPO (Group-Relative Policy Optimization) is used for post-training.
- **Fine-tuning:** LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) is applied to all linear projections with rank 128 and $\alpha=128$, using a constant learning rate of $6 \times 10^{-6}$.
- **Training Details:** Batches of 10 tasks, with eight rollouts each, are sampled from the 242-task train split. Tasks are sampled to ensure even year-range coverage per batch. A 128K-token context window is used.
- **Long-Horizon Stability:**
 - **Multi-sample Judge:** Rollout-level reward is the mean of three independent judge evaluations.
 - **Turn-level Credit Assignment:** The judge also produces turn-level weights, attributing credit over a rollout's turns. These are normalized and averaged over the three judge draws, then used to scale the per-token advantage during GRPO, redistributing credit without changing overall reward magnitude.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Rubric Judge Reliability

- **Human Agreement:** In a human study comparing the rubric judge against a baseline judge (Codex GPT-5.5 with a constant prompt), the rubric judge correlated better with human rankings (Kendall $\tau$ of 0.19 vs. 0.15 for the baseline judge). The rubric judge also exhibited more consistent rankings and lower noise compared to the baseline judge and even human judges (0.66 agreement vs. 0.46 for baseline judge and 0.30 for humans).
- **Noise Reduction:** The rubric judge required fewer samples to reduce the fraction of group variance due to judge noise compared to the baseline judge (e.g., three rubric judge samples matched the noise level of eight baseline judge samples).

#### 4.2. Replica Tasks Are Challenging for Frontier Agents

- **Baseline Performance:** Frontier coding agents like Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.5 (Codex) do not saturate the Replica task space, with Claude Opus 4.8 performing as the strongest baseline.
- **Difficulty Trends:** Task performance consistently decreased with the publication year for all agents, suggesting more recent papers are harder to replicate due to less pre-training data density or higher compute requirements.
- **Domain Variation:** Task difficulty varied by research topic, with NLP and LLM papers being the hardest, and classical ML/statistics papers the easiest. AI-for-science papers in the test split were generally harder than ML papers in the train split. Faraday's base model (Qwen3.6-27B) performed weakest and degraded fastest with paper recency.

#### 4.3. Faraday Replicates Better Than Claude and Codex

- **Quantitative Performance:** Faraday demonstrated a comprehensive performance uplift over its base Qwen model and outperformed both Claude and Codex on:
 - 73% of in-distribution ML tasks.
 - 60% of held-out AI-for-science tasks.
- **Average Improvement:** Faraday achieved an average 6% improvement over Claude and 8% over Codex on the test split.
- **Distribution Shift:** Faraday's advantage manifested as an upward shift of the entire performance distribution, and this gap was consistent across different subdomains.
- **Rubric Dimension Breakdown:** Faraday was stronger than baselines in experimental depth, claim reproduction, and visual fidelity, while matching Claude on scientific integrity and implementation fidelity.
- **Prompt Optimization Ineffectiveness:** Automated prompt optimization for Codex did not significantly reduce the performance gap with Faraday, suggesting the improvements are not achievable through prompting alone.

#### 4.4. Faraday Is Qualitatively a More Rigorous Scientist

- **Rigorous Approach:** Qualitative analysis of rollouts where Faraday significantly outperformed baselines revealed two recurring patterns:
 - Faraday implemented the underlying mechanism an experiment was designed to test, rather than hard-coding outputs or oversimplifying.
 - Faraday demonstrated more thoroughness in experimental scope, reproducing more of the original experiment without unnecessary omissions.
- **Moments of Insight:** Faraday exhibited "moments of insight" within rollouts, accumulating knowledge and improving scores without an explicit evolutionary harness or test-time reward function, similar to prior AI Scientist systems.
- **Human Preference:** In a human study focused on tasks where Faraday had a strong rubric-judge advantage, humans preferred Faraday over both Claude and Codex in 71% of cases (29 out of 41 rollouts), which was statistically significant.

#### 4.5. Generalization

- **"Innovation" Tasks:** Faraday's rollouts were preferred by the rubric judge on 19 out of 20 "imagined" replication tasks, which involved either changing the dataset/environment or modifying the scientific claim, suggesting an ability to generalize beyond direct replication.
- **Stronger Coding Agent Tool:** A Faraday checkpoint trained with GPT-5.4 mini as the coding tool showed improved performance on held-out tasks when switched to the more powerful GPT-5.5 coding tool at evaluation time, indicating an ability to generalize to stronger underlying models.
- **Full-scale Replication:** Faraday outperformed Claude on average across eight held-out tasks requiring full-scale replication (up to eight hours and eight B300 GPUs), suggesting generalization to longer time horizons and larger compute resources.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work demonstrates the successful development and post-training of an "AI Scientist" agent, Faraday, that exhibits enhanced scientific rigor in replicating research papers. The introduction of Replica, a scalable and automatically generated task space, alongside a reliable rubric-based judging system, addresses key limitations in training AI agents for open-ended scientific tasks.

The findings have several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Pathway Towards Innovation:** The ability to reliably replicate and infer underspecified details in experiments is identified as a foundational skill for scientific innovation. Faraday's demonstrated capacity in this area suggests it could serve as a stepping stone towards AI agents capable of generating original research, mirroring how human scientists often start by replicating existing work.
- **Validation of the Coding Agent as a Tool (CAT) Paradigm:** The success of a smaller, 27B-parameter model like Faraday effectively directing a much larger, 5T-parameter coding agent (Codex GPT-5.5) validates the CAT paradigm. This approach offers a flexible and generalizable alternative to building complex, hard-coded harnesses, allowing a "scientific intelligence" layer to compound with advances in frontier coding models.
- **Implications for AI Safety and Oversight:** The CAT paradigm inherently allows a less powerful, potentially open-weights model to oversee the actions of a more powerful, closed-weights model. This architecture could provide inspectable reasoning traces, contributing to scalable oversight mechanisms and potentially mitigating risks associated with misaligned powerful AI systems.
- **Mitigation of Reward Hacking:** By moving away from well-specified tasks with verifiable rewards to non-verifiable, open-ended tasks judged in hindsight, the system may inherently reduce incentives for reward hacking, as the "target" for manipulation is dynamic. Faraday's qualitative scientific rigor supports this idea.
- **Generalization Capabilities:** The demonstration of Faraday's ability to generalize to "imagined" (innovation) tasks, use stronger coding agent tools, and undertake full-scale replications, suggests a transferable scientific judgment rather than mere memorization of procedures.
- **Addressing the Replication Crisis and Enhancing Peer Review:** High-quality AI-driven replication tools like Faraday could become a public good, strengthening the scientific foundation and potentially assisting in the increasingly strained paper review system by grounding AI-assisted reviewing in verified replications.
- **Community Engagement:** The authors invite engagement from the scientific community to explore potential uses of Faraday, indicating a desire for real-world validation and integration.

While promising, the authors acknowledge limitations, such as the early stage of AI paper replication, the need for further validation of judges on larger-scale and innovation tasks, and the importance of human oversight. The mixed feedback from paper authors, while generally positive regarding inventiveness and fidelity, also highlighted areas for improvement in write-ups and code clarity. Overall, this work represents a notable advance in developing AI agents with scientific reasoning capabilities that can act as collaborators in the research process.
