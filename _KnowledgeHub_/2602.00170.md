# The Blessing of Dimensionality in LLM Fine-tuning: A Variance-Curvature Perspective

> [!summary] Summary
> This research analyzes why Evolution Strategies (ES) effectively fine-tune large language models (LLMs) with small populations and why training reward often peaks and then declines. It attributes these phenomena to a "blessing of dimensionality," where LLM fine-tuning landscapes possess low-dimensional, heterogeneous curvature dominated by a few "stiff" directions that govern early learning, while fixed noise accumulating along numerous "flat" directions causes the observed reward degradation. The study demonstrates that the population size needed for ES to find improving perturbations does not increase with model scale, providing a new understanding of high-dimensional optimization.

## Problem

- Evolution Strategies (ES) effectively fine-tune billion-parameter LLMs with surprisingly small populations, contradicting classical "curse of dimensionality" intuitions.
- Training reward consistently exhibits a "rise-then-decay" pattern during stochastic LLM fine-tuning under fixed hyperparameters.
- There is a lack of a unified theoretical framework to explain both the scalability of ES and the non-monotonic training dynamics in LLM fine-tuning.

## Method

- Propose a =="geometry–variance perspective"== where LLM fine-tuning landscapes are low-dimensional in curvature, characterized by a few =='stiff' and many 'flat' directions==.
- Empirically probe LLM fine-tuning landscapes using ==Evolution Strategies (ES)== to measure the accessibility of reward-improving perturbations across various model scales.
- Develop a ==minimal quadratic stochastic-ascent model== to analytically demonstrate how heterogeneous curvature interacting with fixed stochasticity leads to the observed rise-then-decay behavior.

## Results

- Training reward consistently exhibits a "rise-then-decay" pattern in both ES and GRPO fine-tuning of LLMs, which is modulated by algorithmic stochasticity (e.g., population size).
- Empirical "best-of-N" analysis shows that the population size required to find an improving perturbation (N ≈ **30-40**) does not systematically increase as LLM parameter count scales from **0.5B** to **7B**, challenging the curse-of-dimensionality.
- A minimal quadratic stochastic-ascent model successfully reproduces the rise-then-decay behavior, demonstrating that heterogeneous curvature interacting with fixed noise is sufficient to cause this phenomenon.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLM fine-tuning landscapes benefit from a "blessing of dimensionality," meaning improvement is primarily governed by a small, stable set of 'curvature-active' (stiff) directions that do not scale with total parameter count.
> - The surprising scalability of small-population ES for high-dimensional LLMs is due to 'degeneracy,' where many random perturbations can effectively contribute to progress within this low-dimensional, curvature-active subspace.
> - The 'rise-then-decay' training reward stems from heterogeneous time scales: rapid progress along stiff directions quickly saturates, while slower, persistent stochastic drift along numerous 'flat' directions leads to variance accumulation and performance degradation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a fundamental tension and an observed phenomenon in the rapidly evolving field of Large Language Model (LLM) fine-tuning. It sits at the intersection of optimization theory, deep learning practice, and the emerging understanding of high-dimensional neural network landscapes.

**The central paradox it aims to resolve is the surprising success of Evolution Strategies (ES) in fine-tuning billion-parameter LLMs with very small populations (e.g., N ≈ 30), which seemingly contradicts classical "curse-of-dimensionality" intuitions in zeroth-order optimization.** Traditional zeroth-order optimization theory (e.g., Duchi et al., 2015) posits that the number of function evaluations needed to make progress should grow rapidly with the number of parameters, making methods like ES impractical for models with billions of parameters. However, recent empirical work, including that by Qiu et al. (2025), has demonstrated ES achieving competitive performance with state-of-the-art reinforcement learning methods like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) for LLM fine-tuning. This paper offers a theoretical and empirical reconciliation of this discrepancy.

**Concurrently, the paper addresses a second, seemingly unrelated, phenomenon: the "rise-then-decay" behavior of training reward observed in stochastic fine-tuning methods (both ES and GRPO).** Under fixed hyperparameters, training reward often improves rapidly, peaks, and then degrades. This non-monotonicity has been observed in various stochastic learning settings, including deep Reinforcement Learning (RL) (Dohare et al., 2023; Moalla et al., 2024) and supervised deep learning ("edge of stability" regimes) (Arora et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2023). The ubiquity of this behavior across distinct methods suggests it reflects an intrinsic property of the fine-tuning landscape rather than a specific algorithmic artifact.

**The paper synthesizes several existing research threads to build its unifying explanation:**
- **Low-dimensional adaptation:** A growing body of evidence suggests that despite their enormous parameter count, deep neural networks often adapt within a much smaller "effective" or "intrinsic" dimension. Studies on intrinsic dimensionality (Li et al., 2018; Aghajanyan et al., 2021) and the success of parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods like LoRA (Hu et al., 2021) point towards this "blessing of dimensionality" where additional parameters might increase redundancy rather than solely the number of critical adaptation directions.
- **Hessian spectra in overparameterized networks:** Empirical analyses of Hessian spectra near trained solutions often reveal a "bulk + outliers" structure, with a large number of eigenvalues concentrated near zero (flat directions) and a few large-magnitude outliers (stiff directions) dominating curvature (Sagun et al., 2017; Ghorbani et al., 2019). This structure is central to the paper's "heterogeneous curvature" hypothesis.
- **Stochastic optimization dynamics:** Work modeling stochastic gradient descent as a noisy dynamical system (Mandt et al., 2017; Li et al., 2017) provides a framework for understanding how noise interacts with curvature to determine late-stage training behavior and stationary noise floors.

By proposing a unified "geometry–variance perspective" that links ES scalability and non-monotonic training dynamics to the low-dimensional and heterogeneous curvature structure of LLM fine-tuning landscapes, this work significantly advances our understanding of high-dimensional optimization in deep learning. It challenges long-held pessimistic views on zeroth-order methods and offers a new conceptual lens for interpreting and improving LLM fine-tuning processes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper's core motivation stems from two observed empirical phenomena in LLM fine-tuning that appear contradictory or unexplained by existing theory:

1. **The remarkable scalability of Evolution Strategies (ES) to billion-parameter LLMs with surprisingly small populations.** This contradicts the conventional "curse of dimensionality" intuition from classical zeroth-order optimization theory, which predicts that the required population size should grow rapidly with parameter count to maintain signal-to-noise ratio. The authors aim to understand *why* ES can be so effective in this high-dimensional setting.
2. **The consistent "rise-then-decay" pattern in training reward observed across various stochastic fine-tuning methods (including ES and GRPO) under fixed hyperparameters.** Reward improves initially, peaks, and then degrades. The authors seek to explain this non-monotonic behavior, suspecting it reflects a fundamental structural property of the fine-tuning landscape rather than a specific algorithmic artifact.

To address these objectives, the authors put forth a **Central Hypothesis: a blessing of dimensionality.** They hypothesize that LLM fine-tuning landscapes are **low-dimensional in curvature**. This implies that:
- Local optimization geometry is dominated by a small set of "curvature-active" (stiff) directions.
- The size of this curvature-active set does not grow proportionally with the total number of model parameters.

This hypothesis acts as a unifying framework to explain both phenomena:
- **For ES scalability:** If improvement is mainly governed by a low-dimensional subspace, random perturbations do not need to estimate a full-dimensional gradient. Instead, many distinct random perturbations can yield comparable progress by sharing similar components in this restricted, important subspace (a concept termed "degeneracy"). This enables small-population methods to remain effective.
- **For rise-then-decay dynamics:** The separation into fast-relaxing (stiff) and slow-relaxing (flat) directions creates heterogeneous time scales. Rapid early progress occurs along stiff directions. Later, persistent stochastic drift along the many flat directions can accumulate variance, dominating the dynamics and causing performance degradation under fixed stochasticity, hence the peak and decay.

The paper's overall objective is thus to provide a unified, geometry-variance perspective that reconciles these empirical observations, offering a deeper understanding of LLM fine-tuning landscapes and the viability of different optimization strategies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a multi-faceted approach, combining theoretical modeling with extensive empirical experimentation, using Evolution Strategies (ES) as a primary tool to probe the geometric properties of LLM fine-tuning landscapes.

**Theoretical Framework and Modeling:**
1. **Geometry–Variance Perspective:** The core conceptual framework posits that both ES scalability and non-monotonic training dynamics arise from the intrinsic geometric properties of the reward landscape, specifically its highly anisotropic (heterogeneous) curvature. A few "stiff" directions dominate improvement, while many others are "flat."
2. **ES as a Geometric Probe:** ES is strategically chosen for its ability to operate directly on reward evaluations (without requiring differentiable objectives or explicit gradients, which are often unavailable or noisy in LLM fine-tuning). Crucially, ES induces controllable Gaussian smoothing (via perturbation scale $\sigma$), allowing it to probe the coarse-grained curvature structure of the landscape and how stochasticity interacts with it.
3. **Minimal Quadratic Stochastic-Ascent Model:** To analytically explain the rise-then-decay phenomenon, the authors develop a local quadratic approximation of the reward landscape around a near-optimal region. This model, essentially an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck (OU) process, assumes a Hessian spectrum with distinct "stiff" (high-curvature) and "flat" (low-curvature) modes. By analyzing the time evolution of expected reward under constant-step noisy ascent, they demonstrate how heterogeneous time scales (fast relaxation along stiff modes, slow variance accumulation along flat modes) combined with fixed noise naturally lead to a peak in reward followed by degradation. This is intuitively summarized by the "water-filling" analogy.
 - **Extension (Appendices):** The theoretical model is extended in appendices to cover anisotropic noise using Lyapunov equations and discusses the implications of saddles and basin-hopping in non-convex landscapes, though the main analysis focuses on within-basin dynamics.

**Empirical Probes and Experiments:**
1. **Observation of Non-monotonic Dynamics:** The authors first document the rise-then-decay behavior in ES and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) fine-tuning of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on the GSM8K dataset. They show how this behavior is modulated by algorithmic stochasticity (population size for ES, group size/temperature for GRPO), suggesting a variance-controlled effect. They further confirm this on the Countdown task.
2. **"Best-of-N" Accessibility as an Operational Metric:** To quantify the accessibility of reward-improving perturbations and test the curse-of-dimensionality intuition, the authors define $\Delta^{*}_N(\theta, \sigma) = E[\max_{i \leq N} \Delta R_i]$, the expected best-of-N improvement. This metric measures whether a small, fixed population can reliably find an improving perturbation.
 - **Experimental Setup:** They fine-tune Qwen2.5-Instruct models (0.5B, 1.5B, 3B, 7B parameters) on three reasoning tasks: GSM8K (mathematical reasoning), ARC-C (commonsense reasoning), and WinoGrande (winograd schema challenge).
 - **Perturbation Protocol:** For each model and task, they generate a pool of M=240 random isotropic Gaussian perturbations ($\epsilon \sim N(0,I)$) and evaluate the reward change $\Delta R = R(\theta + \sigma\epsilon) - R(\theta)$ for various perturbation scales $\sigma$. Rewards are binary (0 or 1).
 - **Headroom Normalization:** Improvements are normalized by $(1-R_0)$, where $R_0$ is the baseline accuracy, to account for task saturation differences across models.
 - **Uncertainty Quantification:** To avoid inflation, best-of-N estimates are computed on a fixed prompt pool, and error bars reflect variability across *independent perturbation batches* (algorithmic randomness), not evaluation-set randomness.
3. **Auxiliary Diagnostics (Appendices):**
 - **Saturation Population ($N_{90}$):** The smallest population size to achieve 90% of the maximum observed best-of-N improvement.
 - **Upper-tail Quantiles ($q_{0.95}$):** The 95th percentile of the headroom-normalized $\Delta R$ distribution. These provide additional evidence for accessibility.
 - **Reward-based Curvature Proxy (Perturbation SLQ):** (Appendix G.10) To directly probe the curvature structure of the ES-smoothed reward objective $J_\sigma(\theta)$, they use a perturbation-based Hessian-vector product estimator and Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature (SLQ) to estimate metrics like participation ratio and effective rank of the Hessian. This allows them to see if curvature-active structure becomes more or less concentrated with model scale.

This comprehensive methodology allows the authors to move from theoretical insights about optimization landscapes to concrete empirical validation using large-scale LLMs, providing strong support for their central hypothesis.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents several key findings that collectively support its central hypothesis about the low-dimensional and heterogeneous curvature of LLM fine-tuning landscapes:

1. **Pervasive Rise-then-Decay Dynamics:**
 - The authors observe that training reward consistently exhibits a "rise-then-decay" pattern during stochastic fine-tuning, peaking before degrading, in both Evolution Strategies (ES) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Fig. 1).
 - This non-monotonic behavior is strongly modulated by algorithmic stochasticity (e.g., population size in ES), suggesting a variance-controlled mechanism.
 - Further experiments on the Countdown task reinforce this finding, showing that it is not specific to a particular task or benchmark (Fig. 10).

2. **Mechanism for Rise-then-Decay: Heterogeneous Curvature and Fixed Noise:**
 - A minimal quadratic stochastic-ascent model (an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process) successfully reproduces the rise-then-decay behavior (Fig. 3).
 - The model demonstrates that heterogeneous curvature (a few high-curvature "stiff" directions and many low-curvature "flat" directions) interacting with fixed stochasticity creates heterogeneous time scales. Rapid early progress occurs along stiff directions, which quickly saturate.
 - Later, persistent stochastic drift along the weakly constrained flat directions leads to variance accumulation, causing the reward to degrade from its peak (the "water-filling" analogy, Fig. 2). This confirms that such spectral heterogeneity is sufficient to cause the observed dynamics.

3. **ES Scalability without Curse of Dimensionality:**
 - Empirical "best-of-N" improvement curves (expected maximum reward change among N random perturbations) on GSM8K, ARC-C, and WinoGrande tasks across Qwen2.5-Instruct models (0.5B to 7B parameters) show striking results (Fig. 6a-c).
 - The best-of-N improvement *saturates rapidly at small population sizes (N ≈ 30-40)* for all model scales and tasks. Crucially, there is **no systematic rightward shift in these curves as model size increases**, indicating that the population size required to find an improving perturbation does not grow proportionally with the ambient parameter dimension. This directly refutes the classical curse-of-dimensionality intuition.
 - The authors show that for each task, a **viable range of perturbation scales ($\sigma$) exists** at which positive best-of-30 improvements are consistently accessible across models from 0.5B to 7B parameters (Fig. 6d-f). This implies that "improvement-supporting regions" in the landscape are not vanishingly rare.
 - Auxiliary diagnostics like the "saturation population" ($N_{90}$) and the 95th percentile of headroom-normalized improvement ($q_{0.95}$) further confirm that the population needed to access the improving tail of the perturbation distribution does not systematically increase with model size (Fig. 16).

4. **Evidence for Concentrated Curvature Structure:**
 - In a complementary analysis (Appendix G.10), the authors directly probe the curvature structure of the ES-smoothed reward objective using perturbation-based Hessian-vector product estimation and Stochastic Lanczos Quadrature (SLQ).
 - They find that concentration measures like the "participation ratio" and "effective rank" of the Hessian of the smoothed objective *decrease with model size* for GSM8K (Fig. 17). This suggests that as models scale, the curvature-relevant structure in the reward landscape becomes *more concentrated* in fewer directions, rather than expanding.

Together, these findings strongly support the central hypothesis: LLM fine-tuning landscapes are low-dimensional in curvature, characterized by a few "stiff" modes that drive early learning and many "flat" modes susceptible to variance. This "blessing of dimensionality" not only explains the rise-then-decay phenomenon but also accounts for the surprising empirical scalability of small-population ES in fine-tuning billion-parameter LLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has significant implications for both the theoretical understanding and practical application of optimizing large language models:

1. **Reconciliation of Theory and Practice:** The paper successfully reconciles the empirical scalability of Evolution Strategies (ES) for LLM fine-tuning with classical zeroth-order optimization theory. By introducing the concept of "low-dimensional curvature" and "degeneracy" in fine-tuning landscapes, it explains why fixed-population random search can remain effective in high-dimensional spaces, challenging the pessimistic "curse-of-dimensionality" dogma. This fundamental shift in perspective can reignite interest in a broader class of optimization methods.

2. **Deeper Understanding of LLM Fine-tuning Landscapes:** The work provides a novel, landscape-centric view of LLM fine-tuning. It highlights that these landscapes are not uniformly rugged but possess a highly structured geometry characterized by:
 - **Low-dimensional curvature:** A small number of "stiff" directions dominate improvement.
 - **Heterogeneous time scales:** These stiff directions drive rapid early progress, while many "flat" directions are susceptible to slow variance accumulation.
 - **Degeneracy:** Improvement is not confined to a single unique direction; many distinct perturbations can yield progress by sharing components in the curvature-active subspace. This refined understanding is critical for developing more effective and theoretically grounded optimization strategies.

3. **Practical Diagnostics and Interventions for Training Dynamics:** The explanation of "rise-then-decay" as a consequence of heterogeneous curvature and fixed stochasticity provides a powerful diagnostic tool. Its presence signals a variance-dominated late-stage regime. This understanding suggests practical interventions:
 - **Early stopping:** Halt training near the peak before variance accumulation dominates.
 - **Noise scheduling:** Dynamically adjust hyperparameters (e.g., increase population size, reduce perturbation scale $\sigma$, or reduce effective update noise) over time to slow "water filling" along flat modes.
 - **Adaptive step sizes:** Shrink step sizes once progress along stiff directions saturates.

4. **Expansion of the Algorithmic Design Space:** By demonstrating that high-dimensional fine-tuning is not necessarily hostile to perturbation-based methods, the paper encourages revisiting and exploring a broader class of optimization algorithms for LLMs, including:
 - Pure population-based methods.
 - Random subspace or coordinate search techniques.
 - Structured perturbations (e.g., low-rank or block-wise noise).
 - Evolution Strategies with learned search covariances (e.g., CMA-ES).
 - Hybrid methods that combine perturbative exploration with gradient updates.
 The focus shifts from "can zeroth-order scale?" to "which perturbation distributions best align with the small set of curvature-active directions?"

5. **Implications for Robustness and Diversity of Solutions:** The "many prize regions" interpretation suggests that different improving perturbations may lead to distinct solution manifolds, each potentially possessing different properties (e.g., robustness, calibration, reasoning style, safety trade-offs). This opens avenues for:
 - **Solution diversity:** Using population-based methods to generate multiple distinct high-performing models.
 - **Multi-objective optimization:** Selecting among these diverse solutions based on secondary objectives beyond simple reward maximization.

**Future Work:** The authors propose several natural extensions: developing scalable methods to estimate curvature-active structure in reward-defined objectives, designing optimizers that better target this subspace (e.g., through structured or learned perturbations), and extending the variance-geometry framework to anisotropic and state-dependent noise, further bridging ES-style probes with policy-gradient methods. Overall, this work lays a strong foundation for future research into efficient and robust LLM optimization.
