# Preparing for the Unknown: Learning a Universal Policy with Online System Identification

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Tech and Google Brain developed UP-OSI, a method for training robotic control policies entirely in simulation that adapt online to unknown dynamic models. This system consistently performs comparably to or better than policies given true parameters, even demonstrating an ability to generalize to parameters outside its training range.

## Problem

- The "Reality Gap" in robotics, where control policies trained in simulation fail to transfer effectively to real-world robots due to discrepancies in dynamic models and parameters.
- The prohibitive cost, time, and safety risks associated with collecting vast amounts of real-world data for deep reinforcement learning in robotics.
- Existing methods like traditional system identification require real-world data, while domain randomization can struggle if the policy is highly sensitive to dynamic discrepancies or if parameters change online.

## Method

- A **Universal Policy (UP)** neural network is trained using ==TRPO== to output control actions, explicitly taking both the current state and ==dynamic model parameters (μ)== as input.
- An **Online System Identification (OSI)** neural network is trained as a ==supervised learning== problem to predict dynamic model parameters (μ) from a short history of state-action pairs.
- Both UP and OSI are trained entirely ==offline using simulated data==, with an ==iterative refinement process== for OSI that includes 'bad cases' to ensure robustness against its own prediction errors.

## Results

- UP-OSI achieved performance comparable to or better than a policy given true, ground-truth model parameters across **four** diverse dynamic motor control tasks (inverted pendulum, manipulator, hopper, cart-pole).
- The Online System Identification (OSI) component accurately identified unknown dynamic parameters (e.g., center of mass, object mass, friction coefficient) based on short observation histories.
- The system demonstrated strong generalization, adapting online to temporally varying parameters (e.g., changing friction for the Hopper) and surprisingly outperforming baselines even when tested on parameters **100% outside** its training range for the Cart-Pole task.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly parameterizing the control policy with dynamic model parameters (μ) allows it to specialize and adapt to a wide range of system dynamics.
> - Training the Online System Identification (OSI) component iteratively with 'bad cases' (where the policy operates based on erroneous parameter predictions) is crucial for its robustness and ability to recover from initial prediction errors.
> - The method relies solely on simulated data for training, making it highly sample-efficient and eliminating the need for costly and risky real-world data collection during learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The core problem addressed by this paper is the **"Reality Gap"** in robotics, a persistent challenge where control policies learned in simulation often fail when transferred to real-world robots. This discrepancy arises from various factors, including simplified dynamic models, inaccurate model parameters, unmodeled hardware limitations, and uncertainties in sensors and actuators. Bridging this gap is crucial for unlocking the full potential of deep reinforcement learning (RL) in real-world robotic applications, as RL methods typically require vast amounts of interaction data, which is expensive, time-consuming, and potentially dangerous to collect with physical robots.

**Current approaches to tackling the Reality Gap broadly fall into a few categories:**

1. **Designing Robust Control Policies:** This involves creating policies that can handle a certain degree of noise or model inaccuracies. However, for complex, dynamic, and contact-rich robotic tasks, simply designing a robust policy based on a hypothesized model is often insufficient due to significant discrepancies between simulation and reality.
2. **Model-Based Reinforcement Learning with System Identification (System ID):** This approach involves learning a more accurate dynamic model from real-world data and iteratively optimizing the control policy. Techniques like linear models, Gaussian processes, or deep neural networks are used for system identification. While effective in reducing real-world data requirements compared to purely model-free RL, this approach still necessitates real-world trials, which remain expensive and time-consuming. Examples include interleaving policy optimization with data collection and model fitting.
3. **Domain Randomization / Transfer Learning:** This involves training policies in simulation across a diverse range of randomized parameters (domain randomization) or adapting a simulated policy to the real world (transfer learning). Some methods use photo-realistic rendering or progressive networks to facilitate this transfer. A key limitation is the implicit assumption that the simulated dynamics, even randomized, are sufficiently similar to the real world, and these methods may struggle if the policy is highly sensitive to dynamics discrepancies.
4. **Learning in Partially Observable Environments (POMDPs):** This category includes approaches where the unknown parameters are implicitly inferred from observations. For instance, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) can learn to identify and memorize task-related signals (like robot mass or target position) from observed data sequences and adapt control accordingly. While powerful, these methods often focus on memory-related tasks and may not explicitly disentangle unknown dynamic parameters from the state, potentially limiting their generalizability to unseen dynamic environments or their ability to explicitly adapt to changing parameters.

**This paper's contribution "UP-OSI" carves out a unique and promising niche within this landscape:**

- **Focus on Simulation-Only Training:** Unlike system identification methods that require real-world data, UP-OSI relies exclusively on *simulated data* for training, making it highly sample-efficient and safe. This is a significant advantage for robotics, where real-world experimentation is costly.
- **Explicit Parameterization of Dynamics:** Instead of implicitly learning to handle unknown dynamics (as in some RNN-based POMDP solutions), UP-OSI explicitly incorporates dynamic model parameters (μ) as an input to the Universal Policy (UP). This allows the policy to specialize and achieve potentially better performance across a larger range of parameters than a policy that only receives state input.
- **Online System Identification for Control:** The Online System Identification (OSI) component continuously predicts these explicit dynamic parameters (μ) from a short history of state-action pairs during online execution. This dynamic adaptation distinguishes it from methods that identify parameters offline or assume static unknown parameters.
- **Iterative Training for Robustness:** The novel iterative training scheme for OSI, which incorporates "bad cases" (mismatched policy and true dynamics), is crucial. This step specifically addresses the challenge of making the system robust when the initial OSI prediction might be inaccurate, teaching it to recover from errors.
- **Generalization Capabilities:** The ability of UP-OSI to generalize to model parameters outside its training range, and even outperform a baseline with true parameters in extrapolated scenarios, is a surprising and significant finding. This suggests a powerful form of robustness and adaptability.

In summary, this work advances the field by providing a principled, simulation-driven approach to creating highly robust robotic controllers that can adapt online to unknown and changing dynamic models. It strategically combines deep reinforcement learning with supervised learning for system identification, aiming to bridge the Reality Gap without incurring the high costs of real-world data collection, positioning it as a significant step towards more adaptable and deployable robotic systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a novel method for learning **control policies that can successfully operate under unknown dynamic models, using only data generated from a generic physical simulator.** This directly targets the pervasive "Reality Gap" issue in robotics, where discrepancies between simulated and real-world physics hinder the transfer of learned skills.

The authors are motivated by several key challenges and limitations of existing approaches:

1. **The High Cost of Real-World Data:** Current deep reinforcement learning (RL) methods, while powerful, often demand an enormous amount of interaction data to learn complex motor skills. Collecting this data from real robots is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and can be dangerous to the robot, surrounding equipment, and even humans. The authors explicitly aim to eliminate the need for *any* real-world samples during the offline training phase.
2. **Inaccuracy of Hypothesized Dynamic Models:** Designing robust control policies based on an assumed or roughly estimated dynamic model often fails for complex robotic systems performing dynamic tasks involving contacts. Real-world dynamics are inherently complex and difficult to model perfectly a priori.
3. **Limitations of Traditional System Identification:** While traditional system identification can learn more accurate models from real-world data, it often requires iterative processes of data collection, model fitting, and policy optimization. This cycle is still too slow and costly for complex, dynamic tasks, and some parameters (like environmental friction or object mass) cannot be identified in advance.
4. **Need for Online Adaptability:** Many robotic tasks involve environments where dynamic parameters can change unpredictably (e.g., varying friction, unknown object weights). Current methods often struggle to adapt dynamically to such sudden or continuous changes. The authors seek a solution that can continuously adapt to varying factors.
5. **Curse of Dimensionality in Pre-computation:** While pre-computing policies for all possible dynamic models might seem ideal, the "curse of dimensionality" typically makes such an approach intractable. However, the authors posit that recent advances in deep learning and large-scale computing capabilities warrant revisiting such pre-computation approaches for "everything that is important for the task of interest."

To address these motivations, the paper sets out two specific objectives:

- **Learning a Universal Control Policy (UP):** To create a single policy capable of controlling a robot across a wide, parameterized space of dynamic models. This policy, unlike conventional ones, takes the dynamic model parameters ($\mu$) as an explicit input, allowing it to specialize its behavior based on the current estimated dynamics.
- **Learning an Online System Identification Model (OSI):** To develop a fast and accurate method that can predict the dynamic model parameters ($\mu$) based on a short, recent history of the robot's state and actions. This model provides the necessary $\mu$ input to the Universal Policy during online execution, enabling adaptation.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to deliver a robust, sample-efficient control policy (UP-OSI) that is "prepared for a wider array of possible conditions when executed in an unknown environment" and can "dynamically adapt to changing factors." This holds significant promise for narrowing the Reality Gap and enabling the deployment of advanced deep RL controllers on real-world robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, UP-OSI, consists of two main components: a Universal Policy (UP) and an Online System Identification (OSI) model. Both are implemented as neural networks and trained entirely offline using simulated data.

**A. Architecture Overview (Figure 1 in paper)**

During online execution, the OSI model ($\varphi$) first takes the current state ($x_t$) and a short history of past state-action pairs ($x_{t-h:t-1}, u_{t-h:t-1}$) as input to predict the dynamic model parameters ($\mu$). These predicted parameters ($\hat{\mu}$) are then fed, along with the current state ($x_t$), into the Universal Policy ($\pi$). The UP outputs the optimal control action ($u_t$), which is executed on the robot. The new state ($x_{t+1}$) and action ($u_t$) are then added to the history queue for the next time step.

**B. Learning Universal Policy (UP)**

- **Definition:** The Universal Policy, $\pi: (x, \mu) \rightarrow u$, is a neural network that maps the current system state ($x$) and the dynamic model parameters ($\mu$) to a control action ($u$). This contrasts with conventional policies that only take the state as input.
- **Training Objective:** The goal is to learn a policy that achieves high rewards across a wide range of possible dynamic models parameterized by $\mu$.
- **Algorithm:** The authors use **Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)**, a widely used policy optimization algorithm for deep reinforcement learning.
- **Modification for UP:** To train UP, the standard TRPO algorithm is modified. Instead of training for a single fixed dynamic model, the training algorithm (Algorithm 1) samples $K$ different $\mu$ values from a uniform distribution ($\rho_\mu$) at each iteration. For each sampled $\mu_i$, a set of rollouts (sequences of state-action-reward tuples) are generated using the policy $\pi(x, \mu_i)$ and the corresponding dynamic model $f_{\mu_i}$. The crucial part is that the policy explicitly receives $\mu_i$ as input during these rollouts. After collecting data from these diverse $\mu$ values, the policy network $\pi$ is updated using the standard TRPO update rule. This ensures the policy learns to condition its output on the provided $\mu$.
- **Network Architecture:** UP is a neural network with two hidden layers, each containing 64 units, using tanh activation functions, followed by a linear fully connected output layer.

**C. Learning Online System Identification Model (OSI)**

- **Definition:** The Online System Identification model, $\varphi: (x_{t-h:t}, u_{t-h:t-1}) \rightarrow \mu$, is a neural network that predicts the dynamic model parameters ($\mu$) given a short recent history of state-action pairs. The history length $h$ was set to 3 in all experiments, which is the minimal length to learn a second-order dynamic model.
- **Training Objective:** This is formulated as a supervised learning problem. The network learns to minimize the squared difference between its predicted $\mu$ and the true $\mu$ under which the observed history was generated (Equation 1: $\theta^* = \text{argmin}_\theta \sum_{(H_i, \mu_i) \subseteq B} ||\varphi_\theta(H_i) - \mu_i||^2$).
- **Initial Training Data Collection (Algorithm 2, Lines 3-14 - "Good Cases"):**
 - The initial training data for OSI is collected by sampling various $\bar{\mu}$ values from $\rho_\mu$.
 - For each $\bar{\mu}$, $N$ rollouts are simulated using the *trained UP* ($\pi(x, \bar{\mu})$) under the *true dynamic model* $f_{\bar{\mu}}$.
 - Short history segments ($H$) from these rollouts are paired with their corresponding true $\bar{\mu}$ and stored in a training buffer $B$.
 - This initial training exposes OSI to "good cases" where the policy is operating optimally under the given dynamics.
- **Iterative Refinement for Robustness (Algorithm 2, Lines 15-28 - "Bad Cases"):**
 - **Crucial Insight:** The authors found that training OSI only on "good cases" was insufficient. When OSI made an initial error in predicting $\hat{\mu}$, the subsequent control action generated by UP (using the erroneous $\hat{\mu}$) would lead to an off-optimal trajectory. OSI, having only seen optimal trajectories, would then struggle to correct its prediction based on this new, "sub-optimal" history.
 - **Solution:** An iterative process is introduced to mix "bad cases" into the training data. In each iteration:
 - New rollouts are generated by sampling a true $\bar{\mu}$ and simulating under the *true dynamic model* $f_{\bar{\mu}}$.
 - However, the control action $u_t$ is computed by UP using the *current OSI's prediction* $\hat{\mu} = \varphi(H)$ (i.e., $\pi(x_t, \hat{\mu})$), *not* the true $\bar{\mu}$.
 - These mismatched scenarios (where UP's control is based on a potentially erroneous $\hat{\mu}$ while operating under a true $\bar{\mu}$) are then added to the training buffer $B$ along with their true $\bar{\mu}$.
 - OSI is then re-optimized using this mixed dataset. This iterative process teaches OSI to be robust to its own prediction errors and to trajectories generated by sub-optimal control. The authors found that 3-5 iterations were sufficient for convergence.
- **Network Architecture:** OSI is a neural network with three hidden layers (256, 128, and 64 units, respectively), using tanh activation functions. Dropout layers with a rate of 0.1 are added after each hidden layer.

By decoupling the problem into these two specialized, yet inter-dependent, neural networks and employing a clever iterative training strategy for OSI, the authors aim to achieve a robust and adaptive control system that can prepare for and react to unknown dynamic models in simulated environments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluate UP-OSI on four distinct dynamic motor control problems in simulation, demonstrating its effectiveness across various unknown dynamic models and challenging scenarios. The performance of UP-OSI is consistently compared against "UP-true," which represents the informal upper bound where the Universal Policy is provided with the *actual* (ground truth) model parameters—a condition not feasible in unknown real-world environments.

**A. Evaluation Tasks and General Performance:**

1. **Double Inverted Pendulum with Unknown Center of Mass (COM):**
 - **Task:** Balance a double inverted pendulum.
 - **Unknown Parameter:** Offset of the lower pole's center of mass (μ ∈ [-0.6m, 0.6m]).
 - **Results:** UP-OSI's performance (average accumulated reward) across the range of μ values is very close to that of UP-true. Both significantly outperform a "regular" controller (a UP without μ input, trained across the same range), highlighting the benefit of explicitly providing model parameters to the policy. The training evolution of OSI shows clear performance improvement over iterations, approaching UP-true. OSI successfully identifies the COM parameter with high accuracy (low mean error, low standard deviation).
2. **Manipulator with Unknown Object Mass:**
 - **Task:** A robot arm grabs a block and throws it to a specific target height (2m).
 - **Unknown Parameter:** Mass of the block.
 - **Results:** UP-OSI achieves performance (maximum height reached by the block) very similar to UP-true across varying block masses. OSI demonstrates accurate identification of the block's mass.
3. **Hopper with Unknown Friction Coefficient:**
 - **Task:** Control a single-leg 2D robot (Hopper) to hop forward as fast as possible without falling.
 - **Unknown Parameter:** Friction coefficient at the contact point (μ ∈ [0.3, 1.0]).
 - **Results:** UP-OSI enables the Hopper to travel distances comparable to UP-true within the effective operating range (μ = [0.6, 1.0]), which is sufficient for practical applications. OSI accurately identifies the friction coefficient.
4. **Cart-Pole Swing-Up with Unknown Pole Length and Attached Mass:**
 - **Task:** Swing up a cart-pole from a hanging position and balance it, with an additional mass attached to the tip.
 - **Unknown Parameters:** Pole length (μ_length ∈ [0.2m, 0.8m]) and attached mass (μ_mass ∈ [0.1kg, 1.0kg]). This involves a 2-dimensional parameter space (R4 including velocity estimation).
 - **Results:** UP-OSI achieves high rewards across the 2D parameter space, demonstrating successful swing-up and balancing. Performance is comparable to UP-true, even when OSI has to estimate system velocity in addition to the two unknown physical parameters.

**B. Key Strengths and Generalization Capabilities:**

1. **Effective Online System Identification:** Across all tasks, the results (e.g., Figures 2d, 3c, 4c) consistently show that the trained OSI network is able to accurately identify the unknown dynamic model parameters based on a short history of observations. The mean predicted parameters closely match the true parameters, with relatively low standard deviations. This confirms that the iterative training process for OSI is effective.
2. **Robustness to Unknown but Trained-for Parameters:** For all tasks, UP-OSI consistently achieves performance very close to UP-true, indicating its strong ability to operate effectively when the dynamic model parameters are unknown at the outset but fall within the range seen during training.
3. **Generalization to Temporally Varying Model Parameters (Hopper):**
 - **Experiment:** The Hopper was tested on a track where the friction coefficient changed dynamically (a "const" friction region and a "vary" friction region).
 - **Results:** UP-OSI showed comparable and sometimes even *better* performance than UP-true, which was given the actual, perfect friction information at every timestep. This is remarkable because UP-OSI correctly identifies changes in friction *online* based on observation history, without being explicitly trained on temporally varying μ. A baseline policy with fixed μ (not aware of changes) performed significantly worse, highlighting the necessity of dynamic adaptation.
4. **Generalization Beyond Training Range (Cart-Pole):**
 - **Experiment:** UP-OSI and UP-true were tested on a cart-pole system with pole lengths and attached masses 100% *outside* their original training ranges (e.g., pole length [0.8m, 1.4m] vs. trained [0.2m, 0.8m]).
 - **Surprising Result:** UP-OSI *significantly outperforms* UP-true in this extrapolated range. The authors hypothesize that OSI learned to modulate the model parameters over time, effectively switching between existing policies or adapting in ways that were not explicitly learned, leading to superior performance in these unseen conditions. This is a very compelling finding, suggesting that the system doesn't just interpolate but can also extrapolate or adapt intelligently.

Overall, the findings strongly support the paper's claims: UP-OSI is a robust, adaptive control policy that can handle unknown and changing dynamic models effectively, leveraging only simulated data for training. Its unexpected superior performance beyond the training range is particularly intriguing and points to advanced generalization capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Preparing for the Unknown: Learning a Universal Policy with Online System Identification" holds significant implications for the field of robotics and reinforcement learning, particularly in bridging the persistent "Reality Gap."

1. **Bridging the Reality Gap:** The primary significance lies in providing a robust framework for transferring control policies learned in simulation to real-world robotic systems. By explicitly addressing unknown dynamic model parameters and adapting to them online, UP-OSI bypasses the need for perfectly accurate models or extensive real-world data collection, which are major bottlenecks for deploying complex RL policies on physical robots. This could dramatically accelerate the development and deployment of advanced robotic skills.
2. **Sample Efficiency and Safety:** Training UP-OSI exclusively with simulated data makes it highly sample-efficient and eliminates the safety concerns and high costs associated with training directly on real robots. This paradigm shift means researchers can "aggressively explore the virtual world" to prepare policies for a vast array of conditions, a much more scalable approach than real-world trials.
3. **Robustness to Unknown and Changing Environments:** The system's ability to operate effectively with unknown parameters (mass, friction, inertia) and to adapt *online* to *temporally varying* parameters (demonstrated with the Hopper on changing friction surfaces) is crucial for practical robotic applications. Real-world environments are inherently uncertain and dynamic; UP-OSI's adaptability makes it suitable for deployment in such conditions.
4. **Superior Generalization Capabilities:** The most surprising and potentially impactful finding is UP-OSI's ability to outperform a baseline with perfect model knowledge (UP-true) when tested with dynamic models *outside its training range*. This suggests that the online system identification component is not merely estimating fixed parameters but is learning a sophisticated form of online adaptation or policy modulation. If this capability holds in more complex real-world scenarios, it implies a powerful mechanism for coping with entirely novel dynamic conditions.
5. **Principled Decoupling of System ID and Control:** Unlike "end-to-end" recurrent networks that implicitly learn unknown parameters, UP-OSI explicitly separates the system identification (OSI) from the control policy (UP) by feeding identified parameters as input. The authors hypothesize that this explicit parameterization allows the Universal Policy to specialize more effectively for different dynamic models, potentially leading to better performance and improved sample efficiency during training compared to a single, large, implicit network. The iterative training strategy for OSI further ensures that this explicit identification is robust.
6. **Path to Real-World Deployment:** While the current work is purely simulation-based, the strong performance and robustness observed lay a promising groundwork for future transfer to real robotic platforms. The authors correctly identify that the next critical step is to test UP-OSI on physical robots, acknowledging the challenge of whether chosen parameterizations can adequately represent real-world dynamics. However, the online, time-varying estimation of dynamic models offers an expressive approach to match real-world complexities.

**Limitations and Future Work:** The authors openly acknowledge limitations, including the challenge of identifying high-dimensional model parameters, the lack of theoretical upper bounds or convergence conditions for OSI training, and the current deterministic assumption of policies and dynamic models. They also point to the need to address sensor/actuator uncertainty and latency. Future work involves testing on different simulators and, most importantly, on real-world robots.

In conclusion, UP-OSI represents a significant conceptual and practical advancement in robust robotic control. By leveraging massive simulation data and intelligently decoupling online system identification from universal policy learning, it offers a compelling solution to the Reality Gap, promising a future where complex, adaptable robot behaviors can be developed more efficiently and deployed more reliably in the face of environmental uncertainties.
