# Zero-Shot Adaptation to Robot Structural Damage via Natural Language-Informed Kinodynamics Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> George Mason University researchers developed Zero-shot Language Informed Kinodynamics (ZLIK), a framework enabling robots to instantly adapt to structural damage by leveraging natural language descriptions to inform their kinodynamics models. This approach achieved a trajectory prediction error of 0.50 ± 1.29 MSE, outperforming non-adaptive and adaptive baselines, and demonstrated generalization from simulation to a physical robot.

## Problem

- Autonomous mobile robots operating in challenging environments frequently sustain structural damage, which fundamentally alters their kinodynamics.
- Relying on nominal kinodynamics models after damage leads to significant tracking errors, instability, and potential mission failure.
- Current adaptation methods require collecting new data through "few-shot" online interactions with the damaged robot, posing safety risks and operational delays.

## Method

- Develops **ZLIK** (==Zero-shot Language Informed Kinodynamics==), a framework for instant adaptation to structural damage without new data collection.
- Utilizes ==natural language descriptions== of damage, encoded by a pre-trained ==Sentence Transformer==, then aligns these embeddings with observed kinodynamic behaviors in a shared latent space via ==self-supervised learning== with ==VICReg==.
- Integrates the learned language embedding into a ==Transformer-based kinodynamics model== to predict future robot states.

## Results

- ZLIK demonstrated superior zero-shot adaptation, achieving a trajectory prediction error (MSE) of **0.50 ± 1.29**, significantly lower than non-adaptive (**1.59 ± 1.55**) and adaptive baselines (**2.59 ± 2.44**).
- The 6-DoF state decomposition was critical, as ZLIK (**0.50 ± 1.09 MSE**) outperformed a Monolithic Transformer baseline (**0.81 ± 1.11 MSE**) for complex damage types.
- The model successfully generalized across embodiments and scales, effectively adapting from a simulated full-size vehicle to a **1/10th scale** physical robot with unseen damages in a zero-shot manner.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Natural language descriptions provide a semantically rich and flexible representation for diverse structural damages, enabling fine-grained adaptation.
> - A self-supervised approach (VICReg) can effectively align semantic language embeddings with actual robot kinodynamic behaviors, grounding linguistic information in physical reality.
> - Decomposing the robot's 6-DoF state into individual dimensions within a Transformer architecture is essential for accurately modeling the complex, independent effects of damage.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous mobile robots operating in uncontrolled or "in-the-wild" environments frequently encounter mechanical stress, leading to structural damage. This damage, ranging from a punctured tire to a broken suspension linkage, fundamentally alters a robot's kinodynamics – the relationship between its motion and the forces acting upon it. Accurate kinodynamics models are crucial for effective control and navigation.

Historically, kinodynamics modeling has relied on analytical formulations such as bicycle or Ackermann steering models. While computationally efficient, these models often oversimplify complex vehicle-terrain interactions. Consequently, data-driven approaches have gained prominence, often leveraging high-fidelity simulators for data generation to circumvent the risks and costs of real-world data collection. Recent advancements in this area include the incorporation of uncertainty quantification methods (e.g., Gaussian Processes) and the enforcement of kinematic constraints via Physics-Informed Neural Networks to enhance prediction accuracy beyond training distributions. These models typically integrate various input modalities, such as visual semantics, elevation mapping, terrain geometry, and inertial measurements, to perceive the external environment. However, these methods primarily focus on external environmental factors and typically assume a structurally healthy robot.

When a robot sustains structural damage, its internal physical state changes, leading to significant kinodynamic shifts that cannot be adequately captured by existing models that rely on a fixed vehicle structure or treat deviations as simple parametric variations. Current state-of-the-art approaches to address changing dynamics, such as adaptive control strategies or meta-learning techniques (e.g., Model-Agnostic Meta Learning, Meta-Reinforcement Learning), require an "n-shot" adaptation phase. This means collecting new data through further vehicle-terrain interactions after damage has occurred to fine-tune the model. This process is inherently risky, as it involves operating a compromised robot with an incorrect kinodynamics model, potentially exacerbating the damage or leading to unsafe maneuvers and mission failure.

The emergence of Transformer architectures and foundation models in robotics has advanced capabilities in capturing temporal context and long-horizon dependencies. While vision-language models have been employed for high-level reasoning or action generation, their application to describe and inform low-level kinodynamics based on the *intrinsic physical state* of the robot has been less explored. Existing robotic implementations of Transformers often tokenize the entire state tuple monolithically, which may obscure the independent effects of damage on specific degrees of freedom.

This research addresses a gap in the current landscape by proposing a zero-shot adaptation approach. Instead of relying on risky online data collection for adaptation, it leverages natural language descriptions of structural damage to immediately inform the kinodynamics model. This framework posits that while structural damages are heterogeneous and difficult to represent with a single metric, they are semantically distinct and describable through language. By aligning linguistic semantics with kinodynamic behaviors, the work aims to enable instantaneous adaptation, thereby mitigating the risks associated with existing n-shot online adaptation methods and extending the utility of language models to direct low-level robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the operational realities of high-performance autonomous mobile robots. These robots, when deployed in challenging "in-the-wild" environments, are subjected to substantial mechanical stresses (e.g., impact forces, torsional stress, vibration). Despite robust engineering, mechanical degradation and structural damage are inevitable consequences. Such damage – ranging from a punctured tire to a bent steering column or a broken suspension linkage – drastically alters the vehicle's kinodynamics, changing how forces are distributed and how the robot responds to control inputs. For instance, a punctured tire reduces traction and introduces rotational bias, even when a straight-line velocity is commanded. If the robot's navigation and control systems operate under the assumption of a healthy, nominal kinodynamics model while the physical platform is damaged, the resulting discrepancy can lead to significant tracking errors, instability, and potentially catastrophic failures such as collisions, rollovers, or falling off cliffs.

Compensating for these kinodynamics mismatches presents a significant challenge due to the heterogeneous nature of structural degradation. Damage can manifest as simple parametric deviations (e.g., tire pressure loss) or complex geometric deformations (e.g., broken suspension linkages) that fundamentally alter the robot's physical constraints. Traditional adaptive control or fine-tuning approaches require an "n-shot" adaptation phase, which necessitates collecting additional data through further vehicle-terrain interactions *after* the damage has occurred. This process introduces delays and compels the robot to operate in a compromised state with an incorrect kinodynamics model, posing safety risks and potentially worsening the mechanical failure during data collection.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **To develop a novel framework, Zero-shot Language Informed Kinodynamics (ZLIK), that enables instant adaptation to robot structural damage in a zero-shot manner.** This means the robot can adjust its internal kinodynamics model immediately upon receiving a damage description, without requiring any new data collection or retraining in the damaged state.
2. **To leverage natural language descriptions to represent and capture the wide variety of heterogeneous structural damages.** The hypothesis is that while damage types are diverse, they are semantically distinct and can be effectively described using language, providing a richer, more generalizable representation than scalar metrics.
3. **To learn a data-driven forward kinodynamics model that is conditioned on these language descriptions of damage.** This involves grounding the semantic information of damage descriptions in the robot's observed kinodynamic behaviors.
4. **To align language embeddings with kinodynamic responses through self-supervised learning.** This alignment ensures that linguistically similar descriptions or kinodynamically similar damage types are represented proximally in a shared embedding space.
5. **To integrate this language-informed representation into a spatiotemporal attention-based kinodynamics model.** The goal is for the model to predict future robot states accurately by accounting for the current structural health state as described by natural language.
6. **To rigorously validate the proposed ZLIK approach** through experiments that demonstrate zero-shot adaptation capabilities, architectural necessity, semantic grounding, and generalization across simulation-to-real and scale differences.

In essence, the research aims to make autonomous robots more robust and resilient to mechanical failures by allowing them to instantly comprehend and adapt to their changed physical state, thereby ensuring safer and more reliable task completion in unpredictable environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, Zero-shot Language Informed Kinodynamics (ZLIK), involves a two-stage learning process: first, self-supervised learning for damage representation, and second, supervised learning for downstream kinodynamics modeling.

**Problem Formulation:**
The robot's state at time $t$ is defined as $s_t \in SE(3)$, a 6-DoF pose ($[x_t, y_t, z_t, roll_t, pitch_t, yaw_t]^\top$). The control input is $u_t \in U$, representing linear and angular velocities ($[v_t, \omega_t]^\top$). Under nominal conditions, state evolution is governed by $s_{t+1} = f_{nominal}(s_t, u_t)$. Structural damage, denoted by $d \in D$, alters this physical mapping. To account for these alterations, the damage-aware forward kinodynamics is formulated as $s_{t+1} = f_{damage-aware}(s_{t-H+1:t}, u_{t-H+1:t}, d_t)$, where $H$ is the history length and $d_t$ is a latent variable representing the damage. The core challenge is to define and utilize $d_t$ effectively.

**Damage Representation:**
To enable zero-shot adaptation, ZLIK constructs a representation space that bridges natural language descriptions with kinodynamic behaviors.
1. **Natural Language Description:** The robot's health state is described by a natural language text $x_t$. A pre-trained Sentence Transformer ($\phi$) encodes $x_t$ into a semantic embedding. This ensures that semantically similar damage descriptions are mapped to spatially proximal embedding vectors.
2. **Kinodynamic Behavior:** The robot's kinodynamic behavior is defined by its state-action trajectory over a history length $H$, denoted as $\tau_{t,H} = ((s_{t-H+1}, u_{t-H+1}), \dots, (s_t, u_t))$.
3. **Shared Representation Space:** Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), $h_\sigma$ and $h_\zeta$, are used to map the natural language description $x$ and kinodynamic behavior $\tau$ into a shared latent space, yielding $z_x = h_\sigma(\phi(x))$ and $z_\tau = h_\zeta(\tau)$.
4. **Alignment using VICReg:** To align the linguistic semantic space with the physical behavioral space, Variance-Invariance-Covariance Regularization (VICReg) is employed as a self-supervised loss function ($L_{align}$). This loss comprises three terms:
 - **Invariance Term:** Minimizes the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the projected text embedding ($y_x$) and the projected kinodynamic behavior ($y_\tau$). This forces the language embedding to serve as a proxy for the physical reality of the damage.
 - **Variance Term:** Ensures diversity among damage embeddings across a batch, preventing model collapse where all embeddings map to a single point. It applies a hinge loss to ensure the standard deviation of each embedding dimension exceeds a threshold.
 - **Covariance Term:** Minimizes the sum of squared off-diagonal coefficients of the covariance matrix of the embeddings. This de-correlates the embedding dimensions, maximizing the information content by ensuring independent factors of dynamics are captured.
This self-supervised pre-training aims to find optimal encoder parameters ($\sigma^*, \zeta^*$) that minimize $L_{align}$ over paired samples of language descriptions and kinodynamic trajectories.

**Downstream Kinodynamics Modeling:**
After the damage representation stage, the projection head $h_\sigma$ is frozen. The learned language embedding $z_t^x$ (derived from $x_t$ provided by an external health monitoring system) replaces $d_t$ in the kinodynamics formulation.
1. **Forward Kinodynamic Function:** The model learns to approximate a forward kinodynamic function $f_\theta$ that predicts future states $s_{t+1:t+P}$ conditioned on the state-action history ($s_{t-H+1:t}, u_{t-H+1:t}$), future actions ($u_{t+1:t+P-1}$), and the damage embedding $z_t^x$.
2. **Supervised Training:** Optimal parameters $\theta^*$ for $f_\theta$ are learned in a supervised fashion by minimizing the MSE between actual and predicted future trajectories on a dataset of damaged robot operations.

**Spatiotemporal Attention (Transformer Architecture):**
$f_\theta$ is approximated using a Transformer Encoder-Decoder architecture, similar to Crossformer, designed to capture spatiotemporal dependencies.
1. **Dimension-Segment Embedding:** The raw state-action history $\tau_{t,H}$ is transformed using a Dimension-Segment Embedding ($E_{seg}$). This partitions the temporal history of each dimension into local windows of length $L_{seg}$, which are then projected into the Transformer's latent dimension $d_{model}$.
2. **Damage Context Injection:** The semantic damage embedding $z_x$ is projected and then added element-wise to every time segment and state dimension of the input feature array, along with learnable positional embeddings. This globally biases the robot's entire history with the semantic signature of the damage.
3. **Transformer Encoder:** A stack of two-stage attention layers processes the input. The first stage uses Multi-head Self-Attention (MSA) across time segments to capture temporal evolution for each dimension. The second stage applies MSA across dimensions for each time segment to capture inter-variate dependencies. This disentangled attention mechanism allows the network to learn how damage specifically alters correlations between physical dimensions. The output is a damage-informed memory, $M_{enc}$.
4. **Transformer Decoder:** A non-autoregressive Decoder, conditioned on future actions $u_{t+1:t+P-1}$, is used to predict the future trajectory. Future actions are projected and added to learnable positional query embeddings. The Decoder performs cross-attention with $M_{enc}$ and projects its output to the state dimension via a linear head to produce the predicted future states $\hat{s}_{t+1:t+P}$.

**Implementation Details:**
Data generation and experimentation use the high-fidelity soft-body physics simulator BeamNG.tech with a full-size vehicle. Six damage classes (e.g., Punctured Tire, Broken Axle, Fall) are used, defined by converting simulator-internal JSON health states to natural language. A random walk algorithm generates 150K training and 30K test data points. For real-world validation, a 1/10th scale Verti-4-Wheeler (V4W) platform is used with manually induced damages. EmbeddingGemma (300M) is the sentence Transformer backbone. The kinodynamic trajectory encoder is a 3-layer Transformer Encoder. MLPs project embeddings to a 128-dimensional shared latent space. The damaged kinodynamics model uses history $H=40$ steps (2 seconds) and prediction horizon $P=10$ steps (0.5 seconds), with input segments of length $L_{seg}=4$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates ZLIK's performance across four core research questions: zero-shot adaptation, architectural necessity, semantic grounding, and cross-embodiment generalization.

**Research Question 1: Zero-Shot Performance**
ZLIK demonstrated significant superiority in zero-shot adaptation to structural failures compared to baselines.
- **Performance Comparison:** ZLIK achieved the lowest trajectory prediction error (MSE) across all tested damage classes. Its overall MSE was 0.50 ± 1.29, compared to 1.59 ± 1.55 for CleanTransformer (non-adaptive) and 2.59 ± 2.44 for ANYCAR (zero-shot, state-of-the-art adaptive baseline).
- **Adaptation Efficiency:** ZLIK generalized to unseen damages without requiring any online data collection or weight updates. Even after 10 minutes of environment-specific fine-tuning, ANYCAR (e.g., 2.76 ± 3.13 for 'Fall' damage) failed to match ZLIK's precision (1.52 ± 2.48 for 'Fall' damage), highlighting the benefits of instant, language-conditioned adaptation over delayed online retraining.
- **Necessity of Damage Representation:** The CleanTransformer, despite a similar backbone, performed poorly (e.g., 3.85 ± 2.46 for 'Fall' damage), underscoring the critical role of explicit damage representation in kinodynamics modeling for compromised robots.

**Research Question 2: Architectural Necessity**
The 6-DoF state decomposition employed in ZLIK was validated as essential for effective damage modeling.
- **State Decomposition Impact:** ZLIK (MSE 0.50 ± 1.09) significantly outperformed a Monolithic Transformer baseline (MSE 0.81 ± 1.11), which processed the robot's state as a single token.
- **Complex Damage Handling:** For complex damage classes like 'Fall' and 'Multiple Tires Punctured & Suspensions Broken,' the Monolithic Transformer exhibited higher prediction errors across individual state dimensions (x, y, z, roll, pitch, yaw). This indicates that without explicit state decomposition, the model struggles to disentangle and address the complex, non-linear disturbances induced by vehicle damage, where specific degrees of freedom may be disproportionately affected.

**Research Question 3: Semantic Grounding**
ZLIK demonstrated successful alignment between linguistic semantics and physical behaviors.
- **Diagonal Dominance:** A confusion matrix (Table III) showed clear diagonal dominance. Prediction error was minimized when the input natural language description correctly matched the actual physical state of the robot. When an incorrect damage description was provided, prediction error increased significantly.
- **Validation of Language-Informed Conditioning:** This finding validates that the model effectively uses the language-informed semantic embedding ($z_x$) to condition its kinodynamics prediction.
- **Subtle Nuance Capture:** Even in cases of severe damage (e.g., 'Multiple Tires Punctured & Suspensions Broken') where maneuverability is restricted and kinodynamic prediction inherently easier, the inclusion of the correct damage embedding provided a measurable positive impact, demonstrating the model's ability to capture subtle nuances in independent degrees of freedom.

**Research Question 4: Cross-Embodiment Generalization**
ZLIK exhibited generalization capabilities across significant domain shifts.
- **Sim-to-Real and Scale Transfer:** The model trained on a full-size simulated vehicle was tested on a 1/10th scale physical Verti-4-Wheeler (V4W) robot with unseen damages (front-left tire punctured, rear-left wheel removed).
- **Performance on Physical Platform:** Despite an expected increase in relative error rates across all models due to unmodeled physical factors, sensor noise, and communication latency inherent to sim-to-real and scale gaps, ZLIK consistently demonstrated improvement over the CleanTransformer, Monolithic Transformer, and ANYCAR baselines in a zero-shot manner on the physical platform.
- **Robust Semantic Prior:** This result suggests that ZLIK's semantic embeddings capture the fundamental nature of damage (e.g., "loss of a wheel leads to drag on that side") rather than overfitting to the specific training vehicle in simulation. The semantic grounding provided a sufficiently robust prior to outperform baselines, effectively bridging the domain gap.

Overall, the findings indicate that ZLIK provides a robust, zero-shot solution for adapting robot kinodynamics to structural damage by effectively leveraging natural language, demonstrating superior accuracy and generalization across different damage types, model architectures, and physical embodiments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of autonomous robotics, particularly concerning the resilience and adaptability of mobile platforms operating in dynamic and unpredictable environments. The core significance and potential impact of ZLIK are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Robot Resilience and Safety:** By enabling zero-shot adaptation to structural damage, ZLIK directly addresses a critical safety and operational challenge. Traditional methods requiring online data collection after damage are inherently risky, potentially leading to further mechanical failure, mission aborts, or catastrophic accidents. ZLIK's ability to instantly adjust the kinodynamics model based on a language description of damage minimizes the period of operating with an incorrect model, thereby enhancing the robot's resilience and operating safety.

2. **Reduction of Operational Downtime and Costs:** Eliminating the need for n-shot online retraining implies that robots can continue their missions, albeit with altered kinodynamics, without extensive downtime for data collection and model re-calibration. This has direct implications for reducing operational costs and improving mission completion rates in critical applications where immediate response to damage is paramount.

3. **Novel Paradigm for Damage Representation:** The work introduces a novel paradigm by leveraging natural language to represent heterogeneous and complex structural damages. Unlike scalar metrics or fixed parameterizations, language offers a semantically rich and flexible way to describe a wide spectrum of failures, from subtle deviations to fundamental changes in robot mechanics. This approach unlocks the potential of large pre-trained language models for low-level physical adaptation, extending their utility beyond high-level reasoning.

4. **Improved Generalization Capabilities:** The demonstrated cross-embodiment generalization, from a full-sized simulated vehicle to a 1/10th scale physical robot, signifies that the learned semantic embeddings capture the fundamental nature of damage rather than being tied to specific physical parameters of the training platform. This robustness across significant sim-to-real and scale gaps suggests broad applicability across diverse robotic platforms and scenarios.

5. **Integration with Health Monitoring Systems:** ZLIK's reliance on a natural language description of damage makes it directly compatible with external vehicle health monitoring systems. As these systems become more sophisticated in diagnosing and articulating robot structural health, ZLIK can instantly integrate this information to update its internal models, forming a robust feedback loop for adaptive control.

6. **Advancement in Data-Efficient Learning:** By achieving zero-shot adaptation, the method drastically reduces the data requirements for handling novel damage types. Instead of needing new damage-specific interaction data, ZLIK leverages pre-existing semantic knowledge embedded in language models, representing a step towards more data-efficient and flexible robot learning.

In the long term, the principles demonstrated by ZLIK could contribute to the development of more autonomous, robust, and intelligent robotic systems capable of operating reliably in highly dynamic and challenging environments where mechanical failures are an expected part of operation. Further integration with environmental context, such as terrain geometry and semantics, could further enhance the model's adaptability to complex interaction scenarios.
