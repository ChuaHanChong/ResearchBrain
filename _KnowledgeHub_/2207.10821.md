# Rethinking Sim2Real: Lower Fidelity Simulation Leads to Higher Sim2Real Transfer in Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Research from Georgia Institute of Technology and Meta AI challenges the conventional view in sim2real, demonstrating that training high-level visual navigation policies for legged robots in lower fidelity kinematic simulations leads to superior real-world transfer. This approach achieved a 100% success rate on a real Boston Dynamics Spot robot, with improved path efficiency and reduced collisions, by enabling substantially larger-scale training compared to high-fidelity dynamic simulations.

## Problem

- The prevailing assumption in sim2real is that increasing simulator physics fidelity directly reduces the sim2real gap.
- High-fidelity dynamic simulations are computationally expensive, limiting the volume of training data and wall-clock time available for deep reinforcement learning.
- Policies trained in high-fidelity simulations risk overfitting to specific, imperfect simulated dynamics or struggling with proprietary black-box low-level controllers on real robots.

## Method

- A ==hierarchical control architecture== is proposed, where a ==high-level visual navigation policy== is trained in simulation and a manufacturer-provided ==low-level controller== handles hardware execution.
- A ==lower fidelity kinematic simulation== is developed that abstracts away complex low-level physics by ==teleporting== the robot to desired poses, significantly increasing simulation speed.
- The performance of policies trained using this kinematic simulation is systematically compared against those trained in traditional ==high-fidelity dynamic simulations== for visual navigation tasks.

## Results

- Kinematic-trained policies achieved a **100%** success rate in zero-shot transfer on a real Boston Dynamics Spot robot for point-goal navigation, exhibiting higher path efficiency (SPL of **82-83%**) and significantly fewer collisions.
- Dynamic-trained policies demonstrated reduced real-world success rates (**40-67%**), lower path efficiency, and substantially more collisions due to the sim2real dynamics gap and overfitting.
- Kinematic simulation enabled approximately **10 times** more training steps (**500 million** vs. **50 million**) within equivalent wall-clock time budgets, directly contributing to superior policy performance and generalization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prioritizing simulation speed over detailed physics fidelity can yield better sim2real transfer for high-level tasks like visual navigation, primarily by enabling greater training scale.
> - Policies trained in simplified kinematic simulations learn more robust and generalizable high-level navigation strategies, preventing overfitting to specific low-level dynamic inaccuracies.
> - Incorporating low-dimensional real-world actuation noise models into fast kinematic simulations further enhances the real-world robustness of policies without sacrificing training speed.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The "sim2real" paradigm, where robots are trained in simulation before deployment in reality, has garnered significant attention and investment in recent years. This includes the development of increasingly sophisticated simulators, the curation of detailed 3D assets, and the formulation of techniques to bridge the gap between simulated and real-world performance. A common underlying assumption in this field is that reducing the "sim2real gap" necessitates increasing the physical fidelity of simulators, aiming to make them as accurate a replica of reality as possible. This approach often involves highly detailed physics engines and intricate modeling of robot dynamics.

This paper directly challenges this conventional assumption. Instead of advocating for higher fidelity, the authors propose a contrary hypothesis: that sim2real transfer for robots, particularly in navigation tasks, might be improved with *lower* fidelity simulation. This work positions itself against the prevailing trend of continuously investing in more complex physics simulations by suggesting that such complexity can be detrimental due to slow simulation speeds and the potential for policies to overfit to simulation inaccuracies.

In the context of visual navigation, previous works have demonstrated the success of end-to-end learning in large-scale indoor environments using simulators like Habitat and iGibson, primarily for wheeled robots. For legged robots, sim2real locomotion has been studied extensively, often focusing on learning low-level skills in simulation and transferring them to hardware, or adapting them online. However, many of these approaches either focus on proprioceptive sensing, or, when using visual input, rely on learned or hand-designed physically simulated low-level controllers. This paper diverges by arguing that these physics simulations can impede the learning of high-performing sim2real policies, even for complex legged robots.

Furthermore, while the concept of abstracted (hierarchical, high-level) action spaces is prevalent in robotics (e.g., task and motion planning for manipulation, locomotion, navigation), typically both high- and low-level policies are learned within simulation. This work, however, proposes an abstracted simulator that intentionally omits modeling low-level physics, allowing high-level policies to be transferred to the real world in a zero-shot manner, in contrast to methods that rely heavily on domain randomization or real-world adaptation during transfer.

By systematically evaluating the impact of simulation fidelity, this research seeks to reframe the discussion around sim2real strategies, particularly for tasks where a hierarchical control architecture can leverage manufacturer-provided black-box low-level controllers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to challenge the widely held assumption that increasing simulation fidelity is uniformly beneficial for improving sim2real transfer in robotics. Specifically, the authors propose and evaluate the counter-intuitive hypothesis that *lower* fidelity simulation may lead to *higher* sim2real transfer for robot navigation tasks.

The motivation for this investigation is multifaceted:

1. **Challenging a Default Hypothesis:** The paper begins by identifying the "natural and almost self-evident presumption" within the robotics community that reducing the sim2real gap inherently involves creating simulators with increasing physics fidelity. The authors aim to systematically test and potentially overturn this default operating hypothesis.
2. **Addressing Practical Limitations of High-Fidelity Simulation:**
 - **Slow Simulation Speed:** High-fidelity physics simulations, especially those modeling complex rigid-body and contact dynamics at high frequencies (e.g., 240 Hz), are computationally expensive. This slow speed limits the amount of experience an agent can acquire within a given wall-clock training budget, which is a critical bottleneck for data-intensive learning methods like deep reinforcement learning (RL).
 - **Overfitting to Simulation Inaccuracies:** Current physics simulators are approximations of reality and contain imperfections. When learning-based methods are trained extensively in high-fidelity simulations, they risk overfitting to these specific, potentially inaccurate, dynamics. This overfitting can lead to learned policies that perform poorly when transferred to the real world, which has different, albeit imperfect, dynamics.
 - **Difficulty with Black-Box Controllers:** Many commercial robots (e.g., Boston Dynamics Spot) come equipped with manufacturer-provided "black-box" low-level controllers whose internal implementations are not publicly accessible or accurately replicable in simulation. Attempting to simulate these complex, proprietary dynamics introduces significant modeling challenges and potential sources of sim2real mismatch.
3. **Proposing a Hierarchical Control Architecture:** To address these issues, the authors motivate a hierarchical control approach. This involves decomposing the robot's policy into a high-level component that is trained in simulation and a low-level controller that operates directly on hardware (potentially a black-box controller). This decomposition means the simulator does not need to model the intricate low-level dynamics, offering several advantages:
 - **Reduced Simulation Complexity and Cost:** By abstracting away low-level physics, the simulation becomes simpler and faster, requiring less computational resources and wall-clock time.
 - **Reduced Development Effort:** Developers are spared the time and effort required to accurately build and tune complex low-level controllers within the simulator, especially for closed-source robots.
 - **Improved Generalization:** An abstracted simulation focuses on higher-level reasoning, reducing the risk of overfitting to low-level physical nuances that may not transfer well.

The paper aims to empirically demonstrate that this approach, using lower-fidelity kinematic simulation for training high-level navigation policies, can lead to superior sim2real transfer performance compared to training with high-fidelity dynamic simulations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research conducts a large-scale, systematic evaluation of its central hypothesis on the task of visual navigation, employing a controlled experimental setup across multiple simulators and robot platforms.

**Task Definition:**
The specific task investigated is PointGoal Navigation in unknown environments. The robot is tasked with navigating to a goal coordinate specified relative to its starting location (e.g., "go to Δx, Δy"). The robot receives egocentric depth sensor input and an egomotion sensor (GPS+Compass) to determine its current pose and relative goal location. An episode is successful if the robot reaches the goal within half its body length, subject to maximum steps and velocity limits. Performance is measured by Success Rate (SR) and Success inversely weighted by Path Length (SPL).

**Robot Platforms:**
Three quadrupedal robots are used in simulation: A1 and Aliengo (from Unitree) and Spot (from Boston Dynamics). Real-world sim2real transfer experiments are conducted with the Spot robot. An Intel RealSense D435 camera is consistently used for visual inputs across all robots in both simulation and reality. Spot's onboard collision-avoidance is enabled with a tight threshold (0.10m), and potential collisions are tracked when the robot approaches within 0.20m of an obstacle.

**Simulation Environments:**
Two simulation platforms are utilized:
- **Habitat:** Known for photorealistic rendering and C++ integration with the Bullet physics engine, leading to fast simulation speeds (approximately 1200% faster than iGibson).
- **iGibson:** Also supports photorealistic rendering but uses PyBullet (Python-based Bullet integration), resulting in slower simulation speeds.
The shared underlying physics engine (Bullet) allows for controlled comparison despite differences in integration efficiency.

**Dataset and Environment Generation:**
Training and evaluation leverage the Habitat-Matterport (HM3D) and Gibson 3D datasets, comprising over 1000 high-resolution 3D scans of real-world indoor environments with realistic clutter. Episodes are generated to ensure a geodesic distance between 1 and 30m, avoiding near-straight paths or those with few obstacles, and checking for navigability using the largest robot's URDF.

**Real-World Test Environment:**
A 325m² commercial office building lobby, named LAB, serves as the real-world evaluation environment. It features furniture and clutter. Five specific waypoints define navigation episodes with an average length of 10m. The furniture layout is matched to a 3D scan for consistent evaluation across simulation and reality. This LAB environment is distinct from the training datasets.

**Hierarchical Control Architecture:**
The proposed approach is hierarchical:
1. **High-level visual navigation policy:** Operates at 1Hz, predicting desired Center of Mass (CoM) forward, lateral, and angular velocities (Vx, Vy, ω).
2. **Low-level controller:** Translates these desired velocities into robot actions.

**High-level Visual Navigation Policy Details:**
- **Input:** Egocentric depth image, goal location relative to the robot's current pose.
- **Output:** 3-dimensional vector (Vx, Vy, ω).
- **Network Architecture:** ResNet-18 visual encoder combined with a 2-layer LSTM policy to capture temporal dependencies. The final layer parameterizes a Gaussian action distribution.
- **Training:** Distributed Reinforcement Learning using DD-PPO.
- **Reward Function:** Based on, incorporating geodesic distance reward, penalties for collisions, falling, and backward velocities, and a slack penalty.

**Low-level Control and Simulation Fidelities:**
The core of the methodology lies in comparing two distinct levels of simulation fidelity for the low-level controller:

- **Kinematic Control and Simulation:**
 - **Mechanism:** The desired CoM velocity from the high-level policy is integrated at 1Hz, and the robot is "teleported" directly to the calculated pose. If the new state results in a collision, the robot remains in its current position.
 - **Purpose:** To abstract away complex low-level physics, avoid detailed modeling of proprietary controllers (like Spot's), and significantly increase simulation speed, enabling larger-scale RL training.
 - **Augmentation:** To improve kinematic fidelity, actuation noise collected from a real Spot robot (difference between commanded and true velocity) is modeled as a bivariate Gaussian distribution (decoupled and coupled methods) and injected into the kinematic simulation during training.

- **Dynamic Control in Simulation:**
 - **Mechanism:** Commands desired CoM velocities are converted into joint torques, which are applied to a rigid-body physics simulation (Bullet) operating at 240Hz. This models contact dynamics.
 - **Controllers Used:**
 - **Raibert-style controller:** An expert-designed controller, modified for lateral movement. Used for *training* dynamic policies due to its comparative speed.
 - **Model-Predictive Control (MPC):** A more advanced controller known for better velocity tracking. Used for *evaluation* of dynamic policies in simulation (too slow for training). This serves as a proxy for unknown real-world black-box controllers and aims to improve dynamic policy performance.

**Experimental Scope:**
The study involves an exhaustive combinatorial exploration: policies are trained under two conditions (kinematic and dynamic simulation), evaluated under two conditions (kinematic and dynamic simulation), across two simulators (Habitat and iGibson), and for three robots (A1, Aliengo, Spot). Each condition is run with three random seeds. The total training budget amounts to 6,912 GPU-hours (288 GPU-days), with policies trained for 3-7 days. Evaluation is conducted across 1,100 episodes from 110 unique scenes in a held-out validation set.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across extensive sim2sim and sim2real evaluations, which consistently support the hypothesis that lower fidelity (kinematic) simulation leads to better transfer in visual navigation.

#### 4.1. Sim2Sim Gap Analysis (Generalization Across Simulators and Controllers)

A large-scale evaluation was conducted across various training and evaluation conditions, robots, and simulators, accumulating 6,912 GPU-hours of training. The average success rates (SR) were analyzed for policies trained under kinematic and dynamic simulation, and then evaluated under both kinematic and dynamic control in Habitat and iGibson.

1. **Kinematic-trained policies demonstrate superior overall performance:** Across all robots and evaluation settings, policies trained in kinematic simulation consistently outperformed those trained in dynamic simulation. This holds true even when kinematic policies were evaluated using dynamic control, an out-of-distribution setting for them. For example, the A1 robot trained kinematically in iGibson achieved 62.1% SR when evaluated kinematically, compared to 24.2% SR for a dynamically trained policy evaluated kinematically. More significantly, kinematic policies often outperformed dynamic policies even when both were evaluated dynamically.
2. **Dynamic policies exhibit poor robustness and generalization:** Dynamic policies trained in one simulator (e.g., Habitat-Dynamic) experienced significant performance degradation when evaluated in another dynamic simulator (e.g., iGibson-Dynamic). For instance, an A1 policy trained in Habitat-Dynamic achieved 49.8% SR in Habitat-Dynamic, but only 22.3% SR in iGibson-Dynamic. This sensitivity indicates that dynamic policies tend to overfit to the specific, approximate dynamics of their training simulator, struggling to generalize to even slightly different dynamic models.
3. **Kinematic policies generalize effectively to different dynamic controllers:** Policies trained with no explicit physics modeling (kinematic) demonstrated robust generalization across different dynamic controllers. This suggests that these policies learn high-level navigational reasoning without becoming over-reliant on or overfit to specific low-level physical interactions.

#### 4.2. Why Kinematic-trained Policies Outperform Dynamic Ones: The Role of Scale

The performance advantage of kinematic policies is attributed primarily to the significant increase in training scale they enable.
- **Faster Training, More Experience:** Kinematic simulation is substantially faster than dynamic simulation. Despite kinematic policies being trained for 2.3 times *less* wall-clock time (3 days vs. 7 days for convergence), they were able to learn from approximately 10 times *more* steps of experience (500 million steps vs. 50 million steps) under identical compute budgets.
- **Intelligent High-level Reasoning:** This increased experience allows kinematic policies to develop more robust and intelligent high-level navigation strategies, which better handle environmental complexities and generalize more effectively. The limitation of wall-clock time, rather than experience itself, is highlighted as a critical factor in learning-based sim2real approaches.

#### 4.3. Sim2Real Gap for Kinematic and Dynamic Trained Policies (Spot Robot in Real-World)

Policies trained in simulation were evaluated for zero-shot transfer on a real Spot robot in a novel, cluttered LAB environment.

1. **Kinematic policies achieve high real-world success:** All kinematic policies (trained in Habitat or iGibson, with or without noise modeling) demonstrated a 100% success rate (SR) in the real-world LAB environment, with high path efficiency (SPL of 82-83%). These policies also commanded fewer actions and resulted in significantly fewer collisions (e.g., 26.4 actions, 3.1 collisions for Habitat Kinematic) compared to dynamic policies. This indicates robust and efficient navigation.
2. **Dynamic policies show reduced real-world performance:** Dynamic policies struggled more in the real world, achieving success rates of 40-67% and lower SPL. They often commanded lower velocities, frequently getting stuck around obstacles. This resulted in a higher number of commanded actions and significantly more collisions (e.g., 107.9 actions, 41.2 collisions for Habitat Dynamic). This performance degradation is attributed to the "dynamics gap" between the low-level controllers used in training simulation and the black-box controller on the real Spot robot, as well as the impoverished training experience.
3. **Actuation noise modeling further enhances kinematic performance:** Injecting real-world actuation noise, collected from the Spot robot, into the kinematic simulation during training further improved path efficiency (SPL increased by 4.6-5.6%) and reduced collisions and commanded actions in the real world, while maintaining 100% success. This demonstrates that incorporating low-dimensional real-world characteristics can make kinematic policies more robust without sacrificing speed or generalizability. Policies trained with noise exhibited less risky actions, leading to safer real-world execution.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant challenge to a fundamental assumption within the sim2real robotics community and offers a new perspective on simulator design and policy training strategies.

**Rethinking Simulation Fidelity:** The most immediate impact is its direct contradiction of the belief that higher simulation fidelity is inherently better for sim2real transfer. The findings suggest that for certain classes of tasks, particularly those amenable to hierarchical control with abstracted action spaces like visual navigation, an overly detailed physics simulation can be detrimental. This necessitates a re-evaluation of current research priorities and investment in simulator development.

**Prioritizing Simulation Speed:** The work strongly advocates for prioritizing simulation speed over physics fidelity when tasks can be effectively represented by abstract action spaces. This implies that future simulator development efforts, for a broad range of tasks, might be better directed towards optimizing computational efficiency and parallelism rather than increasing the complexity of physical modeling. This could democratize access to large-scale RL training by reducing the computational resources (wall-clock time) required.

**Advocating for Hierarchical Policy Architectures:** The paper reinforces the value of hierarchical control. By training a high-level policy in a simplified, fast simulation and leveraging existing, robust black-box low-level controllers on hardware, the sim2real gap can be effectively managed. This approach is particularly impactful for commercial robots where low-level controller details are proprietary and cannot be accurately replicated in simulation. It streamlines the development process by removing the need for complex, often iterative, low-level controller modeling in simulation.

**Improved Generalization and Robustness:** Kinematic policies demonstrated superior generalization across different simulators and dynamic controllers, and robust zero-shot transfer to the real world. This indicates that abstracting away low-level dynamics encourages the learning of more fundamental, task-relevant reasoning, rather than overfitting to specific, potentially flawed, simulation characteristics. The successful integration of real-world actuation noise models into kinematic simulation further demonstrates a pragmatic path to enhancing realism without sacrificing speed.

**Broader Applicability and Limitations:** While the results are compelling for legged locomotion and visual navigation, the underlying principle of using an abstracted simulation for high-level policy training in conjunction with hardware-specific low-level controllers is broadly applicable to other tasks that can be represented with high-level actions (e.g., object rearrangement like pick-and-place). However, the authors acknowledge that this approach is not universal. Tasks requiring intricate, low-level physical interactions with the environment, such as dexterous manipulation or the development of novel low-level walking controllers, would still necessitate high-fidelity physics simulations. Future work aims to enhance the modeling of real-world actuation noise, potentially using neural networks, for more nuanced simulation of hardware characteristics within the kinematic framework.

In summary, this research challenges a core tenet of sim2real, proposing that "lower fidelity" can yield "higher transfer" by enabling greater scale and preventing overfitting, thereby offering a practical and effective strategy for training intelligent embodied agents.
