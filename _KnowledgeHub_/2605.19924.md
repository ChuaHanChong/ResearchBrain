# RoHIL: Robust Human-in-the-Loop Robotic Reinforcement Learning Against Illumination Variations

> [!summary] Summary
> The RoHIL framework enhances Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning policies to operate robustly under diverse illumination conditions by using an offline fine-tuning approach with world-model relighting and anti-forgetting mechanisms. It enables HIL-RL policy deployment costs to reduce from O(N) to O(1) and achieves 1.00 success rates on tasks like USB insertion even under extreme lighting variations, outperforming existing baselines.

## Problem

- Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning (HIL-RL) policies, while effective in training environments, severely degrade when deployed in new workstations due to visual domain shifts caused by illumination variations.
- Current remedies are impractical: re-collecting demonstrations and retraining is costly (O(N)), simple image augmentations fail to capture complex light changes, and naive fine-tuning leads to catastrophic forgetting.
- The challenge is to make HIL-RL policies robust to significant illumination variations without incurring prohibitive retraining costs or losing proficiency in the original training environment.

## Method

- A world-model relighter (Cosmos-Transfer1-DiffusionRenderer) generates diverse and realistic illumination variations from existing source-workstation data, avoiding the need for new real-robot interaction.
- Illumination-Retention Replay (IRR) is a data-level mechanism that carefully mixes original-light policy data with relit policy and demonstration data in the replay buffer (with an optimal original-light fraction of "alpha"=0.75) to balance adaptation and retention.
- Anchored Bellman–Actor Regularization applies model-level anti-forgetting by adding feature and actor-side reference-action anchors to the Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) objective, regularizing the visual encoder and policy towards a frozen source-workstation policy on expert demonstrations.

## Results

- The full RoHIL framework achieved 1.00 success rates on both source and shifted lighting conditions for the critical `usb_insertion` task after offline fine-tuning, significantly outperforming a standard SAC objective which resulted in lower success rates (0.57 source, 0.73 shifted).
- An ablation study demonstrated that the combination of both feature and actor-side reference-action anchors was necessary to achieve simultaneous high success rates (1.**00/1**.00) on source and shifted lighting, with individual anchors only partially addressing the problem.
- RoHIL consistently achieved the highest success rates across four real-robot manipulation tasks (e.g., RAM 1.00, USB 1.00, wiping 0.87, breaker 0.83) at a **60%** illumination shift, demonstrating a significant robustness advantage over five HIL-compatible baselines that experienced substantial performance degradation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Generating diverse and realistic illumination variations from existing real-robot trajectories via world-model relighting is an efficient way to create training data for illumination robustness without costly new physical interaction.
> - Combining both data-level (Illumination-Retention Replay) and model-level (Anchored Bellman–Actor Regularization) anti-forgetting mechanisms is crucial for stable adaptation to new illumination conditions while simultaneously preventing catastrophic forgetting of the source task.
> - Separate anchor terms targeting the visual encoder (feature anchor) and the actor's action distribution (reference-action anchor) address distinct failure modes, synergistically contributing to robust performance across illumination shifts and preserving source-workstation proficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning (HIL-RL), exemplified by methods like HIL-SERL, has emerged as a methodology for achieving sample-efficient robotic manipulation in real-world settings. These systems typically involve seeding a Reinforcement Learning (RL) agent with human demonstrations and allowing an operator to intervene during online interaction to correct failures. This approach has demonstrated the capacity to achieve high success rates on contact-rich tasks within the specific workstation where training occurs.

However, a significant limitation of existing HIL-RL methods is their lack of robustness to visual domain shifts, particularly those caused by variations in illumination. When a robot trained in one workstation is moved to another, even a short distance away, changes in lighting (e.g., new lamp positions, window light, shadows, specular highlights) can cause the learned policy to degrade substantially. This degradation is attributed to shifts in the visual input distribution, which the policy's visual encoder is not prepared for.

Current approaches to address this problem generally involve trade-offs. Simple pixel-level augmentations (e.g., RAD, DrQ) can improve sample efficiency but often fail to realistically capture the structured changes induced by different light transport phenomena. Re-collecting demonstrations and re-running the HIL process for each new workstation is a direct but resource-intensive solution, rendering deployment costs linear with the number of environments (O(N)). Conversely, naive fine-tuning of a source-workstation policy on data from new lighting conditions frequently leads to "catastrophic forgetting," where the policy loses its proficiency on the original source environment.

This research addresses the challenge of making HIL-RL policies robust to illumination variations without incurring the O(N) cost of retraining or suffering from catastrophic forgetting. It positions itself within the broader context of Domain-Incremental Learning and fine-tuning strategies designed to mitigate the stability-plasticity dilemma in machine learning. By leveraging world models for realistic image relighting and integrating specific anti-forgetting mechanisms at both the data and model levels, this work aims to provide an offline solution that bridges the visual domain gap efficiently. It builds upon foundational RL methods like SAC and replay mechanisms like RLPD, while also drawing inspiration from continual learning techniques that use replay and regularization to preserve knowledge. The paper differentiates its approach from generalist Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models or broader skill transfer methods by focusing on a narrower, but critical, domain shift problem: illumination robustness for an already-solved HIL policy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that enables Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning (HIL-RL) policies to operate robustly under significant illumination variations between their training environment and deployment environments. This objective is driven by several motivations and aims to overcome specific challenges inherent in deploying HIL-RL systems.

**Motivation and Problem Statement:**

1. **Deployment Challenge of HIL-RL Policies:** While HIL-RL systems, such as HIL-SERL, achieve high success rates on robotic manipulation tasks within their training workstation, their performance severely degrades when the robot is moved to a new workstation. This degradation stems from changes in the visual input distribution (e.g., shifts in lamp positions, daylight direction, shadows, specular highlights) that were not present during initial training.
2. **Impracticality of Current Remedies:**
 - **Re-collection and Retraining (O(N) Cost):** The current de facto approach of re-collecting human demonstrations and initiating a new HIL-RL training session for every new workstation is cost-prohibitive and incompatible with large-scale deployment. This method scales linearly with the number of deployment environments, making it impractical for real-world scenarios requiring widespread application.
 - **Insufficient Augmentations:** Simple image augmentation techniques (e.g., pixel jitter) are inadequate for modeling the complex and structured changes in light transport that characterize real-world illumination variations. They fail to generate visually realistic and diverse lighting conditions.
 - **Catastrophic Forgetting from Naive Fine-tuning:** Directly fine-tuning the pre-trained policy on data from new lighting conditions, without specific safeguards, causes "catastrophic forgetting." The policy overwrites the representations and learned behaviors crucial for the original source workstation, leading to a collapse in performance in the environment it was initially trained for.

**Specific Objectives Addressed by RoHIL:**

To close the "cross-domain gap" and overcome these limitations, the research outlines three specific problems that RoHIL aims to solve:

1. **Realistic Illumination Variation without New Robot Interaction:** The framework needs a method to generate diverse and realistic illumination variations from existing data, thereby avoiding the need for new, costly real-robot interactions in different lighting environments.
2. **Effective Policy Adaptation to Shifted Lighting Domains:** Once varied illumination data is available, the policy must be adapted to these new visual inputs in a way that allows it to generalize to unseen lighting conditions.
3. **Preservation of Source-Workstation Policy during Adaptation:** Crucially, during the adaptation process, the framework must ensure that the policy's performance on the original source workstation is maintained, preventing catastrophic forgetting and ensuring continued proficiency in the initial training environment.

By addressing these specific problems, the overarching goal is to develop an offline fine-tuning framework that allows a single HIL-RL training run on a source workstation to be amortized across numerous deployment-time lighting environments, thereby reducing deployment costs to O(1) in terms of human interaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RoHIL is presented as an offline fine-tuning framework designed to adapt a source-workstation HIL-SERL policy to various illumination shifts without requiring additional real-robot interaction post-initial training. The methodology comprises three interconnected stages: world-model relighting, Illumination-Retention Replay (IRR), and anchored Bellman–actor regularization.

**Stage 1: Source-Workstation Collection and World-Model Relighting**

This initial stage focuses on generating illumination-diverse training data from existing real-robot trajectories.
1. **Data Collection**: A standard HIL-SERL session is performed on a single "source workstation." This generates real robot trajectories, stored as transitions `(o_t, a_t, r_t, o_{t+1}, d_t)`. Here, `o_t` and `o_{t+1}` are visual observations, `a_t` is the action, `r_t` is the reward, and `d_t` is the termination flag.
2. **Relighting Principle**: The core idea is that `a_t`, `r_t`, and `d_t` correspond to physical interactions and remain valid regardless of illumination. Therefore, RoHIL preserves these components while re-synthesizing only the visual observations `o_t` and `o_{t+1}` under different lighting conditions. This process generates realistic visual variation while maintaining the grounding in real robot experiences.
3. **World-Model Relighter Implementation**: The framework uses `Cosmos-Transfer1-DiffusionRenderer` for this task. This is an HDRI-conditioned video relighter that takes a recorded RGB stream and a target HDRI environment map to produce a relit RGB stream. The relit stream alters the illumination state (e.g., shadows, highlights, color temperature) while preserving the scene's geometry, object layout, and material properties. For each original trajectory, four distinct target lighting conditions are synthesized, resulting in five illumination variants (the original plus four relit versions) sharing the same actions and rewards. DiffusionRenderer was chosen for its balance of visual quality and computational cost compared to other candidate relighters.

**Stage 2: Illumination-Retention Replay (IRR)**

IRR is a data-level mechanism designed to prevent catastrophic forgetting by carefully managing the replay buffer.
1. **Four Data Pools**: After relighting, the original source-workstation data is expanded into four distinct pools:
 - `R⁰_π`: Original-light policy-driven RL data (containing both successes and failures).
 - `D⁰`: Original-light human demonstrations (expert data).
 - `R^{rel}_π`: Relit policy-driven RL data.
 - `D^{rel}`: Relit human demonstrations.
2. **IRR Mechanism**: It adapts the standard RLPD (Reinforcement Learning from Play Data) symmetric replay structure, which typically splits minibatches 50/50 between RL and demonstration data. IRR introduces a retention coefficient `α` into the RL portion to balance original-light data with relit data.
 - The replay minibatch `B` is composed of two halves: `B = 0.5 * Sample(R_α) ∪ 0.5 * Sample(D_anc)`.
 - `R_α = α * R⁰_π + (1-α) * (R^{rel}_π ∪ D^{rel})`. This formulation ensures a mix of original-light policy-driven data with relit policy-driven and relit demonstration data. Empirically, `α=0.75` was determined as the optimal mixing ratio.
 - `D_anc = D⁰ ∪ D^{rel}`. This combined pool of all original and relit expert demonstrations serves as the "anchor pool" for the next stage.
3. **Rationale**: This data-level anti-forgetting mechanism ensures that the policy receives adaptation signals from the relit data while simultaneously retaining Bellman coverage over the source workstation's full distribution of experiences, including non-expert trajectories and failure states. Anchoring for the next stage is focused on high-quality expert data to avoid reinforcing suboptimal behaviors.

**Stage 3: Anchored Bellman–Actor Regularization**

This stage provides representation- and policy-level anti-forgetting by regularizing the fine-tuning process.
1. **Problem Addressed**: While IRR manages data, fine-tuning can still cause the visual encoder and policy to drift away from the successful source-workstation configuration.
2. **SAC Update Foundation**: The fine-tuning process begins with the standard Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) updates for the twin critics (`L_Bellman`) and the actor (`L_SAC`), using the mixed minibatches `B` from IRR.
3. **Frozen-Source Anchors**: Two regularization terms are added, referencing a `frozen source-workstation policy (θ_0)` parameters, applied on the `D_anc` (expert demonstrations) pool:
 - **Feature Anchor (`L_feat`)**: This term is added to the critic update: `L_feat = λ_feat * ρ(t) * E_{o∼D_anc} [ ||ϕ_θ(o) - sg(ϕ_{θ_0}(o))||²_F ]`. It constrains the current visual encoder `ϕ_θ` to maintain feature similarity to the frozen source encoder `ϕ_{θ_0}` on expert observations. The weight `λ_feat` is set to 0.2.
 - **Actor-Side Reference-Action Anchor (`L_mse`)**: This term is added to the actor update: `L_mse = β_mse * ρ(t) * E_{s∼D_anc} [ ||μ_θ(s) - sg(μ_{θ_0}(s))||²_2 ]`. It regularizes the mean `μ_θ(s)` of the actor's Gaussian action distribution to match that of the frozen source policy `μ_{θ_0}(s)` on expert states. The weight `β_mse` is set to 0.1.
4. **Full Anchored Objective**: The final objectives for fine-tuning become: `L_Critic = L_Bellman + L_feat` and `L_Actor = L_SAC + L_mse`.
5. **Decay Schedule**: Both anchor terms incorporate a decay function `ρ(t) = 1 - (1 - ρ_end)t/T` with `ρ_end = 0.33`. This allows stronger regularization early in training to preserve source knowledge, gradually reducing its influence to facilitate adaptation to new illumination conditions later. The chosen actor anchor (`L_mse` on action mean) was found to be more effective than a full KL divergence anchor, as the latter could throttle adaptation by constraining variance too much.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of RoHIL was conducted across four real-robot manipulation tasks: `ram_insertion`, `usb_insertion`, `circuit_breaker`, and `table_wiping`. Performance was assessed under a gradient of ten illumination conditions, ranging from 0% (source lighting) to 100% (extreme shifts).

#### 4.1. Loss Study: Anchored Objective Outperforms Standard SAC

- **Observation**: On the `usb_insertion` task (identified as the most lighting-sensitive), the full anchored Bellman–actor objective (`L_Critic = L_Bellman + L_feat`, `L_Actor = L_SAC + L_mse`) facilitated stable convergence. The policy achieved near-perfect success rates (1.00 for both source and shifted lighting) after 15,000 offline fine-tuning steps.
- **Contrast**: When fine-tuned using a standard SAC objective without the anchor terms, the policy exhibited oscillations during training and experienced a performance collapse towards the end, resulting in lower success rates (0.57 on source, 0.73 on shifted lighting). This supports the hypothesis that the anchored objective mitigates catastrophic forgetting.
- **Component Ablation of Anchors**:
 - `L_feat` (encoder-level anchor) alone significantly improved shifted-light success (from 0.67 to 0.97) but negatively impacted source-workstation Q-values (success rate dropped from 0.77 to 0.57).
 - `L_mse` (reference-action mean anchor) alone restored source-workstation success to 1.00 but was less effective on shifted lighting (0.80 success rate).
 - The combination of both `L_feat` and `L_mse` in RoHIL achieved 1.00/1.00 success rates on both source and shifted lighting, along with the shortest successful episode durations. This indicates that the two anchor terms address distinct failure modes and are complementary.

#### 4.2. IRR Study: Optimal Original-vs.-Relit Mixing

- **Experiment**: A sweep of the original-light fraction `α` in `R_α` (from 0.0 to 1.0) was performed on the `usb_insertion` task.
- **Finding 1**: The anchored Bellman–actor objective consistently outperformed the standard SAC loss across almost the entire spectrum of `α` values, demonstrating its general advantage.
- **Finding 2**: The joint optimal operating point for `α` was identified as 0.75. At this ratio, RoHIL achieved 1.00 success on both source and shifted lighting and a mean successful episode duration of 2.48 seconds on shifted lighting.
- **Analysis**: Values of `α` below 0.75 led to a loss of source-workstation Bellman coverage, while values approaching 1.0 (indicating minimal relit data) resulted in degraded adaptation to shifted lighting. This confirms the importance of balancing retention and adaptation data.

#### 4.3. Component Ablation: IRR × Anchored Objective (2x2)

- **Experiment**: A 2x2 ablation study was conducted to evaluate the individual and combined contributions of IRR and the anchored objective on `usb_insertion`.
- **Results**:
 - **Baseline (no IRR, no anchors)**: Exhibited low success rates (0.33 source, 0.57 shifted).
 - **Anchored objective only (no IRR)**: Improved success (0.50 source, 0.67 shifted), primarily by stabilizing the visual encoder.
 - **IRR only (no anchors)**: Maintained source-workstation success better (0.77 source) but showed similar shifted-light performance to the baseline (0.67), suggesting that replay alone is insufficient to prevent unconstrained encoder drift.
 - **RoHIL (IRR + anchored objective)**: Achieved 1.00/1.00 success rates, demonstrating that both data-level (IRR) and model-level (anchored objective) retention mechanisms are necessary and synergistically contribute to robust performance.

#### 4.4. Cross-Method Comparison

- **Comparison**: RoHIL was benchmarked against five HIL-compatible baselines (BC, ACT, HG-DAgger, HIL-SERL, IBRL) across all four tasks at a 60% illumination shift.
- **RoHIL Performance**: RoHIL consistently achieved the highest success rates on all tasks (RAM 1.00, USB 1.00, wiping 0.87, breaker 0.83).
- **Baseline Performance**: All baselines experienced material degradation:
 - HIL-SERL maintained only about half its source success on `usb_insertion` and collapsed to 0.13 on `circuit_breaker`.
 - HG-DAgger and Behavior Cloning (BC) performed below 0.4 success on all tasks.
 - IBRL, identified as the strongest baseline, still trailed RoHIL by 0.4 to 0.6 success points on three of the four tasks.
- **Full Illumination Gradient**: Across the entire illumination shift gradient (0% to 100%), RoHIL maintained consistently high performance, remaining near 1.00 on `usb_insertion` and showing only modest degradation on other tasks at extreme shifts. In contrast, baselines generally degraded monotonically with increasing shift intensity.
- **Conclusion**: RoHIL demonstrated a consistent and significant robustness advantage over existing methods, indicating that the framework's design is effective across different manipulation tasks and varying degrees of illumination change.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RoHIL framework addresses a critical limitation in the deployment of Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning (HIL-RL) policies: their fragility to visual domain shifts caused by illumination variations. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood in several dimensions:

1. **Enabling Practical HIL-RL Deployment**: The primary impact is transforming the deployment cost of HIL-RL policies from an O(N) scaling (requiring re-collection and retraining for each new workstation) to an O(1) cost (fine-tuning offline without new robot interaction). This eliminates a major barrier to the practical adoption and scaling of HIL-RL in varied industrial or operational environments where lighting conditions are not perfectly controlled or known a priori.
2. **Enhanced Policy Robustness**: RoHIL significantly improves the robustness of robotic manipulation policies against realistic and substantial illumination changes. By effectively adapting to shifted lighting while simultaneously preventing catastrophic forgetting of the original training domain, the framework produces policies that are more reliable and resilient in diverse real-world settings.
3. **Leveraging Existing Data and Resources**: The methodology maximizes the utility of previously collected HIL-RL data by using a world-model relighter to synthesize diverse visual experiences. This reduces the dependency on continuous, costly human intervention and real-robot interaction during deployment.
4. **Contribution to Continual Learning and Domain Adaptation**: The work provides a concrete instantiation of solutions to the stability-plasticity dilemma within the context of visual robotic control. The combination of data-level retention (IRR) and model-level regularization (anchored Bellman–actor objective) offers a principled approach to adapt to new domains without sacrificing performance on learned ones. This approach may inform strategies for other forms of visual domain adaptation in robotics.
5. **Blueprint for Future Research**: The modular nature of RoHIL, particularly the drop-in relighter component, suggests that advancements in generative visual models for relighting could directly translate into improved performance for illumination robustness. Furthermore, the paper identifies avenues for future work, such as developing anchor-free variants for scenarios lacking initial demonstrations, and extending the anchored regularization principle to address mixed visual and geometric domain shifts, indicating the potential for broader impact on robotic generalization.

In summary, RoHIL represents a step towards making advanced HIL-RL systems more practical and deployable by addressing a fundamental challenge of visual robustness, thereby expanding the potential applications of human-guided robotic learning.
