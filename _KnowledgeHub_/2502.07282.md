# Leader-follower formation enabled by pressure sensing in free-swimming undulatory robotic fish

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at The Pennsylvania State University experimentally demonstrated leader-follower formation swimming in free-swimming undulatory robotic fish, leveraging only two bilateral flow pressure sensors. An imitation learning framework with recurrent neural networks enabled a follower robot to track a randomly moving leader by interpreting complex hydrodynamic cues, achieving tracking performance comparable to an expert policy.

## Problem

- Replicating fish's fluid sensing capabilities in robots is challenging due to complex self-generated and external hydrodynamics.
- Integrating effective flow sensors into small, undulatory robots is difficult, and existing sensors often suffer from noise and struggle to distinguish self-induced flow.
- Achieving coordinated behaviors like formation swimming in underwater robot swarms often relies on unreliable visual/acoustic cues or global positioning in challenging environments.

## Method

- Equipped a free-swimming undulatory robotic fish (μBot) with two bilateral pressure sensors and incorporated IMU and previous motor commands as policy inputs.
- Utilized an imitation learning framework (==Behavior Cloning== followed by Dataset Aggregation) to train a recurrent neural network (LSTM) policy to map sensor inputs to steering commands.
- The expert policy maintained a specific staggered formation, identified through preliminary experiments, to ensure robust hydrodynamic signal reception.

## Results

- The Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) approach significantly improved follower performance, achieving cumulative rewards approaching the expert policy with approximately 48 minutes of experimental training data.
- The DAgger-learned policies enabled the robotic fish to successfully track a randomly moving leader and maintain a staggered formation using only pressure feedback.
- Learned policies demonstrated adaptability, including correcting positions by crossing behind the leader, showcasing generalization beyond the expert's fixed-side training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A minimal setup of two bilateral pressure sensors, complemented by internal state data, is sufficient for a free-swimming undulatory robot to perceive and react to a leader's hydrodynamic wake.
> - Recurrent neural networks (LSTMs) can effectively extract actionable temporal patterns from sparse and noisy pressure sensor data to enable robust control in dynamic hydrodynamic environments.
> - Strategic positioning in a staggered formation enhances the detectability of leader-induced pressure signals, allowing the follower to leverage fluid interactions for stable tracking.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Fish exhibit sophisticated abilities to sense and exploit fluid dynamics, utilizing their lateral lines to detect flow structures and pressure gradients. This biological capability enables them to perceive objects, navigate complex environments, and engage in social behaviors such as schooling, even in the absence of visual cues. The replication of these natural sensing and control mechanisms in robotic platforms is a significant area of research within bio-inspired robotics.

Current efforts in robotic fish often incorporate off-the-shelf static pressure sensors to mimic the lateral line system. However, these sensors typically face challenges such as susceptibility to static pressure changes, water splashes, and body roll, which can interfere with the detection of relevant hydrodynamic signals. Furthermore, the robot's own movements generate complex flow patterns, making it difficult to discern external flow stimuli from self-induced ones. Previous studies on hydrodynamic sensing in underwater robots have often simplified the problem by using rigid-bodied robots, fixed flow sources, or limiting the robot's degrees of freedom, thus not fully addressing the complexities of free-swimming, undulating systems.

This paper addresses a gap in the literature by focusing on leader-follower formation swimming in free-swimming, undulatory robotic fish (μBots) using flow pressure sensing. The work aims to overcome the challenge of distinguishing self-generated flow from leader-induced flow in a dynamic, undulatory system with limited and potentially noisy sensor data. By employing imitation learning and recurrent neural networks, the research explores a data-driven approach to complex hydrodynamic perception and control, providing a model-free alternative to rigorous analytical methods for interpreting imperfect pressure signals. This work contributes to the advancement of autonomous underwater vehicles, particularly those capable of forming intelligent swarms that leverage fluid dynamics for improved perception, navigation, and efficiency, similar to their biological counterparts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to experimentally demonstrate leader-follower formation swimming in free-swimming, undulatory robotic fish (μBots) solely through the use of flow pressure sensing. This involves enabling a follower μBot to track a randomly moving leader by decoding hydrodynamic pressure information.

The motivation for pursuing this objective stems from several factors:

- **Replicating Biological Capabilities:** Fish demonstrate remarkable abilities to perceive and interact with their fluid environment using their lateral line systems. Replicating this flow-sensing capability in robotic platforms could lead to more agile, autonomous, and energy-efficient underwater robots capable of navigating complex, turbid, or GPS-denied environments where visual or acoustic sensors may be less effective.
- **Addressing Hydrodynamic Complexity:** The interaction between two undulating robots in a fluid environment generates complex, nonlinear hydrodynamic flows. Distinguishing the pressure variations caused by the leading robot from those generated by the follower's own undulatory movements presents a significant challenge. Traditional analytical models for such interactions can be computationally intensive and may not generalize well to dynamic scenarios.
- **Overcoming Sensor Limitations:** Practical constraints in integrating sensors into small robotic swimmers, such as the μBot, often result in a limited number of noisy sensor channels. The research is motivated by the need to develop robust control strategies that can extract meaningful information from these sparse and imperfect temporal pressure patterns.
- **Advancing Robotic Swarm Capabilities:** The ability to maintain formation using local hydrodynamic cues is a fundamental requirement for the development of coordinated multi-robot systems and biomimetic schooling behaviors. Achieving leader-follower formation without reliance on global positioning or inter-robot communication (beyond local flow sensing) can enhance the autonomy and resilience of underwater robot swarms.
- **Exploring Data-Driven Control:** Given the complexity of hydrodynamic interactions and the limitations of analytical approaches, the researchers are motivated to explore a data-driven imitation learning framework. This approach, particularly with recurrent neural networks like Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), is hypothesized to be effective in learning an end-to-end mapping from raw sensor inputs to control actions, thereby providing a practical solution for perception and control in this challenging domain.
- **Filling a Research Gap:** To the best of the authors' knowledge, this work represents the first experimental demonstration of leader-follower formation in free-swimming, undulating robotic fish using only pressure feedback. This addresses a need for empirical validation of flow-based perception in dynamic, bio-inspired robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a combination of robotic hardware integration, experimental setup, and machine learning techniques, specifically imitation learning, to achieve its objectives.

**A. Integration of Pressure Sensors**
The follower μBot was equipped with two Honeywell MPRSL0001PG00001C pressure sensors, strategically placed bilaterally on its head. These sensors were selected for their low 6.9 kPa range, integrated 24-bit Analog-to-Digital Converter (ADC), I2C communication capability, water compatibility, and compact size. Custom modular Printed Circuit Boards (PCBs) were designed to mount the sensors, regulate voltage, and configure I2C addresses, allowing seamless integration with the existing μBot's microcontroller and Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) (Bosch BNO055) via flat-flex cables. To ensure consistent voltage and enable prolonged experiments, the μBots were powered externally via thin 42 AWG magnet wires carried on a linear stage, designed to minimally affect hydrodynamic interactions.

**B. Experimental Setup**
Experiments were conducted in a water tank measuring 305 cm L × 58 cm W × 56 cm H. An overhead infrared camera (Basler acA2000-165umNIR) tracked the 2D positions and headings of both μBots in real-time. Communication between the μBots and a host laptop occurred via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE), with a one-way latency of 60-80 ms. All image and data processing, including real-time control, were performed at 50 Hz using MATLAB 2024a.

**C. Generating Forward Swimming and Steering Gaits**
A Kuramoto Central Pattern Generator (CPG) was utilized to generate undulatory swimming gaits. The CPG parameters were tuned to match previous μBot experiments, with a frequency of 5 Hz and an intersegmental phase offset of -65° from head to tail. Steering was achieved by adjusting the amplitude offset across the actuators, causing the μBot to bend in the desired direction. The steering offset was constrained to ±30% of the oscillation amplitude to prevent excessive bending and thrust reduction, serving as the sole control variable.

**D. Random Path Generation for the Leader**
The leader μBot's paths were generated by fitting splines through its initial position and three randomly chosen control points distributed along the tank's length. A Line-of-Sight (LOS) guidance algorithm was then used to determine the leader's reference heading and generate steering commands proportional to the heading error.

**E. Imitation Learning**
An imitation learning framework was employed to train the follower μBot's control policy.

1. **Expert Policy:**
 - **Initial Data Collection:** Preliminary experiments were conducted with a fixed follower and a free-swimming leader at various relative positions. These experiments revealed that pressure variations were most pronounced in staggered formations, where the leader's caudal fin excited flow near the follower's sensors, especially within 10 cm of lateral separation.
 - **Staggered Formation:** Based on these observations, the expert policy for free-swimming was designed to maintain a *staggered* configuration. Using external motion tracking data and LOS guidance, the expert follower maintained a path offset laterally by 60 mm (0.3 body lengths) from the leader's random path. This staggered position was chosen to provide rich and robust flow information to the follower.

2. **Learner Policy:**
 - The learner policy received inputs only from onboard sensors: the two bilateral pressure signals, three Euler angles (yaw, pitch, roll) from the IMU, and the previous motor commands to the actuators. Euler angles and motor commands were included to implicitly help the policy differentiate between self-generated and leader-induced pressure signals. Pressure biases and yaw angle biases were removed at the start of each experiment.
 - The output of the policy was the steering offset for the follower.
 - The policy was implemented as a recurrent neural network comprising an LSTM layer for temporal pattern extraction, a hidden fully-connected layer (64 units), dropout layers for regularization, and a fully-connected output layer with a tanh activation and scaling to constrain steering outputs. The network had a total of 22,912 parameters.

3. **Behavior Cloning (BC):**
 - The initial phase involved Behavior Cloning (BC), where the expert provided demonstrations. 120 demonstrations were collected by steering the follower using the expert's LOS guidance while the leader moved randomly. The learner's onboard sensor readings and the expert's steering commands were recorded.
 - The learner policy was then trained to map its sensor inputs to the expert's observed steering actions.

4. **Dataset Aggregation (DAgger):**
 - To mitigate the "covariate shift" problem (where the learner encounters states not present in the expert's demonstrations), Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) was implemented.
 - In DAgger iterations, the learner policy was rolled out in a closed-loop fashion (using its own predictions to steer), while the expert's LOS guidance simultaneously provided steering commands for those encountered states.
 - After each set of 20 rollouts, the collected data (learner inputs and expert actions) was added to the existing training dataset (initially the BC data), and the policy was retrained. This iterative process continued until satisfactory leader-following performance was achieved.

5. **Training the Learner:**
 - All input and output data were centered and normalized by their standard deviations. A 90/10 split was used for training and validation in each iteration.
 - The Adam optimizer was used for 10,000 epochs, with a base learning rate of 0.005. Weights corresponding to the epoch with the lowest validation loss were selected to prevent overfitting.

**F. Experimental Methodology for Rollouts**
In each rollout, the leader started 0.4 seconds (two undulation cycles) before the follower. Both μBots started parallel, with the follower's nose positioned near the leader's last two segments and a lateral separation of approximately 60 mm. Rollouts terminated if the follower's nose moved more than 200 mm (1 body length) from the leader's tail, if the follower touched the leader, or after 10 seconds if neither condition was met. An equal number of rollouts had the leader starting on the left or right of the follower.

**G. Evaluating Leader-Following Performance**
Two metrics were used for evaluation:
- **Mean Absolute Error (MAE) in Steering:** The average absolute difference between the learner's steering commands and the expert's steering commands per rollout. Lower error indicated better imitation.
- **Cumulative Reward 'r' per Rollout:** A reward function was defined (Eq. 1 in the paper) to quantify how well the follower maintained a 60 mm distance between its nose and a square outline centered at the leader's tail. The reward ranged from 0 to 1 per time step, with a maximum cumulative reward of 500 over 10 seconds. Early termination of a rollout resulted in a lower cumulative reward. The expert policy and a "no-steering" policy served as upper and lower performance bounds, respectively.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the performance of the imitation learning policies through the Behavior Cloning (BC) and Dataset Aggregation (DAgger) phases.

**Performance Trends:**
- **Behavior Cloning (BC):** In the initial BC phase, policies trained with 40, 80, and 120 rollouts showed a marginal improvement over the no-steering baseline in both steering error and cumulative reward metrics. However, increasing the number of expert demonstrations beyond 40 rollouts did not yield further significant improvements, suggesting a limitation of pure BC in generalizing to novel states.
- **Dataset Aggregation (DAgger):** The DAgger phase resulted in substantial performance gains. The learner policy, after several DAgger iterations, demonstrated a cumulative reward approaching that of the expert policy. This improvement was achieved with approximately 48 minutes of total experimental training data.
- **Variability:** The results indicated high variability in performance between individual rollouts, reflected in large error bars in the metric distributions. This variability was attributed to variations in leader paths and initial conditions. Performance gains appeared to plateau after the 6th DAgger iteration, suggesting a limit to the attainable performance with the current hardware and policy design.

**Final Policy Performance:**
- Overall, the final DAgger-learned policies performed between the expert and no-steering baselines on both steering error and cumulative reward. Even the expert policy occasionally exhibited suboptimal performance, particularly when the follower fell significantly behind, highlighting the inherent limitations of a steering-only control strategy.
- Low-performing DAgger rollouts were frequently characterized by the follower failing to detect a closing distance and contacting the leader. This contrasts with BC rollouts, which more often terminated due to the follower moving too far away from the leader. This suggests DAgger improved the ability to stay close but faced challenges in avoiding collisions.

**Successful Trajectories:**
- Examples of successful leader-following trajectories demonstrated the learner policy's ability to track the leader effectively, maintaining a staggered configuration. The follower was observed to alternate movements towards and away from the leader to preserve this formation.
- The follower sometimes maintained tracking even when directly behind the leader, a position where pressure signals were expected to be reduced based on initial fixed-follower experiments.
- In one instance, the follower successfully corrected its position after overshooting, crossing from the right to the left of the leader and then stabilizing on the leader's left side. This capability was notable as the expert policy was designed to maintain a fixed side, indicating the learner's adaptability.

**Challenges in Perception:**
- Analyzing the relationships between motion tracking data, onboard sensor readings (pressure, IMU), and control outputs proved complex. Distinguishing self-generated pressure variations from those induced by the leader was not straightforward, particularly given that the amplitude of pressure feedback observed during free-swimming was approximately twice that measured in the fixed-follower experiments. Despite this complexity and uncertainty, the LSTM model was able to approximate the expert's steering trends.

**Failure Modes:**
- Several failure scenarios were observed. These included collisions with the leader, where the follower failed to steer or slow down sufficiently to avoid contact. Other failures occurred when the leader made sudden turns that the follower failed to detect or react to, possibly due to weakened flow and thrust generated by the leader during its steering maneuvers. In some cases, the follower lost track of the leader after attempting to maintain distance or failed to sense the leader's change in direction, leading to further collisions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research demonstrates, for the first time in experimental settings, the successful implementation of leader-follower formation swimming in free-swimming, undulatory robotic fish using only flow pressure feedback. This achievement holds several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Validation of Bio-inspired Sensing:** The work validates the feasibility of using simplified lateral line systems (two bilateral pressure sensors) to perceive complex hydrodynamic interactions in dynamic, free-swimming robotic platforms. This directly replicates a fundamental sensory capability observed in biological fish, enabling perception in environments where other sensing modalities (e.g., vision) might be limited.
- **Effectiveness of Recurrent Neural Networks:** The successful application of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural networks within an imitation learning framework highlights their efficacy in extracting relevant temporal patterns from limited and noisy pressure sensor data. This provides a robust, data-driven approach to perception and control in challenging, model-unaware hydrodynamic environments.
- **Hydrodynamic Interaction for Formation Control:** The explicit design of the expert policy to maintain a staggered formation, based on preliminary experiments showing stronger pressure feedback in such configurations, suggests a principled approach to leveraging hydrodynamic interactions for improved perception. This aligns with biological observations that staggered formations can offer hydrodynamic efficiency benefits in fish schools, potentially implying a co-evolution of sensing and energetic advantages.
- **Advancement in Robotic Swarming:** The ability of a robotic fish to autonomously track a leader using local flow cues is a foundational step towards developing sophisticated multi-robot systems capable of coordinated behaviors like schooling, patrolling, and environmental mapping without reliance on global positioning or extensive inter-robot communication. This could lead to more robust and scalable underwater robot swarms.
- **Potential for Enhanced Autonomy:** By enabling robots to perceive and react to their immediate fluid environment, this research contributes to the development of more autonomous underwater vehicles. Such capabilities are crucial for applications in underwater exploration, infrastructure inspection, and ecological monitoring, especially in turbid waters or confined spaces where conventional navigation systems are ineffective.

**Potential Future Impact:**
The insights gained from this work could influence future designs of bio-inspired robots, leading to:
- **More Sophisticated Lateral Line Systems:** The demonstrated effectiveness with two sensors encourages the exploration of larger arrays for enhanced perception and state estimation of leaders or other environmental features.
- **Advanced Control Strategies:** The foundation laid by this imitation learning approach can be extended to more complex control objectives, such as optimizing undulation frequency, amplitude, and phase offset for improved maneuverability, efficiency, and perception, possibly through reinforcement learning.
- **Improved Human-Robot or Robot-Robot Interaction:** Understanding how robots can implicitly perceive each other through fluid dynamics opens avenues for more intuitive and robust interactions in underwater collaborative tasks.
- **Model Explanation and Biomechanical Understanding:** The application of model explanation techniques (e.g., SHAP) to the learned policies, combined with flow visualization methods like Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV), could provide deeper insights into the specific hydrodynamic cues utilized by the robots, potentially informing our understanding of biological fish behavior.
