# ARMADA: Autonomous Online Failure Detection and Human Shared Control Empower Scalable Real-world Deployment and Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> ARMADA introduces a system for scalable real-world deployment and rapid adaptation of visuomotor robot policies by integrating an autonomous failure detector and a multi-robot shared control framework. The system achieves nearly 95% failure detection accuracy and reduces human intervention rates by 23.3%, significantly improving policy robustness and adaptation efficiency.

## Problem

- Pretrained visuomotor policies often lack robustness for consistent real-world performance due to insufficient in-domain data.
- Existing human-in-the-loop (HIL) systems require full-time human surveillance, limiting scalability to a 'one human, one robot' paradigm.
- Human-collected demonstrations, crucial for imitation learning, frequently suffer from mixed quality and redundancy, hindering optimal policy learning.

## Method

- **FLOAT** (==FaiLure detection based on OptimAl Transport==) is a plug-in-and-play autonomous online failure detector that compares real-time policy embeddings against expert trajectories, adapting its threshold dynamically.
- A ==multi-robot shared control== system enables parallel policy rollout across multiple robots, efficiently channeling intervention requests from FLOAT-detected failures to available human operators.
- An ==adaptive rewinding mechanism== ensures that when a failure is detected, the robot reverts to a recoverable state, facilitating the collection of high-quality, informative human corrective demonstrations.

## Results

- FLOAT achieved an average failure detection accuracy of nearly **95%** across four real-world tasks, outperforming state-of-the-art baselines by over **20%**.
- Policies trained with data collected by **ARMADA**'s adaptive rewinding mechanism showed an average success rate improvement of **25.9%**, more than **4x** the improvement of a prominent human-in-the-loop learning method.
- ARMADA reduced the human intervention rate by **23.3%** after two fine-tuning stages, demonstrating its ability to make robots more autonomous over time and improve scalability for multi-robot operations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Autonomous online failure detection based on Optimal Transport of policy embeddings can robustly and accurately identify robot failures, significantly reducing the need for constant human supervision.
> - Efficiently collecting high-quality human corrective demonstrations from recoverable states during online deployment dramatically accelerates policy adaptation and improves overall robustness.
> - A shared control framework, enabled by reliable failure detection, allows a single human operator to supervise multiple robots, vastly increasing the scalability of real-world robot deployment and data acquisition.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen a burgeoning of data-driven approaches, particularly imitation learning, which aims to teach robots complex tasks by observing human demonstrations. While large-scale datasets and pretraining have enabled robots to acquire rudimentary skills, a significant challenge remains in deploying these policies robustly in diverse real-world environments and adapting them to new, unseen scenarios. This paper directly addresses these limitations, positioning ARMADA at the intersection of imitation learning, human-in-the-loop (HIL) systems, and real-world robot deployment.

**The Landscape of Imitation Learning and Real-World Deployment:** Pretrained policies often suffer from a lack of robustness due to insufficient in-domain data and the distribution shift between training environments and real-world deployment. Furthermore, human-collected demonstrations, while essential, frequently contain mixed-quality data and redundant information, hindering optimal policy learning. The broader research aims to bridge this "reality gap" and improve the generalization and adaptability of learned policies.

**The Role of Human-in-the-Loop Systems:** HIL systems have emerged as a promising solution to these challenges. They allow human operators to interactively collect domain-specific data, provide corrective feedback, and guide policies through shared control or interventions. Existing HIL systems, such as those leveraging human preference rankings or shared autonomy paradigms, contribute valuable data for policy fine-tuning and adaptation. However, a critical limitation across most of these systems is the requirement for **full-time human surveillance**. This "one human, one robot" paradigm severely constrains the scalability of real-world deployment, making it resource-intensive and impractical for large-scale operations.

**Addressing the Scalability Bottleneck:** ARMADA directly tackles this scalability bottleneck. It proposes two key desiderata for enhancing HIL systems:
1. **Autonomous Online Failure Detection:** By offloading the task of monitoring policy rollout from humans to an autonomous system, the need for constant human attention is reduced. This allows human operators to manage multiple robots concurrently.
2. **Multi-Robot Shared Control:** Implementing a system that can orchestrate policy rollouts across multiple robots in parallel, with human intervention channeled efficiently when necessary, drastically speeds up data collection and adaptation.

**Comparison with Related Work:**
- **Online Failure Detection:** The paper builds upon and significantly advances prior work in failure detection. While some approaches use OOD detection via policy embeddings or VAE reconstruction errors, they often rely on pre-collected failure data or large training datasets, and can be sensitive to environmental changes. Newer methods, like STAC and LLM/VLM-based classifiers, have shown promise but can be computationally intensive or depend on zero-shot capabilities. ARMADA's FLOAT method, based on Optimal Transport (OT) of policy embeddings, offers a plug-in-and-play solution that demonstrates superior accuracy without requiring explicit failure data or heavy model fine-tuning.
- **Data Curation and Post-training:** The paper also contributes to improving data quality for policy post-training. Existing methods like SCIZOR and DemInf focus on filtering suboptimal trajectories or optimizing data mixtures. ARMADA's adaptive rewinding mechanism provides a novel way to ensure that human corrective demonstrations are always informative and collected from recoverable states, thus improving the efficiency and quality of the collected data.
- **Shared Autonomy:** While some prior works explore shared autonomy with time-varying control ratios, ARMADA focuses on a human-intervention-on-demand model, allowing more autonomous operation until a failure is detected.

In summary, ARMADA pushes the boundaries of scalable robot learning by integrating an advanced, highly accurate failure detection system with an efficient multi-robot shared control framework and intelligent data curation. It represents a significant step towards practical, widespread deployment of learning-based robotic systems by mitigating the intensive human supervision currently required.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **enable scalable real-world deployment and rapid adaptation of visuomotor robotic policies**, particularly those learned through imitation. The authors identify critical limitations in current approaches that hinder this scalability, which directly motivate the development of ARMADA.

The key motivations and objectives can be broken down as follows:

1. **Addressing Policy Robustness and Data Deficiency:** Pretrained imitation learning policies, while offering foundational capabilities, often lack the robustness required for consistent performance in real-world scenarios. This deficiency stems from insufficient "in-domain" data that captures the nuances and variations of the deployment environment. A core objective is to efficiently acquire this crucial in-domain data.
2. **Improving Data Quality from Human Demonstrations:** Human-collected demonstrations, the cornerstone of imitation learning, frequently suffer from mixed quality, redundancy, and suboptimal segments. These issues can impede the robot's ability to learn superior performance. The research aims to develop mechanisms that ensure the collected human feedback data is consistently high-quality and informative.
3. **Overcoming the Scalability Barrier of Human Supervision:** Existing human-in-the-loop (HIL) systems, while effective for data collection and policy improvement, necessitate full-time human surveillance. This "one human per one robot" control paradigm is a major bottleneck, severely limiting the number of robots that can be simultaneously deployed and adapted. The central motivation is to significantly reduce reliance on constant human supervision to enable a "one human for multiple robots" control setting.
4. **Developing an Autonomous Online Failure Detection System:** To achieve reduced human supervision, a critical objective is to create a robust, real-time, and autonomous method for detecting policy failures during rollout. This detector should alert human operators only when necessary, freeing them from continuous monitoring. This module, named FLOAT, is designed to be plug-in-and-play with existing visuomotor imitation learning policies.
5. **Designing a Multi-Robot Shared Control Framework:** To truly enable scalable deployment, the system needs to support parallel policy rollout across multiple robots. When a failure is detected by FLOAT, the system should efficiently route the intervention request to an available human operator.
6. **Facilitating Faster Adaptation to New Scenarios:** By enabling efficient, high-quality data collection from multiple robots in diverse environments, the system aims to accelerate the post-training process, leading to quicker adaptation of policies to novel and unseen scenarios.
7. **Ensuring Informative Human Interventions:** When human intervention is required, the system should facilitate the collection of useful corrective data. The objective is to prevent robots from entering unrecoverable states and to ensure that human corrections are recorded in meaningful contexts for policy improvement.

In essence, ARMADA is motivated by the need to make learning-based robotics more practical and widely deployable by enhancing the efficiency of data collection, improving data quality, and, most importantly, vastly increasing the scalability of human-supervised robot operations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ARMADA is presented as a comprehensive multi-robot deployment and adaptation system built upon three core components: the FLOAT failure detector, an adaptive rewinding mechanism, and a multi-robot shared control system. The underlying robotic policy is based on Diffusion Policy.

#### 3.1. FLOAT: Autonomous Online Failure Detection

FLOAT (FaiLure detection based on OptimAl Transport) is designed as a plug-in-and-play method for visuomotor imitation learning.
- **Policy Embeddings:** Given a pretrained visuomotor policy and its observation encoder ($\phi$), expert demonstrations ($D_e$) are first converted into a set of expert policy embeddings ($F_e$). During a robot's rollout, current observations are similarly converted into a trajectory of policy embeddings ($F_b$).
- **Optimal Transport (OT) for Trajectory Matching:** FLOAT leverages Optimal Transport to measure the "distance" or cost between the current rollout trajectory embeddings ($F_b$) and each expert demonstration's embeddings ($F_e$). Cosine similarity is used as the cost function within the OT computation. The OT algorithm finds an optimal transport matrix that minimizes the total cost of transforming one distribution (rollout trajectory) into another (expert trajectory).
- **FLOAT Index Calculation:** For each expert demonstration, an OT cost ($\lambda_n(T_b)$) is computed. The `FLOAT index` ($\lambda(T_b)$) for the current rollout trajectory is defined as the minimum of these OT costs across all expert demonstrations, effectively finding the "closest" expert trajectory.
- **Universal FLOAT Threshold (Λ):** A threshold ($\Lambda$) for failure detection is calibrated from a collection of successful rollout trajectories. This threshold is defined as the $(1 - \delta)$ percentile of the FLOAT indices of these successful rollouts, where $\delta$ is an adaptively updated hyperparameter.
- **Adaptive Threshold Update:** The hyperparameter $\delta$ is dynamically adjusted during deployment. If a robot fails but FLOAT does not detect it (false negative), $\delta$ is decreased, making the threshold more conservative. If FLOAT raises a warning but the robot is operating normally (false positive), $\delta$ is increased, making the threshold less conservative. This adaptive mechanism allows FLOAT to calibrate itself online to the specific environment and policy performance.
- **Asynchronous Operation:** To prevent latency in robot control, FLOAT runs asynchronously with the policy rollout, ensuring real-time performance without introducing delays.

#### 3.2. ARMADA: Multi-robot Shared Control System

Empowered by FLOAT, ARMADA enables scalable, parallel policy rollout across multiple robots with human intervention on demand.
- **Message Queue:** A central message queue facilitates communication between robot nodes and human teleoperation nodes.
- **Autonomous Rollout & Intervention Request:** Robots continuously execute their policies. If FLOAT detects a failure on a robot, that robot node places a message requesting human intervention into the queue.
- **Human Operator Allocation:** Teleoperation nodes monitor the message queue. An idle human operator is assigned the earliest intervention request, taking over control of the specified robot. This effectively transforms the "one human, one robot" paradigm into "one human, multiple robots," with humans acting as supervisors rather than constant controllers.

#### 3.3. Adaptive Rewinding Mechanism

To address scenarios where a robot might enter an unrecoverable state after a failure (e.g., knocking over objects), ARMADA incorporates an adaptive rewinding mechanism to ensure high-quality human intervention data.
- **Retracing Timestep:** When a failure signal is raised at timestep $t_0$, the system computes an optimal retraced timestep $t < t_0$. This timestep is the latest point in the trajectory where the FLOAT index was below a certain adaptive threshold, calculated as $\epsilon \cdot \lambda(T_b)^{1:t_0}$ (with $\epsilon=0.2$).
- **Scene Reset and Intervention:** The robot reverts to this retraced timestep, allowing the human operator to efficiently reset the scene to a pre-failure state. This ensures that the subsequent human corrective demonstration is informative and starts from a tractable state, preventing the collection of unrecoverable or "noisy" failure recovery data.

#### 3.4. Policy Architecture and Training Recipe

- **Imitation Learning Backbone:** The core imitation learning algorithm used is Diffusion Policy, a state-of-the-art method for visuomotor policy learning.
- **Action Generation:** A transformer architecture serves as the backbone for action generation.
- **Visual Encoder:** A pretrained DINOv2 ViT-B/14 model is used as the visual encoder to extract rich policy embeddings from images. A linear head is appended to compress these high-dimensional latents for efficient OT computation.
- **Proprioceptive Observations:** End-effector pose (represented by 6D rotation for continuity) is included as proprioceptive feedback.
- **Training Protocol:** Initial training uses 50 human-teleoperated demonstrations per task. During deployment, online collected data (including human interventions) are merged with the initial dataset for subsequent policy fine-tuning (post-training), forming a continuous adaptation loop.

This integrated methodology allows ARMADA to effectively detect failures, manage multiple robots with reduced human supervision, and generate high-quality data for continuous policy improvement and adaptation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across four real-world tasks (Pour marbles into bowl, Hang mug on holder, Grasp mango and put it into drawer, Fold towel) demonstrate the significant effectiveness of ARMADA's components and its overall system.

#### 4.1. Superior Performance of FLOAT Failure Detector

- **High Accuracy:** FLOAT achieves an average accuracy of nearly 95% across all four real-world tasks.
- **Outperforms SOTA:** This represents an improvement of over 20% compared to state-of-the-art baseline failure detection methods like STAC and logpZO.
- **Robustness:** While logpZO suffered from severe overfitting and low True Negative Rate (TNR) due to its sensitivity to the I.I.D. assumption, FLOAT demonstrated robust performance.
- **Standalone Effectiveness:** FLOAT's performance is comparable to FLOAT+Action (a variant that integrates action inconsistency detection from STAC), indicating its strong standalone ability to detect various failure modes solely based on policy embeddings.

#### 4.2. Enhanced Data Quality and Policy Improvement via Adaptive Rewinding

- **Increased Success Rate:** Policies trained with data collected by ARMADA, utilizing the adaptive rewinding mechanism, showed a remarkable improvement in success rate. Over three evaluation rounds, ARMADA yielded an average success rate improvement of 25.9%, which is more than four times the improvement achieved by Sirius, a prominent human-in-the-loop learning method.
- **Addressing Unrecoverable States:** The rewinding mechanism was particularly beneficial for tasks prone to unrecoverable states (e.g., "Hang mug on holder," "Fold towel"), where Sirius made minimal progress. This highlights how ARMADA ensures that human corrective demonstrations are always high-quality and informative, starting from a recoverable state.

#### 4.3. Significant Reduction in Human Intervention Reliance

- **Reduced Intervention Rate:** Despite an initial higher intervention rate in the first round (attributed to the adaptive rewinding providing more opportunities for human correction), ARMADA managed to reduce the human intervention rate by 23.3% after two fine-tuning stages. This is more than double the reduction observed with Sirius.
- **Scalability Potential:** This finding is crucial for scalability, as it demonstrates ARMADA's ability to make robots more autonomous over time, allowing human operators to supervise a larger fleet of robots without a proportional increase in effort.

#### 4.4. Improved Scalability and Adaptation to Unseen Scenarios

- **Faster Adaptation:** ARMADA significantly expedites policy adaptation to unseen scenarios. By deploying a pretrained "Fold towel" policy on three robots simultaneously across diverse in-domain scenes, and then evaluating it on an out-of-distribution scene, ARMADA showed steady progress in success rate with increasing deployment duration. This suggests that parallel data collection from varied environments enhances policy generalization.
- **Efficient Data Acquisition:** In terms of human operator occupancy (measured by intervention duration), ARMADA, with three robots, collected over twice the amount of human intervention samples compared to a single-robot setting within the same time frame. This efficiency in acquiring high-quality human trajectories from diverse domains directly contributes to the policy's robustness and adaptability to novel scenarios.

In summary, the results convincingly validate ARMADA's core claims: its failure detection is highly accurate, its data collection strategy yields higher quality demonstrations, it substantially reduces the need for human intervention over time, and it scales up deployment and accelerates adaptation to new, challenging environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ARMADA represents a significant leap forward in addressing the critical bottleneck of scalability in real-world robotic deployment, particularly for learning-based manipulation. Its contributions have profound implications for the widespread adoption and practical utility of intelligent robotic systems.

#### 5.1. Enhanced Efficiency and Scalability in Robot Deployment

The most significant impact of ARMADA lies in its ability to transform the human-robot interaction paradigm from a restrictive "one human per robot" to a more efficient "one human for multiple robots." By introducing an autonomous, high-accuracy failure detection system (FLOAT) and a multi-robot shared control framework, ARMADA dramatically reduces the reliance on constant human supervision. This directly translates into:
- **Reduced Operational Costs:** Less human labor is required to supervise and train robot fleets.
- **Faster Data Collection:** Multiple robots can collect in-domain data in parallel, accelerating the learning and adaptation cycle.
- **Broader Application Scope:** This scalability makes learning-based robotics more viable for large-scale industrial, logistics, or service applications where deploying and maintaining many robots with constant human oversight is economically prohibitive.

#### 5.2. Improved Policy Robustness and Generalization

The combination of FLOAT and the adaptive rewinding mechanism ensures that the data collected during online deployment is of consistently high quality.
- **High-Quality Demonstrations:** The rewinding mechanism allows humans to provide corrective demonstrations from recoverable states, which are far more informative for policy fine-tuning than interventions initiated in irretrievable failure modes. This directly leads to more robust and higher-performing policies.
- **Accelerated Adaptation:** The ability to efficiently collect diverse, high-quality human intervention data from multiple robots operating in varied scenes enables policies to adapt faster and generalize better to unseen or out-of-distribution environments. This is crucial for real-world scenarios where conditions are constantly changing.

#### 5.3. Advancements in Failure Detection

FLOAT itself is a notable contribution. Its high accuracy (nearly 95%) and plug-in-and-play nature for visuomotor imitation learning policies are significant. It offers a practical and robust alternative to existing methods, especially those that struggle with I.I.D. assumptions or require explicit failure data. This could inspire further research into using Optimal Transport and policy embeddings for robust monitoring of robotic systems.

#### 5.4. Open Science and Future Research

The commitment to open-sourcing ARMADA's implementation will undoubtedly foster further research and development in the community. Researchers can build upon this framework, test new policies, and explore novel failure detection methods.

#### 5.5. Addressing Current Limitations and Paving the Way for General Intelligence

While ARMADA currently requires human-collected expert demonstrations and is evaluated in single-task settings, the authors acknowledge these limitations and envision future work focusing on general-purpose progress estimators. This ambition aligns with the broader goal of developing more generally intelligent and autonomous robotic systems. By creating a scalable infrastructure for deployment and adaptation, ARMADA lays a crucial groundwork for testing and refining such general-purpose learning algorithms in real-world contexts.

In conclusion, ARMADA is not just an incremental improvement but a foundational system that tackles a core challenge in robot learning. By enabling more efficient, robust, and scalable real-world deployment and adaptation, it brings us closer to a future where intelligent robots can reliably and autonomously perform complex tasks in dynamic human environments.
