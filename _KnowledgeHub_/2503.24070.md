# HACTS: a Human-As-Copilot Teleoperation System for Robot Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Beijing Innovation Center of Humanoid Robotics and the Beijing Institute of Technology developed HACTS, a low-cost, kinematically equivalent teleoperation system that provides bilateral joint synchronization for robot arms. This system enables human operators to act as copilots, facilitating the collection of action-correction data which significantly improves robot learning models' failure recovery, generalization to novel scenarios, and human-in-the-loop reinforcement learning.

## Problem

- Most existing robot teleoperation systems provide only unilateral control, lacking real-time feedback of the robot's current joint positions to the human operator.
- The absence of bilateral synchronization hinders effective human intervention in autonomous robot tasks and complicates the collection of valuable action-correction data.
- Current bilateral control solutions often rely on expensive, specialized hardware and complex algorithms, limiting their accessibility and widespread adoption in research.

## Method

- **HACTS** is designed as a low-cost, kinematically equivalent leader-follower system built with off-the-shelf servo motors (under $300) and 3D-printed components.
- The system implements real-time bilateral joint position synchronization, allowing the teleoperation device to mirror the robot's state and vice-versa.
- It features a manual override interface (e.g., a foot pedal) that enables human operators to seamlessly switch between autonomous robot execution and direct intervention, acting as a 'copilot'.

## Results

- HACTS-ACT models achieved higher success rates (e.g., **80%** for OpenBox) compared to full-ACT models (**60%**), demonstrating that intervention data is more valuable than an equivalent amount of pure expert demonstrations for imitation learning.
- Action-correction data collected via HACTS significantly improved failure recovery in in-distribution scenarios (e.g., UprightMug success from **10%** to **70%**) and enabled generalization to out-of-distribution static and dynamic environments (e.g., 30-**50%** success rates where baselines failed completely).
- RLPD-HACTS achieved an **80%** success rate in a challenging CloseBin task within 45 minutes of online fine-tuning, improving from an initial **50%** BC policy, and human intervention lengths decreased as the policy learned, indicating efficient human guidance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Bilateral joint position synchronization, without complex force or tactile feedback, is a simple yet effective mechanism for enabling precise human intervention and copilot functionality in robot teleoperation.
> - Action-correction data, derived from human interventions during robot failures or guidance in novel scenarios, is particularly valuable for improving robot learning policies, often outperforming equivalent amounts of pure expert demonstrations.
> - Utilizing accessible, low-cost hardware designs for teleoperation systems can democratize advanced robot learning research, fostering broader adoption and accelerating data-driven advancements.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of HACTS addresses a critical gap in the rapidly evolving landscape of robot learning, particularly in manipulation tasks. Robot learning paradigms such as Learning from Demonstration (LfD), Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, and Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning (HITL RL) have achieved significant success, largely relying on human input for demonstrations and corrections. Teleoperation systems are fundamental to gathering this crucial human data, allowing operators to guide robots through complex actions.

However, the existing teleoperation landscape presents significant limitations. Most systems, whether based on VR, exoskeletons, motion capture, or even kinematically equivalent controllers like ALOHA and Gello, primarily offer *unilateral control*. This means the human operator sends commands to the robot but receives no real-time feedback regarding the robot's current joint positions or movements. This lack of bilateral synchronization becomes a severe impediment when the robot is executing tasks autonomously and requires human intervention. Without synchronized feedback, human intervention can be jarring, lead to loss of control, and is akin to trying to steer an autonomous car whose wheel is not synchronized with the driver's input.

Some research has explored bilateral control systems, often incorporating complex tactile or force feedback. While promising, these systems typically demand expensive and specialized follower hardware and sophisticated algorithms, making them inaccessible for most robotic platforms. For instance, the Bi-ACT system could operate without force feedback but was only tested on identical leader-follower setups, limiting its generalizability.

HACTS enters this landscape by proposing a novel, yet simple and highly accessible, solution: *bilateral joint synchronization*. Instead of complex force or tactile feedback, HACTS focuses on real-time positional synchronization between the robot and the teleoperation hardware. This approach is compatible with a broad range of robotic arms, including common platforms like the UR5, making it a highly practical advancement. By providing this fundamental feedback, HACTS transforms the human operator from a mere controller into a "copilot," enabling seamless intervention and continuous data collection.

Furthermore, HACTS directly impacts two key areas of robot learning:
1. **Imitation Learning (IL):** Traditional IL focuses on learning from successful human demonstrations. HACTS, through its copilot functionality, facilitates the collection of *action-correction data*, including data from failures and subsequent human rectifications. Recent research has highlighted the importance of failure data for robust model training, a need that HACTS directly addresses by enabling efficient capture of such critical information.
2. **Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning (HITL RL):** HITL RL paradigms, such as HACO and HIL-SERL, benefit from human guidance to accelerate exploration and ensure safety. HACTS provides the necessary dexterous and precise control for timely human intervention, allowing operators to fine-tune robot behavior in challenging or dynamic scenarios more effectively than with conventional unilateral systems. The paper distinguishes itself from concurrent work like RoboCopilot by specifically demonstrating a reinforcement learning implementation based on the HACTS framework.

In essence, HACTS offers a bridge between the necessity for rich human interaction data in robot learning and the practical constraints of hardware accessibility and complexity. It simplifies bilateral control to its most essential form – joint position synchronization – thereby broadening its applicability and accelerating data-driven robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind HACTS stems from the critical limitations of existing teleoperation systems in supporting advanced robot learning paradigms. The core problem identified is the lack of real-time, bilateral feedback in most teleoperation hardware, which restricts effective human intervention and data collection for complex robot tasks.

Building upon this, the key objectives of the HACTS project are:

1. **Establish Bilateral Joint Synchronization:** To develop a novel teleoperation system that enables real-time, bidirectional (bilateral) joint synchronization between a robot arm and its teleoperation hardware. This allows the teleoperation device to reflect the robot's current joint positions, providing crucial feedback to the human operator.
2. **Enable Human-As-Copilot Intervention:** To create a system where humans can act as "copilots," seamlessly intervening in autonomous robot tasks without disrupting the robot's ongoing motion or losing control. This functionality is crucial for guiding robots through challenging phases, correcting errors, and providing context-rich demonstrations.
3. **Facilitate Action-Correction Data Collection:** To design a system that not only allows for interventions but also effectively collects the "action-correction data" generated during these human-guided rectifications. This data, encompassing both successful corrections and interventions during failures, is hypothesized to be invaluable for improving robot learning models.
4. **Ensure Accessibility and Scalability:** To implement HACTS using low-cost, off-the-shelf components and 3D-printed parts, making the system affordable, easy to replicate, and adaptable to a wide range of robotic platforms. This objective aims to democratize access to advanced teleoperation capabilities for the broader robotics research community.
5. **Enhance Imitation Learning Performance:** To demonstrate that by leveraging action-correction data collected via HACTS, imitation learning models can achieve improved failure recovery capabilities, enhanced data efficiency, and better generalization across both in-distribution and out-of-distribution scenarios.
6. **Support Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning:** To show that HACTS can facilitate more complex human-in-the-loop reinforcement learning setups, allowing robots to learn and adapt from a combination of autonomous exploration and precise human interventions, thereby advancing online robot learning systems.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to bridge the gap between human intuition and robotic autonomy by providing a more effective, intuitive, and accessible interface for human operators to teach and guide robots, ultimately accelerating the development of more capable and robust robot learning systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HACTS (Human-As-Copilot Teleoperation System) is designed as a low-cost, kinematically equivalent leader-follower system that crucially incorporates bilateral position control. This allows for both leader-to-follower synchronization for traditional demonstrations and follower-to-leader synchronization for real-time human intervention.

#### 3.1. A. Hardware Design

The hardware design prioritizes **low-cost**, **lightweight**, and **easy-to-use** principles:
- **Low-Cost Components:** Instead of expensive robotic modules, HACTS utilizes affordable servo motors. Specifically, DYNAMIXEL XL430-W250-T motors are used for the first three links requiring higher torque, and XL330-M288-T motors for the gripper and lighter links. The total hardware cost is kept under $300 (detailed in Table I), making it highly accessible.
- **Lightweight Construction:** To ensure the low-cost motors can effectively support the system, the frame and structural components are predominantly 3D-printed using PLA material, significantly reducing the overall weight.
- **Kinematically Equivalent and Easy-to-Use:** The system's structure is kinematically equivalent to the target robot arm (e.g., UR5), derived from Denavit–Hartenberg parameters with scaled lengths. This miniature design allows for intuitive control, similar to prior successful kinematically equivalent devices like ALOHA and Gello. A key advantage is its generalizability, as it only requires synchronization of joint position information, which is available on most robotic arms.

#### 3.2. B. Software Design

The software stack is streamlined for efficient communication and synchronization:
- **DYNAMIXEL API:** This serves as the primary interface for directly monitoring and adjusting motor positions.
- **Bidirectional Data Flow:**
 - **Leader-to-Follower (Demonstration):** After an initial offset calibration, processed control joint values from the HACTS leader are transmitted to the robot controller.
 - **Follower-to-Leader (Intervention/Copilot Mode):** For reverse synchronization, robot position data is received, undergoes reverse offset compensation to restore original positional values, and is then relayed to the HACTS motors, ensuring the teleoperation device mirrors the robot's state.
- **Manual Override Interface:** An external foot pedal allows the operator to seamlessly switch between autonomous operation (robot controlled by policy) and human intervention states (human taking control via HACTS).

#### 3.3. C. Experimental Setup and Evaluation

The efficacy of HACTS is evaluated through comprehensive experiments on a real-world UR5 robotic arm workstation equipped with a Robotiq 2f-85 gripper and multiple wrist-mounted and external cameras. The experiments address four key research questions across imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms.

### 4. Imitation Learning Experiments

- **Tasks:** Three real-world manipulation tasks are designed: OpenBox (pressing a switch), SteamBun (grasping and placing), and UprightMug (flipping and placing).
- **Algorithms:** Two state-of-the-art visuomotor policy learning methods are used: Action Chunking Transformer (ACT) and Diffusion Policy (DP).
- **Data Collection Protocol:**
 - 100 expert trajectories collected via teleoperation.
 - "Pre-ACT/DP" models trained on 50 random expert trajectories.
 - "Full-ACT/DP" models trained on all 100 expert trajectories (baseline).
 - "HACTS-ACT/DP" models trained on the initial 50 expert trajectories plus 50 human intervention trajectories collected using HACTS with "pre-ACT/DP" executing tasks.
- **Four Experimental Settings for HACTS Data Collection:**
 - **Equal Amount Data Comparison (EADC):** Intervention data recorded at certain task progress, regardless of success, to compare its value against expert data. (Q1)
 - **Failure Correction in In-Distribution Scenarios (FCID):** Action-correction data specifically collected when preliminary models fail on in-distribution object placements. (Q2)
 - **Generalization for Out-of-Distribution Static Scenarios (ODSS):** Action-correction data collected when objects are in novel, unseen static positions. (Q3, part 1)
 - **Generalization for Out-of-Distribution Dynamic Scenarios (ODDS):** Action-correction data collected when objects are moved manually *during* task execution. (Q3, part 2; uses DP due to ACT's temporal chunking limitation).
- **Evaluation Metric:** Success rate over 10 evaluation rollouts.

### 5. Reinforcement Learning Experiments

- **Approach:** RLPD-HACTS, combining HACTS with RLPD (a sample-efficient off-policy RL algorithm that uses demonstrations and corrections). (Q4)
- **Training Stages:**
 1. **Reward Classifier Training:** A binary classifier (ResNet-10 based) is trained using 200 positive and 1,000 negative data points collected via HACTS, indicating task completion.
 2. **Behavior Cloning (BC) Pre-training:** A BC policy is trained on 20 expert trajectories collected via HACTS.
 3. **Online Fine-tuning with RLPD:** The pretrained actor network is fine-tuned online with RLPD, incorporating short, frequent human corrections provided via HACTS.
- **Task:** CloseBin, requiring precise motion control to close a randomly placed and oriented trash bin.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Success rates and episode lengths over 10 trials, and average human intervention length during online training.

This comprehensive methodology allows for a thorough assessment of HACTS's capabilities across different learning paradigms and challenging robotic tasks, demonstrating its utility in both offline data collection and online human-in-the-loop learning.

### 6. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results robustly demonstrate the effectiveness of HACTS in enhancing robot learning capabilities across both imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) tasks.

#### 6.1. A. Imitation Learning Findings

The IL experiments addressed three key research questions (Q1, Q2, Q3), utilizing ACT and DP algorithms across various task scenarios.

- **Q1: Can human intervention data collected via HACTS serve as a viable substitute for an equivalent volume of direct teleoperation data?**
 - **Finding:** Yes. In the Equal Amount Data Comparison (EADC) setting (Table II), HACTS-ACT consistently achieved higher success rates than full-ACT, even though both were trained on the same total amount of data (50 initial expert + 50 HACTS intervention vs. 100 full expert demos). For instance, in OpenBox, HACTS-ACT achieved 80% success compared to full-ACT's 60%. This indicates that HACTS's ability to collect intervention data, particularly in scenarios where preliminary models struggled (e.g., picking actions), makes the collected data more valuable and effectively replaces or even outperforms traditional expert teleoperation data.

- **Q2: Can the action-correction data in HACTS further improve the model’s success rate under in-distribution (ID) scenarios?**
 - **Finding:** Yes. In the Failure Correction in In-Distribution Scenarios (FCID) setting (Table II), HACTS-ACT significantly improved success rates over both pre-ACT and full-ACT. For the UprightMug task, HACTS-ACT achieved a remarkable 70% success rate, a substantial increase from pre-ACT's 10% and full-ACT's 40%. This demonstrates that targeting and collecting corrective data for specific failure modes within known environments is highly effective in boosting model performance and reliability.

- **Q3: Can the action-correction data in HACTS enhance the model’s generalization capability for out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios?**
 - **Finding:** Yes. For both Out-of-Distribution Static Scenarios (ODSS) (Table II, using ACT) and Out-of-Distribution Dynamic Scenarios (ODDS) (Table III, using DP), baseline models (pre-ACT/DP, full-ACT/DP) showed 0% success rates, indicating a complete inability to generalize to unseen or dynamically changing situations. However, with the integration of HACTS-generated action-correction data, both HACTS-ACT and HACTS-DP demonstrated significant generalization capabilities, achieving success rates like 30% (OpenBox, ODSS), 50% (SteamBun, ODSS), 40% (UprightMug, ODSS), and 40% (OpenBox, ODDS). This highlights HACTS's crucial role in making robot policies more robust and adaptable to novel and dynamic environments.

#### 6.2. B. Reinforcement Learning Findings

The RL experiments investigated HACTS's ability to support human-in-the-loop online learning (Q4) using an RLPD-HACTS framework.

- **Q4: Can HACTS further support human-in-the-loop online reinforcement learning?**
 - **Finding:** Yes. The RLPD-HACTS system achieved an 80% success rate in the challenging CloseBin task after 10 minutes of offline pretraining and 45 minutes of online finetuning (Table IV). This performance significantly improved upon the initial BC policy (50% success), demonstrating the effectiveness of integrating HACTS-facilitated human corrections into an online RL loop.
 - Furthermore, the average episode length decreased from 32 steps to 19 steps (Table IV), indicating that the policy became more efficient in completing the task due to the human-assisted trial-and-error learning and sparse reward settings.
 - Analysis of intervention length (Fig. 6) showed an initial increase in average intervention length, reflecting the human operator's need to provide substantial guidance during the early exploration phases of the Q-network. As training progressed and the policy refined, the intervention length gradually decreased to below six steps. This indicates that human interventions became more precise and targeted, providing fine-grained corrections rather than extensive guidance, thereby preventing overly aggressive actions and improving overall policy quality.
 - The authors emphasize that HACTS's superior dexterity and precision, compared to conventional teleoperation methods like a 3D spacemouse, are particularly advantageous for complex manipulation tasks requiring significant twisting or large-scale movements in HITL RL.

In summary, HACTS provides high-quality human copilot data that significantly refines robot learning, improves task performance, and enhances the robustness and generalization capabilities of both imitation learning and reinforcement learning algorithms.

### 7. Significance and Potential Impact

HACTS represents a significant advancement in the field of human-robot interaction and robot learning, offering a novel, practical, and accessible solution to long-standing challenges in teleoperation. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling True Human-As-Copilot Functionality:** The most profound contribution of HACTS is its introduction of bilateral, real-time *joint synchronization*. This simple yet highly effective feedback mechanism transforms traditional unilateral teleoperation into a true "human-as-copilot" system. Operators no longer passively command but actively participate, feeling the robot's movements and seamlessly intervening. This intuitive connection is vital for complex, dynamic tasks where humans need to provide agile and precise corrections, moving beyond the analogy of a driver intervening in an unsynchronized autonomous vehicle.

2. **Democratizing Advanced Teleoperation:** By leveraging low-cost, off-the-shelf components and 3D-printed designs, HACTS is highly affordable (under $300) and scalable. This accessibility lowers the barrier to entry for researchers and developers, enabling wider adoption of advanced teleoperation capabilities. Its compatibility with almost all robotic systems through mere position synchronization means it can be rapidly deployed and adapted, fostering innovation across diverse labs and applications without requiring specialized, expensive hardware.

3. **Revolutionizing Data Collection for Robot Learning:** HACTS addresses a critical bottleneck in data-driven robot learning: the scarcity of rich, context-specific training data.
 - **Action-Correction Data:** The system's ability to collect "action-correction data" generated during human interventions is particularly impactful. This data, encompassing not just successful demonstrations but also human rectifications of robot failures and guidance in novel scenarios, is invaluable for training more robust and generalizable policies. It directly supports research into learning from imperfect demonstrations and failure cases, a growing area in robot learning.
 - **Data Efficiency:** The experiments clearly show that HACTS data significantly boosts data efficiency and failure recovery in imitation learning, allowing models to learn more effectively from smaller, targeted datasets.

4. **Enhancing Imitation Learning and Generalization:** HACTS has been demonstrated to substantially improve the performance of visuomotor policies in imitation learning. It enhances failure recovery in in-distribution scenarios and, critically, enables models to generalize to out-of-distribution static and dynamic environments where baseline models completely fail. This capability is essential for deploying robots in unstructured, real-world settings that inherently involve variability.

5. **Advancing Human-in-the-Loop Reinforcement Learning:** The successful integration of HACTS with RLPD for online human-in-the-loop reinforcement learning opens new avenues for robot autonomy. HACTS provides the precision and dexterity required for humans to deliver effective, fine-grained corrections during online training, accelerating learning and improving task success rates. The observed reduction in intervention length over time indicates that HACTS facilitates a synergistic learning process where human guidance becomes more efficient as the robot's policy improves.

6. **Paving the Way for More Interactive Human-Robot Collaboration:** Beyond just data collection, HACTS fosters a more intuitive and collaborative relationship between humans and robots. By allowing humans to fluidly assist and guide robots, it moves towards a future where robots are not merely autonomous agents but intelligent partners that can leverage human expertise on demand. This has implications for various applications, from industrial assembly and assistive robotics to hazardous environment exploration.

In conclusion, HACTS is a pivotal development that simplifies and democratizes a crucial aspect of robot learning infrastructure. Its focus on accessible, bilateral joint synchronization has the potential to significantly accelerate the development of more capable, robust, and interactive robotic systems, shaping the future of human-robot collaboration and data-driven artificial intelligence in robotics.
