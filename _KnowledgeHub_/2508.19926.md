# FARM: Frame-Accelerated Augmentation and Residual Mixture-of-Experts for Physics-Based High-Dynamic Humanoid Control

> [!summary] Summary
> The FARM framework enhances physics-based humanoid control by combining frame-accelerated augmentation with a residual Mixture-of-Experts architecture, achieving a 42.8% reduction in tracking failure rates and a 14.6% decrease in global mean per-joint position error on high-dynamic motions while preserving performance on low-dynamic actions, alongside introducing the High-Dynamic Humanoid Motion (HDHM) dataset.

## Problem

- Existing physics-based humanoid controllers struggle to generalize robustly to highly dynamic, explosive motions, despite performing well on low-dynamic, everyday movements.
- The lack of a public, standardized benchmark dataset specifically for high-dynamic humanoid motions hinders systematic evaluation and progress in this challenging area.
- Current approaches often require significant engineering overhead for task-specific reward redesign and hyperparameter tuning, limiting large-scale adoption.

## Method

![[2508.19926-method.png]]
*The overall FARM pipeline, including (a) hard sample mining from existing datasets, (b) the training process using PPO, and (c) inference on high-dynamic motion benchmarks.*

- Develops the **FARM** framework, which uses frame-accelerated data augmentation to expose the controller to rapid pose changes during training.
- Incorporates a residual Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) module with a ==Speed-Aware Router== and Dynamic Expert-Assignment, enabling adaptive computational resource allocation based on motion intensity.
- Curates and releases the High-Dynamic Humanoid Motion (HDHM) dataset to serve as a public benchmark for high-dynamic control evaluation.

## Results

- FARM significantly improved high-dynamic motion tracking, reducing failure rates by **42.8%** and global mean per-joint position error (MPJPEg) by **14.6%** on the HDHM dataset compared to the FC baseline.
- The framework preserved near-perfect performance on low-dynamic AMASS motions, achieving a **100%** success rate with minimal increase in MPJPEg (from **40.8** mm to **42.1** mm) relative to the FC baseline.
- The residual MoE adaptively allocated computational resources, activating more experts for high-dynamic motions, and learned structured representations correlated with motion complexity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simply accelerating low-dynamic motion data is insufficient; a fundamental architectural adaptation, such as an adaptive Mixture-of-Experts, is required for robust high-dynamic control.
> - A residual Mixture-of-Experts architecture, when combined with a speed-aware router and dynamic expert assignment, can effectively extend the dynamic range of a base controller without degrading performance on low-dynamic tasks.
> - Targeted training on "hard samples" (mined failure cases from accelerated motions) significantly improves training efficiency and performance for high-dynamic control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Physics-based humanoid motion imitation is a critical area within robotics and character animation, underpinning applications in advanced robotics, digital character creation, and immersive AR/VR experiences. The objective is to develop control policies that enable simulated humanoid characters to accurately track motion-capture data while adhering to physical laws.

Previous advancements in this field, such as the Universal Humanoid Controller (UHC) (Luo et al. 2021), Perpetual Humanoid Control (PHC) (Luo et al. 2023), PHC+ (Luo et al. 2024), and the Fully Constrained (FC) Controller in MaskedMimic (Tessler et al. 2024), have progressively improved motion diversity and generalization. These methods have demonstrated high tracking success rates on large-scale datasets like AMASS (Mahmood et al. 2019), which primarily consists of low-dynamic, everyday motions. However, a significant limitation across these approaches is their reduced ability to generalize to highly dynamic, explosive actions. The incremental learning process of methods like PHC+ can also complicate training and scalability, while unified architectures like FC simplify training but still exhibit generalization gaps for high-dynamic content.

Separately, the application of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures has gained traction in motion control, with works like GMT (Chen et al. 2025), MoRE (Wang et al. 2025), and MoE-Loco (Huang et al. 2025) employing MoE for specialization and scalability in tasks such as whole-body motion tracking, gait learning, and multitask locomotion. These methods typically use a fixed number of experts or predefined expert assignments.

This work addresses the identified gap in universal humanoid control by focusing specifically on robust control of high-dynamic motions. It seeks to develop a controller that maintains strong performance on low-dynamic motions while overcoming the challenges posed by explosive actions. Furthermore, it introduces an adaptive MoE approach that differs from prior MoE applications by dynamically adjusting expert capacity based on motion intensity, rather than using a fixed allocation. The creation of a dedicated high-dynamic motion benchmark also fills a void in public resources for evaluating progress in this specific domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified physics-based humanoid controller capable of robustly tracking both low-dynamic, everyday motions and challenging, high-dynamic actions. This aims to reduce the current engineering overhead associated with per-task reward redesign and hyperparameter tuning, thereby promoting the large-scale adoption of physics-based motion imitation.

The motivation for this work stems from several observations:

1. **Limitations of Existing Universal Controllers:** While state-of-the-art controllers such as FC, PHC, and PHC+ achieve high success rates on datasets like AMASS, which are predominantly composed of gentle motions, they consistently struggle with rapid, high-dynamic actions. This limits their practical applicability in scenarios requiring explosive movements.
2. **Ineffectiveness of Simple Data Augmentation:** An initial hypothesis explored was to artificially accelerate low-dynamic motion clips to simulate high-dynamic conditions. However, experiments revealed that directly fine-tuning existing controllers on such frame-accelerated data provided minimal improvement for high-dynamic actions and, in some cases, degraded performance on low-dynamic sequences. This indicated that simply increasing the "speed" of input data was insufficient; a more fundamental architectural adaptation was required.
3. **Analogy to Human Motor Attention:** The authors draw an analogy to human motor control, where routine movements demand minimal cognitive resources, while complex, explosive actions require heightened attention and additional processing capacity. This analogy motivates the design of a controller that can dynamically adjust its computational capacity based on the intensity of the motion being tracked. The goal is to allocate more resources to challenging high-dynamic segments without over-provisioning for or degrading performance on simpler, low-dynamic movements.
4. **Absence of a Dedicated Benchmark:** The research community lacked a public, standardized dataset specifically curated for evaluating high-dynamic humanoid control. This hampered systematic progress and comparative analysis in this challenging area. A secondary objective was therefore to create and release such a benchmark.

These motivations collectively drove the development of the FARM framework, which combines data augmentation techniques with an adaptive neural network architecture to address the challenges of high-dynamic humanoid control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The FARM framework is an end-to-end system designed to improve physics-based humanoid control for high-dynamic motions. It comprises three main components: frame-accelerated augmentation, a robust base controller, and a residual Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) module.

#### 3.1. Overall Framework Pipeline

The training pipeline initiates by mining "hard samples" from the AMASS dataset. This is achieved by running the baseline FC controller on AMASS motions accelerated by 1.25x. Any motion where the global mean per-joint position error (MPJPEg) exceeds 0.5 meters at any frame is flagged as a hard sample. These hard samples are then used for training the FARM model. During training, these samples undergo further frame-accelerated augmentation. The trained policy is subsequently evaluated on the High-Dynamic Humanoid Motion (HDHM) dataset to assess its generalization capabilities on unseen high-dynamic motions. The policy optimization uses Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL) with the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm. The agent’s state includes proprioception and terrain information, the goal is a sequence of future target poses, and actions are PD target joint angles. The reward function encourages motion tracking and penalizes excessive joint torques.

#### 3.2. Frame-Accelerated Augmentation

For each training clip, a constant acceleration factor `v` is uniformly sampled from `U[1.0, 1.5]`. The original motion, typically at 30 Hz (∆t = 1/30 s), is resampled using a new effective time step ∆t_new = v ∆t. Poses at these new virtual timestamps are obtained via linear interpolation from the original frames. The resulting sequence is then fed to the controller at the original 30 Hz rate. This process effectively widens the inter-frame pose displacement, exposing the policy to more rapid transitions characteristic of high-dynamic motions.

#### 3.3. Residual Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)

To enhance the dynamic range of a base controller without compromising its performance on low-dynamic motions, FARM incorporates a residual MoE module. The input MLP and Transformer layers of a pretrained FC controller are frozen, acting as a robust base. Only the output MLP of the FC controller is updated during training. The MoE module takes the input features (z_in) to the base Transformer and computes a residual correction (∆z). This residual is added to the output of the frozen base Transformer (z_base) to form a combined representation (z = z_base + ∆z), which is then fed into the updated output MLP to generate actions. This design ensures that the original controller's behavior is largely preserved, with the MoE providing adaptive corrections for more challenging segments.

The residual MoE is equipped with two mechanisms:
- **Speed-Aware Router (SAR):** The SAR directs input data to specialized experts based on motion intensity. During training, the average joint velocity of the reference frame for each sample in a batch is calculated. Samples are then sorted by speed and partitioned into three evenly sized speed groups (0, 1, 2). These labels serve as an auxiliary supervision signal for the router, optimized using a cross-entropy loss (L_CE) combined with the main reinforcement learning objective (L_router = L_RL + λ_speed L_CE). This encourages experts to specialize in specific velocity regimes.
- **Dynamic Expert-Assignment (DEA):** Inspired by human attention, DEA activates a variable number of experts based on the detected motion intensity. The router's softmax output `p` (over `E` experts) determines `k = arg max(p)`. Experts from 1 to `k` are then activated. The residual `∆z` is computed as a weighted sum of the outputs from these `k` active experts, where weights `w_i` are derived from the router's probabilities. If `k=0`, no experts are activated, and the model reverts to the base FC controller. Each expert module within the MoE copies the Transformer architecture and weights of the FC controller and appends a zero-initialized linear projection layer, ensuring that the residual path does not alter the initial behavior of the system.

#### 3.4. High-Dynamic Humanoid Motion (HDHM) Dataset Curation

Recognizing the absence of a dedicated public benchmark for high-dynamic motions, the authors curated the HDHM dataset. This dataset comprises 3593 physics-plausible motion clips from five sources: AIST++, EMDB, Motion-X Kungfu, and two in-house collections named Text-Convert and Video-Convert. All clips are resampled to 30 Hz and stored in SMPL format. A rigorous manual filtering process was applied to remove clips with non-physical artifacts such as ground penetration, floating, severe joint jitter, or body self-intersections.
- **Text-Convert:** Generated using motion diffusion models conditioned on high-dynamic text prompts.
- **Video-Convert:** Created by converting martial arts videos from YouTube and PKU-DyMVHumans using the GVHMR model.
The HDHM dataset is intended to serve as a new open benchmark for high-dynamic humanoid control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated FARM against state-of-the-art baselines (FC and PHC+) on both the newly curated HDHM dataset and the AMASS dataset, using metrics such as motion tracking success rate, global mean per-joint position error (MPJPEg), and root-relative MPJPE (MPJPEl).

#### 4.1. Performance on High-Dynamic Motions (HDHM)

- **Quantitative Performance:** FARM demonstrated a notable improvement in tracking high-dynamic motions. On the overall HDHM dataset, FARM reduced the tracking failure rate by 42.8% and lowered the global mean per-joint position error (MPJPEg) by 14.6% relative to the baseline FC controller. Specifically, FARM achieved a 95.6% success rate and an MPJPEg of 95.0 mm, compared to FC's 92.3% success rate and 111.3 mm MPJPEg. These improvements were consistent across all five subsets of the HDHM dataset (AIST++, EMDB, Motion-X/Kungfu, Text-Convert, Video-Convert), with FARM consistently achieving the highest success rates and lowest MPJPE scores.
- **Qualitative Performance:** Visual comparisons indicated that FARM robustly completes complex high-dynamic motions, successfully maintaining balance and closely following the reference trajectories. In contrast, the baseline FC model frequently exhibited drifting or outright failures (e.g., falling or losing balance) during rapid pose changes.

#### 4.2. Performance on Low-Dynamic Motions (AMASS)

- FARM preserved near-perfect accuracy on low-dynamic motions. On the AMASS-test dataset, FARM achieved a 100% success rate, comparable to the FC baseline, with only a minor increase in MPJPEg (42.1 mm for FARM versus 40.8 mm for FC, an increase of less than 2 mm). Similar performance was observed on the AMASS-train dataset. This indicates that FARM successfully extends dynamic range without degrading performance on standard, less challenging motions.

#### 4.3. Expert Behavior Analysis

- **Correlation with Motion Dynamics:** A case study on a cartwheel motion demonstrated that the activation of experts in the residual MoE module correlated with motion intensity. Fewer experts were engaged during low-motion phases, while high-dynamic segments triggered the activation of more experts.
- **Capacity Allocation:** Statistical analysis of expert usage confirmed that the FARM controller engaged more experts on the HDHM dataset (over half of frames involving one or two experts) compared to the AMASS-test set (80% of frames using only the base controller). This indicated that the model adaptively allocated more computational capacity for high-dynamic motions.
- **Structured Representations:** A t-SNE visualization of the residual MoE's output embeddings revealed clear spatial separation corresponding to different numbers of activated experts. This indicated that the MoE learned structured and distinct representations based on motion complexity, supporting the effectiveness of the Speed-Aware Router (SAR) and Dynamic Expert-Assignment (DEA) mechanisms.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Frame Acceleration (FA):** Applying FA alone yielded minimal improvement, suggesting that the base model could not fully leverage the augmented high-dynamic data without architectural enhancements.
- **Residual MoE Design:** Combining FA with MoE provided clear performance gains. The residual MoE (Res-MoE) consistently outperformed the "No MoE" baseline and a "Full MoE" variant (where both base and expert modules were jointly updated). The Full MoE degraded performance on AMASS-test, highlighting the importance of preserving the pretrained base controller.
- **Router Configurations:** The Speed-Aware Router (SAR) was found to be crucial; its absence resulted in noticeable performance drops. The Dynamic Expert-Assignment (DEA) mechanism outperformed fixed expert selection strategies (e.g., Top1 or Top2 experts), indicating a more effective balance of expert capacity.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** Heatmap analysis showed that both insufficient or excessive frame acceleration factors, as well as too few or too many experts, led to suboptimal performance, emphasizing the importance of parameter tuning.
- **Fine-tuning Data:** Fine-tuning on mined failure cases from AMASS achieved slightly better performance on both HDHM and AMASS-test, while dramatically reducing training time from 35 hours to 6 hours compared to fine-tuning on the full dataset. This highlighted the efficiency of targeted training on challenging samples.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FARM framework represents an advancement in the field of physics-based humanoid control, particularly for highly dynamic motions. Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Humanoid Control Capability:** FARM establishes a new baseline for robust physics-based control of high-dynamic humanoid actions. It demonstrates that controllers can achieve high accuracy and robustness for explosive movements (reducing tracking failure rates by 42.8% and MPJPEg by 14.6% on HDHM) without compromising their established performance on low-dynamic, everyday motions. This broadens the spectrum of motions that can be reliably imitated in physics simulations.
2. **Introduction of a Standardized Benchmark:** The curation and release of the High-Dynamic Humanoid Motion (HDHM) dataset address a critical gap in public resources. This benchmark provides a standardized, diverse, and physics-plausible collection of high-velocity motion clips, which can serve as a common ground for future research, enabling more consistent evaluation and accelerated progress in high-dynamic humanoid control.
3. **Methodological Innovations:**
 - **Frame-Accelerated Augmentation:** This data augmentation technique offers an effective strategy to leverage existing low-dynamic datasets to synthesize training samples that expose models to high-velocity pose changes, thereby improving generalization to high-dynamic scenarios.
 - **Residual Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) with Adaptive Capacity:** The integration of a residual MoE module with a speed-aware router (SAR) and dynamic expert-assignment (DEA) introduces a novel adaptive architecture. This design allows the model to dynamically allocate computational resources based on motion intensity, akin to human motor attention, ensuring efficiency and targeted intervention for challenging movements. This approach avoids the common pitfalls of fixed-expert MoE systems and preserves the reliability of the base controller.
 - **Efficient Training Paradigm:** The research demonstrates that focusing training on "hard samples" (failure cases mined from accelerated motions) can significantly reduce training time (from 35 hours to 6 hours) while maintaining or even improving performance. This suggests a more efficient training paradigm for complex control tasks.
4. **Broader Applications:** The ability to control humanoids across a wide dynamic range can have a substantial impact on various applications. In robotics, it could enable more agile and adaptable bipedal robots capable of performing complex physical tasks. In digital animation, gaming, and immersive AR/VR experiences, it could lead to more realistic, expressive, and physically accurate character behaviors, especially for action-oriented content.
5. **Direction for Future Research:** The paper identifies current limitations, such as failures on motions with subtle physical artifacts (e.g., ground penetration), and suggests future work on artifact elimination at the data generation stage and architectural improvements for noise robustness. The goal of deploying the FARM framework on real-world humanoid robots also outlines a clear path for future applied research.

Overall, FARM contributes both a robust control framework and a valuable benchmark, advancing the state-of-the-art in physics-based humanoid control and laying groundwork for more capable and versatile virtual and robotic avatars.
