# QuadRocket: An Aerial Robotic Testbed for Adaptive Thrust-Vector Control of Rocket-Like Vehicles

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the Universidade de Lisboa and University of Macau developed QuadRocket, an aerial robotic testbed employing a quadrotor as a dynamically reconfigurable thrust-vector actuator for rocket-like vehicles. The platform successfully validated a nonlinear adaptive control strategy, demonstrating centimeter-level trajectory tracking accuracy in experiments.

## Problem

- High costs and safety risks associated with validating advanced, complex control algorithms on actual aerospace launch vehicles.
- The need for robust nonlinear and adaptive control strategies that provide global stability and tracking performance for demanding and uncertain aerospace missions.
- Limitations in existing low-cost test platforms, such as gimbal-specific constraints or reliance on simpler linear control methods, which do not fully replicate complex rocket dynamics and advanced control challenges.

## Method

- Conceptualized and designed QuadRocket, an aerial robotic testbed where a commercial quadrotor is mechanically coupled to a larger cylindrical body via a universal joint, functioning as a thrust-vector actuator.
- Developed a hierarchical nonlinear adaptive control strategy combining an adaptive backstepping controller for the rigid body's trajectory tracking and a dynamic-surface-based controller for the quadrotor's attitude.
- Utilized a reduced-attitude representation on the 2-sphere for the main body's orientation and introduced a control-point transformation to mitigate non-minimum phase behavior in the coupled dynamics.

## Results

- Achieved accurate trajectory tracking in experiments with a mean position error of 1.82 cm, demonstrating centimeter-level precision and robustness against real-world noise and delays.
- The adaptive control layers successfully estimated and compensated for unmodeled disturbances, including mechanical misalignments and time-varying thrust degradation due to battery voltage.
- Validated the concept of using a quadrotor as a thrust-vector actuator, as the quadrotor's attitude tracking was sufficiently fast and accurate to align the thrust vector of the main body.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A quadrotor can effectively serve as a dynamically reconfigurable thrust-vector actuator for a larger rocket-like body, providing an alternative to traditional gimbal-based systems and simplifying actuation.
> - Combining a reduced-attitude representation on the 2-sphere with a specific control-point transformation can simplify the control design and effectively mitigate non-minimum phase behavior in axisymmetric, thrust-vector-controlled aerial vehicles.
> - Nonlinear adaptive backstepping controllers can provide robust, almost-global asymptotic trajectory tracking and disturbance compensation for complex, underactuated systems even in the presence of real-world unmodeled dynamics and varying disturbances.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of the QuadRocket testbed is situated within the broader context of research into autonomous vehicle control, particularly for systems with high operational costs and strict safety requirements, such as launch vehicles. Validating advanced control algorithms, especially nonlinear and adaptive strategies, is a critical step before their deployment in real-world aerospace applications. These advanced algorithms offer enhanced stability and tracking performance in the presence of unknown disturbances and parametric uncertainties, but their complexity necessitates thorough testing.

Existing efforts to create low-cost, low-risk test platforms for thrust-vectoring control include electric brushless motor-driven contra-rotating propellers attached to gimbal mechanisms, as seen in projects from Switzerland. These platforms often leverage Model Predictive Control (MPC) for trajectory planning and tracking. The QuadRocket differentiates itself by achieving thrust vectoring through multirotor attitude and force generation, thus avoiding gimbal-specific constraints such as servo angle/rate limits and backlash. Furthermore, while the mentioned works primarily validate optimization-based Guidance, Navigation, and Control (GNC) approaches, this paper focuses on developing a closed-form nonlinear adaptive controller with a design emphasis on stability and robustness guarantees without requiring online optimization.

In the domain of quadrotor-based solutions, prior research from some of the authors addressed trajectory tracking for a flying inverted pendulum via adaptive backstepping, treating the pendulum as a point mass. Other quadrotor-based schemes have been proposed to represent launch vehicle dynamics, sometimes incorporating flexible inverted pendulums or hanging masses to simulate aerodynamic instability and fuel sloshing. These typically use the quadrotor as the main body with an attached pendulum replicating launch vehicle modes, and often employ linear control methods like Linear Quadratic Regulators (LQR). The QuadRocket presents an alternative configuration where a substantial cylindrical body, rather than the quadrotor, is the primary mass contributor, mimicking a rocket-like vehicle where the quadrotor functions as a thrust-vector actuator.

The control strategy employed in this work also contributes to the field of nonlinear and geometric adaptive control techniques. These methods are commonly used to achieve stability guarantees in the presence of constant disturbances. The paper applies these techniques to an original model of a thrust-vector-controlled rocket-like vehicle, utilizing a reduced-attitude representation on the 2-sphere. This approach addresses a recognized gap in rocket trajectory control literature, where integrated tracking solutions offering (near) global performance are less prevalent. Reduced-attitude representations have a history in rigid-body attitude control for regulating body-fixed axes and managing torque underactuation. More recently, these techniques have been applied to aerial vehicles, such as quadrotors, where thrust direction governs position tracking independent of yaw. This work extends this methodology to axisymmetric aerial vehicles controlled by thrust vectoring.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is the conceptualization, design, and experimental validation of QuadRocket, an aerial robotic testbed. This platform aims to provide a low-cost and low-risk environment for validating advanced thrust-vector control (TVC) strategies, particularly those relevant to launch-vehicle-type systems.

The motivation for this work arises from several factors:
- **High Costs and Risks in Aerospace:** The launch vehicle industry is characterized by high operational costs and stringent safety requirements. This context makes the testing and validation of novel control algorithms challenging and expensive, underscoring the need for reliable, affordable, and safe preliminary testing platforms.
- **Evolving Mission Demands:** Contemporary and future aerospace missions often entail complex and demanding scenarios. These require advanced control algorithms capable of adapting to a broad range of operating conditions and varying trajectory tracking requirements across different mission phases.
- **Suitability of Nonlinear and Adaptive Control:** Nonlinear and adaptive control strategies are considered appropriate for these demanding scenarios due to their inherent ability to achieve global or near-global stability and tracking performance in the presence of unknown disturbances and parametric uncertainties. However, these algorithms are generally more complex and less established than traditional linear control approaches, requiring robust verification.
- **Leveraging Existing Technology:** The project leverages the widespread availability, cost-effectiveness, and agile characteristics of quadrotor Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs). Quadrotors also benefit from an extensive body of control-oriented research, making them a suitable foundation for a testbed.
- **Addressing Control Challenges:** The QuadRocket is designed to replicate the essential physical coupling between thrust-vector direction and rigid-body attitude, which is characteristic of rockets. This design enables realistic evaluation of GNC strategies that contend with coupled translational and rotational dynamics through an off-center actuation point.

The control design for the QuadRocket is bifurcated into two specific objectives:
1. **Flight Controller Design:** To develop a trajectory tracking controller for a generic axisymmetric rigid body. This body is actuated by a vectored force applied along its longitudinal axis, specifically below the center of mass. This controller defines the desired thrust-vector.
2. **Actuator Control Design:** To develop a control law for the angular velocity command of the quadrotor. This ensures that the quadrotor accurately tracks the desired thrust-vector provided by the flight controller, effectively treating the quadrotor as a thrust-vector actuator for the main cylindrical body.

Beyond its primary role as a testbed, the researchers envision additional applications for the QuadRocket's design, including autonomous cargo transportation, where the cylindrical structure could carry payloads, and autonomous inspection of civil infrastructures, particularly ceilings, due to its agile close-proximity operational capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research encompasses the mechanical design of the QuadRocket platform, the derivation of a simplified dynamic model, and the development of a hierarchical nonlinear adaptive control strategy.

**Platform Design:**
The QuadRocket is designed as an aerial robotic testbed. It consists of a commercial quadrotor UAV mechanically coupled to a cylindrical body via an off-the-shelf aluminum universal joint. This configuration results in a flying inverted pendulum with non-negligible inertia, mirroring the dynamics of a thrust-vector-controlled rocket. The cylindrical body is constructed from carbon-fiber tubes and disks, with carbon-fiber legs for ground stability. A battery for the quadrotor is strategically placed near the top of the cylindrical structure to shift the overall center of mass, enhancing control authority by increasing the torque lever arm. The universal joint allows two degrees of freedom for rotational motion between the quadrotor and the cylindrical body (gimbal angles up to 40° tilt) while imposing a rotational constraint on the third axis, meaning the quadrotor and main body share the same yaw motion. The fully assembled vehicle has a total mass of 1.55 kg, with the carbon fiber structure being the largest mass contributor (41.3%). The quadrotor itself is an FPV racing type, equipped with brushless motors and 5-inch propellers capable of producing 55 N maximum thrust. It uses a BrainFPV RADIX 2 HD Flight Controller running BetaFlight software, configured for manual mode to accept external thrust and angular velocity commands via an RC link.

**Modeling Abstraction:**
To simplify the control problem, the coupled multibody system is abstracted into a single axisymmetric rigid body (Assumption 1). In this model, the cylindrical body defines the inertial position, velocity, and orientation of the vehicle, while the abstracted rigid body possesses the combined mass and approximate inertia properties of both physical components. The quadrotor is treated as a thrust-vector actuator, providing an instantaneous force vector `u` (magnitude `T` and direction determined by the quadrotor's orientation relative to the main body). It is assumed that the quadrotor's angular velocity can be regulated by a sufficiently fast inner-loop controller, effectively treating it as a direct control input (Assumption 2). An unknown, constant, or slowly time-varying exogenous disturbance `b` is also considered (Assumption 3).

**Dynamic Modeling and Reduced Attitude Representation:**
The kinematics (position and orientation) and dynamics (translational and rotational) of this abstracted rigid body are formulated. To exploit the vehicle's axial symmetry and decouple yaw from thrust-vector direction, a reduced attitude representation on the 2-sphere is adopted. This involves projecting the vehicle's full attitude (rotation matrix `R`) into a unit vector `r_3` representing the direction of its longitudinal axis. This simplification allows for a more intuitive control design for pitch and roll (grouped as `Ω_12`) while treating yaw as a decoupled motion. The inertia tensor `J` is simplified to a diagonal matrix `diag(j_⊥, j_⊥, j_∥)` due to axial symmetry (Assumption 4).

**Non-minimum Phase Behavior Mitigation:**
The derived dynamics exhibit a coupling between force and torque actuation, which is characteristic of underactuated vehicles controlled by vectored forces and can lead to non-minimum phase behavior. To mitigate this, a coordinate change is introduced. The system dynamics are re-expressed with respect to an arbitrary control point `p_δ` located at a distance `δ` above the vehicle's center of mass along its longitudinal axis. By setting `δ = j_⊥ / (mL)` (where `j_⊥` is transverse inertia, `m` is mass, and `L` is the distance between CoM and universal joint) and ensuring `ω_3 = 0` (rotational motion around the longitudinal axis), the coupling term is canceled, simplifying the model for control design.

**Nonlinear Control Design:**
The control strategy is structured into two main parts:

1. **Virtual Thrust-Vector Control (Problem 1):** An adaptive backstepping controller is developed to achieve almost-global trajectory tracking for the single rigid-body model in the presence of unknown constant disturbances.
 - **Error Definitions:** Position `z_1`, velocity `z_2`, reduced attitude `z_r` (defined on the 2-sphere), and angular velocity `z_Ω` errors are progressively defined.
 - **Lyapunov Functions:** A series of Lyapunov candidate functions (`V_1` to `V_6`, and final `V`) are used to systematically derive control laws and adaptation laws.
 - **Disturbance Adaptation:** Three adaptation laws (`˙b_b1`, `˙b_b2`, `˙b_b3`) are derived to estimate the constant exogenous disturbance `b`, enhancing robustness.
 - **Control Inputs:** The desired direction for the longitudinal axis (`r_3d`) is derived, and the longitudinal component of the virtual thrust-vector (`u_3`) is determined. The control torque input (`Γ_c`) for attitude control is formulated.
 - **Final Virtual Thrust-Vector:** The complete virtual thrust-vector `u` (magnitude and direction) is computed based on `u_3` and `Γ_c`. The controller is proven to render the tracking error variables almost globally asymptotically convergent to zero, with bounded closed-loop signals and convergence of the primary disturbance estimate `b_b1` to the true value.

2. **Quadrotor Attitude Control (Problem 2):** A dynamic-surface-based attitude controller is designed to enable the quadrotor to track the desired thrust-vector output from the virtual thrust-vector controller.
 - **Desired Thrust-Vector Filtering:** The desired thrust-vector `u_d` from the flight controller is passed through a first-order low-pass filter (Dynamic Surface Control technique) to generate `u_d`, avoiding explicit differentiation of virtual control signals. This introduces a filtering error but simplifies control design.
 - **Quadrotor Control:** The desired thrust magnitude `T` for the quadrotor is set as `||u_d||`. The desired direction `r_3dq` for the quadrotor's third axis (corresponding to the thrust direction) is derived from `u_d`.
 - **Angular Velocity Command:** A control law for the quadrotor's angular velocity `ω_q` is derived, ensuring that the quadrotor's attitude aligns with `r_3dq` and its yaw component `ω_3q` is zero (to satisfy the condition for non-minimum phase behavior cancellation). This controller ensures almost global asymptotic stability for the quadrotor's attitude tracking.

**Experimental Implementation:**
The control architecture was implemented in a MATLAB/Simulink environment. A VICON motion capture system (30 Vantage cameras, 100 Hz, sub-millimeter accuracy) was used to measure the QuadRocket's state (position, velocity, orientation). These measurements were pre-processed and fed into the Simulink controller, which computes thrust and angular velocity commands. These commands are sent to the quadrotor via an RC communication link at 100 Hz, with the onboard Betaflight software regulating motor currents at 3.2 kHz. Conservative adaptation gains were selected for the real-world experiments compared to simulations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research involved both computer simulations and flight experiments to validate the QuadRocket platform and the proposed control architecture.

**Computer Simulation Results:**
- **Trajectory Tracking:** Simulations demonstrated accurate trajectory tracking even with significant initial position errors and an initial tilt. The QuadRocket successfully converged to and maintained the desired trajectory.
- **Error Convergence:** The position, velocity, and attitude errors exhibited asymptotic convergence to the origin within the initial seconds of flight. Residual errors, characterized by spikes, were observed during the transition intervals of the desired velocity profile, propagating from the quadrotor's attitude tracking error.
- **Disturbance Compensation:** The QuadRocket demonstrated the ability to track the desired trajectory in the presence of a simulated unknown constant disturbance. The first disturbance estimate, `b_b1`, converged closely to the true disturbance value. The other disturbance estimates, `b_b2` and `b_b3`, exhibited steady-state errors, which is consistent with adaptive backstepping designs without persistent excitation.
- **Actuation Performance:** The hover thrust magnitude stabilized at a value lower than the vehicle's weight due to adaptive compensation of the simulated negative disturbance along the z-axis. The quadrotor's angular velocity commands were primarily excited during trajectory transition intervals. The actual thrust-vector components produced by the quadrotor closely tracked their desired values, supporting the modeling assumption of the quadrotor as an effective thrust-vector actuator. The simulation results provided empirical confirmation of the closed-loop stability of the interconnected system.

**Experimental Results:**
- **Accurate Tracking:** Flight experiments conducted in an indoor motion-capture arena confirmed the viability of the control scheme. The QuadRocket achieved centimeter-level precision in trajectory tracking, with a mean position error (`||z_1||`) of 1.82 cm after an initial transient of approximately 5 seconds.
- **Actuation Effort and Dynamics:** The vehicle operated with moderate actuation effort. Similar to simulations, transient intervals in the reference velocity induced spikes in the errors, particularly in linear and angular velocity. The experiments revealed the presence of high-frequency noise in linear and angular velocity estimates, which impacted the angular velocity command, a difference from the noiseless simulation.
- **Non-minimum Phase Behavior:** While minimized by the control-point transformation, non-minimum phase behavior was not entirely eliminated during experiments. This suggested minor inaccuracies in the physical parameters used to compute the `δ` value for the control point shift. Despite these uncertainties, the control law demonstrated robustness, maintaining accurate tracking.
- **Adaptive Disturbance Estimation:** The adaptive layers effectively counteracted disturbances. The first adaptation layer, `b_b1`, estimated a significant disturbance, notably along the y-axis, attributed to potential misalignment between the quadrotor's attachment point and the vehicle's center of mass. The estimated z-axis disturbance varied over time, reflecting thrust performance degradation due to decreasing battery voltage, indicating the controller's ability to handle time-varying and state-dependent disturbances beyond its theoretical constant disturbance assumption.
- **Quadrotor as Actuator:** The quadrotor's attitude tracking was sufficiently fast and accurate for it to function as a thrust-vector actuator for the main body. This validated the core assumption for the applicability of the platform as a rocket prototype and the extension of closed-loop stability to the interconnected system.
- **Dynamic Surface Control Performance:** A comparison between the actual quadrotor attitude tracking error and the error used in the dynamic surface control showed that while the latter allowed for a simplified control scheme by avoiding explicit differentiation, the actual error exhibited larger steady-state values and poorer transient performance. However, this did not compromise the overall trajectory tracking performance of the QuadRocket.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The QuadRocket represents a significant contribution to the field of aerial robotics and control systems for rocket-like vehicles, with several implications:

- **Novel Testbed Architecture:** The QuadRocket is presented as the first quadrotor-based rocket prototype where the cylindrical main body contributes the largest mass and is connected to the quadrotor via a universal joint, forming a distinct flying inverted pendulum configuration. This architecture provides a unique physical analogy for thrust-vector-controlled rocket dynamics.
- **Validation of Advanced Control Strategies:** The platform offers a low-cost, low-risk, and repeatable environment for the validation and verification of advanced nonlinear and adaptive guidance, navigation, and control (GNC) algorithms. This addresses a critical need in the aerospace industry, where the development and deployment of new technologies face high safety requirements and operational costs.
- **Demonstrated Actuation Paradigm:** The research successfully demonstrates the concept of using a quadrotor as a dynamically reconfigurable thrust-vector actuator. This approach avoids the mechanical complexities and limitations (e.g., servo limits, backlash) of traditional gimbal-based thrust vectoring systems, opening avenues for simpler and more agile actuation mechanisms in similar systems.
- **Original Control Framework:** The development of an original dynamic model, explicitly exploiting reduced-attitude representation on the 2-sphere and incorporating a control-point transformation to mitigate non-minimum phase behavior, is a key methodological contribution. This integrated adaptive backstepping controller, merging position and attitude control within a single loop, advances the state of the art for trajectory tracking of axisymmetric aerial vehicles.
- **Robustness to Real-World Conditions:** The successful experimental validation in a motion-capture arena, in the presence of actuation delays, sensing noise, and unmodeled disturbances/uncertainties, confirms the robustness and practical applicability of the proposed control framework. This is crucial for bridging the gap between theoretical control design and real-world system performance.
- **Versatile Platform for Future Research:** The QuadRocket platform is presented as a versatile tool for future research. This includes exploring more aggressive maneuvers and complex disturbance scenarios, integrating with onboard state estimation and sensing systems (moving beyond VICON), and extending the control framework to multi-vehicle cooperative scenarios or other types of thrust-vector-controlled robotic systems.
- **Potential for Broader Applications:** Beyond its primary role as a research testbed, the design of the QuadRocket inherently suggests practical applications such as autonomous cargo transportation (where the cylindrical body could carry payloads) and autonomous inspection of civil infrastructures, particularly in confined or elevated spaces like ceilings, due to its ability to perform agile, close-proximity operations.
