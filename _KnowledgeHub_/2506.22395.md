# Test-Time Consistency in Vision Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of British Columbia and Vector Institute introduce a test-time consistency framework that adaptively tunes Vision-Language Model parameters at inference. This approach significantly improves semantic consistency and overall performance on the MM-R3 benchmark across various models and tasks.

## Problem

- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) often produce inconsistent or contradictory outputs when presented with semantically equivalent inputs, such as rephrased questions or restyled images.
- This semantic inconsistency undermines the reliability, interpretability, and trustworthiness of VLMs, particularly in high-stakes applications.
- Existing consistency-enhancing methods typically involve computationally intensive training-time solutions like architectural modifications or large-scale fine-tuning, which are impractical for many deployment scenarios or proprietary models.

## Method

- A lightweight, post-hoc ==test-time consistency framework== that applies a small number of gradient-based updates to the VLM's language modeling head at inference time, using only the single test input.
- The method uses two complementary objectives: ==Cross-Entropy Agreement Loss (L_CE)== to align token-level output distributions across semantically equivalent input variants, and ==Pseudo-Label Consistency Loss (L_PL)== to guide predictions towards a dominant self-consistent pseudo-label.
- An ==adaptive step selection mechanism== automatically determines the optimal number of update steps per test instance by maximizing an internal consistency score, eliminating the need for manual tuning.

## Results

- The proposed test-time consistency framework achieved substantial gains in semantic consistency and overall performance across LLaVA 1.5M, LLaVA-Next, and Qwen2-VL models on the MM-R3 benchmark.
- For LLaVA-1.5M, the overall score (O_all) improved from **52.73** to **64.08** on Question Rephrasing, with consistency (Con) rising from **48.55** to **79.11**.
- The test-time approach yielded performance competitive with, and in some cases superior to, resource-intensive full fine-tuning, while demonstrating that consistency gains did not degrade base model accuracy on unperturbed data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Semantic consistency in Vision-Language Models can be improved at inference time through lightweight, instance-specific parameter adaptation without requiring supervised retraining.
> - Combining token-level distributional alignment (Cross-Entropy Agreement Loss) with sequence-level pseudo-label guidance (Pseudo-Label Consistency Loss) effectively enforces VLM consistency.
> - An adaptive selection of update steps is important to prevent over-adaptation and optimize consistency for individual test instances, while generally preserving or improving base model accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) has witnessed remarkable advancements, with models achieving impressive performance across a wide array of multimodal tasks, including visual question answering, image captioning, and complex visual reasoning. Traditional evaluations of these models primarily emphasize metrics such as accuracy, which measures a model's ability to produce correct answers. However, a growing body of recent research, including significant contributions from the authors' own group (Chou et al., 2024), has revealed a critical and often overlooked shortcoming: semantic inconsistency. This phenomenon manifests as VLMs generating divergent or contradictory outputs when presented with semantically equivalent inputs – for instance, different phrasings of the same question or stylistically varied versions of the same image. Such inconsistency severely undermines the reliability, interpretability, and trustworthiness of VLMs, particularly in high-stakes deployment scenarios where consistent behavior is paramount.

Prior approaches to addressing this inconsistency largely fall into two categories: modifying model architectures or conducting extensive fine-tuning on large, specially curated datasets. These training-time solutions, while effective to some extent, often come with significant drawbacks. They are typically computationally intensive, require access to vast amounts of labeled training data, necessitate intrusive modifications to the model's internal structure (e.g., adapter insertion), and are impractical for proprietary models or environments with limited computational resources. The MM-R3 benchmark, developed by this research group, specifically highlights these issues by systematically evaluating VLM robustness under controlled semantic perturbations.

This paper distinguishes itself by proposing a novel paradigm: **test-time consistency**. Unlike the majority of prior work that focuses on improving consistency during training, this approach operates entirely at inference time. It falls under the umbrella of "test-time adaptation" (TTA), a broader field that aims to improve model performance on unseen test data without retraining on the original dataset. However, most existing TTA methods primarily focus on enhancing *accuracy* under distribution shifts (e.g., MEMO, Test-Time Prompt Tuning, MedAdapter), whereas this work specifically targets *semantic consistency*. By being entirely post-hoc, model-agnostic, and plug-and-play, this framework offers a highly practical solution that circumvents the limitations of training-centric methods. It shifts the focus from pre-training or fine-tuning to leveraging the information inherent in a single test input itself, thereby opening a new and promising direction for enhancing the robustness and dependability of multimodal AI systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel and effective method for improving the semantic consistency of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) at inference time. The authors aim to enhance VLM reliability by ensuring that models produce stable and coherent responses even when faced with semantically equivalent variations of a single input.

The motivation for this work stems from the critical observation that despite their impressive average accuracy, state-of-the-art VLMs often exhibit "inconsistent behavior when faced with semantically equivalent inputs." This inconsistency, as highlighted by benchmarks like MM-R3 (Chou et al., 2024), means that subtle linguistic rephrasing of a question, stylistic changes to an image, or minor contextual occlusions can lead to entirely different, yet equally plausible-sounding, model outputs. This lack of semantic invariance fundamentally undermines a VLM's robustness, interpretability, and suitability for real-world deployment, especially in sensitive or high-stakes applications where unreliable or unpredictable behavior is unacceptable.

The authors are specifically motivated to address the limitations of existing consistency-enhancing methods. Prior work typically relies on modifying model architectures or performing large-scale fine-tuning on curated datasets, which are computationally intensive, require access to original training data and model internals, and are often infeasible for proprietary models or environments with constrained computational resources. Therefore, a crucial motivation for this paper is to propose a solution that is entirely **post-hoc, model-agnostic, and applicable without supervised re-training**. The core idea is to leverage the inherent semantic information within the test input itself, performing a lightweight adaptation at inference time rather than during the costly and data-intensive training phase. This objective aims to provide a practical, plug-and-play framework that can seamlessly integrate into any VLM pipeline, making consistency a more attainable goal for deployed systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is a lightweight, test-time consistency framework designed to improve the semantic invariance of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) without the need for supervised retraining or access to original training data. The approach operates by performing a small number of gradient-based updates to the VLM's parameters (specifically, the language modeling head) at inference time, leveraging information derived solely from the single test input.

The problem setting aligns with the **MM-R3 benchmark**, where a given test input `x = (I, Q)` (image and question) is augmented into `K` semantically equivalent variants `(Ik, Qk)`. These variants are generated through three types of controlled perturbations:
1. **Question Rephrasing:** Paraphrased variants of `Q` while keeping `I` fixed.
2. **Image Restyling:** Stylized versions of `I` (e.g., Mosaic, Candy, Grayscale) with `Q` unaltered.
3. **Context Reasoning:** Variants of `I` with different occlusions applied to a specific object region, while `Q` remains fixed.

The core of the methodology lies in two complementary objectives that guide the test-time adaptation:

1. **Cross-Entropy Agreement Loss (L_CE):**
 This loss aims to align the token-level output distributions of the VLM across all `K` semantically equivalent input variants. For each variant `k`, the VLM produces a sequence of token-level logits. These logits are averaged across the decoded sequence to form `¯zk`, and then softmax is applied to obtain the normalized token distribution `pk`. The `L_CE` is then computed as the average of all pairwise symmetric cross-entropies between these `K` output distributions (`CE(pi, pj) + CE(pj, pi)` for all `i < j`). This objective encourages the model to generate responses that are distributionally consistent, even if the exact phrasing might vary slightly between variants.

2. **Pseudo-Label Consistency Loss (L_PL):**
 This loss enforces consistency at the output sequence level by aligning each variant's predicted text to a common consensus. The method first decodes the textual outputs `{y1..., yK}` from the `K` variants using greedy decoding. A string similarity function (based on normalized Levenshtein distance) is used to cluster these `K` responses. From the largest cluster, the most frequent response is selected as the "pseudo-label" (`ˆy_pseudo`). This `ˆy_pseudo` then serves as a supervision target for all `K` variants, and `L_PL` is calculated as the average cross-entropy loss between the pseudo-label tokens and the predicted token distributions for each variant (`CE(ˆy_pseudo, pk)`). This objective ensures that all perturbed inputs converge towards a dominant, self-consistent semantic answer.

**Final Objective and Inference:**
The total loss for each update step is a weighted sum: `L_total = α · L_CE + β · L_PL`. The model (specifically, the LM-head layer) is updated for a small number of gradient steps (typically 1-4) using this objective.

**Adaptive Step Selection:** To prevent over-adaptation and ensure optimal performance for each specific test instance, the method incorporates an adaptive mechanism to select the best number of update steps. After each update step, the system decodes the model's responses for the `K` variants and computes an "internal consistency score" by averaging the pairwise token-set similarities (normalized Levenshtein distance) among the `K` decoded answers and the previously generated pseudo-label. The step `t*` that yields the highest consistency score is chosen as the final output. This fully unsupervised mechanism allows for per-instance adaptation without requiring ground-truth annotations.

The framework is evaluated in two variants: "Test-time (constant T)" with a fixed number of updates (T=2) and "Test-time (adapt. T)" using the adaptive step selection. This post-hoc, model-agnostic design, requiring only access to model weights and outputs, makes it highly practical for diverse VLM architectures and deployment scenarios.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments on the MM-R3 benchmark to evaluate their test-time consistency framework across three distinct tasks: Question Rephrasing, Image Restyling, and Context Reasoning. They evaluated three widely used, state-of-the-art open-source Vision-Language Models: LLaVA 1.5M, LLaVA-Next, and Qwen2-VL. Performance was assessed using a suite of metrics: Accuracy (Acc), Similarity with Ground Truth (S_GT), Consistency Accuracy (Con), Consistency Similarity (S_C), and an Overall Score (O_all), which is the harmonic mean of correctness (Acc, S_GT) and consistency (Con, S_C) metrics.

**Overall Results (Table 1):**
The core finding is that the proposed test-time consistency framework consistently yields substantial gains in semantic consistency and overall performance across all models and tasks, with the adaptive step selection variant ("Test-time (adapt. T)") generally achieving the best results.

- **Question Rephrasing:** For LLaVA-1.5M, the O_all score improved from 52.73 (base) to 64.08, with consistency (Con) rising from 48.55 to 79.11, while also slightly increasing accuracy. Similar significant gains were observed for LLaVA-Next and Qwen2-VL, demonstrating the method's ability to enforce semantic invariance across linguistic variations without compromising accuracy.
- **Image Restyling:** This task, involving substantial visual domain shifts, saw particularly large improvements in consistency. On LLaVA-Next, consistency (Con) jumped from 55.34 to 91.85, contributing to an O_all of 46.00. Qwen2-VL achieved near-perfect consistency (99.14) and the highest O_all of 51.20, showcasing the robustness of the framework under visual perturbations.
- **Context Reasoning:** The method also improved performance in reasoning tasks under partial information. LLaVA-1.5M's O_all increased from 41.47 to 55.26, and Qwen2-VL's consistency reached an impressive 99.53. These results suggest that enforcing test-time consistency can also enhance factual grounding in ambiguous scenarios.

**Comparison with Fine-Tuning (Table 2):**
A crucial comparison was made against a fully fine-tuned LLaVA-1.5M model from the MM-R3 paper, which underwent large-scale supervised training. Despite being significantly more resource-efficient (single test point, few gradient steps, no ground-truth labels), the authors' test-time approach achieved competitive or even superior performance. On Question Rephrasing, "Test-time (adapt. T)" (O_all = 64.08) outperformed the fine-tuned model (62.02). While full fine-tuning yielded higher overall scores for Context Reasoning and Image Restyling (due to learning novel task capabilities from voluminous data), the proposed method still provided substantial improvements over the base models and demonstrated comparable or better consistency (Con). This highlights the efficiency and practicality of the test-time adaptation for consistency.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Contribution of Components (Table 3):** Both the Cross-Entropy Agreement Loss (L_CE) and Pseudo-Label Consistency Loss (L_PL) individually contribute to improvements, but their combination achieves the highest overall performance, proving their complementary roles in aligning token distributions and guiding predictions to a consensus.
- **Number of Update Steps (Figure 3):** Performance initially improves but can degrade with too many steps (over-adaptation), validating the utility of the adaptive step selection mechanism.
- **Loss Weighting Coefficients (Table 4):** The method is robust to hyperparameter variations, with optimal results typically achieved when both losses are appropriately balanced (e.g., α=0.5, β=1).
- **Preservation of Base Model Capabilities (Table 5):** Evaluations on the unperturbed OKVQA dataset showed that the test-time adaptation generally preserves or even slightly *improves* the accuracy of the base models on their original tasks, indicating that consistency gains do not come at the cost of original capabilities.

These results collectively demonstrate the effectiveness, efficiency, and broad applicability of the proposed test-time consistency framework for Vision-Language Models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) by addressing a critical yet underexplored aspect of their reliability: semantic inconsistency. The ability of VLMs to generate divergent responses to semantically equivalent inputs poses a major hurdle for their deployment in real-world applications, where trust, interpretability, and predictable behavior are paramount. By proposing a novel **test-time consistency framework**, this work offers a practical and impactful solution to this challenge.

The primary significance lies in its departure from traditional, resource-intensive training-time approaches. The framework is **post-hoc, model-agnostic, and plug-and-play**, requiring no supervised retraining, additional curated datasets, or intrusive architectural modifications. This means it can be seamlessly applied to existing pre-trained VLMs, including potentially proprietary ones (given access to model weights), making it highly practical for developers and researchers. This is particularly valuable in scenarios where access to original training data is restricted, computational resources are limited, or retraining large foundation models is simply infeasible.

The methodology's strength lies in its innovative use of two complementary objectives – Cross-Entropy Agreement Loss and Pseudo-Label Consistency Loss – which leverage the inherent information within a single test input's semantic variations. This allows the model to adapt its behavior and enforce consistency with just a few gradient steps at inference time, achieving substantial gains in consistency metrics on the MM-R3 benchmark. Furthermore, the adaptive step selection mechanism ensures optimal performance per instance, preventing over-adaptation and increasing overall robustness. The finding that this method often achieves performance competitive with, and in some cases superior to, full fine-tuning (despite being vastly more efficient) underscores its high practical utility. Crucially, the work also demonstrates that enhancing consistency does not degrade (and can even improve) the model's original capabilities on unperturbed inputs, alleviating concerns about potential trade-offs.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced VLM Reliability:** By making VLMs more robust to minor input variations, this work directly contributes to building more reliable and trustworthy AI systems. This is vital for high-stakes applications in domains like healthcare, autonomous driving, or legal assistance.
- **Broader Adoption:** The plug-and-play nature and efficiency of the method lower the barrier to deploying consistent VLMs, encouraging wider adoption in various industries.
- **New Research Direction:** The success of test-time adaptation for *consistency* opens up a novel research avenue, encouraging the community to explore similar inference-time optimization strategies for other VLM challenges beyond accuracy, such as fairness, interpretability, or safety.
- **Shift in Evaluation Paradigm:** The paper advocates for consistency to become a "core evaluation criterion" and "central design goal" in future multimodal learning systems, pushing the field beyond sole reliance on accuracy metrics.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
While impactful, the work acknowledges several limitations. Its scope is currently bounded by the MM-R3 dataset and its predefined perturbation types, which may not encompass all real-world consistency challenges. The method increases inference-time latency due to multiple forward and backward passes, though it remains significantly more efficient than full fine-tuning. Additionally, as a local adaptation method, it may not correct broader systemic biases or fundamental deficiencies of the base model. Finally, the requirement for access to model parameters means it might not be suitable for strictly frozen or entirely closed-source model environments. Future work could explore reducing inference latency, extending the framework to a broader range of real-world inconsistencies, and investigating its interplay with broader model biases.
