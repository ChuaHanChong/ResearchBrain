# Discovering Avoidable Planner Failures of Autonomous Vehicles using Counterfactual Analysis in Behaviorally Diverse Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> A planner-testing framework for autonomous vehicles that generates collision scenarios using behaviorally diverse RL traffic agents, then counterfactually classifies each collision as avoidable or not by rewinding two seconds and re-running with either re-optimized planner parameters or a CMDP-trained greedy-safe policy. On an IDM-based planner in intersections, diverse policies found 153 and 235 failures with far fewer unavoidable ones.

## Problem

- Existing driving simulators and motion-planning benchmarks contain traffic participants with narrow behavior repertoires, so test coverage of realistic safety-critical interactions stays limited.
- Injecting randomness or adversarial vehicles yields mostly unrealistic situations where no planner could respond well, leaving genuinely relevant safety-critical situations untested.
- Prior stress-testing work targets pedestrians with hard-coded responsibility-sensitive safety rules, and treats failure diversity and planner relevance as separate objectives rather than jointly.

## Method

- Large-scale scenario generation via Diverse Policy Selection over RL driving policies with lateral steering freedom, giving 50-policy diverse and less-diverse sets scored by an inter-policy trajectory-distance diversity metric.
- Planner-specific avoidability check: rewind to the human reaction-time horizon, then use Optuna parameter search over IDM attention parameters to test whether some setting resumes the simulation without collision.
- Generic avoidability check: replace the planner with a greedy-safe policy that minimizes a neural threat function learned under a constrained MDP from a random baseline, sampling 30 candidate actions per step.

## Results

- Diverse policies found 153 versus 100 failures in right-turn and 235 versus 23 in crossing scenes, roughly 1.5x and 10x more than the less-diverse baseline over 15,000 tests each.
- Unavoidable share dropped with diversity: 15 of 153 versus 28 of 100 in right-turn, 80 of 235 versus 11 of 23 in crossing; generic avoidable failures were zero for less-diverse crossing.
- Diverse policies widened collision-angle, collision-location, and t-SNE collision-state distributions, and induced avoidable collisions with the leading vehicle that less-diverse testing never produced.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Counterfactual re-simulation from a fixed reaction-time rewind, not log replay, separates defects the system under test owns from environment-intrinsic accidents worth deprioritizing.
> - Behavioral diversity in background agents raises both failure count and the fraction that are actionable, so diversity is a signal-quality lever, not merely a coverage lever.
> - Avoidability checks also filter degenerate learned-agent behavior: agents leaving their route to ram the ego vehicle surfaced automatically as unavoidable collisions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development and deployment of autonomous vehicles (AVs) necessitate rigorous verification of their planning algorithms to ensure safety and robustness across a wide array of real-world scenarios. Simulation-based testing has emerged as a critical component of this verification process due to the impracticality and risks associated with extensive physical road testing.

Existing traffic simulators, such as CARLA, SUMO, and Fluids, along with benchmarks like CommonRoad, provide platforms for evaluating AV systems. However, a significant limitation of many conventional simulation environments is their lack of diversity in the behavior of other traffic participants. This often results in limited test coverage, as planners are not exposed to the full spectrum of complex and safety-critical interactions they might encounter. While approaches involving random driving policies or adversarial agents (e.g., Tuncali et al., Koren et al.) aim to increase behavioral diversity, they can frequently generate highly unpredictable or unrealistic situations. Such scenarios might only highlight overly conservative planning behaviors or necessitate drastic evasive maneuvers, thus failing to identify defects in the planner relevant to more common, yet still challenging, safety-critical situations.

Other related work focuses on generating challenging scenarios through methods like importance sampling and cross-entropy (O’Kelly et al., Zhao et al.). These often rely on naturalistic driving data to model other vehicles, but obtaining data for rare or highly critical scenarios remains difficult. Corso et al. built upon adaptive stress testing by focusing on planner-relevant failures using Responsibility-Sensitive Safety (RSS) conditions and diversifying failure cases for pedestrian interactions.

This work addresses the identified gaps by proposing a framework that leverages recent advancements in Reinforcement Learning (RL) to generate behaviorally diverse traffic participants. Critically, it introduces a counterfactual analysis method to classify detected collisions, distinguishing between those that are "unavoidable" (inherent to the scenario) and those that are "avoidable" (attributable to the planner's decision-making and thus actionable for improvement). Unlike previous methods that might rely on predefined safety conditions like RSS or focus solely on pedestrians, this research offers a more flexible and generalizable approach for identifying diverse, relevant planner defects in multi-vehicle intersection scenarios. By doing so, it aims to provide more targeted and actionable feedback for AV planner development.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the necessity for thorough verification of planning algorithms in autonomous vehicles. Given that the planning algorithm is the core decision-making component, its defects can directly lead to safety-critical incidents, including collisions. Current simulation-based testing methods often fall short in generating a sufficiently diverse range of realistic, safety-critical scenarios and in differentiating between failures that are genuinely due to planner limitations versus those that are inherently unresolvable by any planning strategy. This limitation can lead to inefficient testing, where time and resources are spent analyzing unfixable situations, or where subtle, yet critical, planner defects remain undiscovered.

Against this background, the paper articulates several key objectives:

1. **Develop a Planner Testing Framework:** To introduce a framework capable of leveraging recent progress in simulating behaviorally diverse traffic participants to generate a wide range of dynamic scenarios.
2. **Generate, Detect, and Characterize Collisions:** To automatically create and identify dynamic scenarios that lead to collisions, and subsequently characterize these collision events.
3. **Distinguish Avoidable from Unavoidable Failures:** To propose methods that can accurately classify detected collisions into "unavoidable" failures (situations where the planner cannot escape a collision irrespective of its actions) and "avoidable" failures (situations where a different planner behavior or parameter setting could have prevented the collision).
4. **Focus on Planner-Specific Defects:** To specifically target the automatic identification of avoidable failures that are indicative of planner-specific defects, thereby providing actionable feedback for improvement before deployment.
5. **Experimental Validation:** To demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed method by conducting experiments in complex multi-agent intersection scenarios and showing its ability to discover a range of critical planner failures.

Ultimately, the motivation is to enhance the robustness and safety of autonomous vehicle planning algorithms by making simulation testing more targeted, efficient, and relevant to actionable planner improvements.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology developed in this work integrates behaviorally diverse traffic simulation with a counterfactual analysis framework to identify and categorize planner failures.

**Traffic Simulator Design:**
The core of the testing environment is a deterministic traffic simulator, based on prior work in intersection scenes. This simulator includes an ego vehicle (the planner under test) and multiple other vehicles. Vehicle movement is governed by a kinematic bicycle model, with states (position, velocity, heading) and actions (acceleration, steering) updated at discrete time intervals (Δt = 0.1s). The simulation terminates upon reaching a predefined goal state, exceeding a maximum number of steps, or entering a failure state (e.g., collision).

**Generating Behaviorally Diverse Other Vehicle Policies:**
To address the lack of diversity in traffic behavior, the authors utilized Reinforcement Learning (RL) to generate driving policies for the other vehicles. These RL agents are trained with navigation routes and reward functions that incentivize following routes and avoiding collisions. Critically, these policies enable both longitudinal (speed) and lateral (steering) control, contributing to more varied driving behaviors.
For experiments, two sets of 50 policies were generated using Diverse Policy Selection (DPS):
- **Diverse policy set:** Policies exhibiting high inter-policy diversity, meaning their behaviors significantly differ from each other, while maintaining a high success rate (≥90%).
- **LessDiverse policy set:** Policies with low inter-policy diversity, representing more homogeneous behaviors.
The inter-policy diversity metric (D_IP) quantifies the average Euclidean distance between trajectories generated by different policies on common scenarios.

**System Under Test (SUT) Model:**
The planner evaluated in this study is a variant of the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM). This IDM-based planner is augmented with an "attentional vehicle" selection mechanism to operate effectively in multi-agent intersection scenarios. It identifies a relevant leading vehicle within a dynamically sized fan-shaped attentional area (defined by attentional angle ψ, maximum φ_max, and minimum φ_min). The ego vehicle then decelerates based on this attentional vehicle's velocity component. The adjustable parameters for this planner, (d_IDM, φ_max, φ_min), are used in the planner-specific avoidability analysis.

**Avoidable Failure Detection through Counterfactual Analysis:**
The central contribution is a two-pronged counterfactual analysis approach to classify collisions detected during simulation:
1. **Rewind Mechanism:** Upon detecting a collision at step `n`, the simulation is rewound to `n - ⌈ρ/∆t⌉` steps, where `ρ` represents a minimum human reaction time (set to 2.0s). This ensures that the counterfactual analysis evaluates the situation from a point where the ego vehicle could realistically react.
2. **Rerunning Policies:** From the rewind point, the simulation is re-executed with a modified ego vehicle policy. All agent policies are rerun from this point, ensuring consistency in the counterfactual scenario.

The two types of avoidable failures are defined as:

- **Planner-specific Avoidable Failure:** A collision is classified as planner-specific avoidable if, after rewinding, there exists a modification to the original planner's adjustable parameters (θ → θ') within its defined parameter space (Θ_p) such that the ego vehicle avoids the collision.
 - **Detection Method:** The Optuna hyperparameter optimization framework is employed to search for such a parameter combination (d_IDM, φ_max, φ_min) by running 100 trials from the rewind state.

- **Generic Avoidable Failure:** A collision is classified as generic avoidable if, after rewinding, replacing the original planner with a "greedy-safe policy" (π_p_η) leads to collision avoidance. This policy aims to minimize the "threat function," which estimates the probability of future failure from a given state and action.
 - **Detection Method:** A greedy-safe policy, derived from a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) approach, is used. It learns a threat function (approximated by neural networks) and then at each step samples 30 actions, choosing the one that minimizes the estimated threat. For the baseline policy (η) to construct the threat function, a completely random action policy is used.

**Experimental Setup:**
The experiments were conducted across two types of intersection scenes governed by Japanese traffic rules: a "Right-turn" scenario (ego vehicle goes straight, interacts with a right-turning vehicle) and a "Crossing" scenario (ego vehicle intersects another vehicle from an adjacent lane). Both scenes include a leading vehicle for the ego vehicle. For each scene, 300 unique initial perturbation patterns were generated. Each pattern was tested against all 50 policies in both the Diverse and LessDiverse sets, resulting in 15,000 simulations per traffic scene and policy set.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated the framework's effectiveness in identifying and characterizing planner failures.

**Avoidable Failure Counts:**
The total number of detected failures was substantially higher when using the Diverse policy sets compared to the LessDiverse sets. Specifically, Diverse policies led to approximately 1.5 times more failures in the Right-turn scene (153 vs. 100) and about 10 times more failures in the Crossing scene (235 vs. 23).

Regarding the classification of failures:
- **Proportion of Unavoidable Failures:** A significant advantage of diverse policies was their ability to generate a lower proportion of unavoidable failures. For the LessDiverse set, about one-quarter (28/100) of Right-turn failures and nearly half (11/23) of Crossing failures were unavoidable. In contrast, for the Diverse set, only about one-tenth (15/153) of Right-turn failures and roughly one-quarter (80/235) of Crossing failures were unavoidable. This suggests that diverse policies are more effective at generating *actionable* failures.
- **Planner-Specific Avoidable Failures:** The proportion of planner-specific avoidable failures remained similar across both policy sets (approximately half of total failures), but the absolute number was higher with diverse policies due to the overall increase in detected failures. These failures provide direct feedback for adjusting planner parameters.
- **Generic Avoidable Failures:** Diverse policy sets generated considerably more generic avoidable failures, especially in the Crossing scene (53 vs. 0 for LessDiverse). These failures are valuable for indicating weaknesses within the general policy class of the planner and its kinematic capabilities, independent of specific parameter settings.

**Diversity of Detected Failures:**
The research examined three aspects of failure diversity:
- **Colliding Vehicles:** Diverse policies generated avoidable collisions with the leading vehicle (car1) in the Crossing scene, which were not detected by the LessDiverse policies. This indicates a broader coverage of interaction types.
- **Collision Angles:** The distribution of collision angles at the moment of impact was wider when using diverse policies (Figure 4), suggesting exposure to a greater variety of collision geometries.
- **Collision Positions:** Similar to collision angles, diverse policies resulted in collision locations spread across a wider area of the simulated map (Figure 5).
- **Unavoidable Collision Characteristics:** Unavoidable collisions were frequently observed early in the course. Further examination revealed these were often caused by other vehicles exhibiting aggressive, off-course behaviors, resembling adversarial testing scenarios. The avoidability check successfully classified these as unavoidable, allowing developers to prioritize other, more actionable failures.
- **Integrated State Space Analysis:** Using t-SNE to embed high-dimensional collision states (position, speed, angle, acceleration, steering) into a 2D space, the visualization (Figure 6) indicated that avoidable failures from diverse policies occupied a wider distribution, confirming the broader range of critical scenarios discovered.

**Planner Weakness Identification:**
The analysis of avoidable failures yielded a concrete example of a planner weakness. In one scenario, the IDM-based ego vehicle initially braked for a leading vehicle (car1) that suddenly stopped while turning. However, the ego vehicle subsequently re-accelerated and collided with car1 (Figure 7). This behavior was attributed to the planner's limited attentional angle (ψ = φ_min_). This was classified as a planner-specific avoidable failure, suggesting that increasing the attentional angle could prevent such collisions, while acknowledging potential trade-offs in other situations.

**Discussion on Near-miss Cases:**
Preliminary experiments using a two-dimensional Time To Collision (TTC) metric explored near-miss situations. Diverse policies generally led to a wider distribution of minimum TTCs, indicating that they generated a more varied set of near-miss scenarios (Figure 8). However, the authors noted that not all near-misses are relevant for planner testing, especially if the ego vehicle is already behaving safely. Distinguishing *relevant* near-misses from those that are less critical remains an area for future work, as the current avoidability checks are not directly applicable to near-misses.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of autonomous vehicle safety verification by offering a structured and scalable approach to identifying planner failures. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Safety and Robustness:** By enabling the systematic discovery of a wider range of relevant planner failure cases, the framework supports the development of more robust and safer autonomous vehicle planning algorithms. This is a critical step for increasing public trust and facilitating the widespread deployment of AVs.
- **Actionable Feedback for Planner Development:** The core innovation of distinguishing between avoidable and unavoidable failures is significant. Planner-specific avoidable failures provide direct, actionable feedback, indicating specific parameters or behaviors within the planner that require adjustment. Generic avoidable failures offer insights into the fundamental limitations of a planner's policy class or the ego vehicle's kinetic capabilities, guiding more fundamental design improvements. This targeted feedback streamlines the development cycle, allowing engineers to focus on correctable defects.
- **Improved Testing Efficiency:** The use of behaviorally diverse RL policies for other traffic agents allows for the generation of a greater number of safety-critical scenarios. Crucially, these diverse policies yield a higher proportion of *avoidable* failures, meaning more of the detected issues are relevant and fixable by the planner developer. This increases the efficiency of the testing process compared to methods that might generate many irrelevant or unfixable failures.
- **Broader Applicability and Scalability:** While demonstrated with an IDM-based planner in specific intersection scenarios, the framework's principles are generalizable. The authors anticipate that its benefits will increase when applied to more complex and flexible planning systems and when the simulation environment's capacity expands to include more diverse maps, traffic lights, pedestrians, and vehicle types. This scalability suggests its utility as AV technology matures and test environments become more sophisticated.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research directions, such as automatically generating diverse traffic maps and rules, and developing methodologies to detect and classify *relevant* near-miss cases. Addressing these challenges would further enhance the comprehensiveness and efficiency of AV testing.

In conclusion, this research provides a methodological advancement in autonomous vehicle verification, moving beyond simple failure detection to intelligent failure characterization. By linking detected collisions to their avoidability, it offers a pathway for developers to iteratively refine and improve planning algorithms, thereby contributing to the safety and reliability required for real-world AV deployment.
