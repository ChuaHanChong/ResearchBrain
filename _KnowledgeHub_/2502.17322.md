# TDMPBC: Self-Imitative Reinforcement Learning for Humanoid Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Westlake University developed TDMPBC, a self-imitative reinforcement learning framework, which significantly improved humanoid robot control. It achieved approximately a 120% increase in normalized return over baseline TD-MPC2 on HumanoidBench, successfully completing 8 out of 14 locomotion tasks within 2 million training steps.

## Problem

- Controlling humanoid robots with dexterous hands involves high-dimensional state and action spaces, making exploration and exploitation challenging for traditional reinforcement learning (RL) methods.
- The feasible regions for task accomplishment, such as maintaining an upright posture, are extremely narrow compared to the vast state space, leading to sparse rewards and sample inefficiency.
- Existing online RL algorithms exhibit limited effectiveness and efficiency when applied to complex humanoid robot control, often failing to leverage task-relevant states quickly.

## Method

- The ==Self-Imitative Reinforcement Learning (SIRL)== framework was proposed, which augments standard RL by enabling the algorithm to imitate its own potentially high-return trajectories generated during exploration.
- SIRL was integrated into ==TD-MPC2==, a model-based RL algorithm, creating **TDMPBC** (TD-MPC2 augmented with ==Behavior Cloning==) by modifying only the policy training loss function.
- A ==dynamic behavior cloning== term was introduced, whose weight is exponentially adjusted based on the cumulative return of the trajectory, thereby emphasizing learning from past successful experiences.

## Results

- TDMPBC achieved approximately a **120%** increase in normalized return compared to baseline TD-MPC2 on HumanoidBench, with a faster convergence rate and only a **5%** increase in computational overhead.
- The algorithm successfully completed **8 out of 14** locomotion tasks within **2 million** training steps, demonstrating significant improvements in learning foundational movements like walking, running, and balancing.
- While showing improvements in some whole-body manipulation tasks, TDMPBC still struggled with fine-grained dexterous hand control, often finding alternative, less precise strategies to achieve rewards.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The cumulative return of a trajectory serves as a strong indicator of whether the robot has entered a task-relevant region, such as maintaining an upright posture.
> - Prioritizing and imitating one's own high-return trajectories can accelerate the discovery and stabilization of fundamental skills, acting as a form of self-guided exploration.
> - In complex, high-dimensional spaces like those of humanoid robots, converging to a beneficial local optimum (e.g., maintaining balance) is a crucial initial step for broader task completion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The control of humanoid robots, particularly those equipped with dexterous hands, represents a significant challenge within the field of reinforcement learning (RL). These systems operate in high-dimensional state and action spaces with many degrees of freedom, making it difficult for traditional RL algorithms to effectively balance exploration and exploitation under limited sample budgets. The feasible regions for task accomplishment in such environments are typically narrow; for instance, maintaining an upright posture in a humanoid robot constitutes a small fraction of the total state space, while falling occupies the majority.

Existing online RL methods, including both model-free algorithms like Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) and state-of-the-art model-based algorithms such as DreamerV3 and TD-MPC2, have shown success in domains like gaming AI and quadrupedal robot control. However, these methods often exhibit limited effectiveness and efficiency when applied to the complexities of humanoid robots with dexterous hands, as highlighted by their performance on benchmarks like HumanoidBench. The sample inefficiency of RL, coupled with the difficulty of exploration in high-dimensional, sparse-reward environments, necessitates approaches that can accelerate learning, especially in identifying and leveraging task-relevant states.

This work addresses this gap by proposing a framework that aims to accelerate online learning in these challenging environments. It draws inspiration from imitation learning (IL) but adapts it to a self-imitative paradigm, where the algorithm learns from its own successful explorations rather than relying on external expert demonstrations. This contrasts with traditional IL, as well as offline RL approaches like TD3+BC, which primarily focus on leveraging pre-collected static datasets and addressing out-of-distribution issues. The proposed method also distinguishes itself from other exploration enhancement techniques for humanoid robots, such as those relying on massive parallelization or direct imitation of human behavior, by attempting to learn from scratch without extensive external data or computational resources.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to accelerate the online learning process for humanoid robots operating in complex, high-dimensional spaces. The authors seek to develop an RL framework that can more effectively and efficiently enable humanoid robots to acquire challenging locomotion and manipulation skills, especially given the typically narrow "feasible regions" for task completion.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations regarding humanoid robot control:

- **Narrow Feasible Regions:** In high-dimensional spaces like those of humanoid robots, the states conducive to task accomplishment are rare. For humanoid motion control, maintaining an "upright posture" is a fundamental prerequisite for almost any downstream task (e.g., walking, running, manipulation). However, the vast majority of possible states correspond to the robot falling, with only a minuscule fraction representing a stable, upright stance.
- **Importance of Upright Posture:** The ability to maintain an upright posture is not only intuitive but also reflected in reward function design, where terms related to uprightness often carry high weights or act as multipliers for other task rewards. This highlights its critical role as a foundational skill.
- **Return as an Indicator of Task Relevance:** The authors observe that the cumulative return of a trajectory serves as a strong indicator of whether the robot has entered a task-completing region, particularly concerning the upright posture. If a robot falls, subsequent rewards become unattainable, leading to a significantly lower total return. Conversely, trajectories where the robot maintains an upright posture tend to accumulate higher returns over time, even with minor differences in uprightness at individual timesteps. This cumulative effect makes return an approximate signal for identifying "potentially task-relevant trajectories."
- **Balancing Exploration and Exploitation:** Traditional online RL algorithms struggle to wisely balance exploration and exploitation in these difficult scenarios, often failing to efficiently discover and leverage the narrow feasible regions.

Based on these observations, the authors hypothesize that if an RL algorithm explores a state that leads to a potentially task-relevant region (e.g., an upright posture), it should place greater emphasis on the data within that region. This insight motivates the development of a framework that guides the robot to imitate its own high-return trajectories, thereby accelerating the learning of fundamental skills like balance and uprightness, which in turn facilitates the completion of more complex downstream tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is the **Self-Imitative Reinforcement Learning (SIRL)** framework. SIRL aims to accelerate online learning by augmenting standard RL algorithms with a mechanism to imitate potentially task-relevant trajectories generated during its own exploration. This approach is termed "self-imitative" because the trajectories are derived from the algorithm's self-generated experience rather than external expert demonstrations.

The authors implemented the SIRL framework by extending **TD-MPC2** (Temporal Difference Model Predictive Control 2), a model-based RL algorithm known for its sample efficiency. The resulting algorithm is referred to as **TDMPBC** (TD-MPC2 augmented with Behavior Cloning).

**TD-MPC2 Preliminaries:**
TD-MPC2 operates by learning a latent decoder-free world model composed of five key components:
- **State Encoder ($h_\phi$):** Maps raw states $s_t$ to latent representations $z_t$.
- **Latent Dynamics ($d_\phi$):** Predicts the next latent state $z_{t+1}$ given current latent state $z_t$ and action $a_t$.
- **Reward Function ($R_\phi$):** Predicts the reward $\hat{r}_t$ based on $z_t$ and $a_t$.
- **Value Function ($Q_\phi$):** Estimates the expected discounted return $\hat{q}_t$ from $z_t$ and $a_t$.
- **Policy Prior ($\pi_\theta$):** A stochastic maximum entropy policy that suggests actions $\hat{a}_t$ given $z_t$.

The world model components ($h_\phi, d_\phi, R_\phi, Q_\phi$) are trained by minimizing an objective function that combines losses for dynamics prediction, reward prediction, and value estimation. The policy prior $\pi_\theta$ is trained to maximize a separate objective, which includes the predicted Q-value and an entropy regularization term. During inference, TD-MPC2 uses a sampling-based planner called Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) to select actions by maximizing an estimated return $\hat{R}$ over a planning horizon, with some trajectories guided by the learned policy prior.

**SIRL Integration (TDMPBC):**
The SIRL framework is integrated into TD-MPC2 by modifying only the policy training loss function. An additional behavior cloning (BC) term is introduced into the policy objective.

The modified policy loss function $L_\pi(\theta)$ is given by:
$L_\pi(\theta) = E_{(s_t,a_t,R_t)_{t=0}^H \sim B} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^H \lambda_t \left( \underbrace{\omega(R_t) \cdot \log \pi_\theta(a_t|z_t)}_{\text{Self-Imitative}} + \underbrace{Q_\phi(z_t, \pi_\theta(\cdot|z_t)) - \alpha H(\pi(\cdot|z_t))}_{\text{Reinforcement Learning}} \right) \right]$

Here, $R_t$ represents the total return of the entire trajectory from which $(s_t, a_t)$ is sampled. All state-action pairs within the same trajectory share the same return $R_t$. The novel component is the **behavior cloning term** $\omega(R_t) \cdot \log \pi_\theta(a_t|z_t)$, which encourages the policy to imitate actions $a_t$ that led to high-return trajectories.

The weight of this behavior cloning term, $\omega(R_t)$, is dynamically adjusted based on the trajectory return $R_t$ using an exponential function:
$\omega(R_t) = \beta \cdot \exp \left( \frac{R_t - G}{G} \right)$
where:
- $\beta$ is a hyperparameter that balances the behavior cloning loss with the standard RL loss.
- $G$ is a **reference return value** used to determine the relative "level" of the current trajectory return $R_t$. The authors explore two choices for $G$:
 1. $G = R_{target}$: The target return for the task, which requires prior information.
 2. $G = R_{max}$: The maximum return observed in the current replay buffer. The authors opted for this choice to avoid introducing prior information and because it dynamically adapts as the policy improves.

**Discussion:**
The introduction of this dynamic behavior cloning term aims to prioritize learning from successful past experiences. When the algorithm accidentally explores a high-return region (e.g., maintaining an upright posture), the exponential weighting amplifies the BC loss for trajectories within that region, encouraging the policy to reproduce those behaviors. This "self-imitative" process is intended to accelerate the discovery and stabilization of fundamental task-relevant skills. The authors acknowledge that, like traditional imitation learning, this approach can introduce conservatism and a risk of converging to local optima. However, they argue that in complex high-dimensional spaces, converging to a beneficial local optimum like maintaining an upright posture is often a desirable first step for humanoid control.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluated TDMPBC on HumanoidBench, a benchmark featuring the Unitree H1 robot with dexterous hands across 31 tasks (14 locomotion, 17 whole-body manipulation). Experiments were conducted with 2 million (2M) interaction steps for TDMPBC and TD-MPC2, and 10M steps for SAC and DreamerV3, reflecting the sample efficiency claims of TD-MPC2.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparison

- **Overall Performance:** TDMPBC demonstrated a significant performance improvement, achieving approximately a 120% increase in normalized return compared to baseline TD-MPC2, with only a 5% increase in computational overhead. It also showed a faster convergence rate and better sample efficiency.
- **Locomotion Tasks:** Out of 14 locomotion tasks, TDMPBC successfully completed (surpassed the target return) 8 tasks within 2M training steps (e.g., walk, stand, run, hurdle, sit simple, sit hard, slide, pole). In contrast, TD-MPC2 only completed 1 task (crawl), and other baselines showed limited success. TDMPBC exhibited significantly faster convergence and higher final performance across most locomotion tasks, with exceptions in 'reach' and 'crawl' where TD-MPC2 or DreamerV3 performed comparably or slightly better.
- **Whole-Body Manipulation Tasks:** While TDMPBC showed improvements over baselines in several manipulation tasks (e.g., truck, bookshelf simple/hard, window, spoon, powerlift, room, insert small/normal), its final performance often remained significantly below the target return. The authors noted that the algorithm still struggled with simultaneously controlling the body and performing dexterous hand grasping. Prematurely converging curves in these tasks implied that the robot might master one aspect (e.g., body posture) while failing at another (e.g., hand manipulation).

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

- **Hyperparameter $\beta$:** Experiments with $\beta = 0.5, 1.0, 2.0$ on 'run', 'hurdle', and 'maze' tasks indicated that TDMPBC's performance is robust to variations of $\beta$ around 1.0.
- **Reference Return Value $G$:** Comparing $G = R_{max}$ (maximum return in replay buffer) and $G = R_{target}$ (task-specific target return) on 'reach', 'hurdle', and 'maze' tasks revealed no significant differences in overall performance. The authors preferred $G = R_{max}$ to avoid introducing prior information, noting that $R_{max}$ could eventually surpass $R_{target}$ in tasks where the target is not a strict upper bound.

#### 4.3. Runtime

TDMPBC introduced a marginal increase in computational burden, primarily for the policy loss calculations and storing maximum return. Measurements on a Tesla V100 GPU across 'walk', 'reach', and 'hurdle' tasks showed an average runtime increase of 4.72% compared to TD-MPC2.

#### 4.4. Training Phenomenon

The authors observed a consistent phenomenon where the policy's evaluated return during training generally lagged behind the maximum return stored in the replay buffer. They suggest that the policy continuously "chases" this maximum return, and the self-imitative behavior cloning in SIRL appears to accelerate this process.

#### 4.5. Behavior Visualization

- **Pole Task:** TD-MPC2-trained robots often collided with poles and fell. TDMPBC-trained robots, however, adopted a strategy of quickly moving towards and hugging a side wall to navigate through the dense pole forest, resembling human-like avoidance behavior. This strategy contributed to rapid convergence.
- **Balance-Hard Task:** The TDMPBC-trained robot demonstrated the ability to recover balance from an unstable initial state by swinging its arms. It maintained balance on a plank with a movable spherical pivot.
- **Window Task:** While TDMPBC achieved a high return, the robot did not perform the task as expected. Instead of precisely controlling the cleaning tool with its dexterous hands, it leveraged its arms to press the tool against the surface, indicating a potentially sub-optimal but effective strategy found by the algorithm.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces the Self-Imitative Reinforcement Learning (SIRL) framework, offering a method to accelerate online learning for humanoid robots in complex, high-dimensional environments. The proposed TDMPBC algorithm, by integrating a dynamic behavior cloning term into the policy objective of TD-MPC2, demonstrates a tangible improvement in learning efficiency and performance.

The work's significance lies in several aspects:
- **Enhanced Sample Efficiency:** By having the algorithm learn from its own high-return experiences, TDMPBC enables more rapid acquisition of fundamental skills, such as maintaining an upright posture, which is crucial for humanoid robots. This addresses a core challenge of sample inefficiency in high-dimensional RL.
- **Improved Locomotion Capabilities:** The significant performance gains, particularly in locomotion tasks on HumanoidBench (solving 8 out of 14 tasks within 2M steps), indicate a substantial step forward in developing more robust and agile humanoid robot controllers.
- **Generalizability of Self-Imitation:** The concept of self-imitation, where the algorithm learns from its own successful explorations without requiring external expert data, offers a promising direction for online RL in environments where expert demonstrations are unavailable or difficult to obtain.
- **Practical Applicability:** The modest computational overhead (less than 5%) for a substantial performance gain (over 120%) suggests that the approach could be practical for deployment, especially as computational resources improve.

Despite its success in locomotion, the paper identifies limitations in whole-body manipulation tasks where the robot still struggles with dexterous hand control. This suggests that while SIRL effectively guides the robot towards fundamental stable behaviors, integrating fine-grained, multi-articulated control remains an open challenge.

Future work outlined by the authors includes transitioning TDMPBC from simulated environments to real-world deployment, which would further validate its robustness and practical utility. The framework's ability to quickly establish foundational skills could potentially reduce the time and resources required for real-robot training, paving the way for more capable and autonomously learning humanoid robots in various application scenarios.
