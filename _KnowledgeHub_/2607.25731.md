# Tri-Manual Visuomotor Imitation Learning of Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> TriManPolicy is a system that overcomes the challenge of demonstrating concurrent tri-manual robot tasks with a single, two-handed operator by implementing Dependency-Aware Tri-Arm Scheduling (DATS). DATS processes sequentially collected human demonstrations by intelligently re-timing them to extract task parallelism, resulting in robot policies that achieve up to 49.9% faster task completion times.

## Problem

- Human operators can typically only control two robot arms simultaneously, creating a mismatch for demonstrating tasks to tri-manual robots with three manipulation channels.
- Single-operator demonstration collection often involves "pairwise mode switching," which introduces artificial serialization and delays in recorded actions.
- Standard behavior cloning on these raw, sequentially recorded demonstrations leads to inefficient robot policies that replicate non-essential delays during deployment.

## Method

- The system employs **Dependency-Aware Tri-Arm Scheduling (DATS)** to re-parameterize demonstration timelines, separating demonstrated motion from collection timing by formulating it as a ==constrained scheduling problem==.
- An offline annotation process, guided by ==Vision-Language Models== and human review, constructs a ==subtask graph== for each episode, specifying required arms and task-mandated predecessor relationships.
- A synchronous three-arm visuomotor policy, based on an ==action-chunked transformer==, is trained on these DATS-retimed demonstrations, which present parallelized supervision to the policy.

## Results

- DATS-trained policies achieved an average **42.5%** reduction in macro-mean task completion time across six real-world tri-manual tasks (ranging from **31.3%** to **49.9%** faster), without compromising success rates.
- The DATS approach substantially increased "co-window coverage," quantifying how often compatible independent subtasks appeared together in training windows, from a macro-mean of **4.2%** (Raw) to **67.1%** (DATS).
- Robot rollouts demonstrated improved coordination, with DATS-trained policies initiating concurrent actions (e.g., hanger retrieval with towel folding) that were previously serialized in raw demonstrations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The temporal composition of demonstration data, specifically the co-occurrence of independent subtasks within training windows, significantly impacts the efficiency and coordination learned by multi-arm robot policies.
> - Offline re-timing of demonstrations using a dependency-aware scheduling approach can effectively decouple interface-imposed sequential timing from task-essential dependencies, enabling policies to learn true task parallelism.
> - By preserving local sensorimotor segments while optimizing their global temporal composition, it is possible to extract inherent concurrency from sequentially collected human demonstrations for more efficient robot behavior.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of visuomotor imitation learning, specifically addressing challenges in collecting demonstrations for complex multi-arm robot systems. Traditional visuomotor imitation learning often relies on direct teleoperation, where human manipulation is recorded as robot observation-action trajectories. This approach is well-established for bimanual systems, where a human operator’s two hands directly correspond to two robot end-effectors, establishing a direct mapping of simultaneous control channels.

The work extends this paradigm to "tri-manual" robot systems, which possess three manipulation channels. This introduces a fundamental mismatch: while a tri-manual robot can coordinate three arms concurrently, a single human operator can typically only continuously control two hands simultaneously. Current methods for demonstrating tri-manual tasks with one operator often involve "pairwise mode switching," where the operator controls different pairs of arms sequentially. This process can inadvertently serialize motions that could, from a task perspective, occur concurrently. Consequently, standard behavior cloning, which learns directly from these recorded trajectories, risks reproducing these interface-imposed delays and sequential executions as part of the learned policy, leading to inefficient robot behavior.

This paper distinguishes itself from related work in several ways. While many visuomotor imitation learning methods, including those using action-chunked transformers and diffusion policies, typically use the recorded global timeline as the supervision target, this work actively *retimes* the demonstration. It differs from other multi-arm systems like MART, which learns coordination from simultaneous multi-user teleoperation, by focusing on a single-operator, pairwise continuous control setup. Furthermore, while task graphs and constraint scheduling are common in multi-robot planning, this research applies scheduling specifically to *construct behavior-cloning supervision* from recorded sensorimotor segments, rather than for online task and motion planning. It also contrasts with contemporaneous approaches that assign assistive arms online (HATS), use staged curricula (SAI), synthesize bimanual trajectories from human video (DexImit), learn temporal constraints (Dreher et al.), or alter deployment-time action aggregation (Proleptic Temporal Ensemble), by directly modifying the temporal composition of training supervision through the rescheduling of executed robot segments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the disparity between the advanced manipulation capabilities of tri-manual robots and the limitations of single-operator demonstration interfaces. Many manipulation tasks benefit from a third end-effector, which can stabilize, support, or present an object, thereby avoiding the need for an arm to alternate between maintaining support and performing primary manipulation, a common issue in bimanual systems. This distribution of complementary roles across three arms can improve efficiency by eliminating additional regrasping, waiting, or sequential execution.

However, as highlighted, a single operator's inability to continuously control three arms simultaneously necessitates strategies like pairwise mode switching during demonstration collection. This mode switching introduces an artificial serialization of actions. For instance, an arm might appear to wait in a demonstration not because the task inherently requires a delay, but because the operator is currently controlling another pair of arms. If a robot policy learns from such raw demonstrations, it may replicate these non-essential delays, resulting in suboptimal and inefficient performance in deployment.

Against this background, the key objectives of the TriManPolicy system are:

- **To extend teleoperated imitation learning to a setting where the robot possesses more simultaneous manipulation channels than a single operator can demonstrate.** Specifically, the work formulates this problem for three cooperating arms, recognizing that the challenge is inherent to the interface rather than the robot's capabilities.
- **To introduce Dependency-Aware Tri-Arm Scheduling (DATS) as a core component.** DATS aims to separate demonstrated motion from collection timing. It re-frames demonstration retiming as a constrained scheduling problem. The objective is to enable independent arm motions to overlap where permitted by task logic, while rigorously preserving necessary task orderings and constraints on arm usage that are reviewed by a human expert.
- **To train synchronous policies that jointly control all three arms based on these retimed demonstrations.** The system seeks to evaluate whether training on such transformed data leads to more efficient coordination in real-world tri-manual tasks, without requiring the explicit dependency graph or scheduler during robot deployment. The overarching goal is to enable a single operator to provide demonstrations that result in a robot policy capable of executing tasks with the full concurrency offered by a tri-manual embodiment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TriManPolicy system integrates three main stages: mode-switched demonstration collection, offline Dependency-Aware Tri-Arm Scheduling (DATS) for supervision construction, and synchronous three-arm visuomotor policy training.

#### 3.1. A. System Overview and Problem Formulation

The process begins with segmenting each recorded demonstration episode into local behavioral units. An annotation pass then assigns the specific arms required for each segment and defines predecessor relationships. DATS subsequently determines new start times for these fixed-duration segments. A human-reviewed graph serves to distinguish task-mandated orderings from those that can be relaxed by the scheduler. Predecessor edges encode finish-to-start relationships, task prerequisites, and specified shared-workspace orderings. Required-arm sets specify resources that preclude concurrent usage. The scheduling objective is to minimize the overall episode makespan while satisfying these constraints. DATS functions as a data transformation step, reorganizing recorded samples during dataset construction, rather than influencing the learned policy's direct actions during deployment. The core idea is that while a raw demonstration might contain correct local motions, their global timing could be distorted by the interface. DATS replaces this interface-imposed timing with one derived from task relations and available arm resources, preserving required holds and static support when necessary.

#### 3.2. B. Mode-Switched Demonstration Collection

Demonstrations are collected by a single operator using a mode-switched interface. This interface allows the operator to continuously control two robot arms from the set {L, R, O} (Left, Right, Other/Auxiliary) at any given time. When an arm is not actively controlled, it remains under a "hold control" state. All relevant data—robot states, gripper states, teleoperation modes, observations, and executed commands—are logged with a unified control clock. An action `a_n,t` concatenates commands for all three arms. The mode trace `m_n,t` records the active teleoperation pair for data inspection, although DATS primarily operates on annotated segments and the checked graph.

#### 3.3. C. Subtask Annotation and Graph Construction

For each raw demonstration episode, an offline annotation procedure constructs a subtask graph `G_n = (V_n, E_n)`. A Vision-Language Model (VLM) initially proposes segment boundaries, required arms, semantic labels, and predecessor IDs. A human author then reviews and edits these proposals, ensuring the accuracy of the episode-level task specification. The scheduler's guarantees for precedence and resource feasibility are relative to this reviewed graph. Only interval boundaries, required-arm sets (`A_j`), and checked predecessor IDs (`P_j`) directly influence the schedule. Predecessor relations are strictly finish-to-start. Each segment `v_j` is defined by its required arms, raw start and end times, predecessor set, and an optional semantic label. Segments are episode-specific temporal units whose durations are fixed by the raw recording. The graph separates precedence relations (task-driven order) from resource occupancy (shared arm preventing overlap). A set `C_n` is identified, comprising raw-ordered, non-overlapping segment pairs that lack a dependency path and use disjoint arms; these are candidates for temporal relaxation by the scheduler.

#### 3.4. D. Dependency-Aware Tri-Arm Scheduling (DATS)

DATS formulates the temporal reparameterization as a fixed-duration interval scheduling problem across the three robot arms. Time is discretized into ticks of 0.01 seconds. Each subtask's raw interval is converted to tick indices, its duration `d_j` is preserved, and its scheduled placement is represented by `(s_j, e_j)`. The scheduler ensures two types of constraints are met:
1. **Predecessor Relations:** For any segment `v_j` and its predecessor `v_p`, the end time of `v_p` must be less than or equal to the start time of `v_j` (`e_p ≤ s_j`).
2. **Resource Exclusivity:** For each arm `α`, no two subtasks requiring `α` can overlap in their scheduled intervals (`NoOverlap(Q_α)`). Multi-arm subtasks occupy all required arms for their full scheduled duration.
The objective function for scheduling is to minimize the episode's makespan (`C_max`), subject to these constraints. This mixed-integer linear programming problem is solved using the CP-SAT solver from Google OR-Tools. Makespan minimization is designed to introduce overlap where the graph and resource constraints allow it.

#### 3.5. E. Constructing Retimed Demonstrations

Following scheduling, for each raw message timestamp `q` within a segment, DATS applies a forward time map `ϕ_n,j(q)` to shift the messages by a calculated offset. All messages for a participating arm are shifted by the same offset, which is also shared by multi-arm segments. The retimed streams are then resampled onto the dataset clock. Images and proprioception use the nearest timestamped sample, while commands use the latest sample at or before each output time. Initial values are used before an arm's first interval, and the most recent values are held between intervals and after the last one. DATS does not introduce interpolation, inverse kinematics, or new motions at segment boundaries; it preserves the local sensorimotor timing within each segment. The resulting observations can synthesize arm-centric streams that were recorded at different raw times, assuming their local meaning remains intact when composed.

#### 3.6. F. Policy Learning and Deployment

For both raw and DATS-retimed datasets, an action-chunked transformer policy is employed. Given an observation `o_t`, the policy predicts a horizon-`H` sequence of synchronized three-arm actions. The training objective minimizes a loss function over valid observation-action windows constructed from the demonstration set. While the loss function, network architecture, and sampling rules are identical for both conditions, DATS produces different horizon-`H` windows by placing compatible cross-arm segment targets within the same window. This modifies the joint supervision presented to the synchronous policy. During deployment, the policy operates directly from live observations without requiring any timing annotations or the explicit dependency graph.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TriManPolicy involved six real-world tri-manual tasks, utilizing 237 processed demonstrations and conducting 300 real-robot trials (25 trials per task and condition). The experiments employed three AgileX Piper-X arms, with policies receiving arm-centric RGB and proprioceptive observations and sending synchronous joint and gripper commands at 50 Hz.

#### 4.1. A. System-Level Real-Robot Outcomes

The core performance metric was task completion time among successful trials. DATS-trained policies consistently exhibited lower mean completion times across all six tasks, with reductions ranging from 31.3% to 49.9% compared to the baseline policies trained on raw demonstrations. The unweighted macro-mean completion time decreased from 83.1 seconds to 47.8 seconds, representing a 42.5% reduction.
Regarding task success rates, DATS recorded 129 successes out of 150 trials, slightly surpassing the baseline's 126 successes. DATS-trained policies achieved equal or higher success counts at the individual task level, indicating that the observed time reductions were not achieved by compromising task reliability.

#### 4.2. B. Supervision Duration and Target Composition (Offline Audit)

To understand the source of performance improvements, an offline analysis investigated changes in supervision duration and the composition of target windows. A "Gap" condition was introduced as a diagnostic, representing raw demonstrations with maximal idle periods removed, but maintaining the recorded order of annotated segments.

- **Supervision Duration:** DATS achieved further episode compression beyond simple idle removal (Gap) for TOWELHANG, BINTOWEL, BAGTAPE, and LIDERASER. For TOTECARDS and TRAYWIPE, the DATS timeline duration was similar to or slightly longer than the Gap timeline. However, even in these cases, DATS enabled earlier initiation of critical subtasks. For instance, in TOTECARDS, card transfer began 29.3±5.4 seconds earlier, despite a slight overall increase in timeline duration. Similarly, in TRAYWIPE, towel preparation and wiping advanced by 22.5±2.3 seconds and 15.1±1.4 seconds, respectively, with negligible change in total duration. This demonstrates that DATS's impact extends beyond merely shortening episode length.

- **Co-Window Coverage:** The study introduced "co-window coverage" (`ρ_X_n`) to quantify the extent to which eligible pairs of independent, disjoint-arm segments (that could logically overlap) appeared within common horizon-H target windows during training. DATS substantially increased co-window coverage across all tasks. The task macro-mean coverage rose from 4.2% for Raw demonstrations and 11.5% for Gap demonstrations to 67.1% under DATS. This increase was observed in all 237 processed episodes. Notably, in TOTECARDS and TRAYWIPE, DATS significantly altered target-window composition (achieving 94.0±16.4% and 71.2±10.6% coverage, respectively) even when the overall supervision duration changed minimally. This finding indicates that DATS changes the specific joint supervision presented to the policy, allowing compatible cross-arm targets to co-occur within the same training window.

#### 4.3. C. Coordination in Selected Rollouts

Visual inspection of successful robot rollouts confirmed the behavioral effects of DATS. For example, in TOWELHANG, the DATS-trained policy initiated hanger retrieval concurrently with towel folding and lifting. In TRAYWIPE, towel pickup began while the tray was being stabilized. For LIDERASER, both erasers were acquired during lid positioning. These examples illustrate that the learned policies maintained required support or task geometry with one arm while other arms concurrently advanced the task, directly mirroring the altered temporal composition of the DATS supervision.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents TriManPolicy, a system that addresses a critical limitation in imitation learning for multi-arm robots: the mismatch between a robot's concurrent manipulation capabilities and the sequential nature of single-operator demonstration interfaces. The introduction of Dependency-Aware Tri-Arm Scheduling (DATS) is a significant contribution, providing a method to systematically decouple interface-induced timing constraints from task-essential motion sequences.

The primary significance of this work lies in its demonstration that behavior cloning can be made substantially more efficient by intelligently re-timing demonstration data. By preserving local sensorimotor segments while re-optimizing their global temporal composition based on task dependencies and resource constraints, DATS enables the training of robot policies that achieve significantly faster task completion (an average of 42.5% reduction in macro-mean completion time) without sacrificing task success rates. This directly translates to increased operational efficiency for multi-arm robotic systems.

The approach has potential broader applicability beyond tri-manual systems, as the core principle of DATS—casting demonstration retiming as a constrained scheduling problem over finite resources—could be extended to other scenarios where a demonstration interface imposes serialization that is not required by the task or where a shared resource (e.g., tool, workspace area) needs to be managed for concurrent operations.

Furthermore, this work enhances the practicality of imitation learning for complex multi-arm robots. It facilitates single-operator data collection, reducing the need for elaborate multi-user teleoperation setups that are often costly and challenging to implement. By extracting the true task parallelism from sequentially collected data, TriManPolicy contributes to making robot learning from demonstration more scalable and effective for advanced manipulation tasks. The finding that supervision timing and target window composition directly impact learned coordination behavior offers a new avenue for improving imitation learning robustness and efficiency.
