# Learning Legged MPC with Smooth Neural Surrogates

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for learned Model Predictive Control (MPC) for legged robots is presented, leveraging Smooth Neural Surrogates (SNS-MLPs) for dynamics modeling and heavy-tailed Cauchy likelihoods for training. This system enables stable gradient-based MPC, demonstrating robust zero-shot generalization on real hardware for various behaviors and terrains, and achieving up to 2x lower cumulative control cost over sampling-based alternatives.

## Problem

- Neural network dynamics for legged robots often inherit stiff, nonsmooth contact transitions, hindering gradient-based optimization.
- Standard neural networks can introduce additional spurious local nonsmoothness and non-physical local optima, destabilizing planning.
- Training data from stiff dynamics results in heavy-tailed, non-Gaussian model errors, which are poorly handled by traditional Gaussian-assuming loss functions like MSE.

## Method

- Introduced Smooth Neural Surrogates (SNS-MLP) with Lipschitz-based weight normalization and exponential parameterization to ensure tunable first- and second-order smoothness in learned dynamics.
- Employed heavy-tailed Maximum Likelihood Estimation, specifically the Cauchy likelihood, for training neural networks to robustly handle non-Gaussian, impulse-like prediction errors.
- Integrated the learned SNS dynamics and a predictor-corrector state estimator into a generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN) ==Model Predictive Control== framework for online planning.

## Results

- Models trained with SNS-MLPs and Cauchy likelihoods achieved 2-**50x** lower cumulative control cost and 5/5 success rates across diverse locomotion tasks, significantly outperforming standard MLPs.
- A single, offline-trained dynamics model and state estimator demonstrated robust zero-shot generalization on a real Unitree Go2 quadruped robot across unseen terrains and tasks.
- Gradient-based GGN-MPC with SNS dynamics proved computationally efficient, achieving **2x** lower cumulative cost at comparable solve times and ~**10x** lower cost with increased decision variables than sampling-based methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The quality of derivatives in learned dynamics models, rather than just zeroth-order prediction accuracy (MAE), is paramount for effective and stable control performance.
> - Explicitly enforcing neural network smoothness and utilizing robust heavy-tailed likelihoods during training are critical for enabling stable and reliable gradient-based MPC with contact-rich dynamics.
> - Gradient-based MPC, when combined with smooth neural surrogates, offers superior computational efficiency, scalability, and reduced hyperparameter sensitivity compared to sampling-based MPC for complex legged robot tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research operates at the confluence of several active and critical areas in robotics and artificial intelligence: **legged robotics**, **model predictive control (MPC)**, and **deep learning (specifically learned dynamics models)**. Each of these fields has seen significant independent success, but their effective integration remains a challenging frontier.

**Legged Robotics**: This field is driven by the desire to create agile and versatile robots capable of navigating complex, unstructured environments that are often inaccessible to wheeled or tracked robots. Achieving robust and dynamic locomotion, especially over varied terrains and during physical interaction (contact), is a central challenge.

**Model Predictive Control (MPC)**: MPC is a powerful control paradigm that enables online behavior shaping by optimizing control sequences over a receding horizon. Its strengths lie in its ability to handle constraints, adapt to changing task objectives, and react to disturbances, making it ideal for dynamic systems like legged robots. However, traditional MPC for legged robots often relies on simplified, scenario-specific analytic models, which limit their generalization and scalability. Furthermore, the hybrid nature of contact dynamics (smooth phases punctuated by abrupt, non-differentiable transitions) poses significant numerical challenges for gradient-based MPC.

**Deep Learning for Dynamics Models**: Deep learning has revolutionized many areas, including reinforcement learning (RL), which has enabled impressive blind locomotion capabilities. Learned dynamics models, often based on neural networks, offer the promise of greater representational flexibility and scalability compared to hand-designed analytic models. This gives rise to **learned MPC**, where neural networks model the robot's dynamics, and MPC then uses these models for planning. This "middle ground" seeks to combine the generalization of learned models with the online adaptability of MPC.

**Challenges in Learned MPC for Legged Robots**: The paper identifies key difficulties in this integration:
1. **Stiff contact dynamics**: Neural networks tend to inherit the abrupt, stiff transitions of ground-truth contact, making optimization difficult.
2. **Spurious nonsmoothness**: Neural networks can introduce non-physical local nonsmoothness and local optima not present in the real physics.
3. **Non-Gaussian errors**: Training data from stiff dynamics often lead to rapid state changes, resulting in heavy-tailed, non-Gaussian model errors that violate common Gaussian noise assumptions in loss functions (e.g., MSE).

Previous attempts to address these issues include:
- **Sampling-based MPC**: These methods (e.g., DIAL-MPC, MPPI) avoid gradients by sampling actions, offering robustness to nonsmoothness but suffering from the curse of dimensionality, high computational cost, and sensitivity to learned dynamics quality.
- **Differentiable simulation**: Approaches that smooth contact forces or relax constraints in analytic simulators to enable gradient-based methods, but these are often scenario-specific.
- **Structured neural dynamics**: Incorporating physics-informed priors or reduced-order models, but these might sacrifice the full flexibility of neural networks.
- **Lipschitz-constrained networks**: Techniques to bound network smoothness, mainly explored in benign, low-dimensional settings, with limited application to stiff legged robot dynamics.
- **Robust optimization**: Using heavy-tailed likelihoods (e.g., Cauchy, Huber) to handle impulsive errors, a concept recognized in broader ML but often overlooked in learned dynamics for contact-rich systems, where Gaussian assumptions remain common.

This work specifically tackles the numerical pathologies that arise when combining neural networks with gradient-based optimization in the context of stiff contact dynamics. By introducing the "smooth neural surrogate" (SNS) and leveraging "heavy-tailed likelihoods," the authors directly address the issues of nonsmoothness and inappropriate error modeling that limit the performance and reliability of learned MPC for legged robots. It aims to bridge the gap between highly flexible, learned models and the efficiency and guarantees (when derivatives are well-behaved) of gradient-based optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a reliable, scalable, and generalizable framework for Model Predictive Control (MPC) of legged robots by effectively integrating deep learning with online planning. The authors aim to overcome critical challenges that arise when learned neural network dynamics interact with gradient-based trajectory optimization, especially in the presence of stiff contact dynamics.

The motivation is explicitly driven by three key difficulties identified in the introduction, which stem from the pathologies of optimizing under contact dynamics compounded by standard neural network modeling:

1. **Inherited Stiff Transitions**: Neural network dynamics often reproduce the abrupt and stiff transitions characteristic of ground-truth contact events. These inherent nonsmoothnesses make the models difficult to optimize through using gradient-based methods.
2. **Additional Local Nonsmoothness**: Beyond inheriting stiffness, standard neural networks frequently introduce spurious local nonsmoothness and non-physical local optima. These artifacts can destabilize learning, lead to poor gradient conditioning, and degrade the performance of optimizers.
3. **Non-Gaussian Training Errors**: Training datasets for stiff dynamics necessarily contain rapid state changes, which can lead to "impulse-like" or heavy-tailed errors. Standard loss functions, such as Mean Squared Error (MSE), implicitly assume Gaussian noise models, which are inappropriate for such heavy-tailed error distributions and can destabilize training by overweighting outliers.

Based on these difficulties, the authors pose several motivating questions that their research seeks to answer:

- **Impact of Smoothness and Robust Likelihoods**: Can explicitly controlling neural network smoothness (via architectural design) and using heavy-tailed likelihoods (for training) improve training stability and enable reliable gradient-based MPC through contact? What is the specific contribution of each component to the overall performance?
- **Benefits for Sampling-Based MPC**: While sampling-based MPC methods are often preferred for nonsmooth dynamics, do these same design choices (smoothness and robust likelihoods) also benefit sampling-based approaches? And if so, what advantages (e.g., scalability, efficiency) remain for gradient-based MPC once smoothness is enforced?
- **Versatility and Generalization**: Can learned MPC and state estimation for legged robots, leveraging domain randomization, achieve the task-level flexibility of MPC combined with the generalization capabilities afforded by deep learning, enabling diverse behaviors in unseen environments without retraining?

In essence, the research is motivated by the promise of learned MPC for legged robots, but critically acknowledges and directly addresses the numerical and statistical challenges that have historically limited its practical efficacy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology lies in addressing the identified challenges through a combination of a novel neural network architecture and a robust training objective, integrated within a single-shooting MPC framework.

#### 3.1. Smooth Neural Surrogates (SNS-MLP)

To tackle the issues of inherited and spurious nonsmoothness (challenges 1 and 2), the authors introduce the **Smooth Neural Surrogate (SNS-MLP)**. This is a multilayer perceptron (MLP) specifically designed to provide tunable smoothness and informative derivatives for trajectory optimization, even when modeling contact-rich dynamics.

- **Lipschitz-based Weight Normalization**: The SNS-MLP builds upon prior work that uses weight normalization to bound the Lipschitz constant of each layer. A function is *c-Lipschitz* if its output changes are proportionally bounded by input changes. For an MLP, the global Lipschitz upper bound is approximately the product of layer-wise Lipschitz constants ($c_{\text{MLP}} \lesssim \prod c_\ell$).
- **Scalable Exponential Parameterization**: A key innovation is the use of an exponential parameterization for the learned layer-wise Lipschitz constants ($c_\ell = \exp(\theta_c^{(\ell)})$). This reparameterization ensures that the gradient of the global Lipschitz regularization objective is distributed more evenly across layers, preventing saturation or collapse during training, particularly for deeper networks or strict smoothness constraints.
- **Second-Order Smoothness (Curvature Budget)**: Beyond bounding the first-order derivatives (sensitivity budget $c_{\text{ub}}$), the SNS-MLP can also bound the Lipschitz constant of the Jacobian, which is related to the second derivative (curvature budget $d_{\text{ub}}$). This provides qualitative control over curvature, yielding smoother transitions similar to analytical smooth functions like softplus. The objective $L_k = L_{\text{main}} + \lambda \max(1, (C S^{k-1}) / B_k)$ allows for first-order ($k=1$) or second-order ($k=2$) regularization.

#### 3.2. Heavy-Tailed Maximum Likelihood Estimation (Cauchy MLE)

To address the issue of non-Gaussian model errors (challenge 3), the authors propose training their models using a **heavy-tailed likelihood model**, specifically the Cauchy distribution.

- **Empirical Observation**: The authors empirically demonstrate that one-step prediction residuals in legged robot dynamics datasets exhibit heavy-tailed behavior, aligning more closely with a Cauchy distribution than a Gaussian (Fig. 7). This is attributed to the "jump" or "impulse-like" nature of state changes during contact events.
- **Robustness to Outliers**: Unlike Gaussian losses (e.g., MSE), which overweight large errors due to their quadratic nature, the Cauchy negative log-likelihood (Mean Cauchy Error, MCE) has gradients that saturate for large errors (Table II). This prevents instability by reducing the impact of outliers and provides more robust training convergence.
- **Parameterization**: The Cauchy likelihood $C(x; \mu, \Sigma)$ is used, where $\mu$ is the location and $\Sigma$ is a dispersion term. For isotropic cases, $\mu$ corresponds to the median, and $\sigma$ (from $\Sigma = \sigma^2 I$) is proportional to the Median Absolute Deviation (MAD), robust alternatives to mean and standard deviation.

#### 3.3. Integrated MPC and State Estimation Framework

The SNS-MLP dynamics and state estimator are integrated into a **single-shooting MPC framework** for legged robots under domain randomization and partial observability.

- **Learned Dynamics Model**: The dynamics model $\hat{\mu}_\theta(X_t, U_t)$ predicts the next state $x_{t+1}$ based on fixed-length histories of states $X_t$ and actions $U_t$. It is trained using the Cauchy MLE with a homoscedastic dispersion $\hat{\Sigma}_D$ estimated from the replay buffer.
- **Predictor-Corrector State Estimator**: For partial observability, a separate SNS-MLP estimator $\bar{\mu}_\zeta$ is trained. It follows a predictor-corrector structure:
 - **Prediction**: The estimator rolls its previous posterior estimate forward using the learned dynamics to obtain a prior history $\bar{X}_{t|t-1}$.
 - **Correction**: It then applies a learned correction based on new measurements, the prior, actions, and the innovation (difference between actual and predicted measurements) to yield a posterior history $\bar{X}_{t|t}$.
- **Concurrent Training**: Both the dynamics model and the estimator are trained concurrently. The dynamics model's loss includes single-step (L_step), multi-step rollout (L_rollout), and corrupted-input (L_corrupt) terms, all based on MCE, plus the SNS smoothness penalty (L_k-SNS). The estimator is trained with a multi-step MCE loss (L_estimator) and its own SNS penalty. Stop-gradient operators are used to prevent training instability.
- **Augmented State Space**: The state includes standard proprioceptive terms, a 6D orientation representation, and critically, **signed distances ($\phi$)** from body geometries to the terrain. These signed distances provide terrain awareness for collision avoidance and gait tracking, especially useful with randomized terrain slopes.

#### 3.4. Generalized Gauss-Newton MPC (GGN-MPC)

For online control, the MPC problem (minimizing cost $\ell$ subject to constraints $g_i$ over an action sequence $u_{0:T-1}$) is solved using a generalized Gauss-Newton method.

- **Action Parameterization**: The action sequence is parameterized using B-splines with $k$ knots, reducing the dimensionality of the decision variables and enforcing continuity.
- **Constraint Handling**: Inequality constraints are handled via a relaxed logarithmic barrier function, augmented into the objective.
- **Backward Pass**: A local descent direction is computed by approximating the Hessian with the generalized Gauss-Newton matrix, which remains positive semi-definite if per-term objective errors are convex in the residual.
- **Forward Pass / Parallel Line Search**: A greedy parallel line search is used to find an optimal step size along the descent direction, leveraging GPU parallelism for evaluating candidate trajectories.

#### 3.5. Offline Training for Online Behavior Synthesis

- **Domain Randomization**: Training is performed under extensive domain randomization (masses, COM, joint properties, friction, terrain tilt, latency, measurement noise) to improve sim-to-real transfer and generalization.
- **Minimal Training Objective**: During training, a simple, general objective (without specific gait tracking or extensive shaping) is used to avoid implicit biases. Task-specific behaviors are synthesized at deployment time purely by adjusting costs and constraints.
- **Hardware Deployment**: The trained dynamics and estimator models are deployed on a real quadruped robot (Unitree Go2) without additional training, demonstrating zero-shot generalization to unseen terrains and tasks.

This comprehensive approach systematically addresses the primary challenges in learned MPC for legged robots by combining architectural inductive biases for smoothness with statistical robustness for error modeling, and integrating them into an efficient gradient-based online planning framework.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several compelling findings, demonstrated through extensive simulation experiments and hardware deployment, addressing the motivating questions directly.

#### 4.1. Impact of Smooth Neural Surrogates and Heavy-Tailed Likelihoods

- **Improved Training Stability and Control Performance**: Models trained with the proposed Smooth Neural Surrogates (SNS-MLPs) and Cauchy heavy-tailed likelihoods exhibit significantly better training stability and lower cumulative control costs compared to standard MLPs or those trained with Gaussian likelihoods.
 - **SNS-MLPs**: Standard MLPs and weight-decay MLPs show rapidly growing Lipschitz bounds during training and perform poorly in control, especially as training progresses. SNS-MLPs, however, remain within their prescribed sensitivity budgets and achieve consistent low costs. A **Pareto trade-off** is observed between smoothness and control performance: moderately smooth models (e.g., $c_{\text{ub}} = 10^4$) perform best, while overly smooth models learn slowly, and insufficiently smooth models degrade planning (Fig. 9).
 - **Cauchy Likelihoods**: Dynamics residuals in legged robots are empirically heavy-tailed (Fig. 7). Training with Cauchy Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) leads to lower prediction error, faster and more stable convergence, and substantially lower control cost (approx. **5x lower at 25,000 steps, 2-3x lower at 100,000 steps**) compared to Gaussian MLE (Fig. 10). This confirms that robust error modeling is crucial for stiff dynamics.
 - **Smoothing Order**: For the generalized Gauss-Newton (GGN) solver (which primarily uses first-order derivatives), there was no substantial difference in prediction accuracy or planning performance between first-order and second-order SNS (Fig. 11).

#### 4.2. Zero-Shot Generalization and the Importance of Derivatives

- **Zeroth-Order Error vs. Control Performance**: Counter-intuitively, standard MLPs often achieve lower one-step prediction error (MAE) on test datasets than SNS-MLPs (Table VII). However, this lower MAE does **not** correlate with better control performance. Instead, SNS-MLPs dramatically outperform standard MLPs in both sampling-based and gradient-based control, especially on challenging, zero-shot locomotion tasks.
- **Derivative Quality is Key**: The discrepancy is attributed to the quality of derivatives. While standard MLPs might capture sharp transients in zeroth-order predictions, their derivatives are noisy, stiff, and highly variable (Fig. 12). SNS-MLPs, despite minor bias in zeroth-order predictions, maintain globally smooth, bounded, and well-conditioned derivatives, which are crucial for stable optimization.
- **Overfitting and Local Nonsmoothness**: The failures of standard MLPs are linked to local nonsmoothness and derivative instability, akin to overfitting for control purposes, which is not fully mitigated by early stopping. Early-stopped MLPs show some smoother derivatives but suffer from substantially higher prediction error overall.
- **Robustness Across Tasks**: SNS-MLPs, particularly when paired with Cauchy likelihoods and GGN-MPC, achieve uniformly low costs and perfect success rates across a diverse set of locomotion tasks (in-place trot, forward trot, lateral trot, forward gallop), where standard MLPs often fail outright (Table VIII). In complex tasks, SNS-MLPs enable reliable execution (0/5 → 5/5 success) and yield **2-50x lower cumulative cost**, indicating orders-of-magnitude improvements in robustness.
- **Learned State Estimation**: Performance using learned SNS-based state estimates closely matches that obtained with ground-truth states, demonstrating the efficacy of the predictor-corrector estimator (Table VIII).

#### 4.3. Advantages of Gradient-Based MPC with SNS

- **Computational Efficiency and Scalability**: When paired with SNS dynamics, the generalized Gauss-Newton MPC (GGN-MPC) demonstrates significant practical advantages over sampling-based methods like DIAL-MPC.
 - **Fewer Rollouts**: GGN-MPC achieves low cost with an order of magnitude fewer evaluations (rollouts) than DIAL-MPC, as its candidates are more informative (Fig. 14, Left).
 - **Faster Convergence**: GGN-MPC shows diminishing returns from additional iterations, converging quickly, whereas DIAL-MPC requires more iterations and still struggles to reach comparable costs. Overall, GGN-MPC achieves approximately **2x lower cumulative cost at equal, realistic solve times** (Fig. 14, Right).
 - **Dimensionality Scalability**: GGN-MPC maintains low costs across higher action-trajectory resolutions, while DIAL-MPC degrades rapidly due to the curse of dimensionality. GGN-MPC achieves **~10x lower cumulative cost at 300 decision variables** (Fig. 15, Left).
 - **Parallelization**: GGN-MPC scales more effectively in GPU-based parallel training (e.g., across multiple environments), being **~7x faster at 1028 environments** due to requiring fewer rollouts (Fig. 15, Right).
- **Reduced Hyperparameter Sensitivity**: GGN-MPC is less sensitive to hyperparameters compared to DIAL-MPC, which shows wide performance variation with temperature and diffusion-related parameters (Fig. 20).

#### 4.4. Hardware Deployment

- **Real-World Generalization**: A single, offline-trained dynamics model and state estimator, leveraging domain randomization and signed-distance states, successfully transferred to a real Unitree Go2 quadruped robot.
- **Diverse Behaviors**: The system synthesized a wide range of whole-body behaviors (trot, bound, gallop, pace, rear, tripod) on unseen terrains (uneven slopes, slippery surfaces, deep debris) and tasks (push recovery, load-carrying) by simply modifying online costs and constraints, without retraining or finetuning (Fig. 1, Fig. 8, Fig. 13, Fig. 16).
- **Adaptive Control**: MPC's task-level flexibility was used to refine behavior (e.g., stable high-speed deceleration via cost tuning, Fig. 17) and enable smooth transitions between gaits with different costs and constraints (behavior stitching, Fig. 18).

In summary, the main findings highlight that explicit control over neural network smoothness and robust error modeling are crucial for enabling reliable gradient-based MPC with learned dynamics for legged robots. These design choices lead to significant improvements in robustness, generalization, and computational efficiency, culminating in successful real-world deployment of highly adaptable control policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the field of legged robotics and model-based reinforcement learning by providing a robust, scalable, and practical framework for combining the representational power of deep learning with the adaptability of model predictive control. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Reliable Gradient-Based Learned MPC for Legged Robots**: The work directly addresses and largely overcomes long-standing challenges associated with integrating neural network dynamics into gradient-based MPC, particularly for stiff, contact-rich systems. By systematically tackling nonsmoothness (via SNS) and heavy-tailed errors (via Cauchy MLE), the authors have shown that gradient-based methods, previously often deemed too fragile for these domains, can actually outperform sampling-based alternatives. This could lead to a resurgence of interest and development in gradient-based approaches for learned control.

2. **Bridging the Gap Between Flexibility and Efficiency**: The proposed methodology offers a powerful middle ground between the highly flexible but often sample-inefficient model-free RL and the efficient but generalization-limited classical MPC with hand-designed models. It provides the "best of both worlds": the scalability and adaptability of learned models combined with the online, task-level flexibility and computational efficiency of MPC.

3. **Improved Generalization and Zero-Shot Transfer**: The demonstration of zero-shot generalization to diverse behaviors and unseen terrains on real hardware, using a single frozen model trained offline, is a major contribution. This capability reduces the need for extensive, task-specific retraining, making robot deployment faster and more cost-effective. The integration of signed-distance states and domain randomization is particularly impactful for terrain-aware planning.

4. **Redefining "Good" Dynamics Models for Control**: The finding that zeroth-order prediction accuracy (MAE) is not a sufficient metric for control performance, and that first-order properties (smoothness and well-conditioned derivatives) are more critical, offers a crucial insight for the broader model-based RL community. This encourages a shift in focus from purely minimizing prediction error to optimizing for properties relevant to downstream control.

5. **Scalability and Computational Advantages**: GGN-MPC with SNS dynamics demonstrates superior scalability with increasing decision dimensionality and more efficient parallelization compared to sampling-based methods. This has substantial implications for developing more sophisticated and complex behaviors, as well as for accelerating large-scale training of robotic policies.

6. **Modular Building Blocks for Future Research**: The Smooth Neural Surrogate and the heavy-tailed likelihood approach can be seen as modular components. They can potentially be integrated into a wide range of existing and future model-based learning and control pipelines, including:
 - **Contact-rich Manipulation and Humanoid Control**: Extending SNS to these domains could enable more general whole-body control beyond quadruped locomotion.
 - **Stabilizing Policy Learning**: Incorporating smooth dynamics into model-based RL (e.g., policy learning with differentiable simulation) could provide more stable and informative gradients for policy updates.
 - **Perceptual Integration**: Combining SNS dynamics and estimators with raw perceptual inputs (vision, depth, tactile sensing) could further enhance robustness and awareness in complex real-world scenarios.
 - **More Expressive Architectures**: Investigating Lipschitz constraints for CNNs and transformers in robotics and control could unlock the benefits of smoothness in higher-capacity models.

In conclusion, "Learning Legged MPC with Smooth Neural Surrogates" provides fundamental architectural and training improvements that enhance the compatibility between learned dynamics and model-based planners. It offers a practical, scalable, and flexible framework for creating versatile legged robot controllers, paving the way for more autonomous and capable robots operating in complex real-world environments.
