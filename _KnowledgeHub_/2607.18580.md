# STeP: Signal Temporal Logic for Precise Specifications for Action Generation with Vision Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The STeP framework integrates Signal Temporal Logic (STL) with Vision-Language Models to enable robots to precisely follow natural language instructions with spatial, temporal, and logical constraints. This approach enhances execution reliability, facilitates dynamic switching between learned policies and Model-Predictive Control, and improves few-shot replanning after failures, demonstrating higher safe success rates in constrained manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models often fail to precisely execute natural language instructions containing nuanced spatial, temporal, and logical constraints, leading to task failures or unsafe operations.
- Current dual-system VLA architectures lack a formal mechanism to represent, monitor, and enforce these precise requirements across the high-level reasoning (System 2) and low-level execution (System 1) boundary.
- Learned policies, adept at complex behaviors, struggle with explicit constraints, while traditional model-predictive control (MPC), effective for constraints, falters with perceptually complex or contact-rich tasks.

## Method

- **STeP** establishes a hierarchical architecture using ==Signal Temporal Logic (STL)== as a formal interface between high-level VLM-based task planning (System 2) and low-level robot execution (System 1).
- System 2 processes natural language instructions into subtasks with grounded parameters and compiles them into ==active STL task specifications==, including safety and temporal constraints.
- System 1 dynamically selects between an ==STL-guided Model-Predictive Control (MPC)== for precise constraint satisfaction and pre-trained learned policies for perceptually complex behaviors, continuously monitoring ==STL robustness== for progress and constraint adherence.

## Results

- STeP achieved higher safe success rates in real-world tabletop manipulation tasks involving spatial, temporal, and logical constraints compared to a VLM-MPC-COST baseline, showing particular improvement in temporal constraint adherence.
- The framework successfully demonstrated dynamic switching between a learned policy and MPC, detecting potential collisions in **29 out of 30** trials and rerouting the robot arm with MPC before returning control to the learned policy.
- STL-guided few-shot replanning corrected **12 out of 16** initial failures within two attempts (and **14 out of 16** within three) by providing detailed violation feedback, outperforming a baseline that corrected only **7 out of 18** failures within two attempts due to scalar feedback.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formalizing natural language constraints into explicit STL specifications provides a precise, interpretable, and verifiable interface for linking high-level VLM reasoning with low-level robot control.
> - Quantitative STL robustness values serve as an effective real-time signal for monitoring execution progress, detecting constraint violations, and enabling dynamic switching between complementary control paradigms (learned policies and MPC).
> - Structured feedback from STL violation traces, detailing specific violated constraints and their robustness margins, significantly enhances the efficiency and targeting of few-shot replanning and failure recovery.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in contemporary robotics: enabling Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models to reliably execute precise natural language instructions that contain spatial, temporal, and logical constraints. While VLA models have demonstrated generalization across diverse manipulation tasks, they often lack mechanisms to represent, monitor, or enforce such specific requirements during execution. This can lead to policies satisfying the broad semantic goal of an instruction but failing to meet critical timing, ordering, or safety conditions.

The paper positions itself within the context of recent dual-system VLA architectures, which typically separate robot behavior into a slow, deliberative System 2 (for vision-language reasoning and high-level guidance) and a fast System 1 (for low-level action generation). The authors contend that current dual-system approaches do not adequately specify how precise language-derived requirements should be formalized or enforced. This research proposes that System 2 should not only plan but also formalize these requirements into a representation that System 1 can actively monitor and enforce.

This work integrates principles from several research areas:
- **Large Language Models (LLMs) in Robotics:** Building on efforts to use LLMs for high-level task planning, grounding language-model plans with robot affordances, generating executable code, constructing optimization-based spatial representations, and supporting long-horizon decomposition and replanning.
- **Temporal Logic in Robot Motion Planning:** Drawing from established methods that utilize temporal logic (such as LTL and STL) for mission planning, reactive synthesis, abstraction-based control, and model-predictive control (MPC) for continuous systems.
- **LLMs for STL Generation:** Leveraging recent advancements where language models translate natural language requirements into formal Signal Temporal Logic (STL) specifications.

The distinguishing contribution of this work is its comprehensive embedding of STL throughout the entire System 1/System 2 robot planning pipeline. Unlike previous approaches that might treat STL primarily as a generated specification or a feasibility check, STeP integrates STL into task decomposition, policy selection, low-level control, runtime monitoring, and replanning, thereby creating a formal interface that spans the entire control loop. This continuous integration aims to make precise language constraints explicit and actionable, rather than implicit within a learned policy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of current VLA models in adhering to precise instructions for robot manipulation. While these models can interpret general goals, they often struggle with the nuanced spatial, temporal, and logical constraints inherent in human language. For instance, an instruction might require placing an object at a specific metric offset, completing a maneuver within a timing window, or avoiding certain regions, conditions that current VLA models may satisfy semantically but violate formally. This imprecision can lead to task failures or unsafe operations.

The dual-system VLA architectures, while separating high-level reasoning from low-level execution, do not inherently provide a mechanism for representing and enforcing these precise requirements across the system boundary. Learned policies, typically used for System 1, lack a clear interface for optimizing against explicit constraints or for incorporating new requirements without extensive re-training. Conversely, traditional model-predictive control (MPC) can optimize explicit constraints effectively, but often struggles with perceptually complex or contact-rich behaviors where learned policies are more adept. This dichotomy highlights the need for a hybrid execution model.

To address these challenges, the research outlines three key objectives, presented as research claims:

1. **Precise Language Following:** The first objective is to demonstrate that STL-guided planning can enhance the robot's reliability in satisfying spatial, temporal, and logical constraints derived from natural language instructions. This must be achieved without compromising overall task performance, aiming for both correctness and efficiency. The motivation here is to move beyond coarse semantic task completion to true adherence to detailed human specifications.

2. **Learned Policy/Motion Planning Switching:** The second objective focuses on developing a framework that can dynamically select between learned policies and STL-guided Model-Predictive Control (MPC) at the subtask level. This switching mechanism is motivated by the complementary strengths of these two control paradigms: learned policies excel in expressive or contact-rich behaviors, while MPC is better suited for situations requiring precise constraint satisfaction or safety enforcement. The goal is to leverage the appropriate control method for each phase of a complex task.

3. **Few-Shot Replanning:** The third objective is to improve the robot's ability to recover from intermediate execution failures efficiently. The motivation is that robots often encounter unexpected events or disturbances. By preserving STL specifications and utilizing quantitative robustness feedback within the replanning context, the framework aims to enable more targeted and efficient plan corrections, grounding the recovery process in the original language instruction rather than requiring a full re-planning from scratch.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to imbue language-conditioned robots with a formal understanding and enforcement capability for precise instructions, thereby increasing their reliability, interpretability, and ability to operate robustly in dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, STeP, employs a hierarchical architecture that uses Signal Temporal Logic (STL) as a formal interface between high-level language understanding and low-level robot execution. This framework consists of two interacting modules: System 2 for high-level task interpretation and formalization, and System 1 for low-level execution, monitoring, and replanning.

#### 3.1. System 2: Language-to-STL Task Planning

System 2 functions as the task planner and formalizer. Its process involves:
- **Input Processing:** It takes a natural language instruction, the current observation of the environment, a library of available robot skills and signal predicates, and a "recall context" (history of previous observations, states, STL robustness values, and plans) as input.
- **VLM-based Decomposition:** A Vision-Language Model (VLM) is queried to decompose the language instruction into a sequence of high-level subtasks. Each subtask is structured to include a skill name, grounded parameters (e.g., specific objects or locations), optional constraints, and a natural language description.
- **Static Task-Spec Checks:** Prior to execution, a set of automated checks validate the VLM's output. These checks ensure adherence to a specified JSON schema, verify the existence of skills in the library, confirm correct parameter types, ascertain the presence of referenced objects/regions in the scene, and ensure constraints are supported by the chosen skill. Specifications failing these checks are returned to the VLM for correction.
- **Deterministic STL Compilation:** The validated task specification is then processed by a deterministic STL compiler. This component maps each skill template and its grounded parameters to a corresponding STL formula, drawing from a predefined predicate library. It also incorporates relevant local and global constraints, such as safety requirements or temporal windows, converting time intervals into controller timesteps.
- **LLM-based Consistency Check (Optional):** An additional LLM-based check can be optionally used to identify logical inconsistencies within the generated STL formulas, such as contradictory constraints or discrepancies between the natural language description and the compiled formal structure.
- **Output:** The final output from System 2 is an "active STL task specification," which is an executable sequence of subtasks, each paired with its associated STL formula.

#### 3.2. System 1: STL-Guided Execution and Monitoring

System 1 is responsible for generating low-level actions based on the STL task specification. Its core functionalities include:
- **Executor Selection:** For each subtask, the system dynamically selects between two execution modes: an STL-guided Model-Predictive Control (MPC) controller or a pre-trained learned policy. MPC is utilized when subtasks involve explicit geometric, temporal, or safety constraints that can be analytically modeled. Learned policies are employed for behaviors that are perceptually complex, difficult to model analytically, or require expressive control (e.g., contact-rich manipulation).
- **STL Monitoring:** During execution, an STL monitor continuously evaluates the quantitative robustness of the active subtask's STL formula. This robustness value provides a continuous, real-valued measure of execution progress and constraint satisfaction.
- **Recall Mechanism:** If the STL robustness value falls below a predefined threshold, indicating a potential constraint violation or deviation, System 1 triggers a "recall" event. The current execution context, including the history log, is passed back to System 2, enabling it to diagnose the failure and revise the plan while remaining grounded in the original task specification.
- **Subtask Completion Detection:** The STL monitor also detects subtask completion. Once the active STL formula is satisfied (i.e., its robustness value is non-negative and sufficiently high), the system either progresses to the next subtask or invokes System 2 for replanning, depending on the overall task structure.

#### 3.3. History Log (Memory)

To facilitate contextualized monitoring and replanning, the system maintains a rolling history log. Updated at a fixed rate, this log stores a limited number (L) of recent records. Each record encapsulates the observation, robot state, natural language description of the subtask, selected low-level executor, calculated STL robustness values, and any detected completion or failure events. This log serves as crucial context when System 1 triggers a recall, providing System 2 with detailed execution history.

#### 3.4. Model-Predictive Control (MPC)

For tasks where MPC is selected, the controller optimizes an action sequence over a finite horizon (H) at each timestep. Given a dynamics model (s_t+1 = f(s_t, a_t)) and an STL specification (φ), the MPC controller solves an optimization problem to minimize a cost function. This cost function includes a standard control cost c(s_k|t, a_k) and a term (-λρ(z_t:t+H|t, φ)) designed to maximize the STL robustness (ρ) of the predicted signal trajectory (z). The robustness computation utilizes a smooth approximation (e.g., softmax) for MAX/MIN operators, enabling gradient-based optimization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of STeP was conducted on a real-world tabletop manipulation platform using a UR3e robotic arm, focusing on long-horizon language-conditioned tasks involving execution monitoring, failure recovery, and dynamic switching between execution modes. The framework was compared against an ablated baseline called VLM-MPC-COST, which used a direct MPC cost function and scalar failure feedback instead of STL specifications and robustness traces. All VLM calls utilized GPT-4o.

#### 4.1. RQ1: Precise Language Following

This research question investigated whether STL-guided planning improved the robot's ability to satisfy explicit spatial, temporal, and logical constraints from language.
- **Experimental Setup:** Real-world tabletop manipulation tasks were designed to test various constraint types, including spatial exclusion zones, metric placement relative to objects, bounded-time responses, and identity-based sorting, along with combinations thereof.
- **Results:** STeP demonstrated higher safe success rates across all categories of tasks compared to the VLM-MPC-COST baseline. The most significant improvements were observed in tasks involving temporal constraints. These findings suggest that formal STL specifications enable the robot to adhere more precisely to language-derived constraints without diminishing overall task completion performance.

#### 4.2. RQ2: Learned Policy/Motion Planning Switching

This question explored the framework's capability to mediate runtime switching between a learned policy and MPC.
- **Experimental Setup:** A ResNet-based behavior cloning (BC) policy was trained on 30 demonstrations to pick a cube from a chest. An obstacle was introduced into the approach path at test time. An STL monitor tracked the robot arm's safe distance from the obstacle.
- **Results:** When the STL robustness for obstacle avoidance dropped below a threshold, the system successfully detected a potential collision in 29 out of 30 trials. It then re-assigned the subtask to MPC, which rerouted the arm around the obstacle in 25 cases. Following MPC's completion of the avoidance maneuver, the learned BC policy resumed, completing the grasp in 22 of these trials. In contrast, the BC policy alone collided with the obstacle in 28 out of 30 trials, while MPC alone succeeded at avoidance but failed at the contact-rich grasping task in 24 out of 30 trials. These results indicate that STL robustness provides an effective signal for dynamic switching between execution modes, allowing each to handle the subtask phases it is best suited for.

#### 4.3. RQ3: Few-Shot Replanning

This objective assessed whether STL violation traces could enable more targeted and efficient plan corrections after failures.
- **Experimental Setup:** The task was to stack three cubes. A common failure mode was the robot knocking over the stack during the third placement due to a low approach height. A tight failure margin was used to ensure interaction before replanning.
- **Results:** In STeP, 16 out of 20 initial attempts resulted in the stack being knocked over. Upon failure, the STL monitor recorded the specific violated constraint (e.g., SAFE END-EFFECTOR), its robustness margin, and the timestep of violation. This structured feedback, along with an updated scene snapshot, was provided to System 2. With this detailed STL feedback, 12 of the 16 failures were corrected on the second attempt, and 14 were corrected within three attempts. In comparison, the VLM-MPC-COST baseline experienced 18 initial failures out of 20 trials. Due to its scalar MPC cost feedback, only 7 of these 18 failures were corrected within two attempts, and 9 within three attempts. Observations indicated that the baseline often either overcorrected the approach height or regenerated the entire plan, rather than adjusting the specific failed parameter. These findings suggest that structured STL feedback facilitates more targeted and efficient recovery mechanisms.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The STeP framework demonstrates a significant advancement in language-conditioned robot planning by introducing Signal Temporal Logic (STL) as a formal and ubiquitous interface between high-level Vision-Language Model (VLM) reasoning and low-level robot execution.

The significance of this work lies in several areas:
- **Enhanced Precision and Reliability:** By converting imprecise natural language instructions into formal STL specifications, the framework enables robots to achieve higher precision and reliability in satisfying spatial, temporal, and logical constraints. This moves beyond simple semantic task completion to ensuring that task execution adheres to explicit, formal requirements.
- **Improved Interpretability and Verifiability:** The explicit nature of STL specifications allows for pre-execution validation, runtime monitoring, and post-failure analysis. This inherent verifiability enhances the interpretability of robot behavior, as deviations from the intended specification can be formally detected and explained.
- **Hybrid Control Paradigm:** STeP successfully integrates the complementary strengths of learned policies and model-predictive control (MPC). Learned policies can handle perceptually complex and contact-rich behaviors, while STL-guided MPC ensures precise constraint satisfaction and safety. The use of STL robustness as a dynamic switching signal between these modes provides a robust mechanism for combining these approaches within a single task.
- **Effective Replanning and Recovery:** The framework's ability to provide structured STL violation feedback to the VLM during replanning facilitates more targeted and efficient recovery from intermediate failures. This is crucial for long-horizon tasks in uncertain environments, allowing the robot to repair specific aspects of a plan rather than necessitating a complete re-generation.
- **Bridging High-Level and Low-Level Control:** The work establishes a robust bridge between the high-level, symbolic reasoning capabilities of VLMs and the continuous control requirements of robot execution. This formal connection ensures that the nuanced intent expressed in natural language is translated into actionable and enforceable low-level commands.

The potential impact of STeP extends to the development of more robust, autonomous, and trustworthy robotic systems. Robots capable of precisely interpreting and executing complex human instructions, while also safely adapting to unforeseen circumstances, could be deployed in a wider range of applications, including manufacturing, logistics, domestic assistance, and hazardous environments. This research contributes to a foundation where language-conditioned robots can reliably interact with the world by explicitly understanding and enforcing the detailed requirements of their tasks, rather than relying solely on implicit learning mechanisms.
