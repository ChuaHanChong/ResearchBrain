# Technical Report: Asynchronous Distributed Trajectory Estimation of Multi-Robot Systems

> [!summary] Summary
> A new asynchronous distributed algorithm for multi-robot trajectory estimation was developed, proving exponentially fast convergence even with communication and computation delays. The method significantly reduces communication overhead by up to 96.9% while achieving up to 64% lower estimation error than synchronous state-of-the-art approaches in simulations.

## Problem

- Existing distributed state estimation (DSE) algorithms for multi-robot systems commonly assume synchronous computations and communications.
- Real-world multi-robot systems frequently encounter variable computation speeds and communication delays, leading to performance degradation and the "straggler effect" when synchronicity is enforced.
- This lack of robustness to asynchrony limits the scalability and practical deployment of multi-robot systems in complex, dynamic environments.

## Method

- The multi-robot trajectory estimation problem is formulated as a ==Maximum A Posteriori (MAP)== optimization problem over a sliding time window.
- An approximation for propagating the ==prior information matrix== is introduced to significantly reduce communication density and enable sparse communication between agents.
- An ==asynchronous Block Coordinate Descent (BCD)== algorithm is developed to solve the approximate MAP problem, designed to function effectively under conditions of bounded asynchronous computation and communication delays.

## Results

- The proposed approximation achieved up to a **96.9%** reduction in required communication edges in an illustrative scenario, with the Root Mean Squared Error from the true MAP bounded at **4.4 x 10^-4**.
- Simulations demonstrated that the asynchronous algorithm achieved up to **64%** less estimation error and better scalability compared to a synchronous state-of-the-art algorithm (DRWT) across various numbers of agents.
- Theoretical analysis proved exponential convergence for the asynchronous algorithm under bounded delays, and hardware experiments on a multi-robot platform confirmed robustness to substantial, varying communication delays with consistent exponential error decrease.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Solving the exact MAP problem in a distributed manner quickly leads to dense information matrices, requiring unscalable all-to-all communication among agents.
> - Approximating the prior information matrix update, rather than the covariance, maintains high estimation accuracy while drastically promoting communication sparsity (e.g., reducing O(N²) communication to O(N) in an example system).
> - Exponential convergence of the Block Coordinate Descent algorithm can be theoretically guaranteed even under partially asynchronous conditions, provided computation and communication delays are bounded.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

State estimation is a foundational problem in autonomous systems, spanning applications from mobile robotics and transportation to collaborative robotics. When multiple sensors are utilized, decentralized sensing offers advantages such as robustness to individual sensor failures and the ability to integrate diverse sensing modalities. Distributed state estimation (DSE) leverages computing and communication capabilities embedded within systems to facilitate collaborative state estimation among a network of "agents" observing "target" robots.

Existing DSE literature commonly operates under the assumption that agents perform computations and communications synchronously. However, this assumption frequently does not hold in real-world multi-robot systems. Such systems encounter various delays, which can stem from physical barriers, adversarial jamming, or saturated network bandwidth. Additionally, computational speeds can vary significantly across agents due to heterogeneous hardware. Attempts to enforce synchronicity in these environments can lead to the "straggler effect," where the overall system performance is limited by the slowest agent, thereby impeding the convergence of multi-agent systems.

The development of asynchronous algorithms has been identified as a significant goal within the robotics research community. This work contributes to that objective by developing a parallelized asynchronous algorithm specifically for distributed state estimation. Prior related work in distributed optimization applied to state estimation includes approaches for specific types of UAV swarms or uses synchronous methods. For instance, a relevant prior work by Shorinwa et al. (2020) employs synchronous CADMM-based updates. This paper distinguishes itself by focusing on estimating states of arbitrary time-varying systems and explicitly addressing the challenges posed by asynchronous operations in both computation and communication.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of existing distributed state estimation (DSE) algorithms, which typically assume synchronous computations and communications among agents. In practical multi-robot systems, this synchronicity is often unattainable due to real-world factors such as variable computation speeds (heterogeneous hardware), communication delays (physical obstacles, network congestion, jamming), and the performance bottleneck known as the "straggler effect" caused by the slowest agent. Attempting to synchronize operations in such environments can significantly degrade system performance and slow convergence.

Given these challenges, the work sets forth several key objectives:

1. **Develop an Asynchronous DSE Algorithm:** The core objective is to propose a decentralized state estimation algorithm specifically designed to operate effectively under asynchronous computation and communication conditions. The algorithm assumes only that delays are bounded, a condition referred to as "partial asynchrony."
2. **Formulate Trajectory Estimation as a MAP Problem:** The work aims to formulate the state estimation problem for multi-robot systems as a Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) optimization problem. This formulation targets the optimal state estimate of the target system's trajectory over a sliding window, allowing for the use of current measurements to refine past state estimates within that window.
3. **Reduce Communication Requirements:** A significant objective is to address the inherent communication density that can arise from solving the exact MAP problem in a distributed manner. The authors aim to derive an approximation of the MAP problem that substantially reduces the required communications among agents while introducing only negligible estimation errors. This is motivated by the observation that exact formulations can necessitate all-to-all communication, leading to scalability issues.
4. **Prove Theoretical Convergence:** A theoretical objective is to provide a formal proof of convergence for the proposed asynchronous algorithm. This proof aims to demonstrate that agents' iterates converge exponentially fast to the optimal estimate of the robots' states, even under conditions of asynchronous communications and computations.
5. **Empirical Validation and Comparison:** The final objectives involve empirically validating the proposed algorithm. This includes comparing its performance against comparable state-of-the-art synchronous algorithms through simulations to quantify error reduction. Additionally, hardware experiments are planned to demonstrate the algorithm's robustness to varying magnitudes of communication delays in a real-world multi-robot setting.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves a multi-faceted approach, commencing with a system model and a centralized MAP formulation, followed by an analysis of communication challenges, the derivation of an approximate objective function to mitigate these challenges, the development of an asynchronous Block Coordinate Descent (BCD) algorithm, and finally, a theoretical convergence analysis.

#### 3.1. System Model and MAP Formulation

The system comprises N agents observing a multi-robot "target system." The target system's state, $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^n$, evolves according to linear dynamics $x_{t+1} = A_t x_t + w_t$, where $w_t$ is Gaussian process noise $N(0, Q_t)$. Each agent $i$ makes measurements $y_{i,t} = C_{i,t} x_t + v_{i,t}$, where $v_{i,t}$ is Gaussian measurement noise. Measurements across agents can be correlated.

The objective is to estimate the state trajectory over a sliding window of time, $\{t-T_t, \ldots, t\}$, where $T_t = \min\{t, T\}$. This estimation is formulated as a Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) problem, minimizing an objective function $J(\hat{x}_{\tau(t)})$ derived from the Gaussian nature of the initial state prior, process noise, and measurement noise. The objective function is quadratic, expressed as $J(\hat{x}_{\tau(t)}) = \frac{1}{2} \hat{x}_{\tau(t)}^\top K_t \hat{x}_{\tau(t)} + f_t^\top \hat{x}_{\tau(t)} + c_t$. The solution to this problem, $\hat{x}_{\tau(t)}$, becomes the prior for the next sliding window via specific update laws for the mean and covariance (Equations 9 and 10).

#### 3.2. Problem-Induced Communications Analysis

To understand communication requirements, the authors first consider a synchronous Block Coordinate Descent (BCD) approach to solve the centralized MAP problem. In this setting, each agent $i$ is responsible for computing its block of the state estimate, $\hat{x}_{\tau(t)}^{[i]}$. The computations for initializing estimates and performing gradient updates involve terms that depend on information from other agents. The paper defines "dependency sets" ($D_i^{A,t}$, $D_i^{\hat{x},t}$, $D_i^{y,t}$, $D_i^{x,t}$) based on the non-zero blocks of the matrices involved in the update equations.

Proposition 1 and an illustrative Example 1 demonstrate that for a general system, the exact MAP formulation leads to an information matrix ($K_t$) that can become completely dense after a few time steps. This densification implies that each agent would need to communicate with *all* other agents (all-to-all communication) to compute its block update, rendering the distributed approach unscalable. Example 1 shows this occurs after just 6 time steps for a 10-agent system.

#### 3.3. Approximate Objective Formulation

To address the communication density issue, the methodology introduces an approximation. Instead of propagating the submatrix of the full covariance matrix $\hat{P}_{\tau(t)}$ to compute the next prior covariance $P_{\pi(t+1)}$ (Equation 10), the proposed approach propagates a submatrix of the *information matrix* $\breve{\Omega}_{\tau(t)}$ to compute the next prior information matrix $\tilde{\Omega}_{\pi(t+1)}$ (Equation 27). This leads to an approximate objective function $\breve{J}(\breve{x}_{\tau(t)})$.

Lemma 2 proves that this approximate update for the information matrix (Equation 27) maintains identity with the exact update (Equation 23) if initialized identically. Example 2, revisiting the scenario from Example 1, demonstrates that this approximation drastically reduces communication requirements. For the 10-agent system, it changes the communication topology from an all-to-all graph (O(N^2) edges) to an undirected line graph (O(N) edges), representing a significant sparsity gain. This approximation is crucial for enabling sparse communication.

#### 3.4. Asynchronous Algorithm (Algorithm 1)

Building on the sparse communication structure induced by the approximate objective, the paper proposes an asynchronous BCD algorithm (Algorithm 1). Key aspects include:
- **Local Information:** Each agent $i$ maintains its local copies of measurements ($y_i^t$), current trajectory estimate ($\breve{x}_{i,\tau(t)}(k)$), and prior estimate ($\tilde{x}_{i,\pi(t)}$).
- **Sparse Dependency Sets:** Lemma 3 characterizes the sparse dependency sets ($\breve{D}_i^{A,t}$, $\breve{D}_i^{\breve{x},t}$, $\breve{D}_i^{y,t}$, $\breve{D}_i^{\tilde{x},t}$) for the approximate problem, defining which agents agent $i$ needs to communicate with for its updates.
- **Asynchronous Updates:** Agents perform initial measurements and prior exchanges. Then, in a main loop, agents asynchronously compute updates to their local block estimate $\breve{x}_{i,\tau(t)}^{[i]}$ (denoted $\theta_i^t(k)$).
- **Outdated Information:** Due to asynchrony, an agent may use outdated information from other agents. The variable $\sigma_{i,[j],t}(k)$ tracks the iteration at which agent $j$ generated the value of $\breve{x}_{i,[j],\tau(t)}$ currently held by agent $i$.
- **Finalization:** Once a stopping criterion is met, each agent calculates its next prior estimate for the advanced sliding window.

#### 3.5. Convergence Analysis

To prove convergence under asynchrony, the authors adopt standard assumptions from partially asynchronous optimization literature:
- **Assumption 1 (Bounded Computation Delay):** Each agent computes an update to its block at least once every B iterations.
- **Assumption 2 (Bounded Communication Delay):** An agent's local copy of another agent's variable is no more than B iterations out of date.

Under these assumptions, Theorem 1 establishes that the sequence of estimates produced by Algorithm 1 converges at least exponentially to the unique minimizer of the approximate objective function. This convergence occurs with a B-step convergence ratio $\rho = 1 - c\eta$, where $c > 0$ is a scalar constant and $\eta$ is the step size. The proof relies on properties of the objective function (strongly convex, L-Lipschitz continuous gradient) and the framework for partially asynchronous gradient projection algorithms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical and empirical findings to validate the proposed asynchronous distributed trajectory estimation algorithm.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Contributions

1. **Sparsity-Promoting Approximation (Lemma 3):** The paper derived an approximated update law for the prior information matrix (Equation 27). This approximation maintains high estimation accuracy while significantly promoting sparsity in the required communications among agents. Specifically, for an example system, this approach was shown to reduce the number of required communication edges from $O(N^2)$ (all-to-all) to $O(N)$ (a line graph), leading to an observed reduction of up to 96.9% in communications.
2. **Exponential Convergence under Asynchrony (Theorem 1):** A formal proof was established demonstrating that the proposed asynchronous Block Coordinate Descent (BCD) algorithm converges exponentially fast to the solution of the approximated MAP problem. This convergence guarantee holds under both asynchronous communications and asynchronous computations, given standard assumptions of bounded delays (partial asynchrony). The theorem specifies an exponential convergence rate with a B-step convergence ratio, affirming the theoretical soundness of the approach.

#### 4.2. Performance Comparison in Simulation

Simulations were conducted using $N \in \{4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128\}$ agents estimating the trajectory of $N$ holonomic robots in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The system dynamics involved an LQR controller regulating robots to a goal state, with process and measurement noise. The LQR parameters were set such that agents in Algorithm 1 communicated with four neighbors. The performance of Algorithm 1 (BCD) was compared against a synchronous state-of-the-art algorithm, DRWT (from).

- **Error Reduction:** For all tested numbers of agents ($N$), the sub-optimality error from the centralized MAP estimate for Algorithm 1 visually overlapped with the approximate MAP curve, indicating successful convergence to the approximation. Crucially, Algorithm 1 achieved up to **64% less error** than the DRWT algorithm.
- **Scalability:** As the number of agents increased, the estimation error of DRWT grew rapidly, while Algorithm 1 maintained a consistently low sub-optimality. This highlights the scalability advantages of the asynchronous approach.
- **Accuracy of Approximation:** The Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) between the true MAP estimate and the approximate MAP estimate was bounded by $4.4 \cdot 10^{-4}$ across all simulations, confirming that the communication-reducing approximation introduced negligible errors.

#### 4.3. Experimental Results

The algorithm was implemented on the Robotarium, a real-world multi-robot platform, using 4 agents to estimate the trajectories of 4 robots. Agent 1 used GPS, while other agents measured relative positions, inducing communication dependencies. The experiment evaluated the algorithm's robustness to varying communication delays, B, across orders of magnitude ($B \in \{25, 50, 500, 1000, 2000, 2500, 5000\}$).

- **Robustness to Delays:** The experiments demonstrated that Algorithm 1 is robust to significant communication delays. As the sliding window advanced and the problem reinitialized, estimation error spiked, but then consistently decreased exponentially as BCD iterations were performed within each time step.
- **Convergence Speed vs. Delay:** The rate of error decrease within each time window was observed to be faster for smaller values of B, consistent with the theoretical expectation that less outdated information leads to quicker convergence. This confirms the practical utility of the asynchronous approach in environments with varying and substantial communication latencies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in distributed state estimation for multi-robot systems by explicitly addressing the practical challenges of asynchrony in real-world environments. The development of an asynchronous Block Coordinate Descent (BCD) algorithm for trajectory estimation over a sliding window offers several notable implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Robustness and Scalability:** By designing the algorithm to inherently handle asynchronous computations and communications, the work provides a robust solution for multi-robot systems operating under realistic conditions, including variable network delays, heterogeneous hardware, and the "straggler effect." This directly contributes to the scalability of multi-robot deployments, as systems can operate efficiently without being bottlenecked by the slowest link or requiring costly synchronization overheads.
2. **Reduced Communication Overhead:** The derivation of an approximate MAP problem that promotes sparsity in communication is a critical contribution. The demonstrated ability to reduce communication edges by up to 96.9% in an example scenario, transforming an all-to-all communication pattern into a localized one, is crucial for energy-constrained or bandwidth-limited multi-robot systems. This optimization can extend battery life, reduce network congestion, and improve overall system responsiveness.
3. **Strong Theoretical Foundation:** The rigorous proof of exponential convergence under partially asynchronous conditions provides a solid theoretical guarantee for the algorithm's performance. This mathematical validation is important for the adoption of such complex distributed algorithms in safety-critical and high-performance robotic applications.
4. **Improved Estimation Accuracy:** The simulation results demonstrating up to 64% less error compared to a comparable state-of-the-art synchronous algorithm highlight the practical performance benefits of the proposed asynchronous approach. This enhanced accuracy in state estimates can lead to more reliable decision-making, improved control performance, and safer operation for multi-robot fleets.
5. **Applicability Across Domains:** The general formulation for estimating states of an arbitrary time-varying system, rather than being restricted to specific robot types or scenarios, broadens the potential impact. This algorithm could be applied in diverse fields such as autonomous vehicle platooning, robotic exploration and mapping, logistics, and surveillance, where accurate and timely state estimation is paramount for coordinated multi-robot operation.
6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work establishes a robust framework that can serve as a basis for further research. The mention of future work exploring independently selected step sizes, for example, suggests avenues for optimizing convergence rates and adapting to dynamic network conditions, further enhancing the algorithm's utility.

In summary, this research provides a theoretically sound, empirically validated, and practically viable solution for distributed state estimation in multi-robot systems, overcoming a major limitation of synchronous approaches. Its impact lies in enabling more robust, scalable, and accurate multi-robot autonomy in complex, real-world environments.
