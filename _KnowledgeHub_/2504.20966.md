# Softpick: No Attention Sink, No Massive Activations with Rectified Softmax

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at MBZUAI developed Softpick, a rectified, non-sum-to-one normalization function for transformer attention, which eliminates attention sink and massive activations. This new function results in functionally sparse attention maps (over 95% sparsity) and substantially enhances quantization performance at lower bit precisions, outperforming softmax in 2-bit and 3-bit settings.

## Problem

- The standard softmax function in transformer attention leads to "attention sink," where irrelevant tokens (like BOS) receive disproportionately high attention scores.
- Softmax also contributes to "massive activations" in hidden states, presenting extreme outlier values that significantly hinder model quantization and low-precision training.
- Existing solutions for these issues often introduce complexity, additional parameters, or require custom optimizers, lacking a straightforward, universally applicable replacement for softmax.

## Method

- ==Softpick== is introduced as a ==rectified, non-sum-to-one attention normalization function==, defined by applying ReLU to (exp(x) - 1) in the numerator and normalizing by the sum of absolute values of (exp(x) - 1) in the denominator.
- The function's asymmetric design (ReLU in numerator, absolute value in denominator) allows for zero-valued attention scores and enables gradient flow for negative inputs, preventing 'dying heads'.
- Softpick is designed as a ==drop-in replacement for softmax==, maintaining compatibility with optimized kernels like FlashAttention-2, and requiring no extra parameters or specialized optimizers.

## Results

- Softpick successfully eliminated attention sink, achieving a **0%** sink rate across all tested models and thresholds, compared to significant rates (e.g., **68.28%**) for softmax models.
- Massive activations were entirely mitigated, with kurtosis in hidden states drastically reduced (e.g., from **33510** to **340** in 340M models) and activation magnitudes reduced by an order of magnitude.
- Attention maps generated by Softpick exhibited high functional sparsity (**99.34%** for 340M, **95.63%** for 1.8B models), allowing for direct opportunities for sparsity-based optimizations.
- Quantized Softpick models consistently outperformed softmax across various bit precisions and quantization techniques (HQQ, GPTQ, BNB), with particularly large gains at **2-bit** and **3-bit**, indicating enhanced robustness to quantization errors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The rectification (ReLU in numerator) allows for genuine sparsity in attention maps, producing many exact zero scores, while the absolute value in the denominator ensures stable gradient flow even for inputs that would otherwise result in zero attention.
> - Eliminating massive activations directly addresses a core challenge for efficient model quantization, enabling models to retain more accuracy at very low bit precisions (e.g., 2-bit).
> - The inherent sparsity in Softpick's attention mechanisms offers potential for significant computational savings through sparse matrix operations and improved model interpretability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The softmax function has been a foundational component of neural networks, particularly in the attention mechanisms of transformer architectures since its initial adoption in models like Bahdanau et al. (2015) and Vaswani ett al. (2017). It converts a vector of real numbers into a probability distribution, which has been intuitively used to weight values based on query-key "matches." Despite its widespread use due to training stability, a bounded Frobenius norm for gradients, and established expressiveness, recent research has identified several characteristics of softmax that may be detrimental in the context of large language models.

Specifically, two phenomena associated with softmax-based attention have drawn increasing attention: "attention sink" and "massive activations." Attention sink, as characterized by Xiao et al. (2024), refers to the disproportionate allocation of attention scores to semantically weak but frequent tokens, most notably the Beginning of Sentence (BOS) token. While some studies suggest these sinks might mitigate overmixing or act as approximate no-ops (Barbero et al., 2025), their widespread occurrence in trained models (Gu et al., 2024) indicates a strong link to the sum-to-one constraint of softmax.

Concurrently, "massive activations" (Sun et al., 2024) describe the presence of rare but extremely large hidden-state values within transformer layers. These outliers pose significant challenges for model quantization and low-precision training, often necessitating complex algorithms and architectural modifications to manage. Research by Sun et al. (2024) and Gu et al. (2024) connects these massive activations to self-attention and softmax-driven attention sink behavior, motivating efforts to relax strict normalization. Previous attempts to mitigate these issues include removing normalization (which leads to sink reappearance), KV bias variants (which can degrade with depth or be unreliable), softmax+1 relaxations, and gated attention (which adds parameters).

This paper positions its proposed Softpick function as a novel, drop-in replacement for softmax that directly addresses both attention sink and massive activations without introducing additional parameters or complex optimizers. By modifying the softmax function to allow rectified, non-sum-to-one outputs, the work aims to offer a simpler and more effective solution to these identified problems, potentially opening new avenues for quantization, sparsity, and interpretability in transformer models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce "softpick," a new normalization function designed as a direct replacement for softmax within the attention mechanism of transformer models. The core motivation behind developing Softpick stems from a critical re-evaluation of the necessity for attention scores to represent non-zero probabilities that sum to one, a fundamental characteristic of the softmax function that has been identified as a source of undesirable behaviors in modern large language models.

Specifically, the authors are motivated to address two key issues inherent in softmax-based attention:

1. **Elimination of Attention Sink:** The sum-to-one property of softmax forces attention heads to distribute scores across all tokens. This often results in a "sink" behavior, where a significant portion of attention is allocated to semantically irrelevant tokens, such as the initial BOS token, even when other tokens might be more relevant. While attention sinks are not explicitly reported to harm downstream performance, they represent an inefficient allocation of model capacity and can indicate a sub-optimal attention mechanism. Softpick aims to eliminate this behavior by allowing attention scores to be precisely zero, thereby preventing the model from being forced to attend to irrelevant tokens.

2. **Mitigation of Massive Activations:** A direct consequence of attention sink and the sum-to-one normalization is the generation of "massive activations" in hidden states. These are extreme, outlying values that tend to grow with model scale. Massive activations are particularly problematic for quantization and low-precision training, as they necessitate specialized algorithms or workarounds to maintain numerical stability and model performance at reduced bit widths. By preventing attention sink and enabling a sparser, more targeted attention distribution, Softpick seeks to suppress these extreme hidden state activations, thereby facilitating more efficient and robust low-precision model deployment and training.

Beyond addressing these specific problems, the broader motivation for developing Softpick includes:

- **Simplifying Quantization and Low-Precision Training:** By intrinsically managing activation outliers, Softpick aims to remove a significant bottleneck in developing efficient quantized and low-precision transformers, potentially eliminating the need for complex, ad-hoc outlier-handling mechanisms.
- **Enabling Sparsity Optimizations and Pruning:** The ability of Softpick to produce exact zero attention scores opens possibilities for enhancing model efficiency through sparse computation kernels and for more effectively identifying and pruning inactive attention heads.
- **Improving Model Interpretability:** Sparse and concentrated attention maps are hypothesized to be more legible and interpretable, providing clearer insights into how the model processes information.

In essence, the research is motivated by the belief that a rectification and a relaxation of the strict sum-to-one constraint in attention normalization can lead to more efficient, robust, and interpretable transformer models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on the introduction and empirical validation of the Softpick function as an alternative to softmax in transformer attention.

#### 3.1. Softpick Function Definition

The Softpick function, given an input vector x ∈ R^N, is defined as:
`Softpick(x)_i = ReLU(e^x_i - 1) / Σ_j=1^N |e^x_j - 1|`
A numerically safe version is provided for practical implementation:
`Softpick(x)_i = ReLU(e^(x_i - m) - e^(-m)) / (Σ_j=1^N |e^(x_j - m) - e^(-m)| + ϵ)`
where `m` is the maximum value in `x`, `ReLU(x) = max(x, 0)`, and `ϵ` is a small positive constant to prevent division by zero. This function serves as a direct, "drop-in" replacement for softmax in the attention mechanism formula: `Attention(Q, K, V) = Softpick(QK^T/√d_k)V`.

#### 3.2. Design Rationale

The design of Softpick is a deliberate modification of the softmax function, aimed at addressing its limitations while retaining desirable properties:
1. **Starting from Softmax:** The design begins with the standard softmax function, `Softmax(x)_i = e^x_i / Σ_j=1^N e^x_j`, acknowledging its robust training characteristics, such as a well-behaved Jacobian matrix and bounded gradient norms.
2. **Inducing "Null Attention" via Rectification:** To allow for zero-valued outputs and enable sparsity, the term `(e^x_i - 1)` is introduced in the numerator. For inputs where `e^x_i - 1` is negative, the ReLU function rectifies it to zero, effectively inducing "null attention" for those elements. An initial experiment with `ReLU(e^x_i - 1)` in both numerator and denominator was explored, which allowed for zero outputs but led to model divergence due to "dead" attention heads (no gradients for negative inputs).
3. **Maintaining Gradient Flow with Absolute Value in Denominator:** To address the "dead head" issue and ensure gradient flow for negative inputs, the denominator is modified to `Σ_j=1^N |e^x_j - 1|`. The absolute value function ensures that all terms in the sum are positive, preventing the denominator from becoming zero (except in extreme numerical cases handled by `ϵ`) and allowing gradients to propagate even when `(e^x_j - 1)` is negative. This asymmetry between the numerator (rectified) and denominator (absolute value) removes the strict sum-to-one constraint, which is identified as the root cause of attention sink.

#### 3.3. Derivative

The partial derivative of the Softpick function is provided, demonstrating its computability. It involves terms like the Kronecker delta, step function, and sign function, which, while more complex than softmax, is stated to be trivial for implementations that recompute inputs, such as FlashAttention.

#### 3.4. FlashAttention Compatibility

The Softpick function is compatible with the FlashAttention algorithm (Dao et al., 2022; Dao, 2024), including FlashAttention-2. This compatibility is attributed to the multiplicative properties of `ReLU(x)` and `|x|` for positive multipliers, allowing for online, single-pass computation of attention without quadratic memory requirements. Algorithms for Softpick-enabled FlashAttention-2 forward and backward passes are provided in the appendix.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Model Architectures:** Llama-style transformers (pre-norm, RoPE, SwiGLU MLP) were trained from scratch.
- **Model Sizes:** Two scales were investigated: 340M and 1.8B parameters. For each size, a softmax variant and a Softpick variant were trained.
- **Training Data:** Models were trained on subsets of the fineweb-edu dataset (Lozhkov et al., 2024): 52 billion tokens for 340M models and 104 billion tokens for 1.8B models.
- **Computational Resources:** Training was performed on 8xH100 GPUs, taking approximately 18 hours for 340M models and 116 hours for 1.8B models.
- **Evaluation Benchmarks:** Standard downstream tasks were used via the LM Evaluation Harness (Gao et al., 2023): AI2 Reasoning Challenge (ARC-e), Lambada (OpenAI variant), PIQA, and SciQ. Wikitext validation perplexity was also measured.
- **Quantization Benchmarks:** The effects of quantization were evaluated using HQQ (Badri and Shaji, 2023), BitsandBytes (BNB) (Dettmers et al., 2022, 2023), and GPTQ (Frantar et al., 2022) at 2, 3, 4, and 8-bit precisions.
- **Analysis Metrics:**
 - **Attention Maps:** Visual inspection and quantitative measurement of sparsity (percentage of exact zeros in the causal attention matrix).
 - **Attention Sink Rate:** Calculated as the percentage of heads with an average attention score above a threshold `ϵ_s` (0.2 or 0.3) for the first token, following Gu et al. (2024).
 - **Hidden State Activations:** Kurtosis, minimum, and maximum values of hidden state outputs across layers.
 - **Scalability:** Passkey retrieval task (Lu et al., 2024) for long-context evaluation, and analysis of "dead attention heads" (heads with maximum absolute output near zero) across training.
- **Comparative Studies:** Short training runs (10k steps) were conducted on 340M models to compare Softpick against other sink-mitigation/sink-free attention variants (e.g., Gated Attention, Softmax+1, Rectified-Only Softmax) in terms of sink rate, activation statistics, and sparsity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate several distinct behaviors and performance characteristics of Softpick compared to the traditional softmax function.

#### 4.1. Training Loss and Gradients

For the 340M models, Softpick exhibited a training loss curve that closely tracked the softmax baseline, with a minimal final training loss gap of 0.004. This suggests Softpick maintains similar training stability at this scale. However, at the 1.8B scale, a larger gap of 0.12 in training loss was observed, indicating a potential divergence in behavior or scalability issues with the current hyperparameters. The gradient norm of Softpick models initially showed higher magnitudes than softmax but decreased to stay below softmax towards the end of training. Gradient clipping (max norm 1.0) was applied, and no training instability was reported due to these gradient magnitudes.

#### 4.2. Benchmarks

On standard downstream tasks, the 340M Softpick model achieved performance comparable to, or slightly better than, the softmax baseline. For instance, on ARC Easy (Acc Norm), Softpick showed a +0.12 increase, and on SciQ (Acc Norm), a +2.40 increase. However, at the 1.8B scale, Softpick generally underperformed softmax across most benchmarks. For example, on ARC Easy (Acc Norm), Softpick showed a -5.17 decrease, and Lambada perplexity increased by +4.43, suggesting that Softpick, with the same hyperparameters, did not scale as effectively as softmax to larger model sizes in terms of raw benchmark performance.

#### 4.3. Quantization

Softpick demonstrated a notable advantage in quantized settings. For the 340M models, Softpick consistently outperformed softmax when quantized using HQQ, GPTQ, and BitsandBytes (BNB) across 2, 3, 4, and 8-bit precisions. The performance advantage was particularly pronounced at lower bit precisions, where quantization typically leads to significant accuracy degradation for softmax models. For the 1.8B models, while the base Softpick model had a performance gap compared to softmax, this gap tended to narrow at lower bit precisions in the quantized settings.

#### 4.4. Attention Maps and Sparsity

Softpick attention maps exhibited distinct characteristics:
- **Sparsity:** Softpick produced functionally sparse attention maps with many exact zero scores, concentrating attention on specific tokens or regions. The measured sparsity rates were 99.34% for the 340M model and 95.63% for the 1.8B model in the causal attention matrix. In contrast, softmax attention maps are theoretically dense, with any observed zeros typically resulting from numerical underflow.
- **Concentrated Attention:** While some heads still attended broadly, many Softpick heads showed highly focused attention patterns, or became completely inactive when not needed.

#### 4.5. Attention Sink Elimination

A key finding is the complete elimination of attention sink behavior with Softpick. The sink rate metric, which quantifies the proportion of heads disproportionately attending to the first token, was 0% for Softpick models at both 340M and 1.8B scales, and across both `ϵ_s` thresholds (0.2 and 0.3). This contrasts with softmax models, which exhibited sink rates up to 68.28% (340M, `ϵ_s`=0.2). Visual inspection of attention maps corroborated this, showing Softpick heads either specifically attending to relevant tokens or being entirely shut off, rather than sinking to the BOS token.

#### 4.6. Massive Activations Suppression

Softpick significantly suppressed massive activations in hidden states. For the 340M model, the kurtosis of hidden state activations was reduced by approximately a hundred-fold (from 33,510.81 for softmax to 340.96 for Softpick). The minimum and maximum hidden state values were also reduced by an order of magnitude. This effect was consistent across layers, with the exception of the final layer, where activations sometimes matched softmax, potentially due to the projection back into the vocabulary space. Similar, though less dramatic, reductions were observed for the 1.8B model.

#### 4.7. Comparison with Other Sink-Free Methods

In comparison with other sink-mitigation/sink-free attention variants, Softpick (and its scaled variant) showed the most robust suppression of sinks, achieving near-zero sink rates and substantial reductions in activation extremes. It also yielded genuinely sparse attention, a characteristic not consistently observed in other methods. Other variants offered partial mitigation but generally maintained denser attention maps or higher activation outliers.

#### 4.8. Scalability and Limitations

- **Long Context and Underscoring:** Softpick models did not outperform softmax on the passkey retrieval task for long contexts. Performance degraded with increasing sequence length, similar to softmax. This was hypothesized to be due to an "underscoring" effect, where Softpick's normalization, in sparse contexts, might reduce the magnitude of relevant token scores, weakening value signals.
- **Dead Attention Heads:** Analysis revealed that Softpick models, especially the larger 1.8B variant, exhibited a higher percentage of "dead heads" (attention heads that output near-zero values for a large proportion of tokens) during training. While the percentage decreased over training, the 1.8B model still had 20.90% dead heads at the end. Masking these dead heads at inference time was observed to damage downstream performance, particularly for the 340M model, suggesting a reduction in effective model capacity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Softpick carries several implications for the development and deployment of transformer models:

1. **Enhanced Quantization and Low-Precision Training:** The primary significance of Softpick lies in its demonstrated ability to eliminate attention sinks and suppress massive activations. These phenomena are identified as key impediments to effective model quantization and low-precision training. By directly addressing the root causes of these outliers, Softpick has the potential to simplify quantization pipelines, enabling models to retain higher accuracy at aggressive lower bit precisions (e.g., 2-bit, 3-bit) without requiring complex outlier-handling mechanisms like double quantization, activation smoothing, or Hadamard transforms. This could lead to more efficient and accessible deployment of large language models on resource-constrained hardware.

2. **Improved Model Efficiency and Pruning:** The inherent sparsity induced by Softpick in attention maps (demonstrated by a high percentage of exact zeros) suggests avenues for inference acceleration. Sparse kernels could be leveraged to reduce unnecessary computation in the attention-value product, leading to faster inference speeds. Furthermore, this clear sparsity could facilitate more effective pruning strategies. Heads that consistently produce zero or near-zero attention scores (referred to as "dead" or "dormant" heads) become easier to identify and potentially remove without significant performance degradation, thereby reducing model size and computational load. This could extend previous work on active-dormant heads and head pruning.

3. **Increased Interpretability:** The visually legible and sparse attention maps generated by Softpick contribute to a clearer understanding of how attention mechanisms operate. Concentrated attention scores on specific, relevant tokens, surrounded by explicit zeros, provide a less ambiguous view of token dependencies compared to dense, diffused softmax maps. This improved interpretability could benefit analysis techniques, such as attention rollout, and foster deeper insights into model decision-making.

4. **Broader Applicability:** Since attention sinks and activation outliers are not exclusive to language models, the principles and benefits of Softpick could extend to other transformer-based architectures in various domains, including vision, video, and multimodal processing, where similar artifacts have been observed. This suggests Softpick could be a foundational improvement applicable across the broader field of deep learning.

5. **Future Research Directions:** While Softpick addresses key limitations of softmax, the paper also highlights open challenges, particularly regarding scalability to significantly larger parameter counts (beyond 1.8B) and maintaining performance in long-context retrieval tasks. The observation of "dead attention heads" in larger Softpick models, along with the "underscoring" hypothesis for long contexts, points to crucial areas for future investigation to fully unlock Softpick's potential. Further hyperparameter optimization and longer training horizons might be necessary to resolve these scaling limitations.
