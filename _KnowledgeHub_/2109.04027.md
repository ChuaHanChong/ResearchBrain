# Taxim

> [!summary] Summary
> **Taxim** is a realistic, high-speed simulation model for vision-based ==GelSight== tactile sensors. It simulates the optical response to elastomer deformation with an ==example-based polynomial look-up table== and reproduces marker-field motion via linear elastic deformation theory, calibrated from fewer than 100 real data points and runnable online on CPU.

## Problem

- Simulating tactile sensors realistically and efficiently has been a long-standing challenge, limiting large-scale data collection and sim-to-real transfer for touch-based manipulation.
- Prior GelSight simulators do not jointly model the optical response and the surface-marker motion field that arises from elastomer stretch.

## Method

- Proposes an ==example-based== optical simulation: a polynomial look-up table maps deformed contact geometry to per-pixel intensity captured by the embedded camera.
- Simulates surface-marker motion using ==linear elastic deformation theory== and the superposition principle, the first framework to combine marker-field and optical simulation.
- Calibrates the model from **fewer than 100 data points** off a real sensor, so it migrates easily to other GelSight variants.

## Results

- Achieves the **lowest pixel-wise intensity error** versus prior optical-simulation work.
- Runs **online on CPU**, making it practical for large-scale tactile data generation and sim-to-real pipelines.

## Takeaways

- A cheap-to-calibrate, CPU-fast tactile simulator that bridges the visuo-tactile sim-to-real gap; a common baseline for differentiable / learned tactile simulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Simulation plays a role in robotics by enabling rapid data generation, system verification, and reduced reliance on physical hardware, thereby lowering costs and mitigating risks associated with real-world experimentation. Established simulation frameworks, such as Gazebo, PyBullet, MuJoCo, Drake, SOFA, and NVIDIA Isaac Gym, facilitate the simulation of rigid-body dynamics, soft-body interactions, and vision and laser sensors. However, these platforms typically lack integrated high-fidelity tactile sensing capabilities.

Recent advancements in vision-based tactile sensors, including GelSight, TacTip, and DIGIT, have introduced the ability to capture high-resolution tactile information. These sensors commonly utilize a soft elastomer (gelpad) as the contact interface, with embedded optical structures or marker arrays to infer contact geometry, forces, and torques from the elastomer's deformation. The challenge in simulating these sensors lies in accurately modeling both the mechanical response of the soft material and the optical phenomena that translate this deformation into sensor readings, including illumination changes and marker movements.

Prior research has addressed components of vision-based tactile sensor simulation. Efforts in mechanical simulation have employed finite element methods (FEM), mass-spring models, particle systems, or learning-based approaches to model elastomer deformation. Optical simulation has utilized physics-based models such as Phong's reflection model or ray tracing to render light paths and reflections. Some work has integrated optical simulation with physics engines or focused on marker motion fields. However, existing simulation models frequently face limitations: they can be computationally intensive, difficult to generalize to different sensor designs or environmental conditions, may not accurately capture the intrinsic noise characteristics of real sensors, or provide only partial simulation capabilities (e.g., optical without marker motion, or vice versa). For instance, physics-based rendering models require detailed knowledge of light sources and material properties, which can be complex to obtain and simulate efficiently. Data-driven methods have been proposed, but often focus on specific aspects or require extensive training data.

Taxim positions itself within this landscape by offering an integrated, example-based simulation model for GelSight-type sensors that aims to overcome the identified limitations. It combines optical and marker motion field simulation within a computationally efficient framework that is calibrated using a relatively small dataset from a real sensor. This approach seeks to provide a realistic simulation that accounts for sensor-specific characteristics and intrinsic noise, making it suitable for a broader range of robotic applications where tactile feedback is critical, and facilitating sim-to-real transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop Taxim, a simulation model designed to provide a realistic and high-speed representation of the output from GelSight vision-based tactile sensors. This overarching goal is driven by several more specific objectives and motivations:

- **Objective 1: Comprehensive Tactile Simulation**: To create a simulation framework that integrates both the optical response of the GelSight sensor (inferring contact geometry and illumination changes) and the motion of surface markers (inferring forces and torques). Prior work often addressed these aspects separately or partially.
- **Objective 2: Realism and Intrinsic Noise Incorporation**: To produce simulated sensor outputs that closely resemble those of real GelSight sensors, including the ability to model the intrinsic noise and specific optical characteristics of the physical sensor. Physics-based models often struggle with replicating these sensor-specific nuances and noise patterns.
- **Objective 3: Computational Efficiency**: To develop a simulation model that is computationally lightweight and capable of running in real-time on standard CPU hardware. This addresses the high computational demands often associated with detailed physics-based simulations, which can hinder their application in online robotic control and large-scale data generation.
- **Objective 4: Ease of Calibration and Generalization**: To design a model that requires minimal calibration data (e.g., less than 100 data points) from a real sensor. This objective aims to simplify the setup process and enable easy migration of the simulation model to other GelSight sensor variations or similar vision-based tactile sensors without extensive re-engineering or data collection.
- **Objective 5: Integration with Robotic Simulation Environments**: To provide a tactile simulation tool that can be incorporated into existing robot simulation engines, thereby offering a more complete environment for developing and testing tactile-based robotic perception and manipulation tasks.

The motivation for these objectives stems from the recognized gap in current robotics simulation frameworks, which largely lack integrated, high-fidelity tactile sensing. Without realistic tactile simulation, roboticists are limited in their ability to:
- Generate large, diverse datasets for training machine learning models for tactile perception.
- Verify control strategies and manipulation algorithms that rely on touch feedback.
- Conduct experiments without the cost, time, and potential damage associated with physical hardware.
- Explore complex human-robot interaction scenarios that necessitate nuanced tactile responses.

By achieving these objectives, Taxim aims to facilitate advancements in robot learning, control, and interaction by providing a robust, accessible, and realistic tactile simulation capability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Taxim employs an example-based methodology to simulate both the optical response and marker motion field of GelSight tactile sensors. The overall pipeline involves calibrating the simulation model with data collected from a real sensor and then applying these calibrated models to generate simulated tactile images and marker displacements.

**A. Optical Simulation**
The optical simulation component aims to map contact geometries to pixel intensities in the tactile images. It is based on an example-based photometric stereo approach, which typically derives surface shape from images captured under varying illumination without explicit knowledge of light sources.

1. **Polynomial Table Mapping**: The core of the optical simulation is a non-linear mapping function that addresses the complex lighting conditions within a GelSight sensor, where LEDs are in close proximity to the gelpad surface, leading to non-parallel and non-uniform illumination. Instead of a simple linear reflection function, a second-order polynomial function is used to approximate the reflection, expressed as $I = \sum_l f_l(\mathbf{n}, x, y)$, where $I$ is pixel intensity, $\mathbf{n}$ is the surface normal, and $(x, y)$ are the pixel coordinates. The function $f_l$ includes polynomial terms of $x$ and $y$ (e.g., $x^2, y^2, xy, x, y, 1$) with learnable coefficients $w_l$ that represent parameters for each light source and color channel.
2. **Calibration**: Calibration involves fitting the parameters of this polynomial table using real data. A small ball with a known radius is pressed onto the gelpad surface at various locations. For each contact, the surface normal at points within the circular contact area is calculated based on the ball's known geometry. Corresponding pixel intensities from the tactile images are collected. These intensity-shape-location pairs ($I_p, \mathbf{n}_p, x_p, y_p$) are then used in a least squares optimization to solve for the coefficients $w_l$. The 3D surface normal vectors are discretized into a 125x125 table, and invalid values are filled via interpolation. This process requires less than 100 contact examples.
3. **Simulation**:
 - **Collision Detection**: When a virtual object contacts the simulated gelpad, a collision is detected.
 - **Deformation Approximation**: The local shape of the contact area is constructed from the object's geometry, and the non-contact areas retain the gelpad's original shape. To simulate the soft body deformation, boundaries between contact and non-contact areas are smoothed using pyramid Gaussian kernels, preserving fine textures within the contact region.
 - **Image Synthesis**: From the resulting height map, normal vectors for each point are extracted. These normal vectors and their 2D locations are then input into the calibrated polynomial table to predict pixel intensity values for the simulated tactile image.

**B. Shadow Simulation**
Shadows, caused by the occlusion of LED light by the deformed gelpad, are modeled as an additional factor influencing pixel intensity.

1. **Unit Shadow Concept**: The method approximates complex object geometries as a collection of "unit pins." A "unit" shadow is defined as the shadow cast by a single standing pin under specific lighting.
2. **Accumulation**: Due to the independent nature of light beams (without inter-reflection considerations), shadows are assumed to accumulate linearly.
3. **Calibration**: Shadow masks are collected by indenting a 1mm-diameter pin normally onto the gelpad at different depths. For each depth, a set of shadow masks corresponding to the three dominant light sources (red, green, blue LEDs) is extracted.
4. **Simulation**: In a general contact scenario, if a point within the contact area is higher than its neighbors, a calibrated shadow mask is applied to all three color channels at that point.

**C. Marker Motion Field Simulation**
This component simulates the planar movement of markers on the gelpad surface due to its elastic deformation under normal and shear loads.

1. **Underlying Principles**: The simulation relies on the linear displacement relationship and the superposition principle. The gelpad surface is represented as a dense mesh of nodes.
 - **Linear Displacement Relationship**: Assumes that the displacement of any node $n_j$ can be linearly influenced by the displacement of another node $n_i$ via a 3x3 tensor $T_{n_i n_j}$.
 - **Superposition Principle**: States that the total displacement of any node is the sum of influences from all "active" nodes (those in direct contact with the object and experiencing external loads).
2. **Node Classification**: Nodes are categorized as "active" (in contact, subject to external and internal elastic forces) or "passive" (not in contact, only subject to internal elastic forces).
3. **Displacement Calculation**:
 - **Virtual Displacements**: Before applying the superposition principle, initial displacements of active nodes (based on external loads) are adjusted to "virtual displacements." This step accounts for the mutual influence among active nodes, where their displacements are not solely determined by external loads but also by the elastic constraints from other active nodes. A matrix equation ($\mathbf{u}_k[z] = M_z \mathbf{\tilde{u}}_k[z]$) is solved via matrix inversion to find these virtual displacements for each directional component (x, y, z).
 - **Resultant Displacements**: The final displacement of any node (active or passive) is then calculated by summing the influences of the active nodes' *virtual* displacements using the calibrated tensors $T_{k_i n_j}$.
4. **Calibration**: The tensors $T_{k_i n_j}$, which encode the gelpad's physical properties, are pre-measured using Finite Element Method (FEM) software (ANSYS). A dense mesh of the gelpad is generated, and its deformation under a unit load on a single node is simulated. To fully calibrate the 3x3 tensors, simulations are conducted for active node motions in z-direction only, z-direction and x-direction, and z-direction and y-direction. Least squares is then used to solve for all $T$ tensors from these unit cases. The calibration references a mesh layer 0.5mm below the top surface, where markers are typically printed.
5. **Simulation**: This occurs in three steps: applying initial displacements to active nodes, calculating virtual displacements for active nodes using the solved matrix, and then computing the resultant displacements for all nodes (active and passive) using the superposition principle with the virtual displacements.

The entire simulation framework is calibrated using a relatively small set of real sensor data for the optical and shadow models, combined with FEM-derived parameters for the marker motion, aiming for a balance between realism, computational efficiency, and ease of deployment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Taxim involved comparing its simulated outputs with data from real GelSight sensors and existing simulation methods across various metrics.

**A. Optical Simulation**
- **Calibration Data**: The optical model was calibrated using 50 contact points with a 4mm-diameter spherical indenter, and the shadow model with 10 contact points using a 1mm-diameter pin indenter. This process was reported to be straightforward, taking less than one hour.
- **Quantitative Comparison**: Taxim's optical simulation was compared against three prior methods: a physics-based model, TACTO, and Phong's model. Evaluated on pixel-wise image similarity metrics (L1 error, Mean Squared Error (MSE), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), and Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR) on 400x400 cropped images), Taxim demonstrated superior performance:
 - L1 error: 5.565 (lowest, indicating best similarity)
 - MSE: 58.358 (lowest)
 - SSIM: 0.882 (highest)
 - PSNR: 30.974 (highest)
 This indicates a closer resemblance of Taxim's simulated images to real sensor data compared to the other methods.
- **Robustness to Indentation Variations**: The optical simulation performed consistently across different indentation depths and locations, though MSE errors increased slightly when indentations were farther from the center or deeper.
- **Fine Texture Simulation**: The model demonstrated the capability to simulate contacts with objects possessing fine textures.
- **Generalizability Across Sensors and Objects**: Taxim was shown to work effectively on four different GelSight sensors (despite manufacturing variations leading to different appearances in real images) and also applied successfully to a DIGIT sensor. It was also tested with various objects from the Google Scan dataset.
- **Speed Test**: On a CPU (AMD Ryzen Threadripper 2950X), Taxim achieved an average frequency of 18.1 frames per second (fps) without shadows and 9.6 fps with shadows. This significantly outperformed TACTO (1.9 fps), Phong's model (3.8 fps), and the physics-based model (0.1 fps), indicating online operational capability.

**B. Marker Motion Field Simulation**
- **Comparison with FEM Simulation**: The dense mesh vertex displacements simulated by Taxim were compared to those generated by FEM simulation (ANSYS). The average interpolated pixel-wise L1 errors across the gelpad surface were:
 - X-axis: 3.58 × 10⁻³ mm
 - Y-axis: 3.32 × 10⁻³ mm
 - Z-axis: 5.43 × 10⁻³ mm
 - XY (surface): 5.40 × 10⁻³ mm
 These low errors suggest a high degree of agreement between Taxim's model and the high-fidelity FEM simulation for dense displacement maps.
- **Comparison with Real Data and FEM Simulation**: Marker motion magnitudes and angular errors were compared between real data, FEM simulation, and Taxim's simulation:
 - Magnitude L1 errors: 1.00 × 10⁻² mm (real & FEM), 1.02 × 10⁻² mm (real & Taxim), 3.96 × 10⁻³ mm (FEM & Taxim).
 - Weighted angular L1 errors: 12.94° (real & FEM), 14.57° (real & Taxim), 4.89° (FEM & Taxim).
 The results indicate that Taxim's marker motion simulation closely approximates the FEM model. However, a notable gap remains between both simulation models and real sensor data. The authors attributed this discrepancy to manufacturing inconsistencies in real gelpads, noise in real marker extraction and tracking, and the simulation's inability to model partial slip, which is common in real contact scenarios.
- **Speed Testing**: The dense marker motion field simulation averaged 9.22 seconds on a CPU, significantly faster than typical FEM simulations in ANSYS, which required 2 to 4 hours.

**C. Integrated Simulation**
The paper also presented results demonstrating the combined output of optical and marker motion simulations, showing both the visual tactile image and the superimposed marker displacement fields.

In summary, Taxim demonstrates improved realism and computational efficiency for GelSight tactile sensor simulation, particularly in optical image generation, and provides a marker motion field that closely matches FEM models, despite observed discrepancies when compared to real sensor data, which are attributed to real-world complexities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Taxim contributes to the field of robotics by addressing the challenge of simulating vision-based tactile sensors with a comprehensive and efficient approach. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Integrated and Comprehensive Tactile Sensing Simulation**: Taxim is presented as the first simulation framework that integrates both the optical response (for geometry measurement) and the marker motion field simulation (for force/torque measurement) for GelSight-type sensors. This holistic approach provides a more complete representation of tactile sensor output than prior models, which often focused on these components in isolation. This integration is crucial for robotic tasks that require simultaneous perception of contact geometry and applied forces.
- **Enhanced Realism and Fidelity**: By employing an example-based method and calibrating with real sensor data, Taxim can implicitly incorporate the unique illumination characteristics, material properties, and intrinsic noise of a specific GelSight sensor. This data-driven realism, particularly highlighted by its superior performance in pixel-wise image similarity metrics compared to physics-based and other data-driven models, can lead to more accurate sim-to-real transfer in robot learning and control applications.
- **Computational Efficiency**: The ability of Taxim's optical simulation to run online on a CPU (9.6-18.1 fps) and its marker motion simulation to be significantly faster than full FEM analysis represents a practical advantage. This efficiency enables the generation of large synthetic datasets, rapid iteration during algorithm development, and potentially real-time simulation within closed-loop control systems, which is often unfeasible with computationally intensive physics-based models.
- **Accessibility and Generalizability**: The methodology requires minimal calibration data (less than 100 contact points), simplifying the setup process for new sensors or sensor variations. This ease of calibration and migration makes Taxim a more accessible tool for researchers and developers working with different GelSight or similar vision-based tactile sensors (e.g., DIGIT).
- **Facilitating Robotic Research and Development**: By providing a realistic, efficient, and integrated tactile simulation, Taxim offers a valuable tool for various areas of robotic research:
 - **Robot Learning**: Enabling the generation of diverse, labeled datasets for training deep learning models for tactile perception, object recognition, slip detection, and grasp stability prediction, reducing the need for costly and time-consuming real-world data collection.
 - **Manipulation and Control**: Allowing roboticists to design, test, and refine manipulation strategies that rely on high-resolution tactile feedback in a simulated environment before deployment on physical robots.
 - **Sim-to-Real Transfer**: The model's realism is intended to bridge the "sim-to-real gap," making policies learned in simulation more directly applicable to real-world robotic systems.
- **Foundation for Future Work**: The current work lays a foundation for future extensions, such as investigating dynamic contact scenarios (e.g., slip modeling) and further optimizing performance through GPU acceleration. This indicates its potential as a continually evolving platform for tactile robotics research.

In conclusion, Taxim provides a practical and effective solution for simulating an important class of tactile sensors, offering a balance of realism, speed, and usability that can accelerate advancements in robot perception, control, and learning.
