# Copper-Policy: Focus on the Representation for Robust Robot Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Copper-Policy is a World Action Model that learns a compact World representation jointly with its policy. The representation is conditioned on the task instruction. Instead of generating pixels or video latents, it uses flow matching to predict future observation embeddings, with targets supplied by an EMA copy of its own encoder. An asymmetric interface gives action decoding extra current-frame detail. The 2B model trains about 6x faster than Fast-WAM and reaches 80.85% on LIBERO-Plus and 96.3% on real robots.

## Problem

- Generative WAMs tie action learning to explicit video or latent-video generation, which carries a heavy training and inference cost, even though co-training gains persist without generating anything at test time.
- Latent-prediction WAMs such as LiLa-WAM and StageWAM predict into frozen DINOv3 or V-JEPA2 spaces, so the target space never co-evolves with the policy that consumes it.
- Pretrained video-generation latents keep appearance detail that only visual synthesis needs, while the control-oriented future targets in FLARE and WoG are pretrained or staged rather than learned jointly with the policy.

## Method

- ==Online-EMA compactor pair==: a trainable compactor ==cross-attends== from frozen V-JEPA features to a T5 task embedding, producing 28-36 compact tokens. A ==BYOL-style EMA copy== supplies stop-gradient future targets.
- ==Temporal joint-embedding prediction==: a ==flow-matching== Future Expert predicts compact future embeddings over the action horizon. It shares a ==mixture-of-transformers== joint attention stack with the Action Expert and is never rolled out at deployment.
- ==Asymmetric prediction-control interface==: a ==structured attention mask== limits future queries to the compact World tokens. The Action Expert also reads grid-sampled current-frame tokens and robot state, but never the predicted futures.

## Results

- Reaches **97.25%** on LIBERO and **80.85%** on LIBERO-Plus, beating VLA-JEPA (**77.9%**) and Fast-WAM (**51.5%**). Only Cosmos-Policy (**82.2%**) and the embodied-pretrained pi0.5 (**85.7%**) score higher.
- Leads methods without embodied pretraining on RoboTwin with a **41.91%** average, but scores only **12.98%** under scene randomization versus **46.00%** for pi0.5. Real-robot average is **96.3** versus **94.3** for pi0.5.
- Removing joint-embedding prediction drops the real-robot average from **96.3 to 43.3** and raises rank-8 overlap between perturbation and temporal-change subspaces from **0.187 to 0.798**. Training is **about 6x** faster than Fast-WAM.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A WAM's control benefit can come from the representation its predictive objective shapes rather than from generated futures, so cheap embedding-space prediction can replace video generation.
> - An unshaped latent pathway can be worse than none: World input without predictive shaping did worse than removing that pathway entirely, on both RoboTwin and the real robot.
> - A compact, temporally stable representation cannot replace instantaneous spatial detail. Precise contact-rich control needs a separate current-frame channel; dropping it collapsed real-robot success to 20.7.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot must use camera views and an instruction to fold a towel, place a banana, or stack bowls. It needs to understand what matters to the task without throwing away the detail it needs to act. Pixel- or video-level prediction makes training sequences longer, yet a policy still needs fine visual detail to execute precise movements. Copper-Policy takes a different route. It trains a compact, task-conditioned representation of how the scene is changing, and it gives action generation a separate route to current-frame visual detail. Across three real-robot tasks with 50 trials per task, it reaches an average task score of 96.3, versus 94.3 for embodied-pretrained π₀.₅. Full success scores 1, half success 0.5, and failure 0, with partial credit allowed only for towel folding.

Put Banana: pick-and-place across seeded object placements, for which the project page reports a score of 96. This is an illustrative rollout.

### 2. Predicting a representation, not a movie

Copper-Policy predicts future observations in an embedding space instead of reconstructing pixels, in the style of JEPA. An online compactor turns the current camera views into a small, task-conditioned World representation. An exponential-moving-average (EMA) copy of the compactor supplies stop-gradient targets from future observations. The Future Expert predicts those compact targets using conditional flow matching.

A separate current-frame pathway makes spatial visual tokens for the Action Expert. The Action Expert also receives robot state and decodes an action chunk. The prediction and action objectives train the representation together.

At deployment, no future tokens are instantiated. The Future Expert still runs once as a context encoder, and the Action Expert then decodes actions over 10 flow steps.

This builds on [[2603.16666|Fast-WAM]], which found that video co-training can help even when the model skips explicit future generation at deployment. Copper-Policy changes the prediction target. Fast-WAM predicts detailed video latents into a target space that is not learned with the action policy. Copper-Policy instead predicts a compact representation that prediction and action training shape jointly.

### 3. What the ablations and probes show

On the real robot, removing joint-embedding prediction drops the average from 96.3 to 43.3. The drop is uneven across tasks: the ablated variant scores 4 on towel, 26 on banana, and 100 on bowls. Removing the World input and Future Expert, a separate comparison, gives 84.7. These ablations support the value of predictive shaping in the tested setup. They do not establish that it is the only cause of the gains.

The representation probes used 100 RoboTwin episodes. At rank 8, the overlap between the perturbation and temporal subspaces is 0.187 with Copper-Policy and 0.798 without joint-embedding prediction. A random subspace gives a reference value of 0.021. Even so, perturbation identity remains linearly decodable from the representation, with balanced accuracy of 0.998. The authors' interpretation is that the objective reorganizes nuisance information rather than deleting it. These diagnostics on synthetic visual perturbations are correlational, not a direct proof that the geometry causes higher task success.

The two input channels complement each other. Linear action-prediction R² is 0.802 from the World representation alone, 0.910 from current-frame tokens alone, and 0.927 from both. Removing current-frame visual features drops the real-robot average to 20.7.

### 4. Where the result stops

On LIBERO-Plus, Copper-Policy scores 80.85% task-weighted success over 10,030 perturbed tasks. That is above several embodied-pretrained VLA baselines but below Cosmos-Policy (82.2%) and π₀.₅ (85.7%). Language is a notable weakness at 67.34%.

On RoboTwin, the average of 41.91% is the highest among the compared methods without embodied pretraining. Under joint randomization, however, Copper-Policy's 12.98% is far below π₀.₅'s 46.00%.

Efficiency is a training claim. The authors report about 6× faster training than Fast-WAM in a matched-batch A100 comparison, and the paper calls this cross-method cost comparison operational, not a controlled systems benchmark. End-to-end inference latency is about 85 ms, compared with 80 ms for Fast-WAM and 75 ms for π₀.₅.

The authors' supported interpretation is that prediction and action training jointly organize a compact representation, while current-frame detail remains available for control. The real-robot result is promising evidence on these three tasks, not evidence of broad superiority across robots or manipulation settings.
