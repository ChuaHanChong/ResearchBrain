# RoboDreamer: Anticipatory Humanoid Locomotion with Predictive State-Space Models

> [!summary] Summary
> RoboDreamer is a two-stage teacher-student framework for humanoid motion tracking on Unitree G1. A PPO teacher with privileged input and 35-frame history trains under a next-observation consistency loss; a student is distilled by DAgger under randomized continuous masking of recent observations. The same mask tokens are reused at inference for action refinement and chunking, cutting tracking error below MLP and Transformer baselines at 4.4 ms latency.

## Problem

- MLP-based humanoid trackers dominate the field but reason poorly over time, so a dropped or delayed observation directly perturbs their action prediction.
- Transformer trackers model longer context but self-attention costs grow quadratically with history length, hindering real-time deployment on 50 Hz humanoid control loops.
- Existing Mamba controllers merely swap the backbone; efficient recurrence alone never teaches a policy to infer absent states or extrapolate near-future dynamics.

## Method

- Stage I trains a ==Mamba== teacher by ==PPO== on 35-frame proprioception plus privileged state, with a dual-head design whose observation head minimizes a next-observation ==consistency loss==.
- Stage II distills a privilege-free student via ==DAgger== while randomized continuous ==masking== replaces the trailing 1-5 observations with learnable mask tokens at probability sampled uniformly up to 0.5.
- Two inference modes reuse mask semantics: explicit ==action chunking== appends k mask tokens for k+1 actions, implicit refinement averages the predicted triad and executes only the current action.

## Results

- On LAFAN1 in IsaacLab it reaches **42.87 mm** keypoint error versus **43.39** for BeyondMimic and **47.32** for GMT; MuJoCo transfer keeps the lead at **57.11**.
- Under matched training, Mamba scores **146.72** on FineDance against **163.35** for MLP and **149.79** for Transformer, at **4.4 ms** latency versus the Transformer's **9.8 ms**.
- Ablations favor mask probability **0.5** (**42.87** vs **58.23** at **0.3**) and history **35**; consistency loss helps most at chunk size **4**, **132.21** versus **158.21**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Masking as a training objective, not just augmentation, converts a reactive controller into one that inpaints missing state from history.
> - Attribute gains carefully: matched-parameter comparison credits masked distillation with much of the robustness, leaving the state-space backbone an incremental tracking and latency edge.
> - Open-loop chunking has a hard physical ceiling; fusing adjacent predictions while replanning every step beats executing longer chunks, which drift past stability margins.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Humanoid locomotion and motion tracking

Humanoid control has historically been approached through model-based whole-body control, reinforcement learning, imitation learning, and motion tracking. Model-based approaches can provide accurate control when robot dynamics, contacts, and system parameters are well characterized, but they require extensive modeling and can be sensitive to unmodeled effects. Reinforcement learning reduces dependence on analytical models and has enabled behaviors such as jumping, recovery, and locomotion over challenging terrain. However, it often requires carefully designed rewards and may not naturally produce coordinated whole-body motion.

Motion tracking based on human demonstrations has become an important alternative. Rather than specifying each behavior through task-specific rewards, these methods use reference trajectories as dense supervision. Systems such as DeepMimic, OmniH2O, ExBody2, GMT, BeyondMimic, RoboPerform, and related methods have addressed increasingly broad motion repertoires, teleoperation, expressive control, and sim-to-real transfer.

#### 1.2. Temporal modeling for control

Many humanoid trackers use multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), which are computationally efficient but generally have limited mechanisms for processing long temporal histories. If recent observations are missing or delayed, an MLP may directly map incomplete information to an unstable action. Transformers provide more explicit sequence modeling, but self-attention has quadratic complexity with respect to history length, creating a latency and computation problem for 50 Hz control.

Selective state-space models, particularly Mamba, have recently been applied to locomotion and humanoid control. They provide recurrent temporal state updates and linear sequence complexity. RoboDreamer builds on this direction but argues that changing the backbone alone does not teach a controller how to reconstruct missing observations. Its central contribution is therefore a masked predictive training framework, with Mamba used as the temporal substrate.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to develop a humanoid motion-tracking policy that remains stable when recent proprioceptive observations are unavailable, delayed, or corrupted. The authors identify several practical causes of imperfect sensing, including sensor dropouts, communication latency, packet loss, and transient observation corruption.

The work is motivated by a distinction between reactive control and anticipatory control. A reactive policy maps the currently available observation history to an action, but it is not explicitly trained to infer what happened during a missing interval. RoboDreamer instead seeks to make the policy infer the current physical state from earlier observations and to predict local future evolution.

The paper pursues four related goals:

1. **Robust state inference under observation masking.** The policy should maintain coherent control when the most recent one to five observations are hidden.
2. **Predictive latent representations.** The internal representation should contain information about near-future physical state, rather than only information needed for immediate action prediction.
3. **Efficient temporal processing.** The controller should use a relatively long history without incurring the latency associated with a Transformer.
4. **Reuse of predictive capability at inference.** The same masking mechanism should support either short action chunks or smoother closed-loop action refinement.

The authors also aim to separate the effects of the training objective from those of the architecture. Matched experiments with MLP, Transformer, GRU, LSTM, and Mamba backbones are used to test whether the reported gains arise primarily from masking and distillation, from Mamba, or from both.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Problem formulation and policy architecture

Humanoid locomotion is formulated as a partially observable Markov decision process. The policy receives a sequence of proprioceptive and motion-reference observations and produces target joint positions. At the standard 50 Hz control frequency, the default history length is 35 frames, corresponding to 0.7 seconds.

The inputs include joint positions and velocities, root angular velocity, projected gravity, previous actions, and reference motion information such as joint targets, joint velocities, and anchor orientation. The policy outputs 29-dimensional target joint positions for the Unitree G1.

RoboDreamer uses a selective state-space model based on Mamba. The model maintains a latent state updated through a data-dependent discretization of a continuous-time state-space system. This allows the model to selectively retain or filter temporal information. The reported teacher uses a two-layer Mamba backbone with width 256 and state dimension 16; the student uses a two-layer model with width 128. The stated sequence-processing cost is linear in history length.

#### 3.2. Stage I: teacher training

The first stage trains a teacher policy using clean observations and reinforcement learning. The action head is optimized with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). The teacher also has an observation-prediction head that estimates the next observation from the temporal latent state.

The auxiliary consistency loss is

\[
\mathcal{L}_{cons} =
\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\hat{o}_{t+1}-o^{GT}_{t+1}\right\|_2^2\right].
\]

This loss encourages the latent state to encode information about physical evolution. The teacher therefore serves two roles: it learns to track reference motions, and it develops a latent representation constrained by next-state prediction.

The teacher benefits from clean observation histories and privileged information available during simulation, particularly for critic training and world-state supervision. It provides a stable control target for the student instead of requiring the student to learn directly through reinforcement learning under severe observation corruption.

#### 3.3. Stage II: masked student distillation

The second stage trains a student policy through a DAgger-style teacher–student procedure. The teacher is frozen, and the student is trained without privileged information. During training, the student receives randomly masked observation histories.

For each environment, a masking activation probability is sampled as

\[
\rho \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,c),
\]

with the default maximum value \(c=0.5\). When masking is activated, the student receives learnable mask tokens in place of a trailing sequence of one to five recent observations. Thus, the policy must infer the missing current state from older temporal information.

The main distillation objective is

\[
\mathcal{L}_{distill} =
\mathbb{E}\left[
\left\|\pi_S(O^{masked})-\pi_T(O^{full})\right\|_2^2
\right].
\]

This is combined with the next-observation consistency loss. The teacher’s clean action output supplies a stable target, while the mask forces the student to use temporal memory rather than simply copying the most recent available observation.

Training also uses motion filtering, reference-state initialization, domain randomization, and action-delay randomization. Physical parameters such as friction, restitution, center-of-mass offsets, root velocities, and external pushes are randomized. The teacher is trained in IsaacLab using 4096 parallel environments, and the resulting policy is evaluated in IsaacLab, transferred without retraining to MuJoCo, and deployed on a Unitree G1.

#### 3.4. Inference strategies

RoboDreamer uses the learned masking interface in two ways.

**Explicit action chunking** appends \(k\) mask tokens to the observation history. A single forward pass produces a sequence of actions for the current and subsequent steps. These actions can be executed sequentially, reducing sensing or inference frequency. However, larger chunks increase the open-loop execution interval and can accumulate tracking error.

**Implicit action refinement** appends one mask token and extracts three adjacent predicted actions, represented as \(a_{t-1}\), \(a_t\), and \(a_{t+1}\). The current command is formed by element-wise averaging:

\[
\bar{a}_t=\frac{1}{3}(a_{t-1}+a_t+a_{t+1}).
\]

Only the refined current action is executed, and the controller replans at the next step. This preserves closed-loop operation while using predicted temporal context to smooth the command.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Motion-tracking performance

RoboDreamer was evaluated on LAFAN1 and FineDance motion data in IsaacLab and MuJoCo. Lower values indicate smaller tracking errors.

In IsaacLab, RoboDreamer obtained the following results:

- **LAFAN1:** 42.87 mm mean keybody position error, 44.11 mm mean joint position error, 0.276 m/s linear velocity error, and 0.295 rad/s yaw-velocity error.
- **FineDance:** 146.72 mm keybody error, 148.43 mm joint error, 0.209 m/s linear velocity error, and 0.222 rad/s yaw-velocity error.

In MuJoCo, it achieved:

- **LAFAN1:** 57.11 mm keybody error, 58.76 mm joint error, 0.279 m/s linear velocity error, and 0.298 rad/s yaw-velocity error.
- **FineDance:** 246.33 mm keybody error, 227.92 mm joint error, 0.226 m/s linear velocity error, and 0.241 rad/s yaw-velocity error.

Across the reported baselines, including ExBody2, GMT, BeyondMimic, and RoboPerform, RoboDreamer generally produced lower errors in both simulation environments. The increase in errors after transfer from IsaacLab to MuJoCo indicates that simulator differences remain relevant, although the policy retained tracking ability without retraining.

#### 4.2. Architecture comparisons

Under the same masked training procedure, the Mamba policy was compared with MLP and Transformer policies on FineDance in IsaacLab. The MLP produced keybody and joint errors of 163.35 and 165.26, respectively, with 6.9 million FLOPs and 3.5 ms latency. The Transformer produced errors of 149.79 and 152.38, with 58.8 million FLOPs and 9.8 ms latency. Mamba produced errors of 146.72 and 148.43, with 43.1 million FLOPs and 4.4 ms latency.

GRU and LSTM comparisons showed similar latency but somewhat higher errors than Mamba. These results support two conclusions: temporal recurrence is useful for this task, and Mamba provides a tracking–latency balance that is more suitable than the tested Transformer for high-frequency control. The ablations also indicate that the masking and distillation framework contributes independently of the backbone.

#### 4.3. Ablations and robustness

A history of 35 frames produced the lowest reported LAFAN1 errors. Shorter histories, such as 20 or 25 frames, performed less accurately, while histories longer than 35 frames did not provide further improvement sufficient to offset additional computation.

Consistency loss improved tracking and became more important as the action chunk size increased. With consistency loss, keybody error increased from 44.11 at chunk size one to 81.29 at chunk size three and 135.94 at chunk size four. Without the loss, the corresponding errors were higher. This suggests that next-observation prediction is particularly relevant when actions are generated farther into the future.

The masking range with maximum probability \(c=0.5\) produced lower errors than values of 0.3, 0.4, or 0.6. The result indicates that the degree of sensory deprivation must be calibrated: insufficient masking may not teach robust inference, while excessive masking can make training more difficult.

Real-robot tests on the Unitree G1 showed stable behavior for action chunks up to size three. Chunk sizes of four or five caused falls in the reported experiments. Qualitative masking tests also showed smaller tracking-error fluctuations for Mamba than for MLP and Transformer policies during observation blackouts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RoboDreamer’s main significance is methodological. It treats missing observations as a structured prediction problem rather than only as a source of random noise. The teacher–student design allows the policy to learn under severe masking while receiving stable action supervision from a clean teacher. The next-observation objective further encourages a latent representation related to local system dynamics.

The framework also connects state inference with predictive control. Because the same mask token is used during training and inference, the model can produce short action sequences or temporally smoothed commands without a separate planner. This could be useful when perception is slower than actuation, when communication is intermittent, or when inference resources are limited.

The results suggest that Mamba is a practical backbone for this setting, particularly when long histories and 50 Hz control are required. Nevertheless, the paper does not establish that Mamba alone is responsible for the gains; its own ablations attribute a substantial portion of the improvement to masking and predictive distillation.

Several limitations constrain the current conclusions. Real-world evaluation is limited to one Unitree G1 platform and does not systematically inject controlled sensing failures. The tested masking pattern primarily removes trailing recent observations and may not represent all forms of asynchronous or corrupted sensing. Action chunking remains vulnerable to open-loop drift, with failures reported beyond three steps. The experiments also focus on reference-motion tracking in simulation and on one humanoid morphology, so transfer to different robots, terrains, or tasks is not demonstrated. Future work would need broader corruption benchmarks, varied environments, cross-morphology evaluation, and more extensive physical testing.
