# OAT: Ordered Action Tokenization

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Harvard and Stanford introduce Ordered Action Tokenization (OAT), a learned autoencoder framework that discretizes continuous robot actions into highly compressed, totally decodable, and causally ordered token sequences. OAT consistently surpasses existing tokenization methods and matches diffusion-based policies across multiple robot control benchmarks, while enabling flexible anytime inference by trading off compute for action fidelity.

## Problem

- Existing action tokenization methods for continuous robot control with autoregressive policies suffer from an inherent trade-off among high compression, total decodability, and modelability (causal ordering).
- Naive discretization generates excessively long token sequences, leading to inefficiencies, while frequency-domain methods can produce undecodable sequences.
- Learned latent tokenizers often lack a structured, causally ordered latent space, which limits their compatibility with the inductive biases of next-token prediction in autoregressive models.

## Method

- **OAT** utilizes a transformer-based autoencoder with learnable "register tokens" and ==Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ)== to discretize continuous action chunks into compact token sequences.
- It induces a left-to-right causal ordering through ==nested dropout== on register tokens and ==causal attention== within the encoder, forcing early tokens to capture coarse details and later tokens to refine them.
- The framework supports ==prefix-based decoding==, allowing any token sequence prefix to be detokenized into a valid action chunk, enabling a direct trade-off between inference cost and action fidelity.

## Results

- OAT consistently outperformed prior tokenization schemes (Bin, FAST, QueST) across four simulation benchmarks (LIBERO, RoboMimic, MetaWorld, RoboCasa) and matched diffusion-based policies, with OAT8_{8}8 achieving average success rates of **56.3%** on LIBERO and **73.1%** on RoboMimic.
- Policy performance monotonically improved with an increasing number of decoded tokens, demonstrating the anytime inference capability (e.g., LIBERO success improved from **11.7%** with OAT1_{1}1 to **56.3%** with OAT8_{8}8).
- Ablation studies confirmed that the ordering-inducing mechanisms were crucial for performance, with OAT trained without ordering exhibiting significant degradation, validating the importance of a structured token space.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simultaneously satisfying high compression, total decodability, and causal ordering in action tokenization is critical for the stability, scalability, and performance of autoregressive robot policies.
> - Explicitly inducing a coarse-to-fine causal ordering in the token space via techniques like nested dropout and causal attention significantly enhances the modelability and robustness of autoregressive learning.
> - Prefix-based detokenization, enabled by ordered tokens, allows for flexible "anytime" inference, where computational effort can be dynamically traded for action precision.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning has seen significant advancements with the emergence of autoregressive sequence models, particularly large transformer-based policies. These models offer benefits such as discrete abstraction, token-level reasoning, and flexible inference capabilities, showing strong generalization when trained on robotic data or adapted from pre-trained vision-language backbones. A critical, yet often underexplored, aspect of applying these models to continuous robot control is the method by which continuous actions are converted into discrete symbols, a process known as action tokenization.

In natural language processing and computer vision, tokenization has been demonstrated to profoundly influence learning dynamics, model capacity utilization, scalability, and overall performance. However, action tokenization for robot control remains less developed compared to its counterparts in other domains. Existing approaches to action tokenization for continuous robot actions generally fall into three categories, each with inherent limitations:

1. **Naive Discretization (e.g., per-dimension binning):** This method, exemplified by approaches like Bin, is simple to implement. However, it generates extremely long token sequences that scale linearly with action dimensionality and prediction horizon. This leads to substantial inefficiencies in training and inference, violating the need for high compression. While it ensures total decodability, it lacks a structured, causally ordered token space amenable to autoregressive generation.
2. **Frequency-domain Compression (e.g., FAST):** Approaches like Frequency-space Action Sequence Tokenization (FAST) aim to achieve high compression by transforming action chunks into frequency coefficients and then using methods like Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) for tokenization. While FAST offers high information density and can induce some ordering (low-frequency to high-frequency components), it suffers from a critical limitation regarding decodability. Due to the variable-length nature of BPE and the fixed-dimensional requirements of robot control, the detokenization process can become a partial function, leading to "undecodable" token sequences and potential runtime failures if the generated token sequence does not match the exact expected structure.
3. **Learned Latent Tokenizers (e.g., QueST):** Recent work has explored learned compression via encoder-decoder architectures utilizing vector quantization (e.g., Quantized Skill Transformer, QueST). These methods can achieve high compression rates and generally ensure total decodability by approximating the mapping with neural networks. However, they typically produce unstructured latent spaces, lacking a consistent ordering or hierarchical abstraction. This absence of a causal left-to-right ordering limits their compatibility with the inductive biases of next-token prediction in autoregressive models, making stable and efficient generation challenging.

The current work identifies a fundamental trade-off among compression rate, modelability under autoregressive learning, and total decodability in existing action tokenization strategies. Improving one aspect often degrades another. OAT aims to bridge this gap by proposing a learned action tokenizer that addresses these limitations simultaneously, thereby offering a more principled approach to action representation for scalable autoregressive robot policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an effective action tokenization scheme for continuous robot actions that specifically addresses the limitations of existing methods when integrated with autoregressive policies. The authors propose that an ideal action tokenizer for this context must simultaneously satisfy three key desiderata:

1. **P.1: High Compression:** The token sequence length ($H_l$) generated by the tokenizer should be sufficiently small to enable efficient autoregressive modeling, particularly for long-context scenarios. This reduces the effective prediction horizon, mitigating the inefficiencies associated with excessively long token sequences prevalent in naive discretization methods.
2. **P.2: Total Decodability:** The detokenization mapping ($T^{-1}$) must be a well-defined total function. This means that every possible token sequence within the discrete token space must map to a valid continuous action chunk. This property is crucial for autoregressive policies, which may generate diverse token sequences during inference. If the decoder is only partially defined, invalid or non-decodable outputs can lead to unpredictable behavior and potential failures in real-world robot execution, as observed in some frequency-domain methods.
3. **P.3: Causal Ordering:** The token sequence ($T_{1:H_l}$) should possess a meaningful left-to-right causal ordering that aligns with the inductive bias of next-token prediction in autoregressive models. This implies that earlier tokens should capture coarse, globally salient aspects of the action chunk, while later tokens progressively refine finer details. Such an ordered structure is hypothesized to improve controllability, robustness, and compatibility with prefix-based generation, enabling a natural coarse-to-fine action synthesis.

The motivation for pursuing these objectives stems from the observation that existing action tokenization strategies inherently face an inescapable trade-off among these three properties. For instance, per-dimension binning (Bin) satisfies P.2 but fails on P.1 and P.3. Frequency-domain methods (FAST) address P.1 and partially P.3 but fail on P.2. Learned latent tokenizers (QueST) satisfy P.1 and P.2 but lack P.3. The authors argue that achieving all three properties simultaneously is fundamental for stable and scalable autoregressive robot learning, unlocking capabilities such as flexible, anytime action generation where inference cost can be traded off against action fidelity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Ordered Action Tokenization (OAT) is introduced as a learned autoencoder framework designed to discretize continuous action chunks into highly compressed and causally ordered token sequences, satisfying all three desiderata (P.1, P.2, P.3).

**A. Tokenization ($T$) and Detokenization ($T^{-1}$) Architecture:**
OAT employs a transformer-based autoencoder structure:
- **Encoder:** A transformer encoder takes a continuous action chunk of shape $H_a \times D_a$ (horizon $\times$ dimension) as input. Crucially, a fixed set of learnable "register tokens" ($r_{1:H_l}$) are concatenated with the input action sequence. These registers act as a compact, read-write memory that aggregates temporal information from the action chunk. After encoding, the register tokens form the bottleneck representation ($z_{1:H_l}$), while the original action tokens are discarded.
- **Quantization:** The continuous register latents ($z_{1:H_l}$) are then discretized using Finite Scalar Quantization (FSQ). FSQ transforms each continuous latent dimension into a discrete index, yielding a sequence of $H_l$ discrete tokens ($T_{1:H_l}$). This process creates a compact, discrete action representation that is compatible with autoregressive models. The FSQ ensures that every quantized latent value maps to a valid codebook entry, contributing to total decodability (P.2).
- **Decoder:** The detokenization mapping ($T^{-1}$) is implemented by a transformer decoder. This decoder takes the discrete token sequence as input and reconstructs the original continuous action chunk of shape $H_a \times D_a$. The decoder is designed as a single-pass transformer, which provides a balance of reconstruction quality and computational efficiency. The tokenizer and detokenizer are trained jointly, end-to-end, using a reconstruction objective, specifically Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the original and reconstructed action chunks. The total decodability (P.2) is inherently satisfied as the decoder is a continuous neural network function that can process any valid discrete token sequence generated by the FSQ. For cases with very small latent bottlenecks, a rectified flow decoder can optionally be used to improve reconstruction quality by training to predict the flow of partially noised action sequences conditioned on the quantized latents.

**B. Inducing Token Ordering for Modelability (P.3):**
To explicitly impose a left-to-right causal ordering over the tokens, aligning them with autoregressive generation, OAT incorporates two complementary mechanisms during training:
- **Nested Dropout:** This technique is applied to the register tokens during training. For a given sequence of $H_l$ register tokens, a random number $K \in [H_l]$ of tokens is sampled. The tokens from $K+1$ to $H_l$ (the "tail" tokens) are then masked out with a special `MASK` token. This forces the encoder to pack the most critical information into the initial register tokens, encouraging an ordered representation where early tokens capture coarse, global structure, and later tokens refine details. The decoder simultaneously learns to reconstruct action chunks from these variably sized token prefixes.
- **Causal Attention:** A causal attention mechanism is applied within the register tokens of the transformer encoder. While action tokens can attend freely, and each register token attends to all action tokens for global aggregation, register-to-register attention is causally masked. Specifically, the $i$-th register token can only attend to the $j$-th register token if $i \ge j$. This causal dependency reinforces a left-to-right information flow among registers, further aligning the learned token sequence with autoregressive prediction and stabilizing generation from partial prefixes.

**C. Information-Theoretic Interpretation:**
The authors provide an information-theoretic perspective on the induced ordering. They suggest that the ordered token sequence acts as an implicit progressive coding. Early tokens encode the most frequent or globally significant patterns in action chunks, which require fewer bits to represent. Subsequent tokens then progressively encode less frequent or finer-grained details. This behavior emerges from the nested dropout objective, as the tokenizer learns to prioritize information in decreasing order of importance for successful reconstruction under partial information budgets.

**D. Autoregressive OAT Policies:**
Once trained, the OAT tokenizer and detokenizer provide the discrete action space for autoregressive policy learning. The policy models the distribution of action tokens sequentially, predicting $p(T_i | T_{<i}, o_{1:H_o})$, where $o_{1:H_o}$ is the observation history. A key feature of OAT is its support for **prefix-based decoding**. Because the tokens are causally ordered, any prefix $T_{1:K}$ (where $K \le H_l$) can be detokenized into a valid action chunk. This allows for an "anytime" trade-off during inference: policies can terminate generation early for faster but coarser actions, or generate longer sequences for more refined actions at higher computational cost. The remaining masked tokens in the prefix-detokenized sequence are treated as `MASK` tokens during detokenization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of OAT involved comparisons with existing action tokenization schemes (Bin, FAST, QueST) and a non-autoregressive diffusion-based policy (DP) across over 20 tasks in four simulation benchmarks (LIBERO, RoboMimic, MetaWorld, RoboCasa) and two real-world manipulation tasks.

- **Overall Policy Performance (Table I, Fig. 1 Right):** OAT consistently outperformed prior action tokenization schemes (Bin, FAST, QueST) across all four simulation benchmarks. It also matched or exceeded the performance of the diffusion-based baseline (DP). Specifically, OAT$_{8}$ (using all 8 latent tokens) achieved the highest success rates across all benchmarks, with average success rates of 56.3% on LIBERO, 73.1% on RoboMimic, 24.4% on MetaWorld, and 54.6% on RoboCasa.
- **Prefix-based Decoding and Anytime Inference (Table I, Fig. 2):** A significant finding is the monotonic improvement in policy performance as the number of decoded tokens (K) from OAT increases (OAT$_{1}$ to OAT$_{8}$). For example, on LIBERO, OAT$_{1}$ achieved 11.7% success, while OAT$_{4}$ reached 46.4%, and OAT$_{8}$ achieved 56.3%. This demonstrates OAT's ability to offer a direct trade-off between inference cost and action fidelity, allowing policies to terminate generation early for lower latency or generate full sequences for higher precision. Figure 2 qualitatively illustrates this coarse-to-fine reconstruction, where early tokens capture global motion structure, and additional tokens refine details.
- **Compression Rate and Inference Latency (Table II):** OAT achieved efficient compression and competitive inference latency. For full decoding (OAT$_{8}$), it had similar token counts (8 tokens) and latency (e.g., 27.4 ms on LIBERO) as QueST. However, unlike QueST, OAT offered flexibility. Bin suffered from extremely high token counts (e.g., 224-384 tokens) and prohibitive latency (e.g., 517.2-888.3 ms), making it impractical. FAST showed moderate compression but still higher token counts (44.2-69.7 mean tokens) and latency (114.4-166.1 ms) than OAT.
- **Importance of Token Space Ordering (P.3) (Table III):** An ablation study demonstrated the critical role of the ordering-inducing mechanisms. OAT trained without nested dropout (OAT$_{x}$) exhibited a consistent and significant performance degradation across all benchmarks compared to OAT trained with ordering. For instance, on LIBERO, OAT$_{x}$ achieved 35.2% success, considerably lower than OAT$_{4}$ (46.4%) and OAT$_{8}$ (56.3%). This result empirically validates that aligning the token space with causal next-token prediction via explicit ordering is beneficial for autoregressive policy learning.
- **Effect of Action Horizon ($H_a$) and Latent Horizon ($H_l$) (Fig. 5):** The study on LIBERO showed that for a fixed latent horizon ($H_l$), performance generally degraded with increasing action horizon ($H_a$), indicating the challenge of compressing longer futures. Conversely, increasing $H_l$ (more register tokens) consistently improved performance by allowing finer-grained temporal encoding. A moderate information bottleneck was found to be beneficial, with excessively small or large bottlenecks leading to diminishing returns or performance degradation.
- **Effect of Codebook Size (Table IV):** Varying the FSQ codebook size showed that performance improved with increasing capacity up to a moderate point, then saturated or slightly degraded with excessively large codebooks. This suggests that while sufficient capacity is needed, overly large codebooks can increase token entropy and sparsity, making downstream autoregressive prediction more difficult.
- **Real-world Results (Table V, Fig. 6):** The advantages of OAT observed in simulation translated to real-world tabletop manipulation tasks (Pick & Place Ball, Stack Cups). OAT consistently achieved the highest success rates, with OAT$_{8}$ being the best performer (16/20 for both tasks). Qualitative observations indicated that OAT produced smoother motions, and early tokens captured coarse movements while later tokens provided the fine-grained precision required for task completion, confirming the intended ordered behavior in a physical setting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Ordered Action Tokenization (OAT) represents a significant advancement in action representation for autoregressive robot policies. By simultaneously satisfying high compression, total decodability, and causal ordering, OAT addresses a fundamental trade-off faced by previous tokenization schemes, providing a more principled and robust foundation for scalable robot learning.

The key significance and potential impacts of this work include:

1. **Enabling Stable and Scalable Autoregressive Policies:** OAT's structured and ordered token space naturally aligns with the inductive biases of next-token prediction, which is crucial for the stability and efficiency of large transformer-based policies. This makes autoregressive models a more competitive and reliable approach for robot control, comparable to or surpassing diffusion-based methods.
2. **Flexible and Anytime Inference:** The prefix-based detokenization capability is a major practical impact. It allows robot policies to operate with a flexible trade-off between computational cost (inference latency) and action fidelity. This is particularly valuable in real-time robotic applications where dynamic latency constraints or varying precision requirements exist. A robot could quickly generate a coarse action plan and refine it as more computation becomes available, or dynamically adapt the level of detail based on task complexity.
3. **Improved Modelability:** The explicit ordering induced by nested dropout and causal attention facilitates learning by guiding the model to progressively encode information, from global structure to fine details. This structure enhances the "modelability" of the action space, making it easier for autoregressive models to capture and generate realistic action sequences.
4. **Integration into Broader VLA Systems:** OAT offers a compatible and powerful intermediate abstraction for emerging Vision-Language-Action (VLA) systems. Its discrete, ordered, and prefix-decodable token space can serve as a natural auxiliary supervision signal, a planning interface for higher-level reasoning, or an effective input for continuous decoders (like diffusion or flow models) within hybrid architectures. This could lead to VLA systems that combine the strengths of discrete reasoning with high-fidelity continuous control.
5. **Foundation for Adaptive Control:** The work opens avenues for future research into adaptive autoregressive depth. Instead of fixing the number of tokens at deployment, future systems could dynamically estimate the intrinsic complexity of an action chunk and generate only the necessary number of tokens to achieve the required precision. This would further optimize computation and responsiveness, pushing towards more intelligent and efficient robot control.

Overall, OAT contributes a critical component to the toolkit of modern robot learning, fostering the development of more capable, flexible, and scalable robotic systems, particularly those leveraging the power of autoregressive generative models.
