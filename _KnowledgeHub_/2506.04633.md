# Unfolding Spatial Cognition: Evaluating Multimodal Models on Visual Simulations

> [!summary] Summary
> STARE, a new benchmark, evaluates multimodal large language models' (MLLMs) spatial reasoning through multi-step visual simulations, demonstrating a substantial performance gap between human abilities and even state-of-the-art MLLMs. The benchmark reveals that current models struggle with complex 3D transformations and inconsistently leverage explicit visual guidance.

## Problem

- Multimodal large language models (MLLMs) largely lack sophisticated spatial reasoning abilities, unlike humans who perform complex mental simulations for spatial tasks.
- Existing AI evaluation benchmarks are insufficient for assessing dynamic, multi-step visual simulation capabilities in MLLMs.
- There is a critical gap in understanding whether MLLMs can perform the kind of multi-step visual simulations characteristic of human spatial intelligence.

## Method

- Introduces **STARE** (Spatial Thinking and Reasoning Evaluations), a benchmark designed to assess MLLMs' ==multi-step visual simulation== capabilities for spatial cognition.
- STARE comprises approximately 4,000 instances across three levels of complexity: ==Foundational Geometric Transformations==, ==Integrated Spatial Reasoning== (e.g., cube folding, tangrams), and ==Real-world Spatial Reasoning== (e.g., temporal/perspective reasoning).
- Tasks are programmatically generated, allowing for precise control over difficulty and the option to provide explicit intermediate visual states to test performance with and without visual guidance.

## Results

- The top-performing MLLM achieved only **57.2%** overall accuracy on STARE, with GPT-4o reaching **53.9%**, significantly below human performance.
- Models struggled with 3D spatial perception (GPT-4o at **~57%** accuracy) and showed cumulative cognitive load effects, with performance often declining beyond two transformation steps without visual simulation.
- A strong correlation (**r = 0.88**) was observed between performance on STARE's synthetic tasks and real-world spatial reasoning, indicating that improvements on the benchmark tasks could translate to practical gains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human participants achieved near-perfect accuracy (87.5% to 99.0%) across all STARE tasks, while even the best MLLMs struggled.
> - MLLMs performed best on scaling operations (~90% accuracy) but poorly on complex transformations like shearing (~54%), and 3D tasks consistently reduced performance.
> - Providing explicit visual simulations offered inconsistent benefits, and verbal step-by-step guidance sometimes degraded MLLM performance, suggesting limitations in integrating sequential information.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Human intelligence relies heavily on spatial cognition—the ability to mentally manipulate and transform objects in space. When we fold a paper into origami, assemble furniture from instructions, or navigate through a complex building, we perform sophisticated mental simulations that guide our actions. Despite impressive advances in multimodal large language models (MLLMs), these systems largely fail to exhibit comparable spatial reasoning abilities.

The paper "Unfolding Spatial Cognition: Evaluating Multimodal Models on Visual Simulations" introduces STARE (Spatial Thinking and Reasoning Evaluations), a comprehensive benchmark designed to assess whether current MLLMs can perform the kind of multi-step visual simulation that characterizes human spatial intelligence. This work addresses a critical gap in AI evaluation, moving beyond static image recognition and verbal reasoning to probe models' capacity for dynamic spatial thinking.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

#### 2.1. The STARE Benchmark

STARE represents a systematic attempt to evaluate spatial cognition in MLLMs through tasks that explicitly require multi-step visual simulation. The benchmark comprises approximately 4,000 instances across diverse spatial reasoning challenges, organized into three levels of increasing complexity:

**Foundational Geometric Transformations** test basic spatial manipulation abilities through 2D and 3D transformations including rotations, translations, scaling, reflections, and shearing. These tasks range from simple visual analogies to instruction-based transformations requiring multiple sequential steps.

**Integrated Spatial Reasoning** evaluates more complex spatial abilities through cube net folding and tangram puzzles. These tasks require understanding how 2D patterns fold into 3D objects or how geometric pieces fit together to form complete shapes.

**Real-world Spatial Reasoning** includes temporal frame reasoning and perspective reasoning tasks that mirror everyday spatial challenges, such as predicting missing frames in video sequences or understanding how scenes appear from different viewpoints.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Methodology and Task Design

The benchmark's design incorporates several key methodological innovations. First, tasks are programmatically generated using tools like Matplotlib and Blender, enabling precise control over difficulty parameters and the creation of explicit intermediate visual states. This approach allows researchers to systematically vary task complexity and provide models with step-by-step visual guidance when needed.

STARE employs two primary evaluation settings: tasks without visual simulations (✗VSim) test models' intrinsic "mental simulation" capacity, while tasks with visual simulations (✓VSim) assess whether models can effectively leverage explicit step-by-step visual guidance. This dual approach helps distinguish between limitations in spatial reasoning versus difficulties in integrating visual information.

The evaluation includes both state-of-the-art closed-source models (GPT-4o, Claude-3.5 Sonnet, Gemini-2.0 Flash) and large open-source models (LLaVA-OneVision-72B, InternVL2.5-78B, Qwen2.5-VL variants), providing a comprehensive assessment across the current landscape of multimodal AI.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Results and Performance Analysis

The results reveal a striking performance gap between humans and MLLMs on spatial reasoning tasks. Human participants consistently achieved near-perfect accuracy (87.5% to 99.0%) across all STARE tasks, while even the best-performing models struggled significantly. The top-performing model, o1, achieved only 57.2% overall accuracy, with GPT-4o reaching 53.9%—barely above chance level for many tasks.

Performance varied considerably across different types of spatial transformations. Models performed best on scaling operations (~90% accuracy) but struggled with more complex transformations like shearing (~54% accuracy). The transition from 2D to 3D tasks consistently reduced performance, indicating particular difficulty with volumetric spatial reasoning.

Visual simulations provided inconsistent benefits across models and tasks. While some models like GPT-4o improved by 11.5% on 2D transformations when given intermediate visual steps, others showed minimal improvement or even performance declines, suggesting fundamental limitations in how these models process and integrate visual information across sequential reasoning steps.

#### 4.2. Key Insights from Detailed Analysis

The research includes several targeted analyses that illuminate specific failure modes in MLLM spatial reasoning. When presented with fully simulated final outcomes, models achieved much higher accuracy (100% for cube nets, 91.6% for tangrams), indicating that poor performance isn't solely due to inability to recognize final states. However, probes revealed that while 2D visual perception was near-perfect, 3D spatial perception remained problematic (~57% accuracy for GPT-4o).

Interestingly, providing explicit verbal reasoning steps offered minimal improvement and sometimes hurt performance. For tangram puzzles, performance actually decreased with step-by-step verbal guidance, suggesting models might rely on simple heuristics (like area calculations) that are disrupted by explicit reasoning instructions.

The analysis of transformation steps revealed that model performance often peaked at 2 steps and declined at 3 steps without visual simulation, indicating cumulative cognitive load effects. With visual simulation, multi-step performance remained more consistently high, though still well below human levels.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Significance and Implications

STARE represents a significant contribution to AI evaluation by providing the first comprehensive benchmark specifically designed to assess spatial cognition through multi-step visual simulation. The benchmark reveals fundamental limitations in current MLLMs that go beyond simple pattern recognition or language understanding deficits.

The work has important implications for several areas of AI development. For embodied AI and robotics, spatial cognition is essential for agents operating in physical environments. The ability to mentally simulate object transformations, plan movements, and interpret spatial relationships directly impacts how AI systems can interact with and understand the physical world. For practical applications, improvements in spatial reasoning could enhance AI assistants' ability to help with tasks ranging from furniture assembly to understanding complex instructional materials.

The research also highlights important differences between closed-source and open-source models. The strong correlation (r = 0.88) between synthetic task performance and real-world spatial reasoning suggests that improvements on STARE's controlled tasks should translate to practical spatial intelligence gains.

#### 5.2. Limitations and Future Directions

The authors acknowledge several limitations in the current benchmark design. The use of simplified synthetic images, while useful for isolating spatial reasoning abilities, doesn't fully capture real-world complexity with factors like clutter, occlusion, or varying lighting conditions. The focus on rigid transformations excludes important spatial reasoning involving flexible objects or articulated systems.

The multiple-choice format, while enabling automatic scoring, may hide important aspects of model reasoning processes. Future work could benefit from open-ended response formats or requirements for explicit step-by-step reasoning explanations that reveal intermediate thought processes.

Several promising directions emerge from this work. Expanding to more realistic visual environments, incorporating additional sensory modalities (audio, depth information), and developing tasks that involve flexible or articulated objects could broaden the benchmark's scope. Additionally, investigating specific architectural improvements or training methodologies that enhance spatial reasoning capabilities represents an important research direction.

#### 5.3. Conclusion

STARE provides a rigorous diagnostic tool for evaluating spatial cognition in multimodal AI systems, revealing substantial gaps between current MLLMs and human-level spatial intelligence. The benchmark's systematic design and comprehensive evaluation expose fundamental limitations in how these models perform multi-step visual reasoning, even when provided with explicit visual guidance.

The work establishes spatial cognition as a critical challenge for the AI community, requiring focused research attention to develop models capable of the flexible, dynamic spatial reasoning that characterizes human intelligence. By clearly demonstrating these limitations and providing a framework for measuring progress, STARE contributes both to our understanding of current AI capabilities and to the development of more cognitively capable multimodal systems.

The benchmark serves as both a wake-up call about current limitations and a roadmap for future development, highlighting the need for AI systems that can truly "imagine in space" rather than simply recognizing static visual patterns or applying linguistic reasoning shortcuts.

 - This is a foundational cognitive science paper cited in the introduction to establish the concept of 'mental rotation.' The authors use it as primary evidence that humans solve spatial problems through an analog mental simulation process, which is the core capability the STARE benchmark is designed to evaluate in AI models.

Mary Hegarty. Mental animation: Inferring motion from static displays of mechanical systems.Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 18(5):1084–1102, 1992.

 - This paper introduces the concept of 'mental animation,' which the authors cite as a key example of how humans incrementally simulate processes to understand complex systems. This citation is crucial as it underpins the motivation for STARE's focus on multi-step visual simulations, particularly for tasks like cube folding and mechanical reasoning.

Peter W Battaglia, Jessica B Hamrick, and Joshua B Tenenbaum. Simulation as an engine of physical scene understanding.Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 110(45):18327–18332.

 - This citation provides the modern computational framework for the paper's central theme, proposing that mental simulation is a core 'engine' for understanding the physical world. The authors reference this to bridge classic cognitive science theories with the goal of building and evaluating AI that can perform similar visual simulations for problem-solving.

Jihan Yang, Shusheng Yang, Anjali Gupta, Rilyn Han, Li Fei-Fei, and Saining Xie. Thinking in Space: How Multimodal Large Language Models See, Remember and Recall Spaces. arXiv preprint arXiv:[[2412.14171|VSI-Bench]], 2024.

 - This is a highly relevant contemporary work that introduces VSI-Bench, another benchmark for spatial reasoning in MLLMs. The authors explicitly contrast STARE with VSI-Bench to highlight their paper's unique focus on explicit, step-by-step visual simulations, making this citation essential for positioning the paper within the current research landscape.
