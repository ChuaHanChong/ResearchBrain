# Learning Important Features Through Propagating Activation Differences

> [!summary] Summary
> DeepLIFT introduces a method for interpreting neural network predictions by propagating activation differences from a reference state. It provides more accurate and robust attribution scores to input features compared to previous gradient-based methods, as demonstrated on tasks like image classification and regulatory DNA sequence analysis.

## Problem

- Deep neural networks operate as 'black boxes,' hindering understanding of why specific predictions are made, which limits their adoption in critical applications.
- Existing interpretability methods struggle with the 'saturation problem' (zero gradients despite input influence), gradient discontinuities, and inaccurate handling of negative feature contributions.
- Previous techniques often lack computational efficiency for complex models or a strong theoretical basis for fair feature attribution.

## Method

- **DeepLIFT** explains predictions by propagating ==differences from a reference state== for output and input activations, rather than relying solely on absolute values or local gradients.
- It defines ==multipliers== and employs a ==chain rule==, similar to backpropagation, to efficiently compute and propagate ==contribution scores== from the output layer to the input layer in a single pass.
- The method incorporates specialized rules, such as the ==Rescale Rule== and the ==RevealCancel Rule== (an improved ==Shapley value== approximation), for handling different network components and nonlinearities, allowing for explicit consideration of positive and negative contributions.

## Results

- On MNIST digit classification, DeepLIFT with the RevealCancel rule **consistently and significantly outperformed** other backpropagation-based methods in identifying truly discriminative pixels.
- For regulatory DNA sequence classification, DeepLIFT accurately reflected motif strength, successfully identified cooperative effects between motifs, and avoided the misattributions common in other gradient-based techniques.
- DeepLIFT effectively addressed critical limitations of prior methods, including the saturation problem, gradient discontinuities, and the inaccurate attribution of negative contributions, yielding more precise and biologically relevant insights.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explaining predictions based on 'differences from a reference' enables the propagation of importance signals through saturated neuron regions and mitigates artifacts caused by gradient discontinuities.
> - The RevealCancel rule, by explicitly separating positive and negative contributions, is crucial for accurately uncovering complex hidden dependencies; a hybrid application (e.g., Rescale for convolutional layers, RevealCancel for fully connected layers) can optimize performance by balancing noise suppression and detailed feature attribution.
> - The choice of an appropriate 'reference input' is critical and problem-dependent, significantly influencing the meaningfulness and context of the resulting feature attributions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a fundamental challenge in the field of artificial intelligence: **interpretablity and explainability of deep learning models**, often termed Explainable AI (XAI). As neural networks achieve state-of-the-art performance across various domains, their "black box" nature—the difficulty in understanding *why* a particular prediction was made—has become a significant barrier to their adoption in high-stakes applications like healthcare, finance, and scientific research.

The research landscape for neural network interpretability can broadly be categorized into two main approaches:

1. **Perturbation-Based Forward Propagation Approaches:** These methods analyze the impact of changing individual inputs or neurons on the network's output. Examples include occluding parts of an image (Zeiler & Fergus, 2013) or "in-silico mutagenesis" for genomic sequences (Zhou & Troyanskaya, 2015).
 - **Limitations:** These methods are often computationally intensive, requiring multiple forward passes for each perturbation. Crucially, they can also underestimate the importance of features that have saturated their contribution to the output (where further changes to the input no longer affect the output).

2. **Backpropagation-Based Approaches:** These methods propagate an importance signal backward from the output neuron to the input features in a single pass, offering greater computational efficiency.
 - **Gradient-based methods:** Early approaches used the gradient of the output with respect to input pixels (Simonyan et al., 2013) to create saliency maps. Variations like Deconvolutional Networks (Zeiler & Fergus, 2013) and Guided Backpropagation (Springenberg et al., 2014) emerged to handle nonlinearities (e.g., ReLUs) differently during backpropagation.
 - **Limitations:** Pure gradients, Guided Backpropagation, and Deconvolutional Networks suffer from several issues: they assign zero importance when the gradient is zero (the saturation problem), they struggle to highlight inputs that contribute negatively, and they can produce artifacts due to gradient discontinuities.
 - **Layerwise Relevance Propagation (LRP) and Gradient × Input:** LRP (Bach et al., 2015) aimed to propagate relevance scores systematically. Subsequent work showed that LRP rules for ReLU networks were often equivalent to an element-wise product of gradients and inputs (Shrikumar et al., 2016; Kindermans et al., 2016).
 - **Limitations:** While often better than raw gradients, `gradient × input` still inherits the saturation and discontinuity problems.
 - **Integrated Gradients:** This method addresses the saturation and discontinuity issues by integrating gradients along a path from a baseline input to the actual input (Sundararajan et al., 2016).
 - **Limitations:** Numerically computing high-quality integrals can add significant computational overhead, and the paper argues it can still yield misleading results in some complex scenarios.
 - **Grad-CAM:** Primarily for Convolutional Neural Networks, Grad-CAM (Selvaraju et al., 2016) generates coarse-grained importance maps by weighting feature maps with gradients. Guided Grad-CAM combines this with Guided Backpropagation for finer detail.
 - **Limitations:** Inherits the issues of Guided Backpropagation and is specific to CNN architectures.

DeepLIFT positions itself within the family of backpropagation-based approaches but introduces a novel conceptual framework: **explaining predictions in terms of differences from a 'reference' state.** This "difference-from-reference" philosophy, combined with rules for handling positive and negative contributions separately, allows DeepLIFT to overcome many of the fundamental limitations of prior gradient-based methods, particularly the saturation problem, gradient discontinuities, and misattribution of negative contributions. Its theoretical connection to Shapley values, which provide a principled way to attribute contributions in cooperative game theory, further grounds DeepLIFT in robust theoretical principles, aiming to provide a more accurate and interpretable attribution of feature importance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind DeepLIFT stems from the inherent "black box" nature of deep neural networks, which acts as a significant impediment to their deployment in critical applications where transparency and trustworthiness are paramount. The authors explicitly state this as a "barrier to adoption."

Given this overarching motivation, the key objectives of DeepLIFT are:

- **To develop a robust and principled method for assigning importance scores to input features** that explain a neural network's specific output prediction. This method should provide clear and understandable insights into which parts of the input are most influential for a given output.
- **To overcome the limitations of existing interpretability techniques.** Specifically, DeepLIFT aims to address:
 - **The "saturation problem":** Gradient-based methods fail to propagate importance signals when a neuron's activation saturates, leading to zero gradients despite significant input influence. DeepLIFT aims to propagate information even in these regimes.
 - **Gradient discontinuities and artifacts:** Discontinuities in gradients (e.g., around ReLU thresholds or bias terms) can cause misleading or unstable importance scores. DeepLIFT seeks to provide a more continuous and stable measure of importance.
 - **Inability to handle negative contributions:** Many gradient-based methods (e.g., Guided Backpropagation) ignore or misattribute negative contributions, obscuring critical dependencies. DeepLIFT aims to accurately account for both positive and negative influences.
 - **Computational inefficiency:** While perturbation-based methods are often too slow for practical use, DeepLIFT aims to be computationally efficient, akin to standard backpropagation, requiring only a single backward pass.
- **To introduce a novel conceptual framework for interpretability based on "differences from a reference state."** This objective seeks to provide a more meaningful and context-aware interpretation by comparing an actual input's effect against a chosen baseline or "neutral" input.
- **To provide an improved approximation of Shapley values** in the context of neural networks, leveraging their theoretical guarantees for fair feature attribution.

In essence, the authors seek to build a more accurate, reliable, and efficient interpretability tool that makes deep learning models more transparent, thereby fostering greater trust and enabling their application in complex real-world scenarios, particularly in scientific discovery like genomics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DeepLIFT introduces a novel framework for attributing output differences to input differences, built around a "difference-from-reference" philosophy. Its core methodology involves defining contributions and multipliers that can be efficiently computed via a backpropagation-like algorithm.

#### 3.1. The DeepLIFT Philosophy: Difference-from-Reference

Unlike traditional methods that compute importance based on absolute values or local gradients, DeepLIFT explains the difference in an output neuron's activation ($ \Delta t = t - t_0 $) from a reference activation ($t_0$) in terms of the differences of its input features ($ \Delta x_i = x_i - x_{i,0} $) from their respective reference inputs ($x_{i,0}$). This allows for:
- **Propagation through saturated regions:** Even if the local gradient is zero, a finite difference can still be non-zero, allowing importance signals to flow.
- **Continuity:** Differences-from-reference are inherently more continuous than local gradients, mitigating artifacts from discontinuous gradient functions.

#### 3.2. Summation-to-Delta Property and Multipliers

DeepLIFT assigns contribution scores $ C_{\Delta x_i, \Delta t} $ such that their sum equals the total difference-from-reference of the output: $ \sum_i C_{\Delta x_i, \Delta t} = \Delta t $. This property ensures that the total "blame" for the output difference is fully distributed among the input differences.
To facilitate backpropagation, the concept of a **multiplier** $ m_{\Delta x, \Delta t} = \frac{C_{\Delta x, \Delta t}}{\Delta x} $ is defined. This is analogous to a partial derivative but for finite differences.

#### 3.3. Chain Rule for Multipliers

Similar to the chain rule for derivatives, DeepLIFT establishes a chain rule for multipliers: $ m_{\Delta x_i, \Delta t} = \sum_j m_{\Delta x_i, \Delta y_j} m_{\Delta y_j, \Delta t} $. This allows for efficient, single-pass backpropagation of contribution scores from the output layer to the input layer.

#### 3.4. Defining the Reference Input

The choice of a reference input ($x_0$) is crucial and problem-dependent.
- For MNIST, an all-zeros image is used as a neutral background.
- For genomic data, a reference input representing expected ACGT background frequencies or shuffled sequences is employed.
- For CIFAR10, a blurred version of the image can serve as a reference.
This highlights DeepLIFT's flexibility and context-awareness, contrasting with methods like `gradient × input`, which implicitly use an all-zeros reference.

#### 3.5. Separating Positive and Negative Contributions

A key innovation is the optional separation of contributions into positive ($ \Delta y^+ $) and negative ($ \Delta y^- $) components. This allows for differential treatment of these contributions, which is critical for revealing dependencies that might be masked by cancellation effects.

#### 3.6. Rules for Assigning Contribution Scores (Backpropagation Rules)

DeepLIFT proposes three rules for calculating multipliers and contributions for different types of neural network layers:

1. **The Linear Rule:** Applied to dense and convolutional layers (excluding nonlinearities). For a linear function $ y = b + \sum_i w_i x_i $, the rule distributes contributions based on $w_i \Delta x_i$, with special handling for cases where $ \Delta x_i = 0 $ but $ \Delta x_i^+ $ or $ \Delta x_i^- $ might be non-zero (i.e., when positive and negative parts cancel out).
2. **The Rescale Rule:** Applied to single-input nonlinearities like ReLU, tanh, or sigmoid. Here, $ C_{\Delta x, \Delta y} = \Delta y $, and $ m_{\Delta x, \Delta y} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} $. This rule naturally addresses saturation (Fig. 1) and thresholding artifacts (Fig. 2) and provides a fast approximation of Shapley values when positive and negative contributions are not separated. When $ \Delta x \rightarrow 0 $, the multiplier approaches the derivative $ \frac{dy}{dx} $.
3. **The RevealCancel Rule (Improved Shapley Approximation):** This is an advanced rule for nonlinearities, designed to provide a more accurate approximation of Shapley values by explicitly considering the average impact of positive and negative input differences both in isolation and in conjunction. It calculates $ \Delta y^+ $ and $ \Delta y^- $ as the average marginal impact of $ \Delta x^+ $ and $ \Delta x^- $ respectively, over the two possible "orderings" of adding these components (e.g., $ \Delta x^+ $ without $ \Delta x^- $, then $ \Delta x^+ $ with $ \Delta x^- $). This rule is particularly effective at revealing hidden dependencies, such as in the `min(i1, i2)` operation (Fig. 3), where the Rescale rule (and gradient-based methods) would misattribute importance. The authors acknowledge a trade-off: Rescale might be preferable for noise suppression in certain thresholded ReLUs.

#### 3.7. Choice of Target Layer

For softmax or sigmoid outputs, DeepLIFT recommends computing contributions to the linear layer *preceding* the final nonlinearity. This prevents attenuation of scores caused by the saturation of the sigmoid/softmax function, where large changes in input logits can result in minimal changes in the final output probability. For softmax, an additional normalization step subtracts the mean contribution across all classes to account for the normalization effect of softmax.

In summary, DeepLIFT's methodology builds upon a strong theoretical foundation of attributing differences, provides flexible rule sets for different network components, and offers computational efficiency comparable to standard backpropagation, setting it apart from prior interpretability methods.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors evaluate DeepLIFT against several leading backpropagation-based interpretability methods (gradient×input, integrated gradients, Guided Backpropagation) on two distinct tasks: MNIST digit classification and regulatory DNA sequence classification.

#### 4.1. MNIST Digit Classification

- **Task:** Identify pixels to erase from an image of an original digit (c_o) to convert it to a target digit (c_t). Performance is measured by the change in log-odds score between c_o and c_t after masking pixels ranked by importance.
- **Performance:** **DeepLIFT with the RevealCancel (RC) rule significantly outperformed all other backpropagation-based methods.** The boxplots in Figure 4 demonstrate a greater increase in log-odds scores for the target class relative to the original class, indicating that DeepLIFT more effectively identifies truly discriminative pixels.
- **Comparison to Integrated Gradients (IG):** IG, computed over 5 or 10 intervals, yielded results comparable to `gradient × input`. This suggests that for MNIST, the saturation and thresholding failure modes (which IG is designed to address) might not be as prevalent or impactful for these specific digit classification tasks as they are in other domains.
- **Guided Backpropagation (Guided Backprop):** Performed poorly, which the authors attribute to its strategy of zeroing out negative gradients during backpropagation, leading to a loss of important information for class discrimination.
- **DeepLIFT-Rescale vs. DeepLIFT-RevealCancel:** On MNIST, using the Rescale rule instead of RevealCancel consistently degraded performance, underscoring the benefits of the RevealCancel rule's ability to handle positive and negative contributions separately.

#### 4.2. Classifying Regulatory DNA (Genomics)

- **Task:** Benchmark interpretability methods on a simulated multi-task classification network identifying the presence of GATA1 and/or TAL1 motifs in synthetic DNA sequences. Ideal methods should assign high scores to relevant motifs, low to irrelevant ones, differentiate importance based on motif strength, and detect cooperativity between motifs.
- **Guided Backprop×Input:** Exhibited significant failures:
 - Assigned positive importance to irrelevant motifs (e.g., TAL1 for Task 1, which labels "GATA1 present," not TAL1).
 - Failed to identify cooperative effects, as scores for sequences with both motifs (red dots) did not distinctly separate from sequences with only one motif (green dots) for tasks requiring detection of both (Task 0).
 - Showed suboptimal behavior regarding motif strength, with a sudden, disproportionate jump in importance at a log-odds score of around 7, but little differentiation at higher log-odds. This highlights the thresholding problem from Figure 2 in a practical genomics context.
 - Suffered from inflated scores due to discontinuous gradients.
- **Gradient×Input and Integrated Gradients:** Also showed suboptimal differentiation based on motif strength, similar to Guided Backprop×Input. They occasionally missed the relevance of TAL1 for Task 0 (detecting both motifs).
- **DeepLIFT Variants:**
 - **DeepLIFT-RevealCancel (RC):** Generally performed well, accurately reflecting motif strength and relevance.
 - **DeepLIFT-fc-RC-conv-RS (Rescale on convolutional layers, RevealCancel on fully-connected layer):** This hybrid approach significantly reduced noise compared to applying RevealCancel across all layers. The authors suggest this is because convolutional layers might act as motif detectors, where non-firing neurons represent noise that should not propagate importance. This demonstrates the practical utility of DeepLIFT's flexible rule application.
 - **Corrections:** DeepLIFT, particularly with the RevealCancel rule, successfully identified cooperative effects (red dots above green dots in Fig. 5a for Task 0), provided more gradual and accurate differentiation of motif strength, and avoided the misattributions seen in other methods. For example, it correctly highlighted both TAL1 and GATA1 motifs for Task 0 (Fig. 6b), where other methods often failed.

In summary, DeepLIFT, especially with its advanced RevealCancel rule and flexible application strategies, consistently demonstrated superior performance in attributing feature importance compared to existing gradient-based methods. It effectively addressed the saturation, discontinuity, and negative contribution issues that plague other techniques, providing more accurate and biologically meaningful insights in the genomics domain.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

DeepLIFT represents a significant advancement in the field of Explainable AI (XAI) and holds substantial potential impact across various domains, particularly in scientific research and critical applications of deep learning.

#### 5.1. Advancing Neural Network Interpretability

DeepLIFT introduces a theoretically principled and empirically effective method for feature attribution. By framing importance in terms of "differences from a reference state" and providing sophisticated rules (especially RevealCancel) for handling positive and negative contributions, it directly addresses several fundamental limitations of prior interpretability techniques:
- **Overcoming Saturation and Discontinuities:** Its ability to propagate information even when gradients are zero (saturation) and to avoid artifacts caused by gradient discontinuities makes DeepLIFT more robust and reliable, especially for complex, non-linear deep networks. This is crucial for models with saturating activations like sigmoid or tanh, commonly found in Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs).
- **Revealing Hidden Dependencies:** The RevealCancel rule's capacity to separately consider positive and negative contributions allows it to uncover subtle but crucial dependencies and interaction effects that other methods often miss or misattribute (e.g., in `min` operations or cooperative binding events).
- **Principled Attribution:** The theoretical connection to Shapley values lends DeepLIFT strong credibility, suggesting it provides a fairer and more consistent attribution of importance.

#### 5.2. Increasing Trust and Adoption of Deep Learning

By offering more transparent and trustworthy explanations for model predictions, DeepLIFT can significantly mitigate the "black box" concern. This increased transparency is critical for fostering the adoption of deep learning in high-stakes fields such as:
- **Healthcare:** Explaining why a model predicts a certain diagnosis or treatment outcome.
- **Finance:** Justifying loan approvals, fraud detection, or investment strategies.
- **Autonomous Systems:** Understanding decision-making processes in self-driving cars or robotics.
- **Scientific Discovery:** Providing mechanistic insights rather than just predictive power.

#### 5.3. Impact on Genomics and Biological Discovery

The application to classifying regulatory DNA highlights DeepLIFT's profound potential in computational biology. Understanding which specific DNA motifs and their combinations drive gene regulation is fundamental to biology. DeepLIFT's demonstrated ability to:
- Accurately assign importance to motifs based on their strength.
- Correctly attribute importance in the presence of complex interactions (like cooperativity between GATA1 and TAL1 motifs).
- Avoid misattributing importance to irrelevant features.
can directly lead to new biological hypotheses and discoveries. This moves beyond simply predicting regulatory elements to *explaining* why they are functional, accelerating our understanding of genomic mechanisms and disease.

#### 5.4. Practical Utility and Future Directions

- **Computational Efficiency:** DeepLIFT's computation in a single backward pass makes it practical for use with large, complex deep learning models, unlike many computationally intensive perturbation-based methods.
- **Model Debugging and Improvement:** Researchers and practitioners can use DeepLIFT to debug models, identify biases, understand unexpected behavior, and ultimately improve model design by visualizing what features the network is truly relying on.
- **Foundational Research:** The paper identifies several open questions, such as applying DeepLIFT to RNNs, developing empirical methods for optimal reference selection, and handling `max` operations. These represent fruitful avenues for future research, indicating that DeepLIFT serves not only as a solution but also as a new foundation for further advancements in interpretability. The finding that a hybrid rule application (Rescale on conv layers, RevealCancel on FC) can optimize performance suggests that tailoring interpretability methods to specific network architectures and problem domains is a promising direction.

In conclusion, DeepLIFT is a powerful and flexible interpretability framework that addresses critical shortcomings of previous methods. Its principled approach, combined with strong empirical performance, positions it to significantly enhance the transparency, trustworthiness, and utility of deep learning models across scientific and industrial applications.
