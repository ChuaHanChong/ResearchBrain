# Development of an Intuitive GUI for Non-Expert Teleoperation of Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Laurentian University researchers developed an intuitive graphical user interface (GUI) for non-expert teleoperation of humanoid robots, enhancing robot navigation and recovery while reducing operator cognitive load during FIRA HuroCup obstacle course challenges. The new GUI offers dynamic control adjustments and expanded motion capabilities like crawling and self-standing.

## Problem

- Existing teleoperation systems for humanoid robots often require expert operators and extensive training due to complex interfaces.
- The previous teleoperation GUI for the target robot had critical functional flaws, including unintuitive layouts, lack of clear labels, missing crucial actions, and no on-the-fly motion tuning.
- Current research in teleoperation frequently prioritizes raw control capabilities over accessibility for novice users, particularly in competitive environments like the FIRA HuroCup.

## Method

- An iterative development methodology was employed to design a scalable GUI, utilizing custom HTML, CSS, and JavaScript for greater control and flexibility.
- Communication between the web-based GUI and the robot's ROS environment was established using ROSbridge and roslib.js for real-time command publishing and feedback subscription.
- ==The GUI== was developed through four main iterations, focusing on core movement controls, keyboard bindings, integrating internal sensor data, and refining visual presentation and micro-adjustment capabilities.

## Results

- The new GUI integrated previously absent motions like crawling and self-standing, significantly enhancing the robot's ability to navigate obstacles and recover from falls.
- Operators can now dynamically adjust movement parameters (step length, turn angle) in real-time using fine-tune sliders, a capability not present in the prior system.
- The redesigned interface offers a reduced visual clutter, clearly labeled controls, and enhanced information display (e.g., battery voltage, FPS), leading to an improved user experience for novice operators.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prioritizing camera-based visualization and clear communication of robot state is crucial for effective environmental feedback in teleoperation.
> - Adhering to established user interface (UI) development and human-robot interaction (HRI) principles, such as consistency, visual hierarchy, and feedback, leads to more intuitive and user-friendly systems.
> - Investing in human-centered design through iterative testing and feedback helps reduce operator cognitive load and improve performance, even for non-expert users.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of teleoperation in humanoid robotics has experienced growth over the past decade, driven by advancements in robotic control and movement. These systems find application in diverse sectors such as healthcare, mining, and space exploration (Liu et al., 2018; Godelius, 2021; Gómez et al., 2021). Robotic competitions, specifically the HuroCup teleoperation event within the FIRA RoboWorld Cup, serve as platforms for testing and refining teleoperated humanoid systems (Baltes et al., 2025).

Despite ongoing progress, a recurring challenge in this domain is the reliance on expert operators for many existing teleoperation systems. Prior research consistently emphasizes the need for graphical user interfaces (GUIs) that facilitate fast, safe, and efficient control by novice users, thereby enhancing accessibility (Rea and Seo, 2022). Effective teleoperation systems are designed to foster situational awareness, manage operator cognitive workload, and mitigate errors (Steinfeld et al., 2006). Strategies such as shared-control schemes have been explored to reduce cognitive load by blending autonomous and manual control (Adamides et al., 2014). The development of intuitive visual feedback and adaptive GUIs is noted for reducing training time for non-experts (Goodrich et al., 2013), while task-specific visuals can simplify complex maneuvers (Sankar et al., 2023). The concept of scalable architectures, exemplified by interfaces like DRC-HUBO, demonstrates how a single design can be adapted across various applications, from competitions to industrial settings (Zucker et al., 2015).

Usability principles from classic human-computer interaction literature are also relevant, advocating for consistent navigation, cohesive color schemes, appropriate hiding of less-used controls, immediate feedback, and adaptability across different screen sizes (Beaird et al., 2020; Boss and Teague, 2016; Karray et al., 2013). Applying these principles to teleoperation aims to reduce frustration and errors for novice users. The importance of user-friendly teleoperation is further underscored in high-risk environments, where remote control can shield workers from danger (Godelius, 2021) or ensure safety in applications like surgical robotics (Sun et al., 2023). Competitions like the DARPA Robotics Challenge (Marion et al., 2018) and FIRA (Baltes et al., 2025) demand reliable and fast teleoperation. Iterative, user-centered design (UCD) is a recognized methodology for improving GUIs (Mao et al., 2005), as high cognitive load is associated with confusion and errors (Goodrich and Schultz, 2007; Chen et al., 2007).

However, the existing research landscape often targets trained specialists, with interfaces in joystick, VR, or wearable studies prioritizing raw control capability over accessibility for novices (Franz et al., 2013; Ryu et al., 2004; Fritsche et al., 2015). The current work aims to address this gap by developing a GUI that is simultaneously simple, extensible, and suitable for competitive environments, making humanoid robot teleoperation more accessible to non-expert teams.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is the development of a scalable Graphical User Interface (GUI) specifically designed to enable non-expert operators to intuitively control a humanoid robot. This control is intended for navigating a FIRA-regulated obstacle course.

The motivation for this work stems from several observed deficiencies in existing teleoperation systems for humanoid robotics:
- **Lack of Non-Expert Focus:** Many current humanoid robotics systems do not heavily invest in developing GUIs tailored for non-expert users. This often results in complex interfaces that require specialized training or extensive prior experience.
- **FIRA HuroCup Requirements:** The FIRA HuroCup teleoperation event presents a challenge of navigating an obstacle course without contact, which necessitates a GUI that offers clear visual feedback and simplified control schemes. The existing interface used by the research team's robot was identified as having significant shortcomings in this regard.
- **Usability Deficiencies in Previous Systems:** The prior interface on the target robot was characterized by several critical functional and design flaws. These included a lack of clear labels, unintuitive layouts, the absence of crucial actions such as crawling, inconsistent camera placement, and no capability for on-the-fly motion tuning. These issues contributed to a high cognitive workload for operators and hindered performance.
- **Accessibility and Training:** In team-based competitive environments like FIRA, new participants frequently inherit systems. A usable and intuitive interface is crucial for reducing the need for extensive retraining and lowering the cognitive burden on operators (White et al., 2020; Rea and Seo, 2022).
- **Scalability for Future Applications:** The project aimed to develop an interface that is not only effective for the immediate competition but also scalable for broader applications in the future.

To address these motivations, the specific goals for the new GUI were established:
- Prioritize camera-based visualization as the primary source of environmental feedback for the operator.
- Ensure clear and unambiguous communication of the robot's current state.
- Design the system with scalability in mind to accommodate future enhancements and broader utility.
- Incorporate established principles from user interface development (UI) and human-robot interaction (HRI) concepts to ensure simplicity and intuitiveness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed an iterative development methodology to construct the user-friendly GUI. This process was structured into recurring cycles, each involving: (1) the creation of wireframes and identification of key features for the current iteration, (2) the implementation and adaptation of these features, (3) usability testing and assessment of layout comfort, and (4) refinement based on observations before proceeding to the subsequent cycle.

#### 3.1. GUI Platform and Tools

The GUI was developed using custom HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, departing from earlier versions that utilized pre-built components like Bootstrap. This custom approach aimed to provide greater control and flexibility over the interface design. The target robot is a bipedal mobile robot conforming to FIRA's kid-size humanoid standards (Baltes, 2025). Critical operational constraints included reliance solely on camera input and terminal console, with no external sensors available, necessitating optimized camera-based environmental awareness.

The system operates on Ubuntu Mint 16.07 with ROS1, leveraging Python 2.7 and C++ within a publisher-subscriber model. An existing ROS node facilitated basic browser-based GUI functionality, but was noted for its limitations. The front-end development emulated a standard web application. For specific functionalities, jQuery was utilized for Document Object Model (DOM) manipulation, and mjpegcanvas.js was employed for streaming live video feeds from the robot's camera.

Communication between the web-based GUI and the robot's ROS environment was established using ROSbridge, which functions as a WebSocket server on the robot, exposing ROS topics and services in JSON format. On the client side, roslib.js was used to create a WebSocket connection to ROSbridge, enabling the web interface to publish movement commands and subscribe to feedback topics in real-time. This setup was designed to abstract the complexities of native ROS network protocols.

#### 3.2. Development and Testing Procedures

Development commenced by implementing essential controls, such as basic movement and camera views, focusing on responsiveness and intuitiveness. Subsequent features added included crawling functionality, battery voltage indicators, and diagnostic logs. Due to FIRA regulations, all feedback was derived from internal robot data and camera visuals. Wireframes guided the layout and organization of elements. Interactivity was managed via JavaScript, while HTML and CSS defined the interface's structure and styling.

Testing was conducted on a regulation-compliant turf track equipped with obstacles, simulating competition conditions. Usability evaluations primarily focused on task success rates and the efficiency of user workflows. Features were either retained or revised based on their perceived contribution to task performance during these tests.

#### 3.3. Ethical and Practical Constraints

Due to time limitations, the project did not include formal external user testing or seek ethics approval. All evaluations were conducted solely by the developer. Furthermore, the robot's behavior was sometimes inconsistently affected by modifications made by other users. These external irregularities were accounted for during evaluations by prioritizing assessments under ideal performance conditions.

#### 3.4. Iterative GUI Development Process

The development proceeded through four main iterations, each building upon the previous:

- **First Draft:** Focused on basic movement controls, non-cluttered content display, and an initial two-screen layout for map setup and robot control. The principle of proximity (Lidwell et al., 2010) guided the grouping of related controls. Emphasized camera placement at the forefront and implemented previously missing left/right shifting movements. Initial testing indicated camera size affected throughput, necessitating future scaling.
- **Second Iteration:** Implemented keyboard bindings for movement (WASD for robot, arrow keys for camera, adhering to common conventions (Krug, 2005)). Added less frequent actions (e.g., get-up, crawl) as selectable on-screen buttons rather than keybinds to prevent operator overload and align with visual hierarchy principles (Johnson, 2010). The camera was resized and given a dedicated screen section to improve throughput.
- **Third Iteration:** Introduced start and reset buttons for robot positioning. Integrated internal sensor data such as rendering frames per second (FPS), battery voltage, and diagnostic logs into the UI, reformatted from the old system to fit the new layout.
- **Fourth Iteration (Visual and Micro-Adjustment Refinements):** Focused on visual improvements, including high-contrast colors (blue and orange), enhanced labeling, and dynamic screen sizing using Flexbox principles for adaptability to smaller displays. Collapsible menus were added to reduce clutter and allow users to customize their view (Norman, 2013; Tidwell, 2010). Tooltips were implemented for most buttons to provide immediate descriptive information (Cooper et al., 2014). A new feature allowed on-the-fly micro-adjustments for walking movement coefficients (distance and angle) through JavaScript event listeners, session storage, and WebSocket communication with the robot.

#### 3.5. Refinements and Comparisons

Several key refinements were made throughout the iterative process:
- The original cluttered map display (Figure 3) was moved to a dedicated initial screen (Figure 4) to reduce operational screen clutter.
- Dynamic page sizing was implemented (Figure 8), addressing the old system's issue of content compression on smaller displays (Figure 7).
- ROS communication for robot movement was enhanced, adding functionality for left and right walks and refactoring the `rosactions.js` code for improved readability and expandability.
- Action buttons were integrated into collapsible sub-menus (Figure 9) to further reduce cognitive load and screen clutter, and nearly all buttons received descriptive tooltips.
- The head position slider was refined for visual clarity, placement, and the addition of a reset button.

The evaluation of the newly developed UI was conducted through a direct qualitative comparison with the previous system used on the robot. This comparison assessed adherence to established UI development practices and overall consistency, with the assumption that better adherence would correlate with improved user-friendliness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The development effort yielded a teleoperation GUI that incorporated several enhancements compared to the legacy interface (Figure 5 vs. Figure 9), addressing the identified shortcomings and aligning with modern UI/HRI principles.

#### 4.1. Presentation of Results

The new GUI demonstrates the following characteristics:

- **Layout and Navigation:** The interface is structured across two distinct pages: one for operation and another for map editing. Functions are organized into clearly grouped panels, ensuring that related controls are situated together and less frequently used tools can be hidden. This separation aims to reduce screen clutter during active teleoperation.
- **Color Scheme:** A dark blue background is utilized to minimize glare. Bright orange buttons are employed to create high contrast against both the background and the live video feed, intended to direct the operator's attention to interactive elements.
- **Camera Emphasis:** The live camera feed is positioned centrally and occupies a larger portion of the display (Figure 9). This is complemented by keyboard shortcuts (WASD for robot motion, arrow keys for camera movement), designed to allow operators to simultaneously monitor the environment and input commands.
- **On-the-fly Tuning:** The interface includes sliders that enable operators to adjust parameters such as step length and turn angle in real-time (Figure 6). These values persist locally, allowing for dynamic adjustments during a run without necessitating a system restart.
- **Responsive Design:** The layout incorporates Flexbox scaling, which maintains the legibility and structural integrity of panels even when the window size is reduced (Figure 8 vs. Figure 7). Scrollbars are dynamically introduced only when components exceed their allocated screen area, preserving usability on smaller displays.
- **Collapsible Menus:** Sidebar buttons activate drop-down sub-menus that expand within the same frame (Figure 9). Each panel is equipped with its own scrollbar, ensuring that controls remain accessible even in confined viewing configurations.

#### 4.2. Discussion of Advances

The key advancements of the new GUI include:

- **Expanded Motion Capabilities:** The integration of previously absent motions such as crawling and self-standing significantly enhances the robot's ability to navigate obstacles and recover from falls, which was not possible with the legacy tool.
- **Dynamic Control Adjustment:** The introduction of fine-tune sliders for step and turn coefficients enables operators to adapt the robot's movements during a mission, a capability not present in the prior system.
- **Improved User Experience:** Compared to manual ROS commands or the legacy GUI, the redesigned layout offers reduced visual clutter, clearly labeled controls, and reliable head-pan sliders. These features collectively contribute to reinforcing the interface's usability for novice operators.
- **Enhanced Information Display:** The incorporation of internal robot status indicators (e.g., battery voltage, FPS, logs) provides operators with more comprehensive feedback regarding the robot's condition.

#### 4.3. Limitations

The research acknowledged two primary limitations:

- **Limited Human-Subject Testing:** The development process involved minimal human-subject testing during iterations, which constrains the empirical validation of the "user-friendly" claims. The evaluation was primarily qualitative and conducted by the developer.
- **Time Constraints:** Project timelines necessitated the postponement of certain additional features that could have further optimized competition performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of this intuitive GUI represents a notable advancement over the previous system utilized for teleoperating the humanoid robot in the FIRA-regulated obstacle course. The project successfully addressed critical issues related to cognitive load, system intuitiveness, and scalability.

#### 5.1. Direct Improvements and Accessibility

The introduction of new motions and actions, such as crawling and self-standing, along with responsive keyboard bindings, significantly enhanced the robot's ability to complete the obstacle course. This directly contributes to a more efficient and less cognitively demanding teleoperation experience. The logically grouped controls and consistent placement of navigational elements aim to provide a familiar and simplistic user experience, which is particularly beneficial for non-expert operators. This emphasis on user-friendly design aims to make humanoid robot teleoperation more accessible, reducing the training burden often associated with such complex systems.

#### 5.2. Broader Implications for Human-Robot Interaction (HRI)

The methodology employed, drawing upon established UI development tactics and iterative testing in realistic environments, reinforces the importance of human-centered design in robotics. The project suggests that prioritizing a human-friendly layout and curated user interactions can lead to more efficient operation and improved output from individual users, an idea supported by existing literature in HRI. The interface's design, which emphasizes camera-based feedback and dynamic control, could serve as a model for similar teleoperation systems.

#### 5.3. Future Directions

Despite the absence of formal user testing and time limitations, the resulting system represents a functional improvement. The project lays a foundation for future research, which could focus on quantitative measurements and evaluations involving non-expert participants. Such studies would provide empirical validation for the claimed usability enhancements and guide further system development. The scalable architecture implemented also suggests that the core principles and framework could be extended to other robotic platforms or more complex tasks beyond the FIRA HuroCup, potentially impacting high-risk domains like industrial automation, remote inspection, or disaster response, where intuitive and reliable teleoperation is paramount. The work highlights that investing in user-centric interface design is critical for unlocking the full potential of advanced robotic systems.
