# CRAFT: A Tendon-Driven Hand with Hybrid Hard-Soft Compliance

> [!summary] Summary
> The CRAFT hand is a tendon-driven robotic hand featuring a hybrid hard-soft compliance design that strategically places soft materials at joints for impact absorption and rigid materials for force transmission. It demonstrated superior holding strength of 15.29 N, reduced current consumption by 50% during holding tasks, and achieved 100% success on all 33 Feix grasp taxonomy types, enhancing robustness and dexterity for manipulating delicate objects compared to rigid counterparts.

## Problem

- Rigid robot hands offer precision and strong actuation but are susceptible to damage from impacts and collisions, often leading to bulky designs.
- Soft robotic hands provide passive compliance and robustness, yet they typically lack kinematic predictability, consistent force transmission, and sufficient load capacity for diverse tasks.
- Many dexterous hands are expensive, large, or require complex active impedance control to manage contact forces, hindering widespread adoption and robust real-world interaction.

## Method

- The **CRAFT** hand uses a ==hybrid hard-soft design== with rigid PLA for finger links and soft TPU for strategically placed ==rolling-contact joints==, ensuring both force transmission and impact absorption.
- ==Tendon-driven actuation==, with all 15 compact motors located in the forearm, to achieve a lightweight, anthropomorphic form factor and allow for 15 active and 5 passive degrees of freedom.
- An open-source, fully 3D-printable hand costing under $600, integrated with a vision-based teleoperation framework and compatible simulation models for accessible research and data collection.

## Results

- Demonstrated superior structural performance with **15.29 N** pull-out strength (nearly double a rigid baseline) and **50% lower** average current consumption during sustained object holding, while maintaining joint angle tracking error below **0.01 rad**.
- Achieved **100% success** across all **33 Feix grasp taxonomy types**, showing comprehensive dexterity and outperforming previous tendon-driven hands.
- Significantly improved teleoperation performance for fragile and low-friction objects, achieving **90% success** on eggs and **100%** on raspberries, where a rigid baseline often failed or caused damage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Strategically localizing soft materials at joints for impact absorption and hard materials for links enables a robot hand to achieve both kinematic predictability and robust interaction.
> - Rolling-contact joint surfaces within soft materials distribute stress and define motion paths, leading to more durable and kinematically predictable compliant joints compared to traditional flexure designs.
> - Integrating tendon-driven actuation with passive compliance allows for reduced motor workload and lower power consumption during sustained grasping, utilizing friction as a passive braking mechanism.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of dexterous robotic manipulation has historically explored two primary approaches: rigid, direct-drive hands and compliant, soft robotic hands. Classical rigid hands, such as the DLR-HIT Hand II and Allegro Hand, have demonstrated high precision, repeatable kinematics, and strong actuation. Recent open-source platforms like the LEAP Hand and D'Manus have made such dexterity more accessible. However, these rigid systems often suffer from significant drawbacks. Their rigid structures transmit impacts directly, making them susceptible to damage during collisions or suboptimal interactions, which are common in real-world data collection and operation. Furthermore, integrating motors within fingers or palms typically results in bulky, non-anthropomorphic form factors and high costs. To manage contact forces, these hands usually rely on complex active impedance control rather than inherent mechanical compliance.

Conversely, soft robotic hands, leveraging compliant materials, attempt to address the robustness issue by absorbing uncertainty passively. Examples include hands with elastomer flexure joints. While this passive compliance can reduce the need for precise control and absorb shocks, it introduces its own set of challenges. Soft hands often have limited load capacity and exhibit configuration-dependent kinematics, making their motion and force transmission difficult to model and repeat consistently. This unpredictability limits their utility in tasks requiring precise, repeatable movements or significant load bearing. Many existing compliant designs are also underactuated, restricting their dexterity for complex in-hand manipulation. Some hybrid soft-rigid architectures exist, such as Leap V2, but they can still be expensive or bulky.

Tendon-driven designs offer a solution to the bulkiness issue by relocating actuators to the forearm, achieving more compact and anthropomorphic form factors, as seen in the Shadow Hand, RUKA, and ORCA hands. However, most tendon-driven hands remain rigid, meaning they still rely on active control for interaction forces and face similar contact-related failure modes as direct-drive rigid hands. The fragility of tendons themselves, or the joints they actuate, remains a concern in some designs.

The current work positions itself at the intersection of these paradigms, aiming to develop a robotic hand that combines the structural predictability and load-bearing capacity of rigid systems with the contact robustness and adaptability of soft systems. The central gap identified is the uniform application of material choices across the hand, leading to either fragility (rigid) or structural unpredictability (soft). The paper proposes that compliance is most beneficial at joints for impact absorption and conforming contact, while rigidity is crucial for links to transmit forces reliably and maintain kinematic predictability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for developing the CRAFT hand stems from the fundamental tension in dexterous manipulation: the need for a robot hand to be sufficiently accurate for precise contact placement while simultaneously being robust enough to withstand the inevitable collisions, slips, and suboptimal interactions inherent in real-world operation, especially during large-scale data collection for robot learning.

The authors identify specific limitations in existing robot hand designs that CRAFT aims to overcome:
1. **Fragility of Rigid Hands:** Rigid hands transmit impacts directly, leading to frequent damage and interruptions in data collection, despite their precision and strong actuation.
2. **Lack of Predictability in Soft Hands:** While soft hands offer passive compliance, they often lack the structural integrity and kinematic predictability needed for consistent force transmission and repeatable motion, limiting their practical application, particularly for tasks requiring precise control.
3. **Bulky Form Factors and High Costs:** Many existing anthropomorphic hands, especially direct-drive ones, are large, heavy, and expensive, hindering their widespread adoption and integration into compact robotic systems. Tendon-driven hands mitigate bulkiness but often lack compliance.
4. **Inadequate Contact Management:** Both rigid and most tendon-driven hands rely heavily on complex active impedance control to manage contact forces, rather than leveraging inherent mechanical properties. This increases control complexity and computational load.
5. **Durability of Compliant Joints:** Traditional flexure joints in compliant designs are prone to fatigue and fracture under repeated bending cycles.

To address these issues, the key objectives and motivations behind the CRAFT hand design are:
- **Achieve Hybrid Hard-Soft Compliance:** The central objective is to localize compliance strategically, placing soft materials at joints to absorb impacts and facilitate conforming contact, while maintaining rigid links to ensure reliable force transmission and kinematic predictability. This aims to resolve the precision-robustness trade-off.
- **Ensure Predictable and Durable Compliant Joints:** A specific motivation is to overcome the challenges of unpredictable control and fatigue in soft joints. This involves developing mechanisms that allow soft joints to maintain repeatable motion paths and withstand repeated high-stress cycles.
- **Maintain a Compact, Anthropomorphic Form Factor:** By using tendon-driven actuation with motors located in the forearm, the goal is to create a hand that is human-like in size and weight, suitable for teleoperation and integration with robotic arms, while keeping fingers light and actuators protected from contact.
- **Reduce Cost and Promote Accessibility:** A significant objective is to make advanced anthropomorphic dexterity accessible. The hand is designed to be fully 3D-printable and cost under $600, with an open-source release to foster research and development in the robotics community.
- **Facilitate Data-Driven Learning:** The design aims to support robust and efficient data collection for reinforcement learning. This includes integration with vision-based teleoperation systems for intuitive control and provision of compatible simulation models.
- **Demonstrate General Dexterity and Manipulation Robustness:** The hand must be capable of executing a wide range of grasps (power, precision) and robustly handling diverse objects, including fragile, low-friction, and deformable items, without damage or failure.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The CRAFT hand employs a hybrid hard-soft design principle, using rigid polylactic acid (PLA) for finger links and soft thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) for finger joints. This structural distinction is motivated by the non-uniform distribution of contact forces across the hand, where impacts concentrate at joints and loads are carried by links.

**Hand Structure and Materials:**
The entire hand, weighing 800g and costing under $600, is designed for 3D printing. It measures 95 mm in palm length and 103 mm in finger length, aligning with human anthropometric data. It features 15 active and 5 passive degrees of freedom (DoF), driven by 15 compact actuators located behind the wrist in the forearm, keeping the hand itself light and compact.

**Finger and Joint Structure:**
Each finger and the thumb (with analogous joints: carpometacarpal (CMC), metacarpophalangeal (MP), interphalangeal (IP)) are designed with specific mechanisms:
- **Coupled PIP/DIP Joints (and MP/IP for thumb):** These joints are composed entirely of soft TPU to allow elastic deformation and absorb impact. A single tendon drives both the PIP and DIP joints through a bidirectional TPU linkage. This linkage mechanically couples the joints, ensuring they bend equally and simultaneously regardless of external load, thereby producing repeatable finger postures.
- **Rolling-Contact Joints:** To enhance durability and predictability, rolling-contact joint surfaces are implemented at the PIP/DIP (and MP/IP) locations. Instead of bending at a fixed point like traditional flexure joints, these joints rotate along a constrained curved interface. This mechanism distributes stress across the TPU surface, reducing stress concentrations that typically lead to fatigue and fracture in flexure joints. It also ensures a well-defined kinematic path, maintaining motion predictability.
- **MCP Joint (and CMC for thumb):** The metacarpophalangeal (MCP) joint at the base of each finger (and carpometacarpal (CMC) for the thumb) utilizes a cylindrical snap-fit interface. This design simplifies assembly and allows the joint to "pop out" under excessive force, preventing breakage. Each MCP joint has two independent DoFs: flexion/extension and abduction/adduction.
- **Actuation and Tendon Routing:** Each finger is actuated by three tendons: one for coupled PIP/DIP flexion, one for MCP flexion/extension, and one for MCP abduction/adduction. These tendons are made of high-strength braided line and are routed over low-friction metal dowel pins, which also provide structural support. Passive elastic bands return the PIP/DIP joints to their neutral (extended) position when tendon tension is released. The motors, arranged in a compact pentagonal shape in the forearm, incorporate ratchet spools for quick tendon re-tensioning.
- **Modularity:** The design emphasizes modularity, allowing individual fingers to be quickly swapped by detaching dowel pins, without requiring extensive disassembly or re-wiring of tendons.

**Teleoperation and Simulation Support:**
- **Teleoperation:** To enable intuitive control for data collection, CRAFT integrates with a vision-based teleoperation framework. This involves:
 1. **Calibration:** A joint-wise calibration procedure aligns the operator's biological range of motion (measured via HaMeR) with the robot's hardware limits. A linear function maps the normalized operator joint position to the robot's corresponding angle.
 2. **Whole-Arm and Hand Control:** A single RGB camera captures the operator. FrankMocap estimates the 3D pose of the operator's wrist for whole-arm retargeting, while HaMeR estimates the operator's hand pose for finger retargeting. These estimated poses are smoothed using an exponential moving average and then mapped to the CRAFT hand, mounted on a robotic arm, allowing simultaneous control without external sensors or gloves on the operator.
- **Simulation:** For reinforcement learning and policy development, CRAFT provides URDF and XML models compatible with widely adopted physics simulators (e.g., MuJoCo). The physical behavior of soft materials is approximated by modeling the rolling-contact joints as revolute joints with equality constraints enforcing surface contact conditions, maintaining computational efficiency while preserving essential kinematics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the CRAFT hand focused on structural performance, manipulation capability via teleoperation, and overall dexterity.

**A. Structural Tests:**
CRAFT was benchmarked against the LEAP hand (a rigid, direct-drive baseline) using Dynamixel XL330-M288-T motors for a fair comparison.
- **Pull-out Test (Strength):** CRAFT demonstrated superior holding strength, withstanding 15.29 N of pull-out force, nearly double the 8.67 N achieved by the LEAP hand. This was attributed to the mechanical advantage provided by CRAFT's tendon routing system, which reduces direct torque demand on the motors.
- **Repeatability Test (Precision):** Both CRAFT and LEAP maintained consistent performance over one hour of continuous grasp-and-release cycles on a plush toy. CRAFT exhibited a mean joint angle tracking error below 0.01 rad, comparable to the rigid LEAP baseline, indicating that the benefits of compliance were achieved without sacrificing kinematic repeatability.
- **Holding Test (Endurance):** When grasping and holding a 5 lb dumbbell for one hour, CRAFT exhibited approximately 50% lower average current consumption compared to LEAP. CRAFT's current draw stabilized well below the motor's limit, with the tendon routing's inherent friction acting as a passive braking mechanism, reducing the active torque required from the motors. In contrast, LEAP, a direct-drive system, required constantly higher torque, leading to greater power consumption and thermal stress.

**B. Teleoperation Tests:**
A user study comparing CRAFT and LEAP on five objects with varying manipulation challenges (ball, wine glass, egg, raspberry, frito chip) demonstrated CRAFT's improved utility, particularly for fragile and low-friction items.
- **Grasping a Ball (Rigid Object):** The LEAP hand experienced longer completion times and lower success rates due to its bulky structure, which hindered effective grasping. CRAFT's passive conformity played a minor role, but its compact design was advantageous.
- **Lifting a Wine Glass (Large, Fragile):** Both hands achieved 100% success. However, users operating CRAFT completed the task significantly faster (more than twice as fast for LEAP), exhibiting higher confidence due to the hand's passive compliance in managing contact forces.
- **Handling an Egg (Low Friction, Fragile):** CRAFT achieved a 90% success rate and was approximately 15 seconds faster on average. LEAP frequently failed (60% failure rate) or took significantly longer to find a stable grasp due to low friction and lack of compliant surfaces. CRAFT's compliant surfaces increased contact area, mitigating these risks.
- **Picking a Raspberry and a Frito Chip (Small, Delicate, Deformable):** CRAFT achieved 100% success on both tasks. The rigid LEAP hand frequently crushed these delicate objects upon contact, resulting in a 60% success rate. CRAFT's soft fingers absorbed contact forces, preventing damage. While precise alignment still took time for both hands, CRAFT's compliance was a deciding factor for success.

**C. Grasp Taxonomy Assessment:**
CRAFT was evaluated against the Feix grasp taxonomy, which includes 33 distinct human grasp types. CRAFT successfully executed all 33/33 grasps, demonstrating comprehensive dexterity. This contrasts with previous tendon-driven hands like RUKA, which achieved 29/33. The hybrid design allowed CRAFT to leverage tendon-driven torque for power grasps (e.g., Large Diameter) and compliant materials for conformal contact in precision grasps (e.g., Prismatic 2 Finger), while maintaining kinematic range for challenging configurations like the Adduction Grip, Writing Tripod, and Extension Type.

**D. Whole Arm Teleoperation:**
CRAFT successfully performed various open-world tasks, including wiping a whiteboard, picking up fragile objects (egg, raspberry), twisting a key, and pouring from a wine glass. These demonstrations showcased the hand's ability to handle diverse manipulation scenarios without object damage or grasp failure, confirming the practical functionality of the hybrid design across a spectrum of tasks.

**E. Structural Comparison to Representative Robot Hands:**
- **RUKA:** CRAFT offers superior dexterity by incorporating abduction/adduction DoF, which RUKA lacks, enabling inter-finger grasps and use of tools like chopsticks/pens.
- **ORCA:** CRAFT's active DIP joint provides more effective curling range and fingertip precision compared to ORCA's fixed DIP joint.
- **Leap V2:** CRAFT maintains a more compact, anthropomorphic form factor due to the full relocation of actuators to the forearm. This allows CRAFT to access confined spaces where the bulkier Leap V2 (with some actuators in the palm/fingers) cannot.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CRAFT hand represents a development in dexterous robotic manipulation by addressing the long-standing trade-off between precision and robustness, particularly for contact-rich tasks in robot learning. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

- **Resolution of Precision-Robustness Trade-off:** The hybrid hard-soft compliance, with rigid links for load transmission and soft rolling-contact joints for impact absorption and force regulation, offers a balanced solution. This design allows for repeatable kinematics while inherently managing unexpected contact, reducing reliance on complex active control strategies.
- **Enhanced Durability and Predictability:** The coupled rolling-contact joints are a key innovation. By distributing stress and constraining motion, they provide soft joints that are both durable under repeated use and kinematically predictable, overcoming common limitations of traditional flexure joints. This has direct implications for reducing hardware maintenance and improving data collection reliability.
- **Cost-Effectiveness and Accessibility:** At under $600 and fully 3D-printable, CRAFT significantly lowers the barrier to entry for researchers and developers in robot learning and dexterous manipulation. The open-source release further promotes widespread adoption, reproduction, and iterative development within the community, accelerating progress in the field.
- **Improved Manipulation of Challenging Objects:** The teleoperation studies demonstrate CRAFT's superior performance in handling fragile, low-friction, and deformable objects. This capability is critical for real-world applications where robots must interact with a diverse array of items without causing damage.
- **Comprehensive Dexterity and Anthropomorphism:** Achieving full coverage of the 33 Feix grasp taxonomy types within a compact, anthropomorphic form factor validates CRAFT's versatility for general-purpose manipulation. The forearm-mounted actuation contributes to a human-like hand size, crucial for integration into human-centric environments and tasks.
- **Facilitation of Robot Learning and Data Collection:** The integrated vision-based teleoperation framework and compatible simulation models (URDF, MuJoCo XML) provide a complete toolchain for efficient data collection and policy learning. The hand's inherent compliance reduces the cognitive load on operators during teleoperation, making demonstrations more tolerant to imprecision and thus accelerating data acquisition for machine learning.
- **Modular Design for Practicality:** The modularity, allowing quick replacement of damaged fingers, enhances the hand's practicality and reduces downtime, which is particularly valuable in research and industrial settings involving high-volume, contact-intensive operations.

In summary, CRAFT provides an affordable, robust, and dexterous robotic hand designed for the practical realities of contact-rich manipulation and data-driven learning. Its open-source nature and demonstrated performance across structural, manipulation, and dexterity benchmarks position it as a significant contribution poised to advance research in robotic manipulation and make dexterous robotics more accessible.
