# Fast-WAM: Do World Action Models Need Test-time Future Imagination?

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tsinghua University and Galaxea AI developed Fast-WAM, a World Action Model (WAM) that achieves competitive robotic task performance with significantly reduced inference latency by leveraging video co-training during learning but skipping explicit future imagination at test time. This approach attains up to 97.6% success rates on LIBERO and RoboTwin benchmarks and over 4x faster inference compared to imagine-then-execute WAMs.

## Problem

- Existing World Action Models (WAMs) often suffer from high test-time latency due to iterative future visual prediction using an "imagine-then-execute" paradigm.
- The source of WAMs' performance benefits (training-time video modeling vs. test-time future imagination) has been unclear, as these factors are typically entangled in current designs.
- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, while generalizable, often lack explicit modeling of physical world dynamics and temporal evolution under actions.

## Method

- **Fast-WAM**, a ==Mixture-of-Transformer== architecture, decouples video co-training during the learning phase from explicit future imagination at inference.
- It employs a ==joint flow matching objective== to train both an action expert and a video backbone on shared initial visual context, with ==structured attention== preventing direct future video leakage into action prediction.
- During inference, the future video branch is entirely removed, enabling a single forward pass for action generation based on learned latent world features, which drastically reduces latency.

## Results

- Fast-WAM achieved competitive success rates on simulation benchmarks (**91.8%** on RoboTwin, **97.6%** on LIBERO) without extensive embodied pretraining, matching or exceeding many state-of-the-art pretrained WAMs.
- Controlled experiments demonstrated that removing the video co-training objective during training resulted in a substantial performance drop (e.g., to **83.8%** on RoboTwin), while Fast-WAM performed comparably to imagine-then-execute variants.
- Fast-WAM exhibited a low inference latency of **190 ms** on a real-world towel-folding task, which is over **4 times faster** than imagine-then-execute variants (e.g., Fast-WAM-IDM at 810 ms), while maintaining strong task performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The video modeling objective applied during training is a more critical factor for WAM performance than the explicit generation of future observations at test time - the performance drop caused by removing video co-training was consistently larger than the differences between Fast-WAM and imagine-then-execute variants.
> - Robust, physically meaningful world representations can be acquired through training-time video co-training without necessitating iterative future video generation during inference.
> - Decoupling training-time world modeling from inference-time generation can yield competitive performance with significantly improved efficiency for real-time robotic control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied control is progressing towards developing general-purpose agents capable of complex interactions with the physical world. This requires policies that can not only map visual observations to actions but also reason about the dynamic evolution of the world under those actions. Two primary paradigms have emerged to address this challenge: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World Action Models (WAMs).

VLA models directly map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions, often leveraging large pretrained vision-language backbones. These models benefit from strong semantic priors acquired through web-scale pretraining on static image-text data, demonstrating generalization across objects, scenes, and language instructions. However, a limitation of standard VLA pretraining is its lack of explicit modeling for how the physical world changes over time in response to actions.

WAMs represent a parallel line of research that integrates future visual prediction with action modeling within a unified framework. The core appeal of WAMs is their explicit modeling of future observations, which is hypothesized to capture physical dynamics and task-relevant temporal structures more effectively than VLAs. Most existing WAMs operate under an "imagine-then-execute" paradigm, where they first generate future observations (often through iterative video denoising) and then predict actions conditioned on these imagined futures. While conceptually intuitive, this approach introduces substantial test-time latency, which can hinder real-time deployment. Some WAMs also jointly model future video and actions within a shared generative process.

This paper positions itself by questioning a fundamental assumption within the WAM paradigm: whether the explicit generation of future observations at test time is genuinely necessary for strong action performance, or if the benefits of video modeling primarily stem from its role during the training phase. Previous WAM systems often entangle these two factors, making it challenging to ascertain the precise source of their observed performance gains. This work aims to disentangle these contributions, proposing an architecture that retains the training benefits of video modeling while eliminating the test-time cost of explicit future imagination, thereby bridging aspects of WAMs and efficient direct-policy interfaces characteristic of VLAs. The research also relates to other efforts exploring video modeling for action prediction while reducing or bypassing explicit test-time video synthesis, such as VPP and UVA, but distinguishes itself by its focused investigation into the disentangled roles of training-time video co-training and test-time future imagination through controlled experimental variants.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate a fundamental question regarding World Action Models (WAMs): "Do WAMs need explicit future imagination at test time, or do their benefits come primarily from learning to model them during training?"

This question is motivated by several considerations:

1. **Test-time Latency of Existing WAMs:** Current WAMs predominantly follow an "imagine-then-execute" paradigm. This typically involves iterative video denoising to generate future observations, which can incur substantial computational overhead and lead to high test-time latency. For real-world robotic applications, high latency is a significant impediment to responsive and real-time control.
2. **Entangled Factors of WAM Effectiveness:** The observed performance improvements in WAMs could originate from two distinct, yet often coupled, sources:
 - **Training-time video prediction objective:** The act of learning to predict future visual states during training may compel the model to acquire more robust, physically meaningful latent representations and stronger priors about world dynamics. This could improve the model's understanding of how actions influence the environment.
 - **Inference-time explicit future generation:** Actively synthesizing future observations during inference could provide the policy with explicit foresight, enabling it to plan actions based on predicted outcomes.
 Existing WAM architectures typically intertwine these two factors, making it difficult to determine their individual contributions to overall performance.
3. **Efficiency and Design Optimization:** If the primary value of world modeling lies in shaping superior latent representations during training, then explicitly generating future videos at inference time might be an unnecessary computational burden. Decoupling these factors could lead to the design of more computationally efficient WAM architectures that retain performance while reducing inference costs, making them more suitable for real-world deployment.

By addressing this question, the authors aim to gain a clearer understanding of the underlying mechanisms that contribute to WAMs' effectiveness, which could inform the development of more efficient and performant embodied agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces **Fast-WAM**, an architecture designed to disentangle the benefits of video co-training during training from explicit future imagination during inference.

**Problem Formulation:**
A standard visuomotor policy models `p(a_1:H | o, l)`, directly mapping observations `o` and language instructions `l` to an action chunk `a_1:H`. Traditional WAMs extend this using an "imagine-then-execute" factorization, `p(a_1:H | o, l) = ∫ p(v_1:T | o, l) p(a_1:H | o, l, v_1:T) dv_1:T`, where `v_1:T` are future visual observations. Fast-WAM, in contrast, predicts actions directly, `p_θ(a_1:H | o, l)`, but parameterizes this distribution using latent world representations `z(o, l)` derived from a video backbone, `p_θ(a_1:H | z(o, l))`. The crucial difference is that `z(o, l)` is obtained via a *single forward encoding pass* at inference time, without explicit sampling or denoising of future observations.

**Model Architecture:**
Fast-WAM is built upon the **Wan2.2-5B video Diffusion Transformer (DiT)**, which serves as the world modeling backbone. It utilizes the pretrained text encoder for language instructions and a pretrained video VAE to map visual observations into latent video tokens.
The overall structure is a **Mixture-of-Transformer (MoT)** architecture with shared attention, comprising a video DiT and an action expert DiT.

- **Input Tokens:** Input tokens are categorized into three groups:
 1. **Clean latent tokens** of the first observation frame (f₀), serving as a shared visual anchor.
 2. **Noisy latent tokens** of future video frames (f₁...fₕ), used exclusively during training for video modeling.
 3. **Action tokens** (a₁...aₕ), processed by the action expert for action generation.
- **Structured Attention Mask:** A carefully designed attention mask controls information flow:
 - **Training:** Future noisy video tokens attend bidirectionally within the video branch and can access the clean first-frame tokens. Action tokens attend bidirectionally within the action branch and can also access the clean first-frame tokens. Critically, action tokens are prevented from attending to future video tokens, ensuring that action prediction is grounded in the current visual context and does not leak future information. The clean first-frame tokens do not attend to any other tokens.
 - **Inference:** The future video branch is completely removed. Only the clean first-frame latent tokens (f₀) are passed through the video backbone in a single forward pass. These resulting latent world features are then used by the action expert for direct action generation, bypassing any explicit future video denoising. This design aims to achieve substantially lower inference cost.

**Training Objective:**
Fast-WAM is trained with a joint **flow matching objective** for both action tokens and future video latents.
Given a target variable `y` (action chunk `a_1:H` or future video latents `z_1:T`), Gaussian noise `ϵ`, and a time step `t`, an interpolated sample `y_t = (1 − t) y + t ϵ` is constructed. The model `f_θ` is trained to predict the velocity field with the objective:
`L_FM(y) = E_{y, ϵ, t} [∥f_θ(y_t, t, o, l) − (ϵ − y)∥²]`.

- **Action Prediction Loss:** `L_act = L_FM(a_1:H)`
- **Video Co-training Loss:** `L_vid = L_FM(z_1:T)`, where `z_1:T` are latent tokens of future video frames from the VAE.
- **Overall Loss:** `L = L_act + λL_vid`, where `λ` is a balancing hyperparameter.

**Controlled Variants for Disentangled WAM Design:**
To specifically address the central research question, the authors implemented several controlled variants within the same framework:

1. **Fast-WAM-Joint:** This variant emulates the "joint-generation" WAM paradigm where future video tokens and action tokens are denoised concurrently with shared attention. This maintains the coupling between action generation and future video modeling throughout the denoising process.
2. **Fast-WAM-IDM (Inverse Dynamics Model):** This variant mimics the "video-then-action" WAM paradigm. Future video tokens are first generated from the current observation and language context, and then action prediction is conditioned on these generated future representations.
3. **Fast-WAM w.o. video co-train:** This ablation variant retains the same architecture and inference procedure as Fast-WAM but removes the `L_vid` objective during training. This directly assesses the contribution of the video modeling objective itself, independent of test-time imagination.

These variants enable a direct comparison to isolate the impact of test-time future imagination from that of the training-time video co-training objective.

**Implementation Details:**
The base model uses the pretrained Wan2.2-5B backbone. The action expert has a reduced hidden dimension (d_a = 1024), resulting in a total model size of 6B parameters. An action horizon `H=32` is used, and video frames are temporally downsampled by 4x to 9 frames per chunk, with multiple camera images concatenated. Training employs AdamW optimizer, a cosine annealing learning rate schedule, mixed precision, and gradient clipping. Inference uses 10 denoising steps with classifier-free guidance. Latency measurements were performed on an NVIDIA RTX 5090D V2 32GB GPU. For Fast-WAM-IDM, noise augmentation is applied to ground-truth video tokens during training as per prior work.

**Experiment Setup:**
Evaluations were conducted on:
- **Simulation Benchmarks:**
 - **LIBERO:** Four suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, Long), 10 tasks each, 500 demonstrations, trained for 20k steps. Success rates are reported over 2000 trials across 40 tasks.
 - **RoboTwin 2.0:** Challenging bimanual manipulation, over 50 tasks, trained on a mixture of 2,500 clean and 25,000 heavily randomized demonstrations for 30k steps. Average success rates reported over 100 trials per task under clean and randomized settings.
- **Real-World Task:**
 - **Towel Folding:** A long-horizon, deformable object manipulation task. 60 hours of teleoperated demonstrations were collected on the Galaxea R1 Lite platform. Models trained for 30k steps. Metrics included average success rate and average completion time.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to evaluate Fast-WAM's overall performance and, crucially, to disentangle the roles of video co-training and test-time future imagination using the controlled variants.

#### 4.1. Overall Comparison on Simulation Benchmarks

- **RoboTwin:** Fast-WAM achieved a 91.8% average success rate. This performance exceeded all baselines that did not utilize embodied pretraining and remained competitive with state-of-the-art pretrained WAMs. Specifically, Fast-WAM significantly outperformed Motus (87.8% with pretraining, 77.3% without) and LingBot-VA without pretraining (80.6%), while being comparable to pretrained LingBot-VA (92.2%). These results suggest that Fast-WAM can recover most of the benefits of previous WAM pipelines without requiring extensive embodied pretraining.
- **LIBERO:** Fast-WAM achieved a strong overall average success rate of 97.6% without embodied pretraining. It surpassed the VLA baseline π0.5 and maintained competitive performance with pretrained WAM baselines such as LingBot-VA (98.5%) and Motus (97.7%). This indicates consistent effectiveness of Fast-WAM across diverse simulation environments.

#### 4.2. Controlled Comparison with Fast-WAM Variants

The controlled experiments, comparing Fast-WAM with its imagine-then-execute variants and the no-video-co-training ablation, revealed a consistent pattern across both simulation benchmarks:

- **Comparability with Imagine-then-Execute:** Fast-WAM, which foregoes explicit test-time future imagination, demonstrated performance highly comparable to its imagine-then-execute counterparts (Fast-WAM-Joint and Fast-WAM-IDM).
 - On **RoboTwin**, Fast-WAM (91.8%) was similar to Fast-WAM-Joint (90.6%) and Fast-WAM-IDM (91.3%).
 - On **LIBERO**, Fast-WAM (97.6%) remained close to Fast-WAM-Joint (98.5%) and Fast-WAM-IDM (98.0%).
- **Significant Performance Drop without Video Co-training:** In stark contrast, removing the video co-training objective entirely resulted in a substantially larger performance degradation.
 - On **RoboTwin**, Fast-WAM without video co-training dropped to 83.8%, a considerable gap compared to all three variants that utilized video co-training.
 - On **LIBERO**, Fast-WAM without video co-training decreased to 93.5%, with particularly noticeable performance reductions on the Spatial and Long subsets.
 This pattern suggests that the training-time video modeling objective is a more dominant factor in achieving performance gains than the explicit generation of future observations at test time.

#### 4.3. Real-World Performance and Efficiency

Evaluation on the long-horizon real-world towel-folding task provided further insights into both task performance and execution efficiency.

- **Task Success and Completion Time:**
 - Pretrained π0.5 achieved the highest success rate and the shortest completion time.
 - Among the Fast-WAM family, variants with video co-training (Fast-WAM, Fast-WAM-Joint, Fast-WAM-IDM) substantially outperformed π0.5 *without* pretraining, indicating that WAM-style video co-training confers significant data efficiency even without additional embodied pretraining.
 - Critically, Fast-WAM without video co-training experienced a dramatic degradation, achieving only a 10% success rate and requiring the longest completion time among all methods. This large gap further reinforces that video co-training is a primary driver of strong real-world performance.
- **Inference Latency:**
 - Fast-WAM achieved a low inference latency of 190 ms, enabling real-time operation.
 - The imagine-then-execute variants were substantially slower; for example, Fast-WAM-IDM registered 810 ms latency. This represents more than a 4x speed improvement for Fast-WAM compared to imagine-then-execute WAMs.

These real-world results underscore that video co-training is the dominant factor for performance, while Fast-WAM's ability to maintain strong task performance with significantly reduced inference cost makes it a more practical design for real-world robotic deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a fundamental insight into the mechanisms driving the effectiveness of World Action Models (WAMs) for embodied control. The core finding is that the primary value of video prediction in WAMs lies in its role during **training**—specifically, in improving the learning of robust and physically meaningful world representations—rather than in the explicit generation of future observations at **test time**.

The significance and potential impact of this work are several-fold:

1. **Re-evaluation of WAM Design Paradigms:** The study challenges the prevalent "imagine-then-execute" paradigm in WAMs. It suggests that while incorporating world modeling is beneficial, the computational overhead associated with explicit future imagination during inference may not yield commensurate performance gains. This could lead to a shift in architectural design, moving towards more efficient WAMs that leverage video modeling for representation learning during training while maintaining a direct, low-latency action generation process during inference.
2. **Improved Efficiency for Real-World Robotics:** A key practical outcome is Fast-WAM's ability to achieve competitive performance while operating in real time with significantly reduced inference latency (190 ms, over 4x faster than imagine-then-execute WAMs). This addresses a critical barrier to deploying WAM-based policies in real-world robotic applications, where responsive and timely control is essential. This efficiency can enable more dynamic, interactive, and high-frequency robotic operations.
3. **Enhanced Understanding of Embodied Intelligence:** By disentangling the contributions of training objectives and inference procedures, the research deepens the understanding of how embodied agents acquire knowledge about the physical world. It emphasizes the importance of learning rich, action-conditioned representations through predictive tasks, irrespective of whether these predictions are explicitly materialized during online execution.
4. **Data Efficiency:** The results indicate that WAM-style video co-training provides strong data efficiency, enabling competitive performance even without extensive embodied pretraining that many state-of-the-art VLA and WAM models rely on. This is significant for scenarios where large-scale embodied pretraining data may be scarce or expensive to acquire.
5. **Future Research Directions:** The findings open new avenues for research into how to best integrate world modeling into robot learning. Future work could explore optimizing video co-training objectives, investigating the impact of different forms of representation learning, and studying how these design principles scale with larger models and datasets. The paper explicitly points to investigating the effect of larger-scale pretraining data and model scaling on this design.

In conclusion, this research provides both theoretical clarification on the benefits of world modeling in robotics and a practical, efficient architecture (Fast-WAM) that can advance the deployment of sophisticated embodied control systems.
