# ActionGround: Training-Free Runtime Refinement of Frozen VLA Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> ActionGround is a training-free, neuro-symbolic runtime layer that wraps frozen VLA policies without access to their weights. A geometric finite-state machine detects the manipulation phase and applies a rule-based correction for that phase. An inertia-weighted Euler-Lagrange term refines the action at every step. All corrections are capped at a 5% blend and add under 1 ms. Across four backbones on LIBERO-Spatial, success rises by up to 6 points and stability by up to 19 points.

## Problem

- End-to-end VLA policies fit demonstrations but encode neither discrete manipulation phases nor rigid-body equations of motion, so they emit inconsistent actions such as lateral motion during grasping.
- Uniform temporal smoothing, such as the ACT and Octo exponential moving averages, reduces jerk but flattens responsive contact motions, cutting OpenVLA success on LIBERO-Spatial from 36% to 28%.
- Physics-informed methods such as PINN losses, Lagrangian networks, CBF filters and MPPI act during training, need dynamics gradients, or require controller redesign, so they cannot improve frozen policies.

## Method

- ==Phase-aware finite-state machine==: geometric distance and height predicates classify each step as approach, grasp, transport or place, then apply a ==grasp veto==, waypoint bias, lift-plus-smoothing, or deceleration ramp.
- Always-on ==Euler-Lagrange== branch: inertia, Coriolis and gravity terms taken from MuJoCo give a residual that is only logged as a diagnostic, while ==softmax inverse-inertia weights== blend the phase-corrected and raw actions.
- ==Global capped blender==: the executed action is 95% VLA output and 5% physics candidate, with a hard gripper override, kept fixed across all backbones and tasks and costing about 0.6 ms per step.

## Results

- LIBERO-Spatial success rises from **36%** to **38%** on OpenVLA, **40%** to **44%** on Force-VLA and **36%** to **42%** on Generalist-VLA, while OpenVLA-OFT ties at **92%**. Stability gains reach **19.3 points**.
- The temporal smoothing baseline lowers success on every single-step backbone, for example Generalist-VLA from **36%** to **26%**. ActionGround regresses only OpenVLA tasks T3 and T6 (**100%** to **60%**), and T4 and T9 stay at **0%**.
- On a Robosuite Lift noise sweep, ActionGround's jerk grows only **17%** versus **175%** for the baseline, roughly **10x** more robust. A matched-seed injected-bias run lifts success from **35%** to **95%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Runtime corrections should depend on the task phase: grasping needs responsive motion while transport tolerates smoothing, so uniform action filters trade task success for trajectory stability.
> - Action chunking already provides most of the step-level coherence that runtime correctors target, so wrapper gains shrink sharply on chunked, near-ceiling policies such as OpenVLA-OFT.
> - A small capped correction applied after the policy cannot fix occlusion or sub-centimetre precision failures. Hand-set geometric phase rules, simulator dynamics and single-seed, 5-trial evaluation limit how far the gains generalize.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot is asked to put a bowl on a plate. Under an injected placement-phase bias, the unmodified policy leaves the plate empty. ActionGround adds a small, phase-aware correction between the policy and the robot. It adjusts the policy's proposed movement just before execution instead of retraining the policy. In the matched-seed Robosuite simulation companion, the same OpenVLA weights are used in both modes. With 20 trials per mode, success rises from 35% for Baseline to 95% for ActionGround. This is a simulation result, not the physical-arm result.

Baseline is labeled "Task FAILED," with the plate empty. ActionGround is labeled "Task PASSED," with the bowl on the plate. With n=20, success is 35% versus 95%. This is a simulation comparison under injected placement-phase bias.

### 2. Why smooth actions are not enough

A vision-language-action (VLA) policy maps camera images and a language instruction to a seven-dimensional robot command. Six dimensions specify a change in end-effector pose, and the seventh controls the gripper. The authors argue that such policies lack an explicit representation of manipulation phases and rigid-body dynamics. They describe two recurring failure modes. Lateral motion at contact can prompt an early close or empty grasp, and entering placement too fast can overshoot a sub-centimetre target. Uniform exponential moving average (EMA) smoothing can dampen jitter, but it can also flatten the responsive movements needed during contact. These are the authors' qualitative diagnoses.

ActionGround reads simulator state and applies a correction chosen for the current task phase. A finite-state machine, which is a small rule-based state tracker, labels approach, grasp, transport, or place from geometry. Each phase gets its own rule:

- **Approach:** it vetoes premature gripper closing.
- **Grasp:** it biases movement toward a grasp waypoint without smoothing.
- **Transport:** it adds a 2 cm upward bias and uses transport-only EMA.
- **Place:** it scales movement down as the robot nears the plate.

The upper, red-outlined branch extracts bowl and plate distances, detects a phase, and shows the approach–grasp–transport–place sequence. The lower, blue-outlined branch takes robot state into a dynamics oracle, a residual diagnostic, and inertia weighting. Both branches feed a candidate correction, and a final capped blend combines that candidate with the VLA action. The diagram labels the lower path a "Gate," but the residual is logged only. It does not gate or scale the executed correction.

### 3. What the comparisons establish

LIBERO-Spatial contains ten rigid-object pick-and-place tasks for a 7-DoF Franka Panda arm. The aggregate table averages success and stability over the ten tasks, with five trials per task, at seed 7. Success is the binary rate of task completion. Stability is one minus normalized cumulative end-effector jerk. The table compares three conditions: Baseline, uniform EMA, and ActionGround. Its ActionGround results are Branch A only, which is the phase-rule branch. The seed is a single representative one, and the full four-backbone comparison was not repeated across ten seeds.

Generalist-VLA shows the main comparison clearly:

- **Success:** 36% for Baseline, 26% with temporal smoothing, and 42% with ActionGround.
- **Stability:** 29.9%, 39.2%, and 49.2%, respectively.

These are absolute percentage-point changes. Smoothing raises stability while lowering success. ActionGround improves both relative to Baseline.

Across the four tested policies, Branch A raises aggregate stability on all four and aggregate success on three. OpenVLA-OFT ties its 92% Baseline success while gaining stability.

There are exceptions at the task level. OpenVLA's aggregate result includes per-task regressions on T3 and T6, each from 100% to 60%. T4 and T9 remain at 0% on every single-step backbone.

### 4. Contribution and limits

The paper shows that a phase-selected runtime rule, placed outside a frozen policy, can improve stability without the success loss observed with uniform smoothing on the tested single-step policies. This contrast does not extend to OpenVLA-OFT, where EMA is redundant rather than harmful.

The Euler–Lagrange branch is tested separately on OpenVLA only. The paper says a constant-weight control is needed to isolate the contribution of the adaptive inertia weighting. The real Agilex Piper experiment is a qualitative deployment demonstration, not a quantified hardware success-rate study. The full evaluation is limited to rigid-object pick-and-place.
