# VIDP: Variable Impedance Diffusion Policy for Compliant Robot Manipulation from Diverse Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> Variable Impedance Diffusion Policy (VIDP) is a framework that learns dynamic robot compliance from diverse position-only demonstrations, predicting variable impedance profiles to enable adaptable manipulation in contact-rich tasks without requiring force sensors. It consistently outperforms fixed-impedance baselines in success rates, reduces interaction forces, and improves energy efficiency across tasks like peg-in-hole insertion and cable routing.

## Problem

- Extracting variable impedance profiles from position-only demonstrations is challenging because stiffness is a hidden, unobservable variable.
- Diverse kinematic demonstrations can create ambiguity, making it difficult to differentiate intentional compliance from simple geometric adaptations to varying spatial layouts.
- Existing imitation learning methods either rely on expensive multi-modal sensing (e.g., force feedback) or lack spatial generalization when learning from kinematic data.

## Method

- Introduces **Variable Impedance Diffusion Policy (VIDP)**, an imitation learning framework that predicts variable impedance profiles solely from position-only demonstrations.
- Employs a novel ==Task-Parameterized Directionality-Aware Mixture Model (TP-DAMM)== to estimate spatially and directionally consistent trajectory distributions from diverse demonstrations.
- Maps the variability within learned trajectory distributions to target Cartesian stiffness profiles and trains a ==Diffusion Policy== to jointly predict pose actions and inferred stiffness for real-time execution.

## Results

- TP-DAMM successfully extracts physically and directionally consistent motion primitives from diverse demonstrations, outperforming GMM, DAMM, and TP-GMM in quantitative metrics.
- VIDP achieved higher success rates in contact-rich tasks, reaching **70%** for Pulley Assembly (vs. **35%** for Stiff-DP) and **75%** for Cable Routing (vs. **0%** for Stiff-DP), demonstrating adaptability.
- Compared to fixed-impedance baselines, VIDP significantly reduced peak and mean interaction forces, maintained lower tracking errors, and improved energy efficiency across tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Trajectory variability in diverse kinematic demonstrations can be leveraged to infer the underlying compliance requirements of a task.
> - Directionality-aware modeling, relative to multiple task-specific frames, is crucial for distinguishing intentional yielding from mere spatial deviations in varied demonstrations.
> - Jointly learning end-effector pose and dynamic stiffness parameters within a diffusion policy enables adaptive, compliant robot manipulation without the need for direct force sensing.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation, particularly for contact-rich tasks, necessitates a balance between precise trajectory tracking and mechanical compliance to ensure operational safety, energy efficiency, and task success. Traditional methods often rely on static compliance, which has limitations in unstructured or dynamically changing environments. This has led to the development of variable impedance control, where a robot's mechanical compliance (stiffness and damping) can be dynamically adjusted throughout a task.

Learning variable impedance skills from demonstrations is a promising paradigm, as it avoids the complex analytical modeling required for manual programming. However, a fundamental challenge arises because desired compliance, typically represented as stiffness, is a hidden variable in kinematic (position-only) demonstrations. While trajectory variations in demonstrations can be indicative of compliance, they can also reflect geometric adaptations to changing spatial layouts, leading to ambiguity in inferring intentional compliance versus mere spatial deviation.

Existing imitation learning frameworks attempt to address this ambiguity through various means. Some approaches integrate multi-modal sensing (e.g., force feedback), which introduces hardware dependencies, synchronization complexities, and cost. Other methods extract compliance from strictly aligned kinematic data, which severely restricts their spatial generalization capabilities. Standard visuomotor policy learning algorithms, such as Diffusion Policy, are adept at spatial generalization but primarily frame contact-rich manipulation as a purely geometric problem, often failing to capture the demonstrator’s underlying physical compliance. Consequently, the ability to extract generalizable variable impedance skills from diverse, force-agnostic demonstrations remains a notable challenge in robotic manipulation research. This work positions itself to directly address this gap by developing a framework that can infer and utilize compliance from kinematic data collected across varying task configurations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce Variable Impedance Diffusion Policy (VIDP), an imitation learning-based framework capable of predicting variable impedance profiles solely from position-only demonstrations. This framework aims to enable compliant robot manipulation without requiring dedicated force sensors.

The motivation for pursuing this objective is multifaceted:

- **Necessity for Dynamic Compliance in Contact-Rich Tasks:** Many real-world robotic tasks, such as tight-tolerance assembly (e.g., peg-in-hole) or manipulation of deformable objects (e.g., cables), require dynamic modulation of robot stiffness. Static compliance is often insufficient, as tasks evolve from free-space movement to constrained contact, each requiring different levels of stiffness.
- **Complexity of Manual Programming:** Manually programming dynamic compliance profiles for robots is a highly complex task. It requires detailed analytical modeling of environmental interactions, which is often intractable in unstructured or varying environments.
- **Limitations of Learning from Kinematic Demonstrations:** While learning from demonstrations offers an attractive alternative, the core difficulty lies in the fact that stiffness, a crucial parameter for compliance, is not directly observable in standard kinematic (position-only) demonstrations. This makes it a "hidden variable."
- **Ambiguity in Diverse Demonstrations:** To create generalizable policies, demonstrations must be diverse, encompassing variations in spatial layouts and object poses. However, this diversity can create ambiguity when trying to infer compliance. A robot might struggle to differentiate between a human demonstrator's intentional yielding to a surface (compliance) and simple spatial deviations adapting to a new environment configuration.
- **Shortcomings of Current Approaches:**
 - Methods relying on multi-modal sensing (e.g., force/torque sensors) introduce hardware complexities, cost, and synchronization issues, limiting their widespread adoption on commercial collaborative robots.
 - Approaches extracting compliance from strictly aligned kinematic data lack spatial generalization, meaning they struggle when environmental layouts change.
 - Standard visuomotor policies, despite their success in geometric tasks, do not inherently capture or reason about physical compliance, rendering them potentially unsafe or ineffective in contact-rich scenarios.
- **Enhancing Safety and Performance:** By learning variable impedance, the goal is to improve task success rates, reduce interaction forces (enhancing safety for both robot and workpiece), minimize tracking errors, and optimize energy efficiency, thereby contributing to more robust and adaptable robotic systems.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need to develop a robust, generalizable, and hardware-agnostic method for learning variable impedance control from diverse demonstrations, addressing the inherent challenges of inferring compliance from kinematic data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Variable Impedance Diffusion Policy (VIDP) framework integrates three main stages to achieve compliant robot manipulation from diverse kinematic demonstrations.

**A. Trajectory Distribution Estimation via TP-DAMM**

The initial stage involves extracting spatially and directionally consistent trajectory distributions from diverse kinematic demonstrations using a novel approach called the Task-Parameterized Directionality-Aware Mixture Model (TP-DAMM). This model addresses the limitations of standard Directionality-Aware Mixture Models (DAMM), which are sensitive to global spatial shifts, and Task-Parameterized Gaussian Mixture Models (TP-GMMs), which struggle with directional ambiguity.

1. **Frame-Specific Directional Augmentation**: The robot's end-effector position ($\xi_t$) and velocity ($\dot{\xi}_t$) are projected into multiple candidate task-specific reference frames. Each frame $j$ is defined by a translation ($b_{t,j}$) and rotation ($A_{t,j}$). To capture directionality, the state is augmented with a unit velocity direction ($u^{(j)}_t$), which is transformed purely via rotation to preserve its unit-norm geometry. A local directional variance ($d^{(j)}_{t,m}$) is computed using the Riemannian logarithmic map, measuring the angular deviation from the cluster's characteristic direction. This allows distinguishing co-located motions with opposing directions. The augmented state vector combines spatial positions and this directional deviation.
2. **Probabilistic Formulation**: Demonstrations are modeled as a mixture of $M$ components, where each component $m$ is represented by a set of Gaussians for each task frame. The covariance matrix for each component is block-diagonal, enforcing independence between the spatial and directional distributions. The probability of an observation given a component assignment is determined by a Product of Gaussians (PoG) across all task frames, ensuring the trajectory respects constraints from all task parameters.
3. **Parallel Inference with Split/Merge Proposals**: Bayesian inference is performed using a parallel split-merge Gibbs sampler. This process independently samples local position covariances and directional variances for each component and frame using Normal-Inverse-Wishart (NIW) and Inverse-Gamma (IG) priors, respectively. The inference alternates between parameter sampling, Gibbs assignment, and split/merge proposals. To handle potential degenerate covariance estimation in the augmented space, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is applied, projecting observations onto a reduced effective dimensionality for robust parameter estimation.

**B. Mapping Distributions to Target Stiffness**

The variability encapsulated in the learned trajectory distributions from TP-DAMM is then mapped to target Cartesian stiffness profiles. The underlying principle is that regions of high trajectory variability indicate low precision requirements (lower stiffness), while regions of low variability signify high precision requirements (higher stiffness).

1. **Stiffness Estimation from Inverse Covariance**: The fused spatial covariance matrix ($\hat{\Sigma}_m$) for each component $m$, derived from TP-DAMM, is used to estimate the translational stiffness matrix ($K_{\text{trans}}$). This is achieved by performing an eigen decomposition of the inverse covariance matrix ($\hat{\Sigma}_m^{-1}$). The eigenvectors ($V_m$) define the principal axes of stiffness, and the eigenvalues ($\Lambda_m$) represent the magnitude along these axes.
2. **Stiffness Rescaling and Reconstruction**: The statistical eigenvalues are rescaled to fit within the robot's admissible stiffness range ($\kappa_{\min}, \kappa_{\max}$). This ensures that components requiring the highest precision are assigned maximum stiffness, while the most variable components receive minimum stiffness. The stiffness matrix for each component is then reconstructed using the rescaled eigenvalues and eigenvectors.
3. **Temporal Smoothing**: A temporal moving average filter is applied to the stiffness profiles along the trajectory. This step is crucial for preventing discontinuous control inputs during transitions between different mixture components, thereby enhancing stability of the variable impedance controller.

**C. Policy Learning and Real-Time Execution**

The final stage involves training a Diffusion Policy to jointly predict pose actions and the inferred stiffness parameters, enabling real-time compliant task execution.

1. **Policy Architecture and Dataset Formulation**: VIDP employs a Conditional Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM). The training dataset comprises observations ($O_t$) and actions ($A_t$). For demonstrations, the variable stiffness profiles inferred by TP-DAMM supervise the policy.
 - **Observation Space**: The policy condition $O_t$ includes visual and proprioceptive information. Visual observations consist of raw RGB images from a wrist-mounted camera, processed by a fine-tuned CLIP-pretrained Vision Transformer (ViT) backbone. Proprioceptive observations include the robot's end-effector pose history (3D position and a continuous 6D rotation representation), which are flattened and embedded using a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP).
 - **Action Space**: The action space is augmented to include compliance control. Each action $a_t$ within an action chunk consists of a target position ($p_t$), continuous rotation representation ($r_t$), and stiffness parameters ($c_t$). To guarantee that the predicted stiffness matrix is symmetric positive definite, $c_t$ represents the flattened non-zero elements of the upper triangular Cholesky factor ($U_t$), from which the full stiffness matrix ($K_{\text{trans},t} = U_t^\top U_t$) is reconstructed during execution. The policy predicts only translational stiffness.
2. **Training Objective**: A 1D Temporal U-Net acts as the noise prediction network. Training minimizes the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the sampled noise and the predicted noise, utilizing the AdamW optimizer and a cosine learning rate scheduler.
3. **Real-Time Policy Optimization**: For real-time inference, the Denoising Diffusion Implicit Model (DDIM) scheduler is employed. To overcome latency-induced pauses typical in synchronous Diffusion Policy execution, Real-Time Chunking (RTC) is integrated. This asynchronously decouples inference from execution and uses a flow-based guidance mechanism, reinterpreting the diffusion model as an Optimal Transport conditional flow. This enables gradient-based trajectory editing to enforce temporal consistency between consecutive action chunks, ensuring smooth, reactive control to new observations while allowing future steps to adapt more freely.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation aimed to address three research questions (RQ1-RQ3) regarding TP-DAMM's clustering performance, VIDP's success rates, and its impact on physical interaction metrics.

**RQ1: TP-DAMM Evaluation**

- **Qualitative Evaluation (2D PC-GMM Dataset)**: On a spatially perturbed 2D PC-GMM benchmark dataset, TP-DAMM demonstrated superior performance compared to baseline methods. Standard GMM incorrectly merged opposing motions. DAMM exhibited sensitivity to global spatial shifts. TP-GMM, while aligning frames, could not differentiate between locally overlapping but opposing motions. In contrast, TP-DAMM successfully preserved both the spatial structure and the local directionality of the trajectories, closely estimating the original unperturbed task distribution.
- **Quantitative Evaluation (Contact-Rich Task Datasets)**: Quantitative metrics on three contact-rich 3D motion datasets (Peg-in-Hole Insertion, Pulley Assembly, Cable Routing) further supported TP-DAMM's effectiveness. TP-DAMM consistently achieved the lowest local and global directional variance, indicating better grouping of physically similar motions. It also maintained the highest cosine similarity (better alignment with principal directions) and full cluster coverage across the tasks. These results indicate that TP-DAMM extracts more physically and directionally consistent motion primitives from diverse demonstrations than GMM, DAMM, or TP-GMM.

**RQ2: VIDP Performance in Contact-Rich Manipulation**

VIDP's performance was compared against two fixed-impedance baselines: Stiff-DP (high stiffness) and Compliant-DP (low stiffness), across three real-world contact-rich manipulation tasks with varying spatial layouts.

- **Peg-in-Hole Insertion**: VIDP achieved an 80% success rate, matching Stiff-DP and substantially outperforming Compliant-DP (15%). While Stiff-DP provided the necessary precision for alignment and insertion, it was prone to failures from rotational misalignment and jamming due to excessive forces. VIDP achieved comparable overall success but with a slightly higher Reach phase success (90% vs. 85%). During insertion, VIDP maintained high stiffness in lateral directions (Kx, Ky) for accurate tracking while adaptively reducing vertical stiffness (Kz) to regulate contact forces and prevent jamming, which was observed with Stiff-DP.
- **Pulley Assembly**: VIDP achieved a 70% success rate, significantly outperforming Compliant-DP (40%) and Stiff-DP (35%). All methods succeeded in the initial Stretch phase. However, Compliant-DP struggled during the Route phase (40%), and both fixed-impedance baselines had difficulty in the Secure phase. Stiff-DP generated excessive forces causing the band to slip, while Compliant-DP lacked sufficient authority, leading to jamming or failed fitting. VIDP adaptively applied higher Kz during stretching for vertical alignment and then moderately increased Ky and Kz during securing to fit the band, balancing force and precision.
- **Cable Routing**: VIDP achieved a 75% success rate, outperforming Compliant-DP (60%) and Stiff-DP (0%). Stiff-DP completely failed at the highly constrained 3rd hook due to excessive pulling forces. Compliant-DP, while better tolerating cable deformation for the first two hooks, exhibited large tracking errors that led to frequent collisions with the base during the 3rd hook. VIDP dynamically adjusted its stiffness, initializing with high Kz for the 1st hook, then lowering Ky for lateral motion, increasing Kx for lateral precision in the 2nd hook, and raising Kz in the 3rd hook to maintain height constraints, resulting in only one collision failure.

**RQ3: Interaction Dynamics with VIDP**

Analysis of physical interaction metrics (forces, tracking errors, and energy efficiency) for successful trials provided further insights.

- **Interaction Forces**: For Peg-in-Hole Insertion and Pulley Assembly, Stiff-DP consistently produced substantially higher peak interaction forces and greater variability in forces compared to VIDP, even in successful trials. This indicated a higher risk of jamming or damage. VIDP, by dynamically modulating stiffness, maintained both peak and mean forces within a narrower and more predictable range, contributing to safer manipulation.
- **Tracking Errors**: In the Cable Routing task, Compliant-DP exhibited higher tracking errors across all Cartesian directions due to its constant low stiffness. VIDP, through adaptive stiffness modulation, maintained tighter precision, keeping tracking errors lower while still yielding appropriately to cable tension. This balance enabled successful cable routing within necessary spatial tolerances.
- **Energy Efficiency**: Measured by absolute mechanical joint work, VIDP demonstrated improved energy efficiency across all tasks. Stiff-DP consumed more energy by persistently resisting rigid contact constraints. Compliant-DP consumed more energy in Pulley Assembly and Cable Routing due to excessive yielding and repeated corrective motions. VIDP's adaptive compliance minimized both the high-force effort of Stiff-DP and the inefficient corrections of Compliant-DP, leading to lower energy consumption during successful executions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Variable Impedance Diffusion Policy (VIDP) as a framework that addresses a notable challenge in robot manipulation: learning compliant behaviors from demonstrations without requiring direct force sensing. The significance and potential impact of this work can be outlined as follows:

- **Bridging the Gap in Imitation Learning:** The work effectively resolves the ambiguity between intentional compliance and geometric adaptation in diverse, force-agnostic kinematic demonstrations. By introducing TP-DAMM, it provides a method to extract physically consistent trajectory distributions, a crucial step for inferring task-specific compliance profiles that generalize across varying spatial layouts.
- **Reduced Hardware Dependency:** A key impact is the elimination of the need for dedicated force sensors, which are often costly, noisy, and require complex synchronization with other sensory data (e.g., vision). This makes advanced compliant manipulation skills more accessible and deployable on a wider range of commercial collaborative robots.
- **Enhanced Performance in Contact-Rich Tasks:** The experimental results demonstrate that VIDP consistently outperforms fixed-impedance baselines in task success rates across complex contact-rich manipulation tasks. This improvement is achieved by dynamically balancing precision and compliance, leading to safer interactions, reduced jamming, and more robust task execution.
- **Improved Safety and Efficiency:** By precisely modulating stiffness, VIDP significantly minimizes peak and mean interaction forces, thereby enhancing safety for both the robot and the manipulated objects or environment. Additionally, it improves energy efficiency by avoiding the persistent resistance of high stiffness controllers and the excessive corrective motions of low stiffness controllers.
- **Generalizable Robot Skills:** The task-parameterized and directionality-aware nature of TP-DAMM contributes to learning more generalizable robot skills. By modeling demonstrations relative to specific task landmarks and accounting for directional variability, the system can adapt to novel configurations of the task environment.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for future research in autonomous compliant manipulation. The identified limitations, such as TP-DAMM’s reliance on predefined task reference frames and vulnerabilities to self-intersecting state spaces, as well as VIDP’s struggles with visually occluded contacts, indicate clear avenues for further development. Future work could focus on more advanced variability estimation techniques and enhancing policy reactivity under partial observability.

In conclusion, VIDP offers a robust, data-driven approach to endow robots with variable impedance capabilities from kinematic demonstrations, contributing to safer, more efficient, and more adaptable robotic systems in complex real-world manipulation scenarios.
