# RobotSmith: Generative Robotic Tool Design for Acquisition of Complex Manipulation Skills

> [!summary] Summary
> RobotSmith, a collaborative effort by researchers from the University of Massachusetts Amherst, MIT, National University of Singapore, and NVIDIA, provides an automated framework for generative robotic tool design and use, integrating Vision-Language Models with physics-based optimization. This framework enables robots to autonomously design and utilize task-specific tools for complex manipulation, achieving an average success rate of 50.0% in diverse simulation tasks involving rigid, deformable, and fluid objects, and demonstrating successful real-world deployment.

## Problem

- Complex manipulation tasks, especially involving deformable materials, fluids, or objects beyond a robot's direct reach, are inherently difficult for robots using only standard end-effectors.
- Current robotic systems often rely on libraries of human-designed tools which are frequently suboptimal or impractical for robotic grippers and manipulation strategies.
- Prior generative frameworks in robotics primarily focus on synthesizing task settings (e.g., 3D scenes) but lack the ability to autonomously design functional, task-specific tools from scratch.

## Method

- **RobotSmith** introduces an automated pipeline that integrates iterative tool design by collaborating VLM agents with precise physics-based simulation for usage planning.
- A modular and parameterized tool representation, inspired by ==Constructive Solid Geometry==, allows for programmatic assembly and refinement of tool geometries, crucial for fine-tuning in optimization.
- The framework employs a Critic Tool Designer for VLM-driven tool generation, a Tool Use Planner for initial robot trajectories, and a Joint Optimizer using CMA-ES and a physics simulator to co-optimize tool geometry and manipulation trajectories.

## Results

- RobotSmith achieved an average best normalized score (P_best) of **0.94** and a success rate (SR) of **50.0%** across 9 complex simulation tasks, significantly outperforming baselines such as tool retrieval (P_best **0.53**, SR **11.1%**) and text-to-3D generation (P_best **0.72**, SR **21.4%**).
- The framework demonstrated the ability to generate diverse tool designs for the same task, each offering different functionalities (e.g., pushing, scooping, enclosing), showcasing its generative flexibility.
- Real-world experiments validated the physical feasibility and effectiveness of 3D-printed tools on an XArm7 robot, successfully executing a multi-step "sesame pancake" task by autonomously designing and using distinct tools for each subtask.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Large Vision-Language Models can effectively guide the iterative design of functional tools by leveraging their implicit physical knowledge, spatial reasoning, and affordance perception.
> - Joint optimization of both tool geometry and robot manipulation trajectories in a physics simulator is critical for achieving high success rates, as VLM-generated initial designs and plans often lack the precision for real-world physics.
> - A parameterized tool representation, allowing for fine-grained adjustments, combined with a VLM-guided generative process, enables the creation of diverse, task-specific tools that extend beyond rigid templates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of intelligent robotic systems capable of complex manipulation in unstructured environments necessitates the ability to use and design tools. This work positions itself within the emerging field of generative frameworks for robotics, which aim to automate and scale up data collection, task generalization, and problem-solving capabilities.

Prior generative frameworks in robotics (e.g.,) have demonstrated potential in synthesizing task settings, such as 3D scenes and reward functions. However, a significant limitation of these frameworks is their failure to address tool-use scenarios adequately. These systems typically rely on retrieving predefined objects or human-designed tools from existing datasets, which are often not optimized for robotic manipulators. For instance, a standard rolling pin, while effective for human use, presents challenges for a robotic arm, suggesting a need for custom-designed alternatives.

Existing approaches to tool design also exhibit limitations. Some methods (e.g.,) rely on predefined tool templates, optimizing only a limited set of parameters within these templates. While efficient for specific tasks, this approach lacks generalizability across diverse tool types. Other methods explore tool generation by optimizing geometry in the latent space of 3D generative models (e.g.,). However, these approaches often depend on latent spaces that are not specifically designed for tool representation or functionality, thus limiting the scope and effectiveness of the generated tools. These generative methods frequently prioritize visual realism over functional utility, leading to designs that appear plausible but fail in physical execution.

Against this backdrop, RobotSmith seeks to bridge the identified gap by proposing an automated framework for generating effective, task-specific tools from scratch. It leverages the implicit physical knowledge embedded in large pretrained Vision-Language Models (VLMs), which have demonstrated capabilities in spatial reasoning, affordance perception, and commonsense physical inference. These VLM capabilities are crucial for inferring appropriate tool shapes, features, and structures for various interactions. By integrating VLM-driven design with precise physics simulation, RobotSmith aims to overcome the limitations of previous approaches, offering a unified pipeline for both tool design and usage planning in complex robotic manipulation tasks involving rigid, deformable, and fluid objects.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to autonomously design and utilize tools to accomplish complex manipulation tasks that would otherwise be difficult or impossible. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Addressing Intractability of Complex Manipulation Tasks:** Many manipulation tasks, particularly those involving deformable materials (e.g., flattening dough), fluids (e.g., pouring water), or objects beyond a robot's direct reach, are inherently difficult for a robot using only its default end-effector. Tool use is a fundamental capability that extends a robot's physical affordances, making such tasks tractable.
2. **Overcoming Limitations of Predefined Toolsets:** Current robotic systems often rely on libraries of human-designed tools. The paper highlights that tools optimized for human hands (e.g., a rolling pin) may be suboptimal or even impractical for robotic grippers and manipulation strategies. This motivates the need for context-specific tool design that considers the robot's morphology and the task's specific requirements.
3. **Enhancing Generalization Beyond Templates:** Existing automated tool design methods are frequently constrained by predefined templates or depend on task-specific data. This limits their ability to generalize to novel scenarios or to create truly bespoke tools. A core motivation is to move beyond template-based optimization to a framework capable of generating novel tool geometries from fundamental principles.
4. **Leveraging Implicit Physical Knowledge in Foundation Models:** Large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in understanding spatial relationships, perceiving object affordances, and inferring commonsense physics. The research is motivated by the potential to harness this implicit knowledge to guide the tool design process, enabling models to reason about connectivity, size, and functional features required for effective interaction.
5. **Achieving Joint Optimization of Design and Usage:** The effectiveness of a tool is intrinsically linked to how it is used. Suboptimal pairings of a plausible tool with an unsuitable trajectory can lead to task failure. Therefore, the research is motivated to jointly optimize both the tool's geometry and the robot's manipulation trajectory, ensuring that the designed tool and its usage plan are synergistically developed for maximal task performance.
6. **Enabling Autonomous, Long-Horizon Task Execution:** By automating tool design and usage, the system aims to facilitate fully autonomous execution of multi-step, long-horizon tasks, where different subtasks may require different, specially designed tools.

In summary, the key objectives are to develop an automated framework, RobotSmith, that can:
- Iteratively propose and refine tool designs using collaborative VLM agents.
- Generate low-level robot trajectories for using the designed tools.
- Jointly optimize both tool geometry and usage plans through physics-based simulation feedback to maximize task success.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RobotSmith is an automated pipeline designed to integrate large foundation models with physics-based simulation for generative robotic tool design and use. The system takes a natural language task description (T), an initial 3D scene configuration (S₀), and a task-specific reward function (M) as input. Its output consists of a 3D mesh of the tool (G), its initial placement (p, e), and a robotic manipulation trajectory (α). The framework comprises three core components: Critic Tool Designer, Tool Use Planner, and Joint Optimizer.

#### 3.1. Tool Representation (Section 3.1)

The framework employs a modular and parameterized representation for tools, supporting both procedural construction and refinement. Each tool is composed of multiple parts, which can be basic geometric primitives (e.g., box, sphere, cylinder) or complex shapes generated by a text-to-3D model. Each part is defined by its geometry type, size parameters, and a `is_graspable` flag.

An `assembly` function, inspired by Constructive Solid Geometry (CSG), programmatically defines how individual parts are positioned and connected. This function uses geometric transformations (translation, rotation, scaling) and binary operations (union, subtraction, alignment) to build the tool, ensuring physical connectivity and avoiding self-intersections. The parameterized nature of all tool parts (e.g., dimensions) is crucial for the joint optimization phase, allowing for fine-tuning without complete redesign.

#### 3.2. Critic Tool Designer (Section 3.2)

This module consists of two collaborating VLM agents, a Proposer and a Critic, both based on `o3-mini`. They engage in an iterative design loop:
1. **Proposer Agent:** Receives the task description and initial scene setup. It proposes an initial tool design, described in a JSON format (see Appendix A.1), specifying parts, their geometries, parameters, and an assembly function.
2. **Rendering:** The proposed design is rendered into multi-view images.
3. **Critic Agent:** Receives these images, the design rationale, task requirements, and user-defined structural principles (e.g., graspability, part connectivity). It evaluates the design and provides detailed feedback or a termination signal ("Done").
4. **Refinement Loop:** If feedback is provided, the Proposer refines the tool design based on the Critic's suggestions. This iterative process continues until the Critic deems the design satisfactory. This explicit embedding of design constraints ensures functional viability.

#### 3.3. Tool Use Planner (Section 3.3)

Once a tool design is approved by the Critic, a Tool User agent generates an initial robot trajectory. This trajectory is a sequence of calls to three high-level APIs:
- `grasp(obj, euler)`: Grasps an object with a specified gripper orientation. This is implemented via a sampling-based search for feasible grasp poses, prioritizing alignment with the input orientation, and caching successful poses for consistent reuse.
- `release`: Opens the gripper.
- `move(pos, euler)`: Moves the gripper to a target position and orientation. This is implemented by solving inverse kinematics for target joint poses, followed by motion planning to avoid collisions.

#### 3.4. Joint Optimizer (Section 3.4)

Recognizing that VLM-generated initial designs and trajectories often lack the precision for real-world physics, RobotSmith employs a joint optimization step. The agent from the previous stages identifies parameters for optimization and their initial values.
- **Parameters:** `q` denotes trajectory parameters (position and Euler angles for `move` API waypoints), and `s` denotes tool shape parameters (dimensions of tool components).
- **Parameter Ranges:** Shape parameters `s_i` are allowed to vary within [0.5 * `s_i_initial`, 2.0 * `s_i_initial`]. Translational trajectory components can vary by ±0.2 m, and rotational components by ±π radians from their initial values.
- **Optimization Algorithm:** The Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) is used. CMA-ES iteratively samples a population of candidate (s, q) pairs (λ=20 population size, 50 iterations per run).
- **Evaluation:** Each candidate (s_i, q_i) is evaluated in the Genesis physics simulator, computing a task-specific scalar objective M(s_i, q_i) to guide CMA-ES toward improved configurations. This joint optimization is critical as a suboptimal pairing of tool and trajectory can lead to task failure.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of RobotSmith was evaluated through a series of experiments in simulation and real-world settings, across a diverse set of 9 robotic manipulation tasks involving rigid, deformable, and fluid objects (Figure 3). Performance was measured by `P_best` (best normalized score out of 8 trials) and `SR` (success rate, where `P` > 0.8).

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

RobotSmith consistently demonstrated superior performance compared to baseline methods in simulation (Table 1):
- **No Tool:** This baseline achieved an average `P_best` of 0.24 and an `SR` of 2.8%, underscoring the necessity of tools for these complex tasks. Even in cases like "Cut Dough," limited success often resulted from unintended interactions rather than precise execution.
- **Retrieval:** This baseline, which selected tools from a curated database based on semantic similarity, yielded an average `P_best` of 0.53 and an `SR` of 11.1%. Its limitations stemmed from selecting human-designed tools unsuitable for robots (e.g., a rolling pin for robot manipulation) and issues with semantic misalignment (e.g., retrieving a hammer for "flatten dough" due to linguistic association, Figure 4).
- **3D Generation (Meshy):** Using a text-to-3D model (`Meshy`) to synthesize tools, this baseline achieved an average `P_best` of 0.72 and an `SR` of 21.4%. While performing reasonably well on tasks requiring specific geometries (e.g., "Dough Calabash," "Fill a Bottle"), `Meshy` tended to prioritize visual realism (smoothness, symmetry) over functional utility. This often resulted in visually plausible but physically ineffectual tools (e.g., a funnel with three pipes, Figure 4).
- **3D Editing (ShapeTalk):** This baseline, representing an iterative 3D editing approach, performed poorly across all tasks. Its reliance on natural language and implicit representations made fine-grained functional modifications difficult, often leading to incoherent or unusable tools.
- **RobotSmith (Ours):** RobotSmith achieved an average `P_best` of 0.94 and an `SR` of 50.0%, significantly outperforming all baselines. For tasks such as "Lift a Bowl," "Hold a Phone," and "Flatten Dough," RobotSmith demonstrated particularly high success rates (50.0% to 87.5%), highlighting its ability to generate functional, task-specific tools and robust usage plans.

#### 4.2. Tool Diversity

The framework demonstrated the ability to generate diverse tool designs for the same task, each offering different functionalities (e.g., pushing, scooping, enclosing). This diversity, as illustrated in Figure 5, arises from the generative process exploring multiple valid solutions guided by task goals and physical constraints.

#### 4.3. Real-world Experiments

To validate physical feasibility and transferability, selected tools were 3D printed and tested on a physical XArm7 robot (Figure 6).
- **Tool Feasibility:** The generated tools for tasks like "Hold a Phone" and "Dough Calabash" were found to be physically feasible, structurally stable, and effective in their intended functions, confirming the practicality of the design and optimization pipeline.
- **Long-horizon Task:** RobotSmith successfully executed a multi-step "sesame pancake" task (Figure 1). For each subtask (flattening dough, scooping sauce, spreading, sprinkling sesame seeds), the system autonomously designed and used distinct tools, demonstrating its capability for decomposing complex tasks and coordinating tool-specific usage plans to achieve multi-step goals.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

An ablation study (Table 2) assessed the contribution of each major component:
- **`Ours w/o Text-to-3D`:** Removing the text-to-3D generator (relying only on primitives) led to a performance drop (`P_best` 0.69, `SR` 33%), indicating the importance of generative modeling for complex geometries.
- **`Ours w/o Optimizer`:** Disabling the trajectory optimizer (relying solely on the VLM's initial plan) resulted in the most significant performance degradation (`P_best` 0.28, `SR` 5%). This highlights the critical role of physics-based trajectory refinement for accurate task execution.
- **`Ours w/o Tool Opt`:** Removing the joint optimization of tool geometry resulted in reduced performance (`P_best` 0.67, `SR` 32%). This shows that even visually correct tools require precise geometric tuning for tasks demanding high precision.
These ablations confirm that all components—generative modeling, trajectory optimization, and tool geometry optimization—are vital for the robustness and effectiveness of the overall pipeline.

#### 4.5. Failure Analysis

Observed failure cases included:
- **Design failures:** Misalignment between the VLM Designer agent's intent and the `Meshy` text-to-3D generator's output, leading to unusable or difficult-to-grasp tools.
- **Tool use failures:** Ambiguous tool orientation specifications by designers, hindering nuanced interactions.
- **Grasping failures:** Limitations of the `grasp` API, especially with heavy tools or abrupt motions.
- **Optimization failures:** Challenges in tasks with numerous shape parameters or long, complex trajectories.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

RobotSmith represents a notable advancement in robotic manipulation by providing a unified framework for automated tool design and use. Its significance lies in integrating the semantic reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) with the precision of physics-based optimization, addressing a critical gap in existing generative robotics research. By demonstrating the ability to design task-specific tools from scratch and generate corresponding usage plans, the work contributes towards more generalizable and adaptive embodied intelligence.

#### 5.1. Positive Impact

- **Enhanced Robotic Autonomy:** RobotSmith allows robots to autonomously solve complex manipulation tasks that were previously intractable or required human-designed tools, moving towards greater autonomy in unstructured environments.
- **Broader Task Capabilities:** The ability to design tools tailored for rigid, deformable, and fluid objects significantly expands the range of tasks robots can perform, from domestic assistance to industrial processes.
- **Efficiency in Manufacturing and Services:** The framework holds potential to benefit sectors such as advanced manufacturing, elder care, and disaster response, by enabling robots to adapt to varied situations and reduce human labor in hazardous or repetitive environments.
- **Accelerated Research and Education:** The proposed open-sourcing of the pipeline, including code and APIs, can foster innovation in robotics research and education by providing a transparent and reproducible platform for further exploration.
- **Physical Plausibility and Transferability:** The successful real-world deployment of generated tools and plans validates the practical applicability and generalization capabilities of the approach, showing that designs translate effectively from simulation to physical execution.

#### 5.2. Limitations

The current framework's optimization process primarily involves scaling, rotation, and translation of tool components. This limits the exploration of more complex structural changes, such as topological modifications or part reconfigurations, which could potentially yield more diverse, functional, and effective tool designs. Future work could investigate richer forms of tool modification.

#### 5.3. Negative Impacts

- **Labor Displacement:** As with many advancements in automation, increasingly capable and autonomous robots equipped with tool design abilities could contribute to labor displacement in industries reliant on manual or semi-skilled tasks, particularly in manufacturing.
- **Misuse of Technology:** The autonomous tool design capability could be misused in adversarial contexts, raising ethical concerns.
- **Bias from Foundation Models:** The system's reliance on large foundation models means it may inherit and propagate biases or inaccuracies present in their training data.

To mitigate potential negative impacts, the authors emphasize the necessity of careful deployment, continuous human oversight, and regular auditing of such systems to ensure their use for socially beneficial purposes.
