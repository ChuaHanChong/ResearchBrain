# Rescaling Egocentric Vision

> [!summary] Summary
> EPIC-KITCHENS-100 is a 100-hour egocentric video dataset built by a rescaled annotation pipeline: participants pause playback to narrate actions in their native language, crowdworkers transcribe with visual context, a parser maps captions to verb and noun classes, and workers mark temporal bounds. The result is 89,977 action segments, 128% more than before, supporting six benchmark challenges.

## Problem

- Prior action datasets support few tasks because YouTube, film and scripted sources are curated and edited, unlike driving or scene benchmarks that host many linked challenges.
- Non-stop narration in the earlier edition overloaded participants, missing short and overlapping actions and producing sparse, temporally imprecise annotations of untrimmed footage.
- Video domain-adaptation benchmarks adapt across separate public datasets, offering no within-dataset compound gap from changed hardware, homes, participants and multi-year time offsets.

## Method

- ==Pause-and-talk== narrator: participants pause the video to speak in their native language and can delete or re-record, capturing short overlapping actions in one viewing pass.
- Crowdsourced transcriber shows three frames around each timestamp, uses three-worker consensus gated by a 0.9 ==Word2Vec== cosine threshold, then a ==spaCy== parser clusters verbs and nouns into minimally overlapping classes.
- A five-worker temporal annotator converts each narration timestamp into start and end bounds, while ==Mask R-CNN== and a hand-object detector add automatic masks and bounding boxes.

## Results

- **100 hours**, **700 videos**, **89,977 segments**, 97 verb / 300 noun / 4,053 action classes: **1.8x** hours and **2.3x** segments over the 55-hour predecessor, **54%** denser per minute.
- SlowFast leads action recognition at **38.54%** val top-1 action accuracy; detection is far harder, with BMN plus SlowFast reaching only **5.21** average action mAP, and anticipation **14.04%** top-5 recall.
- Models trained on 2018 footage degrade on the 2020 videos; adding new training data saturates around **50%**, and multi-modal TA3N recovers **2.5%** verb and **2.4%** noun accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Letting annotators pause while narrating removes mental load and yields denser, better-localised labels than adding more workers or more annotation passes would.
> - Datasets age: accuracy drops on footage recorded two years later, so temporal re-collection is a legitimate generalisation test rather than mere rescaling.
> - Extra data saturates quickly; diversity of environments and participants matters more than volume, especially for generalising to unseen people and tail classes.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large-scale datasets has been a foundational element in advancing machine learning for computer vision, initially for single tasks such as classification (e.g., ImageNet), detection, captioning, and segmentation. Over time, the research community has moved towards datasets enriched with multiple annotations and tasks to facilitate learning intermediate representations through downstream and multi-task learning. This trend is evident in domains such as autonomous driving (e.g., KITTI, Cityscapes) and scene understanding (e.g., Taskonomy).

In the context of action and activity understanding, many datasets like ActivityNet and AVA often rely on curated or edited videos from sources such as YouTube or movies, which can limit the diversity and unscripted nature of activities. While these datasets have enabled significant challenges, their video sources impose certain constraints on the types of tasks that can be robustly defined, particularly for fine-grained, continuous, and unscripted actions.

Egocentric vision, which utilizes first-person perspectives from head-mounted cameras, offers a unique vantage point for understanding human-object interactions and daily activities. Previous egocentric datasets (e.g., Pirsiavash et al., Fathi et al., EGTEA Gaze+) have demonstrated this potential but were often limited in scale. The introduction of EPIC-KITCHENS in 2018 marked a shift by providing a larger-scale, unscripted egocentric dataset, along with a scalable collection pipeline.

This current work, EPIC-KITCHENS-100, extends the original EPIC-KITCHENS dataset. It aims to address the limitations of existing datasets by significantly scaling up the volume of unscripted, fine-grained egocentric video data. Critically, it introduces a novel annotation pipeline designed to produce denser and more complete annotations, which enables the definition of a broader set of challenging tasks. Furthermore, the dataset explicitly incorporates a "test of time" dimension, investigating how models generalize to data collected two years later, which exposes and provides a benchmark for research into real-world domain adaptation challenges. This positions EPIC-KITCHENS-100 as a comprehensive resource for advancing egocentric action understanding beyond controlled or artificially curated scenarios, fostering research into more robust and generalizable computer vision models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and detail the collection and annotation pipeline for **EPIC-KITCHENS-100**, a substantial extension of the existing EPIC-KITCHENS dataset. This extension is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Scaling Dataset Size and Scope**: A central motivation was to significantly expand the scale of the largest egocentric vision dataset. The goal was to increase the total footage to 100 hours, encompassing 20 million frames, approximately 90,000 action instances within 700 variable-length videos, recorded across 45 distinct kitchen environments. This expansion aims to provide a more comprehensive and diverse collection of real-world, unscripted activities.

2. **Enhancing Annotation Quality and Density**: The previous EPIC-KITCHENS-55 dataset, while large, utilized a "non-stop narration" approach that could lead to missed actions or reduced accuracy due to participant mental load. A key objective for EPIC-KITCHENS-100 was to develop a novel annotation pipeline capable of producing denser (aiming for 54% more actions per minute) and more complete (targeting 128% more action segments) annotations of fine-grained actions in untrimmed videos. This improvement aims to provide more granular ground truth data for robust model training and evaluation.

3. **Enabling New and More Challenging Research Tasks**: The enriched dataset and higher-quality annotations are specifically designed to enable six distinct challenges, supporting a broader range of research in video understanding. These challenges include:
 - **Action Recognition**: Predicting the verb, noun, and combined action classes from trimmed video segments, under both full and weak supervision.
 - **Action Detection**: Identifying and localizing all action instances within untrimmed videos.
 - **Action Anticipation**: Predicting future actions based on observed video segments.
 - **Cross-modal Retrieval**: Ranking relevant captions for query videos or vice versa, considering semantic similarities beyond exact matches.
 - **Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) for Action Recognition**: Addressing the "test of time" by adapting models trained on older data to new, unlabelled footage collected two years later, accounting for various domain shifts.

4. **Investigating Scalability and Generalizability (The "Test of Time")**: A critical motivation was to assess the resilience and scalability of computer vision models. By collecting new footage two years after the initial dataset, the authors sought to explicitly evaluate "whether models trained on data collected in 2018 can generalise to new footage collected two years later." This directly addresses the practical challenge of domain shift in real-world applications due to changes in hardware, environments, objects, and participant behaviors over time.

5. **Leveraging Domain-Specific Knowledge**: Given the dataset's focus on kitchen-based activities, the authors aimed to provide two-level annotations (fine-grained verbs/nouns and broader categories) to facilitate the incorporation of domain knowledge, potentially aiding models in understanding common patterns and interactions within kitchen environments.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to provide a more robust and realistic benchmark for developing advanced egocentric computer vision systems capable of understanding complex, unscripted human activities over time.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for creating EPIC-KITCHENS-100 involved a multi-stage process encompassing data collection, a novel annotation pipeline, and subsequent data enrichment and splitting for challenging benchmarks.

#### 3.1. Data Collection

- **Participant Recruitment**: The team re-contacted participants from the previous EPIC-KITCHENS-55 dataset. Of the 32 original participants, 16 expressed interest, and notably, 8 of these had moved homes in the intervening two years. Additionally, 5 new participants were recruited. This resulted in a total of 37 subjects contributing footage across 45 distinct kitchen environments.
- **Recording Protocol**: Participants were instructed to record 2-4 days of their typical kitchen activities, similar to the original dataset's unscripted nature.
- **Hardware**: Footage was captured using a head-mounted GoPro Hero7 Black camera, a newer model compared to that used for EPIC-KITCHENS-55, featuring built-in HyperSmooth video stabilization. Videos were recorded at 50 frames per second (fps) to mitigate issues like overhead light flickering observed in previous collections.

#### 3.2. Novel Annotation Pipeline

The core of the methodology is a refined, scalable annotation pipeline designed to improve annotation density and accuracy. This pipeline consists of four main stages:

- **a) Narrator ("Pause-and-Talk")**:
 - **Mechanism**: Participants watched their own recorded videos and used a custom interface to pause the video to narrate their actions in their native language. The video resumed upon key release. This "pause-and-talk" approach was a departure from the "non-stop narration" of EPIC-KITCHENS-55.
 - **Rationale**: This method aimed to reduce mental overload, allowing participants to capture short, overlapping, and previously missed actions more accurately. The interface also provided options to listen, delete, or re-record narrations, enhancing quality.
- **b) Transcriber**:
 - **Process**: Audio narrations were first transcribed and then, if necessary, translated into English. Unlike the previous pipeline where transcription and translation were a single step, these were separated for improved consistency and correctness.
 - **Quality Control**: Three Amazon Mechanical Turk (AMT) workers transcribed each narration. To provide context and improve agreement, the transcriber interface displayed three images sampled around the narration timestamp. Transcriptions were accepted based on a 2-out-of-3 consensus or if Word2Vec embeddings similarity exceeded 0.9. Manual review handled disagreements. A curated list was used for spell-checking and problematic word substitutions.
- **c) Parser**:
 - **Functionality**: The spaCy natural language processing library was used to parse the English captions into verbs and nouns. These were then manually grouped into minimally overlapping classes.
 - **Refinement**: The parsing process was reworked to improve the handling of compound nouns and to identify missing verbs/nouns. All previous annotations from EPIC-KITCHENS-55 were re-parsed using this updated pipeline, and verb/noun clusters were adjusted to reduce ambiguities.
- **d) Temporal Annotator**:
 - **Method**: An AMT interface was developed where five workers (an increase from four in EPIC-KITCHENS-55) labeled the start and end times of action segments.
 - **Precision**: Annotators started from the precise narration timestamps generated by the "pause-and-talk" system. They were specifically instructed to mark temporal bounds for *each instance* of an action, even if consecutive and identical, preventing the merging of distinct action occurrences.

#### 3.3. Quality Improvements

Comparative analysis indicated that the "pause-and-talk" method resulted in denser annotations, fewer gaps, more labeled frames, shorter actions, and higher overlap compared to non-stop narration. Narration timestamps were also found to be closer to the actual actions.

#### 3.4. Automatic Spatial Annotations

The dataset was enriched with automatic spatial annotations, although these were not directly used for reporting baseline challenge results. These included:
- Mask R-CNN (trained on MSCOCO) for detecting and segmenting large objects.
- A hand-object interaction model (Shan et al., 2020) for detecting hands and interacted objects.

#### 3.5. Dataset Splits and Challenges

- **Train/Val/Test Splits**: Videos were divided into Train (74.7 hours), Val (13.2 hours), and Test (12.1 hours) splits, approximately 75/10/15. Each video remained entirely within one split. Crucially, the Test split comprised only newly collected videos. The original EPIC-KITCHENS test sets were repurposed as the new validation set.
- **Special Subsets**: The Val and Test splits included subsets for specific evaluation: "Unseen Participants" (participants not in the training set) and "Tail Classes" (infrequently occurring verb/noun/action classes).
- **Challenges and Baselines**: Six distinct challenges were defined: Action Recognition (full and weak supervision), Action Detection, Action Anticipation, Cross-modal Retrieval, and Unsupervised Domain Adaptation for Action Recognition. For each challenge, baselines were established using state-of-the-art models (e.g., TSN, SlowFast, BMN, RU-LSTM, TA3N, JPoSE), and evaluation metrics were specified. The implementation details for these baselines are provided in an appendix for reproducibility.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides a comprehensive overview of the EPIC-KITCHENS-100 dataset, its inherent characteristics, and baseline performance across six defined challenges.

#### 4.1. Dataset Scale and Characteristics

- **Overall Size**: EPIC-KITCHENS-100 comprises 100 hours of video footage, 700 variable-length videos, and 89,977 fine-grained action segments. It contains 20,580 unique narrations, 97 verb classes, 300 noun classes, and 4,053 combined action classes.
- **Scaling Factor**: Compared to the previous EPIC-KITCHENS-55, the dataset represents a 1.8x increase in video hours and a 2.3x increase in action segments.
- **Annotation Density**: The new "pause-and-talk" pipeline achieved 54% more actions per minute and 128% more action segments, demonstrating a significant improvement in the completeness and density of annotations.
- **Distribution**: Verb and noun classes exhibit a long-tail distribution, with newly collected videos introducing new classes not present in the original footage.
- **Enrichment**: The dataset includes 66 million object masks, 31 million hand bounding boxes, and 38 million object bounding boxes from automatic spatial annotation models, though these were not directly used for baseline reporting.

#### 4.2. Scalability and the "Test of Time"

- **Domain Gap**: Models trained solely on the original EPIC-KITCHENS-55 data (collected in 2018) showed a notable performance drop when evaluated on the newly collected videos (2020), referred to as the "Test of Time" effect. This highlights a significant domain gap.
- **Scalability Impact**: Performance improved as more new data was added to the training set. However, a saturation point was observed, particularly for models generalizing to unseen participants, after adding approximately 50% of the new data. This suggests that merely increasing data quantity might not be sufficient; developing more robust models or specifically diverse data might be necessary.
- **Compound Domain Gap**: The domain gap was characterized as a "compound domain gap" arising from multiple factors: changes in hardware (newer camera), locations (participants moving homes, new kitchens), participant appearance/behavior, and short-term/long-term temporal offsets (lighting, object rearrangement, wear and tear over two years).

#### 4.3. Challenge Results

- **Action Recognition (Fully Supervised)**:
 - Baselines (TSN, TRN, TBN, TSM, SlowFast) achieved Top-1 action accuracy ranging from 33.19% to 38.54% on the validation set. SlowFast and TSM generally performed best.
 - Performance consistently dropped for "Unseen Participants" and "Tail Classes" subsets, indicating challenges in generalization to new subjects and rare actions.
- **Weakly-supervised Action Recognition**:
 - Using single timestamp supervision, the iterative refinement method showed a 1-3% Top-1 accuracy improvement over a fixed-segment baseline.
 - However, even the best weakly-supervised baseline performed approximately 11% worse in Top-1 action accuracy compared to its fully supervised counterpart (TSN), indicating the difficulty of this task given the dataset's dense and overlapping actions.
- **Action Detection**:
 - The two-stage BMN+SlowFast baseline yielded low Mean Average Precision (mAP) values (e.g., 3.43% action mAP @IoU=0.5 on Val).
 - Performance decreased significantly with stricter IoU thresholds, confirming the challenge of accurately localizing fine-grained actions of varying lengths and overlaps within untrimmed videos.
- **Action Anticipation**:
 - The RU-LSTM baseline achieved Top-5 class-mean recall values (e.g., 14.04% for actions on Val), significantly outperforming a chance baseline.
 - Nouns were generally easier to anticipate than verbs. "Tail Classes" presented a substantial challenge, with significantly lower recall. Oracle knowledge of the current action provided a strong prior but was surpassed by the RU-LSTM, suggesting learned contextual reasoning.
- **Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA) for Action Recognition**:
 - The "Source-Only" baseline (no adaptation) demonstrated the domain gap.
 - The TA3N baseline, particularly when considering multi-modal features (RGB, Flow, Audio), achieved a modest improvement of 2.5% in verb accuracy and 2.4% in noun accuracy over "Source-Only," indicating partial success in adapting to the new, unlabelled domain.
 - The UMAP visualization showed that TA3N partially aligned feature spaces between the source and target domains.
- **Multi-Instance Action Retrieval**:
 - The JPoSE baseline (disentangling captions into verb, noun, and action spaces) significantly outperformed a simple Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) baseline across both video-to-text and text-to-video retrieval (e.g., JPoSE averaged 44.0% mAP and 53.5% nDCG compared to MLP's 38.5% mAP and 48.5% nDCG).
 - The use of nDCG with non-binary relevance, defined by IoU of verb/noun classes, allowed for a more nuanced evaluation of semantic similarity. The baseline struggled with longer captions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of EPIC-KITCHENS-100 represents a notable contribution to the fields of egocentric vision and general video understanding, with several significant implications:

1. **Advancing Egocentric Vision Research**: By providing the largest and most densely annotated egocentric dataset, EPIC-KITCHENS-100 establishes a new, high-quality benchmark. This will drive progress in understanding fine-grained, unscripted human-object interactions from a first-person perspective, which is crucial for applications such as augmented reality, assistive robotics, and wearable computing.

2. **Addressing Real-World Generalization Challenges**: The explicit "Test of Time" challenge and the characterization of the "compound domain gap" (due to changes in hardware, environment, and participant behavior over two years) are highly significant. This aspect of the dataset directly confronts a major hurdle in deploying AI models in real-world scenarios: their ability to generalize to unseen conditions and evolving environments without continuous, expensive re-labeling. This focus will catalyze research in robust domain adaptation techniques.

3. **Fostering Multi-Task and Multi-Modal Learning**: The comprehensive annotations (fine-grained verbs, nouns, action segments, free-form captions, and auxiliary spatial annotations like hand/object bounding boxes and masks) support a wide array of tasks. This encourages the development of models that can learn shared representations across multiple tasks and modalities (RGB, optical flow, audio), leading to more holistic and robust video understanding systems.

4. **Innovation in Annotation Methodologies**: The novel "pause-and-talk" narration method and the visually-supported transcription interfaces are publicly available and can serve as a blueprint for future large-scale dataset collection efforts. These methods demonstrate a scalable approach to achieving higher density and accuracy in annotations, which is critical for creating high-quality ground truth for complex, continuous activities.

5. **Benchmarking Weakly-Supervised and Temporal Understanding Tasks**: The challenges in weakly-supervised action recognition, action detection in untrimmed videos, and action anticipation, particularly with dense and overlapping actions, provide difficult yet realistic benchmarks. These will push the boundaries of models that can infer temporal boundaries and predict future events from minimal supervision or limited observations.

6. **Developing Semantically-Aware Retrieval Systems**: The multi-instance action retrieval challenge, with its emphasis on non-binary semantic relevance, offers a unique opportunity to develop more sophisticated video-to-text and text-to-video retrieval systems. This moves beyond simple keyword matching to understanding conceptual similarities between actions, which has broad applications in content indexing and human-computer interaction.

In conclusion, EPIC-KITCHENS-100 is positioned to accelerate research across multiple facets of computer vision, particularly in making egocentric models more capable, generalized, and adaptable to the complexities and variability of real-world human activities over time.
