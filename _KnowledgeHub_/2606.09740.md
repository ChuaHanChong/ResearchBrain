# ProbeAct: Probe-Guided Training-Free Failure Recovery in Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of California Los Angeles developed ProbeAct, a training-free framework that improves Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model robustness by detecting and recovering from manipulation failures using internal VLA states. It achieved a 74.1% success rate on the LIBERO-plus benchmark, outperforming the baseline and other state-of-the-art VLAs, especially under geometric distribution shifts.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models often fail in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, such as varying lighting or camera viewpoints, despite proficiency in their training distributions.
- OOD failures are frequently attributed to a 'memory trap,' where the VLA's action head overfits and collapses to memorized kinematic sequences, even when its visual backbone maintains accurate object perceptions.
- Existing online failure recovery methods typically require extensive retraining, external reasoning modules, additional sensors, or explicit 3D modeling, adding considerable overhead.

## Method

- **ProbeAct** introduces a training-free, inference-time framework utilizing a multi-target ==hidden-state probe== to extract accurate 3D object positions directly from the VLA's internal visual backbone features.
- An object-agnostic kinematic state machine is employed to detect grasping and placement failures based on relative state changes, gripper width, and end-effector/object synchronization.
- A hierarchical Control Barrier Function (CBF) filter applies minimal, closed-form corrections to the VLA's actions, deflecting the robot from repeated failure locations (memory traps) without altering policy weights.

## Results

- ProbeAct achieved an overall success rate of **74.1%** on the LIBERO-plus benchmark, surpassing the baseline OpenVLA-OFT (**69.6%**) and outperforming seven other state-of-the-art VLA models.
- It demonstrated the most substantial performance gains in geometric distribution shifts, improving 'Robot Initial States' by **8.4%** and 'Camera Viewpoints' by **7.4%**, effectively mitigating 'Phantom Grasp' failures.
- The framework also improved the robustness of fine-tuned VLAs (OpenVLA-OFT-mixdata) by up to 6.8 points on the 'Robot Initial States' category across LIBERO suites, showcasing complementary benefits.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The 'memory trap' hypothesis was empirically validated; VLA visual backbones often retain accurate spatial information during OOD failures, while the action head significantly drifts from the target.
> - Critical 3D geometric details can be reliably extracted from the shallow-to-mid layers of a VLA's architecture using a simple probe, providing robust object localization without external sensors.
> - Combining internal latent perception with an object-agnostic kinematic state machine and a hierarchical CBF filter offers an effective and resource-efficient solution for real-time failure recovery in VLAs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models have become a prominent paradigm in robotic manipulation, enabling robots to perform complex tasks conditioned on natural language commands. These models are typically trained through behavioral cloning on extensive datasets of expert demonstrations, achieving proficiency within their training distributions. However, a significant challenge within the field is the inherent brittleness of VLAs when confronted with out-of-distribution (OOD) perturbations. Minor environmental changes, such as variations in lighting, camera viewpoints, or slight deviations in initial object configurations, can lead to substantial performance degradation and catastrophic failures.

Existing research has attributed this fragility to VLAs overfitting to nominal training trajectories, resulting in a "memory trap" where the policy fails to dynamically ground its actions in the current visual context. Instead, it tends to reproduce memorized kinematic sequences. A crucial observation, corroborated by this paper's own probing experiments, is that the VLA's visual backbone often maintains accurate spatial representations of target objects even when the action head fails. This suggests that the bottleneck for OOD failures lies primarily in the action execution rather than a complete breakdown of visual perception.

Various approaches have been explored to mitigate OOD failures, including improved pre-training methodologies, online corrections, and learned recovery policies. However, many current online correction methods introduce considerable structural overhead, often requiring external reasoning modules, additional visual symbols, explicit 3D reprojection, supplementary sensing infrastructure, or complete overrides of the VLA's native action stream. Traditional failure recovery techniques, such as hierarchical task and motion planning or reinforcement learning-based recovery, often demand extensive environment modeling, known failure probabilities, or prohibitively expensive failure-case training data.

PROBEACT contributes to this landscape by introducing a training-free, runtime intervention framework that addresses VLA fragility by leveraging the accurate spatial features preserved within the VLA's internal hidden states. It departs from methods requiring external sensors or extensive retraining, instead framing recovery as a minimal spatial constraint derived directly from the VLA's internal representations. This approach is positioned as an orthogonal "safety net" that can be applied to existing VLA policies without altering their weights or demanding new demonstrations, providing a complementary solution to advancements in VLA architecture and training data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the PROBEACT framework stems from the observed limitations of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in generalizing to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. Despite their capabilities in language-conditioned robotic manipulation within their training distributions, VLAs frequently exhibit a lack of robustness, failing when exposed to common real-world perturbations like lighting changes, altered camera viewpoints, or minor variations in initial object states.

A core diagnostic finding, which serves as a central motivation, is the "memory trap" phenomenon. The authors, supported by prior and their own probing experiments, identified that even during task failures, the VLA's internal visual backbone often maintains accurate spatial representations of the target objects. The failure point is frequently localized to the action head, which, constrained by an overfitted mapping, collapses to reproduce a nominal, memorized kinematic sequence rather than adapting to the current visual context. This decoupling between latent perception and motor execution means that the necessary geometric information for correct action is present internally, but not effectively utilized by the policy's output.

Given this understanding, the key objectives of the PROBEACT framework are:

1. **Develop a training-free, runtime intervention framework:** The aim is to create a system that can detect and recover from grasping and placement failures in pre-trained VLA policies without requiring any modifications to their weights or additional training data or demonstrations. This emphasizes a "plug-and-play" design that is orthogonal to existing VLA training pipelines.
2. **Extract robust 3D geometric references from internal VLA states:** A major objective is to leverage the accurate spatial information available within the VLA's intermediate hidden states. This involves designing a mechanism (the hidden-state probe) that can reliably predict 3D positions of task-relevant objects directly from these internal features, thereby eliminating the need for external vision models, additional sensors, or explicit 3D reprojection.
3. **Implement an object-agnostic failure detection mechanism:** The framework seeks to identify execution anomalies (e.g., empty grasps, object drops) without relying on task-specific supervision or domain-specific knowledge. This objective is achieved by evaluating relative state changes and kinematic synchronization between the robot's proprioception and the probe-grounded target positions.
4. **Design a hierarchical safety filtering mechanism for action correction:** To address repeated failures and prevent the robot from entering catastrophic failure loops, an objective is to formulate a system that can impose minimal, closed-form corrections to the VLA's nominal actions. This involves using Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) to encode repeated failure locations as soft safe-set constraints, deflecting the policy away from memory traps while preserving its original kinematic intent and baseline competence.
5. **Act as a universal safety net:** Ultimately, PROBEACT aims to provide a broad-applicability solution that can enhance the robustness of various VLA policies, including both base and fine-tuned models, across diverse visual and spatial perturbation categories.

These objectives collectively aim to bridge the gap between the perceptual capabilities and the brittle motor execution of contemporary VLA models, enabling more reliable robotic manipulation in perturbed environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PROBEACT is presented as a training-free, inference-time intervention framework designed to detect and recover from grasping and placement failures in frozen Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. It operates continuously via three interconnected components: a multi-target hidden-state probe for perception, an object-agnostic kinematic state machine for logic, and a hierarchical Control Barrier Function (CBF) filter for control.

#### 3.1. Multi-Target Hidden-State Probe

**Architecture:** A multi-head Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), denoted as `ϕ`, is employed to map a PCA-reduced spatial feature to up to `K` object positions and their corresponding confidence scores. The input feature is derived from VLA layer 8 activations. Specifically, the 16x16 spatial tokens from the VLA's visual backbone are averaged into a 4x4 grid of 4096-dimensional vectors. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is then applied to reduce these vectors to `d_pca = 1024` dimensions. The MLP outputs `K` predicted 3D positions (`p_k ∈ R^3`) and `K` confidence scores (`c_k ∈`) obtained via a sigmoid activation. Predictions with confidence scores below 0.5 are discarded. The probe itself is a 4-layer MLP with hidden dimensions, with each hidden layer followed by Layer Normalization, a ReLU non-linearity, and a dropout layer (p = 0.1).

**Training with Hungarian Matching:** The probe is trained using a bipartite matching loss, specifically a Hungarian matching loss, to handle scenes with variable numbers of task-relevant objects (1 to `K`). Ground-truth objects are padded with `∅` (no object) up to `K` slots, and a target mask `m_i` is defined. The loss function minimizes the sum of squared Euclidean distances between matched predicted and ground-truth positions, combined with a Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss for confidence scores. This formulation ensures permutation invariance during training, meaning the output slot indices do not maintain temporal consistency. The probe is trained for 200 epochs using AdamW optimizer with a batch size of 512 and a cosine learning rate schedule.

**Inference-time Identity Tracking:** To address the lack of temporal consistency from permutation-invariant training, PROBEACT implements online Hungarian matching during inference. At each timestep, newly predicted coordinates are assigned to existing object tracks by minimizing pairwise Euclidean displacement. A spatial gating threshold, bounded by the maximum plausible inter-frame object velocity, is applied to prevent erroneous ID switching or noise. Unmatched predictions beyond this threshold automatically initiate new object tracks, providing stable 3D position estimates essential for subsequent control logic.

#### 3.2. Object-Agnostic Kinematic State Machine

This component is responsible for detecting execution failures based on object-agnostic signals. It operates through six mechanical phases: APPROACH, MONITOR, GRASPING, POST GRASP, and one event, PLACED. Transitions between these phases and failure detections are evaluated using gripper width (`q`), end-effector pose (`e = (x_e, y_e, z_e)`), and the 3D object positions tracked by the probe. The state machine prioritizes generalizability by avoiding task-specific hyperparameters, instead relying on relative state changes and kinematic synchronization, thresholded by embodiment-specific hardware tolerances (e.g., nominal lift clearance).

- **Pre Grasp → Grasping:** The transition occurs when the end-effector comes within a specified distance (`ρ_enter`) of a probe-tracked target. Upon entry, the system records the target's identity, its initial position (`p_enter`), and the initial end-effector height (`z_enter`). These values serve as a baseline for subsequent relative movement calculations.
- **Grasp Verification:** A successful grasp is confirmed by the synchronous upward movement of the end-effector and the target object over a rolling observation window. Specifically, the gripper width must stabilize above its mechanical minimum (`q > ϵ_limit`), and both the end-effector's vertical displacement (`∆z_e > τ_lift`) and the object's vertical displacement (`∆z_obj > τ_track`) must exceed hardware noise floors. This ensures an actual object lift.
- **Grasping Failure Modes:**
 - **Hard empty grasp:** Detected if the gripper width closes to its physical minimum (`q ≤ ϵ_limit`), indicating no object was grasped.
 - **Soft empty grasp:** Detected if the end-effector exhibits significant upward displacement (`∆z_e > τ_lift`) while the target object remains stationary (`∆z_obj ≤ τ_noise`), indicating kinematic decoupling.
- **Mid-transport drop:** During the POST GRASP phase, a drop is identified by the gripper width snapping to its mechanical limit (`q ≤ ϵ_limit`) while the end-effector is in motion. The drop location is then encoded as a CBF zone, triggering a recovery action to the original grasp target.
- **Placement verification:** The PLACED event is triggered when three conditions are met: the gripper width increases relative to its holding state (`q > q_hold + ϵ_release`), the end-effector initiates a diverging retreat trajectory from the object, and the held object's spatial coordinates stabilize within the tolerance of the target location.

#### 3.3. Hierarchical Control Barrier Function Filter

This component provides a safety mechanism that intervenes with the VLA's actions. To avoid excessive constraint, a two-tier hierarchical strategy is used:

- **Initial Failure:** For an initial failure event, the system executes a stateless push-back, allowing the VLA policy to attempt self-correction without imposing persistent constraints.
- **Repeated Failure (Memory Trap):** If the robot fails again in the same spatial region, it is diagnosed as a memory trap. A persistent Control Barrier Function (CBF) zone is then instantiated at the failure coordinate `c` with a safety radius `r_safe`.
- **Barrier Function and Filtering:** The barrier function is defined as `h(x; c, r_safe) = ||x - c||^2 - r_safe^2`, where the safe set `H = {x: h(x) ≥ 0}`. During the PRE GRASP phase, the VLA's nominal translational action `u_vla ∈ R^3` is continuously filtered to satisfy the first-order CBF condition `∇h(x)ᵀu ≥ -γh(x)`. This is solved by a minimal-intervention Quadratic Program (QP), yielding a closed-form corrected action `u_filtered`. If the VLA's proposed action naturally respects the safety boundary, the filter acts as an identity mapping, preserving baseline competence.
- **Dynamic Zone Management:** To prevent an artificial accumulation of constraints, CBF zones are dynamically flushed when the end-effector approaches the true target (`ρ_clear`) or upon the triggering of the PLACED event. The filter is also intentionally bypassed during the GRASPING and TRANSPORTING phases to allow unhindered physical interaction with the object.

#### 3.4. Multi-Step Task Support

For tasks involving multiple pick-and-place operations, the framework parses the task language for keywords (e.g., "both," "two," "and") to decompose the task into `N` (pick, place) subtask pairs. After a PLACED event, the system appends the just-placed object to a blacklist (preventing re-selection), resets the probe tracker, and clears all active CBF zones. The kinematic state machine then re-enters the PRE GRASP phase for the next pick, ensuring isolation of failure modes across subtasks while reusing the core detection infrastructure.

The VLA backbone used for evaluation is OpenVLA-OFT, and the benchmark is LIBERO-plus, which includes seven perturbation categories: Background Textures, Lighting condition, Camera Viewpoints, Robot Initial States, Sensor Noise, and Language Instructions. The probe is trained on 50,000 (hidden-state, object-positions) pairs collected from baseline VLA rollouts, with object positions extracted from the simulator.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PROBEACT primarily focuses on its ability to enhance the robustness of pre-trained VLA models against out-of-distribution (OOD) perturbations, as measured on the LIBERO-plus benchmark.

**Overall Performance and Robustness:**
PROBEACT achieved an overall success rate of 74.1% on the LIBERO-plus benchmark, which is the highest among all evaluated VLA models. This represents an improvement over the baseline OpenVLA-OFT model, which achieved 69.6%, and surpasses seven other state-of-the-art VLAs. This finding suggests that a training-free runtime intervention framework can lead to performance gains comparable to or exceeding those achieved through more extensive training paradigms.

**Impact on Perturbation Categories:**
The most significant performance improvements were observed under geometric distribution shifts, specifically in categories like "Robot Initial States" and "Camera Viewpoints." In these scenarios, the baseline policy frequently exhibited the "Phantom Grasp" pathology, where it correctly identified the target but executed a spatially misaligned motor action due to collapsing to a memorized trajectory. PROBEACT's ability to evaluate relative kinematic synchronization and use the CBF filter to deflect the trajectory back to the true target proved effective in these cases. Improvements were also observed across other categories, including Camera (+7.4%), Robot (+8.4%), Language (+2.5%), Light (+4.9%), Background (+0.2%), Noise (+1.0%), and Layout (+6.7%).

**Generalization to Fine-tuned VLAs:**
To assess its orthogonality to training-based remedies, PROBEACT was evaluated on OpenVLA-OFT-mixdata, a VLA fine-tuned on LIBERO-plus perturbations. The results showed that PROBEACT consistently improved the mixdata baseline on the "Robot Initial States" category across all four LIBERO suites (Spatial, Object, Goal, 10). For example, it yielded gains of +2.0, +6.8, +4.9, and +3.1 points respectively. This demonstrates that even with explicit exposure to perturbed initial states during training, memorization-induced execution failures can persist, and PROBEACT can still provide complementary benefits by mitigating these residual failures.

**Action-Output Drift Analysis (Validation of Memory Trap Hypothesis):**
An analysis was conducted to localize the source of OOD failures in OpenVLA-OFT by comparing the error of the hidden-state probe output and the VLA's action endpoint against the ground-truth object position. The findings empirically validated the "memory trap" hypothesis:
- On successful episodes, both the probe and the action endpoint were relatively accurate (probe: 3.4 cm, endpoint: 7.8 cm error).
- On failed episodes, a dramatic divergence occurred: the action endpoint drifted significantly (34.9 cm error from ground truth), while the hidden-state probe maintained comparatively better accuracy (10.4 cm error). This indicated that the spatial information needed for a correct grasp remained present in the VLA's intermediate representation, but was corrupted during the projection to motor commands, thus confirming the action head as the primary failure bottleneck.

**Probe Training Data Selection:**
Evaluation of different intermediate VLA layers and pooling schemes for probe training revealed that Layer 8 with image-spatial pooling yielded the highest 3D position regression R² (0.968). This suggests that crucial geometric detail is best preserved in the shallow-to-mid layers of the VLA's architecture and that spatial layout information is beneficial for accurate object localization, rather than global summaries.

**Step Efficiency Analysis:**
Analysis of average step counts across task groups demonstrated PROBEACT's efficiency:
- For tasks where both the baseline and PROBEACT succeeded, PROBEACT incurred a minimal overhead of only 6 additional steps (approximately 5%).
- For the 151 tasks where PROBEACT rescued a baseline failure, recovery was completed in an average of 197 steps, significantly below the 600-step timeout reached by the baseline.
- Across the full benchmark, PROBEACT achieved a lower average step count (255 steps) compared to the baseline (275 steps), indicating that the time saved on rescued tasks outweighed the small overhead in successful tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PROBEACT framework introduces a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in addressing the robustness limitations of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

One of the primary significances of this work is its **training-free and plug-and-play nature**. By operating entirely at inference time and requiring no modifications to pre-trained VLA policy weights or additional demonstrations, PROBEACT offers a highly practical and resource-efficient solution. This allows it to be easily integrated as a "universal safety net" into existing VLA deployments, mitigating failures without incurring the substantial computational and data collection costs associated with retraining large foundation models. This characteristic positions it as a complementary approach to current VLA development, which often focuses on improving architectures or pre-training data.

The paper provides **empirical validation for the "memory trap" hypothesis**, a critical diagnosis for VLA out-of-distribution failures. By demonstrating through action-output drift analysis that VLA visual backbones often retain accurate spatial information even when the action head collapses into an overfitted, memorized trajectory, PROBEACT deepens the understanding of VLA failure modes. This finding is significant because it shifts the focus of robustness research from solely improving perceptual capabilities to also developing mechanisms for robust action grounding and execution. This understanding could inform future architectural designs and training objectives for VLAs.

PROBEACT's methodology, which **synthesizes probe-based latent perception with Control Barrier Function (CBF)-constrained kinematic correction**, represents a novel approach to failure recovery. Leveraging internal VLA hidden states for 3D object localization eliminates the need for external visual sensors or complex external reasoning modules, simplifying system architecture and reducing hardware requirements. The object-agnostic kinematic state machine further enhances generalizability, as it detects failures based on relative state changes and hardware tolerances rather than task-specific heuristics. The hierarchical CBF filter provides a mathematically rigorous way to ensure safety by minimally correcting VLA actions, preventing catastrophic failure loops while preserving the policy's original intent.

The consistent performance improvements demonstrated across diverse perturbation categories on the LIBERO-plus benchmark highlight the **broad applicability and effectiveness** of PROBEACT. Its ability to generalize even to VLAs fine-tuned on perturbation data suggests that its failure recovery mechanism addresses a fundamental vulnerability not fully resolved by data augmentation or expanded training.

The potential impact of PROBEACT extends to enhancing the **safety and reliability of robotic systems** in real-world scenarios. By autonomously detecting and recovering from common manipulation failures, it could enable more dependable operation in environments with unexpected variations. While currently validated in simulation, the framework's reliance on relative spatial displacements and proprioceptive signals suggests a foundation for potential sim-to-real transfer, although further validation on physical hardware with realistic contact dynamics and sensor noise profiles would be a crucial next step for deployment.

In summary, PROBEACT offers an innovative, practical, and effective approach to improving the robustness of VLA models, contributing both a functional solution for failure recovery and a deeper insight into the nature of VLA fragility.
