# DINS-IO: Learned Inertial Odometry via Differentiable INS Consistency

> [!summary] Summary
> DINS-IO presents a two-stage learning framework for inertial odometry, leveraging differentiable INS consistency for self-supervised pretraining on unlabeled IMU data and parameter-efficient fine-tuning for metric calibration. This self-supervised approach effectively recovers motion direction, achieving median errors of 14.0 degrees on the TLIO dataset and 21.1 degrees on the Tango dataset.

## Problem

- Learned Inertial Odometry (LIO) models heavily depend on dense, high-precision position or velocity ground truth for training, which is costly and limits dataset scale.
- Ground truth acquisition is often from constrained environments, leading to domain bias and poor generalization of supervised LIO models in real-world conditions.
- Models trained solely on labels may lack physical consistency with underlying strapdown kinematics, hindering robustness and generalization across diverse users or platforms.

## Method

- A two-stage learning framework, **DINS-IO**, is introduced, consisting of self-supervised pretraining using raw IMU data and attitude estimates, followed by label-efficient fine-tuning.
- A ==differentiable INS consistency loss== is designed, casting the ==strapdown velocity recursion== as a ==sliding-window least-squares== problem, where the residual guides self-supervised learning without labels.
- ==Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)== is applied during fine-tuning, updating only low-rank adapters in the regression head with minimal labeled data to calibrate metric scale while preserving physically grounded features learned during pretraining.

## Results

- The self-supervised pretraining (DINS-IO SSL) recovered motion direction with median errors of **14.0 degrees** on the TLIO dataset (**82.4%** samples within 30 degrees) and **21.1 degrees** on the Tango dataset (**69.1%** samples within 30 degrees).
- Qualitative trajectory reconstructions from the uncalibrated self-supervised velocities accurately reproduced the coarse shape and heading of ground-truth paths, demonstrating capture of fundamental motion geometry.
- Integrated self-supervised paths showed correct relative geometry but exhibited noticeable scale discrepancies compared to ground truth, confirming the theoretical prediction of metric ambiguity in self-supervised INS consistency.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The IMU signal inherently contains a powerful supervisory signal derived from the fundamental strapdown velocity recursion, which can be leveraged directly for self-supervision.
> - Formulating the INS velocity recursion as a differentiable least-squares problem allows a neural network to learn physically consistent body-frame velocity without explicit position or velocity labels.
> - While self-supervision effectively learns motion direction and temporal profile, the metric scale of velocity remains ambiguous and requires explicit, label-efficient calibration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Inertial Odometry (IO) involves estimating the motion of a platform using data from an Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU). IMUs are advantageous due to their small size, low cost, power efficiency, and independence from external infrastructure, making them prevalent in devices like smartphones, wearables, robots, and drones. Traditional strapdown inertial navigation, which integrates gyroscope and accelerometer signals, suffers from cumulative error, causing position error to grow cubically over time, rendering it impractical for long-term use with low-cost sensors.

Learned Inertial Odometry (LIO) emerged to address this limitation by employing neural networks to regress motion (velocity or displacement) from short IMU data windows. This data-driven approach implicitly leverages statistical regularities in motion, leading to improved drift characteristics and underpinning various tracking systems. However, a significant constraint in the LIO paradigm is its dependence on dense, high-precision position or velocity ground truth for training. This ground truth is typically obtained from specialized systems such as motion-capture rigs, visual-inertial systems, or offline Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM). This reliance presents several bottlenecks:
1. **Cost and Scale:** Acquiring high-precision ground truth is expensive and limits the size of labeled datasets compared to the vast amounts of unlabeled IMU data generated continuously.
2. **Limited Coverage and Domain Bias:** Reference systems operate under specific, constrained conditions, leading to labeled trajectories that are biased towards narrow environments and motion patterns. This can result in supervised models degrading in real-world, "in-the-wild" conditions.
3. **Lack of Physical Grounding:** Models trained solely to match labels primarily fit the motion statistics of the dataset rather than adhering to underlying strapdown kinematics, potentially leading to a lack of consistency guarantees and weak generalization across different users, carrying modes, or platforms.

The current work by Qiao et al. positions itself within the LIO landscape by directly addressing this ground truth dependency. While existing LIO models (e.g., IONet, RoNIN, TLIO, IDOL, CTIN, M2EIT, LLIO, IMUNet, ResMixer, AirIO, TartanIMU) have advanced architectures and performance, they all remain reliant on supervised position or velocity ground truth for both training and adaptation.

To circumvent the label bottleneck, a growing body of research explores self-supervised learning (SSL) for inertial sensing. These methods often pretrain on unlabeled inertial data using pretext tasks. Examples include masking-and-reconstruction (e.g., LIMU-BERT), contrastive objectives (e.g., Contrastive Predictive Coding, CrossHAR, ContrastSense) for human activity recognition, rotation-equivariance for improved generalization (RIO), or physics-informed losses enforcing dynamics and temporal consistency (SSPINNpose). However, these SSL approaches typically learn either generic representations or auxiliary regularizers; the pretext task does not directly address the navigation geometry itself. Consequently, a metric motion estimate still requires downstream learning from labels.

DINS-IO differentiates itself by proposing a pretext task directly derived from the strapdown velocity recursion, which is a fundamental principle of inertial navigation. This allows self-supervision to directly yield a body-frame velocity that is consistent in direction and temporal profile, rather than merely a representation requiring further supervised decoding. Furthermore, DINS-IO incorporates parameter-efficient fine-tuning, specifically Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA), which is a technique for adapting large pre-trained models to specific tasks by updating only a small fraction of parameters. This approach is gaining traction in various domains, including vision and inertial odometry (e.g., QLoRA, DoRA, and adapter-style tuning in LIO models). DINS-IO applies LoRA to calibrate the self-supervised prediction to metric scale using limited labeled data, ensuring that the physically grounded features learned during pretraining are preserved.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation behind DINS-IO stems from the recognition that conventional learned inertial odometry (LIO) models are heavily dependent on costly and scarce dense, high-precision position ground truth (e.g., from motion capture, visual-inertial odometry, or SLAM). This dependency introduces several critical limitations:

1. **Scalability and Cost:** The acquisition of specialized hardware and calibrated reference systems for ground truth generation makes labeled inertial datasets small and expensive to scale. This contrasts sharply with the abundance of continuously generated unlabeled IMU data.
2. **Limited Coverage and Domain Generalization:** Reference systems often operate under constrained conditions, leading to labeled trajectories that may not represent the full diversity of environments and motions encountered in real-world scenarios. This can introduce domain bias, causing supervised models to perform poorly when deployed "in-the-wild."
3. **Lack of Physical Consistency:** Training models solely on labels may cause them to learn statistical correlations specific to the training data rather than the underlying physical laws of motion. This can result in a lack of consistency guarantees and hinder generalization across different users, carrying modes, or platforms.

The authors postulate that the IMU signal inherently contains a powerful supervisory signal derived from the fundamental laws of inertial navigation, which has been underutilized in learned approaches. Although direct double integration of IMU data is prone to error accumulation, the strapdown velocity recursion maintains a precise and exact relationship between measured specific force, platform attitude, and velocity. This relationship holds universally, irrespective of the availability of position labels.

Building on this insight, DINS-IO establishes the following key objectives:

1. **Self-Supervised Learning from Raw IMU Data:** To develop a method for learning inertial odometry directly from raw IMU streams without requiring any position or velocity labels. This is achieved by formulating the strapdown velocity recursion as a differentiable consistency prior.
2. **Differentiable INS Consistency Loss:** To design a novel self-supervised loss function that casts the strapdown velocity recursion as a sliding-window least-squares problem. The residual of the closed-form solution to this problem serves as a differentiable objective, allowing the network to be trained to produce physically consistent velocity estimates.
3. **High-Frequency Velocity Prediction:** To design a neural network architecture capable of emitting dense body-frame velocity predictions at the IMU rate. This high-frequency output is essential for the per-sample consistency constraint imposed by the INS velocity recursion.
4. **Label-Efficient Metric Calibration:** To address the inherent scale ambiguity that self-supervised INS consistency leaves unobserved. This involves calibrating the network's output to true metric velocity using only a small fraction of labeled trajectories.
5. **Preservation of Learned Features:** To ensure that the metric calibration process, performed with limited labeled data, does not overwrite or degrade the physically grounded motion features learned during the extensive self-supervised pretraining phase. This is achieved through parameter-efficient fine-tuning techniques, specifically Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA).

In summary, the overarching motivation is to decouple the quality of learned inertial odometry models from the availability of expensive ground truth, thereby enabling more scalable, robust, and physically consistent motion estimation systems.

### 3. Methodology and approach

DINS-IO is structured as a two-stage learning framework that leverages a shared network backbone and a least-squares (LS) solving mechanism. The stages are self-supervised pretraining, followed by label-efficient fine-tuning.

**Overall Framework:**
- **Stage 1 (Self-supervised Learning):** The network is trained using only raw IMU data and attitude estimates. Predicted velocities are constrained to satisfy the strapdown INS recursion via a differentiable least-squares solve, where the residual of this solve acts as the self-supervision loss.
- **Stage 2 (LoRA Fine-tuning):** A small amount of position-labeled data is used to metrically calibrate the body-frame velocity predicted by the pretrained network. This is achieved by direct supervision of the velocity output, with updates restricted to low-rank adapters to preserve learned features.

**High-Frequency Inertial Velocity Network:**
To enable a per-sample loss computation based on INS consistency, the network is designed to output dense body-frame velocity (`v_b_pred`) at every IMU sample. This contrasts with typical LIO regressors that often output single per-window displacements. The architecture consists of three main stages:

1. **Input and Patch Embedding:** IMU data (accelerometer and gyroscope) and associated attitude are organized into sequences. Each one-second window is divided into patches, which are then linearly projected into a feature dimension `F`.
2. **MetaFormer Backbone:** A stack of MetaFormer blocks processes the patched features. These blocks use residual connections with `TokenMixer` (exchanging information across patches) and `ChannelMixer` (an expand-contract MLP) to model intra-window structures. A per-layer initialization scaling is applied for training stability.
3. **Temporal Block:** The patch dimension is reduced to produce a single feature vector per second. A Recurrent Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) block then processes these per-second features along the temporal dimension to capture cross-second dependencies. This block supports both batched forward passes for training and single-step forward passes for online inference, ensuring consistent behavior.
4. **High-Frequency Regression Head:** This crucial component generates the dense velocity output. From each per-second temporal feature, a time-generator linear layer re-expands the single vector into `P` patch tokens. A dedicated MetaFormer block within the head then maps these tokens back to a full-rate window of length `W`, yielding the dense `v_b_pred` for each IMU sample. This "short-feature to long-output" expansion allows a compact temporal model to produce the high-rate velocity required by the differentiable INS observation.

**Differentiable INS Consistency for Self-Supervision (Stage 1):**
The core of the self-supervised loss relies on the strapdown INS velocity recursion (Eq. 6).
`v_n[k] = v_n_0 + S_f[k] - S_R[k] * b_a + g_corr[k]`
where:
- `v_n[k]` is navigation-frame velocity at time `k`.
- `v_n_0` is the initial navigation-frame velocity for the window.
- `S_f[k]` is the cumulative specific force integrated over the window.
- `S_R[k]` is the cumulative rotation matrix integrated over the window.
- `b_a` is a constant accelerometer bias.
- `g_corr[k]` is the cumulative gravity term.
All terms except `v_n_0` and `b_a` are directly computed from IMU measurements and attitude.

1. **Linear Observation Formulation:** The network's body-frame velocity prediction `v_b_pred[k]` is rotated into the navigation frame to form `v_n_obs[k] = R[k] * v_b_pred[k]`. If this prediction is correct, it must satisfy the INS recursion. This is rephrased as a linear observation for each sample `k`:
 `z[k] = H[k] * x`, where `x = [v_n_0; b_a]` are the unknowns, `z[k] = v_n_obs[k] - S_f[k] - g_corr[k]` contains known terms, and `H[k] = [I_3 | -S_R[k]]`.
2. **Sliding-Window Joint Least-Squares:** Instead of integrating over a very long sequence (which accumulates sensor noise), the sequence is divided into `W` non-overlapping windows of length `ws`. Crucially, a single, *globally shared accelerometer bias* (`b_a`) is assumed across all windows. The unknowns are stacked as `X = [v_n_0,0; v_n_0,1; ...; v_n_0,W-1; b_a]`.
 The LS system is constructed from two types of rows:
 - **Within-window INS rows:** Eq. (8) for every sample `k` within each window, coupling `v_n_obs[k]` with the initial velocity of its window and the shared bias.
 - **Boundary continuity rows:** Enforcing that the end velocity of window `w` equals the start velocity of the subsequent window `w+1`, thus linking consecutive initial velocities through the shared bias.
3. **Differentiable Loss:** This complete system `H_full * X = z_full` is solved in closed form using `X* = (H_full^T * H_full + λI)^-1 * H_full^T * z_full`, where `λ` is a Tikhonov regularization term. The self-supervised loss, `L_INS`, is the L2 norm of the residual: `||H_full * X* - z_full||^2 / M`. Since `X*` is an analytic function of `z_full` (which contains `v_n_obs`, a function of network output), `L_INS` is differentiable with respect to `v_b_pred`, allowing gradients to flow back to the network without any position or velocity labels.

**Label-Efficient Metric Calibration via LoRA (Stage 2):**
The self-supervised stage learns physically consistent motion (direction and temporal profile) but leaves the metric scale underdetermined. This stage addresses that.

1. **Direct Body-Frame Velocity Supervision:** For a small subset of labeled trajectories, ground truth navigation-frame velocity (`v_n_gt`) is transformed into the body frame (`v_b_gt[k] = R[k]^T * v_n_gt[k]`) to match the network's output format. A supervised loss `L_sup = (1/N) * Σ ||v_b_pred[k] - v_b_gt[k]||^2` is applied, directly calibrating the predicted velocity to the true metric scale.
2. **LoRA Fine-Tuning:** To prevent overfitting to scarce labels and preserve the physically grounded features learned during pretraining, Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is employed. Instead of fully fine-tuning the entire network, only low-rank adapters (`ΔW = BA`) are learned and added to existing frozen linear layers (`y = Wx + b + (α/r)BAx`). The `B` matrix is zero-initialized, ensuring that fine-tuning starts from the pretrained state. Crucially, these LoRA adapters are *only inserted into the linear layers of the high-frequency regression head*. The input layer, MetaFormer backbone, and temporal model are frozen. This strategic placement allows the head to absorb the low-dimensional, largely multiplicative metric calibration without altering the core representation of motion direction and temporal profile learned in Stage 1. This limits the trainable parameters to less than 10% of the total.

### 4. Main findings and results

The paper presents findings primarily concerning the effectiveness of the self-supervised pretraining stage (Stage 1) in capturing motion geometry without any position or velocity labels. The evaluation addresses whether the label-free INS-consistency objective can yield a usable motion estimate. The results from Stage 2 (LoRA fine-tuning for metric calibration) are discussed in the context of requiring a small fraction of labels to match or surpass fully supervised baselines, though specific quantitative results for this stage are not provided in the abstract and introductory sections.

**Self-Supervision Recovers Velocity Direction (Stage 1 Results):**

1. **Velocity-Direction Error:** The model pretrained purely through self-supervision (DINS-IO SSL, no fine-tuning) was evaluated on its ability to predict the correct direction of motion. This was measured as the per-sample angle between the predicted and ground-truth navigation-frame velocities, considering only samples where ground-truth speed exceeded 0.15 m/s (to avoid undefined directions at rest).
 - On the TLIO dataset, the model achieved a median direction error of 14.0 degrees. More than 82.4% of the samples had a direction error within 30 degrees.
 - On the Tango dataset, the median direction error was 21.1 degrees, with over 69.1% of samples within 30 degrees.
 These low directional errors indicate that the label-free pretraining successfully captures the direction of motion, and this accuracy is stable across sequences, not degrading over time.

2. **Qualitative Trajectory Reconstruction:** To visualize the learned motion geometry, the raw, uncalibrated velocity predicted by the label-free Stage 1 model was integrated into position tracks.
 - On both the Tango and TLIO datasets, the integrated trajectories (without any metric calibration) reproduced the coarse shape and heading of the ground-truth paths. For example, turns in the estimated path occurred at locations consistent with the ground truth, and the initial segments of the estimated trajectories generally overlapped with the reference paths.
 - This qualitative assessment confirms that self-supervision alone can effectively capture the fundamental geometry of motion.
 - However, the integrated paths demonstrated a noticeable discrepancy in scale compared to the ground truth. For instance, on the Tango dataset, the recovered path appeared compressed relative to the true multi-loop trajectory.
 This observation aligns with the theoretical prediction that INS consistency, while constraining direction and temporal profile, leaves the metric magnitude of velocity largely undetermined under degenerate motion conditions. This metric ambiguity is precisely what Stage 2 (label-efficient calibration) is designed to address. The paper highlights that after a similarity transform (rotation and scaling) is applied to align the self-supervised trajectory to the ground truth, its shape and heading demonstrate a strong match, isolating the scale as the primary remaining uncalibrated aspect.

The findings from Stage 1 establish that the differentiable INS consistency objective effectively imbues the network with a physically consistent understanding of motion direction and temporal dynamics, even in the complete absence of ground truth position or velocity labels.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The DINS-IO framework represents a significant development in learned inertial odometry, with the potential to impact several areas of research and application.

**Addressing the Label Bottleneck:** The primary significance lies in its ability to largely decouple the quality of learned inertial odometry models from the availability of dense, high-precision position ground truth. This addresses a major bottleneck in LIO, as acquiring such ground truth is costly, time-consuming, and limits the scalability and diversity of training data. By enabling effective pretraining on abundant, unlabeled IMU data, DINS-IO can significantly reduce the data collection burden for LIO systems.

**Enhancing Physical Consistency and Generalization:** Integrating the strapdown INS velocity recursion as a differentiable self-supervised loss introduces a fundamental physical prior into the learning process. This approach helps the network learn motion patterns that are inherently physically consistent, rather than merely fitting statistical regularities of specific datasets. This physical grounding can improve the model's robustness and generalization capabilities when encountering novel environments, motion types, or sensor characteristics that differ from limited labeled training sets. This is particularly relevant for "in-the-wild" applications where diverse conditions are common.

**Promoting Scalable LIO Deployment:** The ability to pretrain on large volumes of unlabeled data and then fine-tune with minimal labeled data makes LIO more practical for widespread deployment. Devices like smartphones and wearables continuously generate vast amounts of IMU data that can be leveraged for self-supervision, leading to more accurate and reliable navigation systems without requiring users to collect extensive labeled trajectories. This can accelerate the development of robust pedestrian and platform tracking systems.

**Modular Learning Approach:** DINS-IO demonstrates a modular approach where different aspects of motion estimation are learned distinctly. The self-supervised stage focuses on learning the direction and temporal profile of motion based on physical principles, while the label-efficient fine-tuning stage specifically addresses the metric scale calibration. This modularity could inspire future research into dissecting complex navigation problems into components that can be optimized with different supervisory signals.

**Reduced Overfitting and Feature Preservation:** The use of Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for metric calibration is impactful. By restricting updates to a small fraction of parameters (specifically in the regression head) and freezing the core feature extractor, LoRA helps prevent overfitting to scarce labeled data. Crucially, it preserves the physically grounded features learned during extensive self-supervised pretraining, ensuring that the model retains its robust understanding of motion dynamics while adapting to the correct metric scale.

**Impact on Robotics and Autonomous Systems:** For robotics and autonomous systems, accurate and robust inertial odometry is foundational. DINS-IO's approach could lead to improved localization systems that are less dependent on external infrastructure (like GPS or visual landmarks) and can operate more reliably in environments where such aids are intermittent or unavailable. This can enhance autonomy and safety in navigation.

In summary, DINS-IO provides a framework for developing more scalable, robust, and physically consistent learned inertial odometry systems by effectively utilizing the inherent physical information within IMU data and minimizing the reliance on expensive ground truth labels.
