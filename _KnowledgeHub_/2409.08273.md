# Hand-Object Interaction Pretraining from Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> HOP learns manipulation priors by lifting hand-object interactions from in-the-wild videos into 3D, retargeting them to robot trajectories via a physics simulator, then pretraining a causal transformer with next-action prediction. Finetuning with RL or behavior cloning gives 2-5x more sample-efficient, more robust, better-generalizing policies than baselines.

## Problem

- Vision-only pretraining on video datasets yields representations lacking a motor component, limiting effectiveness on downstream manipulation tasks.
- Methods extracting 3D human motion from videos require close alignment between training videos and downstream robot tasks, compromising generality.
- Prior hand-pose-prior approaches from egocentric video capture only contact-pose or hand-motion information, discarding object trajectories, pre/post-contact dynamics, and intuitive physics.

## Method

- **Simulator-in-the-loop retargeting**: solves a per-timestep optimization matching robot ==forward kinematics== to estimated 3D hand keypoints, executed in a high-fidelity simulator to yield physically grounded trajectories.
- 3D hand-object lifting pipeline built on ==MCC-HO== and ==HaMeR==, extended with ==time-smoothed hand-anchored tracking== to convert monocular in-the-wild video frames into hand-object 3D pose sequences.
- **HOP** base policy: a ==GPT-2==-style ==causal transformer== pretrained via an ==L1 conditional-distribution-matching objective== over observation-action histories, later finetuned end-to-end with ==PPO== or ==behavior cloning==.

## Results

- On the hardest real-world task (Grasp and Lift, 4 objects), HOP outperforms the best baseline (ImageNet finetuned) by a **30 percentage-point** success-rate margin.
- In simulation RL finetuning, HOP gives **2-5x** sample-efficiency gains over training from scratch and outperforms DAPG and AMP baselines across tasks.
- HOP policies show over **3x** more robustness to disturbance forces and over **2x** better generalization to unseen YCB objects than PPO trained from scratch.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Object trajectories carry information beyond hand motion alone; masking them during pretraining measurably degrades grasp robustness and out-of-distribution task adaptation.
> - A physics simulator can recover force and contact information lost in monocular video, letting noisy in-the-wild human data become usable robot training signal.
> - Task-agnostic, unaligned pretraining data can still accelerate downstream RL by biasing exploration toward human-like, higher-affordance behaviors rather than requiring aligned demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

The field of robotics has long struggled with a fundamental challenge: how to efficiently teach robots complex manipulation skills without requiring extensive task-specific data collection. While humans can easily learn from observing others and quickly adapt their skills to new situations, robots typically need thousands of demonstrations or trial-and-error attempts to master even simple tasks. This paper introduces Hand-Object interaction Pretraining (HOP), a method that bridges this gap by learning general manipulation skills from abundant human video data.

Developed by researchers at UC Berkeley, including prominent figures Pieter Abbeel and Jitendra Malik, HOP represents a significant departure from traditional approaches to robot learning. Instead of relying solely on expensive robot demonstrations or starting from scratch for each new task, the method leverages the vast repository of human hand-object interaction videos available online to create a general-purpose manipulation prior for robots.

The core insight is that human videos contain rich information about how objects should be manipulated, including implicit knowledge about object affordances, natural hand movements, and effective interaction strategies. By extracting this knowledge and translating it into robot-compatible representations, HOP enables robots to learn new manipulation tasks with dramatically improved sample efficiency and robustness.

### 2. Problem Definition and Motivation

Current approaches to robot manipulation learning face several critical limitations that HOP aims to address:

**Data Scarcity**: Traditional robot learning requires extensive datasets of robot-specific demonstrations. Collecting such data is expensive, time-consuming, and often needs to be repeated for each new task or robot platform. This creates a significant bottleneck for scaling robot capabilities.

**The Embodiment Gap**: While human demonstration videos are abundant, translating human actions to robot actions is challenging due to fundamental differences in kinematics, dynamics, and sensory capabilities between humans and robots.

**Limited Generalization**: Existing methods often learn task-specific policies that struggle to generalize to new objects, environments, or slight variations in task requirements. This limits their practical applicability in real-world scenarios.

**Insufficient Priors**: Training robots from scratch ignores the wealth of knowledge embedded in natural human manipulation behaviors. Humans instinctively understand object affordances, physics constraints, and efficient movement patterns - knowledge that could benefit robot learning if properly extracted and transferred.

HOP addresses these challenges by proposing a three-stage pipeline that transforms abundant human video data into effective robot manipulation priors. The method hypothesizes that by learning from diverse human hand-object interactions, robots can acquire general manipulation knowledge that enables faster adaptation to new tasks while exhibiting more natural and robust behaviors.

### 3. The HOP Methodology

The HOP methodology consists of three interconnected stages that progressively transform human video data into robot-usable knowledge.

#### 3.1. Stage 1: 3D Hand-Object Interaction Extraction

The first stage processes "in-the-wild" human videos to extract 3D hand-object interaction trajectories. This builds upon recent advances in computer vision, specifically a modified version of MCC-HO, which jointly infers hand and object geometry from single RGB images.

The key modifications for generality include:
- **Removing CAD-based constraints**: Unlike the original MCC-HO, HOP skips object-specific post-processing to maintain generality across diverse objects
- **Temporal smoothing**: Hand detections are temporally smoothed to improve trajectory quality
- **Simplified assumptions**: The method assumes a static camera to reduce complexity

This stage outputs sequences of 3D hand poses and object point clouds that capture the essential geometry of human-object interactions.

#### 3.2. Stage 2: Simulator-in-the-Loop Retargeting

The second stage addresses the embodiment gap by translating human hand trajectories into robot action sequences using a physics simulator. For each timestep of a human trajectory, an optimization problem is solved:

$$\min_{a[k]} \|FK(a[k]) - h[k]\|^2 + \lambda \|a[k] - \phi[k-1]\|^2$$

where $FK(a[k])$ represents the robot's forward kinematics, $h[k]$ is the human hand pose, and the regularization term encourages smooth motions.

Crucially, object dynamics are intentionally disregarded during retargeting due to noise in video estimates and unknown physical properties. The object is simply positioned at its observed 3D location, allowing the optimization to focus on achieving kinematic correspondence.

The resulting robot actions are executed in a high-fidelity IsaacGym simulator with randomized scene configurations to generate diverse, physically grounded sensorimotor trajectories. This produces approximately 70,000 robot trajectories from 450 human videos.

#### 3.3. Stage 3: Robot Trajectory Pretraining

The final stage trains a causal transformer (GPT-2 architecture) on the generated robot trajectories using a next-action prediction objective:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \mathcal{D}} \sum_{t} \|a[t] - \hat{a}[t]\|_1$$

The model takes as input the past 16 timesteps of proprioceptive information (joint angles) and visual observations (depth images or point clouds) to predict the next action. This pretraining embeds manipulation knowledge into the model weights, creating a general prior that can be adapted to downstream tasks through fine-tuning.

### 4. Experimental Setup and Results

The effectiveness of HOP is evaluated through comprehensive experiments in both simulation and real-world settings, comparing against strong baselines across multiple dimensions.

#### 4.1. Real-World Performance Comparison

The real-world experiments compare HOP against visual pretraining baselines (ImageNet ViT, R3M, VIP, MVP) and diffusion policies across three tasks of increasing difficulty:

- **Grasp and Drop** (simple): Basic grasping and releasing
- **Grasp and Pour** (medium): Requires object rotation and coordination
- **Grasp and Lift** (hard): Diverse objects with different affordances

The results demonstrate HOP's superior performance, particularly on the most challenging task where it achieves 65% success compared to the next best baseline at 35%. This significant improvement highlights HOP's ability to capture and utilize object affordances from limited demonstrations.

#### 4.2. Simulation Experiments

Simulation experiments provide more controlled evaluation across three tasks: Grasp and Lift, Lift and Throw, and Open Cabinet. HOP consistently outperforms baselines in sample efficiency, requiring 2-5x fewer environment interactions to reach comparable performance levels.

#### 4.3. Robustness and Generalization Analysis

Additional experiments reveal that HOP-trained policies exhibit superior robustness to external forces and better generalization to novel objects. When subjected to random disturbances during grasping, HOP policies maintain significantly higher success rates compared to policies trained from scratch.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

The paper includes important ablation studies examining the value of joint hand-object modeling versus hand-only priors. Results show that hand-only priors are insufficient for complex tasks, confirming that learning joint hand-object interactions provides richer and more useful manipulation knowledge.

### 5. Key Contributions and Significance

HOP makes several important contributions to the field of robot learning:

**Methodological Innovation**: The simulator-in-the-loop retargeting approach provides an elegant solution to the embodiment gap problem, enabling effective transfer from human videos to robot actions without requiring perfect physical modeling.

**Empirical Validation**: Comprehensive experiments demonstrate substantial improvements in sample efficiency (2-5x), robustness (3x), and generalization capabilities compared to existing approaches.

**Scalability**: By leveraging abundant human video data rather than requiring robot-specific demonstrations, HOP opens a path toward more scalable robot learning that can benefit from the vast amount of human interaction data available online.

**General Manipulation Priors**: The learned representations are task-agnostic and can be adapted to diverse downstream tasks, moving beyond brittle task-specific solutions toward more general robot capabilities.

The significance extends beyond the immediate technical contributions. HOP represents a paradigm shift toward learning robot skills from passive observation of human behavior, potentially democratizing robot learning by reducing the need for expensive data collection and expert demonstrations.

### 6. Limitations and Future Directions

While HOP demonstrates significant advances, several limitations point toward future research directions:

**3D Reconstruction Quality**: The method's effectiveness is bounded by the quality of 3D hand-object pose estimation from monocular videos. As computer vision techniques improve, HOP could benefit from more accurate scene reconstructions.

**Scene Complexity**: Current experiments focus on relatively simple tabletop scenarios. Extending to more complex environments with clutter, articulated objects, or multi-object interactions remains an open challenge.

**Embodiment Specificity**: While the simulator-based retargeting helps bridge the embodiment gap, the approach may need adaptation for significantly different robot morphologies or capabilities.

**Long-Horizon Tasks**: The evaluation focuses on relatively short manipulation tasks. Scaling to longer-horizon behaviors that require planning and sequencing multiple manipulation primitives presents additional challenges.

**Real-World Deployment**: Despite promising real-world results, broader deployment will require addressing factors like varying lighting conditions, camera calibration, and environmental noise that can affect performance.

Future work could explore incorporating more sophisticated 3D scene understanding, extending to bi-manual manipulation, and investigating how the learned priors transfer across different robot platforms. The fundamental approach of learning from human videos appears highly promising and likely to inspire further research in this direction.

The work establishes HOP as a significant step toward robots that can learn complex manipulation skills by observing human behavior, bringing us closer to the vision of general-purpose robots that can quickly adapt to new tasks and environments through natural interaction paradigms.
