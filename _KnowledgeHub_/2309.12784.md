# Learning to Walk and Fly with Adversarial Motion Priors

> [!summary] Summary
> A learning-based method leveraging Reinforcement Learning and Adversarial Motion Priors enables an aerial humanoid robot to autonomously execute smooth, energy-efficient transitions between legged and aerial locomotion. The robot adaptively switches modes based on environmental context and terrain, achieving both human-like walking and optimized flying behaviors.

## Problem

- Developing autonomous and smooth transitions between legged and aerial locomotion for humanoid robots.
- Overcoming limitations of traditional methods like computationally intensive trajectory optimization or reliance on explicit state machines for mode switching.
- Addressing the challenge of enabling robots to implicitly decide when and how to switch locomotion modes based on environmental feedback.

## Method

- Proposed a ==Reinforcement Learning (PPO)== framework integrated with Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) to learn a control policy.
- Incorporated a dual reward function, combining task-specific goals (waypoint tracking, energy efficiency) with a style reward derived from a discriminator.
- Leveraged two distinct motion datasets as priors: human motion capture for walking and trajectory optimization for flying.

## Results

- The iRonCub robot autonomously performed smooth transitions between walking, flying, taking off, and landing in simulation.
- The robot exhibited terrain-aware locomotion, using an elevation map to switch to flying over inaccessible ground like wide gaps or high obstacles.
- Policies trained with both walking and flying motion priors achieved the most efficient locomotion, demonstrating lower thrust usage compared to policies with no priors or single priors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adversarial Motion Priors enable the spontaneous emergence of multimodal locomotion and adaptive mode switching without explicit high-level planners or state machines.
> - The integration of both walking (human-like) and flying (optimized) motion priors is crucial for achieving efficient, versatile, and natural-looking multimodal behaviors.
> - A thrust penalization term in the reward function effectively guides the robot towards energy-efficient locomotion, preferring walking over flying when feasible.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is actively exploring multimodal locomotion, which involves robots transitioning between different forms of movement. While various bio-inspired robotic systems have demonstrated bespoke locomotion capabilities—such as bat-inspired robots that fly and walk, propeller-driven bipedal robots, morphing rovers, rolling quadrupeds, underwater robots, and flying humanoids,—a significant challenge remains in understanding *how and when* to effectively transition between these locomotion forms. Existing methods often rely on breaking controllers into submodules, where each handles an individual locomotion task, or using complex trajectory optimization (TO) layers. These TO approaches can be computationally intensive, require prior knowledge of the environment, and struggle to exploit the robot's full dynamics without significant computational cost.

In parallel, model-free techniques such as Reinforcement Learning (RL) have emerged as an alternative, capable of learning policies that map system states to actions without explicit models. However, pure RL can sometimes lead to unnatural or inefficient behaviors due to simulator inaccuracies or the difficulty of designing complex reward functions. To address this, research has increasingly focused on integrating data-driven approaches, inspired by natural movements, to train robots to imitate animal-like motions. Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), a method building on Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL), represents one such approach, enabling agents to learn and replicate motion "styles" from reference datasets.

This paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by addressing the challenge of autonomous and smooth multimodal locomotion transitions in aerial humanoid robots. It proposes a learning-based method that leverages AMP and RL, specifically targeting the spontaneous emergence of mode-switching. The work distinguishes itself from prior research by demonstrating transitions without explicit trajectory tracking from TO or reliance on state machines for mode switching. The robot in this study learns to select locomotion modes implicitly based on environmental feedback, which represents an advancement in autonomous multimodal control for aerial humanoids.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an approach that enables aerial humanoid robots to perform automatic and smooth transitions between legged and aerial locomotion. This overarching goal is driven by several more specific objectives and motivations:

- **Addressing the mode-switching challenge:** A central motivation is the identified gap in the research landscape regarding autonomous and intelligent mode switching for multimodal robots. The paper aims to move beyond predefined state machines or explicitly computed trajectories for transitions, instead seeking a method where the robot itself learns when and how to switch.
- **Leveraging motion priors for natural and efficient behavior:** The authors aim to integrate Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) to enable the robot to learn specific locomotion patterns. This includes learning human-like walking gaits from motion capture data and efficient flying patterns derived from trajectory optimization solutions. The motivation here is to imbue the robot with natural-looking and efficient movements, overcoming limitations of naive RL.
- **Achieving spontaneous emergence of locomotion patterns:** A key objective is for the robot to spontaneously adapt its locomotion strategy (walking or flying) based on environmental feedback and task requirements, rather than through an explicit high-level planner or predefined logic. This includes adapting to complex terrains and choosing the most suitable locomotion modality.
- **Simplifying reward function design:** By incorporating AMP, the method aims to reduce the need for designing complex task-specific reward functions that often plague RL approaches. The style reward implicitly guides the agent towards desired motion characteristics.
- **Minimizing energy expenditure:** The research motivates the inclusion of an energy proxy term (thrust penalty) in the reward function to encourage the robot to use legged locomotion when feasible and only resort to aerial locomotion when necessary, thereby promoting energy-efficient behavior.
- **Advancing aerial humanoid capabilities:** The ultimate motivation is to enhance the capabilities of aerial humanoid robots, enabling them to operate effectively in diverse and challenging environments. This is highlighted by potential applications in search and rescue, surveillance, and exploration missions, where the ability to traverse varied terrains and obstacles is critical.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method combines Reinforcement Learning (RL) with Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) to enable multimodal locomotion and automatic mode switching for aerial humanoid robots. The robotic platform used for experimentation is iRonCub, a jet-powered aerial humanoid robot equipped with 23 joints and four JetCat P250 engines.

**A. Problem Formulation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP)**
The problem is modeled as an MDP, where the agent (robot) learns a policy π_σ (a_t|s_t) to maximize an expected discounted return.

**B. Reward Function**
The total reward r_t is a weighted sum of a task reward (G_r_t) and a style reward (S_r_t): `r_t = w_G * G_r_t + w_S * S_r_t`.

- **Style Reward (S_r_t):** This term encourages the policy to generate locomotion patterns that resemble a provided motion dataset (D). It is derived from a discriminator (D_Φ), which is a neural network trained to distinguish between motion transitions from the dataset and those produced by the agent. The discriminator's training objective minimizes the classification error and includes a gradient penalty for stability. The style reward itself is formulated such that the agent receives a higher reward when its produced motion transitions are deemed "real" by the discriminator.
- **Task Reward (G_r_t):** This component guides the robot towards a specified goal (waypoint tracking) and promotes efficient behavior. It consists of four sub-rewards:
 - **Waypoint tracking (c_r_t):** An exponential reward inversely proportional to the squared distance from the robot's base to the target position (x_d).
 - **Desired velocity (v_r_t):** An exponential reward encouraging the robot to approach the target with a specified velocity (v_d).
 - **Facing target (f_r_t):** A reward component that encourages the robot's x-axis to align with the horizontal projection of the vector pointing from the robot to the target.
 - **Thrust penalty (T_r_t):** A quadratic penalty on the magnitude of the thrust applied, designed to minimize propulsion expenditure and implicitly encourage walking over flying when possible.
 The total task reward is a weighted sum of these four components.

**C. Observation Space**
The robot's observation space (`o`) is a concatenation of three components:
- **Robot observation (o_robot):** Includes the base orientation (quaternion), base linear and angular velocities in the base frame, joint positions and velocities, relative positions of end-effectors (hands and feet), and the current thrust acting on the system.
- **Environment observation (o_env):** An elevation map (V x L grid) centered at the robot's base, providing height information of the surrounding terrain.
- **Task observation (o_task):** The position of the target waypoint relative to the robot's base.

**D. Action Space**
The action space (`a`) comprises:
- **Desired joint positions (θ_d):** These are fed to a proportional-derivative (PD) controller to control the robot's joints.
- **Desired thrust dynamics input (u):** This input affects the thrust intensities (T) of the jet engines. In the ideal thrust case, `u` represents the rate-of-change of thrust. In the specific jet-powered actuation case, `u` is a throttle input to a learned jet engine dynamics model.

**E. Discriminator Observation Space**
The discriminator's input (`χ`) is defined as the robot observation vector (`o_robot`). The discriminator is trained using pairs of consecutive observations (`χ_t`, `χ_t+1`) from both the motion dataset and the agent's generated movements.

**F. Motion Priors (Datasets)**
Two types of motion datasets are utilized to provide the "style" for AMP:
- **Walking motions:** Derived from recorded human motion capture clips, which are then retargeted to the iRonCub model using inverse kinematics. In these motions, thrust is set to zero.
- **Flying motions:** Generated using trajectory optimization for aerial humanoid robots. These datasets contain the full kinematic state of the robot, including joint positions, base poses, and thrust intensities during flight.

**G. Training Environment and Algorithm**
The training is conducted in the Nvidia Isaac Gym simulator, leveraging its massive parallel training capabilities. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used as the reinforcement learning algorithm, training 4096 agents concurrently over approximately 2 hours on an NVIDIA Quadro RTX 6000.

**H. Jet-Powered Actuation Modeling**
For a realistic assessment, the authors model the jet engines using a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) neural network. This LSTM model is trained on real-world data collected from JetCat P250 engines, predicting thrust based on throttle input and previous thrust values. This model is then embedded into the simulation environment to represent the actual jet dynamics during training and evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings based on simulated experiments with the iRonCub robot, addressing the feasibility and characteristics of the proposed AMP-based multimodal locomotion method.

**A. Smooth Multimodal Locomotion Achievement**
The robot successfully learned to execute walking, take-off, flying, and landing maneuvers, demonstrating smooth and automatic transitions between these locomotion modes. This was observed without the need for an explicit high-level planner or state machine to dictate mode switching. In a scenario involving flat ground with waypoints at both ground level and in the air, the robot effectively switched between walking to reach ground targets and flying to reach aerial targets (Fig. 6).

**B. Terrain-Aware Locomotion**
When presented with diverse terrains, including flat ground, rough patches, stepping stones, and pits, the robot utilized an elevation map in its observation space to adapt its locomotion strategy. The robot learned to walk across reachable terrain and automatically transitioned to flying when the ground became inaccessible, such as over wide gaps or high obstacles (Fig. 7). The effectiveness of this terrain-aware behavior was found to be dependent on the tuning of the thrust penalization term in the reward function, which balances the preference for walking over flying.

**C. Ablation Studies on Motion Priors**
Ablation studies were conducted to assess the contribution of the different motion priors:
- **No Motion Prior:** A policy trained without any motion priors achieved the task but exhibited higher thrust usage, indicating less efficient locomotion compared to policies with priors.
- **Only Walking Motion Prior:** This policy learned to walk but did not develop flying capabilities, preventing it from reaching aerial waypoints and resulting in lower overall rewards. It showed minimal thrust usage due to limited aerial capability.
- **Only Flying Motion Prior:** This policy learned to fly but not walk. It could reach aerial waypoints but demonstrated higher thrust usage than the policy with both priors, suggesting the flying prior, while optimized from TO, was not as efficient in a combined context without the walking option.
- **Walking + Flying Motion Prior (Proposed Method):** This policy, utilizing both walking and flying priors, successfully learned both locomotion modes and their transitions. It achieved similar task rewards to the "no prior" and "only flying prior" cases but with the lowest thrust usage, indicating the most efficient and versatile locomotion. This outcome suggests that the integration of both priors facilitates efficient mode-switching and optimized energy expenditure (Fig. 8).

**D. Comparison with Trajectory Optimization**
A comparison with a traditional trajectory optimization (TO) method highlighted the advantages of the proposed learning-based approach (Table IV):
- **Computational Efficiency:** The proposed method, once trained (approximately 2 hours), allows for online, adaptive control, whereas the TO method requires significant computation time (around 30 minutes for a single trajectory) for offline planning, necessitating replanning for dynamic environments.
- **Terrain Awareness:** The AMP-based policy inherently incorporates terrain information from the elevation map, enabling adaptive locomotion. In contrast, the compared TO method does not natively deal with complex terrain information and would require separate modules for terrain interpretation and reactive replanning.
- **Automatic Transition:** Both methods can achieve transitions, but the proposed method learns to do so automatically and adaptively based on environmental context, unlike TO which often requires predefined contact sequences or precomputed trajectories.

**E. Use Case: Jet-Powered Actuation**
The approach was validated using a realistic LSTM model of the JetCat P250 jet engines, identified from real-world data (Fig. 9, Table III). This model captures the comparatively slower response dynamics of real jet propulsion systems. The policy trained with this realistic actuation model still learned to transition between walking and flying. However, due to the slower thrust response, the transitions were observed to be slower than in the ideal thrust case, and the thrust penalization had to be reduced to encourage aerial locomotion when needed. This demonstrates the method's applicability to more realistic robotic systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotics by addressing the fundamental challenge of multimodal locomotion and autonomous mode switching in aerial humanoid robots. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Advancement in Autonomous Multimodal Control:** The work demonstrates a learning-based method that enables aerial humanoid robots to autonomously and smoothly transition between walking and flying. This marks a departure from reliance on predefined state machines or computationally intensive trajectory optimization for mode selection, providing a more adaptive and integrated control strategy.
- **Enhanced Robot Versatility:** By allowing robots to learn *when* and *how* to switch locomotion modes based on environmental feedback, the method significantly enhances their versatility. Robots equipped with such capabilities could navigate diverse and unstructured environments more effectively, combining the stability and energy efficiency of legged locomotion with the speed and obstacle-avoidance capabilities of aerial movement.
- **Reduced Engineering Effort for Complex Behaviors:** The use of Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP) to embed desired locomotion styles (human-like walking, optimized flying) simplifies the reward function design in reinforcement learning. This reduces the extensive engineering effort typically required to craft reward functions for complex, multi-modal behaviors, making the development of advanced robotic skills more tractable.
- **Improved Efficiency and Adaptability:** The learned policies, particularly when trained with both walking and flying motion priors, demonstrated more efficient propulsion expenditure and superior adaptability to varied terrains compared to policies without priors or with only single priors. This suggests that incorporating domain-specific motion knowledge can lead to both robust and optimized performance.
- **Foundation for Future Robotic Applications:** The ability to learn multimodal locomotion and intelligent mode switching is critical for the deployment of aerial humanoid robots in practical applications. This research paves the way for their use in demanding real-world scenarios such as:
 - **Search and rescue missions:** Robots could fly over obstacles and difficult terrain, then land and walk through rubble or confined spaces to assist in disaster areas.
 - **Surveillance and inspection:** Robots could efficiently cover large areas by flying, then precisely inspect structures or environments by walking or perching.
 - **Exploration missions:** Enabling robots to navigate highly varied and unknown terrains, from rugged landscapes to complex indoor environments.
- **Bridge to Realistic Deployment:** The validation using a realistic jet engine dynamics model, identified from real-world data, is a crucial step towards bridging the sim-to-real gap. While further real-world reliability investigations are noted as necessary, this aspect of the research indicates a consideration for practical robot deployment.

In conclusion, this research provides a significant step towards developing truly versatile and autonomous aerial humanoid robots, offering a promising paradigm for control design that integrates learned, natural behaviors with adaptive decision-making for multimodal locomotion.
