# Closing the Train-Test Gap in World Models for Gradient-Based Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Columbia University and NYU introduced Online World Modeling (OWM) and Adversarial World Modeling (AWM) to mitigate the train-test gap in world models for gradient-based planning (GBP). These methods enabled GBP to achieve performance comparable to or better than search-based planning algorithms like CEM, while simultaneously reducing computation time by an order of magnitude across various robotic tasks.

## Problem

- World models suffer from a "train-test gap" where they encounter out-of-distribution latent states during gradient-based planning, leading to compounding prediction errors.
- The planning objective, derived from learned world models, often presents a complex, non-smooth loss landscape that hinders the effectiveness of gradient-based optimization.
- Gradient-based planning has historically underperformed search-based methods empirically, despite its potential for computational efficiency in model-based control.

## Method

- **OWM** (Online World Modeling) iteratively expands the world model's training data by executing GBP-generated action sequences in the true environment simulator and adding the resulting "corrected" ground-truth trajectories.
- **AWM** (Adversarial World Modeling) finetunes the world model by training it on ==adversarially perturbed latent states== and actions, improving robustness and smoothing the planning ==loss landscape==.
- The approach leverages a fixed, pretrained ==DINOv2== self-supervised visual encoder to embed observations into a ==latent space==, with a ==Vision Transformer== serving as the learnable latent world model.

## Results

- OWM and AWM substantially improved gradient-based planning success rates by up to **+30%** in open-loop settings, with AWM+Adam+MPC matching or exceeding the performance of search-based CEM.
- The enhanced GBP performance was achieved with an order of magnitude (**10x**) reduction in computation time compared to the Cross-Entropy Method (CEM).
- The finetuning methods effectively closed the train-test gap, reducing the world model error on planning trajectories and demonstrably smoothing the planning loss landscape for more stable optimization.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Actively addressing the train-test gap through either online data expansion or adversarial robustness training is critical for unlocking the full potential of gradient-based planning.
> - Adversarial training can effectively smooth the action-level optimization landscape, making gradient-based methods more robust and efficient for continuous control tasks.
> - Combining powerful self-supervised visual encoders with specialized finetuning of latent dynamics models provides a robust framework for high-performance and computationally efficient robotic planning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper operates at the intersection of several rapidly advancing fields within artificial intelligence and robotics: **Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL)**, **World Models**, **Self-Supervised Learning (SSL)**, and **Planning in High-Dimensional Spaces**.

Historically, robotics relied on classical control methods that derived environment models analytically from first principles. While precise, these methods struggle with complex, uncertain, or unknown dynamics. Learning-based approaches, in contrast, infer dynamics directly from data, offering greater generalization and robustness. Among these, **world models (Ha & Schmidhuber, 2018)** have emerged as a prominent paradigm. These models learn to predict future states given current states and actions, often from high-dimensional sensory inputs like images, enabling perception, prediction, and control from raw data.

The paper specifically builds on recent advancements in latent world models that leverage **pretrained visual representations** obtained via self-supervised learning, such as DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) and DINO-WM (Zhou et al., 2025). This trend, championed by researchers like Yann LeCun, emphasizes learning rich, general-purpose embeddings that can then be used by downstream models, effectively decoupling perception from dynamics modeling. This approach significantly reduces the data and computational requirements for learning effective world models in visually complex environments.

Within the context of planning with world models, different algorithms exist:
- **Classical methods (DDP, iLQR):** Effective in low dimensions but computationally prohibitive for large, non-linear world models.
- **Search-based methods (CEM, MPPI):** Widely adopted and effective, but computationally intensive as they require numerous expensive world model rollouts for sampling and evaluation, scaling poorly with high-dimensional action spaces or long horizons.
- **Gradient-based planning (GBP):** Offers a computationally efficient alternative by exploiting the differentiability of modern neural network-based world models, optimizing action sequences directly via backpropagation. However, GBP has historically lagged in empirical performance due to challenges like local minima and susceptibility to model inaccuracies.

This paper directly addresses the **empirical shortcomings of gradient-based planning** by identifying and tackling the "train-test gap." It positions itself as a crucial step towards making GBP a practical and scalable solution for real-world robotic tasks, thereby enhancing the utility of learned world models. Furthermore, its use of adversarial training connects it to the broader research in **robust machine learning** and **adversarial examples**, extending these concepts to the domain of model-based control. The Online World Modeling method is also reminiscent of **dataset aggregation (DAgger)** techniques from imitation learning, adapting them for dynamics model improvement in latent space.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to **close the fundamental train-test gap in world models to enable efficient and robust gradient-based planning (GBP)**. The authors are motivated by the immense potential of GBP as a computationally superior alternative to traditional search-based planning methods, provided its empirical performance can be elevated.

The paper identifies two primary reasons for the observed "train-test gap" and the poor empirical performance of GBP:

1. **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) State Encounters:** World models are typically trained offline on fixed datasets of "expert trajectories." During planning, particularly when gradient descent iteratively optimizes actions, the agent may explore action sequences that lead to latent states never (or rarely) encountered during the initial training. In these OOD regions, the world model's predictions become unreliable, causing errors to compound over multi-step rollouts and rendering the model ineffective for guiding optimization.
2. **Challenging Optimization Landscape:** The planning objective, being induced by the learned world model, may present a complex, non-smooth, or rugged loss landscape for action optimization. This can lead to issues like numerous poor local minima or flat regions, hindering the convergence and effectiveness of gradient-based optimizers.

By addressing these two intertwined challenges, the authors aim to:
- **Improve the accuracy of world model predictions** in states relevant to planning, even if they deviate from the initial training distribution.
- **Smooth the action-level optimization landscape**, making it more amenable to gradient descent.
- Ultimately, **unlock the computational efficiency** of GBP, allowing it to match or surpass the performance of slower search-based methods like CEM, which is critical for real-world robotic applications requiring fast inference and decision-making.

The motivation stems from the desire to leverage the differentiability of modern neural networks within world models for highly efficient planning, transforming GBP from a promising theoretical concept into a practical and high-performing tool for robotic control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose two novel finetuning methods, **Online World Modeling (OWM)** and **Adversarial World Modeling (AWM)**, built upon a foundation of latent world models.

**Foundation: Latent World Models for Planning**
1. **Encoder (Φμ):** They utilize a fixed, pretrained self-supervised visual encoder, specifically DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024), to map high-dimensional observations (images) into a lower-dimensional latent space (Z). This encoder remains frozen throughout their finetuning process.
2. **Latent World Model (fθ):** A learnable transition function (fθ: Z × A → Z), implemented using a Vision Transformer (ViT) architecture, predicts the next latent state given the current latent state and an action.
3. **Training Objective:** The world model is initially trained offline on a next-state prediction objective: minimizing the ℓ2 distance between the predicted next latent state and the ground-truth next latent state (from expert trajectories).
4. **Planning Objective:** At test time, planning involves finding an optimal sequence of actions ({ât}H t=1) that minimizes the ℓ2 distance between the world model's predicted final latent state (rolloutf(z1, {ât})) and a target goal latent state (zgoal). Gradient-Based Planning (GBP) solves this via gradient descent. They also introduce a "Weighted Goal Loss" (WGL) that encourages intermediate latent states to be close to the goal, which empirically improves performance.
5. **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** For practical robustness, MPC is used, where an H-step action sequence is optimized, but only the first K actions are executed before replanning from the new state.

**Proposed Finetuning Methods to Close the Train-Test Gap:**

1. **Online World Modeling (OWM):**
 - **Problem Addressed:** Compounding prediction errors due to encountering out-of-distribution states during planning.
 - **Approach:** OWM iteratively expands the world model's training data distribution to cover regions of the latent space explored by GBP.
 - It starts with a pretrained world model.
 - For each online iteration, it samples an initial and goal state from the expert dataset.
 - It then performs GBP using the current world model to generate a candidate action sequence ({ât}H t=1).
 - Crucially, this action sequence is then executed in the **true environment simulator (h)** to obtain a "corrected" ground-truth trajectory (z1, â1, z'2, â2..., z'H+1), where z't are the actual latent states produced by the simulator.
 - These corrected trajectories are added to a new dataset, and the world model is finetuned on this expanded dataset.
 - **Analogy:** This process is likened to DAgger in imitation learning, where the simulator acts as an "expert" to correct the planner's potentially poor rollouts.

2. **Adversarial World Modeling (AWM):**
 - **Problem Addressed:** Non-smooth planning loss landscape and susceptibility to adversarial action sequences.
 - **Approach:** AWM uses adversarial training to improve the world model's robustness and smooth its input gradients.
 - It finetunes the world model by optimizing its performance under "worst-case perturbations" to input latent states and actions.
 - For each state-action pair in a minibatch, it generates perturbations (δz, δa) that maximally increase the world model's next-state prediction loss.
 - These perturbations are generated efficiently using a single step of a Projected Gradient Descent (PGD)-like attack, specifically drawing inspiration from the Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM), by taking a signed gradient ascent step on the loss with respect to the input perturbations.
 - The world model is then trained on these adversarially perturbed inputs (z_t + δz, a_t + δa) to predict the original ground-truth next state (z_t+1).
 - **Advantage:** Unlike OWM, AWM does not require an active environment simulator during finetuning (only the initial dataset) and directly targets the smoothness of the loss landscape, making it suitable for situations where simulation is expensive or unavailable.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methods are evaluated on three robotic tasks: PushT (object manipulation), PointMaze (navigation), and Wall (navigation with obstacles), using a pretrained DINO-WM as the base. Performance is measured by success rate, comparing GBP (with GD and Adam) against the search-based Cross-Entropy Method (CEM). Computational efficiency (wall clock time) and the "train-test gap" in world model error are also analyzed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, demonstrating the effectiveness of both proposed finetuning methods:

1. **Substantial Improvement in Gradient-Based Planning (GBP) Performance:** Both Online World Modeling (OWM) and Adversarial World Modeling (AWM) significantly boost the success rates of GBP compared to the baseline DINO-WM across all tested tasks (PushT, PointMaze, Wall).
 - In open-loop planning, improvements reached up to +30% in success rate (e.g., Wall task).
 - In the Model Predictive Control (MPC) setting, AWM with Adam consistently outperformed or matched the baseline.

2. **Matching or Exceeding Search-Based CEM Performance:**
 - Critically, AWM combined with the Adam optimizer for GBP, and used in an MPC setting, achieved performance comparable to or better than the traditionally strong Cross-Entropy Method (CEM). For instance, on PointMaze and Wall, AWM+Adam+MPC outperformed DINO-WM+CEM, and on PushT, it matched it. This is a major breakthrough, as CEM is a widely adopted but computationally expensive baseline.

3. **Dramatic Improvement in Computational Efficiency:**
 - The enhanced performance of GBP with AWM was achieved with an **order of magnitude (10x) reduction in computation time** compared to CEM. This efficiency makes GBP a far more practical choice for real-time robotic control and planning in environments with high-dimensional action spaces.

4. **Closing the Train-Test Gap:**
 - The paper empirically demonstrates that the proposed methods effectively narrow the train-test gap. The "difference in world model error" between expert (training) trajectories and planning (test) trajectories significantly decreased, and in many cases became negative (meaning better performance on planning trajectories). This indicates that the finetuned models generalize better to the out-of-distribution states and actions encountered during planning.
 - Specifically, Adversarial World Modeling was shown to visibly **smooth the planning loss landscape**, creating a broader basin around the optimum, which contributes to more stable and effective gradient-based optimization (Figure 2).

5. **Robustness and Generalizability:**
 - **Architectural Agnosticism:** AWM also improved planning performance when applied to a different world model architecture (IRIS, Micheli et al., 2023), suggesting its applicability beyond the DINO-WM framework.
 - **Perturbation Generation Efficiency:** Ablation studies confirmed that the computationally inexpensive Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) for generating adversarial perturbations performed comparably to more expensive multi-step Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attacks, making AWM practical for large datasets.
 - **Long-Horizon Tasks:** Both OWM and AWM demonstrated advantages in long-horizon planning tasks (e.g., 50 steps), further underscoring their robustness.

6. **Comparison between OWM and AWM:** While both methods improved GBP, AWM generally showed superior performance, particularly when paired with the Adam optimizer. This suggests that directly encouraging robustness and smoothness through adversarial training might be more effective than purely expanding the data distribution, especially given the computational efficiency of the AWM approach.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant advancement in the field of model-based reinforcement learning and robotic planning, with several profound implications:

1. **Practical Viability of Gradient-Based Planning (GBP):** The most immediate and impactful outcome is the demonstration that GBP, when paired with appropriately finetuned world models, can match or even exceed the performance of leading search-based methods like CEM, all while being significantly more computationally efficient. This addresses a long-standing challenge and positions GBP as a practical, scalable, and highly attractive alternative for real-time robotic control, especially in complex, high-dimensional environments.

2. **Robustness to Distribution Shift:** The paper offers effective strategies to mitigate the critical "train-test gap" – a pervasive issue in learned dynamics models. By either actively exploring and correcting out-of-distribution trajectories (OWM) or proactively enhancing model robustness and smoothing the optimization landscape (AWM), the methods improve the reliability of world models under planning-induced distribution shifts. This directly contributes to more generalizable and dependable AI agents.

3. **Advancing World Models and Self-Supervised Learning:** The work further validates the power of combining self-supervised visual encoders (like DINOv2) with learned latent dynamics models. By demonstrating that enhancements to the dynamics model training can significantly improve downstream planning performance, it strengthens the case for Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) and similar SSL paradigms as foundations for intelligent agents.

4. **Broader Impact on Machine Learning Robustness:** The successful application of adversarial training (AWM) to improve the *optimization landscape* for control tasks extends its utility beyond traditional classification robustness. This highlights a new avenue for using adversarial techniques to improve the stability and performance of gradient-based optimization in dynamic, sequential decision-making contexts.

5. **Enabling Complex Robotic Applications:** The enhanced computational efficiency opens doors for deploying world model-based planning in real-world robotic systems where fast, adaptive decision-making is paramount. This could accelerate progress in areas like dexterous manipulation, autonomous navigation in unstructured environments, and human-robot interaction. The ability to plan over longer horizons (as shown in appendix) is also crucial for complex, multi-step tasks.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The paper lays a strong foundation for future work. Evaluating these methods on real-world hardware, exploring their effectiveness in multi-timescale or hierarchical world models for even longer-horizon planning, and investigating adversarial training's role in handling environmental stochasticity or external adversaries are natural next steps.

In conclusion, this research provides a crucial step forward for world model-based control by making gradient-based planning a highly competitive and efficient approach. Its practical methods for addressing distribution shift and optimizing loss landscapes are likely to have a lasting impact on the development of robust and scalable intelligent agents.
