# Humanoid-DART: Humanoid Loco-Manipulation using Diffusion-guided Augmentation through Relabeling and Tracking

> [!summary] Summary
> The Humanoid-DART framework presents a self-supervised, iterative approach for humanoid robots to acquire diverse, goal-conditioned loco-manipulation skills from a sparse set of initial demonstrations. This system successfully deployed learned behaviors on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid, demonstrating robust task-space coverage up to 96.6% and generalization beyond the initial demonstration range.

## Problem

- Existing humanoid loco-manipulation methods require extensive human demonstrations, which are costly and difficult to scale for contact-rich and diverse tasks.
- The combinatorial complexity of task spaces involving robot body motion, object states, and environmental interactions limits the generality of current imitation learning approaches.
- Generating dynamically feasible and goal-conditioned motions from minimal data for complex humanoid behaviors remains a significant challenge.

## Method

- **Humanoid-DART** employs an iterative curriculum that bootstraps a ==goal-conditioned Diffusion Transformer== (motion generator) with a physics-based Reinforcement Learning policy (motion tracker).
- Kinematic trajectories generated for sampled goals are evaluated by the motion tracker in simulation, and successful (re-labeled) rollouts are added to an 'elite archive' to progressively expand the skill set.
- The motion generator incorporates a dual-branch backbone and structured partial unmasking for enhanced expressivity, while the motion tracker uses PPO with domain randomization for robust policy learning and sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- Learned loco-manipulation trajectories were successfully deployed on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid for push, kick, and pick-and-place tasks, confirming dynamic feasibility and hardware transferability.
- The framework achieved near-complete task-space coverage (e.g., **96.6%** for pick-and-place) from as few as four initial demonstrations, demonstrating generalization to goals 4-5 times further than the base data.
- Humanoid-DART outperformed baseline methods in task-space coverage across all evaluated tasks, showcasing its superior ability to expand the repertoire of achievable behaviors, despite sometimes having lower average fitness due to broader task-space exploration.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An iterative feedback loop between a kinematic motion generator and a physics-based tracking policy effectively enables structured exploration and learning of complex loco-manipulation skills from sparse initial data.
> - Decomposition of the problem into motion generation and motion tracking provides dense, task-agnostic supervision and reduces the computational burden on each component, leading to efficient skill acquisition.
> - Specific architectural enhancements in the diffusion model, such as the dual-branch backbone and structured partial unmasking, are crucial for generating kinematically consistent, goal-conditioned motions with improved coordination between locomotion and manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics has seen significant advancements in loco-manipulation, which requires coordinated whole-body control and dynamic object interactions. A predominant paradigm for acquiring these skills involves imitating human demonstrations. These demonstrations are typically collected via motion capture or teleoperation, then retargeted to a robot's embodiment, and subsequently used to train tracking-based reinforcement learning (RL) policies. Recent work has demonstrated broad locomotion capabilities by training generalist humanoid tracking policies on large-scale human motion datasets.

However, existing approaches face several challenges, particularly when scaling to contact-rich loco-manipulation tasks. Such tasks involve large, continuous task spaces defined by robot body motion, object states, and environmental interactions. The variability in object pose, transport configuration, grasp strategies, and interaction timing creates a combinatorial space that is challenging and costly to cover adequately with demonstrations alone. Consequently, many current methods often focus on tracking a single reference behavior, limiting their generality. The high cost of collecting diverse demonstrations and the need for continuous human intervention to correct policy failures hinder the scalability of these imitation learning approaches.

In parallel, diffusion models have emerged as a powerful class of generative models for high-dimensional data synthesis, including human and humanoid motion generation. These models can synthesize diverse motions conditioned on various labels. While some works extend diffusion models to humanoid robots for motion sequence tracking, they typically rely on the availability of large-scale datasets. This paper aims to augment small initial datasets, using diffusion models for kinematic trajectory exploration rather than end-to-end control.

Curriculum learning in RL is another related area, where agents acquire advanced behaviors by progressively adapting task distributions. While common in humanoid control, these methods usually rely on a single policy that must both explore and acquire skills through environment interaction, often requiring meticulous reward engineering. This work proposes a curriculum-like framework but decomposes the problem into distinct components: a motion generator and a motion-tracking policy, which alternate in a bootstrapping process. This decomposition provides advantages such as structured exploration, dense task-agnostic supervision, and a reduced burden on the generative model compared to policy-only RL curricula. The work differentiates itself from iterative simulation-based augmentation frameworks by focusing on goal-conditioned loco-manipulation in continuous object-goal spaces, requiring explicit reasoning over robot-object contact.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a self-supervised framework capable of learning diverse, goal-conditioned humanoid loco-manipulation policies from a sparse set of initial demonstrations. The motivation stems from the limitations of current imitation learning approaches, which struggle with the high cost of collecting varied demonstrations and the combinatorial explosion of task spaces in contact-rich manipulation.

The specific objectives and motivations are detailed as follows:

- **Overcoming Data Scarcity:** To enable learning complex loco-manipulation skills without requiring large-scale human motion datasets. The framework aims to bootstrap from minimal demonstrations and progressively expand its behavioral repertoire.
- **Achieving Goal-Conditioned Policies:** To learn policies that can automatically explore and execute tasks across a continuous goal space with minimal expert supervision. This contrasts with methods that are limited to tracking a predefined set of behaviors.
- **Structured Exploration and Increased Behavioral Diversity:** To employ a motion generator to facilitate structured exploration in trajectory space. This is intended to improve the diversity of behaviors, particularly in settings with sparse initial data, by synthesizing novel but plausible motion sequences.
- **Efficient and Scalable Learning:** To decompose the learning process into a high-level motion generation component and a low-level motion-tracking policy. This separation is motivated by the desire to reduce the computational and engineering burden on each component, leading to a more efficient and scalable learning pipeline. Specifically, tracking rewards are designed to provide dense, task-agnostic supervision, thereby minimizing extensive reward engineering.
- **Iterative Trajectory Augmentation:** To develop an iterative pipeline that continually refines and expands its knowledge base. The work aims to be the first of its kind for learning goal-conditioned loco-manipulation skills through such an augmentation process.
- **Novel Motion Generator Architecture:** To design and implement specific architectural choices for the motion generator that enhance its ability to synthesize novel trajectories, even from limited initial data. This includes a dual-branch diffusion transformer and a structured partial unmasking scheme to improve feature correlation learning.
- **Curriculum-Based Learning with Goal Relabeling:** To integrate a curriculum-based algorithm with a goal relabeling strategy. This is motivated by the need to convert near-missed rollouts into valid training signals, thereby maximizing the utility of generated data and guiding exploration efficiently across the task space.
- **Real-world Applicability:** To validate that the generated and tracked behaviors are dynamically feasible and can be deployed on physical humanoid robots, ensuring practical relevance.

In summary, the core motivation is to develop a robust, data-efficient, and scalable framework for humanoid loco-manipulation that can autonomously expand its skill set to cover a broad, continuous task space, significantly reducing the reliance on extensive human demonstrations and interventions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Humanoid-DART framework operates as a self-supervised, iterative curriculum designed to learn loco-manipulation skills from sparse initial demonstrations. The pipeline consists of three main, tightly coupled components: a Kinematic Motion Generator, a Physics-Based Evaluator, and a Motion Tracking Policy. The overall algorithm alternates between refining a goal-conditioned motion generator and a physics-based motion-tracking policy, allowing them to bootstrap each other.

**A. Algorithm Overview**

The pipeline starts by pretraining the motion tracking policy on initial demonstrations (if dynamically feasible) and seeding an "elite archive" (E) with dynamically feasible rollouts. If the initial demonstrations are only kinematically feasible, they directly seed the archive. Subsequently, the motion generator is pretrained on this initial elite set. The core of the algorithm is an iterative loop across generations and iterations:

1. **Goal Sampling:** In each iteration, $N_{sampled}$ goal states are sampled around the current distribution of skills within the elite archive, facilitating exploration into nearby or underrepresented regions of the task space.
2. **Kinematic Trajectory Generation:** The goal-conditioned motion generator ($\pi_{\theta}$) produces kinematic reference trajectories for the sampled goals. These trajectories may contain physical inconsistencies.
3. **Physics Evaluation:** The motion tracking policy ($\pi_{\phi}$) executes these kinematic references in a physics simulator. This policy acts as a dynamic filter, converting potentially infeasible motion plans into physically consistent rollouts.
4. **Elite Archive Update:** The resulting trajectories are evaluated using a fitness function. Only those exceeding a defined fitness threshold ($\tau_f$) are retained in the elite archive E. Crucially, before addition, these rollouts are relabeled with their *achieved* goal to provide a better learning signal for the motion generator, even if they didn't perfectly match the *sampled* goal.
5. **Motion Generator Training:** The motion generator ($\pi_{\theta}$) is retrained on the expanded elite archive E to improve its goal-conditioned synthesis capabilities.
6. **Motion Tracker Training:** At the end of each generation (multiple iterations), the motion tracking policy ($\pi_{\phi}$) is refined via reinforcement learning (PPO) using the entire expanded elite archive E to improve its ability to reproduce the growing set of behaviors.

This cycle progressively expands the diversity of feasible trajectories and the coverage of the task space, enabling learning from minimal initial data.

**B. Kinematic Motion Generator**

The motion generator is implemented as a goal-conditioned Diffusion Transformer (DiT1D), designed to produce kinematically plausible motions close to the demonstration manifold.

1. **Motion Representation:** Expert demonstrations are converted into a segmented egocentric representation. Features are expressed relative to the robot's local coordinate frame at $t_0$, translated, and yaw-aligned. This normalization reduces dependence on global position/heading. The state at each frame $t$ includes:
 - Root x–y displacement ($\Delta p_{rob,xy}$)
 - Absolute root height ($p_{rob,z}$)
 - Root yaw rotation ($\Delta\psi_{rob}$)
 - Joint angles ($j_{pos}$) relative to the default pose
 - Root pitch and roll ($r_{rob,xy}$) in 6D rotation representation
 - Object position and orientation ($^{rob}p_{obj}$, $^{rob}r_{obj}$) in the robot base frame.
 This hierarchical representation helps mitigate kinematic artifacts and mirrors the generator's structure.

2. **Network Architecture:** Two key design choices enhance the generator's expressivity and kinematic consistency:
 - **Dual-branch backbone:** The state is factorized into "world" features (root motion and object-relative features) and "local" features (body pose). A global stream processes world features, conditioned on task goals and motion history via AdaLN modulation. A local stream processes body pose features and cross-attends to the global stream to maintain synchronization. This decouples coarse navigation from fine-grained pose synthesis.
 - **Structured partial unmasking:** During training, per-token noise levels are used, and noised feature groups from future frames are selectively revealed. This encourages the planner to infer missing motion components from partially observed states and learn correlations between feature groups. Cross-attention analysis demonstrated that with unmasking, the local stream attends more to object-relative pose, indicating improved coordination.

3. **Inference:** The motion denoiser models the conditional distribution of a trajectory segment using 10 deterministic DDIM steps. Long-horizon sequences are synthesized auto-regressively over overlapping segments, conditioned on history frames (inpainted for temporal consistency) and the task goal. Classifier-free guidance is applied with a tunable weight.

**C. Physics-Based Motion Evaluator**

This component rigorously selects high-quality elites. The fitness function evaluates both physical feasibility and task correctness as a product of several terms:
- $R_{pos}, R_{vel}, R_{root,pos}, R_{root,ori}, R_{leg}$: Gaussian kernel terms penalizing deviations from the reference trajectory for key body positions, link velocities, pelvis-root position and orientation, and lower-limb joint pose.
- $R_{contact}$: An indicator term verifying expected hand-object contacts.
- $R_{obj}$: A Gaussian term on the 6-DoF object pose error.
A fall or early termination sets the fitness to zero.

**D. Dynamic Motion Tracker**

A DeepMimic-inspired reinforcement learning controller, extended for object-robot contacts, is used.
- **Rewards:** Penalties are applied for deviations from the reference root pose, body keypoints, joint positions, and end-effector contact states.
- **Policy Architecture:** An asymmetric actor-critic network is employed. The actor observes a 5-step reference horizon, proprioceptive state, and object pose. The critic additionally receives privileged simulation state.
- **Training:** Massively parallelized Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE) is used. Adaptive trajectory- and state-level sampling (initializing episodes mid-trajectory) accelerates learning. Domain randomization (over object pushes, friction, and mass) is applied to enhance robustness for sim-to-real transfer.

**E. Curriculum**

The curriculum dictates how goals are sampled in the task space to balance refinement of existing skills with exploration of new ones.

1. **Frontier Target Sampling:** The task space is divided into bins (e.g., a 2D grid for pick-and-place). For each bin, the minimum distance to an elite in the archive is calculated. This partitions the task space into three zones:
 - **Refine (R):** Bins near existing elites (distance < $\delta_{ref}$).
 - **eXplore (X):** Bins at intermediate distances ($\delta_{ref} \le \text{distance} < \delta_{exp}$).
 - **Frontier (F):** Far-away bins (distance $\ge \delta_{exp}$).
 Each zone is assigned a sampling weight ($w_{ref}, w_{exp}, w_{frontier}$), with exploration bins typically receiving the highest weight, to guide the sampling process.

2. **Goal Relabeling:** If an executed trajectory achieves a task metric close to a sampled target but not perfectly, it is relabeled with its *actual achieved* task metric before being added to the elite archive. This mechanism, analogous to Hindsight Experience Replay, recovers learning signals from near-successes, which is particularly beneficial in early stages when the generator is still developing.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of Humanoid-DART was evaluated through real-world deployment, extensive simulation studies, and comparisons with baseline methods on four loco-manipulation tasks: push, kick, hand-off, and pick-and-place.

**A. Real-World Deployment**
The loco-manipulation trajectories produced by Humanoid-DART were successfully deployed on a physical Unitree G1 humanoid for the push, kick, and pick-and-place tasks. This confirms that the generated motions are dynamically feasible and transferable to hardware.

**B. Curriculum Performance**
- **Task-Space Coverage:** The pipeline demonstrated a progressive expansion of task-space coverage. In early iterations, generated trajectories were concentrated around the initial base demonstrations. As the evolutionary process advanced, the elite set expanded its coverage, reaching near-complete coverage (e.g., 96.6% for pick-and-place) by the fourth generation.
- **Generalization:** A notable finding was the pipeline's ability to generate and track long-horizon, task-conditioned trajectories significantly beyond the scope of the base demonstrations. For instance, in pick-and-place, starting with base trajectories of approximately 5 seconds duration and goal distances of 1-2 meters, Humanoid-DART successfully generated trajectories for goals 4-5 times further away, indicating strong generalization capabilities across the full task space.

**C. Comparison with Baselines**
The framework was compared against "Parameterized Motion" and "Hierarchical Diffusion + RL" (a single-stage approach without the evolutionary loop). The comparison, summarized in Table I, focused on task-space coverage, average fitness, and object stability.
- **Humanoid-DART:** Achieved dominant task-space coverage across all four tasks. For example, 96.4% coverage for pick-and-place, 61.1% for push. While its average fitness was sometimes lower than "Parameterized Motion" (e.g., 4.9 vs 10.0 for pick-and-place), this was attributed to its ability to cover a significantly broader and more diverse set of goals, where maintaining peak quality across the entire expanded space is inherently more challenging.
- **Parameterized Motion:** Showed higher average fitness in some tasks (hand-off, pick-and-place) but with substantially limited task-space coverage (e.g., 5.7% for pick-and-place). This indicates that it could produce high-quality trajectories only within a narrow region.
- **Hierarchical Diffusion + RL:** Consistently underperformed on both coverage and fitness metrics. This result directly supports the conclusion that the iterative evolutionary loop of Humanoid-DART is a critical driver of its performance and ability to expand skills.

**D. Motion Generator Architecture Ablations**
Ablation studies on the motion generator architecture (Table II) highlighted the importance of the two key design decisions:
- **Dual-branch backbone:** This was critical for goal-reaching accuracy. Collapsing the global and local streams into a single "flat" Diffusion Transformer (DiT) led to a significant drop in success rate (from 1.00 to 0.25) when evaluated on novel goals, indicating poor coordination between locomotion, manipulation, and whole-body pose.
- **Structured partial unmasking:** This feature improved path quality and grasp consistency. It resulted in straighter and shorter object paths and lower hand-object distances during airborne phases. The analysis of cross-attention weights (Figure 3) demonstrated that structured partial unmasking enabled the local stream to attend more effectively to the object-relative position and orientation, establishing a crucial correlation between whole-body pose synthesis and object interaction.

**E. Pipeline Ablations**
Ablations on the number and type of base demonstrations (Table III) provided insights into initialization:
- **Number of Base Demonstrations:** More initial dynamically feasible (DF) demonstrations led to faster task-space coverage and higher overall coverage. For instance, 4 DF demonstrations achieved 96.4% coverage in 3.3 hours (to 80% coverage), compared to 77.3% coverage for 1 DF demonstration.
- **Type of Base Trajectory:** Dynamically feasible (DF) seeds, which are artifact-free, resulted in higher-quality elites, faster convergence, and improved coverage. In contrast, kinematically feasible (KF) seeds (produced via kinematic retargeting only, potentially with physical inconsistencies) produced lower-quality candidates and significantly slower coverage growth (e.g., 2 KF demos achieved only 28.8% coverage compared to 91.2% for 2 DF demos). This indicates that the diffusion generator inherits and amplifies biases present in the seed trajectories.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

Humanoid-DART represents a notable contribution to the field of humanoid robotics, particularly in the domain of loco-manipulation. Its primary significance lies in providing a framework for learning complex, goal-conditioned skills from significantly fewer demonstrations than traditional imitation learning methods. This addresses a major bottleneck in robotics: the high cost and labor intensity of data collection.

The potential impacts of this work include:

- **Reduced Data Dependency:** By demonstrating task-space coverage from as few as four base demonstrations, the framework substantially lowers the data requirements for training advanced humanoid behaviors. This could accelerate the development and deployment of humanoid robots in real-world scenarios where extensive demonstration collection is impractical.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** The ability to generalize to goals far beyond the initial demonstration range (e.g., 4-5 times further) suggests a significant step towards more versatile and autonomous humanoid robots capable of adapting to novel task specifications without retraining for every new goal.
- **Structured Exploration:** The integration of a diffusion-based motion generator provides a structured and efficient mechanism for exploring the continuous task space. This is a departure from purely policy-driven exploration in RL, which can be inefficient in high-dimensional spaces.
- **Robustness and Physical Feasibility:** The coupling of the kinematic motion generator with a physics-based RL tracking controller and a rigorous fitness evaluation ensures that the generated behaviors are dynamically feasible and robust, as demonstrated by successful real-world deployment on the Unitree G1 humanoid.
- **Modular Learning Paradigm:** The decomposed architecture (motion generation, physics evaluation, motion tracking) offers a modular approach to tackling complex robotics problems. This modularity could simplify future research by allowing independent development and improvement of each component.
- **Advancements in Generative Models for Robotics:** The specific architectural innovations within the diffusion model, such as the dual-branch backbone and structured partial unmasking, demonstrate effective strategies for applying generative models to complex robotic motion synthesis, particularly in sparse-data regimes.
- **Curriculum Learning and Goal Relabeling:** The application of a goal relabeling strategy within a curriculum framework is significant for maximizing the learning signal from collected data, especially when generated trajectories do not perfectly match the target. This enhances the sample efficiency of the overall pipeline.

Future extensions of this work, such as expanding to more diverse object geometries, contact properties, and uneven terrain, or incorporating adaptive/learned fitness functions, could further broaden its applicability and reduce reliance on manual tuning, moving towards even more autonomous skill acquisition for humanoid robots.
