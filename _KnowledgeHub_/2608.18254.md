# GAPL: Grounded Action-effect Policy Learning for LLM-Based Trajectory Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> GAPL is a closed-loop LLM trajectory-planning framework for autonomous driving that pairs an LLM effect evaluator with a simulation-trained effect grounder and a distiller-calibrated PPO policy. Grounding LLM effect estimates against simulator rollouts reduces collision rate, ADE, and FDE while raising reward across four Highway-env scenarios versus seven baselines.

## Problem

- LLM-driven planners generate actions from language alone, so vehicle interactions, road geometry, and physical feasibility stay opaque, producing plans that are semantically plausible but physically inconsistent.
- LLM-assisted planners delegate execution to external planners but leave the LLM operating open-loop without environment-interaction feedback, so hallucinated reasoning still propagates unchecked through the pipeline.
- Existing methods rely on free-form language or implicit scalar rewards rather than structured multi-dimensional effect estimates, leaving safety, energy, and comfort dimensions unmodeled and control imprecise.

## Method

- ==LLM-based Effect Evaluator== prompts the LLM with contextual, action-conditioned, effect-grounded definitions to output structured four-dimensional action-effect estimates (reward, crash risk, energy, comfort) per candidate action.
- ==Simulation-based Effect Grounder== is a multi-headed Transformer pretrained via warm-up regression on simulator rollout targets to predict dynamics-consistent trajectory-level effects for the same four dimensions.
- **Effect-Aware Decision Maker** uses a ==grounding distiller== to calibrate LLM effects toward frozen grounder-derived utilities, fusing them with environment reward into a shaped signal that drives ==PPO== policy optimization.

## Results

- Across highway, merge, roundabout, and intersection scenarios GAPL cuts collision rate by **0.61 to 0.93** points, ADE by **0.57 to 1.13**, and FDE by **1.06 to 3.16** versus the strongest baseline LLMCWM, all at p less than 0.05.
- Reward improves by **0.57 to 3.17** over LLMCWM across scenarios, averaging a **1.44** gain, with GAPL outperforming all seven baselines including classical, LLM-driven, and LLM-assisted planners.
- Ablations show removing simulation grounding hurts most, raising ADE and FDE by **3.05** and **3.95** on average and cutting reward by **3.67**, while **24k** warm-up samples cut intersection ADE and FDE by **4.36** and **3.62** versus no warm-up.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Calibrating an LLM's semantic effect estimates against a learned dynamics-consistent reference, rather than trusting language alone, is what actually suppresses hallucinated planning decisions.
> - Structured multi-dimensional effect vectors (reward, risk, energy, comfort) carry denser supervisory signal for policy learning than a single scalar reward, especially in interaction-heavy scenarios.
> - A frozen grounder trained only during warm-up risks degrading under policy-induced distribution shift, so static simulation grounding is a ceiling that online adaptation would need to lift.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Trajectory planning is a fundamental aspect of autonomous driving, requiring the generation of safe and efficient paths in complex, dynamic environments. Traditional planning methods often exhibit limitations in their adaptability to diverse traffic conditions, as they are typically designed for specific scenarios.

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) has introduced a new paradigm, offering semantic-rich planning capabilities due to their strong reasoning and contextual understanding. LLMs have been explored for tasks ranging from high-level action generation to complex policy learning in various robotic and embodied agent applications. However, their application in high-stakes domains like autonomous driving faces several challenges. These include issues such as hallucinated reasoning, where LLMs generate physically inconsistent or incorrect information; poor grounding in real-world environment dynamics, leading to plans that are semantically plausible but unexecutable; and limited numerical precision in control outputs, which is critical for precise vehicle maneuvers.

Current LLM-based trajectory planning methods can generally be categorized into two groups:
- **LLM-driven planners:** These directly generate actions from language descriptions. While they leverage detailed prompts to integrate domain knowledge, they often fail to capture real-time environment dynamics such as vehicle interactions, road geometry, and physical constraints. This can result in plans that are conceptually sound but practically unreliable.
- **LLM-assisted planners:** These methods confine LLMs to high-level decision-making and delegate trajectory execution to external, specialized planners. While this approach can improve numerical precision, the LLM component itself often remains ungrounded, operating without real-time feedback from environment interactions. Consequently, errors stemming from LLM hallucinations or gaps in domain knowledge can propagate through the planning pipeline, compromising overall reliability.

This work positions itself within this landscape by seeking to address these identified limitations through a unified framework that explicitly integrates LLM-based semantic reasoning with simulation-based dynamic grounding and policy optimization. The aim is to create a closed-loop system that can leverage the semantic reasoning power of LLMs while ensuring their predictions are constrained by, and aligned with, environment dynamics and translated into precise, executable control policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper identifies three tightly coupled challenges in current LLM-based trajectory planning approaches that motivate the development of GAPL:

**C1: How to constrain hallucinated planning outputs of LLMs?**
LLMs often produce hallucinated reasoning due to a lack of explicit, structured action evaluation. Existing methods frequently rely on free-form language descriptions or implicit rewards, which make it difficult for LLMs to systematically assess the multi-dimensional consequences of candidate actions. In autonomous driving, an action simultaneously impacts multiple critical effect dimensions, such as safety (crash risk), efficiency (task progress, energy consumption), and comfort. Without a structured framework to model these dimensions, LLMs may generate unsafe or suboptimal actions. The objective is to introduce structured, multi-dimensional representations of action effects into LLM-based planning to mitigate hallucinations.

**C2: How to align language-based effect estimation with environment dynamics?**
Even with structured effect representations, LLM-derived estimates can be inconsistent with real environment dynamics. Factors like vehicle interactions, road geometry, and physical execution constraints are challenging to fully encode through language prompts alone. While fine-tuning LLMs on extensive driving data could enhance grounding, it is computationally expensive. Therefore, a key motivation is to develop a data-efficient method to align language-based effect assessments with the actual dynamics of the environment.

**C3: How to ensure numerically precise and executable control decisions?**
LLMs excel at high-level reasoning but are not designed to generate the precise numerical control parameters (e.g., position, velocity, acceleration) required for autonomous driving. Even if high-level decisions are semantically correct, imprecise control signals can lead to unsafe trajectories. The objective is to translate calibrated action effects into executable control policies that meet the numerical precision demands of driving, moving beyond vague linguistic instructions.

To address these challenges, GAPL aims to develop a unified framework that integrates structured action-effect estimation from LLMs, simulation-based grounding to align these estimates with environmental dynamics, and precision policy learning to translate decisions into executable control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GAPL (Grounded Action-effect Policy Learning) is a unified framework designed to integrate LLM-based effect estimation, simulation-based effect grounding, and policy optimization into a closed-loop system for trajectory planning. It comprises three main modules: an LLM-based Effect Evaluator, a Simulation-based Effect Grounder, and an Effect-Aware Decision Maker.

**A. LLM-based Effect Evaluator**
This module addresses Challenge C1 by prompting an LLM to perform structured, multi-dimensional effect estimation for candidate actions. It aims to constrain hallucinated planning outputs by requiring explicit quantitative assessment across critical dimensions. The evaluator receives the current state ($s_t$) and uses a structured multi-dimensional effect prompt to guide the LLM. The prompt incorporates:
- **Contextual Observation:** Instructs the LLM to summarize the current traffic and ego history trajectories, providing essential context.
- **Action-conditioned Evaluation:** Asks the LLM to prospectively reason about the consequences of executing each candidate action over a future horizon.
- **Effect-grounded Semantic Definitions:** Defines four key effect dimensions—Reward (task progress, rule compliance), Crash (collision probability), Energy (control effort), and Comfort (smoothness)—using grounded language criteria to ensure numerically interpretable and dynamics-consistent estimates.
- **Structured Quantitative Outputs:** Requires the LLM to return numerical estimates for these four dimensions in a JSON array format (e.g., expected reward, crash risk, energy consumption, and comfort over an L-step horizon).

The LLM (specifically GPT-4.1-nano) acts as an effect function, directly mapping the state and action to trajectory-level action-effect estimates. This structured approach aims to encourage explicit assessment of long-term consequences, thereby reducing hallucinated decisions.

**B. Simulation-based Effect Grounder**
This module addresses Challenge C2 by learning to predict dynamics-consistent trajectory-level action effects, aligning LLM estimates with environment dynamics.
- **Effect Grounder Model:** A multi-headed Transformer, denoted as GRO$_{\phi}$, takes the current state ($s_t$) and a set of candidate actions as input and predicts the same four trajectory-level action effects (reward, crash risk, energy, comfort) as the LLM evaluator.
- **Effect Target Generation via Simulator Rollouts:** To train GRO$_{\phi}$, supervision targets are generated using the Highway-env simulator. For each candidate action, a rollout trajectory is simulated over a finite horizon ($L$). The simulator provides instantaneous effect functions ($g_r, g_p, g_e, g_c$) for reward, crash risk, energy, and comfort, respectively. These instantaneous effects are aggregated over the rollout horizon to form trajectory-level targets ($o_{i,SIM_t}$):
 - Reward is accumulated with a discount factor.
 - Crash risk models the probability of at least one collision.
 - Energy and comfort are accumulated to measure total control effort and motion smoothness.
- **Warm-up Pre-training:** The effect grounder is pre-trained using data collected via a uniform random policy to ensure diverse state-action coverage. This minimizes a multi-task regression loss between the grounder's predictions and the simulator-derived targets. After warm-up, the grounder is fixed to provide stable action-effect estimates.

**C. Effect-Aware Decision Maker**
This module addresses Challenge C3 by using the dynamics-consistent grounder to calibrate LLM effects into a grounded utility, which then guides a PPO-based policy learning process to ensure precise and executable control.
- **Grounding Distiller Module:** A distiller ($D_{\theta}$) is employed to calibrate the LLM’s semantic effect estimates ($o_{i,LLM_t}$) towards the simulation-grounded estimates from the grounder ($o_{i,GRO_t}$), which serve as a reference. A utility function $\psi(\cdot)$ aggregates the four grounder effect dimensions into a scalar value. The distiller learns to reproduce this grounder-grade utility from the LLM's estimates. Both the LLM utility ($\tilde{o}_{i,LLM_t}$) and grounder utility ($\tilde{o}_{i,GRO_t}$) incorporate a residual environment reward term, balanced by a weight $\alpha$.
- **Reward Shaping for Policy Optimization:** A shaped reward ($\hat{r}_t$) is formulated by combining the calibrated LLM utility, the grounder utility, and the raw environment reward ($r_{env_t}$). Weights ($\omega_{env}, \omega_{LLM}, \omega_{GRO}$) control the contribution of each term. This provides a dense and informative learning signal for the policy, aligning it with both the true task objective and dynamics-consistent guidance.
- **Policy Execution and Trajectory Generation:** A stochastic policy $\pi_{\eta}$ over the environment's discrete action space is learned. At each step, an action is sampled from this policy and executed in the simulator, generating a trajectory. Policy parameters are optimized on-policy using the shaped reward.

**D. Joint Training**
The framework is trained jointly, optimizing both the policy and the distiller.
- **Policy Training:** The clipped Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used to optimize the policy, maximizing the expected return under the shaped reward $\hat{r}_t$. The advantage estimate is computed using the shaped reward.
- **Distiller Training:** The distiller ($D_{\theta}$) is trained to regress its output from the LLM’s effects to the stable, dynamics-grounded utility derived from the grounder ($\psi(o_{i,GRO_t})$), using a stop-gradient operator to ensure the grounder target remains fixed. This process effectively corrects miscalibrated LLM estimates towards physically consistent values.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted on the Highway-env simulator across four scenarios: highway, merge, roundabout, and intersection. Performance was evaluated using Collision Rate (CR), Average Displacement Error (ADE), Final Displacement Error (FDE), and cumulative Reward.

**A. Comparison Performance**
GAPL was compared against seven baselines:
- **Classical planners:** CaDet, FUSION.
- **LLM-driven planners:** SayCan, Reflexion.
- **LLM-assisted planners:** Plan-R1, Cartographer, LLMCWM.

The results demonstrated that GAPL consistently achieved superior performance across all scenarios and metrics (Table I). Compared to LLMCWM, the strongest baseline, GAPL showed:
- **Collision Rate (CR):** Average reductions of {0.93, 0.86, 0.64, 0.61} percentage points in highway, merge, roundabout, and intersection scenarios, respectively.
- **Average Displacement Error (ADE):** Average reductions of {1.00, 0.57, 0.73, 1.13} units.
- **Final Displacement Error (FDE):** Average reductions of {3.16, 1.49, 1.06, 2.30} units.
- **Cumulative Reward:** Average gains of {3.17, 0.87, 0.57, 1.16} units, with an overall average gain of 1.44 across scenarios.
Statistical significance tests (p-values < 0.05) confirmed the effectiveness of GAPL over the best baseline.

The performance gains were attributed to the structured multi-dimensional action-effect estimation constraining LLM planning, the simulation-based effect grounder aligning LLM predictions with dynamics, and the grounding distiller providing stable supervision for policy optimization. LLM-driven planners performed poorly, while LLM-assisted planners showed improvements over classical methods, highlighting the benefit of grounded dynamics. GAPL further improved upon these by integrating semantic reasoning, simulation-based grounding, and closed-loop policy learning.

**B. Ablation Study**
An ablation study with three variants (w/o LLM, w/o SIM, w/o Distiller) revealed the contribution of each component (Table II):
- **w/o SIM:** Removing the simulation-based effect grounder resulted in the most significant performance degradation, with average increases of 3.05 in ADE and 3.95 in FDE, and a reward decrease of 3.67. This indicates that LLM-only estimation fails to capture environment dynamics reliably.
- **w/o LLM:** Removing the LLM led to notable degradation, with average increases of 1.74 in ADE and 2.22 in FDE, and a reward drop of 1.98. This suggests a reduction in semantic generalization capabilities.
- **w/o Distiller:** Removing the distiller resulted in the smallest overall performance drop but still caused noticeable declines, especially in interactive scenarios like merge and intersection.

The study concluded that all three components play complementary roles, with simulation grounding being critical, LLM providing semantic estimation, and the distiller integrating signals for stable policy learning.

**C. Sensitivity Study**
- **Warm-up Data for Grounder:** Increasing the amount of warm-up data for the grounder consistently improved performance in the Intersection scenario (Table III). Using 24k samples, compared to no warm-up, reduced ADE by 4.36 and FDE by 3.62, and increased cumulative reward by 3.22, indicating more reliable dynamics grounding.
- **Rollout Length (L):** GAPL maintained consistently low ADE/FDE and high reward for rollout lengths varying from 8 to 16 steps across all scenarios, with only mild performance variations (Fig. 3). This robustness was attributed to the alignment of LLM semantic estimation with dynamics-consistent grounding.
- **LLM Backbones:** Replacing GPT-4.1-nano with GPT-3.5-turbo showed that stronger LLM backbones yielded more accurate effect estimation, with GPT-4.1-nano outperforming GPT-3.5-turbo across all metrics (Table VI). However, the performance gaps were relatively small, suggesting that even lightweight LLM backbones can be competitive when combined with the GAPL framework.
- **Weight Configurations:** A sensitivity study on the weights for the shaped reward ($\omega_{LLM}, \omega_{GRO}, \omega_{env}$) demonstrated that configurations assigning a dominant weight to the simulation-based grounder while retaining moderate LLM and minimal environment reward contributions achieved the highest final reward (Fig. 6). This indicated that leveraging both language priors and dynamics-consistent effect estimation is crucial for stable planning.

**D. Case Study**
A case study in intersection and roundabout scenarios demonstrated GAPL's ability to generate safer and smoother trajectories compared to baselines (Fig. 4). In an intersection, GAPL showed appropriate curvature early, stabilizing the trajectory and aligning with the target lane, unlike baselines that exhibited abrupt turns and deviations. In a roundabout, GAPL maintained smooth curvature, contrasting with the sharp corners and unstable steering observed in baseline trajectories, which reflects degraded comfort.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper offers a significant contribution to the field of LLM-based trajectory planning for autonomous driving by directly addressing key limitations of existing approaches.
- **Addressing LLM Hallucinations:** By introducing a structured, multi-dimensional action-effect evaluation system, GAPL provides a mechanism to constrain the LLM's reasoning, reducing the likelihood of hallucinated or logically unsafe planning outputs. This structured feedback improves the reliability of LLM-derived decisions in complex, real-world driving scenarios where multiple factors (safety, efficiency, comfort) are simultaneously critical.
- **Grounding Language in Dynamics:** The integration of a simulation-based effect grounder provides a robust method to align the LLM's semantic reasoning with physically consistent environmental dynamics. This closes the gap between abstract linguistic understanding and the tangible laws of physics, ensuring that plans are not only semantically plausible but also dynamically feasible. This is crucial for real-world deployment where physical consistency is paramount.
- **Achieving Numerical Precision:** By distilling the grounded action effects into a unified training signal for a PPO-based policy, GAPL enables the learning of numerically precise and executable control strategies. This bypasses the inherent limitation of LLMs in generating exact numerical parameters, providing a bridge from high-level reasoning to low-level control, which is essential for safe and smooth autonomous driving.
- **Unified, Closed-Loop Framework:** The development of GAPL as a unified, closed-loop system is a key architectural advancement. It demonstrates that tightly coupling semantic reasoning, dynamics-grounded simulation, and policy learning can lead to substantially improved performance in terms of safety, accuracy, and reward compared to approaches that treat these components in isolation.
- **Practical Applicability:** The computational overhead analysis indicates that GAPL achieves significantly lower latency and fewer LLM API calls per decision step compared to other LLM-based baselines. This efficiency gain, primarily from offloading numerical control to a lightweight policy, suggests that GAPL is a practical candidate for real-time autonomous driving applications, reducing both computational costs and latency.

The consistent outperformance of GAPL across various challenging driving scenarios and metrics, coupled with its architectural robustness demonstrated in ablation and sensitivity studies, suggests its potential to enhance the safety, efficiency, and comfort of autonomous driving systems. While the paper acknowledges limitations such as the grounder being frozen after warm-up (potentially leading to degradation under policy-induced distribution shift) and the reliance on a simulator (sim-to-real gap), the framework lays a foundation for future research in areas like online adaptation, grounder-guided LLM fine-tuning, and expanded effect dimensions (e.g., traffic-rule compliance).
