# Deliberate Practice: Learning Robot Skills under a Budget

> [!summary] Summary
> Deliberate Practice (DP) is an active skill-learning algorithm for robots with a fixed practice budget. It predicts each skill's competence gain as a function of practice episodes, then solves a bilinear program - an exact single-level reformulation of a bilevel budget-allocation problem via MDP LP duality - to pick the budget-optimal task plan and skills to drill. It provably allocates budget optimally and beats greedy active-learning baselines on long-horizon manipulation.

## Problem

- Pretrained general robot policies do not cover every deployment scenario, and RL remains too sample-inefficient for the short downtime windows robots actually get.
- Prior active skill-learning methods target individual skills in isolation, ignoring that long-horizon tasks need several skills mastered jointly to unlock a plan.
- Existing practice-planning methods pick greedily by one-step task improvement, so they miss high-reward plans needing multi-episode deliberate practice across several skills.

## Method

- ==Competence prediction==: models expected competence after b practice rounds with a ==piecewise-linear== (or ==saturating-exponential==) improvement function whose rate is estimated online as a smoothed running average.
- ==Budget allocation==: recasts the ==bilevel budget problem== as a max-min via the ==MDP linear program==, then uses ==LP duality== to get an exact single-level ==bilinear program== solvable globally by Gurobi.
- Skill practice: builds a ==curriculum== that sequentially masters reachable skills on the budget-optimal plan, using each new skill to plan to the next skill's preconditions; policies are ==Composable Interaction Primitives== with ==impedance controllers== tuned by ==CMA-ES==.

## Results

- On simulated Cleanup, DP adapts its target plan to the budget - top drawer (reward **1**) at **100** episodes, middle (reward **2**) at **150**, bottom (reward **4**) at **250** - while all baselines stay on the easiest plan.
- DP matches EES at low budget but significantly outperforms EES, CI, LCF, and Random at medium and high budgets over **5** seeds; on Cleanup-Multi random practice even beats EES, whose greedy signal goes flat.
- Scales to Cleanup-Multi with **22** skills and **~5000** abstract states, computing optimal allocations within **6** minutes; on the real Franka Breakfast task DP toasts bread at **30** episodes and microwaves oatmeal (reward **2**) at **60**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Known deployment downtime is exploitable information: budget size should change which skills a robot practices, not merely how long it practices them.
> - Greedy one-step improvement criteria are structurally myopic; benefits that only appear after jointly mastering several skills are invisible to them.
> - Bilevel learning-allocation problems can sometimes be reformulated exactly through LP duality, converting nested search into a bilinear program that off-the-shelf solvers globally optimize.
> - Optimality claims rest on competence priors - optimistic priors can commit a robot to plans it cannot actually learn within budget.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in large-scale robot learning have facilitated the development of general-purpose robot policies capable of executing diverse skills and adapting to new environments. When integrated with classical planning methodologies such as Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) and search algorithms, these policies enable robots to undertake complex, long-horizon tasks, thereby enhancing robotic autonomy. However, pre-training alone frequently proves insufficient for covering the full spectrum of scenarios encountered in real-world deployment, which can result in inconsistent task execution. A potential solution involves robots learning through autonomous practice and experience.

Current reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms are characterized by high sample inefficiency, which restricts their utility in real-time deployment learning. Given that robots typically have limited downtime for practice during operational periods, there is a recognized need for learning methods specifically engineered to function under explicit budget constraints.

Within the existing active skill learning paradigm, prior research has largely concentrated on the efficient acquisition of individual skills, whether through robot practice, human demonstrations, or transfer across multiple tasks. These approaches generally do not account for the sequential nature of long-horizon problems or integrate explicit budget constraints into the learning process. Similarly, in the domain of TAMP, while some methods explore planning for autonomous skill improvement in long-horizon tasks, they often rely on greedy practice strategies, which can lead to suboptimal allocation of practice time.

This work addresses a gap in the literature by focusing on active skill learning for sequential tasks within a strictly limited practice budget. It distinguishes itself from prior active learning and TAMP methods by proposing an approach that computes a provably budget-optimal allocation of practice time, rather than relying on myopic or greedy strategies. This aims to enable robots to leverage predefined practice budgets effectively for maximizing long-horizon task performance.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an algorithm that enables robots to autonomously learn new skills for sequential tasks under a constrained practice budget, with the goal of maximizing overall task reward. The motivation stems from several key observations and challenges in current robotic systems:

- **Reliability Gap:** While large-scale robot learning offers general-purpose policies, these policies often prove unreliable in scenarios not extensively covered during initial pre-training. Autonomous practice is a viable strategy for improving robustness in deployment.
- **Sample Inefficiency of RL:** Contemporary reinforcement learning algorithms typically demand a large volume of data and practice episodes to achieve proficiency. This demand conflicts with real-world deployment constraints, where robots often have limited downtime or "practice budget" to learn new behaviors or refine existing ones.
- **Leveraging Known Budgets:** The authors identify a crucial insight: practice downtime is frequently known in advance. This information presents an opportunity for robots to adapt their learning strategies to the specific budget available. For instance, a larger budget might allow for the acquisition of more complex but higher-rewarding skills, whereas a smaller budget might necessitate focusing on easier, immediately beneficial skills.
- **Shortcomings of Greedy Approaches:** Previous active skill learning methods for sequential tasks often employ greedy strategies. These approaches typically prioritize practicing skills that offer the most immediate improvement to expected task performance in a single practice round. However, this one-step reasoning is inherently myopic. It can lead to suboptimal outcomes by overlooking task plans that require deliberate practice over multiple episodes and the coordinated mastery of several skills to achieve higher long-term rewards. An illustrative example in the paper demonstrates how a greedy approach might settle for a lower reward task plan (e.g., reward 1) by practicing a single easy skill, while a more optimal strategy with a larger budget would allocate practice time to two harder skills to achieve a significantly higher reward (e.g., reward 4).
- **Formalizing Budgeted Learning:** The research aims to formalize this problem as "budgeted skill learning." Within the standard Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) framework, robots are provided with high-level skill specifications, including preconditions, termination conditions, and effects. The objective is for the robot to learn parameterized control policies that translate these abstract skills into low-level actions through interaction with the environment.
- **Computational Challenge:** The problem of computing a budget-optimal allocation for skill practice is intrinsically challenging. It involves reasoning about a combinatorially vast number of possible skill plans over a potentially large practice budget, which translates into a complex bilevel optimization problem. The inner problem involves solving the resulting task MDP to determine expected planning performance after a given budget allocation, while the outer problem seeks to optimize this allocation.

Therefore, the key motivation is to overcome the limitations of myopic active learning by developing an algorithm, Deliberate Practice (DP), that can compute a *provably budget-optimal allocation* of practice time to maximize expected long-horizon task reward, specifically addressing the combinatorial and bilevel nature of the problem.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed algorithm, Deliberate Practice (DP), is designed to allocate a limited practice budget across multiple robot skills to maximize long-horizon planning performance. The approach consists of three sequential steps: Competence Prediction, Budget Allocation, and Skill Practice.

#### 3.1. Environment and Skill Modeling

The environment is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) with continuous state and action spaces. An abstract MDP ($M_h$) is derived, where abstract states ($S_h$) are projections of low-level states, and abstract actions ($A_h$) correspond to a library of skills ($U$). Each skill in $U$ is defined by:
- **Precondition (PRE):** States where the skill can be initiated.
- **Effect (EFF):** The outcome of successful skill execution.
- **Control Policy (π):** Computes low-level actions.
- **Termination Condition (β):** Specifies when the skill concludes.
- **Competence (p):** The probability that the skill successfully achieves its desired effect. If unsuccessful, it transitions to an absorbing failure state.

For contact-rich interactions, skills are structured as object-centric Composable Interaction Primitives (CIPs), comprising a pre-interaction motion plan, a parameterized interaction policy (learned through practice), and a post-interaction motion plan. The robot's overall goal is to maximize a task reward function ($R$) defined over goal states.

#### 3.2. Budgeted Skill Learning Problem Formulation

The problem is formalized as an optimization to determine a budget allocation $b_u$ for each skill $u \in U$ that maximizes the expected planning performance (cumulative task reward) under a total budget $B$. This is expressed as:

$\max_b \sum_{s \in S} e_s v_s$
subject to $\sum_{u \in U} b_u \leq B$
$v_s \in \text{SolveMDP}(M_h, b)$

Here, $e_s$ is the initial state distribution, and $v_s$ is the state value function obtained by solving the task MDP $M_h$ with skill competences influenced by the budget allocation $b$. This is a bilevel optimization problem, where the outer problem selects the budget allocation, and the inner problem solves the MDP given that allocation.

#### 3.3. Competence Prediction

DP models the improvement in skill competence as a function of allocated practice budget $b_u$. The function $f_{improv}(p_u, b_u)$ predicts the expected competence after $b_u$ rounds of practice. While the framework supports linear, piecewise linear, and exponential models, a piecewise linear model is empirically used: $f_{improv}(u, b) = \min(1, p_u + \Delta_u b)$, where $\Delta_u$ is the skill's rate of improvement. $\Delta_u$ is estimated online using a running average of observed competence improvements. More complex models, such as a saturating exponential $f_{improv}(u, b) = p_u + (1 - p_u)(1 - e^{-\alpha b})$, are also supported for highly non-linear dynamics.

#### 3.4. Budget Allocation: Exact Single-Level Reformulation

A core contribution of this work is addressing the computational challenge of solving the bilevel optimization problem. Traditional approaches often rely on greedy approximations, which can lead to suboptimal solutions. DP reformulates this problem into an exact single-level, structured bilinear program using duality theory and the linear programming (LP) formulation of MDPs.

The key steps in the reformulation are:
1. **Replacing Inner Optimization with LP:** The inner MDP solving problem is replaced by its LP formulation for computing optimal value functions.
2. **Converting to Max-Min:** Since both the leader (outer problem) and follower (inner problem) share the objective of maximizing total reward (or minimizing cost in the LP dual), the bilevel problem can be seen as a max-min problem.
3. **Applying LP Duality:** By strong duality, the inner minimization problem (primal LP) can be replaced by its dual maximization problem. This transforms the max-min problem into a single-level maximization problem where the budget allocation variables and the dual variables (state-action occupancy measures $\mu_{as}$) are jointly maximized.

The resulting bilinear program is:

$\max_{b, \mu} \sum_{\forall s \in S} \sum_{\forall a \in A} r_a^s \mu_a^s$
subject to
$\sum_{\forall a \in A} \mu_a^s - \gamma \sum_{s'} \sum_{\forall a} \bar{P}_{s's}^a(b) \mu_{s'a} = e_s, \forall s$
$\bar{P}_{s's}^a(b) = f_{improv}(P_{s's}^a, b)$
$\sum b_{ij} \leq B$
$\mu_a^s \leq \frac{1}{1-\gamma}, \forall s, a$

The terms $\bar{P}_{s's}^a(b) \mu_{s'a}$ are bilinear due to the dependence of the predicted transition function $\bar{P}$ on the budget $b$ and its product with the dual variables $\mu$. This non-smooth, non-convex problem can be solved to global optimality using spatial branch and bound methods, which handle bilinear constraints via convex relaxation techniques such as Piecewise McCormick Envelopes. The authors provide a theorem asserting that Deliberate Practice computes a globally optimal allocation of the practice budget.

#### 3.5. Skill Practice Curriculum

After computing the budget-optimal task plan and allocation, the robot constructs a curriculum. This involves using existing skills to reach the preconditions of skills slated for practice. The robot sequentially masters skills on the budget-optimal plan, using newly acquired skills to access and practice subsequent, downstream skills.

#### 3.6. Skill Implementation and Optimization

Learnable skills are implemented as Cartesian-space impedance controllers, parameterized by waypoints and impedance settings. These parameters are optimized using CMA-ES (Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy), a gradient-free optimizer suitable for non-differentiable black-box objectives evaluated through rollouts in the environment. For each skill, a specific reward function is defined to guide this optimization process.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of Deliberate Practice (DP) was evaluated across three long-horizon table-top manipulation tasks: two in simulation and one on a real robot. The results were compared against several active learning baselines.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Simulated Environments (MuJoCo, LIBERO):**
 - **Cleanup:** A Franka Panda robot places an object into one of three drawers (top, middle, bottom), offering rewards of 1, 2, and 4 respectively. The task involved 47 abstract states and 10 skills, with the longest plan having 4 skills. Skills like Pick, Place, OpenDrawer, CloseDrawer were specified.
 - **Cleanup-Multi:** A more complex version of Cleanup, requiring four objects to be placed into a drawer. This task featured 5000 abstract states and 22 skills, with the longest plan involving 10 skills.
- **Real-world Environment:**
 - **Breakfast:** A Franka Panda robot either toasts bread (reward 1, requires one skill: StartToaster) or microwaves oatmeal (reward 2, requires two skills: OpenMicrowave, CloseMicrowave). Other skills like Pick and Place were provided. These tasks involved forceful interactions with articulated objects, requiring learned policies.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Estimate, Extrapolate, Situate (EES):** Greedily practices the skill that yields the highest one-step expected task performance improvement.
 - **Competence Improvement (CI):** Practices the skill with the highest expected competence improvement.
 - **Least Competent First (LCF):** Practices the skill with the lowest estimated current competence.
 - **Random (R):** Randomly samples a reachable skill for practice.

#### 4.2. Key Results

- **Budget-Awareness of DP:**
 - In the Cleanup task, DP demonstrated adaptive behavior based on the allocated practice budget.
 - With a low budget (100 episodes), DP conservatively chose to learn skills for placing items in the top drawer, yielding a reward of 1. Under this budget, its performance was comparable to baselines.
 - With a medium budget (150 episodes), DP adapted to place items in the middle drawer, achieving a reward of 2.
 - With a high budget (250 episodes), DP utilized the additional time to learn skills necessary for placing items in the bottom drawer, maximizing the reward at 4.
 - In contrast, the baselines (EES, CI, LCF, R) consistently converged to the easiest and lowest-reward task plan, irrespective of the available practice budget, indicating a lack of budget-aware planning.

- **Superior Performance over Prior Active Learning Methods:**
 - Figure 5 illustrates that while DP and EES performed similarly under a low budget, DP significantly outperformed all baselines in medium (150 episodes) and high (250 episodes) budget scenarios.
 - The greedy active learning baselines (EES, CI) failed to identify and pursue higher-reward plans that necessitated practicing multiple skills over several episodes, demonstrating their susceptibility to local minima due to myopic reasoning.
 - In the more complex Cleanup-Multi task (requiring 10 sequenced skills), Random practice occasionally outperformed EES. This occurred because EES's myopic task improvement measure could be zero for all skills, leading to stagnation, whereas Random exploration continued. DP, with its look-ahead capability, did not encounter this limitation.

- **Scalability for Larger Problems:**
 - The Cleanup-Multi task, featuring 22 skills and 5000 abstract states, represented a larger problem instance. Despite its complexity, DP consistently computed the optimal budget allocation within a maximum solve time of 6 minutes.
 - For even larger problems where global optimality might not be proven within a given time limit, the optimization procedure can still yield a bounded-suboptimal allocation, complete with an optimality-gap certificate, demonstrating robustness in resource-constrained computational settings.

- **Real-World Validation:**
 - Experiments conducted on a real Franka Panda robot for the Breakfast task further validated DP's design.
 - With a smaller budget of 30 episodes, DP correctly opted for the "toast bread" plan, which required learning only one skill and yielded a reward of 1.
 - Under a larger budget of 60 episodes, DP successfully allocated practice time to learn the two skills (OpenMicrowave and CloseMicrowave) required for the "microwave oatmeal" plan, thereby achieving the higher task reward of 2.
 - These real-world results confirm DP's practical utility in enabling robots to make informed decisions about skill acquisition based on available practice time.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Deliberate Practice (DP), an active skill learning algorithm that provides a structured and provably optimal approach to managing practice budgets for robots learning sequential tasks. The work makes several contributions with significant potential impact:

#### 5.1. Theoretical Significance

- **Novel Formulation:** The paper presents the first known exact formulation of the budgeted robot skill learning problem as an optimization problem. This addresses a long-standing challenge in robot learning that traditionally relied on heuristic or greedy approximations.
- **Bilevel Optimization Reformulation:** A key theoretical contribution is the derivation of an exact single-level reformulation of a challenging bilevel optimization problem. By leveraging linear programming duality and properties of MDPs, the authors transform the problem into a structured bilinear program that can be solved to global optimality. This provides a rigorous mathematical framework for budget allocation in active learning.
- **Provably Optimal Allocation:** The algorithm is formally proven to compute a globally optimal allocation of the practice budget, a characteristic that distinguishes it from prior methods limited by local optima due to myopic reasoning.

#### 5.2. Practical Impact

- **Efficient Resource Utilization:** DP enables robots to intelligently and optimally utilize limited practice time, which is a critical constraint in real-world deployments. This leads to more efficient skill acquisition compared to methods that do not consider the global impact of practice decisions.
- **Enhanced Autonomy and Reliability:** By allowing robots to strategically master relevant skills based on available budget, DP contributes to improved reliability and autonomy in executing complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks in diverse environments such as homes and factories.
- **Overcoming Sample Inefficiency:** The approach directly addresses the sample inefficiency of many reinforcement learning algorithms by providing a mechanism to allocate a fixed number of practice episodes (the budget) in a maximally effective way.
- **Adaptive Learning Behavior:** DP allows robots to exhibit adaptive learning behavior, where the complexity and reward potential of learned tasks scale with the available practice budget. This adaptability is crucial for robots operating in dynamic environments with varying learning opportunities.
- **Scalability for Complex Tasks:** Demonstrated effectiveness in tasks involving a considerable number of abstract states and skills suggests that DP can be applied to increasingly complex robotic problems, with mechanisms for bounded-suboptimal solutions available for very large instances.
