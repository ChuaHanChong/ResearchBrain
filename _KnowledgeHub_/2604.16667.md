# Emergency Stopping for Liquid-manipulating Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a real-time emergency stopping system for robots manipulating open liquid containers, utilizing a Model Predictive Control framework to compute time-optimal, spill-free trajectories. The system achieved a 0.87-second stop with a maximum slosh of 5.3 degrees in physical robot experiments, effectively preventing spills within predefined limits while maintaining reactive control.

## Problem

- Existing liquid manipulation methods primarily focus on planned, slosh-free trajectories and lack real-time reactive capabilities for unexpected emergency stops.
- Uncoordinated emergency stops by liquid-carrying robots can cause severe spills, leading to potential safety hazards (e.g., injury from hot liquids, chemical contamination) or equipment damage.
- Current emergency stopping systems for robots typically consider empty manipulators or are limited to simpler 1D motions, inadequately addressing the complex 6D motions and 2 DoF liquid slosh dynamics of a multi-DoF robot.

## Method

- Formulates the emergency stopping problem as an optimal control problem, continuously solved within a ==Model Predictive Control== (MPC) framework to generate real-time, spill-free trajectories.
- Employs a computationally efficient, linearized spherical pendulum model to account for liquid slosh dynamics, specifically retaining bilinear coupling terms for improved accuracy under vertical accelerations.
- Integrates the linearized liquid model with robot kinematics into a Quadratic Program (QP) that minimizes stopping time (cumulative Cartesian velocity) while adhering to no-spill constraints on liquid tilt and robot kinematic limits.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved a stopping time of **0.87** seconds with a maximum measured slosh of **5.3** degrees in real-world robot experiments, successfully keeping the liquid within a specified 5-degree limit.
- Compared to a baseline without slosh modeling, which stopped in **0.73** seconds but caused a significant **22.3**-degree slosh, the system effectively mitigated spills while maintaining controlled deceleration.
- Simulation and real-world robustness tests to modeling errors (misspecified rod length) demonstrated a moderate sim-to-real gap, with real-world errors leading to higher slosh violations (e.g., **8.1** degrees vs. **5.5** degrees for **+50%** error).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining a carefully linearized spherical pendulum model with an MPC-based QP formulation allows for efficient, real-time optimization of emergency stopping trajectories that prevent liquid spillage.
> - The method serves as a 'plug-and-play' safety layer, enabling robust emergency reactions for liquid-manipulating robots without requiring extensive redesign of existing motion planning systems.
> - Prioritizing spill avoidance through appropriately weighted slack variables in the objective function is crucial for ensuring liquid containment during rapid deceleration, even at the cost of slightly longer stopping times.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The manipulation of open liquid containers by robots presents a significant challenge due to the complex, nonlinear dynamics of liquids, which are highly sensitive to container accelerations and jerks. The broader research landscape in this area has primarily focused on spill-free liquid manipulation and slosh suppression. Existing solutions often employ simplified mechanical models of liquid slosh (e.g., spherical pendulum, mass-spring models) or methods that minimize slosh-inducing forces, such as input shaping, trajectory optimization, or approaches utilizing concepts like virtual pendulums or quadrotors. More recent work has explored learned dynamics for path and trajectory generation. These methods are typically concerned with planning an optimal trajectory from an initial state to a goal state while avoiding spills.

Despite the extensive research on spill-free motion planning, a recognized gap exists concerning the robot's ability to react to unexpected, execution-time hazards that necessitate an abrupt stop for safety. Current methods for liquid manipulation do not account for the real-time adaptation and re-planning required in such emergency scenarios. An aggressive emergency stop, while preventing a collision, could cause significant liquid spillage, potentially leading to hazardous outcomes if the liquid is hot or chemically active.

The problem of emergency stopping has been studied in other domains, such as autonomous driving, but the environmental assumptions and system dynamics differ substantially. In robotic manipulation, emergency stops are typically considered for empty manipulators, where the strategy simplifies to full-force braking without considering payload dynamics. Conceptually, the closest analogue is emergency braking systems for overhead cranes, which also deal with payload swing, a phenomenon similar to liquid slosh. However, these systems are generally limited to 1D linear motion and one-directional swinging, making them less complex than the 6D motions of a multi-DoF robot manipulating a liquid with 2 DoF sloshing.

This work addresses this identified gap by introducing a real-time emergency trajectory planning method designed for liquid-carrying robots. It distinguishes itself from prior slosh-free planning methods by not relying on a pre-specified reference path or goal pose. Instead, it focuses on bringing the system to a safe and time-optimal stop from an arbitrary initial state. The proposed method falls into the category of trajectory optimization techniques that use slosh modeling, similar to existing approaches, but adapts this framework specifically for reactive emergency stopping.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the safety and operational challenges inherent in robotic manipulation of open liquid containers. While robots are increasingly deployed in scenarios requiring liquid handling, such as serving beverages or transferring industrial chemicals, the inherent instability of liquids poses a significant risk. Unexpected events, like human intrusion into a workspace or equipment malfunction, necessitate an immediate and decisive response from the robot. An abrupt, uncoordinated stop in such situations can lead to severe liquid spills, which could cause injury (e.g., from hot liquids) or contamination (e.g., from hazardous chemicals). Existing liquid manipulation techniques focus on planned, slosh-free movements and do not adequately address these real-time emergency scenarios. Therefore, there is a need for a robust emergency stopping system that can bring a liquid-carrying robot to rest quickly and safely.

The key objectives of this work are:
- **Formalize the emergency stopping problem:** To define the problem of bringing a robot manipulating an open liquid container to rest as quickly as possible without spilling the liquid, specifically in response to an unexpected hazard.
- **Develop a time-optimal, spill-free stopping method:** To design an approach that minimizes the stopping time while ensuring that the liquid remains within the container.
- **Incorporate nonlinear liquid dynamics in real-time:** To account for the complex and nonlinear behavior of liquids efficiently enough for real-time computation, without compromising predictive accuracy or limiting the robot's motion capabilities in 6D space.
- **Create a "plug-and-play" safety layer:** To develop a system that can be easily integrated as a safety feature on top of existing slosh-free motion planning methods, enabling immediate activation upon detecting a hazard.
- **Validate the method through simulation and hardware experiments:** To provide empirical evidence of the method's effectiveness in both simulated environments and on a physical robot with actual liquid.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed emergency stop system formulates the problem as an optimal control problem, which is solved repeatedly within a Model Predictive Control (MPC) framework. This allows for continuous optimization of the stopping trajectory in real-time.

**Problem Description and Overview:**
The system aims to bring a robotic manipulator arm, carrying a liquid container, to rest as quickly as possible without spilling the liquid, upon activation of an emergency stop. This is achieved by minimizing the cumulative sum of Cartesian velocities while adhering to no-spill constraints on the liquid-surface inclination. The method is designed to build upon existing slosh-free motion generation techniques, which ensure that the liquid surface is initially perpendicular to the container’s vertical axis, thus providing a known initial liquid state.

**Liquid Slosh Modeling:**
A simplified **spherical pendulum model** is employed to model liquid slosh, chosen for its computational efficiency and accuracy in real-time applications.
- **Dynamics:** The model's state is defined by two tilt angles (θ, ϕ) and the pendulum rod length (l). The full nonlinear Euler-Lagrange equations are provided for the pendulum's behavior under pivot point accelerations (`¨x, ¨y, ¨z`).
- **Linearization:** To enable real-time tractability, the full nonlinear equations are linearized. A distinction is made from previous work by retaining the bilinear coupling terms between vertical accelerations (`¨z`) and the pendulum angles (`θ, ϕ`). This avoids oversimplification and maintains the influence of vertical dynamics. The problematic bilinear terms are then linearized around a nominal trajectory (derived from the previous MPC iteration) at each time step within the optimization horizon.
- **Rod Length Estimation:** The pendulum rod length `l` is estimated based on the container's geometry and liquid height, using the natural frequency of the first sloshing mode for a cylindrical container (`f_n`), which is then related to `l` by `f_n = sqrt(g/l)`. This approach is extensible to non-cylindrical containers.

**Robot Manipulator Model:**
Standard kinematic relations are used: forward kinematics (`x = f(q)`) to map joint space to task space, the robot's Jacobian (`˙x = J(q)˙q`) for velocities, and its derivative (`¨x = ˙J(q)˙q + J(q)¨q`) for accelerations.

**State-Space Model:**
The system's state space `x` is defined by 13 variables: pendulum angles and angular velocities, end-effector's 6-DoF Cartesian velocities, and end-effector's 3-DoF Cartesian orientation. The control input `u` consists of 6 variables: container's Cartesian accelerations. A discrete-time, time-varying state equation `x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k` is used, where `A_k` and `B_k` are state transition and control matrices, incorporating the linearized liquid dynamics.

**Quadratic Program (QP) Formulation:**
The optimal control problem is formulated as a QP to minimize the cumulative sum of the end-effector's Cartesian velocities.
- **Objective Function:** `min_u Σ (c_1 ||v_k||^2 + c_2 ||j_k||^2 + c_3 δ_{θ,k} + c_4 δ_{ϕ,k})`. This minimizes velocity, with a small penalty for jerk (`j_k`), and higher penalties for non-negative slack variables (`δ_{θ,k}, δ_{ϕ,k}`) representing violations of the no-spill constraints. The cost coefficients `c_1, c_2, c_3, c_4` are weighted such that `c_1 < c_2 << c_3 = c_4`, prioritizing spill avoidance and then rapid stopping.
- **Constraints:**
 - State dynamics: `x_{k+1} = A_k x_k + B_k u_k`.
 - No-spill constraints: `θ_min ≤ θ_{p,k} - θ_{c,k} - δ_{θ,k} ≤ θ_max` and `ϕ_min ≤ ϕ_{p,k} - ϕ_{c,k} - δ_{ϕ,k} ≤ ϕ_max`. These ensure the liquid's tilt relative to the container remains within limits.
 - Robot kinematic limits: Bounds on Cartesian velocities (`v_k`) and accelerations (`u_k`).
 - Slack variable non-negativity: `δ_{θ,k}, δ_{ϕ,k} ≥ 0`.

**Resolved Acceleration Control (RAC):**
To translate the computed task-space control actions (`u`) into robot joint-space commands while respecting joint kinematic limits, RAC is used. This is formulated as a second, smaller-scale QP, which ensures fast solvability due to its convexity. The objective minimizes a quadratic form involving joint positions, velocities, accelerations, and slack variables, with high weights on slack variables to prioritize fulfilling task-space accelerations.

**Implementation Details:**
The system operates with two loops:
- **Optimization Loop:** Runs at 20 Hz, continuously solving the QP with a receding horizon of 2 seconds (40 time steps). It uses the previous iteration's solution as the nominal trajectory for linearization.
- **Real-time Control Loop:** Operates at a higher frequency (e.g., 1000 Hz for the Franka Emika Panda robot). It reads the first control action from the shared memory (updated by the optimization loop) and communicates it to the robot. This loop also reads the robot's current state and writes it to shared memory for the optimization loop.
Upon emergency stop activation, the system integrates the pendulum state using the full nonlinear dynamics (Eq. 1 and 2) for enhanced precision, based on the robot's realized Cartesian accelerations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated the proposed emergency stop method through both simulation and real-world experiments.

**A. Simulation Analysis**
1. **Stopping Time vs. Rod Length and Allowed Slosh:**
 - The stopping time was observed to be highest for small rod lengths and when the maximum allowed slosh angle was small.
 - For small allowed slosh angles, longer rod lengths facilitated faster stops. Conversely, as the allowed slosh increased, shorter rod lengths enabled faster stopping.
 - Spill constraint violations were generally small. The maximum average violation was 0.29 degrees, with a peak of 0.81 degrees. The average duration of constraint violation was 0.63 seconds. These small violations suggest that a practical approach involves slightly underestimating the allowed slosh to maintain safety margins.

2. **Robustness to Modeling Errors (Rod Length):**
 - Experiments were conducted by intentionally misspecifying the rod length (true value 50 mm, allowed slosh 5 degrees) by up to ±50%.
 - The maximum slosh violation increased consistently with the magnitude of the modeling error. A +50% error (i.e., specifying rod length as 75 mm) resulted in the highest violation of 2.03 degrees.
 - The model demonstrated relative robustness to parameter uncertainty, with the worst-case error leading to a 2-degree violation of the 5-degree limit. The direction of the modeling error (longer or shorter rod length) had a similar impact on the maximum slosh violation in simulation.

**B. Real-World Experiments**
Experiments were performed using a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda robot manipulating a cylindrical container of water (rod length 21 mm, maximum allowed slosh 5 degrees). The proposed method was compared against a baseline that minimized cumulative velocity and included the same jerk cost but lacked slosh modeling.

1. **Performance Comparison (Proposed Method vs. Baseline):**
 - **Proposed Method (exact model):** Achieved a stopping time of 0.87 seconds with a maximum slosh of 5.3 degrees. This indicates that the method maintained the liquid within the specified 5-degree slosh limit with a minimal overshoot of 0.3 degrees. The simulated results were closely aligned (0.86 s stopping time, 5.1 degrees max slosh).
 - **Baseline:** Resulted in a faster stopping time of 0.73 seconds but produced a significantly higher maximum slosh of 22.3 degrees, indicating substantial spilling.
 - **Velocity Profiles:** The baseline exhibited more aggressive deceleration. The proposed method's real-world velocity profiles were slightly less aggressive than simulated ones and did not show overshoots, a difference attributed to small velocity oscillations present in real-world robot motion, which the optimizer compensates for.

2. **Robustness to Modeling Errors (Real World):**
 - **Under-specified Rod Length (-50%):** When the rod length was intentionally set to be shorter (10.5 mm), the stopping time was 0.99 seconds, and the maximum measured slosh was 1.8 degrees. This suggests that under-specification led to overly smooth braking, almost entirely suppressing sloshing. This behavior, likely influenced by real-world velocity oscillations making the pendulum model more sensitive, differed from the simulation where similar errors in both directions led to comparable violations.
 - **Over-specified Rod Length (+50%):** When the rod length was intentionally set to be longer (31.5 mm), the stopping time was 0.86 seconds, and the maximum measured slosh was 8.1 degrees. This constituted a 62% violation of the 5-degree limit, indicating overly aggressive braking. While simulation with the same error showed a faster stop (0.70 s) and a smaller slosh violation (5.5 degrees), the real-world experiment demonstrated a moderate sim-to-real gap in robustness, with greater violations in reality.

**Summary of Experiments:**
The real-world experiments validated the proposed method's efficacy with actual robot hardware and liquid, demonstrating its ability to prevent spills within defined limits, unlike a jerk-penalized baseline without slosh modeling. While a moderate sim-to-real gap was observed in robustness tests, the measured slosh constraint violations were generally small enough to be acceptable in various applications.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work addresses a significant safety and functional gap in robotic manipulation by introducing a dedicated emergency stop system for robots handling open liquid containers. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

- **Enhanced Safety in Robotic Applications:** The primary impact is the prevention of hazardous liquid spills during unexpected events. This is critical in applications involving human-robot interaction (e.g., assistant robots serving beverages) and industrial settings where hazardous or hot chemicals are manipulated. By enabling time-optimal, spill-free stopping, the method reduces the risk of injury, contamination, and damage to equipment or infrastructure.
- **Real-Time Reactive Capabilities:** Unlike existing slosh-free motion planning methods that focus on pre-planned trajectories, this system provides real-time reactive capabilities. The MPC-based quadratic programming formulation ensures that the emergency stop trajectory can be computed and executed instantaneously upon hazard detection, which is crucial for safety-critical scenarios.
- **Plug-and-Play Integration:** The design allows the method to function as a "plug-and-play" safety layer over existing slosh-free motion planning techniques. This modularity simplifies its integration into current robotic systems and workflows without requiring extensive redesign of existing motion controllers.
- **Advancement in Liquid Manipulation Robotics:** The research formalizes the previously unaddressed problem of emergency stopping for liquid-manipulating robots and offers a practical, empirically validated solution. This contributes to the robustness and reliability of robots performing complex manipulation tasks with unactuated components like liquids.
- **Generalizability and Versatility:** The use of a spherical pendulum model, adaptable to various container shapes (cylindrical or rectangular cuboid) and sizes, enhances the method's generalizability. Furthermore, its ability to handle 6D robot motions and 2 DoF sloshing makes it more broadly applicable compared to simpler systems like 1D crane braking.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that provide avenues for future research:
- **Workspace Constraints and Directional Hazards:** The current method assumes the robot is free to move in any direction and that all directions are equally hazardous. Future work could extend this to constrained workspaces and incorporate directional hazard assessments, penalizing motions along certain axes more heavily.
- **Small-Angle Approximation:** The method implicitly assumes containers are filled close to the brim, keeping slosh within the valid range of the small-angle approximation of the pendulum model. Research into methods that maintain accuracy for larger slosh angles or lower fill levels would be beneficial.
- **Complex Container Geometries:** The spherical pendulum model is analytically fitted primarily to simple geometries. Addressing complex container shapes, common in real-world scenarios (e.g., coffee mugs), requires further research for parameter tuning and model applicability.
- **Sensing and Feedback:** Further exploration into the role of advanced sensing and feedback mechanisms could enhance the method's robustness and adaptability.

Overall, this work presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by providing a practical and real-time solution for safe emergency stopping when handling liquids, thereby improving the safety and applicability of robots in a wider range of scenarios.
