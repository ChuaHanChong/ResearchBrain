# A Physics-Consistent Benchmark for Contact-Rich Human-Robot Interaction in Assistive Care

> [!summary] Summary
> A physics-consistent simulation benchmark for contact-rich human-robot interaction in assistive care, instantiated as robot-assisted bathing. It couples a deformable, passively responding articulated human calibrated against manikin force-indentation measurements with physics-aware scores (peak force, joint-limit margin, motion activity, force stability) and a frozen vision-only/scorer-only protocol. Across 140 runs per method, an LLM-augmented state machine falls from 72.9% task success to 56.4% after force-safety screening.

## Problem

- Existing human-robot collaboration benchmarks drive the human kinematically or by motion capture, so contact-induced deformation, force transmission, and passive joint motion never emerge.
- Assistive-care systems and language-grounded manipulation pipelines score only whether the task finished, via success, trajectory, or collision measures, not how physical contact was executed.
- Simulation protocols often leak privileged state such as ground-truth joint angles or soft-anchor values, confounding policy capability with simulator access.

## Method

- ==Deformable care recipient== couples an articulated rigid skeleton to region-wise soft-tissue shells through ==anchor Jacobians== and ==spring-damper== reactions, so contact forces deform tissue and move passive joints.
- ==Real-to-sim calibration== replays matched Franka impedance push schedules on a medical-care manikin, fitting shell stiffness and damping to measured forearm and abdomen force-indentation curves.
- ==Frozen T1-T7 protocol== exposes only RGB-D, point clouds, segmentation masks, and vision-estimated keypoints to policies, while scorer-only streams compute C1 force, C2 joint margin, C4 motion activity, C5 force stability.

## Results

- The LLM-augmented state machine reached **72.9%** task success but only **56.4%** safety-gated success under the **0.35**-discounted pain-onset limits, dropping from **102/140** to **79/140** runs.
- VoxPoser kept the lightest, most stable contact (**1.46 N** median peak, **0.21 N** force SD) yet completed just **27.9%** of trials, with insufficient progress the dominant failure in **68/101** runs.
- Zero-shot transfer of a released flow-based VLA checkpoint scored **0.7%** task success, zero region-correct contacts, and **106/140** no-contact failures, with every logged contact off-target.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Task completion is not evidence of physically valid contact; a post-hoc force and region screen can separate nominal success from deployment-usable success.
> - Optimizing a safety proxy alone drives policies into a safe but ineffective regime, suggesting reaching and sustained-contact phases need distinct progress-driven objectives.
> - Reusing one body region's calibration for another, and calibrating against a manikin rather than tissue, limits claims to comparative benchmark scores, not biomechanical validation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Human–Robot Interaction (HRI) is expanding, with a growing focus on robots assisting humans in daily life, particularly in assistive care. A significant challenge in this domain is evaluating robot performance in contact-rich tasks, where physical interaction with the human body is inherent. Traditional evaluation metrics, primarily focused on task-level success (e.g., whether a specified action is completed), often overlook critical aspects of physical contact, such as force exertion, tissue deformation, passive joint motion, and contact stability.

Existing HRI benchmarks and robotic systems have made progress in various components but typically address them in isolation. For instance, early care systems like I-SUPPORT, Manip4Care, and VTTB demonstrate robots completing local care subtasks, but they do not provide reusable protocols for scoring physical contact under a frozen observation contract. Language grounding and foundation models, such as VoxPoser, ReKep, π0, π0.5, and OpenVLA, have advanced robotic manipulation by enabling language-conditioned control and open-set generalization. However, these models often achieve high scores on general manipulation benchmarks that do not account for sustained, safe contact with deformable human bodies.

Furthermore, current simulation environments for HRI frequently simplify human models, treating them as rigid or using motion-replayed kinematic targets rather than physically responsive, deformable entities. While advanced soft-body simulation methods exist (e.g., XPBD, VBD, SoftMAC), their integration into comprehensive evaluation benchmarks for contact-rich HRI is limited. Safety standards, such as ISO 15066, define collaborative force limits, but they typically offer fixed guidelines rather than reusable apparatus for scoring dynamic contact quality under a standardized observation protocol. Some works, like those by Behrens et al. and PrioriTouch, explore biomechanical pain-onset forces and contact preferences, providing foundational knowledge for safety criteria. However, a unified evaluation protocol that integrates a deformable, passively responding human model, calibrated contact response, leak-free policy observations, and physics-aware scoring remains largely absent.

This work addresses these limitations by introducing a physics-consistent benchmark that aims to bridge the gap between task-level evaluation and physically valid interaction. It integrates previously separate ingredients—deformable simulation, physical safety criteria, and robust policy evaluation—into a unified framework. The paper argues that successful task completion alone is insufficient for contact-rich assistive care and seeks to provide a diagnostic tool to assess the physical quality and safety of human-robot contact.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce a physics-consistent benchmark for evaluating robot policies in contact-rich human–robot interaction, specifically instantiated within assistive care tasks like robotic bathing. The motivation for this benchmark stems from several limitations observed in conventional robot policy evaluation, particularly in scenarios involving physical contact with humans.

Conventional task-level evaluations typically assess whether a robot policy successfully completes a predefined action. However, in contact-rich assistive tasks, this approach is insufficient because it can miss critical failures that emerge only during physical human contact. A robot might geometrically reach an instructed body region but exert excessive force, push a limb beyond its joint limits, or fail to maintain stable contact. Such scenarios, while technically "task complete" by some metrics, are physically invalid and potentially unsafe or uncomfortable for a human recipient.

To address these limitations, the authors identify three core challenges that motivate the benchmark's design:
1. **Modeling a physically responsive human:** Existing benchmarks often simplify the human as a rigid object or a motion-replay target, preventing realistic deformation, force transmission, and passive joint motion in response to robot contact. A key motivation is to incorporate a human model that dynamically reacts to physical interaction.
2. **Evaluating interaction quality beyond task success:** Current metrics frequently focus on success/failure rates, trajectory analysis, or collision detection. There is a need for metrics that quantify the *quality* of physical contact, such as peak force, contact stability, and proximity to physiological joint limits, which are crucial for safety and comfort in assistive care.
3. **Ensuring leak-free observation protocols:** Many simulation-based evaluation protocols expose "privileged" simulator states (e.g., ground-truth joint angles, soft-anchor states, future contact labels) to the tested policy. This can artificially inflate policy capabilities by providing information unavailable in real-world deployments, thereby compromising the sim-to-real observation contract. A motivated objective is to create an evaluation protocol that rigorously separates policy-visible observations from scorer-only physical states.

By addressing these challenges, the benchmark aims to enable diagnostic evaluations that reveal method-specific failure modes in force, joint-limit margin, contact stability, and region grounding. Ultimately, the motivation is to demonstrate that task completion alone does not imply physically valid contact and to provide a robust pre-deployment physical/safety screen for contact-rich assistive robot policies. The instantiation in robotic bathing serves as a concrete application to test these principles.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The benchmark is designed to ensure physics consistency at an evaluation level, defined by three criteria: (i) simulated human response measurable correspondence with real interaction, (ii) dynamic generation of contact-induced physical states, and (iii) evaluation of these states without exposing privileged simulator information to the policy.

#### 3.1. A. Benchmark Environment

The simulated environment includes a care recipient model and a structured observation interface.
- **Representation:** The human model comprises an articulated rigid skeleton combined with region-wise deformable soft-tissue shells. Load-bearing areas (forearm, upper arm, abdomen, torso/limb patches) feature flexible surface shells anchored to bone segments, while low-compliance areas (fingers, head) are treated as rigid. Crucially, passive joint behavior is always enabled, allowing contact forces to induce posture changes dynamically, rather than relying on an active human controller or pre-defined motions.
- **Physical Interaction:** The benchmark utilizes an existing physics simulation framework to implement physical interaction. The system models shell-bone load transfer through equations that describe how contact on a soft region deforms the shell, transmits force to the skeleton via regional anchors, and can move passive joints. Key equations are provided for skeleton dynamics, shell dynamics, and anchor reaction forces, illustrating the dynamic coupling between deformable tissue and the articulated skeleton. This design ensures policies encounter deformation, tissue-mediated force, and passive joint motion.
- **Observation Interface:** To prevent evaluation leakage, the platform strictly separates physics states from policy-visible perception. Rigid bones and flexible shells are used solely for mechanics. An SMPL-X visual skin is driven unidirectionally from the skeleton's state for rendering. Policies interact with the environment through a snapshot query interface that provides:
 1. RGB and depth images.
 2. 3D point clouds.
 3. Zero-shot semantic segmentation masks for human, robot, and scene entities.
 4. Skeletal landmarks derived from vision-based keypoint estimators.
 Ground-truth joint angles, soft-anchor states, and other scorer-only contact metadata are explicitly withheld from evaluated policies, ensuring a fair sim-to-real observation contract.

#### 3.2. B. Benchmark Tasks and Evaluation Dimensions

The benchmark focuses on continuous physical interaction with deformable body regions. It includes seven micro-tasks (T1-T7) related to robotic bathing: Touch, Scrub, and Push on the forearm, upper arm, and abdomen. Natural-language prompts define the region and intent (e.g., "gently touch the right forearm"). Tasks are designed to probe soft-tissue involvement and sustained contact.

Evaluation involves both a task-level baseline and physics-aware scores derived from scorer-only ground truth (Table III):
- **C1 Force:** Peak contact force ($F_{peak}$) during interaction, compared against a conservatively discounted biomechanical pain-onset limit ($F_{max}(b) = \gamma' F_{pain}(b)$, with $\gamma'=0.35$).
- **C2 Joint-limit margin:** Minimum margin to human kinematic joint limits, assessing the risk of joint overextension.
- **C3 Success:** A closed-loop task completion metric based on geometric and contact gates, with failure attribution. This serves as the conventional task-level baseline.
- **C4 Motion activity:** The active-motion fraction, indicating execution continuity and inversely relating to stop-go interruptions.
- **C5 Force stability:** Within-run standard deviation of the contact force, assessing the consistency of contact.

#### 3.3. C. Evaluation Protocol

A unified and "frozen" evaluation protocol ensures consistency across methods and tasks:
- **Assets/Embodiment:** Fixed nursing-bed scene, human, and robot models.
- **Randomization:** Comfort-arm orientation randomized within a fixed angular range, with a fixed seed set per task-method cell.
- **Trial Budget:** 20 initializations per task, totaling 140 runs per method across seven tasks.
- **Success/Safety Gates:** Task-specific geometric/contact gates for C3, and $\gamma'$-scaled safety force gates with human joint envelopes for physical safety.
- **Logger Schema:** All RGB-D, point clouds, masks, and robot proprioception are logged. Scorer-only force and joint streams are logged offline.
- **Replay:** Metrics can be regenerated from logged trajectories using the same scorer binary.
- **NA Rules:** Runs without valid contact or trajectory data are reported as NA for physics-aware scores and C3=0.

#### 3.4. D. Benchmark Setup and Calibration

- **Evaluated Methods:**
 - **LLM-augmented State Machine (State Machine):** An LLM-augmented controller consistent with finite-state coordination. It uses an initial RGB snapshot for CV segmentation, scripting routines to fit contact keypoints, and then an LLM selects motion primitives. It does not perform execution-time visual replanning.
 - **VoxPoser:** A method that generates zero-shot 3D value-map trajectories from LLMs/VLMs, utilizing the vision interface.
 - **Zero-shot π0.5 DROID checkpoint (π0.5):** A publicly released vision-language-action model, applied without fine-tuning, as a stress test for zero-shot transfer.
- **Physical Validity Calibration:** Real-world force-indentation measurements were performed using a Franka Panda robot on a medical-care manikin with compliant surfaces and movable joints. Robot pose, contact force, and displacement were recorded. These matched pose schedules, contact locations, and directions were replayed in simulation to compare force-indentation curves. The simulated responses were calibrated against these real-world measurements for the forearm and abdomen regions, providing evidence of the environment's physical validity. The upper-arm simulation reused forearm calibration parameters.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that conventional task-level evaluation can mask significant physical interaction failures, highlighting the utility of the physics-consistent benchmark.

#### 4.1. A. Physical Validity of the Benchmark Environment

The benchmark's physical validity was established by calibrating simulated region-wise responses against real-world force-indentation measurements on a medical-care manikin. For forearm and abdomen pushes, the calibrated simulation's force-indentation curves showed agreement with the raw measured trials and an identified response. This correspondence supports that the simulated human model exhibits measurable physical behavior under contact, consistent with the benchmark's definition of physics consistency.

#### 4.2. B. Overall Method Performance and Gate Sensitivity

Table V summarizes the binary results across 140 runs per method:
- **State Machine:** Achieved the highest task-level success at 72.9% (102/140 runs). However, when subjected to correct-region and force-safety screening ($\gamma' = 0.35$), its success rate dropped to 56.4% (79/140 runs). This indicates that a substantial portion of task-level successes involved physically invalid contact, primarily due to force exceedances.
- **VoxPoser:** Demonstrated a task-level success of 27.9% (39/140 runs). Importantly, its safety-gated success rate remained at 27.9%. This suggests that when VoxPoser successfully completes a task, it tends to do so with physically safer contact, without exceeding force limits.
- **π0.5:** Showed a low task-level success of 0.7% (1/140 runs). Its correct-region and safety-gated success rates were 0.0%, indicating that its rare successful contacts were either off-target or violated safety criteria. This reflects a substantial sensitivity to zero-shot transfer for contact-rich assistive tasks.

Gate sensitivity analysis revealed that while State Machine's gated success increased with a more permissive $\gamma'$ (e.g., from 35.0% at $\gamma'=0.20$ to 72.1% at $\gamma'=1.00$), it consistently remained below its nominal success. VoxPoser's and π0.5's gated success rates were unaffected by changes in $\gamma'$, underscoring their distinct failure modes.

#### 4.3. C. Physics-Aware Scores

Table VI presents the pooled physics-aware scores:
- **State Machine:** Exhibited a median peak contact force (C1) of 19.48 N, which is relatively high and indicative of force exceedances. Its median within-run force standard deviation (C5) was 3.52 N, suggesting less stable contact.
- **VoxPoser:** Produced the lightest and most stable conditional contact, with a median peak force (C1) of 1.46 N and a median force standard deviation (C5) of 0.21 N. It also showed high motion activity (C4) at 0.883, indicating continuous execution.
- **π0.5:** For its limited off-target contacts, it showed a median peak force (C1) of 15.39 N and a median force standard deviation (C5) of 4.42 N.

These results show that high task completion (State Machine) does not guarantee desirable physical contact properties, while methods producing safe contact (VoxPoser) may struggle with task completion.

#### 4.4. D. Diagnostic Evaluation and Failure Modes

Detailed analysis revealed specific failure modes:
- **State Machine:** Its primary failure mode, despite high task success, was exceeding force limits. 36 out of 140 trials involved force exceedances, with a subset of these being originally successful and region-correct. This highlights that its structured approach, while effective for task completion, still carries a "force tail" that can compromise safety.
- **VoxPoser:** While producing light and stable contact, its main diagnostic finding was a systematic gap between reaching a target region and sustaining contact to complete tasks like Scrub and Push. The dominant failure mode was "insufficient progress" (68/101 failed runs), rather than localization errors or unsafe contact. High motion activity (C4) did not translate into task progress, suggesting that its safety-oriented objective, while maintaining safe contact, was often too weak or poorly directed for effective task completion.
- **π0.5:** Its near-zero success rate stemmed almost entirely from "no target contact" (106/140 runs) or "off-target contact" (30/140 runs). This indicates fundamental challenges in transferring its pretraining distribution to the contact-rich assistive care setting, rather than issues with contact quality itself.

Region-wise contact outcomes (Table VIII) further showed that for the forearm, both State Machine and VoxPoser established target-region contact on 95% of runs, yet VoxPoser's task success was significantly lower (33.3%). This underscores that establishing contact is not equivalent to task completion.

#### 4.5. E. What Conventional Evaluation Misses

The findings demonstrate that conventional task-level evaluation (C3) alone is insufficient for contact-rich HRI. The benchmark explicitly surfaces dissociations:
- State Machine: Can achieve task completion but with an unstable force tail and frequent exceedances of safety limits.
- VoxPoser: Can maintain light and stable contact but often fails to complete sustained tasks, getting stuck in a "safe but ineffective" regime.
- π0.5: Can move continuously but consistently misses the target region, rendering it ineffective.

These results collectively indicate that task completion does not imply physically valid contact, necessitating physics-aware screening.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a physics-consistent benchmark for contact-rich Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) in assistive care, offering a substantial contribution to the field. Its primary significance lies in addressing a critical gap in current robot policy evaluation methodologies. By demonstrating that conventional task-level success metrics often conceal fundamental physical interaction failures, the benchmark provides a more rigorous and diagnostic framework for assessing robot capabilities in sensitive, contact-rich scenarios.

The potential impacts are multi-faceted:
- **Enhanced Safety and Reliability:** The benchmark allows for a systematic pre-deployment physical and safety screening of robot policies. By identifying policies that achieve task success but violate safety thresholds (e.g., excessive force or joint-limit infringements), it facilitates the development of safer and more reliable assistive robots. This is particularly crucial in applications like elder care, where physical contact with vulnerable individuals demands high standards of safety and comfort.
- **Improved Policy Development:** The diagnostic capabilities of the benchmark, which measure not only task success but also peak force, contact stability, joint-limit margins, and motion activity, provide detailed feedback to developers. This can guide the design of more sophisticated robot control strategies that effectively balance task completion with physical validity and interaction quality. For instance, the findings suggest that different control regimes might be necessary for reaching and sustained-contact phases in value-map-based methods to ensure progress while maintaining safety.
- **Advancement of Simulation Fidelity:** By calibrating simulated human responses against real-world force-indentation measurements, the benchmark contributes to the development of more physically realistic human models in simulation environments. This enhanced fidelity is essential for developing and testing robots in conditions that closely mimic real-world interactions.
- **Standardization of Evaluation:** The introduction of a "frozen vision-only / scorer-only" evaluation protocol establishes a robust and leak-free observation contract. This standardization is vital for fair comparisons between different robot policies and for fostering reproducible research in contact-rich HRI, promoting transparent assessment of policy capabilities without reliance on privileged simulator information.
- **Broader Applicability:** While instantiated in robotic bathing, the principles of physics-consistent evaluation, deformable human modeling, and physics-aware scoring have broader implications for other contact-rich HRI applications, such as physical therapy, patient repositioning, or collaborative assembly tasks where human-robot physical contact is integral.
- **Open-Sourcing for Community Engagement:** The public availability of the benchmark code and assets encourages wider adoption and facilitates comparative studies across research groups, accelerating progress in the development of physically intelligent and safe assistive robots.

The study acknowledges limitations, such as the use of a medical-care manikin instead of human tissue for calibration, the reuse of calibration parameters for some body regions, and a limited set of evaluated methods. However, within its scope of benchmark characterization, it effectively highlights the critical distinction between nominal task success and physically valid, deployment-usable success, thereby motivating a paradigm shift in the evaluation of contact-rich human-robot interaction.
