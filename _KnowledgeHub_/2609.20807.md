# Score Centering Stabilizes Off-policy Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Score centering is an additive correction for LLM reinforcement learning under training-inference mismatch. It subtracts the sampler's expected score from each token score, cancelling the drift term that distills the trainer toward its own biased sampler, with no importance ratios, clipping, or hyperparameters. It matches or beats importance-sampling corrections from 0.6B to 30B models, reaching 30% training accuracy versus 12% for truncated importance sampling under INT8/INT4 sampler quantization.

## Problem

- RL for LLMs can collapse under small training-inference mismatch from kernel nondeterminism, precision differences, quantized rollouts, or asynchronous staleness, yet eliminating the mismatch sacrifices rollout efficiency and GPU utilization.
- Existing corrections (TIS, MIS, PPO, DAPO, GSPO, DPPO) all rely on importance ratios, which inflate gradient variance on rare tokens and must be clipped or masked, reintroducing bias.
- Prior work observed that mismatch bias compounds over training but did not isolate why online RL is fragile while offline distillation from a different model stays stable.

## Method

- **Drift-signal decomposition**: via the ==covariance identity==, the off-policy gradient splits into a ==drift term== (mean reward times the sampler's expected score) that distills trainer toward sampler, plus a reward-score covariance signal term.
- **Score centering**: subtract the sampler-expected score from each token score, a deterministic additive, hyperparameter-free correction that cancels drift exactly at every prefix and composes with ==TIS== or ==MIS== importance weights.
- **Top-k approximation**: log only the sampler's top-128 logprobs, fill the tail with trainer probabilities rescaled to the sampler's tail mass, and implement the correction as a scalar ==stop-gradient loss==.

## Results

- Qwen3-30B-A3B on INTELLECT-2 math: with FP4 KV cache score centering holds **52%** while policy gradient collapses; with INT8/INT4 sampling it reaches **30%** versus **12%** for TIS, others below **5%**.
- On Qwen3-0.6B Countdown with Gaussian sampler weight noise at **σ=0.05**, only score centering, alone or composed with TIS/MIS, trains stably; DPPO collapses near step **160**, **80**, **20** as noise grows.
- With the sampler synced only every **64 steps**, score centering plus TIS or MIS beats score centering alone; PPO and DAPO survive staleness but collapse under quantization; **top-32** logprobs match full centering.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - What destabilizes online RL is not mismatch size but a teacher that tracks the student: distilling toward a biased, periodically resynced copy of yourself compounds error instead of converging.
> - Group-centered advantages do not remove bias off-policy: drift lives at individual prefixes, whose expected advantage is nonzero exactly where learning signal exists, so group-level centering only shrinks it.
> - When an expectation is exactly computable, as a next-token sum is for LLMs, an additive deterministic correction can replace high-variance multiplicative importance weights, and the two compose rather than compete.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning for large language models increasingly relies on policy-gradient methods, including PPO, GRPO, DAPO, and related algorithms. These methods generate model responses with an inference or sampling engine, assign rewards to the responses, and then update the model using gradients computed by a training engine. Although the sampler and trainer are intended to represent the same policy, they commonly differ numerically or operationally.

This discrepancy is referred to in the paper as the **training-inference mismatch (TIM)**. It can result from different numerical precisions, quantized weights or activations, distinct GPU kernels, non-associative floating-point operations, different input shapes, independent software implementations, or stale model parameters in asynchronous training systems. The problem is particularly relevant to large-scale RL systems, where training and inference are often separated to improve hardware utilization.

The standard response to TIM has been to use **importance sampling (IS)**. Importance sampling reweights training gradients according to the probability ratio between the trainer and sampler. In principle, this provides an exact correction for off-policy data. In practice, the ratios can have high variance, especially for rare tokens, so practical methods clip or mask them. These modifications reduce variance but introduce bias and can fail to remove the systematic error associated with TIM.

The paper contributes to this area by proposing a different analysis. It argues that the central failure mechanism is not merely variance from off-policy sampling, but a systematic **drift term** in the policy-gradient update. This drift behaves like distillation toward the sampler. Because the sampler is usually a quantized, stale, or otherwise biased version of the trainer and is periodically synchronized with it, the bias can accumulate through a feedback loop.

The proposed method, **score centering**, corrects this drift through an additive subtraction of the sampler-expected policy score. It therefore differs structurally from importance sampling, which uses multiplicative weights. The authors also show that the two approaches can be combined.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper has three principal objectives.

First, it seeks to explain why RL training of language models is more sensitive to small implementation differences than supervised fine-tuning. In supervised fine-tuning, training on data produced by another model can remain stable because the data source is effectively fixed. In online RL, by contrast, the sampler is repeatedly updated from the trainer. The authors hypothesize that this moving-teacher relationship allows small systematic mismatches to compound.

Second, the work aims to identify the specific component of the policy-gradient update that causes instability. The authors distinguish between useful reward-dependent learning signal and an unwanted mismatch-induced contribution. At a particular prefix, the sampler generates the next token according to distribution \(q\), while the trainer computes a score using distribution \(p\). If these distributions differ, the expected trainer score under the sampler is not necessarily zero. The paper interprets this nonzero expectation as drift.

Third, the paper introduces a practical correction that can be used without storing the sampler’s full vocabulary distribution and that can operate alongside existing importance-sampling methods. This is motivated by the high memory and computational cost of exact distributional corrections for large-vocabulary language models.

The authors also investigate the role of reward signs and online versus offline training. Their toy experiments compare positive-only rewards, positive/negative rewards, and group-centered rewards. These experiments are intended to separate two forms of instability: offline training with negative rewards and online training under a moving, mismatched sampler.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Analytical Decomposition

The analysis considers one token at a fixed prefix. Let \(p_v\) denote the trainer’s probability for vocabulary token \(v\), and \(q_v\) the sampler’s probability. The trainer score for token \(v\) is

\[
s_v = \nabla_\theta \log p_v.
\]

Under exact on-policy training, the expected score is zero because

\[
\sum_v p_v \nabla_\theta \log p_v
= \nabla_\theta \sum_v p_v
= 0.
\]

Under TIM, however, the token is sampled from \(q\), so the relevant expectation is

\[
\bar{s} = \sum_v q_v s_v,
\]

which may not be zero. Using a covariance decomposition, the expected update is written as

\[
\mathbb{E}_q[R s]
=
\mathbb{E}_q[R]\bar{s}
+
\operatorname{Cov}_q(R,s).
\]

The first term is identified as **drift**. It depends on the mean reward at the prefix rather than on which sampled tokens actually lead to success. The second term is the useful reward-dependent **signal**.

The drift term is interpreted as a cross-entropy or distillation gradient that pulls the trainer toward the sampler. If the sampler is stale or quantized, this process repeatedly transfers its bias into the trainer. After synchronization, the altered trainer becomes the basis for a new, biased sampler, potentially creating a positive feedback loop.

#### 3.2. Score-Centering Correction

Score centering replaces the token score with

\[
\tilde{s}_v = s_v - \bar{s}.
\]

Because \(\bar{s}\) is the expected score under the same distribution that generated the token,

\[
\mathbb{E}_q[\tilde{s}] = 0.
\]

Consequently, the drift term is canceled at each prefix, leaving only the reward-score covariance under the sampler distribution. The correction is additive and deterministic for a given prefix; it does not depend on which token was sampled.

The method is inactive when the trainer and sampler match, since then \(\bar{s}=0\). The authors distinguish score centering from reward baselines and conventional control variates. Those methods generally reduce variance while preserving the expected on-policy gradient. Score centering is presented primarily as a bias correction under off-policy mismatch.

#### 3.3. Practical Top-\(k\) Approximation

Computing the sampler’s full next-token distribution is impractical for large vocabulary models. The authors therefore log only the sampler’s top-\(k\) log probabilities, using \(k=128\) in most experiments. The remaining probability mass is modeled using the trainer’s distribution, rescaled so that the tail mass matches the sampler’s tail mass.

This reconstruction allows the expected score to be calculated from the top-\(k\) tokens alone. The method is implemented as a scalar loss whose gradient produces the centered score. The sampler probabilities are treated with stop-gradient operations so that gradients do not flow through the sampling distribution.

The paper also derives a generalized form for composing score centering with importance sampling. If an IS method applies weight \(w_v\) to a token score, score centering subtracts the expected weighted score,

\[
\mathbb{E}_q[w_v s_v].
\]

This preserves the drift-cancellation property of score centering while retaining the distributional adjustment of the selected IS rule.

#### 3.4. Experimental Design

The experiments use a common base objective: REINFORCE with group-centered rewards, one stochastic-gradient-descent update per batch, and a fixed learning rate of \(10^{-2}\). Each prompt generates eight completions. The authors use SGD rather than AdamW, partly because it avoids optimizer-state memory costs.

The main model and task combinations are:

- Qwen3-0.6B-Instruct on Countdown.
- Qwen3-1.7B on Countdown for the reward-mode analysis.
- Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base on the mathematical subset of INTELLECT-2.

Score centering is compared with naive policy gradient, naive importance sampling, truncated importance sampling, masked importance sampling, PPO, DAPO, GSPO, TOPR, and DPPO. To isolate the correction mechanisms, the authors omit broader algorithm-specific features such as dynamic sampling and certain separate policy-version objectives.

TIM is induced through four settings:

1. **Synthetic fixed weight noise**, added to the sampler parameters.
2. **Sampler quantization**, including reduced precision for weights, activations, and KV caches.
3. **Severe staleness**, where the inference engine is updated only every 64 training steps.
4. **Large-scale mixture-of-experts training**, where sampler and trainer may also disagree in expert routing.

The study uses multiple seeds for most small-model experiments, while the 30B experiments use fewer seeds because of their computational cost. The authors report approximately 6,180 H100 GPU-hours across the experiments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Drift Explains Online Instability

The reward-mode experiments show different stability patterns for offline and online training. Offline training is stable with positive-only \(+1/0\) rewards but becomes unstable when negative rewards are included. The authors relate this offline behavior to the unbounded effect of negative log-probability gradients.

Online training under TIM exhibits the opposite pattern. Positive-only rewards are the least stable, while group-centered rewards containing both positive and negative values are more stable. The paper interprets this result as evidence that online instability is driven by the moving sampler and the mismatch-induced drift rather than simply by the presence of negative rewards.

Group centering reduces the average magnitude of drift but does not eliminate it, because the expected advantage can remain nonzero at individual prefixes even when advantages sum to zero across a group.

#### 4.2. Synthetic Weight Noise

With artificial sampler weight perturbations, score centering, truncated importance sampling, and masked importance sampling generally perform best at lower mismatch levels. Their compositions with score centering also remain competitive.

As the noise increases, the distinction becomes clearer. Under the most severe perturbation, score centering alone and its combinations with truncated or masked importance sampling continue training stably, while several other methods collapse earlier. The collapse point shifts earlier as the mismatch becomes larger, supporting the paper’s claim that drift accumulates over training rather than necessarily causing immediate failure.

#### 4.3. Quantization and Staleness

Under severe sampler quantization, score centering performs well both alone and when combined with importance sampling. Several clipping-based methods, including PPO and DAPO, are less reliable in this setting. The authors argue that PPO-style clipping is designed primarily to constrain policy movement, whereas quantization produces numerical discrepancies that may not resemble ordinary policy-version ratios.

Under severe staleness, the combined methods perform better than score centering alone. The explanation is that score centering cancels drift but leaves the covariance evaluated under the sampler distribution rather than the trainer distribution. Importance sampling partially corrects this residual distributional difference.

The paper therefore recommends combining score centering with an importance-sampling method when the trainer may move substantially away from the sampler between synchronization events.

#### 4.4. Scaling to a 30B Mixture-of-Experts Model

The largest experiments use Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base with progressively more severe sampler quantization.

With an FP8 sampler, uncorrected policy gradient remains stable and reaches approximately 58% training accuracy. When the KV cache is reduced to FP4, uncorrected policy gradient collapses within roughly 200 steps, while score centering reaches about 52% and truncated importance sampling about 51%.

Under the most severe configuration, INT8 weights and activations with an INT4 KV cache, score centering reaches approximately 30% training accuracy, truncated importance sampling reaches about 12%, and the other compared methods remain below 5% by the end of the run. These results indicate that the additive correction is particularly useful when numerical mismatch is severe.

#### 4.5. Top-\(k\) Approximation

The top-\(k\) ablation finds that \(k=128\), and often \(k=32\), performs similarly to full-vocabulary score centering across the tested settings. The top-128 tokens cover more than 99.9% of sampler probability mass on average in most settings. Even in the most heavily quantized 30B condition, where tail distortion is larger, the approximation remains close to the full-distribution version.

The reported runtime overhead for \(k=128\) is below approximately 1% relative to baseline runs on matched hardware, although the paper notes that this measurement used a custom JAX sampler and does not establish the overhead for all inference frameworks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is conceptual and practical. Conceptually, it reframes RL instability under TIM as a systematic bias problem, not solely as an importance-weight variance problem. The drift decomposition provides an explanation for why online RL can fail under small numerical discrepancies even though offline distillation from a different fixed model is often stable.

Practically, score centering may allow RL systems to retain efficient, disaggregated, asynchronous, or low-precision inference pipelines without requiring exact numerical agreement between sampler and trainer. It requires no tunable clipping threshold, does not rely on multiplicative ratios, and can be added to existing policy-gradient objectives. Its compatibility with importance sampling is relevant because the two corrections address different residual errors.

Several limitations constrain the interpretation. The headline experiments deliberately use severe mismatch and relatively short sequences to produce observable differences within manageable compute budgets. These conditions serve as a proxy for milder mismatch accumulated over longer training, but the equivalence is not directly demonstrated. The 30B comparisons also contain fewer seeds for some methods, and the shared-objective design does not reproduce the full training recipes of every baseline. Finally, score centering removes drift but does not fully correct the difference between sampling and training distributions; this is why it benefits from importance sampling under severe staleness.

Overall, the work proposes a mathematically motivated correction with relatively low implementation cost and presents evidence across synthetic noise, quantization, staleness, and model scale. Its broader impact will depend on validation in longer-context, longer-duration RL training and in production systems with less artificially severe mismatch.
