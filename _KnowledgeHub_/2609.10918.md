# ObstaDiff: Generalizable Diffusion Policy Learning via Obstacle-aware Representations

> [!summary] Summary
> ObstaDiff is a decomposed diffusion policy for cluttered greenhouse manipulation. A lightweight encoder converts eye-in-hand RGB-D into a four-channel target-obstacle-background representation, with separate semantic and depth branches plus spatial softmax feeding a conditional diffusion U-Net. It aligns to a bottleneck pose, then replays close-range interaction, reaching 75.41% success and 8.20% collision rate over 61 real-robot trials.

## Problem

- Imitation learning assumes clean tabletop backgrounds, lacking explicit mechanisms for obstacle-aware motion when unstructured foliage forms narrow irregular passages around targets.
- Standard end-to-end visual encoders entangle target, obstacle, and background cues in one latent, denying the policy any explicit signal on what to reach versus avoid.
- Prior obstacle-aware motion methods are open-loop planners, not closed-loop visuomotor policies, while engineered agricultural pipelines need manual calibration and break under clutter and illumination shift.

## Method

- ==Target-obstacle-background observation==: a fine-tuned ==YOLO-World== open-vocabulary detector plus ==MobileSAM== produces three binary semantic masks, concatenated with normalized aligned depth into a four-channel 240x320 input.
- **StructuredObsEncoder** splits semantic and depth channels into two small CNN branches, each ending in ==spatial softmax== keypoints, concatenated with end-effector pose into a 103-D conditioning vector.
- Decomposed execution: a ==conditional diffusion policy== handles alignment, then a ==gated nearest-neighbor rule== over a 26-trajectory replay library, using image-plane and one-sided depth error, triggers replay-based interaction.

## Results

- ObstaDiff reaches **75.41%** mean success versus **49.18%** for Diffusion Policy on RGB and **45.90%** for ACT, with mean collision rate **8.20%** versus **26.23%** and **14.75%**.
- Adding depth alone barely helps: RGB-D Diffusion Policy gains one trial (**50.82%**) with collision counts identical to RGB in every setting (**7/20, 7/21, 2/20**).
- Feeding the same ResNet encoder the identical four-channel input degrades to **45.90%** success and **27.87%** collisions, isolating a **29.5-point** gap attributable to the structured encoder.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured semantic input alone confers no obstacle awareness; without an encoder matched to its channel structure it can perform worse than raw RGB.
> - Lower supervised validation loss does not predict task success: the structured input stabilized training for ACT yet still dropped its real-robot success rate.
> - Splitting learned alignment from replayed close-range interaction sidesteps unreliable eye-in-hand depth near contact and swaps task objectives without retraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

This work addresses a problem at the intersection of robotic imitation learning, diffusion-based visuomotor policies, semantic scene representation, and obstacle-aware motion generation. Imitation learning has become a widely used approach for teaching robots manipulation behaviors from expert demonstrations. Methods such as Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT) and Diffusion Policy have shown that policies can learn complex trajectories directly from visual observations without requiring a manually designed motion planner for every task.

However, many existing imitation-learning systems are evaluated in relatively controlled environments, such as clean tabletop scenes or settings with limited visual clutter. In these conditions, an image encoder can often learn an implicit relationship between the object to be manipulated and the appropriate robot motion. In agricultural, household, or industrial environments, this assumption is weaker. Targets may be surrounded by stems, leaves, wires, tools, or other objects that create narrow and irregular passages. The policy must simultaneously identify what to approach and what to avoid.

Recent research has attempted to improve generalization through visual pretraining, object-centric representations, three-dimensional point-cloud inputs, equivariant architectures, and diffusion-based trajectory generation. Other work on cluttered agricultural manipulation has used explicit perception, shape completion, view planning, or motion-planning systems. ObstaDiff occupies a middle position between these approaches. It retains the data-driven, end-to-end alignment behavior of imitation learning while introducing an explicit semantic decomposition of the visual scene into target, obstacle, and background regions.

The paper’s central claim is that the representation used to condition a policy is as important as the policy-generation mechanism itself. In particular, simply adding depth or semantic masks to a conventional image encoder does not necessarily produce obstacle-aware behavior. The authors therefore combine a structured visual input with an encoder designed to preserve the different semantic roles of its channels.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to improve the generalization of visuomotor imitation policies in cluttered real-world environments. The authors focus on greenhouse manipulation, where a robot must approach a pepper target while avoiding surrounding plant structures. This setting introduces several forms of distribution shift:

1. **Target-pose variation.** The target can appear at different positions and orientations within the reachable target region.
2. **Obstacle-layout variation.** Obstacles may consist of known plant types arranged in spatial configurations not observed during demonstrations.
3. **Target-appearance variation.** The target may have an appearance that was absent from training, such as a different pepper variety or color.

The motivation is that conventional visual encoders tend to entangle target appearance, obstacle geometry, and background cues into a single latent representation. Under visual or spatial distribution shifts, the learned policy may move approximately toward the target without accounting for the safest approach direction. This can lead to collisions, particularly when the target is located near foliage or stems.

ObstaDiff is designed around two related goals. First, it should provide the policy with an explicit description of which pixels correspond to the target, obstacles, and irrelevant background. Second, it should separate the relatively general obstacle-aware approach behavior from the task-specific close-range interaction behavior. The authors argue that a single policy may not need to learn both components jointly: approaching a target through clutter can be shared across tasks, whereas touching, harvesting, or inspecting the target may require different contact trajectories.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Task Decomposition

ObstaDiff divides manipulation into two stages:

- **Alignment stage:** A diffusion policy generates end-effector trajectories that move the robot from a home position toward a target-centered bottleneck pose.
- **Interaction stage:** Once the robot reaches a suitable visual configuration, it replays a previously demonstrated close-range trajectory.

The bottleneck is not defined by a fixed Cartesian pose. Instead, the system compares the current target state with the initial target states associated with 26 stored interaction trajectories. The target state contains its image-plane center and an estimated depth. A replay trajectory becomes eligible when the target depth is within a specified five-millimeter gate. The system then selects the eligible replay whose target center is closest in image coordinates. Switching occurs when the normalized image-plane error is below 0.2.

This design allows the learned policy to focus on obstacle-aware approach motions and avoids relying heavily on eye-in-hand depth during close-range interaction, where depth measurements may become unreliable because of occlusion, proximity, or missing values.

#### 3.2. Target–Obstacle–Background Representation

At each time step, an eye-in-hand Intel RealSense D455 camera provides aligned RGB and depth images. A fine-tuned YOLOv8s-Worldv2 open-vocabulary detector identifies the target and obstacle regions. The detector was fine-tuned on approximately 80 manually annotated greenhouse images. Detected obstacle regions are refined into pixel-level masks using MobileSAM.

The resulting scene is converted into three binary masks:

- A **background mask** for pixels assigned to neither the target nor an obstacle.
- An **obstacle mask** for surrounding plant structures.
- A **target mask** for the pepper fruit being approached.

These masks are concatenated with a normalized depth image to produce a four-channel observation called **TOB**, with channels for background, obstacle, target, and depth. The input is resized to 240 × 320 pixels. This representation discards much of the target and obstacle appearance information while preserving their spatial layout and relative geometry.

#### 3.3. Structured Observation Encoder

The TOB input is not processed by the standard ResNet-18 image encoder used in the baseline Diffusion Policy. Instead, ObstaDiff uses a lightweight encoder with separate branches for semantic masks and depth.

The semantic branch receives the three masks and produces a 64-dimensional feature through four convolutional blocks followed by spatial softmax. The depth branch processes the single depth channel and produces a 32-dimensional feature using a corresponding network. Spatial softmax converts feature maps into expected two-dimensional coordinates, encouraging the network to represent compact geometric relationships rather than dense appearance features.

The two visual features are concatenated with the seven-dimensional end-effector pose, consisting of three-dimensional position and a unit quaternion. Each observation therefore produces a 103-dimensional conditioning vector. Two consecutive observations are supplied to the diffusion policy, resulting in a 206-dimensional temporal conditioning input.

#### 3.4. Diffusion-Based Alignment Policy

The alignment policy predicts a sequence of 16 seven-dimensional end-effector poses. During training, Gaussian noise is progressively added to expert action sequences. A neural network learns to predict the noise required to denoise the sequence, conditioned on the encoded observations and the current diffusion step.

During deployment, the policy iteratively denoises a sampled action sequence using 100 diffusion steps and a DDIM scheduler. The first eight actions are executed before the policy replans using new observations. This receding-horizon procedure allows the system to update its motion in response to changes in the target view and obstacle arrangement.

#### 3.5. Experimental Design and Baselines

The authors collected 90 expert alignment demonstrations using gravity-compensation teaching on a seven-degree-of-freedom Sawyer robot. Demonstrations used a purple pepper target and three obstacle configurations. The interaction replay library contained 26 trajectories.

ObstaDiff was compared with six alignment-policy configurations:

- ACT with RGB input.
- ACT with TOB input.
- Standard Diffusion Policy with RGB input.
- Diffusion Policy with RGB-D input.
- Diffusion Policy with TOB input and a conventional ResNet-18 encoder.
- ObstaDiff with TOB input and the structured encoder.

All methods used the same demonstrations, bottleneck switching rule, replay library, evaluation trials, and, for the diffusion variants, comparable training settings. The evaluation contained 61 distinct configurations per method: 20 target-pose trials, 21 obstacle-layout trials, and 20 target-appearance trials. Across six methods, this produced 366 real-robot executions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ObstaDiff achieved a pooled task success rate of **75.41%**, compared with 45.90% for ACT using RGB and 49.18% for the standard Diffusion Policy using RGB. Its average obstacle collision rate was **8.20%**, compared with 14.75% for ACT RGB and 26.23% for Diffusion Policy RGB.

Performance improved across all three evaluation settings:

- **Target-pose generalization:** ObstaDiff achieved 75.00% success and 5.00% collision rate.
- **Obstacle-layout generalization:** It achieved 71.43% success and 19.05% collision rate.
- **Target-appearance generalization:** It achieved 80.00% success and no observed obstacle collisions.

The other diffusion controls indicate that the improvement was not explained by depth alone or by the TOB representation alone. Adding depth to the conventional RGB diffusion encoder increased success only from 49.18% to 50.82%, with no change in collision rate. Using the same TOB input with the conventional encoder produced 45.90% success and a higher 27.87% collision rate. The comparison between DP (TOB) and ObstaDiff therefore attributes much of the difference to the structured encoder matched to the semantic channel organization.

The ACT comparison showed a related pattern. ACT with TOB reduced collision rate from 14.75% to 11.48% and produced smoother trajectories, but its success rate decreased from 45.90% to 40.98%. This suggests that lower supervised validation loss or smoother motion does not necessarily translate into more successful manipulation. The policy must generate trajectories that are both geometrically safe and task-completing.

ObstaDiff also had the shortest reported target-pose completion time among successful trials, at 15.57 seconds, compared with approximately 18–21 seconds for the baselines. Its trajectory smoothness value was 1.13 × 10⁻⁴, which was lower than ACT RGB and the RGB-based diffusion policy but higher than ACT TOB and DP (TOB). The principal result is therefore improved success and reduced collisions rather than uniformly improved performance on every metric.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper provides evidence that task-structured visual representations can improve imitation-learning behavior in cluttered scenes, particularly when they are paired with an encoder designed for their semantic structure. The results also show that adding sensor information or masks to a conventional backbone may be insufficient. This has implications for robotic learning systems that combine perception and action: representation design should reflect the roles that visual elements play in the task.

The decomposition into alignment and replay-based interaction is another practical contribution. It reduces the amount of contact-rich behavior that must be learned and permits different interaction objectives to be implemented through different replay libraries. This could be useful in agricultural manipulation, inspection, or object acquisition tasks where the approach phase is shared but the final interaction differs.

The results should be interpreted within the study’s scope. The target is human- or prompt-specified, the interaction stage is replayed rather than learned, and the evaluation uses one indoor greenhouse testbed, one target crop, and three obstacle plant species. The method also depends on the reliability of target and obstacle detection. Its generalization has been demonstrated across selected poses, layouts, and target appearances, not across entirely new environments or obstacle categories. In addition, the paper does not provide a quantitative comparison with point-cloud policies such as DP3 because the authors could not deploy that baseline reliably with their moving eye-in-hand camera.

Overall, ObstaDiff suggests a direction for extending diffusion-policy learning beyond relatively clean manipulation settings. Its potential impact lies in integrating explicit semantic roles, geometric conditioning, and modular execution into real-world robotic systems. Future work would need to test the approach in larger and more diverse environments, learn interaction behaviors rather than replay them, and incorporate autonomous target selection and higher-level task planning.
