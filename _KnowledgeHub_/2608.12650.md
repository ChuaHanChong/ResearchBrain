# Attune: A Self-Annotation Tool for Understanding Robot Operator Attention Profiles

> [!summary] Summary
> Attune is a pre-deployment elicitation tool that builds robot operators' attention profiles for multi-robot supervision. It records eye gaze over two-robot, six-view feeds, auto-curates meaningful gaze shifts, guides operators to retroactively annotate why they left and arrived at views, then uses LLMs to cluster patterns, propagate annotations, and summarize. A 12-participant study yielded a SUS score of 75.21 and revealed scenario-shared but individually varying gaze patterns.

## Problem

- Designers lack empirical guidance on how robot behavior should capture, sustain, or relinquish operator attention during multi-robot supervision, leaving feed content design unguided.
- Eye tracking alone cannot reveal why a shift occurred; top-down, knowledge-driven shifts vary per person, so raw gaze signals underdetermine individual attention profiles.
- Existing annotation tools classify fixations, saccades, and areas of interest, but none couple gaze-shift detection with robot replay and operator-authored causal rationales in one workflow.

## Method

- A five-phase pipeline: ==six-stream sensemaking== with Tobii gaze recording aligned to a ==hierarchical robot-activity taxonomy== (goal, subgoal, action, motion, joint state) and ==YOLO-segmented visual cues==.
- A ==gaze-shift curation algorithm== bins video and selects cross-robot and cross-view shifts by 100ms dwell and 200ms unknown-region rules, splitting them into manual and auto-annotation pools.
- ==Annotation cards== elicit why-leave/why-arrive rationales, reactive/proactive labels, and confidence, aided by shift replay and ==nonlinear timeline magnification==; Gemini-2.5-Flash clusters patterns, Llama-3.3-70B propagates them to unseen shifts.

## Results

- Twelve participants scored Attune **75.21** SUS (SD **14.48**, 'Good' anchor) and rated their personal summaries reflective and helpful at **5.92/7** (SD **0.67**).
- Attraction scores exceeded **1.0** for fine manipulator motions and goal-directed actions but fell below **1.0** for gross locomotion and idle states across participants.
- Action-level agreement was modest overall (Kendall's W = **0.24**, p < **.001**) yet far stronger within a shared scenario (W = **0.54-0.70**); motion agreement was weak (W = **0.14**, p = **.07**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Attention profiles are relational, not fixed traits: gaze anchors to legible robot intent and drifts when behavior turns ambiguous, so profiling also diagnoses behavior design.
> - Retrospective annotation trades recall decay for uncontaminated gaze; replay, timeline magnification, and confidence ratings reduce but never eliminate post-hoc rationalization.
> - AI annotation assistance needs roughly 80-90% accuracy to beat manual effort; anchor suggestions in operator-confirmed patterns and keep humans as final interpretive authority.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of Human-Robot Interaction (HRI), specifically focusing on human supervision of multi-robot fleets in complex, real-world environments. The deployment of autonomous robot systems often necessitates human operators to monitor and intervene, particularly in scenarios involving multiple robots. Prior research in HRI has highlighted that operator performance is significantly influenced by factors such as stress, workload, and attention. Consequently, there have been efforts to optimize operator interface design to manage these factors.

The paper draws upon several areas of established research:

- **Human Attention:** Building on theories like the Premotor Theory of Attention, which links eye movements (saccades) to attentional shifts, the work acknowledges that attention is influenced by factors such as visual salience, expectancy, value, and effort. It also recognizes that attention can be a "top-down" (knowledge-driven) process, varying individually based on goals and experiences. Existing research explores predicting and leveraging attention in human-centered computing and demonstrates how robot behaviors can be designed to manage human attention. However, eye tracking alone cannot definitively determine the cause of an attentional shift, and human reasoning behind these shifts often remains implicit.
- **Interfaces for Robot Operators:** Previous work has explored various forms of robot operation, from direct teleoperation to remote monitoring of semi-autonomous systems. Interface design for remote monitoring, common in delivery, search-and-rescue, and hazardous environment applications, is crucial. Such interfaces can range from continuous monitoring with multiple camera feeds to "manage-by-exception" alert systems. Research indicates that how a robot presents its autonomy, reliability, and behavior duration impacts operator situation awareness and overall effectiveness, leading to the development of specialized remote viewing interfaces.
- **Behavioral Annotation Interfaces:** The work relates to a broader lineage of tools for qualitative analysis, including text and multimodal (video/audio) content. Tools like BORIS, DOTE, and ELAN are used for analyzing human-human or human-robot interactions. While existing research addresses the classification of eye fixations and saccades and mapping fixations to areas of interest, there is a recognized gap in tools specifically designed for enabling viewers themselves to rationalize the causes of saccades, particularly in multi-robot HRI contexts.

This research aims to bridge the gap between objective gaze data and subjective operator reasoning by developing a tool that enables operators to retroactively attribute causes to their gaze shifts. By characterizing operator attention profiles, the work seeks to provide empirical guidance for designers to calibrate robot behavior and interfaces, thereby moving beyond generic interface improvements to personalized and context-specific solutions for multi-robot supervision. The focus is on understanding *why* attention shifts occur, a dimension largely unexplored by existing gaze analysis or annotation tools in this specific context.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the increasing deployment of multi-robot fleets in various complex, real-world environments (e.g., college campuses, hospitals, warehouses). In these settings, human operators are often tasked with supervising multiple robots simultaneously, necessitating effective management of their attention to ensure safety and operational efficiency. A fundamental challenge lies in designing multi-robot supervision interfaces and, more specifically, in shaping robot behaviors to appropriately capture, sustain, or relinquish operator attention. Currently, designers lack empirical guidance on how to modify a robot’s behavior to achieve these attentional goals.

The paper posits a future vision where designers can utilize such guidance to calibrate robot behaviors according to specific operator attention profiles. To realize this vision, a crucial initial step is to understand an operator's individual attention profile – defined as a collection of recurring gaze-shift patterns, grounded in the operator’s retrospective rationales and the surrounding robot and environmental context. This understanding would allow for the customization of interfaces or the adjustment of robot behaviors to better align with an operator's attentional needs and mitigate distractions.

The key objectives of this work are therefore:

- **To develop and evaluate Attune:** This pre-deployment elicitation tool is designed to facilitate a calibration step where operator attention profiles can be characterized. Attune's specific functionalities include automatically identifying meaningful gaze shifts, providing AI assistance for attributing causes to these shifts, and summarizing operator gaze patterns for review. The initial focus is on minimal two-robot configurations, serving as a foundational step for broader applicability.
- **To investigate the design of annotation tools for causal attribution (RQ1):** The research aims to explore how annotation tools can be structured to effectively elicit retroactive causal explanations for gaze shifts, moving beyond mere recording of where operators look to understanding *why* they looked there.
- **To characterize gaze shifts during multi-robot supervision (RQ2):** The study seeks to uncover general characteristics and individual variations in operator gaze patterns when supervising multiple robots, linking these patterns to robot behaviors and environmental cues.
- **To provide design implications:** Based on the evaluation of Attune, the work aims to offer actionable recommendations for improving self-annotation tools and broader calibration systems in HRI.

By achieving these objectives, the research intends to provide a pathway for designers to empirically inform the design of robot behaviors and supervisory interfaces, ultimately leading to more effective and personalized human-robot collaboration.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors designed Attune as a pre-deployment operator calibration tool for multi-robot monitoring, initially focusing on a two-robot scenario. The system integrates multi-video monitoring, eye-tracking-based gaze recording, and both manual and LLM-assisted annotation to capture and explain operator gaze shifts. The overall process is structured into five sequential phases, forming a one-time, human-in-the-loop calibration step.

#### 3.1. System Design: The Attune Pipeline

1. **Phase 1: Multi-Robot Video Sensemaking:**
 - **Interface:** A sensemaking interface was developed to display two robots concurrently executing independent tasks. Each robot provided three synchronized camera views: ceiling, robot head point-of-view (POV), and gripper POV, resulting in six video streams. This design facilitates the characterization of both intra-robot (cross-view) and inter-robot (cross-robot) gaze shifts.
 - **Data Recording:** While operators monitor the feeds, Attune records eye gaze at the pixel level and discretizes it to specify the viewed video region. Gaze data is synchronized with video playback.
 - **Contextual Alignment:** Gaze is aligned with hierarchical robot activity labels (Goal, Subgoal, Action, Motion, Gripper State, Wrist State, Head State), which can be sourced from the robots themselves. Additionally, YOLO object detection is used to extract visual cues (object labels and maskable regions) from each video frame, providing further context for gaze shifts.

2. **Phase 2: Data Annotation Interface:**
 - **Gaze Shift Selection:** After the sensemaking session, Attune automatically selects and curates gaze shifts for annotation. Selection occurs within the final five minutes of each session to reduce novelty effects and recall decay. Gaze shifts are partitioned into temporal bins, and valid cross-robot and cross-view shifts are sampled and curated based on specific rules (e.g., minimum dwell time of 100ms on source and destination views, 200ms for "unknown" regions). These curated shifts are divided into a manual annotation pool (M) and an auto-annotation pool (A).
 - **Shift Inspection and Replay:** Operators inspect shifts from pool M via an annotation tool. The tool displays the shift timestamp, "from" and "to" camera views, and time-aligned robot context. Two replay modes are provided: "replay shift" (focusing on the transition) and "replay context" (5s before to 0.5s after). Gaze points and trajectories are overlaid during replay.
 - **Nonlinear Timeline Magnification:** A piecewise nonlinear temporal mapping emphasizes the interval around the gaze shift ([t-30s, t]) while compressing earlier history and post-shift context, aiding recall.
 - **Structured Annotation:** Operators retroactively attribute causes using structured explanations: "why leave" (previous view) and "why arrive" (new view). They can link these to robot activities or visual cues (YOLO-segmented objects, or user-drawn masks for unclassified objects). Shift types are categorized as "reactive" (bottom-up, involuntary) or "proactive" (top-down, voluntary). Confidence and memory clarity ratings are also collected.

3. **Phase 3: Attention Profile Pattern Analysis:**
 - **Pattern Inference:** Attune’s backend organizes operator-annotated shifts (rationales, robot activities, visual cues, gaze timings, ratings) and submits them to a zero-shot LLM (Gemini-2.5-Flash) for clustering. The LLM groups shifts based on semantic similarity of explanations, grounds them against shared context, and proposes candidate patterns with descriptions and supporting evidence.
 - **Operator Review and Refinement:** Operators review these candidate patterns, replay individual supporting gaze shifts, and either accept, modify, or reject them. Confirmed patterns are added to a "reviewed pattern library" for the operator's attention profile.

4. **Phase 4: Pattern-Guided Auto-Annotation:**
 - **LLM-Assisted Annotation:** Attune uses the operator's reviewed pattern library and local shift context (robot states, visual cues, gaze timings) to construct a structured prompt for a faster, lower-cost LLM (Groq Llama-3.3-70B-Versatile). This LLM proposes candidate annotations (shift type, why leave, why arrive) for shifts in pool A, along with confidence levels and reasoning.
 - **Operator Verification:** Operators review these LLM-generated suggestions, treating them as editable, and can accept, modify, or discard them. They also provide confidence and memory clarity ratings.

5. **Phase 5: Personal Summary and Reflection:**
 - **Summary Generation:** Attune synthesizes the complete annotation history (manual and auto-annotated) and the reviewed pattern library into an operator-facing summary (using Gemini-2.5-Flash).
 - **Summary Components:** This summary includes a reflective annotation summary (operator's annotation style, use of labels, confidence/memory clarity distribution), an attention profile summary (describing confirmed monitoring patterns), and an auto-annotation operationalization section (which patterns were invoked, acceptance/revision rates). This phase allows the operator to understand and refine their profile before it is delivered to a designer.

#### 3.2. Implementation

Attune is implemented as a desktop application with a React/TypeScript frontend and a FastAPI backend. It integrates with a Tobii Pro Spark (60 Hz) eye tracker. Different LLMs (Gemini-2.5-Flash for clustering/summarization, Llama-3.3-70B for auto-annotation) are chosen based on a quality-latency tradeoff. Video playback is pre-recorded.

#### 3.3. Evaluation

- **Procedure:** A user study with 12 naïve participants (8 male, 4 female) was conducted. Participants engaged in three phases:
 1. **Monitoring:** Each watched 8 minutes of video from one of three scenarios (kitchen, living room, hallway) involving a physical Stretch 3 robot, recorded from three POVs, mimicking two robots. Participants were instructed to verbalize aloud.
 2. **Annotation:** Participants manually annotated 5-8 shifts from pool M and 5 shifts from pool A, providing rationales, shift types, confidence, and memory clarity.
 3. **Review:** Participants reviewed AI-generated patterns and auto-annotations, then answered questions about their experience.
- **Data and Measures:**
 - Continuous eye gaze data (60 Hz).
 - User annotations (rationales, shift types, confidence, memory clarity).
 - Interviews via phase-embedded and end-of-session questions.
 - Audio recordings to characterize verbalization behaviors.
 - Questionnaires, including the System Usability Scale (SUS).
- **Analysis:** Gaze allocation was analyzed using heatmaps and "attraction scores" (ratio of activity's share of cross-robot shift-in events to its proportional duration). Pairwise rank correlation (Kendall's 𝜏) and overall agreement (Kendall's 𝑊) were computed for attraction scores. Causal attribution effectiveness was assessed via Likert scale responses, cross-checking proactive/reactive labels against free-response rationales, and thematic analysis of interview data. Usability was measured by SUS scores and interview data.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Attune involved analyzing participant verbalizations, gaze patterns, the effectiveness of causal attribution, and system usability.

#### 4.1. Verbalization Behaviors

All 12 participants verbalized during the monitoring sessions, adopting varied strategies. Some continuously spoke to the robots as if companions, while others verbalized only when noting something noteworthy. The authors suggest that these verbalization strategies might have influenced observed gaze patterns and later recall of gaze shift rationales, indicating a potential confounding factor for interpreting individual differences as stable attentional traits.

#### 4.2. Gaze Patterns

- **Gaze Allocation:** Analysis of gaze heatmaps showed that participants did not monitor all six video views uniformly. Instead, they concentrated attention on specific camera regions, forming distinct "hotspots." Gaze patterns exhibited variation across participants, with some focusing on a few views while others distributed their attention more broadly. The spatial distribution of attention also shifted across the three evaluated scenarios (Living Room, Kitchen, Hallway).
- **Attraction Scores:** The authors quantified participant gaze patterns using an "attraction score," which measures how frequently participants shifted attention to specific robot actions or motions, normalized by the prevalence of that activity. Cross-robot shifts (attention shifts between different robots) were primarily analyzed.
 - **General Trends:** Fine-grained manipulator motions and goal-directed actions consistently yielded attraction scores above 1.0, indicating they attracted gaze shifts more than expected based on their frequency. Conversely, gross locomotion and idle states generally had scores below 1.0, suggesting they were less attractive. These findings indicate that precise manipulation and task execution by one robot often serve as shared attentional anchors for operators.
 - **Individual and Scenario Variation:** While some shared patterns existed, participant-level monitoring strategies introduced notable variation in cross-robot gaze allocation, underscoring the need for personalized calibration.
- **Rank Correlation of Attraction Scores:**
 - **Actions:** Overall agreement in attraction scores for actions across all participants was modest but statistically significant (Kendall's 𝑊 = 0.24, 𝑝 < .001). This agreement was substantially stronger within scenario-based subgroups (𝑊 = 0.54–0.70, all 𝑝 < .01), indicating more consistent gaze patterns among participants viewing the same scenario. Patterns were less consistent across different scenarios.
 - **Motions:** Overall agreement for motion was lower and only marginally significant (𝑊 = 0.14, 𝑝 = .07), suggesting greater heterogeneity in how participants attended to robot motions. Variability was evident across scenarios, with one group showing strong agreement (P9–P12: 𝑊 = 0.57, 𝑝 = .007), another moderate (P5–P8: 𝑊 = 0.40, 𝑝 = .08), and a third minimal (P1–P4: 𝑊 = 0.18, 𝑝 = .74).
These results suggest that similarities in how operators attend to robot actions are more pronounced within shared scenarios, while individual and scenario-specific differences are more prominent for robot motions. The authors note that the unique video pairings and verbalization strategies per participant mean these differences cannot be solely attributed to stable individual or scenario-based attentional tendencies.

#### 4.3. Effectiveness of Causal Attribution

- **Perceived Helpfulness:** Participants generally found Attune effective in helping them articulate their gaze-shift reasoning. The average response to whether their personal summary was "reflective and helpful" on a 7-point Likert scale was 𝑀 = 5.92 (𝑆𝐷 = 0.67), indicating agreement.
- **Proactive vs. Reactive Gaze:** Analysis of participants' annotations indicated that both "proactive" (deliberate monitoring goals) and "reactive" (perceptually salient events) gaze shifts were reported, suggesting participants understood these distinctions. However, the authors caution that these are participant-endorsed rationales rather than objective causal classifications, given their retrospective nature.
- **Thematic Analysis of Interviews:** Four themes emerged regarding Attune's performance as a retrospective causal attribution tool:
 - **Theme 1: Annotation as a Scaffold for Externalizing Monitoring Reasoning:** Participants reported that the annotation process surfaced previously unrecognized attentional patterns and generated new self-insight, suggesting it elicits tacit knowledge. The structured "why-leave/why-arrive" and "reactive/proactive" classifications facilitated this reflection.
 - **Theme 2: Retrospective Reconstruction:** Participants acknowledged the difficulty of faithfully recalling in-the-moment reasoning, noting the risk of post-hoc justification due to knowledge of clip outcomes. The replay function, gaze-shift timeline, and confidence ratings partially mitigated this, but the issue of memory degradation persisted.
 - **Theme 3: Robot Intentionality as Gaze Anchor:** When asked to rank contextual cues, "Robot Context" (robot behavior) was ranked highest (Borda score = 28), followed by "Visual Context" (score = 24), "Task Context" (score = 20), and "Others" (score = 0). This suggests that operators’ interpretation of robot intent was the dominant driver of attention shifts. Gaze was sustained when robot behavior was legible but became passive when intent was ambiguous. This indicates that attention profiles are co-constructed with the legibility of robot behavior.
 - **Theme 4: Accuracy Threshold for AI Annotation as Cognitive Scaffold:** Participants reacted positively to AI-generated summaries when they effectively compressed behavior into interpretable profiles. They estimated an "80 or 90% accuracy" threshold for preferring AI annotation over manual effort. While AI reduced time, some noted increased cognitive load for verification. Participants favored a validation-oriented workflow, where AI assistance supported review rather than replacement of manual effort.

#### 4.4. Usability and Usefulness

Attune received a mean System Usability Scale (SUS) score of 75.21 (𝑆𝐷 = 14.48), categorized as "Good." Valued features included the replay function, gaze-shift timeline for context restoration, and the "why-leave/why-arrive" structure for comprehensive shift reconstruction. The primary usability complaint was information density, with participants suggesting progressive disclosure for options and dynamic narrowing of choices based on context. Participants found it easier to describe reasoning in natural language than to map it onto predefined categories.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presents Attune, a novel self-annotation tool designed to elicit and characterize robot operator attention profiles. This work contributes to the field of Human-Robot Interaction by addressing the empirical gap in understanding *why* operators shift their attention during multi-robot supervision, rather than merely *where* and *when*.

**Significance:**

- **Bridging Gaze Data and Operator Intent:** Attune provides a structured method for operators to retroactively attribute causes to their gaze shifts, integrating objective eye-tracking data with subjective cognitive rationales. This move from descriptive gaze analysis to explanatory profiles is a key advancement for informing HRI design.
- **Eliciting Tacit Knowledge:** The study demonstrates that the self-annotation process within Attune can elicit tacit knowledge from operators, revealing attentional patterns they had not consciously recognized. This highlights the value of calibration as a knowledge externalization process beyond simple data collection.
- **Context-Dependent Attention Profiles:** The findings underscore that operator attention profiles are not static, intrinsic traits but are co-constructed with the legibility and context of robot behavior. This emphasizes the interactive nature of attention management in HRI, suggesting that understanding attention also involves evaluating how effectively robots communicate their state and intent.
- **Human-in-the-Loop AI for HRI Annotation:** Attune integrates LLM-assisted annotation into a human-in-the-loop workflow, demonstrating how AI can scaffold reflection and reduce annotation burden while preserving operator authority over interpretation. This model is significant for designing scalable yet trustworthy annotation systems in complex domains.
- **Empirical Guidance for Design:** The research generates design implications for future calibration systems, emphasizing the need to support operator memory during retrospective annotation, account for robot behavioral legibility, and prioritize human-in-the-loop verification in AI-assisted workflows, particularly regarding privacy.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Personalized HRI Design:** The primary potential impact is enabling designers to create more personalized and effective multi-robot supervisory interfaces and to calibrate robot behaviors to individual operator attention profiles. This could lead to improved operator performance, reduced workload, and enhanced situation awareness in real-world deployments across various sectors like logistics, healthcare, and search-and-rescue.
- **Improved Robot Behavior Design:** By understanding which robot actions or contextual cues reliably attract or relinquish operator attention, designers can proactively adjust robot behaviors to better manage human attention, for instance, by designing robots to be more (or less) salient depending on task criticality.
- **Foundational for Advanced Attention Management:** This work lays a foundation for future research in developing more sophisticated attention management systems in HRI, potentially leading to adaptive interfaces that dynamically adjust based on an operator's live attention profile or robots that can autonomously adapt their behaviors to optimize operator attention.
- **Ethical Considerations:** The research also implicitly impacts the discussion around privacy and surveillance in HRI. By highlighting the potential for detailed operator profiles, it underscores the importance of explicit data retention policies, access boundaries, and ethical guidelines for using such sensitive information.
- **Methodological Advancement:** The Attune system and its evaluation methodology provide a blueprint for future studies aiming to understand cognitive processes in complex human-robot teams, particularly in scenarios where subjective rationales are crucial.

While the study acknowledges limitations such as its two-robot pre-recorded setup and the retrospective nature of annotation, it marks a significant step towards a more nuanced and empirically driven approach to managing operator attention in multi-robot systems. Future work extending Attune to larger fleets, live deployments, and integrating with alert-driven workflows could further amplify its impact.
