# CLIFT: Turning Gemini Robotics On-Device into Humanoid Specialists via Non-Invasive Closed-Loop Iterative Fine-Tuning

> [!summary] Summary
> A team from UC Berkeley, Google DeepMind, and NVIDIA Research introduced CLIFT, a non-invasive, API-only pipeline that uses closed-loop deployment feedback to iteratively fine-tune robot foundation models for humanoid specialists. This method enhanced Gemini Robotics On-Device (GROD) performance, achieving up to 100% success rates on complex manipulation tasks without additional human demonstrations.

## Problem

- Powerful robot foundation models are often proprietary and accessible only via managed APIs, restricting adaptation through traditional means.
- Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) from human demonstrations is inadequate for achieving task mastery in agile, contact-rich humanoid manipulation due to distribution mismatch between demonstrations and real-world deployment.
- Existing closed-loop policy improvement methods require invasive access to model internals (e.g., gradients, architectures), which is incompatible with black-box, managed API interfaces.

## Method

- An API-only, non-invasive pipeline called **CLIFT** converts deployment reward feedback into supervised data, iteratively fine-tuning the base foundation model from scratch on accumulated positive/negative-labeled chunks via the ==managed SFT API==.
- A ==preference-calibrated dense reward model==, distilled from VLM candidates guided by human pairwise preferences, evaluates policy rollouts.
- ==Retrieval-based advantage conditioning== labels individual action chunks as "positive" or "negative" by comparing their returns to visually similar historical states.

## Results

- CLIFT improved Gemini Robotics On-Device (GROD) success rates on contact-rich humanoid tasks from a baseline of **53-93%** to **96-100%**, achieving near-task mastery with API-only access.
- The dense advantage-conditioned CLIFT variant outperformed episode-selection on challenging tasks like Bimanual Plate Handover (**96%** vs. **~84%**), by leveraging granular feedback from both successful and failed attempts.
- While CLIFT enhanced both GROD and open-weight π₀.₅, GROD attained higher absolute performance and developed emergent corrective/pre-manipulation behaviors (e.g., reorienting objects, retrying failed insertions), suggesting a strong pretrained prior drives both.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Non-invasive closed-loop adaptation, converting deployment feedback into supervised data, enhances performance in managed API settings, though the underlying pretrained foundation model's strength remains the primary determinant of peak performance, often outweighing invasive adaptation methods' benefits.
> - Dense, chunk-level advantage conditioning, which extracts value from both successful and failed rollout segments, surpasses episode-level selection for complex manipulation tasks.
> - Iterative closed-loop practice allows the robot policy to develop novel, emergent behaviors not explicitly shown in human demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robot foundation models (RFMs) and vision-language-action (VLA) models represents a significant trend in robotics, aiming to create broadly competent robot policies across diverse tasks and embodiments. These models, often trained on extensive and varied datasets, are demonstrating increasing capabilities, potentially reducing the need for large amounts of task-specific data and improving generalization.

However, a tension exists between the power of these models and their accessibility. Many of the most advanced robot foundation models are developed by proprietary entities, trained on private datasets, and remain closed-source. This restricts the ability of external researchers and downstream users to adapt these powerful models to novel tasks, robot platforms, or deployment environments, as direct access to model weights, gradients, or internal training procedures is withheld.

In response to this, an emerging paradigm, paralleling developments in the large language model (LLM) community, involves exposing closed-weight models through managed adaptation interfaces, such as supervised fine-tuning (SFT) APIs. In this regime, users can submit their own training data and receive a fine-tuned policy without gaining direct access to the model's internal architecture or training pipeline. Gemini Robotics On-Device (GROD) and Physical Intelligence’s partner API are examples of this trend in robotics.

While managed SFT APIs offer a way to leverage powerful proprietary models, they traditionally limit policy improvement to pure imitation learning from human demonstrations. This approach often falls short for tasks requiring agile, contact-rich humanoid manipulation. The discrepancy arises because SFT policies are trained on a fixed distribution of human teleoperated data, which often differs significantly from the policy's own closed-loop observation distribution during real-world deployment. Factors such as execution quality, precise contact dynamics, latency, and specific controller failure modes are often not fully captured or addressed by demonstration-only SFT. Existing humanoid VLA systems, primarily relying on demonstration-based SFT, have shown limitations in achieving high deployment-level success rates on dexterous manipulation tasks.

Current research efforts to bridge this gap for robot VLAs have explored methods such as generating residual-Reinforcement Learning (RL) data for SFT, implementing PPO-style RL with learned rewards, or steering policies in their latent action space. However, these methods typically require invasive access to the learning system, involving auxiliary architectural components (e.g., critic networks), modifications to training losses, or direct access to internal signals like policy gradients and action likelihoods. Such invasive approaches are incompatible with the restricted-access nature of managed SFT APIs.

This work positions itself at the intersection of these challenges, investigating the efficacy of the managed-API paradigm for humanoid adaptation and developing a non-invasive methodology for closed-loop policy improvement within these constraints. It aims to demonstrate that even without internal model access, it is possible to achieve task mastery on complex humanoid tasks by converting real-world deployment feedback into an API-compatible supervised training signal.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate the effectiveness of the emerging managed-API access regime for humanoid adaptation and to demonstrate how closed-loop policy improvement can be realized within this regime to achieve task mastery.

The motivation for this study stems from several factors:

1. **Leveraging Powerful Closed-Weight Foundation Models:** The strongest robot foundation models, such as Gemini Robotics On-Device (GROD), are often proprietary and accessible only through managed APIs. This presents a practical dilemma: these models offer high adaptation potential, but their closed nature limits extensive customization. The research aims to explore the practical utility of this intermediate access regime for real-world robotics.
2. **Addressing Limitations of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for Humanoids:** While managed APIs enable SFT on task-specific demonstrations, this approach has inherent limitations, particularly for agile, contact-rich humanoid manipulation.
 - **Distribution Mismatch:** SFT policies are trained on a fixed distribution of human teleoperation data, which can differ substantially from the observation distribution encountered during autonomous closed-loop deployment. This mismatch can lead to performance degradation in novel or off-distribution states.
 - **Beyond Task Completion:** For humanoids, task success involves more than just completion; factors like smoothness, contact quality, and safety are critical. Demonstration-only SFT often struggles to capture and optimize these nuanced aspects of execution quality.
 - **Controller Coupling:** The VLA policy is tightly coupled to the robot's downstream whole-body controller. Identical policy actions can lead to varying physical outcomes due to tracking dynamics, latency, compliance, and environmental contact. This complexity makes it difficult for SFT alone to bridge the gap between predicted actions and deployed behavior.
3. **Incompatibility of Traditional Closed-Loop Methods with API Constraints:** Classical reinforcement learning (RL) and other closed-loop improvement techniques typically rely on access to internal model components such as weights, gradients, likelihoods, or architectural modifications (e.g., critic networks). These internal signals are not exposed through managed SFT APIs, rendering conventional closed-loop methods inapplicable in this setting.
4. **Necessity of On-Device Practice:** To overcome the limitations of demonstration-only learning and the challenges of humanoid control, policies require on-device closed-loop practice. This involves allowing the policy to execute on the real robot, observe its own behavior, and adapt based on feedback from its actual performance rather than solely from a fixed set of teleoperated demonstrations.

By addressing these motivations, the research seeks to provide an empirical understanding of managed-API adaptation for humanoids and to develop a non-invasive mechanism, CLIFT, that facilitates closed-loop policy improvement without requiring direct access to the model's internal architecture or training parameters. This approach aims to push API-only robot foundation models towards task mastery, demonstrating their potential utility in a constrained access environment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This research introduces CLIFT (Closed-Loop Iterative Fine-Tuning), a non-invasive, API-only pipeline designed to enable closed-loop policy improvement for robot foundation models, specifically in the context of humanoid manipulation. CLIFT operates by converting deployment-time reward feedback into supervised training data compatible with managed SFT APIs. The pipeline consists of three primary steps, forming an iterative flywheel.

#### 3.1. CLIFT Pipeline Overview

CLIFT works by iteratively improving a policy without accessing its internal weights, gradients, or loss functions. The process begins with an initial policy trained on human demonstrations. In each cycle, the current policy is deployed to gather experience, this experience is evaluated and labeled with a specific advantage token, and then this relabeled data is used to fine-tune the model again via the managed SFT API, producing an improved policy for the next cycle.

#### 3.2. Step (i): Policy Bootstrap from Demonstrations

The initial policy, denoted 𝜋₀, is obtained by performing supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a fixed dataset of human teleoperated demonstrations (D_demo). This fine-tuning is conducted through the managed SFT API (F_SFT), which treats the process as a black-box operation, mapping the demonstration dataset to a fine-tuned policy. This step establishes a baseline policy before any closed-loop self-improvement.

#### 3.3. Step (ii): Scoring Rollouts and Injecting Reward Signal

This step focuses on generating feedback from policy deployments and transforming it into a format suitable for SFT.

- **Preference-Calibrated Dense Reward Model (R_𝜃):**
 - **Motivation:** A dense reward model is required to assess task progress, execution quality (e.g., smoothness, contact quality), and safety, going beyond binary task completion.
 - **Two-Stage Select-Then-Distill Scheme:**
 1. **Human Preference Collection:** A comparison pool is created by mixing human teleoperated trajectories (including failures) with initial SFT policy rollouts to ensure a diverse range of behavior quality. 100 rollout pairs are sampled from this pool and presented to an expert evaluator. The evaluator provides pairwise preferences, indicating which rollout better satisfies the task criteria across multiple viewpoints (robot egocentric and third-person).
 2. **VLM-Generated Reward Candidates:** For each rollout in a human-labeled pair, a zero-shot Vision-Language Model (VLM, specifically GPT-5.5) is prompted with the task description, a reward rubric, and hard failure conditions. It generates K=12 candidate per-step reward sequences using temperature sampling.
 3. **Preference-Consistent Selection:** From these candidates, a single reward sequence per rollout is selected. This selection is optimized to maximize agreement with the human pairwise preferences (i.e., if human preferred A over B, the selected rewards should yield a higher cumulative return for A than B).
 4. **Reward Model Distillation:** The selected dense, per-step reward labels ({𝑟\*_1:T_}) are then used to train a generative reward model (R_𝜃). This model, implemented with a Qwen3-VL backbone, is fine-tuned to regress its predicted per-step rewards onto these labels using a mean-squared error loss. The reward model is trained once on a pooled dataset from all tasks and kept fixed across all subsequent flywheel cycles.

- **Retrieval-Based Advantage Conditioning:**
 - **Motivation:** Direct dense rewards are insufficient; a chunk's quality must be judged relative to what is achievable from its starting state. Traditional RL value function learning is incompatible with the API.
 - **Advantage Token:** Each action chunk is augmented with a binary token (𝐼 ∈ {positive, negative}) to indicate whether it led to a better or worse outcome compared to alternatives from similar states.
 - **Observation Encoding:** A frozen, pre-trained vision encoder (DINOv3 ViT-S/16) embeds every frame of every rollout into a normalized feature vector.
 - **Visual Similarity and Comparison Set:** For any given chunk (𝜏_𝑖) starting at observation (𝑜_𝑡𝑖), a comparison set of peer chunks is constructed. For each *other* rollout, the frame with the most visually similar initial observation (based on cosine similarity of embeddings) to (𝑜_𝑡𝑖) is identified. If this similarity exceeds a threshold (𝛿), the corresponding chunk from that rollout is added to the comparison set. This ensures comparisons are made against genuinely similar initial states.
 - **Advantage Labeling:** Each chunk (𝜏_𝑖) is scored by its discounted return (𝐺(𝜏_𝑖)) over a fixed look-ahead window (H steps) using the preference-calibrated R_𝜃. The chunk is labeled "positive" if its return falls within the top 30% of returns from its comparison set, and "negative" otherwise. This percentile-based labeling dynamically calibrates to the difficulty of the starting state.

#### 3.4. Step (iii): Iterative Self-Improvement

In each cycle (k), the current policy (𝜋_k) is deployed. The resulting rollouts are scored by R_𝜃, and their constituent chunks are assigned binary advantage labels using the retrieval-based method. These newly labeled rollout chunks are then appended to a cumulative dataset (D_k = D_demo ∪ D_{1:k}rollout). Importantly, demonstrations in D_demo are always labeled as "positive." The next policy (𝜋_{k+1}) is then obtained by fine-tuning the *base model from scratch* on the entire cumulative dataset (D_k) through the managed SFT API. This re-initialization from the base checkpoint, rather than continuing from 𝜋_k, is stated to prevent compounding distributional drift and re-exploit the full pretrained capacity in each cycle. The process of deployment, scoring, labeling, and fine-tuning constitutes one flywheel cycle.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Robot Platform:** Unitree G1 humanoid robot, featuring dual arms, dexterous hands, and two head-mounted RGB cameras. It uses a hierarchical whole-body control scheme, where the VLA policy outputs high-level upper-body joint angles and lower-body velocities, tracked by an RL-learned whole-body controller.
- **Tasks:** Three contact-rich humanoid manipulation tasks: Box Packing, Cup Insertion, and Bimanual Plate Handover. These tasks vary in complexity, dexterity, and agility requirements, and involve whole-body balance.
- **VLA Models for Adaptation:**
 - **Gemini Robotics On-Device (GROD):** A closed-weight model accessible via a managed SFT API.
 - **𝜋₀.₅:** An open-weight VLA model from Physical Intelligence, allowing direct access to weights and training code.
- **Controlled Comparison:** Both GROD and 𝜋₀.₅ are subjected to the identical CLIFT pipeline: same human demonstrations, rollout collection budget, preference-calibrated reward model (R_𝜃), and advantage relabeling procedure. Both are fine-tuned with a standard supervised loss for 10 epochs using their default SFT configurations. Two independent training runs are performed for each model.
- **Human Demonstration Collection:** 2 hours of whole-body VR teleoperation data were collected per task, recording egocentric camera images, proprioceptive states, and executed actions.
- **CLIFT Protocol and Evaluation:** The initial policy (𝜋₀) is fine-tuned from the base model on the 2 hours of demonstrations. The reward model is trained once. Two cycles of CLIFT are then executed. Each cycle involves deploying the current policy for 100 rollouts per task under fixed evaluation configurations to ensure consistent comparison conditions across models and cycles. The same rollouts serve as both evaluation data and training data for the subsequent cycle.
- **Deployment:** All policy variants use a consistent deployment function, conditioning on the "positive" advantage token and employing classifier-free guidance (𝛽=0.2) to enhance high-quality actions.
- **CLIFT Variants Evaluated for GROD:**
 - **Dense Advantage-Conditioned:** Every action chunk from deployment rollouts is scored and assigned a positive or negative advantage label. All chunks are used for SFT.
 - **High-Return Episode Selection:** Deployment rollouts are ranked by accumulated reward, and only the top 30% (advantageous rollouts) are selected for the next SFT dataset, similar to a DAgger-style approach.
- **Invasive Baseline for 𝜋₀.₅ (Appendix):** A comparison is also made with an invasive adaptation of 𝜋₀.₅, which uses FiLM-style conditioning to inject the preference signal directly into the network architecture, an approach CLIFT deliberately avoids.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot across three contact-rich manipulation tasks provided several key findings regarding the effectiveness of managed-API adaptation and the CLIFT pipeline.

#### 4.1. CLIFT's Effectiveness in Achieving Task Mastery for GROD

CLIFT, particularly its dense advantage-conditioned variant, demonstrated substantial improvements for policies based on Gemini Robotics On-Device (GROD). Both CLIFT variants improved the success rates of the demonstration-trained baseline at every cycle across all three tasks.

- **Near-Task Mastery:** The dense advantage-conditioned variant of CLIFT pushed GROD to near-perfect success rates without additional human demonstrations:
 - Box Packing: Increased from 93% (SFT baseline) to 100%.
 - Cup Insertion: Increased from 70% to 98%.
 - Bimanual Plate Handover: Increased from 53% to 96%.
 These results were achieved using only API-only access to GROD.

- **Comparison of CLIFT Variants:** On simpler tasks like Box Packing and Cup Insertion, both the dense advantage-conditioned and high-return episode selection variants performed comparably, achieving high success rates. However, on the more challenging Bimanual Plate Handover task, the dense variant significantly outperformed the episode selection variant (96% vs. approximately 84%). This difference is attributed to the dense variant's ability to extract valuable training signals from failed rollouts by relabeling individual chunks, treating well-executed parts of a failed rollout as positive examples and poorly executed parts as negative. The episode selection variant, in contrast, discards entire failed rollouts, losing this granular information.

#### 4.2. Generalizability of CLIFT Across Foundation Models

CLIFT's underlying mechanism was also applied to 𝜋₀.₅, an open-weight VLA model, using the identical pipeline (same reward model, rollout budget, and advantage relabeling).

- **Improved Performance for 𝜋₀.₅:** CLIFT improved 𝜋₀.₅'s success rates:
 - Box Packing: Increased from 59% to 76%.
 - Cup Insertion: Increased from 50% to 56%.
 - Bimanual Plate Handover: Increased from 5% to 30%.
This indicates that the non-invasive closed-loop improvement mechanism can transfer across different foundation models and access regimes.

#### 4.3. Impact of Foundation Model Choice on Performance Ceiling

Despite CLIFT improving both models, GROD consistently achieved a higher absolute performance ceiling compared to 𝜋₀.₅ under the same adaptation pipeline. This performance gap was more pronounced with increasing task difficulty.

- **GROD Outperforms 𝜋₀.₅:** For instance, on the agile, contact-rich tasks, GROD reached 84% on Bimanual Plate Handover compared to 𝜋₀.₅'s 46%, and 88% versus 46% on Cup Insertion.
- **Pretrained Prior as a Factor:** The researchers attribute this gap primarily to the strength of the pretrained prior. GROD is built on a foundation model pretrained on substantially larger and more diverse datasets than 𝜋₀.₅ (which uses a PaliGemma backbone).
- **Invasive Adaptation Does Not Close the Gap:** Critically, even when 𝜋₀.₅ was subjected to an *invasive* adaptation method (FiLM-style conditioning, which directly modifies the network architecture to inject preference signals), it still lagged behind GROD adapted through the API-only CLIFT (e.g., 40% vs. 84% on Bimanual Plate Handover, 48% vs. 93% on Cup Insertion). This finding suggests that the intrinsic capability of the base model, likely derived from its extensive pretraining, can be a more significant determinant of peak performance than the level of access granted for adaptation.

#### 4.4. Emergent Behaviors from Closed-Loop Practice

By the final flywheel cycle, GROD exhibited novel corrective and pre-manipulation behaviors that were not present in the initial human teleoperated demonstrations.

- **Box Packing:** The policy learned to use its fingers and wrist to nudge and reorient the box, setting up an easier grasp before lifting it.
- **Cup Insertion:** The policy developed a retry mechanism, re-approaching and succeeding on a second insertion attempt after an initial failure, rather than stalling.
These emergent behaviors suggest that CLIFT's process of reinforcing high-return chunks, including those salvaged from failed episodes, enables the policy to compose new actions beyond mere imitation of the demonstration distribution. A stronger pretrained prior (like GROD) appeared better equipped to acquire these emergent capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robot learning by addressing a practical challenge in the deployment and adaptation of advanced robot foundation models (RFMs), particularly for complex humanoid platforms. Its significance and potential impact include:

- **Enabling Access to Powerful Proprietary Models:** The work demonstrates a viable methodology for leveraging powerful, closed-weight robot foundation models (like Gemini Robotics On-Device) for complex real-world tasks. By operating entirely through managed SFT APIs, CLIFT allows downstream users to specialize these models without requiring access to their internal architecture, weights, or training pipelines, thus bridging the gap between cutting-edge RFM capabilities and practical application.
- **Advancing Humanoid Manipulation Capabilities:** CLIFT pushes the performance of humanoid manipulation policies beyond the ceiling typically imposed by demonstration-only supervised fine-tuning (SFT). For agile, contact-rich tasks on a real humanoid, the method achieved near-task mastery and facilitated the emergence of corrective and pre-manipulation behaviors not present in the original human demonstrations. This indicates a significant step towards more robust and adaptive humanoid control.
- **Novel Non-Invasive Closed-Loop Learning:** The introduction of CLIFT provides a novel approach to closed-loop policy improvement that is compatible with black-box, API-only models. By encoding reinforcement feedback directly into API-compatible supervised data through preference-calibrated rewards and retrieval-based advantage conditioning, CLIFT circumvents the need for invasive methods (e.g., direct access to gradients, critic networks) commonly used in reinforcement learning. This expands the toolkit for robot policy optimization under resource and access constraints.
- **Highlighting the Importance of Pretrained Foundation Model Strength:** The empirical comparison between GROD and 𝜋₀.₅, under identical adaptation pipelines, strongly suggests that the underlying capability of the pretrained foundation model is a critical determinant of the achievable performance ceiling. Even advanced, invasive adaptation techniques failed to close the performance gap between a weaker open-weight model and a stronger, API-only model. This finding emphasizes the value proposition of securing access to highly capable, extensively pretrained base models, even if it entails restricted access.
- **Practical Framework for On-Device Self-Improvement:** CLIFT offers a practical framework for incorporating real-world deployment experience into robot learning pipelines. The ability to automatically generate and utilize feedback from robot rollouts to iteratively improve policy performance addresses the inherent mismatch between training distributions and real-world deployment conditions. This is particularly relevant for embodiments where simulations might not fully capture complex physical interactions or controller dynamics.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The limitations acknowledged by the authors, such as the cost and safety concerns of real-robot rollouts, open avenues for future research into integrating control-aware world models. Such models could anticipate deployment outcomes, potentially reducing the need for extensive physical robot interaction per cycle while maintaining the benefits of closed-loop improvement. Expanding comparisons to a broader range of open-weight models would also further clarify the landscape of model capabilities and adaptation strategies.
