# Repetition as Reinforcement: Enhancing Sample Efficiency via Instant Episode Repetition in Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Instant Episode Repetition (IER) is a data-collection mechanism for off-policy RL: whenever an episode sets a new highest cumulative reward, the agent immediately re-executes that exact action sequence for RN subsequent episodes under new initial conditions, instead of sampling from the policy. Integrated into SAC and TD3 with no architecture changes, it improves sample efficiency and convergence speed on MuJoCo, DeepMind Control Suite, and a real-world gripper manipulation task.

## Problem

- Experience replay and prioritized replay reuse stored transitions only passively during policy updates, never altering how the agent behaves during ongoing environment interaction.
- Self-Imitation Learning re-samples high-return episodes from the buffer to shape gradients, but actions during rollout still come from the current policy, delaying reinforcement of success.
- Existing SIL extensions rely on expert demonstrations, hindsight relabeling, or intrinsic-motivation signals, limiting generality and leaving action-sequence-level repetition unexplored as a general mechanism.

## Method

- **Instant Episode Repetition (IER)**: tracks the running maximum episode reward and, upon a new best, stores the full action sequence and re-executes it verbatim for RN consecutive subsequent episodes.
- ==Interaction-level intervention==: repetition modifies only the data-collection/rollout phase, leaving reward function, network architecture, loss, and optimizer of ==SAC==/==TD3== completely untouched.
- ==Repetition-under-perturbed-initial-conditions== design: fixed action sequences replay from new reset-distribution start states, generating locally varied trajectories rather than duplicate transitions.

## Results

- IER-SAC beats SAC baseline on **6 of 8** continuous-control tasks and is comparable on the rest; IER-TD3 beats TD3 on **6 of 8** tasks and beats SIL-TD3 on **5 of 8**.
- Sensitivity sweep over RN in 0-7 shows mean AUC improvement peaks at **RN3** with **10.66%** gain over no-repetition, forming a unimodal curve across SAC and TD3.
- On the real-world dynamic cube-translation gripper task, IER-SAC converges faster with higher final reward than SAC and SIL-SAC, and IER-TD3 outperforms TD3 and SIL-TD3 with narrower margins.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Biologically-inspired repetition of whole action sequences, not just transition-level replay, can meaningfully raise sample efficiency without touching network or loss design.
> - Repetition strength is algorithm-dependent: deterministic policies like TD3 tolerate larger RN, while entropy-regularized SAC degrades faster under excessive repetition due to exploration conflict.
> - Self-imitation methods can reinforce suboptimal trajectories and underperform plain baselines when exploration is incomplete, a risk IER avoids by anchoring to verified high-reward sequences.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in various domains, including robotic manipulation, autonomous driving, and game playing. However, a persistent challenge limiting its widespread application is sample efficiency, referring to the large number of interactions typically required for an agent to learn effective policies. Unlike biological systems, which often learn complex behaviors from relatively few experiences, current RL agents frequently demand millions of interactions, making deployment in real-world environments with expensive or time-consuming interactions particularly difficult.

Existing approaches to improve sample efficiency in off-policy RL primarily involve reusing past experiences. Experience Replay (ER) and Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) store individual state-action-reward-next state transitions in a buffer and passively re-sample them during policy updates. While effective for data reuse, these methods do not directly influence the agent's behavior during environmental interaction. Self-Imitation Learning (SIL) extends this by encouraging agents to imitate past experiences that yielded high cumulative rewards. However, SIL also operates passively by sampling from the replay buffer and adjusting policy updates, rather than actively altering the agent's interaction strategy with the environment. More recent extensions of SIL have incorporated expert demonstrations, intrinsic motivation, or hindsight mechanisms, but these often introduce additional complexities or specific requirements like expert data or goal-conditioned settings, and generally retain a passive role in data collection.

This research draws inspiration from neuroscientific findings that highlight reward-driven repetition as a fundamental mechanism in biological learning. In biological systems, revisiting successful action sequences under varying conditions strengthens memory, consolidates skills, and enhances future performance. The paper identifies a gap in current RL methodologies: while experience is reused, the direct and immediate re-execution of successful *action sequences* during environmental interaction, analogous to biological repetition, is not systematically integrated. This work introduces Instant Episode Repetition (IER) as a mechanism to bridge this gap, aiming to actively reinforce valuable behaviors directly within the data collection process, thereby influencing the agent's interaction with the environment in a novel manner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from observations of efficient learning in biological systems. Biological agents frequently employ reward-driven repetition to strengthen memories, consolidate skills, and improve performance. For instance, successfully executing a complex motor skill often leads to its repetition, which refines the action pattern and stabilizes it at a neural level. In contrast, current reinforcement learning agents, while capable of learning complex tasks, often suffer from poor sample efficiency, requiring a vast number of interactions to achieve competence. This inefficiency is a significant barrier for deploying RL in real-world applications where data collection is expensive, time-consuming, or risky.

Building on this biological inspiration, the key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:

1. **Introduce Instant Episode Repetition (IER):** The foremost objective is to propose and define IER, a novel, biologically inspired mechanism designed to enhance sample efficiency in reinforcement learning. This mechanism aims to actively re-execute action sequences from high-reward episodes directly during environment interaction. This contrasts with existing replay-buffer methods, which passively reuse stored transitions during policy updates, and is intended to directly influence the data collection process itself.

2. **Facilitate Episode-Level Behavioral Consolidation:** The research seeks to enable the consolidation of successful behaviors at an episode level. Unlike approaches that treat experience as independent transition samples, IER focuses on repeating complete action sequences from high-reward episodes. This objective aims to shift the paradigm of experience reuse from passive replay-buffer sampling towards active, episode-level action replay, allowing agents to revisit and reinforce successful behaviors in the environment while concurrently collecting new exploratory transitions.

3. **Ensure Simple and General Integration:** A significant objective is to demonstrate that IER can be integrated into standard off-policy continuous-control algorithms without requiring architectural modifications to the underlying neural networks, alterations to loss functions, or changes to optimization procedures. This objective highlights the method's simplicity and generality, aiming for seamless compatibility with established algorithms like Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) and Twin-Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) by operating solely at the interaction level.

4. **Empirically Validate Performance Improvements:** The research aims to empirically evaluate the effectiveness of IER. This involves comprehensive testing on a diverse set of continuous control benchmarks, including tasks from MuJoCo, the DeepMind Control Suite, and a real-world robotic manipulation task. The objective is to demonstrate that immediate episode repetition improves learning efficiency and accelerates policy convergence when compared against standard off-policy baselines and self-imitation learning (SIL)-enhanced variants under consistent training settings.

Collectively, these objectives aim to develop and validate a straightforward yet effective mechanism that leverages structured action-sequence repetition to address the critical challenge of sample efficiency in reinforcement learning, drawing parallels with efficient learning observed in biological systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the introduction of Instant Episode Repetition (IER), a mechanism designed to directly modify the data collection phase in off-policy reinforcement learning. IER operates by temporarily reinforcing episodes that yield high rewards, without altering the reward function, loss formulation, network architecture, or optimization procedure of the underlying RL algorithm.

#### 3.1. Instant Episode Repetition (IER) Mechanism

The IER mechanism functions at the boundaries of episodes:
- **Reward Tracking:** At the conclusion of each episode, the agent calculates the total cumulative reward ($R_{ep}$) for that episode. This value is then compared against $R_{max}$, which represents the highest episode reward observed thus far during training.
- **Repetition Trigger:** If the current episode achieves a new maximum episode reward ($R_{ep} > R_{max}$), two actions are taken:
 1. The complete action sequence ($a^* = (a^*_0, \dots, a^*_T)$) from this successful episode is stored.
 2. The agent enters a "repetition phase" for a fixed number of subsequent episodes, determined by a hyperparameter denoted as $R_N$.
- **Action Selection During Repetition:** During the $R_N$ repetition episodes, the agent does not sample actions from its current policy. Instead, it deterministically executes the stored action sequence $a^*$.
- **Standard Exploitation:** If no new maximum-reward episode is detected, or once the $R_N$ repetition episodes are completed, the agent reverts to its standard behavior of sampling actions from its current policy, potentially with added exploration noise ($a_t \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot | s_t) + \epsilon_t$).
- **Experience Storage:** Critically, all transitions collected during both the repetition phase and the standard exploitation phase are inserted into the replay buffer. These transitions are then used for the conventional off-policy updates of the actor ($\pi_{\theta}$) and critic ($Q$) networks, maintaining the integrity of the underlying RL algorithm.

#### 3.2. Re-execution Under New Initial Conditions

A fundamental aspect of IER is that the re-execution of a stored action sequence ($a^*$) does not result in identical, duplicated episodes. Even in deterministic simulation environments, each new episode typically begins from an initial state sampled from a reset distribution ($p_0$). Therefore, re-executing $a^*$ under new initial conditions leads to trajectories that, while guided by the same actions, unfold through different state sequences. This process generates a family of related episodes in the vicinity of the previously successful behavior, increasing the sampling density in empirically high-reward regions of the state-action space without simply cloning past experience. In stochastic environments, or real-world robotic systems, additional variability from factors like sensor noise, contact dynamics, and actuation uncertainty further diversifies these re-executed trajectories, promoting robustness and generalization.

#### 3.3. Integration with Off-Policy Algorithms

IER is designed for simple and general integration into off-policy actor-critic RL algorithms. The study specifically integrates IER into TD3 (Twin-Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient) and SAC (Soft Actor-Critic), yielding IER-TD3 and IER-SAC variants. The method operates exclusively at the interaction level and does not necessitate any modifications to the network architectures, loss functions, or optimization procedures of these base algorithms.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

The methodology includes a comprehensive evaluation across diverse environments and against relevant baselines:
- **Baseline Algorithms:** Standard implementations of TD3 and SAC, alongside their Self-Imitation Learning (SIL) enhanced variants (SIL-TD3 and SIL-SAC). The standard SIL formulation was chosen to avoid confounding variables from more complex SIL extensions.
- **Simulation Environments:** Eight continuous control tasks from widely recognized benchmarks:
 - **MuJoCo:** Ant-v4, HalfCheetah-v4, Humanoid-v4, Hopper-v4. These are characterized by dynamic locomotion and varying levels of complexity.
 - **DeepMind Control Suite (DMC):** Walker-Walk, Cheetah-Run, Cartpole-Swingup, Finger-TurnHard. These emphasize precision control and robustness across different task types.
 All simulated tasks used low-dimensional, vector-based observations.
- **Real-World Task:** A dynamic object translation task involving a robotic gripper performing in-hand manipulation. This environment introduced real-world stochasticity, sensor noise, and hardware constraints. An automated reset mechanism facilitated efficient data collection.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Performance was assessed using learning curves showing mean episodic return over multiple random seeds, and for sensitivity analysis, the normalized Area Under the Learning Curve (AUC).
- **Sensitivity Analysis:** An extensive analysis was conducted on the hyperparameter $R_N$, varying its value from 0 (no repetition) to 7, to understand its impact on learning performance across tasks and algorithms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Instant Episode Repetition (IER) demonstrated consistent improvements in learning efficiency and convergence across simulated and real-world continuous control tasks.

#### 4.1. Performance in Simulated Environments

- **IER-SAC Performance:** IER-SAC generally outperformed the standard SAC baseline in six out of eight tasks and showed comparable performance in the remaining two. Notable gains in dynamic locomotion tasks such as Ant-v4, HalfCheetah-v4, Walker-Walk, and Cheetah-Run were observed, suggesting that IER effectively reinforces coordinated and temporally extended movement patterns, leading to faster convergence and higher long-term rewards.
- **IER-TD3 Performance:** IER-TD3 similarly surpassed standard TD3 in six out of eight tasks and exceeded SIL-TD3 in five tasks, with significant improvements reported for HalfCheetah-v4, Humanoid-v4, and Walker-Walk. These results further corroborated that IER's repetition mechanism strengthens beneficial trajectory segments, contributing to enhanced cumulative reward.
- **Comparison with SIL:** Self-Imitation Learning (SIL) variants (SIL-SAC and SIL-TD3) performed competitively in some specific environments, such as HalfCheetah-v4 and Walker-Walk. However, their performance was inconsistent, with some instances showing performance below the standard baselines in more unstable tasks like Humanoid-v4 and Cartpole-Swingup. This aligns with known challenges of SIL, where the reinforcement of suboptimal trajectories can occur if exploration is incomplete.
- **Algorithm-Specific Differences:** The relative performance gains for IER-TD3 were generally smaller than those observed for IER-SAC. This difference is attributed to the inherent exploration mechanisms of the base algorithms. SAC, with its entropy regularization, promotes broader exploration, which appears to complement IER by reinforcing effective behaviors without excessively limiting policy diversity. In contrast, TD3's more deterministic policy updates might be more sensitive to repeated behavior sequences, potentially leading to a higher risk of premature convergence if repetition is overused. Despite this, IER-TD3 still yielded consistent improvements.

#### 4.2. Real-World Evaluation

- Experiments on a physical robotic manipulation task (dynamic object translation via in-hand manipulation) were conducted to assess IER's robustness under real-world stochasticity, including sensor noise, contact inconsistencies, and actuation delays.
- **IER-SAC in Real-World:** IER-SAC demonstrated faster convergence and achieved higher final performance compared to both standard SAC and SIL-SAC.
- **IER-TD3 in Real-World:** IER-TD3 also outperformed TD3 and SIL-TD3, though with marginally narrower performance differences. SIL-TD3 exhibited an initial temporary improvement but quickly saturated, allowing both TD3 and IER-TD3 to eventually surpass it.
- These findings indicate that the repetition strategy implemented by IER remained effective in a real-world setting, improving data efficiency and promoting more stable learning dynamics despite environmental complexities.

#### 4.3. Sensitivity Analysis of the Repetition Number (RN)

- A sensitivity analysis examined the impact of $R_N$, the number of consecutive repetitions of a high-reward episode, on performance across all tasks. Performance was measured by the normalized Area Under the Learning Curve (AUC).
- **Aggregate Trends:** The mean percentage improvement in AUC ($\Delta$AUC%) across all tasks, averaged for both SAC and TD3, peaked at $R_N=3$ (10.66%), with $R_N=2$ and $R_N=4$ also showing strong gains. Performance tended to decline at larger repetition levels, suggesting a unimodal relationship where moderate repetition provides the strongest learning acceleration, while excessively high repetition can reduce state diversity.
- **Task-Specific Trends and Algorithm Sensitivity:**
 - Environments with higher instability or reward variance (e.g., Finger-Turn-Hard, Walker-Walk) generally benefited more significantly from repetition.
 - The optimal $R_N$ varied across tasks. For instance, Walker-Walk and Cheetah-Run often showed improving performance with increasing $R_N$, peaking at intermediate levels ($R_N=3$ to $R_N=5$).
 - TD3 typically saw peak performance at moderate $R_N$ values ($R_N=2$ to $R_N=3$), while SAC tended to benefit from smaller $R_N$ values and exhibited greater sensitivity to larger repetition levels. This further highlighted the interplay between IER and the base algorithm's exploration mechanisms.
- **Conclusion on RN:** While the optimal $R_N$ can vary, moderate repetition (typically $R_N=2$ to $R_N=4$) consistently achieved competitive and near-peak performance. The analysis also revealed that $R_N=0$ (no repetition) consistently resulted in weaker performance, underscoring the general benefit of the IER mechanism.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Instant Episode Repetition (IER), a mechanism that modifies the data collection process in reinforcement learning, drawing inspiration from biological learning principles. The work presents a simple yet effective approach to improve sample efficiency, which addresses a fundamental challenge in the broader applicability of RL.

The primary significance of IER lies in its novel intervention during the agent's interaction with the environment. Unlike conventional methods that passively reuse stored experience, IER actively re-executes entire action sequences from successful episodes. This approach facilitates episode-level behavioral consolidation, which can strengthen memory and stabilize skill acquisition in a manner analogous to how repetition contributes to learning in biological systems. By concentrating sampling density around empirically rewarding regions of the state-action space, IER can lead to more efficient exploration and exploitation.

The integration of IER is designed to be simple and general, requiring no architectural modifications to existing off-policy algorithms like SAC and TD3, nor changes to their loss functions or optimization procedures. This ease of integration broadens its potential applicability across a wide range of current and future continuous-control RL frameworks. The empirical results, demonstrating consistent improvements in data efficiency and accelerated convergence across diverse simulated benchmarks (MuJoCo, DeepMind Control Suite) and a real-world robotic manipulation task, underscore the practical utility of this approach. The method's effectiveness in a stochastic real-world environment highlights its robustness against typical challenges like sensor noise and actuation uncertainties.

The potential impact of IER is significant, particularly in contexts where data collection is costly or time-consuming. For instance, in real-world robotics, where physical interactions are expensive and potentially damaging, accelerating the learning process can substantially reduce development time and resources. By improving sample efficiency, IER could enable RL agents to learn complex motor skills with fewer interactions, thereby facilitating faster deployment and broader adoption of RL in practical applications, including autonomous systems, industrial automation, and other domains requiring sophisticated control policies. Furthermore, this research contributes to the growing body of work that explores biologically inspired mechanisms to enhance artificial intelligence, potentially opening new avenues for developing more sample-efficient and robust RL algorithms.
