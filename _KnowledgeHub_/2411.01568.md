# Addressing Failures in Robotics using Vision-Based Language Models (VLMs) and Behavior Trees (BT)

> [!summary] Summary
> Combines a vision language model with behavior trees so robots handle failures they were never programmed for. Before execution, GPT-4 inspects scene images plus the tree, skills and conditions, predicts failure, names the missing precondition, and proposes a recovery skill template; a reactive planner rebuilds the tree. Across simulated peg-in-hole, lift and door tasks, detection and recovery succeeded in every trial.

## Problem

- Human intervention, manual failure analysis and scripted recovery routines are slow, expertise-heavy and rigid, so they cover only anticipated disturbances in controlled settings.
- Prior behavior-tree and motion-generator recovery work assumes failure detection and identification are already solved, requiring the failure to be known in advance.
- Existing automated recovery handles only known failures with predefined solutions, leaving no mechanism to synthesize new conditions or skills for unforeseen ones.

## Method

- ==VLM monitoring==: ==GPT-4== is queried before execution with environment images, the ==behavior tree==, and the available skill and condition libraries, then predicts task failure and its cause.
- ==Condition generation==: the model names the missing precondition, such as hole free, and a ==backchaining reactive planner== regenerates the behavior tree with that precondition attached to the failing skill.
- ==Skill template generation==: when no recovery skill exists, the VLM emits a structured template, such as a push skill for an ungraspable obstacle, filtered by ==gripper-affordance feasibility checks==.

## Results

- Across **10** trials per scenario on peg-in-hole with small and large obstacles, lift and door-opening tasks, detection and recovery were consistent at **100%**.
- Vision ablation: the VLM hit **100%** on all four scenarios; the text-only LLM matched it on three but fell to **30%** on peg-in-hole with a large obstacle.
- Adding skill-feasibility checks raised the text-only LLM from **30%** to **60%** on the large-obstacle case, still short of the VLM's **100%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visual input matters mainly where geometry decides feasibility: text-only reasoning matched vision until obstacle size determined whether grasping was physically possible.
> - Writing a recovery back as a precondition in the tree turns one-off repair into persistent policy, so the same failure is pre-empted later.
> - Validation is thin: simulation only, four hand-built scenarios, 10 trials each, and generated skill templates still need manual completion before execution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is continuously advancing, enabling systems to perform increasingly complex tasks in controlled environments. However, transitioning these systems into dynamic and less structured settings, such as small-batch manufacturing, introduces significant challenges, particularly concerning failure management. Traditional approaches to managing failures in robotics often involve human intervention, detailed failure analysis by experts, or reliance on pre-defined automated recovery strategies. While these methods have their utility, they are limited by factors such as time consumption, the need for specialized knowledge, or a lack of flexibility when confronting unforeseen or "unknown" failures.

Previous research, including the authors' own work, has explored automated recovery behaviors modeled as robotic skills with parameters, preconditions, and postconditions, executed through Behavior Trees (BTs) and Motion Generators (BTMG) policy representation. These methods have demonstrated success in optimizing recovery policies using reinforcement learning and adapting parameters to task variations. However, these prior approaches typically operate under two primary assumptions: that failure detection and identification are already resolved, and that solutions exist for these known failures. This implies a prerequisite for prior knowledge of potential failure modes, restricting their applicability to novel or unpredicted scenarios.

This paper addresses these limitations by integrating Vision-Language Models (VLMs) into a BT-based framework. VLMs represent a rapidly evolving area in artificial intelligence, capable of processing and interpreting both visual data and natural language inputs. Their application in robotics is expanding, with studies demonstrating their utility in tasks such as scene interpretation, object recognition, and the generation of control skills based on multimodal cues. Recent systems like ReplanVLM and AHA have explored VLMs for reasoning over failures and generating dynamic solutions. By leveraging VLMs, this work aims to bridge the gap between existing BT-based recovery strategies and the need for autonomous detection, identification, and generation of recovery mechanisms for unknown failures, thereby enhancing the robustness and adaptability of robotic systems in dynamic environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate an approach that enhances the failure management capabilities of robotic systems by integrating Vision Language Models (VLMs) with Behavior Trees (BTs). This overarching goal is driven by several specific objectives and motivations:

- **Address Limitations of Current Robotic Systems in Managing Unknown Failures:** Modern robotic systems are often adept at handling known failure modes for which pre-existing recovery strategies have been designed. However, they typically lack the ability to autonomously manage novel or unforeseen failures (anomalies). Such unknown failures can lead to significant operational disruptions, costly delays, and safety risks, especially in collaborative or dynamic environments. The paper aims to overcome this critical limitation by enabling robots to react intelligently to situations they have not been explicitly programmed to handle.
- **Utilize VLMs for Autonomous Failure Detection and Identification:** A core motivation is to move beyond the assumption that failure detection and identification are pre-solved problems. The authors propose to employ VLMs as a real-time monitoring tool during task execution. The objective is for VLMs to perceive the task environment, recognize when a task is not progressing as expected (detection), and subsequently articulate the nature and cause of the failure (identification) in a way that can be integrated into the robot's decision-making process.
- **Generate Adaptive Recovery Strategies through VLM-BT Integration:** Following the detection and identification of a failure, the objective is for VLMs to contribute directly to the system's recovery capabilities. This involves using VLMs to generate missing conditions or new skill templates that are necessary to address the observed failure. These VLM-generated elements are then intended to be incorporated into the robot’s Behavior Tree policy, allowing the system to autonomously adapt its execution strategy. This process aims to ensure that once an unknown failure is encountered and addressed, the system learns from the experience and can handle similar occurrences in future tasks without requiring human intervention.
- **Enhance System Robustness and Reduce Dependence on Prior Knowledge:** By enabling autonomous failure detection, identification, and adaptive recovery strategy generation, the research seeks to significantly enhance the overall robustness and resilience of robotic systems. The motivation is to reduce the reliance on extensive pre-programming for every conceivable failure scenario and minimize the need for human expertise during unexpected events, thereby fostering more autonomous and adaptable robotic operations.
- **Validate the Proposed Approach in Simulation:** A crucial objective is to experimentally demonstrate the practical effectiveness of the VLM-BT integration. This involves conducting simulations across various failure scenarios to assess the system's ability to consistently detect, identify, and recover from unknown failures, as well as to evaluate the quality and feasibility of the conditions and skill templates generated by the VLMs.

In summary, the motivation stems from the operational gap between current robotic capabilities and the requirements of dynamic, uncertain environments, where the ability to autonomously manage unknown failures is paramount for efficiency, safety, and system autonomy. The paper posits VLMs as a viable technology to bridge this gap when integrated with established control architectures like Behavior Trees.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology extends an existing robotics framework by integrating Vision Language Models (VLMs) to address unknown failures. The core of the approach involves using VLMs for real-time monitoring, failure detection, identification, and the generation of adaptive recovery components, which are then incorporated into a Behavior Tree (BT) structure via a reactive planner.

#### 3.1. Core Components

The approach relies on three primary computational components:

- **Behavior Trees (BTs):** BTs serve as the hierarchical model for task execution. They are composed of control-flow nodes (e.g., Sequence, Fallback/Selector) that manage the execution flow and return statuses (success, failure, running), and execution nodes (leaf nodes) representing skills or conditions. Skills perform specific actions, while conditions evaluate environmental states. BTs are utilized for their modularity and flexibility in dynamic robotic applications.
- **Reactive Planner:** This component is responsible for dynamically generating and updating BTs. It employs a backchaining approach, iteratively selecting skills whose postconditions satisfy a given goal, taking into account their preconditions. This recursive process constructs a BT that can achieve the specified task, allowing for dynamic adaptation to environmental changes without extensive re-planning. The planner ensures that new conditions or skills generated by the VLM can be integrated into the existing BT structure.
- **Vision Language Models (VLMs):** VLMs are central to the novel aspects of this approach. They combine visual data (images) with natural language inputs, enabling sophisticated scene interpretation, object recognition, and the generation of textual outputs. In this framework, VLMs are leveraged for their ability to understand complex environments, reason about task progression, and generate descriptions or instructions based on visual cues.

#### 3.2. Failure Detection and Identification Process

The system's ability to handle unknown failures begins with two stages:

- **Failure Detection:** This involves recognizing when a robotic task cannot be completed as intended due to unforeseen disturbances or errors (e.g., hardware malfunctions, environmental obstacles). Detection is primarily achieved by monitoring sensor data (e.g., camera feeds) and comparing the observed environment or task state against the expected postconditions of the currently executing skill. For instance, in a "peg-in-hole" task, if the "insert" skill's postcondition (peg inserted) is not met, a failure is detected.
- **Failure Identification:** Once a failure is detected, the VLM is tasked with describing the failure. This involves understanding *why* the task could not be completed and expressing this in terms of existing system conditions or, if necessary, generating new ones. For example, if a peg-in-hole task fails because an object blocks the hole, the VLM might identify the missing precondition for the "insert" skill as "Not any obstacle at hole."

#### 3.3. Monitoring and Adaptation using VLM

The VLM plays a continuous monitoring role:

- **Pre-execution Assessment:** Before a task begins, the VLM is provided with images of the task environment, the current BT structure, and a list of available skills and conditions. The VLM then assesses the likelihood of task success. If it anticipates a failure, it identifies its probable cause.
- **In-execution Monitoring and Suggestion:** During task execution, if a failure occurs (as detected by the system), the VLM is re-queried. Based on the current visual state and the BT context, the VLM identifies the specific failure and suggests a missing condition that, if met, would prevent the failure. If a suitable recovery skill is not available in the robot's library, the VLM suggests an appropriate recovery skill template.
- **BT Update Mechanism:** When the VLM identifies a missing condition or generates a skill template, this information is used to update the BT. For a missing condition (e.g., "hole free" if an obstacle is present), this condition is added as a precondition to the relevant skill (e.g., "insert"). The reactive planner then regenerates the BT, incorporating this new condition. This modification ensures that similar failures encountered in the future will trigger the newly defined condition, potentially leading to a different, successful branch of the BT. If a recovery skill is missing (e.g., "push" to remove a large obstacle), the VLM generates a structured skill template. While this template initially requires some manual input to be fully realized, the long-term goal is to fully automate this skill generation process.

#### 3.4. Experiments and Validation

The proposed approach was validated through simulations using robosuite and OpenAI's GPT-4, testing three distinct robotic tasks, each featuring unknown failure scenarios:

- **Peg-in-Hole Task:** Two scenarios were tested: (A) a small obstacle inside the hole, and (B) a large obstacle positioned in front of the hole.
- **Lift Task:** An additional, unexpected cube was placed on top of the target object, hindering the lift operation.
- **Door Opening Task:** The robot attempted to open a door without first turning the handle, which was a missing precondition.

For all experiments, the VLM model parameters (temperature and top p) were set to 0.1 to elicit deterministic and focused outputs, thereby reducing randomness in responses.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the performance of the Vision Language Model (VLM) in conjunction with Behavior Trees (BTs) across several failure scenarios using three key metrics: consistency in failure detection and recovery, the importance of visual input, and considerations for skill feasibility.

#### 4.1. Evaluation Metrics and Parameters

The evaluation metrics focused on:
- **Consistency:** The VLM's reliability in consistently detecting failures and suggesting appropriate recovery actions across multiple trials.
- **Vision Importance:** An ablation study comparing the performance of the VLM (with visual input) against a Language Model (LLM, without visual input) to quantify the contribution of visual information.
- **Skill Feasibility:** The capacity of the models to suggest recovery skills that are practically executable by the robot, considering its capabilities (e.g., gripper affordance, object location).

All experiments were conducted with VLM model parameters for temperature and top p set to 0.1. This configuration was selected to reduce the stochasticity of the model's outputs, promoting more deterministic and concentrated responses.

#### 4.2. Consistency of Failure Detection and Recovery

The VLM demonstrated consistent performance in detecting and recovering from failures across all simulated tasks and scenarios. Over 10 trials for each scenario (Peg-in-Hole with small obstacle, Peg-in-Hole with large obstacle, Lift Task, and Door Opening Task), the VLM achieved a 100% success rate in both detecting the occurrence of a failure and identifying an appropriate recovery strategy. This indicates that the integrated VLM-BT system reliably recognized when a task deviated from its expected progression and formulated corrective actions.

#### 4.3. Vision Importance Ablation Study

An ablation study was performed to assess the specific impact of visual input on the model's ability to handle failures. This involved comparing the VLM (which processes both visual and language data) with an LLM (which only processes language data, lacking direct visual perception).

- **Scenarios where both models performed comparably:** In the Peg-in-Hole task with a small obstacle, the Lift task, and the Door Opening task, both the VLM and the LLM achieved 100% accuracy in identifying the failure and suggesting a recovery. This suggests that for certain types of failures, where the underlying logic or missing condition can be inferred primarily from textual context or simplified environmental descriptions, direct visual input may not be the sole determinant of success.
- **Scenarios highlighting the importance of vision:** A notable difference emerged in the Peg-in-Hole task involving a large obstacle. In this more complex scenario:
 - The VLM, with its visual understanding, achieved 100% accuracy in correctly identifying the failure and proposing a recovery strategy.
 - The LLM, without visual input, reached only 30% accuracy when skill feasibility considerations were not explicitly applied. When skill feasibility was incorporated into the LLM's reasoning, its accuracy improved to 60%.
 This discrepancy indicates that for tasks requiring a detailed understanding of spatial relationships, object sizes, and their physical interactions (e.g., determining if an obstacle is too large to be grasped or needs to be pushed), direct visual input provided by the VLM is a critical factor. The LLM's limited performance, even with feasibility checks, suggests a deficit in comprehending the physical constraints purely from textual descriptions.

#### 4.4. Skill Feasibility Considerations

The evaluation also considered the feasibility of the recovery skills suggested by the models.

- The VLM consistently generated feasible recovery skills, particularly in complex scenarios like the Peg-in-Hole task with a large obstacle. Its ability to process visual information allowed it to assess object affordances and spatial arrangements more accurately, leading to suggestions such as a "push" skill when a "grasp" skill would be impractical for a large obstacle. This autonomous generation of feasible skills is a key finding.
- While incorporating skill feasibility improved the LLM's performance in the Peg-in-Hole (large obstacle) scenario (from 30% to 60%), it still did not match the VLM's 100% accuracy. This further underscores the VLM's advantage in reasoning about physical interactions and constraints through visual perception, which is essential for generating truly executable recovery behaviors.

In summary, the results demonstrate that the VLM-integrated approach offers high consistency in failure detection and recovery. Crucially, visual input significantly enhances the system's ability to address complex, spatially-dependent unknown failures, leading to the generation of more feasible and effective recovery strategies compared to language-only models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a method for integrating Vision Language Models (VLMs) with Behavior Trees (BTs) to enhance autonomous failure management in robotic systems. This integration contributes to the field by addressing existing limitations in how robots handle unforeseen operational challenges.

The primary significance of this work lies in its approach to **autonomous detection, identification, and recovery from unknown failures.** Traditional robotic systems often depend on pre-programmed recovery strategies for known failure modes or necessitate human intervention for novel issues. By enabling VLMs to monitor task execution, detect anomalies, identify their causes, and generate missing conditions or skill templates, the system moves towards a greater degree of autonomy. This capability is critical for robots operating in dynamic, unstructured, or partially known environments, such as advanced manufacturing or service robotics, where the complete spectrum of potential failures cannot be anticipated and pre-coded.

A key impact is the **enhanced adaptability and robustness of robotic systems.** The capacity to update the BT policy with VLM-generated conditions or skill templates means that the robot can learn from encountered failures. This adaptive learning allows the system to autonomously address similar failures in future tasks, reducing the need for costly human supervision and intervention. This translates into increased operational uptime and improved efficiency, particularly in scenarios involving small-batch production or highly variable tasks where robot downtime due to unexpected issues is a significant concern.

The research also highlights the **critical role of multimodal intelligence** (vision and language) in robotic problem-solving. The ablation study demonstrated that while language models can infer solutions for simpler failures, vision-based reasoning is instrumental for complex scenarios involving spatial understanding and physical interaction. This finding reinforces the growing recognition of VLMs as essential tools for robots to interpret their environment comprehensively and reason about the feasibility of actions.

In terms of future impact, the development of mechanisms to generate not just skill templates but **directly executable and feasible skills** will further reduce manual input and accelerate the robot's ability to adapt. This could lead to a paradigm shift where robots proactively evolve their operational policies based on real-world experiences, rather than relying solely on human programmers for error handling. Furthermore, the potential to fine-tune open-source VLM models could democratize access to such advanced failure management capabilities, making robust autonomous robotics more accessible to a broader range of applications and research initiatives.

Overall, this work lays groundwork for more resilient and intelligent robotic systems capable of navigating uncertainty and adapting to unforeseen circumstances, thereby contributing to the broader goal of truly autonomous and self-sufficient robotics.
