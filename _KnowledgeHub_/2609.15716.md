# Continuous Manifold-Decomposed Impedance Retargeting for Contact-Rich Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> CMDIR turns fixed-impedance contact-rich demonstrations into continuous variable-impedance controller fields. It represents an evolving task frame plus spline-parameterized gain instructions, constrains candidates by demonstration-relative displacement, impulse and sensitivity criteria, and solves them with a compiled Quality-to-Fast optimizer. Over 225 real trials it raises task retention and cuts force fluctuation and peak force versus discrete pointwise retargeting, with 5.8-9.4x faster solves.

## Problem

- Prior impedance retargeting worked pointwise per sample, so extending it to a continuous field leaves basis transport, parameter rates and overlapping response windows unaccounted for.
- Moving task frames and mismatch between control and physical metrics couple responses even in orthogonal task coordinates, which idealized unit-inertia dynamics models ignore.
- Per-demonstration constrained optimization is slow, and existing warm-start or active-set acceleration transfers numerical values rather than reusable solver structure across new demonstrations.

## Method

- **Continuous TMIR**: an evolving ==Work-Exertion-Support== metric-orthonormal frame paired with seven cubic ==B-spline== fields over stiffness fractions, damping ratio and command offsets, queryable at policy rate.
- Demo-relative **Compromise dynamics** keep projected ==operational-space inertia== and frame transport, yielding ==NDRI== displacement and ==NIRI== impulse constraints plus an ==NSRI== perturbation-sensitivity objective inside **C-MPO**'s ==augmented Lagrangian==.
- **Quality-to-Fast MPO** compiles a solver structure from development paths in a frozen finite space, re-instantiates it per demonstration, and certifies it; **MetricReg** turns overlapping policy predictions into executable structured actions.

## Results

- Across **225** real trials, full **CMDIR** lifts task proxy from **0.696** to **0.847** (curved wiping), **0.796** to **0.835** (planar) and **0.929** to **0.960** (pushing) over discrete MDIR.
- Curved-wiping pose deviation drops **0.617%** to **0.245%**, with force fluctuation **-25.74%** and peak force **-28.05%** versus the demonstration; Compromise beats Ideal dynamics on task proxy **0.869** vs **0.775**.
- FastMPO cuts solve time **5.8-9.4x** with task-proxy differences within **0.022**; downstream, CMDIR supervision reaches **29/30** simulated and **6/10** real successes versus **27/30** and **9/10** ordinary.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Lifting pointwise criteria directly to continuous fields fails: spline coefficients couple overlapping response windows, so constraints must be redefined on the shared field.
> - Comparing a candidate against the demonstration about the same trajectory cancels zeroth-order terms, letting cheap first-order inertia and frame-transport corrections capture metric mismatch.
> - Gentler contact does not imply better policies: real-robot completion fell under structured supervision, and jointly changed data, actions and losses forbid component-level attribution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot wiping a curved surface must keep advancing the task while maintaining contact without provoking excessive force. High impedance can amplify contact transients. Simply lowering every gain can weaken progress and support. CMDIR turns a fixed-impedance demonstration into a smoothly varying controller, changing task-relevant directions selectively rather than softening everything uniformly. Across 225 real-robot controller trials, Full CMDIR (M5) improves the task proxy and lowers mean pose deviation, force fluctuation, and peak force relative to discrete MDIR (M1).

Representative curved-wiping rollout: source demonstration (top) and Full CMDIR (bottom). Red arrows indicate measured contact force; green ellipsoids visualize local impedance geometry. This is a representative rollout, not an aggregate result.

### 2. From fixed gains to a changing controller

Impedance is the controller's coupling of motion and force. The method follows three evolving roles: Work for progress, Exertion for interaction, and Support for the remaining support response. These task channels are changing directions, not fixed XYZ axes. The method adjusts their controller instructions over time. The closest prior work, discrete MDIR ([arXiv:2607.29271](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.29271)), introduced pointwise task-channel retargeting.

TMIR represents an evolving frame and its stiffness, damping, and offsets as a continuous field. This field can be queried over the source trajectory up to its sampling rate.

To judge a candidate controller, the response model retains projected physical inertia and moving-frame transport. It then compares candidate and source responses along the same demonstration. One criterion limits changes to Work displacement. Another limits changes to the reaction impulse needed to preserve nonmoving contact directions. A sensitivity term moderates responses to perturbations within those retention limits. The model is first-order and demo-relative. Its criteria are modeled response criteria, not guarantees about every real-world force.

### 3. Two decisive comparisons

Schematic. Left: "Ideal" and "Compromise" response models. Work, Exertion, and Support feed displacement retention (NDRI), impulse retention (NIRI), and disturbance-response moderation (NSRI). Right: development paths cross an illustrative objective surface, followed by a compiled solver structure re-instantiated per task. Compilation uses three development demonstrations.

The study ran on a real robot across planar wiping, curved wiping, and box pushing. It used five source demonstrations per task, five method variants, and three executions per source–method case, for 225 trials. The method variants include:

- M1: discrete MDIR.
- M3 and M4: C-MPO with Ideal and Compromise dynamics, respectively.
- M5: Compromise plus FastMPO.

For wiping, the task proxy combines cosine similarity of spatial coverage and a square-root-coverage-weighted normal-force map. For pushing, it is object displacement relative to the demonstration. Pose deviation is mean SE(3) deviation, with rotation scaled at 0.05 m/rad and normalized by demonstration path length. Force fluctuation is the mean of within-window standard deviations of bias-corrected force magnitude during Stable contact. Peak force is its Stable maximum.

**Full CMDIR versus discrete MDIR.** In curved wiping, moving from M1 to M5 changes the source-level means as follows:

- Task proxy rises from 0.696 to 0.847.
- Mean pose deviation falls from 0.617% to 0.245%.
- Force-fluctuation change goes from −3.93% to −25.74%.
- Peak-force change goes from −2.64% to −28.05%.

These are means, and the force changes are relative to the paired demonstration. Planar wiping and pushing show the same direction of mean task-proxy improvement.

**Ideal versus Compromise dynamics.** M3 and M4 both use C-MPO. M4 uses Compromise dynamics, which retain projected physical inertia and moving-frame transport. In curved wiping, the task proxy rises from 0.775 to 0.869, and pose deviation falls from 0.320% to 0.235%. Mean task proxy also improves in planar wiping and pushing. However, Ideal has lower mean force fluctuation and peak force in planar wiping. The authors interpret this comparison as support for retaining those modeled effects to help task preservation. It tests the effects jointly and does not identify which effect is solely responsible.

### 4. A controller, then supervision

Continuous retargeting makes selective changes to a demonstrated controller possible while explicitly preserving modeled task responses. The resulting field can also supervise imitation learning. Downstream results show learnability, not policy superiority or proof that any one supervision component caused the force changes.

The controller study assumes matched environments. Changing surface placement, friction, or tool contact can alter responses. Moderation varies across demonstrations, and no uniformly preferable impedance schedule is established.
