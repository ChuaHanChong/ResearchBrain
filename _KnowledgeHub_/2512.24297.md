# Figure It Out: Improve the Frontier of Reasoning with Executable Visual States

> [!summary] Summary
> FIGR, developed by WeChat AI, Tencent Inc., enhances large language models' reasoning by integrating executable visual construction into multi-turn inference via reinforcement learning. It improves problem-solving involving implicit spatial and geometric relationships, achieving an an average accuracy of 74.11% on mathematical benchmarks, marking a 6.77% gain over its base policy and over 11% on complex geometric reasoning tasks.

## Problem

- Text-only reasoning methods (e.g., Chain-of-Thought) struggle with complex problems requiring implicit spatial or geometric understanding, leading to errors due to the high cognitive burden on internal representations.
- Unconstrained visual generation models (Large Vision-Language Models) lack precise control over their visual outputs, often generating noisy or spatially imprecise images that are unreliable for tasks demanding fine-grained geometric accuracy.
- Tool-augmented visual reasoning models operate on given images with fixed sets of predefined transformations, preventing them from autonomously constructing novel, task-specific diagrams or abstract visual representations from scratch.

## Method

- Introduces ==executable visual construction== within the reasoning loop, where the model dynamically generates code to construct and refine diagrams, with a sandboxed interpreter providing precise visual and textual feedback.
- Employs a reinforcement learning procedure with ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== to train the model, optimizing long-horizon, multi-turn reasoning trajectories using a combined reward for final answer correctness, output format, and visual construction appropriateness.
- Utilizes an ==Adaptive Reward Mechanism (ARM)== to guide purposeful visual thinking by conditionally rewarding diagrammatic reasoning based on a predicted suitability for the given problem, encouraging effective use of visual construction.

## Results

- FIGR achieved an average accuracy of **74.11%** across seven mathematical reasoning benchmarks (e.g., AIME, BeyondAIME, MATH 500), representing a **6.77%** improvement over its base policy and a **4.88%** gain over a text-only reinforcement learning baseline.
- Demonstrated substantial performance gains on benchmarks requiring complex geometric and structural understanding, with increases of **13.12%** on AIME 2025 and **11.00%** on BeyondAIME.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the Adaptive Reward Mechanism is critical for guiding purposeful visual construction, and that explicit, executable visual feedback is superior to passively injected visual information or static image generation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human reasoning for complex problems involving spatial or structural relationships benefits from externalizing information visually, a capability that AI models need to emulate more effectively.
> - Active, executable construction and iterative refinement of visual states within the reasoning loop are crucial for enforcing geometric consistency and providing actionable feedback, unlike static or passively injected images.
> - End-to-end reinforcement learning, particularly with an adaptive reward mechanism, can effectively teach AI models when and how to judiciously invoke multimodal (visual construction) capabilities without relying on extensive supervised visual reasoning annotations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of advanced reasoning capabilities in AI models is a central challenge in artificial intelligence, particularly when problems involve implicit spatial, geometric, or structural relationships. FIGR emerges from a critical examination of existing paradigms for complex reasoning, each with inherent limitations:

- **Text-only Reasoning:** Approaches like Chain-of-Thought (CoT), Program-of-Thought (PoT), and Tree-of-Thought (ToT) have significantly improved LLMs' ability to perform multi-step symbolic and arithmetic reasoning. However, as the paper highlights, these models rely on implicitly maintaining all intermediate states within their symbolic representations. This becomes a severe bottleneck for tasks requiring geometric or spatial understanding, where subtle misinterpretations of structural constraints can lead to cascading algebraic errors. The cognitive burden on internal representations is simply too high.

- **Unconstrained Visual Generation Models:** More recent Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) and unified multimodal architectures aim to make spatial relationships explicit by generating images alongside text during the reasoning process. While conceptually appealing, these models typically lack precise control over the visual content they produce. The generated images are often "noisy or spatially imprecise," and without grounding in executable constraints, even minor visual inconsistencies can propagate, limiting their reliability for tasks demanding fine-grained geometric accuracy. These are represented by models like Bagel-7B-MoT and Qwen-Image.

- **Tool-Augmented Visual Reasoning Models:** Another line of research integrates external visual tools or predefined APIs (e.g., zooming, cropping, drawing auxiliary lines) into LVLMs. These methods offer improved controllability and execution fidelity by delegating visual operations to explicit tools. However, their fundamental limitation is that they operate on *given* images and a fixed set of predefined transformations. They cannot autonomously construct *new*, task-specific diagrams or abstract visual representations from scratch, restricting their applicability to dynamic reasoning problems. Examples include DeepEyes and Chain-of-Focus.

FIGR directly addresses these limitations by introducing a novel paradigm: **executable visual construction within the reasoning loop**. Instead of merely describing visual states in text, generating uncontrolled images, or manipulating predefined images with fixed tools, FIGR generates *executable code* that constructs and refines diagrams dynamically. This approach bridges symbolic reasoning with visual rendering, creating precise, revisable, and stateful visual feedback. By doing so, FIGR aims to replicate how humans naturally sketch and update diagrams to externalize information, reduce cognitive load, and enforce structural consistency, thereby pushing the frontier of AI reasoning, especially in domains like mathematics and science where visual precision is paramount.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind FIGR stems from the observation that human reasoning, especially for complex problems involving spatial or structural relationships, is intrinsically linked to the ability to externalize information visually. People intuitively draw diagrams, sketch intermediate states, or construct spatial layouts to clarify complex constraints and discover patterns that are difficult to discern from text alone. Current AI models, despite advancements, largely fail to effectively emulate this powerful cognitive process.

The paper identifies specific shortcomings in existing AI reasoning paradigms that FIGR seeks to overcome:
1. **Inability to capture structural constraints:** Purely text-based reasoning struggles with implicit spatial and geometric relationships, leading to errors in complex problem-solving.
2. **Lack of precise control in visual generation:** Unified multimodal models can generate images but lack the fine-grained control needed to ensure geometric accuracy and consistency, making their visual outputs unreliable for precise reasoning.
3. **Limited autonomy in tool-augmented models:** While tool-augmented models offer controlled visual operations, they are restricted to predefined actions on existing images, preventing them from dynamically constructing novel diagrams essential for many reasoning tasks.

Against this backdrop, the **key objectives** of the FIGR research are:

- **To integrate executable visual construction directly into the multi-turn reasoning process:** This involves enabling the model to generate code that constructs diagrams, allowing for active visualization and iterative refinement of intermediate hypotheses.
- **To leverage these dynamically constructed diagrams as explicit, stateful feedback:** The rendered figures should serve as a consistent external representation that helps enforce geometric and structural consistency throughout the reasoning trajectory.
- **To develop an adaptive reward mechanism:** This mechanism is crucial for selectively regulating when visual construction should be invoked, encouraging beneficial diagrammatic reasoning while avoiding unnecessary or spurious visual actions.
- **To demonstrate substantial improvements in complex reasoning tasks:** Specifically, to outperform strong text-only and other multimodal baselines on challenging mathematical benchmarks that require maintaining global geometric or structural constraints.
- **To achieve these improvements via end-to-end reinforcement learning:** This allows the model to autonomously learn when and how to integrate visual reasoning without requiring a supervised visual reasoning cold-start stage or task-specific supervised visual data.

By achieving these objectives, FIGR aims to make AI models more robust, reliable, and "thoughtful" when faced with problems demanding a deep understanding and manipulation of spatial and structural information.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FIGR's methodology is built upon three interconnected components: an **executable visual construction within the reasoning loop**, a **reinforcement learning procedure with multi-turn executable reasoning**, and an **adaptive reward mechanism**.

#### 3.1. Executable Visual Construction within the Reasoning Loop

The core innovation of FIGR lies in its dynamic interleaving of textual inference with *executable code generation* to construct and interact with diagrams. This contrasts sharply with models that merely describe figures or generate static images.
- **State Representation:** At each reasoning step `t`, the model maintains a comprehensive state comprising a textual context (`h_t`, including prior reasoning steps, code outputs, and interpreter feedback) and an optional rendered diagram (`I_t`).
- **Action Space:** The policy (`π_θ`) can take one of two actions: (i) generate a textual continuation, or (ii) generate a code snippet (`c_t`).
- **Code Execution and Feedback:** When a code snippet is generated, it is executed by a sandboxed interpreter. This interpreter can produce two forms of feedback: textual output (`T_{t+1}`) and/or a rendered diagram (`I_{t+1}`).
- **Context Update:** The model's context is then updated with the executed code, textual feedback, and the newly rendered diagram. This loop continues until a solution is reached or a step limit is met.
- **Key Advantage:** By representing diagrams as executable and reproducible states, FIGR ensures precise and controllable visual feedback. This allows for iterative refinement and direct enforcement of geometric consistency, mimicking how humans interact with sketches during problem-solving.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning with Multi-turn Executable Reasoning

Training FIGR to effectively interleave textual and visual reasoning requires optimizing long-horizon policies. The utility of individual actions (e.g., when to generate code) is often only apparent at the end of a multi-step reasoning trajectory. To address this, FIGR employs a reinforcement learning approach:
- **Trajectory-level Supervision:** Instead of step-wise rewards, learning is framed at the trajectory level. For each input question, the model samples a group of `G` candidate reasoning trajectories under the current policy. Each trajectory includes actions, execution feedback, and concludes with a termination token.
- **Reward Function:** A scalar reward (`R_i`) is assigned to each trajectory, combining three components: `R_acc` (final answer correctness), `R_fmt` (output format compliance), and `R_vis` (appropriateness of visual construction, discussed below). This joint reward encourages a holistic optimization of reasoning behavior.
- **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO):** To stabilize learning with delayed rewards, GRPO (Shao et al., 2024b) is used. It compares trajectories generated from the same prompt, using the group-average reward as a baseline. The "relative advantage" (`Â_i`) of each trajectory (its reward minus the group average) serves as a low-variance learning signal, emphasizing behavioral differences that lead to better outcomes.
- **Policy Update:** The policy is updated by maximizing a surrogate objective (`J_GRPO(θ)`), which increases the likelihood of trajectories achieving higher relative rewards while maintaining stability through KL regularization against a reference policy.
- **Base Model and Training Data:** FIGR is initialized from an instruction-tuned model, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct, and trained on DeepMath-103K, a large dataset of challenging mathematical problems.

#### 3.3. Adaptive Reward Mechanism (ARM)

The ARM is crucial for guiding FIGR on *when* to invoke visual construction, balancing the benefits of diagrams with the potential for unnecessary complexity.
- **Suitability Prediction:** For each question, an auxiliary language model classifier (Deepseek-V3) first estimates if diagrammatic reasoning is beneficial, assigning a binary suitability label `s ∈ {0, 1}`. This prediction is for the entire question, not step-wise.
- **Conditional Visual Reward:** The visual-invocation reward (`R_vis`) is coupled with final answer correctness and the suitability label:
 - `R_vis = 1.0` if the final answer is correct (`y = ŷ`), diagrammatic reasoning was predicted as suitable (`s = 1`), and code was successfully executed (`exec = 1`).
 - `R_vis = 0.2` if the final answer is correct (`y = ŷ`), diagrammatic reasoning was predicted as *not* suitable (`s = 0`), but code was still successfully executed (`exec = 1`). This provides a smaller positive signal for correct solutions even when visual reasoning was not initially deemed critical, allowing for flexibility.
 - `R_vis = 0` otherwise.
- **Purposeful Visual Thinking:** This mechanism biases the policy towards using visual construction purposefully and effectively, tying visual actions directly to overall task success. It discourages redundant or spurious drawing actions, enabling the model to learn diagram construction as a meaningful intermediate state rather than a mandatory operation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of FIGR on eight challenging mathematical benchmarks demonstrates its significant efficacy, particularly in problems requiring spatial and geometric reasoning.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Gains

- **Superior Accuracy:** FIGR achieves an average accuracy of **74.11%** across seven diverse mathematical reasoning benchmarks (AIME 2024, AIME 2025, BeyondAIME, MATH 500, AMC, MinervaMath, OlympiadBench). This represents a substantial improvement of **6.77%** over its base policy, Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct, and **4.88%** over a text-only reinforcement learning baseline.
- **Problem-Specific Strengths:** The performance gains are most pronounced on highly challenging benchmarks like **AIME 2025 (+13.12%)** and **BeyondAIME (+11.00%)**, which are specifically designed to test reasoning requiring the maintenance of global geometric or structural constraints. This validates FIGR's core hypothesis that executable visual states are crucial for such problems.
- **Outperforming Strong Baselines:** FIGR not only surpasses its own base model but also outperforms other competitive models:
 - **Equivalently sized text-only LLMs:** (e.g., Qwen3-32B Thinking)
 - **Larger LVLMs:** (e.g., GLM-4.5V 108B), highlighting that the architectural innovation (executable visual construction) is more critical than mere model scale in this domain.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

A detailed ablation study further clarifies the contribution of FIGR's components:

- **Prompt Engineering (PE):** While PE on the base model can yield moderate gains, it does not involve model parameter updates, leading to unstable and inconsistent drawing behaviors without a learning signal.
- **Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT):** Directly fine-tuning the base model on the DeepMath-103K dataset for visual construction surprisingly leads to a *performance drop* (e.g., from 66.20% to 53.33% on AIME 2025). This suggests that SFT often overfits to training trajectories and fails to generalize robustly, underscoring the necessity of outcome-driven reinforcement learning.
- **Passively Injected Visual Information:** Baselines that merely inject passively generated diagrams (from Bagel-7B-MoT or Qwen-Image) as static context do not consistently improve performance. This crucial finding confirms that *active, executable construction and integration of visual feedback* are necessary, not just the presence of images. Static images, without a closed reasoning-execution loop, cannot enforce global constraints or provide actionable feedback.
- **Unified Multimodal Models (UMMs) and Tool-Augmented Vision-Language Models (TAVLMs):** Both categories show consistently weak performance. UMMs struggle with precision, leading to cascading errors, while TAVLMs are restricted to predefined operations on existing images, lacking the flexibility to construct novel diagrams. This stark contrast highlights FIGR's unique advantage in dynamic, precise visual generation.
- **Ablations on FIGR Components:**
 - **Removing the Adaptive Reward Mechanism (w/o ARM):** Disabling ARM causes a rapid collapse in code usage during training. Code ratio and length drop to zero, indicating that without the selective reward signal, the model learns that indiscriminate code invocation is ineffective, leading to abandonment. This confirms ARM's critical role in guiding purposeful visual construction.
 - **Removing Visual Feedback (w/o visual feedback):** The model still performs multi-turn rollouts and executes code, but only receives textual feedback. This leads to performance degradation (e.g., on AIME 2025, 79.32% drops to 76.67%), demonstrating that explicit visual feedback, beyond mere code execution outputs, is vital for forming a stable and effective reasoning loop.

#### 4.3. Evolution of Active Visual Construction

Analysis of training dynamics on the BeyondAIME dataset reveals distinct behavioral patterns:
- **FIGR's Stable Visual Thinking:** FIGR maintains a consistently high code ratio throughout training, with stable and substantially longer code blocks, and a high execution pass rate. This indicates that FIGR develops structured and sustained executable visual construction behaviors, leveraging the external visual state for explicit validation of global structures.
- **Collapse without ARM:** In contrast, the `w/o ARM` variant shows an initial surge in code usage followed by a rapid decline, exemplifying an "overuse-abandonment" cycle when effective reward guidance is absent.

#### 4.4. Case Study

A case study illustrates FIGR's qualitative advantage. For a problem involving function properties and root existence, the base Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct relies on text-only symbolic manipulation, prone to subtle analytical oversights. The variant without visual feedback uses code execution for numerical or symbolic verification but lacks global visual cues (e.g., the function's overall shape). FIGR, however, generates code to plot the function, visually observing its behavior, critical points, and intersection with the x-axis, leading to a more robust and interpretable solution. This confirms that the benefit of FIGR is not just executing code, but actively integrating *visual construction* to obtain complementary structural information.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FIGR research marks a significant step forward in the field of AI reasoning, particularly for problems demanding sophisticated spatial and geometric understanding.

#### 5.1. Scientific Significance

- **Novel Reasoning Paradigm:** FIGR introduces a pioneering paradigm by embedding **executable, revisable visual states** directly into the multi-turn reasoning loop, driven by end-to-end reinforcement learning. This moves beyond static image understanding or fixed tool use, enabling dynamic and active visual thinking.
- **Bridging Symbolic and Perceptual Reasoning:** The work effectively bridges the gap between traditional symbolic reasoning and visual perception by making visual representations an integral, interactive component of the logical inference process. This mimics a fundamental aspect of human cognition where diagrams aid complex thought.
- **Enhancing Reliability and Consistency:** By enforcing geometric consistency through executable code and leveraging visual feedback, FIGR addresses a critical limitation of previous models: the struggle to maintain global structural constraints. This leads to more reliable and accurate problem-solving, particularly in domains where subtle misinterpretations can lead to large errors.
- **Adaptive Control of Multimodal Interaction:** The Adaptive Reward Mechanism is a key methodological contribution, demonstrating how reinforcement learning can effectively regulate the judicious invocation of multimodal capabilities (visual construction) without requiring explicit, step-wise supervised annotations. This is vital for developing more autonomous and efficient AI agents.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Advancing Mathematical and Scientific AI:** The immediate impact is a substantial improvement in AI's ability to solve complex mathematical problems, especially those from competitive exams or requiring geometric insight. This can be extended to various scientific domains (e.g., physics, chemistry, engineering) where understanding and manipulating visual-spatial information is critical for problem-solving and discovery.
- **Towards More Human-like AI:** By integrating diagrammatic reasoning, FIGR brings AI systems closer to human-like problem-solving processes. This could lead to AI that is not only more capable but also more interpretable and collaborative, as its intermediate "thoughts" can be visualized and understood.
- **Foundation for Future Multimodal AI:** The principles demonstrated by FIGR—dynamic visual state generation, feedback integration, and adaptive control—can serve as a foundation for developing next-generation multimodal AI systems. This could impact areas such as:
 - **Program Synthesis:** Generating visual code for designing interfaces or simulating physical systems.
 - **Robotics and Embodied AI:** Enabling robots to reason about their environment by dynamically sketching plans or simulating outcomes.
 - **Design and Creativity:** Assisting human designers with visual ideation and iterative refinement.
 - **Education:** Creating intelligent tutors that can visually explain complex concepts and guide students through problem-solving steps.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point to promising avenues for future research:

- **Computational Cost:** The multi-turn reasoning and code execution inherently incur additional computational overhead. Future work could focus on more efficient reasoning-execution loops, perhaps through compact visual representations or smarter adaptive control strategies to reduce unnecessary visual invocations.
- **Task Regime Identification:** The benefits of executable visual construction are most pronounced for problems requiring global structural or relational constraints. Precisely characterizing task regimes where visual externalization is most impactful remains an open research question, which could lead to more targeted and efficient application of the method.
- **Domain Expansion:** The current evaluation is focused on mathematical reasoning benchmarks. Extending the FIGR paradigm to other diverse domains, such as scientific reasoning, program synthesis, and long-horizon planning, represents a natural and high-impact direction for future work. This would demonstrate the generalizability of executable visual states as a powerful reasoning primitive.

In conclusion, FIGR represents a significant contribution to AI's reasoning capabilities, offering a compelling step towards systems that can "think visually" with the precision and controllability required for truly complex problem-solving.
