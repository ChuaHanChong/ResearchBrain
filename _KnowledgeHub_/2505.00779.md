# Uncertainty-aware Latent Safety Filters for Avoiding Out-of-Distribution Failures

> [!summary] Summary
> Carnegie Mellon University researchers introduce UNISafe, a framework that enhances latent safety filters by incorporating epistemic uncertainty and out-of-distribution (OOD) detection. This allows robots to proactively avoid both known and previously unseen safety hazards when operating from high-dimensional sensor data, achieving superior failure rates in visual manipulation tasks and real-world Jenga.

## Problem

- Traditional safe control methods struggle with high-dimensional sensor observations, requiring accurate, low-dimensional system models.
- Learned latent safety filters, while powerful, can generate overconfident or incorrect predictions when world models encounter out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, leading to catastrophic failures.
- Existing OOD detection methods often lack actionable mitigation strategies, and formal safety methods typically do not explicitly address epistemic uncertainty (lack of model knowledge).

## Method

- UNISafe augments latent-space Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis with quantified epistemic uncertainty and calibrated OOD detection.
- Epistemic uncertainty is derived from a probabilistic ensemble of latent dynamics predictors using Jensen-Rényi Divergence (JRD).
- A conformal prediction-calibrated threshold is used to define an uncertainty-aware failure set that penalizes high epistemic uncertainty, allowing the safety filter to proactively avoid OOD regions.

## Results

- UNISafe achieved the lowest failure rates (0.20) and model errors (43.1 ± 1.2) in a vision-based block plucking simulation, outperforming the LatentSafe baseline (0.30 failure rate).
- In 3D Dubins Car simulations, JRD-based epistemic uncertainty quantification achieved the highest balanced accuracy of 0.94 for OOD classification compared to other uncertainty methods.
- On a real-world Jenga task, UNISafe significantly reduced failure rates (0.08 vs 0.82 for LatentSafe) and maintained lower model uncertainty (28.49 vs 33.25 model loss) during replays of failure trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating epistemic uncertainty directly into the safety value function allows for proactive avoidance of OOD states rather than merely detecting them.
> - Explicitly distinguishing epistemic uncertainty from aleatoric uncertainty is critical for reliably identifying and responding to novel, unseen hazards.
> - Calibrating OOD thresholds via conformal prediction provides statistical guarantees, ensuring robust and sensitive detection of OOD conditions for safety-critical decisions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape and Problem Context

The ability for robots to operate safely in complex, open-world environments is a paramount challenge in modern robotics. Traditional safe control methods, such as Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis and Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), offer strong theoretical guarantees but typically rely on accurate, low-dimensional system models and explicit safety specifications. Scaling these methods to complex, high-dimensional sensor observations (e.g., images) has been difficult.

Recent advancements in **generative world models**, particularly those based on latent representations (e.g., DreamerV3), have offered a promising avenue. These models learn compact latent representations of observations and predict their dynamics, allowing robots to "imagine" future states and plan actions directly from raw sensor data. This has enabled the application of classical safe control methods, such as latent safety filters, within these high-dimensional, learned latent spaces.

However, a critical limitation arises: **model uncertainty and out-of-distribution (OOD) failures**. World models are trained on finite datasets, which inherently cannot cover all possible safety-critical scenarios. When a robot encounters a novel, unseen situation (an OOD input or an OOD imagination of future states), the learned world model may "hallucinate" overconfident or incorrect predictions. This can lead latent safety filters, built upon these models, to misclassify risky OOD situations as safe, failing to prevent known hazards or even missing entirely novel ones.

The existing solutions in the landscape often fall short:
- **OOD detection methods** can identify anomalous observations or uncertain predictions, but they typically lack actionable mitigation strategies, leaving the robot aware of uncertainty but unable to act appropriately.
- **Safety filtering methods** provide robust safety assurances under model uncertainty (e.g., worst-case disturbances, aleatoric uncertainty) but generally do not explicitly address **epistemic uncertainty** – the lack of knowledge of the model itself, which is crucial for identifying OOD scenarios.
- While some recent works prevent systems from entering OOD states, they might restrict in-distribution to safe trajectories or lack calibrated OOD detection, limiting their scalability and reliability in complex settings.

UNISafe directly addresses this crucial gap, aiming to combine the proactive nature of formal safe control with the robustness of uncertainty-aware OOD detection, specifically targeting epistemic uncertainty in learned world models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a **reliable, policy-agnostic safety mechanism** that can proactively steer robots away from *both known and unseen safety hazards*, even when operating directly from high-dimensional sensor observations using a learned latent world model.

The core motivations stem from the limitations of current approaches:
1. **Overconfidence in Latent Safety Filters:** Existing latent safety filters, while powerful, rely on the world model's imaginations. If the world model is trained on limited data, it may optimistically predict safe outcomes in OOD scenarios, leading to catastrophic failures in deployment. This paper explicitly uses the example of a Jenga game where a model might not see all ways a tower can fall and thus make unsafe predictions.
2. **Lack of Actionable OOD Mitigation:** While OOD detection can identify anomalous situations, it typically does not provide control strategies to avoid or recover from them. The robot might know it's uncertain but not know how to act safely.
3. **Distinguishing Uncertainty Types:** Many uncertainty quantification methods conflate aleatoric uncertainty (inherent noise/stochasticity) with epistemic uncertainty (lack of model knowledge). For OOD detection and proactive hazard avoidance, it is critical to specifically target epistemic uncertainty, as it signals unexplored regions where the model's predictions are unreliable.
4. **Bridging Formal Safety and Learning:** The paper seeks to integrate the robustness of formal control methods (like HJ reachability) with the data-driven power of world models, while explicitly addressing the inherent uncertainties of learned components.

In essence, the motivation is to make learned safe control genuinely safe and deployable in dynamic, open-world environments by ensuring robots do not fail due to a lack of understanding or imagination beyond their training data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

UNISafe (UNcertainty-aware Imagination for Safety filtering) proposes a principled framework that augments latent-space reachability analysis with epistemic uncertainty quantification and calibrated OOD detection.

#### 3.1. Latent Safety Filters via World Models

The foundation of UNISafe is the concept of latent safety filters. A **world model** (e.g., DreamerV3) is trained on an offline dataset to:
- **Encode** high-dimensional observations (`o`) into compact latent representations (`z`).
- **Predict dynamics** in this latent space (`f_z`), allowing anticipation of future states.
- **Identify known failures** (`F_known`) via a learned failure margin function (`l_z`) that classifies latent states as safe or unsafe based on training labels.

**Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability analysis** is then performed in this latent space to synthesize:
- A **safety value function** (`V_è`), which quantifies how close the robot is to failure despite its best efforts. `V_è < 0` indicates inevitable failure.
- A **safety-preserving policy** (`π_è`), a fallback policy that steers the system away from known failures.
At runtime, a monitor checks `V_è` for the task policy's proposed action. If the action is predicted to lead to an unsafe state, `π_è` overrides it.

#### 3.2. Formalizing Uncertainty-aware Latent Safety Filters

The core innovation is to augment the safety problem by incorporating the world model's epistemic uncertainty.
- **Epistemic Uncertainty (`u`):** This is quantified for a latent-action transition `(z, a)`, representing the model's lack of knowledge about the dynamics in that region.
- **Augmented Latent Space (`~z`):** The latent state is extended to `~z = (z, u)`.
- **Uncertainty-aware Failure Set (`~F`):** This combines `F_known` (predictable failures) with `F_OOD` (OOD imaginations where `u` exceeds a threshold `ε`). Formally, `~F = {~z | l_~z(~z) < 0}` where `l_~z(~z) = min(l_z(z), κ(ε - u))`. A high uncertainty `u > ε` makes `l_~z` negative, effectively marking the state as an OOD failure.
- **Uncertainty-aware Latent Dynamics (`f_~z`):** The latent dynamics are extended to predict both the next latent state `z'` and its associated uncertainty `u'`.
- **Uncertainty-aware Latent Reachability Analysis:** HJ reachability (Eq. 2) is performed in this augmented `(~z)` space using `f_~z` and `l_~z`. This ensures that states with high epistemic uncertainty are penalized, making the safety filter proactively avoid OOD regions. The resulting safety filter `V_è(~z)` and `π_è(~z)` are thus aware of both known and unseen hazards.

#### 3.3. Quantifying Epistemic Uncertainty in World Models

To obtain the epistemic uncertainty `u`, the authors propose:
- **Probabilistic Ensemble Latent Predictor:** Instead of directly ensembling the entire generative world model (which is challenging for RSSMs), a separate ensemble of `K` latent dynamics predictors (`E = {^f^k_z}`) is trained. Each member `^f^k_z` is a neural network that predicts the next latent state `z_{t+1}` as a Gaussian distribution `N(μ, Σ)` given `(z_t, a_t)`. The covariance `Σ` models aleatoric uncertainty.
- **Epistemic Uncertainty Quantification via Jensen-Rényi Divergence (JRD):** To specifically isolate epistemic uncertainty (lack of knowledge) from aleatoric uncertainty (inherent stochasticity), the JRD of the ensemble's Gaussian predictions is used. For `α = 2`, JRD has a closed-form expression, making it computationally efficient for high-dimensional latent spaces. JRD measures the disagreement among the ensemble members, where high disagreement indicates high epistemic uncertainty.

#### 3.4. Detecting OOD Imaginations via Conformal Prediction

Setting the uncertainty threshold `ε` for `F_OOD` is crucial. A loose threshold misses OOD failures, while a strict one leads to over-conservatism.
- **Conformal Prediction (CP):** This statistical framework is used to automatically calibrate `ε` in a principled way.
- **In-distribution Recall Guarantee:** CP, specifically a class-conditioned and trajectory-level approach, is applied to a held-out calibration dataset (`D_calib`) containing only in-distribution trajectories. It identifies an `ε` such that in-distribution transitions are detected with a probability of at least `1 - α_cal`.
- **Trajectory-Level Calibration:** To account for sequential dependencies in robot trajectories, a trajectory-level nonconformity score `Q^α_trans_τi` is defined as the `(1 - α_trans)`-quantile of epistemic uncertainties within a trajectory. The final `ε` is then set as the `(1 - α_cal)`-quantile of these trajectory-level scores. This guarantees a low false positive rate (i.e., in-distribution data rarely exceeds `ε`), enabling reliable classification of `u > ε` as OOD.

#### 3.5. Runtime Safety Filtering with Halting Condition

At runtime, the UNISafe filter acts as a monitor for the task policy (`π_task`). If `V_è(~z') > δ` (where `~z'` is the imagined next state and uncertainty from `π_task`), `π_task` is allowed. Otherwise, `π_è` overrides it.
An additional **halting condition** is introduced for system-level safety: if even the fallback action `π_è(~z)` results in an epistemic uncertainty `D(z, π_è(z)) > ε`, the system halts. This prevents the robot from attempting actions that the safety filter itself cannot confidently predict as safe and in-distribution, signaling an unrecoverable OOD situation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper evaluates UNISafe across various tasks, demonstrating its effectiveness in both simulation and real-world hardware.

#### 4.1. Simulation: 3D Dubins Car

This benchmark task involves a Dubins car avoiding failure regions, with limited training data for some failure types, making them entirely OOD.
- **Reliable OOD Failure Identification:** UNISafe successfully infers OOD failure sets from quantified uncertainty, even when these failures were never observed in training. The resulting safety value function accurately identifies unsafe regions.
- **Superior Uncertainty Quantification:** Compared to baselines like `TotalUncertainty` (conflates aleatoric and epistemic), `MaxAleatoric`, and `DensityEst` (likelihood-based), JRD achieves the highest balanced accuracy (0.94 vs 0.93 for `TotalUncertainty` and lower for others) in classifying OOD conditions. Methods that do not target epistemic uncertainty or rely only on latent states (vs. latent-action transitions) show higher false-positive rates or lower true-negative rates.
- **Calibrated Threshold Quality:** The conformal prediction-calibrated OOD threshold (`ε`) yields value functions with high true positive and true negative rates, demonstrating its robustness and sensitivity compared to perturbed thresholds.
- **Robustness to Uncertain World Models:** In scenarios with known failure sets but high uncertainty around boundaries due to limited data coverage, UNISafe robustly learns safety monitors with higher balanced accuracy and higher safety rates in rollouts (0.82 vs 0.63 for `LatentSafe`), preventing `LatentSafe` from overconfidently misclassifying unsafe states.

#### 4.2. Simulation: Vision-Based Block Plucking

This complex visual manipulation task involves a Franka manipulator plucking a block from a tower without collapse.
- **Minimized Failures and Model Errors:** UNISafe achieves the lowest failure rates (0.20) and model errors (43.1 ± 1.2) compared to all baselines. `LatentSafe` (0.30 failure rate) overestimates safety for OOD actions.
- **Ineffectiveness of Other Baselines:** `SafeOnly` (trained only on successful data) shows limited effectiveness (0.28 failure rate), highlighting the insufficiency of OOD detection from success-only data. Offline RL methods with conservative losses (CQL, COMBO) perform even worse than `LatentSafe`, suggesting that conservatism alone cannot substitute for explicit failure set identification and uncertainty quantification for safety.
- **Non-Conservative yet Safe Behavior:** While all OOD detection methods improve performance over `LatentSafe`, methods targeting aleatoric uncertainty (e.g., `TotalUncertainty`, `MaxAleatoric`) tend to be overly conservative, leading to higher incompletion rates and frequent interventions. UNISafe with JRD, by targeting epistemic uncertainty, achieves the most reliable performance with a good balance of safety and task completion.

#### 4.3. Hardware: Vision-Based Jenga with a Robotic Manipulator

UNISafe is validated on a real-world Jenga task, with a teleoperator acting as the task policy.
- **Reliable Filtering of Known and Unseen Failures:** UNISafe reliably intervenes when the teleoperator attempts erratic or OOD actions that risk tower collapse, correcting behavior and maintaining stability within the in-distribution region.
- **Preemptive OOD Detection:** Unlike `LatentSafe`, which fails to preemptively detect unsafe boundaries due to optimistic OOD imaginations, UNISafe effectively blocks unseen actions that risk collapse.
- **Quantitative Hardware Performance:** In replays of 50 failure trajectories, UNISafe results in a significantly lower failure rate (0.08 vs 0.82 for `LatentSafe`) and maintains lower model uncertainty (28.49 vs 33.25 model loss). The system also demonstrates a robust halting mechanism for truly unrecoverable OOD visual inputs (e.g., significant visual distractions not seen in training).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

UNISafe makes a highly significant contribution to the field of safe learning-based control for robotics by bridging a critical gap between learned world models and formal safety guarantees.

### 6. Significance

- **Enabling Robust Deployment:** By proactively preventing both known and *unseen* failures, UNISafe enhances the reliability and trustworthiness of robots operating in complex, uncertain environments. This is crucial for real-world deployment of autonomous systems, where comprehensive training data for all failure modes is impractical.
- **Principled Integration of Uncertainty:** The method provides a principled way to incorporate epistemic uncertainty into a classical formal safety framework (HJ reachability), transforming passive OOD detection into actionable, proactive safety control.
- **Addressing Overconfidence in Learned Models:** It directly tackles the pervasive problem of overconfidence in deep learning models when encountering OOD data, which is a major barrier to safety-critical applications.
- **Scalability to High-Dimensional Systems:** UNISafe's use of latent world models allows it to handle high-dimensional sensor inputs (vision) and complex robot dynamics, extending safe control beyond low-dimensional, perfectly modeled systems.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Safer Human-Robot Interaction:** Robots in collaborative or shared spaces can benefit significantly from an ability to recognize and avoid novel unsafe situations, improving safety and human trust.
- **Autonomous Navigation and Manipulation:** Applications like autonomous driving, logistics, and complex manipulation tasks can achieve higher safety standards, reducing accidents caused by unexpected events.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** UNISafe provides a strong foundation for future work in areas such as safe exploration, active learning, and online adaptation in the presence of uncertainty.

**Limitations (as identified by the authors):**
- **Component vs. System-Level Assurances:** While UNISafe offers statistical assurances for OOD transition detection, it does not formally guarantee zero failure rates at a system level, especially given the approximations from reinforcement learning in high-dimensional spaces.
- **Reliance on Learned World Model:** The performance is inherently tied to the world model's generalization capabilities. Imprecise or biased predictions, even within in-distribution regions, can lead to an imprecise safety filter. This highlights the need for continued improvement in world model robustness and generalization.
- **Challenges in Uncertainty Quantification:** The method assumes Gaussian distributions for latent predictions and uses a separate ensemble module, which might not perfectly capture the world model's true, potentially multimodal, uncertainty. It also currently optimizes for expected safety without explicitly accounting for aleatoric uncertainty in the Bellman equation, which could further improve robustness to worst-case outcomes.
- **Initial State and Disturbances:** The framework assumes an in-distribution safe initial state and no unknown disturbances or visual distractions. A system-level monitoring mechanism is necessary for robust deployment, indicating when the system loses confidence and requires intervention.

Despite these limitations, UNISafe represents a significant step forward in making learned robotic control systems more robust and trustworthy by explicitly accounting for and proactively mitigating risks arising from the inherent uncertainties of learning in open-world environments.
