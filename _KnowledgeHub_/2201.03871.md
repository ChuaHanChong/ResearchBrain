# Combining Learning-based Locomotion Policy with Model-based Manipulation for Legged Mobile Manipulators

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from ETH Zürich developed a control framework for legged mobile manipulators that integrates a model-based manipulation controller with a learning-based locomotion policy. This system enables the robot's base to proactively compensate for manipulator disturbances using predicted wrench sequences, achieving robust end-effector tracking on rough terrain and zero-shot adaptation to varying arm configurations on hardware.

## Problem

- Controlling legged mobile manipulators is challenging due to high dimensionality, complex contact dynamics, and unstructured operational environments.
- Model-based control struggles with the computational cost of hybrid legged systems and difficulties in handling rough terrain or unexpected disturbances.
- Purely learning-based locomotion policies lack transferability, often requiring extensive re-training for different robot configurations or tasks.

## Method

![[2201.03871-method.png]]
*Overview of the training and deployment pipeline. We train the RL policy with a wrench sequence generator and replace it with the wrench plan from MPC for deployment.*

- A ==decoupled control architecture== was developed, combining a ==whole-body Model Predictive Control (MPC)== for the manipulator with a ==Reinforcement Learning (RL)== policy for base locomotion.
- The MPC controller provides a sequence of ==predicted external wrenches== exerted by the manipulator on the base, which are fed as ==anticipatory observations== to the RL locomotion policy.
- During RL training, a dedicated ==wrench sequence generator== simulates manipulator disturbances, avoiding the computational overhead of running MPC, while robust unobserved disturbances are also introduced.

## Results

- The system demonstrated robust end-effector tracking on rough terrain with a physical **ALMA** robot, successfully performing manipulation tasks while maintaining stable locomotion.
- The approach enabled **zero-shot adaptation**, where the base policy maintained stability across different manipulator weights (e.g., light, heavy) and lengths without needing to be re-trained.
- The predictive controller allowed the robot to proactively lean against external forces, stabilizing against a **150 N** lateral push and showing up to **208%** improvement over reactive or naive controllers.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Providing the locomotion policy with predictive external wrench sequences allows it to anticipate and proactively counteract disturbances from manipulator movements, significantly improving base stability.
> - Decoupling the manipulator control from base policy training through a predictable wrench model simplifies the learning process and enables the base policy to adapt to various manipulator configurations without re-training.
> - Training the locomotion policy with unobserved wrench disturbances enhances its robustness against unmodeled dynamics and real-world system mismatches, improving performance during dynamic tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The control of legged mobile manipulators presents a challenging problem due to their high dimensionality, contact-rich dynamics, and often non-smooth operational environments. Two primary, often complementary, approaches have emerged in robotics control: model-based control and learning-based (or data-driven) control.

Model-based control algorithms, such as Model Predictive Control (MPC), are capable of precise control and robustness to external disturbances for systems like wheeled mobile manipulators. However, their application to legged mobile manipulators faces significant hurdles. The hybrid nature of legged systems, characterized by drastically expanding contact configurations, leads to high computational costs for optimal planning, often precluding real-time performance. Previous efforts have addressed this by using simplified template models (e.g., zero-moment-point or centroidal models) to enable online planning on flat ground. These simplifications, however, struggle with rough terrains and unforeseen disturbances like unplanned contacts or slippage, making even basic tasks like end-effector tracking while walking difficult.

Conversely, model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated success in learning robust legged locomotion policies for challenging terrains. RL approaches intrinsically handle contact dynamics and unexpected events by learning from environment interactions. Policies can be made robust to unobservable disturbances and model mismatches by introducing controlled disturbances during training (e.g., random external forces, constant wrenches, or adversarial disturbances). A limitation of purely RL-based approaches is their transferability; policies often require re-training for different robot configurations or tasks, which is time-consuming.

Recent research has explored combining RL with model-based control to leverage the strengths of both paradigms and improve transferability. Examples include RL agents outputting desired twists for the base or desired footholds, which are then tracked by model-based leg controllers. This hybrid approach aims to mitigate the limitations of each method when applied in isolation, particularly for complex systems like legged mobile manipulators operating in unstructured environments. The current work positions itself within this context by proposing a pipeline that integrates a learning-based locomotion policy with a model-based manipulation controller.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties in achieving robust and precise control of legged mobile manipulators operating in complex, unstructured environments. While legged robots offer the potential to traverse terrains inaccessible to wheeled robots and interact with human-oriented infrastructure, mounting an arm significantly increases control complexity. The authors identify the limitations of both purely model-based and purely learning-based approaches for this domain:

- **Model-based limitations:** High computational cost due to hybrid system dynamics and contact configurations, difficulty in handling rough terrain and unexpected disturbances, and reliance on accurate models.
- **Learning-based limitations:** Time-consuming re-training for diverse robot configurations or tasks, and limited adaptability to systems not seen during training.

Given these limitations, the key objectives of this work are to develop a control pipeline that:

1. **Combines strengths:** Integrates the precision and planning capabilities of Model Predictive Control (MPC) for manipulation with the robustness and agility of Reinforcement Learning (RL) for legged locomotion.
2. **Decouples control:** Decouples the manipulator control from the base policy training by modeling manipulator disturbances as predictable external wrenches. This aims to simplify the training process and reduce computational overhead.
3. **Utilizes predictive information:** Enables the learning-based locomotion policy to utilize predicted external wrench sequences (force and torque) as observations to proactively counteract disturbances caused by arm movements.
4. **Achieves zero-shot adaptation:** Allows the base policy to adapt to various manipulator configurations (e.g., different arm weights or lengths) without requiring re-training, which is a common challenge for end-to-end RL systems.
5. **Validates on hardware:** Demonstrates the efficacy of the combined control system on a physical legged mobile manipulator (ALMA platform) performing manipulation tasks while traversing rough terrain.

Specifically, the authors aim to show that using wrench sequence prediction as an observation improves the base RL policy's performance compared to using only the current wrench. They also intend to prove that a dedicated wrench sequence generator can facilitate policy training without directly integrating the arm controller or solving MPC during this phase, thus streamlining the learning process.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a decoupled control architecture that integrates a model-based manipulator controller with a learning-based legged locomotion policy. The core idea is to model the dynamic effects of the manipulator on the base as predictable external wrenches, which the locomotion policy then uses to anticipate and counteract disturbances.

**A. Overview of the Pipeline**
The system is divided into two primary controllers: a whole-body Model Predictive Control (MPC) for the manipulator and a Reinforcement Learning (RL) policy for base locomotion. During training, the RL policy for the base is trained independently, with external wrench disturbances simulated by a "wrench sequence generator." For deployment, this generator is replaced by wrench predictions derived from the MPC that controls the actual manipulator. The system was deployed on the ALMA platform (ANYmal base with Dynaarm manipulator) and simulated using the Raisim physics engine.

**B. MPC for the Manipulation System**
The manipulator is modeled as an N-degrees-of-freedom arm on a floating base.
- **State and Input:** The state vector `x` includes the base pose, manipulator joint positions (θ), and velocities (ω). The input vector `u` consists of base linear velocities (v_x, v_y), angular velocity (ω_z), and manipulator joint accelerations (α).
- **System Dynamics:** A simplified first-order system models the base dynamics, assuming perfect tracking of commanded linear (x, y) and angular (yaw) velocities. Base height, roll, and pitch are modeled with passive first-order dynamics decaying to desired values, with coefficients (κ) tuned from the trained student policy. Manipulator joints are modeled with second-order dynamics.
- **Cost Function and Constraints:** The MPC aims to minimize a cost function that includes end-effector tracking error, input costs, state deviation costs (penalizing arm joints away from nominal values), and joint velocity costs. Joint position limits are incorporated as inequality constraints.
- **Solver:** The MPC problem is solved using Sequential Linear-Quadratic (SLQ) with the OCS2 framework.
- **Wrench Prediction:** After solving MPC, the planned state-input trajectory is used to compute the sequence of joint torques and the external wrench exerted on the base using inverse dynamics, implemented with Pinocchio. This wrench sequence is then fed as a prediction to the base RL policy. MPC runs at a high rate (over 200 Hz) to maintain small state deviations despite dynamics mismatch.

**C. Wrench Sequence Generator (for RL Training)**
During the RL policy training phase, the manipulator's influence is simulated by a wrench sequence generator, avoiding the computational cost of running MPC. This generator comprises two components:
- **Observed Wrench:** This component generates smooth, predictable wrench sequences similar to those from planned manipulator motions. It uses randomized quadratic functions for each of the six wrench dimensions (3 force, 3 torque) over a two-second horizon. At the start of an episode, three wrench values are sampled (at t=0, 1.0, 2.0s) within a uniform range, and a quadratic function is fitted. Subsequent wrench values are derived from this polynomial, with an additional randomized component controlled by a parameter `β` that dictates the rate of change. The generated wrench values at specific future time steps (e.g., [0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8]s) are converted to the robot base frame and fed to the policy as observation.
- **Unobserved Disturbances:** To enhance robustness against unmodeled dynamics (e.g., base motion effects on external loads, motor cogging), this component introduces additional disturbances. Two random vectors are sampled for force and torque. These are multiplied by the robot's base linear and angular accelerations, respectively, and applied as wrenches. Gaussian noise is added to further simulate unmodeled disturbances.
The sum of these two simulated wrench components is applied to the robot's base in the simulator. The RL agent receives an observation `o_w` which includes the predicted wrench sequence in the body frame, along with base velocity commands and base twist.

**D. Base Policy Training**
The base locomotion policy is trained using a privileged training method, employing a teacher-student curriculum:
- **Teacher Policy:** A teacher agent is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). It receives "privileged" observations, including internal simulation states like ground friction and reaction forces, which are unavailable on physical hardware. Its wrench observation consists of the polynomial observed wrench, base velocity commands, and base twist. The reward function emphasizes base orientation, vertical velocity, and angular velocities in pitch and roll to encourage stable locomotion.
- **Student Policy:** The student policy is trained to imitate the teacher's behavior without privileged information. It receives noisified wrench observations, proprioceptive information, and height scan data. These observations are processed by a recurrent neural network (RNN) for wrench encoding. The policy outputs leg phases and residual joint positions for a joint-level PD controller, generating a trotting gait.
- **Distillation Loss:** The student policy is distilled from the teacher by minimizing a loss function (`L_total`) comprising three components:
 - `L_action`: Penalizes differences between student and teacher actions.
 - `L_embedding`: Regresses student wrench and exteroceptive embeddings towards those of the teacher.
 - `L_decoder`: Includes terms for decoding privileged information (`L_privileged`, `L_scan`) and external wrenches (`L_w1`, `L_w2`). `L_w1` minimizes the mean-squared error between decoded and actual external wrench, while `L_w2` regresses decoded unobserved wrench characteristics towards the magnitude of the disturbance vectors, helping the policy to correct for unobserved disturbances. Two separate recurrent networks are used to prevent interference between predicted wrench observations and proprioceptive/perceptive decoding.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments focused on evaluating the benefits of integrating wrench predictions, the system's adaptability, and its robustness in both simulation and hardware.

**A. Teacher Policy Performance**
Comparative analysis of teacher policies showed that incorporating external wrench prediction observations significantly improved the learning progress and final performance of the teacher policy. Training with a smooth polynomial model for predictable wrench also resulted in a smoother learning process compared to intermittent pushes. This indicated that the policy could effectively utilize predictive wrench information to counteract disturbances.

**B. End-effector Tracking**
Simulated end-effector tracking experiments, using ALMA with a 3 kg end-effector, assessed different student policy structures across two scenarios:
- **Experiment 1 (Rough Terrain, Static Target):** Policies utilizing explicit external wrench information (Ours, Current prediction, Instantaneous wrench) demonstrated similar and improved performance in base tilt, angular velocity, and linear velocity tracking error compared to the policy with no wrench observation. This indicated the general benefit of wrench information, even for relatively static manipulator motions where the wrench is primarily from the manipulator's weight.
- **Experiment 2 (Dynamic Target, Oscillating End-effector):** For dynamic manipulator motions, the policies with wrench prediction sequences (Our approach with RNN, Current prediction with MLP) performed better than policies only observing instantaneous wrench or no wrench. The "Ours" policy, which used an RNN to process the wrench prediction sequence, exhibited better stability and lower base tilt compared to the "Instantaneous wrench" policy, showing the advantage of anticipating future wrench trends for faster and more dynamic movements.

**C. Adaptation to Different Manipulator Configurations**
The combined control system was tested for its adaptability to varying manipulator setups (light arm, long arm, heavy arm) using Experiment 2's dynamic target scenario. The proposed policy, which received wrench observations, maintained base stability (minimal tilt) across these configurations. In contrast, a policy trained without external wrench observation showed significantly increased base tilt when switching to manipulators exerting higher wrenches. This demonstrated the zero-shot adaptation capability of the approach to previously unseen manipulator configurations without requiring re-training of the base policy.

**D. End-effector Tracking under Base Disturbance**
A hardware experiment involved manually disturbing ALMA's base while the robot was in stance, to evaluate the MPC's ability to maintain end-effector position. The system achieved a 57% reduction in end-effector position deviation relative to the base movement when disturbed in the x-direction. This indicated MPC's effectiveness in isolating the manipulator from base disturbances.

**E. Response to External Push: Anticipated Leaning**
This experiment simulated a lateral external force applied as a step function to the robot's base. The policy, informed by wrench predictions, started leaning against the incoming force *before* it was applied, proactively increasing stability. The proposed controller was able to stabilize against a 150 N force without stepping, significantly outperforming a "Reactive Controller" (observing only current wrench, 170% improvement) and a "Naive Controller" (no wrench observations, 208% improvement). This confirmed the advantage of anticipatory behavior enabled by wrench prediction.

**F. Explicit Wrench Compensation**
A simulation compared the proposed controller (using MPC predictions) with a controller that replaced wrench observations with "decoded" wrench information (estimated from proprioceptive data by the RL agent). When the manipulator moved slowly, both controllers showed similar angular velocity tracking performance. However, during fast and dynamic manipulator motions (higher angular frequencies of arm movement), the MPC prediction-based controller significantly reduced the base's angular velocity error and decreased the likelihood of falling, highlighting the superior accuracy and timeliness of explicit MPC predictions over decoded estimates for dynamic scenarios.

**G. Dynamics Mismatch**
The importance of training with unobserved wrench disturbances was evaluated by comparing the proposed policy (trained with base-acceleration induced disturbances) against a policy not exposed to such disturbances. When an unobserved 1 kg payload was added to the end-effector in simulation, the policy trained with unobserved disturbances showed improved tracking error across all metrics, with a notable reduction in mean angular base velocity error, leading to less undesired body motion. This demonstrated the enhanced robustness against dynamics mismatch provided by this training component.

**H. Dependency on Wrench Observations**
To understand the policy's reliance on different parts of the wrench prediction sequence, segments of the observation were occluded (replaced with noise). Occluding imminent wrench predictions (0.0 s and 0.2 s in the future) led to the most significant performance drop. Interestingly, predictions further in the future (0.8 s) were found to be more informative than those at 0.4 s and 0.6 s, possibly because they provided insight into longer-term wrench trends.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a novel and effective pipeline for controlling legged mobile manipulators by combining the strengths of learning-based locomotion and model-based manipulation. The key contributions and their potential impact are:

1. **Enhanced Performance and Robustness:** By enabling the locomotion policy to anticipate and counteract external wrenches from the manipulator, the system achieves improved performance in terms of base stability, reduced tracking errors, and increased robustness against disturbances. This is particularly relevant for dynamic tasks and unstructured terrains, areas where traditional model-based approaches struggle.
2. **Zero-Shot Adaptation:** The decoupled training approach allows the base locomotion policy to adapt to various manipulator configurations (different weights, lengths, or no end-effector) without requiring time-consuming re-training. This significantly reduces the development and deployment overhead for legged mobile manipulators that may need to perform diverse tasks with different payloads or tools. This adaptability is a substantial advantage over end-to-end RL systems that typically require retraining for such changes.
3. **Real-world Applicability:** The validation of the combined control system on physical hardware (ALMA platform) performing manipulation tasks on rough terrain demonstrates the practical feasibility and readiness of the approach. This is reported as the first published result of legged mobile manipulation on rough terrain using this combined methodology, indicating a step forward in the field.
4. **Foundation for Advanced Tasks:** The capability for precise manipulation while maintaining stable locomotion on challenging terrain opens avenues for a broader range of applications for legged mobile manipulators, including industrial inspection, construction, disaster response, and assistance in hazardous environments. Beyond direct manipulation, the principles can be extended to tasks like load carrying or pulling, where external forces need to be robustly handled.
5. **Efficiency in Training and Deployment:** The use of a wrench sequence generator during RL training avoids the need to run computationally intensive MPC throughout the learning phase, streamlining the policy development process. For deployment, the MPC provides accurate, physically consistent wrench predictions that the RL policy can effectively leverage.

A potential future impact identified by the authors is to transition from the base policy passively responding to external wrenches to having partial control over them, possibly by actively tracking desired wrenches with the manipulators. This could enable even more complex and challenging maneuvers, further expanding the capabilities of legged mobile manipulation systems.
