# Orienteering Problem with Uncertain Time-Varying Rewards: Framework and Benchmark for Everyday Service Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> Introduces OP-UTVR, an orienteering-problem variant where vertex rewards drift stochastically via an unknown Markov transition matrix instead of being static or known in advance. Compares three planners (one-step, offline, adaptive online) with theoretical suboptimality bounds, then validates on a pedestrian-interaction robot benchmark where adaptive replanning nearly matches an oracle (2.1% gap) versus 8.9% and 22.5% for offline and one-step planners.

## Problem

- Classical orienteering problem formulations assume rewards are static and known in advance, which fails for applications like shifting delivery demand or crowd-seeking service robots.
- Existing time-varying reward OP extensions require the full future reward function to be known exactly, an assumption invalid when rewards depend on unpredictable agents like pedestrians.
- No benchmark exists for evaluating routing planners under genuinely uncertain, stochastically evolving rewards in realistic indoor robot navigation settings.

## Method

- **OP-UTVR** formulation models rewards as evolving under an unknown ==Markov chain transition matrix==, letting agents estimate and predict reward dynamics from limited observations rather than assuming known dynamics.
- Three planners exploit predicted rewards differently: ==One-Step Planner== greedily maximizes next-step reward, ==Offline Planner== solves a fixed full-horizon plan once via ==A* with branch-and-bound pruning==.
- ==Adaptive Online Planner== replans the full-horizon path at every vertex arrival using updated transition-matrix-based reward estimates, with derived theoretical suboptimality bounds tied to prediction error and reward stochasticity.

## Results

- Mobile service robot benchmark across 100 test instances (10 indoor HM3D/Gibson maps times 10 vertex placements) shows Adaptive Online Planner wins highest reward in **64** cases versus **25** for Offline and **11** for One-Step.
- Optimality gap relative to the oracle planner is **2.1%** for Adaptive Online, **8.9%** for Offline, and **22.5%** for One-Step, with pairwise Wilcoxon tests significant at p less than 0.001.
- Environment analysis shows higher free-space ratio correlates with greater reward variance (r equals **0.36**), which correlates with larger Adaptive-over-Offline improvement (r equals **0.29**), for example **194** versus **135** reward in open spaces.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining long-horizon planning with runtime replanning against updated observations closes most of the gap to oracle performance, more than either component alone.
> - Reward stochasticity, not transition-matrix estimation error, is the fundamental obstacle: the suboptimality bound from limited demonstration samples shrinks with more data, unlike irreducible reward variance.
> - Fixed offline plans degrade specifically in open, high-variance environments where dynamics shift fastest, so planner choice should be conditioned on environment geometry, not just budget.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of selective routing problems, specifically addressing a gap in the existing literature on the Orienteering Problem (OP). The classical OP, introduced by Tsiligirides in 1984, involves finding a path to visit a sequence of locations that maximizes accumulated rewards under resource constraints, typically a time budget. Traditional OP formulations and many of its variants assume that rewards are either static or follow dynamics that are fully known to the agent in advance. These assumptions simplify the planning process, allowing for solutions based on precomputed optimal paths or dynamic programming on spatio-temporal graphs.

Previous extensions of the OP have incorporated various complexities such as time-window constraints, team orienteering, and applications in robotics. In robotics, OP has been adapted for persistent monitoring, unknown terrain exploration, and search-and-rescue operations by integrating concepts like spatial correlations, information gain, and survival constraints. While some prior work has addressed uncertainty in OP, these often model stochastic travel costs or rewards with known, stationary probability distributions, or time-varying rewards where future dynamics are entirely specified. For instance, approaches using Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) can model dynamic rewards, but typically require knowledge of the underlying state transition and reward functions.

The presented work, introducing the Orienteering Problem with Uncertain Time-Varying Rewards (OP-UTVR), differentiates itself by relaxing the assumption of known reward dynamics. It specifically tackles scenarios where rewards vary stochastically over time due to uncontrollable factors, and the agent can only estimate and forecast these dynamics for a limited horizon from observations, leading to inherent prediction errors. This addresses a practical challenge in real-world applications—such as mobile service robotics, where customer demand or pedestrian interactions are inherently dynamic and unpredictable—that existing OP formulations with static, known, or known-stochastic rewards do not fully capture. By enabling agents to adapt to observed reward changes and account for prediction errors, OP-UTVR aims to bridge the performance gap that arises when actual rewards deviate from static or perfectly predictable expectations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable mobile agents to plan and execute effective paths for collecting rewards that are inherently uncertain and vary over time across multiple destinations. This goal is motivated by the observation that in many real-world applications, rewards, such as customer demand for delivery agents, the probability of successful rescue in disaster zones, or pedestrian interactions for service robots, are neither static nor deterministically predictable.

The authors identify two specific challenges that existing Orienteering Problem (OP) formulations do not adequately address:
1. **Stochastic and Time-Varying Rewards:** Actual rewards in dynamic environments fluctuate in an unpredictable manner, making planning based on fixed or known-pattern rewards suboptimal.
2. **Limited Prediction Horizon and Inherent Errors:** Agents can only predict future reward dynamics for a limited time horizon, and these predictions are subject to errors relative to the true, unobservable reward evolution.

To address these challenges, the research introduces and formalizes a new variant of the problem, the Orienteering Problem with Uncertain Time-Varying Rewards (OP-UTVR). The key motivations for this formulation and its proposed solutions include:
- **Relaxing Strong Assumptions:** Moving beyond the common OP assumptions that rewards are static or their time-varying dynamics are fully known in advance. This aims to increase the practical applicability of OP to scenarios with genuine environmental stochasticity.
- **Enabling Informed Routing Decisions:** Developing a framework where agents can estimate underlying reward dynamics from observations, forecast future rewards, and use this information to make routing decisions, despite the inherent uncertainty and prediction errors.
- **Allowing Online Adaptivity:** Recognizing that in dynamic environments, fixed plans can quickly become suboptimal. The work motivates the need for online decision-making, where agents can continuously update their understanding of the environment and adapt their paths.
- **Supporting Re-visitation of Locations:** Acknowledging that rewards in certain applications are not one-time payments upon the first visit but can be accrued continually, or that the value of a location can change over time, necessitating multiple visits. This expands the problem's scope to include waiting at locations or repeatedly visiting them.
- **Providing Theoretical Foundations:** Investigating different planning approaches and deriving theoretical bounds on their performance under reward stochasticity, offering insights into the factors influencing planning effectiveness.
- **Developing a Realistic Benchmark:** Creating a mobile service robot benchmark based on real-world 3D scans and realistic pedestrian behaviors to rigorously evaluate and compare different planning strategies in a context that embodies the challenges of OP-UTVR. This provides a platform for future research and development in this domain.

The overarching motivation is to bridge the gap between theoretical OP formulations and the operational realities of autonomous agents in complex, dynamic, and unpredictable environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involves the formalization of the OP-UTVR, the development and theoretical analysis of three distinct planning algorithms, and the creation of a mobile service robot simulation benchmark for empirical evaluation.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation (OP-UTVR)

The OP-UTVR is defined by a tuple $(V, E, v_0, r_{tv}, \hat{P}, P, d, T_{\text{max}})$. Key features differentiating it from classical OP and its known time-varying reward extensions include:
- **Unknown True Rewards ($r_{tv}$):** The true time-varying reward $r_{tv}(v, t)$ at a vertex $v$ at time $t$ is unknown until $t$.
- **Markovian Reward Dynamics:** The underlying true reward dynamics $P$ is assumed to be a Markov chain over vertices, describing how rewards transition between locations. The agent does not know $P$ but has access to an estimate $\hat{P} \approx P$.
- **Reward Prediction:** Agents use $\hat{P}$ to estimate future rewards. Initially, $E[\hat{r}_{tv}(t) | r_{tv}(0)] = r_{tv}(0)\hat{P}^t$. Critically, agents can update their estimates during traversal using current observations: $E[\hat{r}_{tv}(t) | r_{tv}(t_{\text{now}})] = r_{tv}(t_{\text{now}})\hat{P}^{t - t_{\text{now}}}$.
- **Online Decision Making:** The solution is a policy $\pi$ that adaptively chooses the next vertex based on current circumstances and updated reward estimates.
- **Revisiting Vertices:** The formulation permits visiting the same vertex multiple times, including consecutively (waiting), with an associated travel/wait time $d(e(v,v)) > 0$.
- **Objective Function:** Maximize the expected total reward $E_{V \sim \pi} [\sum r_{tv}(v_i, T_i)]$ within the time budget $T_{\text{max}}$.
The authors also demonstrate that if $\hat{P} = P$, an instance of OP-UTVR can be cast as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).

#### 3.2. Planning Algorithms

Three distinct planning algorithms are investigated, differing in their planning horizon and adaptivity:
- **One-Step Planner ($\pi_{\text{one-step}}$):** A greedy approach that, at each vertex arrival, selects the next vertex maximizing only the predicted immediate reward (single-step prediction) using the most recent observations.
- **Offline Planner ($\pi_{\text{offline}}$):** Computes a complete path in advance at the beginning of the traversal (at $t=0$). This path maximizes the expected total reward over the entire time budget, using the initial reward observation $r_{tv}(0)$ and its estimated evolution via $\hat{P}$. This planner does not adapt during execution.
- **Adaptive Online Planner ($\pi_{\text{adapt}}$):** Plans an entire path to maximize expected total reward up to $T_{\text{max}}$, similar to the Offline Planner. However, it re-executes this planning process every time the agent arrives at a new vertex. This replanning incorporates updated reward observations $r_{tv}(t_{\text{now}})$ to refine future reward predictions, allowing it to adapt to deviations from earlier forecasts.

For Offline and Adaptive Online Planners, the path computation is performed using A\* search, treating rewards as negative costs. Branch-and-bound pruning is used with an upper bound on remaining expected reward as the admissible heuristic.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Analysis

The paper provides theoretical insights into the performance of the proposed planners:
- **Theorem 1:** States that if the estimated reward transition $\hat{P}$ is accurate (i.e., $\hat{P} = P$), the Adaptive Online Planner achieves the optimal solution, effectively reducing the problem to a known-dynamics MDP.
- **Theorem 2:** Quantifies the suboptimality of the Adaptive Online Planner when $\hat{P}$ is an empirical distribution learned from $|D|$ i.i.d. samples. It bounds the difference between its expected total reward and the optimal policy's reward, showing it shrinks with more transition samples. The bound is $|R_{\text{tot}}(\pi^*) - R_{\text{tot}}(\pi_{\text{adapt}})| \leq 2r_{\text{max}}T_{\text{max}}\sqrt{\frac{\ln(2|V|^2/\delta)}{2|D|}}$, with probability $1-\delta$.
- **Theorem 3:** Compares Adaptive Online Planner to Offline Planner, showing the advantage of adaptation. It states that $R_{\text{tot}}(\pi_{\text{adapt}}) - R_{\text{tot}}(\pi_{\text{offline}}) \leq r_{\text{max}}T_{\text{max}}\sqrt{N \ln |V| / 2}$, where $N$ is the number of reward objects with nondeterministic transitions. This suggests that the benefit of adaptation increases with higher reward stochasticity.

#### 3.4. Mobile Service Robot Benchmark

A simulation environment is developed to evaluate OP-UTVR:
- **Environment Construction:** Built on IR-SIM, using real-world 3D scans from HM3D and Gibson datasets. It features 10 indoor environments (25m x 25m), each with 13 gathering spots (vertices) defined by Voronoi regions. Shortest paths between spots are precomputed using A\* search.
- **Robot Modeling:** An omnidirectional robot with a maximum velocity of 1.2 m/s. It uses the Reciprocal Velocity Obstacles (RVO) model for collision avoidance with pedestrians. The travel time $d$ is approximated, introducing uncertainty.
- **Pedestrian Modeling:** 20 pedestrians per environment, whose movements between gathering spots follow a Markov chain with a specified transition matrix $P$. This matrix is designed to create stochastic crowd behaviors conditioned on latent intent (e.g., specific loops). Pedestrians also use RVO for collision avoidance.
- **Transition Matrix Estimation:** $\hat{P}$ is estimated from 240 seconds of observed pedestrian trajectory data (without robot presence) using least-squares optimization.
- **Reward Dynamics:** Reward $r_{tv}(v,t)$ is defined as the number of pedestrians within the Voronoi region of vertex $v$ at time $t$. A waiting time of 5 seconds is set for remaining at a vertex.

#### 3.5. Evaluation Setup

- **Baselines:** A simple Greedy Planner ($\pi_{\text{greedy}}$) that always moves to the vertex with the highest current pedestrian count, and an Oracle Planner ($\pi_{\text{oracle}}$) which plans using true future pedestrian distributions (providing a coarse upper bound).
- **Metrics:** Total reward ($R_{\text{tot}}$), Optimality gap ($\Delta_{\text{opt}}$) relative to $\pi_{\text{oracle}}$, and Computation time ($CT$). Experiments were conducted across 100 test instances.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted using a mobile service robot benchmark across 100 test instances, provided quantitative and qualitative results on the performance of the proposed planners.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Results

- **Overall Performance:** The Oracle Planner ($\pi_{\text{oracle}}$) consistently achieved the highest total reward, as it operates with knowledge of true future pedestrian distributions. The Greedy Planner ($\pi_{\text{greedy}}$), which relies solely on immediate observations, yielded the lowest total reward.
- **Validation of Predicted Dynamics:** All three proposed planners (One-Step, Offline, and Adaptive Online), which incorporate predicted reward dynamics, demonstrably outperformed the $\pi_{\text{greedy}}$ baseline. This indicates that leveraging predicted dynamics is beneficial even when the true dynamics are not fully known.
- **Planner Comparison:**
 - The **Adaptive Online Planner ($\pi_{\text{adapt}}$)** achieved the highest total reward in the majority of cases (64 out of 100), followed by the Offline Planner ($\pi_{\text{offline}}$) (25 cases) and One-Step Planner ($\pi_{\text{one-step}}$) (11 cases).
 - **Optimality Gap:** Relative to $\pi_{\text{oracle}}$, $\pi_{\text{one-step}}$ exhibited a 22.5% optimality gap due to its myopic decision-making. $\pi_{\text{offline}}$ reduced this gap to 8.9% by planning over the full time horizon. $\pi_{\text{adapt}}$ achieved the smallest gap of 2.1%, demonstrating near-oracle performance. Statistical analysis confirmed that $\pi_{\text{adapt}}$ significantly outperformed both $\pi_{\text{offline}}$ and $\pi_{\text{one-step}}$ (pairwise Wilcoxon signed-rank, Holm-corrected p < 0.001).
- **Computational Trade-offs:**
 - $\pi_{\text{one-step}}$ required minimal computation time, as it only evaluates immediate rewards for the next step.
 - $\pi_{\text{offline}}$ involved moderate computation, solving the planning problem once at the start.
 - $\pi_{\text{adapt}}$ incurred the highest computational cost due to its continuous replanning. However, this increased cost correlated with its superior performance.
- **Conclusion on Planning Strategy:** The quantitative results suggest that an effective strategy involves combining long-horizon planning with adaptive replanning that integrates runtime observations.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Results

The paper presents visual examples of robot trajectories for the three planners, illustrating their behaviors:
- In one instance, $\pi_{\text{adapt}}$ achieved a total reward of 184, while $\pi_{\text{offline}}$ and $\pi_{\text{one-step}}$ yielded 118 and 121, respectively.
- **Offline Planner:** Tend to repeatedly visit a few vertices. Its initial plan, being fixed, could not adapt to evolving reward dynamics. Over time, its initial predictions would converge, offering diminished expected gains from further movement.
- **One-Step Planner:** Demonstrated responsiveness to current observations but its myopic nature prevented it from achieving high cumulative rewards as it failed to optimize over a longer horizon.
- **Adaptive Online Planner:** Exhibited dynamic behavior, visiting a diverse set of vertices where pedestrians congregated. This was attributed to its ability to replan with updated observations while maintaining a multi-step optimization horizon.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Environmental Effects

The study also analyzed the relationship between environmental characteristics and planner performance:
- **Free Space and Reward Variance:** Environments with a higher free space ratio were found to correlate with greater temporal reward variance (Pearson correlation, r = 0.36, p < 0.001). This suggests that more open spaces allow for increased pedestrian movement and thus more fluctuating rewards.
- **Adaptivity Improvement:** Higher reward variance was correlated with a greater improvement of $\pi_{\text{adapt}}$ over $\pi_{\text{offline}}$ (r = 0.29, p < 0.01). This empirically supports Theorem 3, indicating that adaptation becomes more valuable in highly stochastic environments.
- **Specific Instances:** Qualitative examples illustrated this trend. In open spaces, $\pi_{\text{adapt}}$ generally performed better (e.g., 194 rewards for $\pi_{\text{adapt}}$ vs. 135 for $\pi_{\text{offline}}$ in one instance). In more constrained spaces, $\pi_{\text{offline}}$ could sometimes outperform $\pi_{\text{adapt}}$ (e.g., 241 vs. 193 in another instance), suggesting that in some less dynamic or predictable constrained environments, a fixed, pre-optimized plan might be sufficient or even marginally superior, potentially due to reduced planning overhead.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces the Orienteering Problem with Uncertain Time-Varying Rewards (OP-UTVR), addressing a significant gap in the existing literature where most OP formulations assume rewards are static or their dynamics are fully known. This novel problem formulation has several implications and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Real-World Applicability:** By explicitly accounting for uncertain and time-varying rewards, OP-UTVR brings the theoretical framework of the Orienteering Problem closer to the complexities of real-world dynamic environments. This is particularly relevant for applications in mobile service robotics, delivery services, disaster response, and urban logistics, where demand, opportunities, or environmental conditions are inherently unpredictable.
- **Theoretical Advancement:** The formalization of OP-UTVR and the investigation of different planning algorithms provide a new dimension to OP research. The theoretical bounds derived for the Adaptive Online Planner, linking its suboptimality to prediction errors and reward stochasticity, offer fundamental insights into the value of adaptivity in such settings. The proof that the Adaptive Online Planner achieves optimality under perfect model knowledge reinforces its theoretical foundation.
- **Practical Planning Strategies:** The empirical results demonstrate that a hybrid approach combining long-horizon planning with online adaptation through runtime observations yields near-optimal performance in uncertain, time-varying environments. This finding guides the design of practical planning systems for autonomous agents operating in dynamic human-centric environments. It underscores that while multi-step planning is crucial, continuous adjustment based on new information is equally vital for robustness and effectiveness.
- **New Benchmark for Robotics:** The introduction of the mobile service robot benchmark, built on real-world 3D scans and simulating realistic pedestrian behaviors, provides a standardized and challenging environment for evaluating and comparing future planning algorithms for OP-UTVR. This benchmark facilitates reproducible research and accelerates progress in embodied AI and service robotics.
- **Insights into Trade-offs:** The research highlights the inherent trade-offs between planning horizon, adaptivity, and computational cost. It shows that while adaptive, long-horizon planning demands more computational resources, its performance gains in dynamic environments are substantial, justifying the increased cost. This informs system designers about resource allocation for autonomous agents.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work opens several avenues for future research, including:
 - Investigating more complex reward dynamics (e.g., non-Markovian transitions).
 - Developing more efficient and scalable algorithms for larger graphs and longer time horizons.
 - Validating these planning strategies on physical robots in real-world scenarios.
 - Exploring learning-based approaches for more robust prediction of reward dynamics in the absence of pre-collected data.

In summary, this research advances the state-of-the-art in combinatorial optimization and robotics by providing a robust framework and empirical validation for intelligent agents to operate effectively in environments characterized by unpredictable, time-varying rewards.
