# Keyframe-Guided Structured Rewards for Reinforcement Learning in Long-Horizon Laboratory Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Science and Technology of China developed a framework that generates structured, dense rewards for reinforcement learning in long-horizon laboratory robotics by automatically extracting kinematics-aware keyframes from demonstrations. The approach achieved an 82% average success rate across four complex laboratory tasks, notably reaching 100% on "Petri Dish De-lidding" and "Precision Liquid Transfer," significantly outperforming existing baselines.

## Problem

- Long-horizon, multi-stage laboratory tasks suffer from reward sparsity, leading to inefficient exploration and unstable convergence in high-dimensional action spaces.
- Standard reinforcement learning methods struggle to leverage the strict physical and logical constraints inherent in laboratory protocols.
- Existing approaches often rely on high-quality expert demonstrations or labor-intensive manual reward engineering, limiting scalability and robustness.

## Method

- A Latent Dynamics-Based Keyframe Extraction module automatically identifies critical operational states (keyframes) from potentially non-expert demonstrations using multi-view latent encoding and kinematics-aware analysis.
- A Generative Diffusion-Based Online Reward System constructs dense, stage-wise rewards by measuring the multi-view latent similarity between the robot's current state and a sequence of keyframe-anchored target states.
- The framework integrates human-in-the-loop policy optimization with a pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model (Octo) backbone, utilizing a mixed-buffer strategy for efficient learning.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved an average success rate of **82%** across four real-world laboratory tasks (e.g., Petri Dish De-lidding, Precision Liquid Transfer), significantly outperforming baselines like HG-DAgger (**42%**) and Hil-ConRFT (**47%**).
- Learning curves demonstrated substantially faster policy convergence and a rapid decrease in human intervention rates, approaching near zero, indicating high sample efficiency.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the kinematics-aware keyframe extraction mechanism significantly improved step-wise coverage (**0.933** vs. **0.742**) and core milestone recall (**0.938** vs. **0.525**), and removing keyframe-guided rewards led to severe performance drops and erroneous behaviors.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Automatically identifying kinematics-aware keyframes from demonstration data is crucial for capturing the intrinsic procedural logic of complex tasks and generating effective intermediate guidance.
> - Providing dense, stage-wise rewards derived from these structural keyframes effectively mitigates reward sparsity and significantly accelerates policy convergence in high-precision, long-horizon manipulation.
> - Combining multi-view perception for robust state representation, keyframe-guided rewards, and human-in-the-loop fine-tuning on a VLA backbone enables efficient and robust learning for delicate laboratory operations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of laboratory automation aims to enable robots to autonomously perform delicate and precise operations, such as pipetting and sample handling, which are fundamental for long-term biological experiments and the transition to flexible, autonomous laboratory systems. Despite advancements in robotic reinforcement learning (RL) and imitation learning (IL), achieving robust, high-precision manipulation under strict procedural constraints in laboratory settings remains a notable challenge.

Existing methods face several difficulties:
- **Reward Sparsity:** Long-horizon, multi-stage tasks common in laboratories (e.g., a complete pipetting procedure involving alignment, insertion, aspiration, transfer, and reset) typically offer only sparse terminal rewards. This leads to inefficient exploration and unstable convergence in high-dimensional continuous action spaces.
- **Multi-Stage Structural Constraints:** Laboratory protocols inherently follow strict stepwise physical and logical constraints, which are not explicitly leveraged by standard RL formulations.
- **Noisy or Imperfect Demonstrations:** Imitation learning approaches, such as HG-DAgger, rely heavily on high-quality expert demonstrations and are sensitive to suboptimal data.
- **Manual Reward Engineering:** Human-in-the-loop (HiL) reinforcement learning methods, like Hil-SERL and Hil-ConRFT, improve safety and sample efficiency but often still rely on sparse or manually designed reward signals. Designing these rewards for complex, precision-critical laboratory tasks can be challenging and task-specific.
- **Perception Ambiguity:** Single-view perception can introduce depth ambiguity and occlusion, increasing uncertainty in state estimation crucial for accurate reward computation.

This research addresses these limitations by focusing on integrating the intrinsic stepwise structure of biological protocols into the reward generation process for RL. It aims to develop a method that provides dense, structurally informed rewards without requiring extensive manual engineering or relying solely on perfect expert demonstrations. The work builds upon recent progress in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which offer a scalable paradigm for generalizable robotic manipulation, and incorporates reinforcement fine-tuning strategies to adapt these large models to specific tasks. By automating the extraction of key operational stages and using them to construct a progress-aware reward system, the paper attempts to bridge the gap between high-level procedural logic and low-level robotic control in complex laboratory environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that can leverage the inherent multi-stage structure of laboratory protocols to mitigate reward sparsity and enhance the learning efficiency of reinforcement learning agents for long-horizon, high-precision manipulation tasks. This is pursued without necessitating meticulously engineered rewards or expert-quality demonstrations.

Specifically, the core motivations and objectives are:

- **Overcoming Reward Sparsity:** In long-horizon laboratory tasks, agents typically receive feedback only upon task completion, leading to a "feedback vacuum" that hinders exploration and stable convergence in high-dimensional action spaces. The objective is to generate continuous, intermediate guidance to address this.
- **Exploiting Intrinsic Procedural Structure:** Biological protocols follow strict physical and logical sequences. The motivation is to automatically identify and transform these structural elements into actionable learning signals for the robot, aligning policy optimization with the task's inherent logic.
- **Reducing Reliance on Expert Demonstrations and Manual Reward Engineering:** Traditional methods often demand high-quality expert demonstrations or labor-intensive manual reward function design. An objective is to create a system that can automatically extract meaningful supervision from non-expert demonstrations and generate rewards without manual specification.
- **Improving Learning Efficiency and Stability:** By providing dense, structured rewards, the framework aims to accelerate policy convergence and improve the stability of learning in continuous control tasks that demand sub-millimeter precision.
- **Integrating Multi-modal Perception:** Addressing perception challenges like depth ambiguity and occlusion from single views by incorporating multi-view visual encoding to create more robust state representations.
- **Developing a Scalable Solution for Laboratory Automation:** The ultimate motivation is to provide a method that can be effectively deployed in real-world laboratory robotics, enabling more flexible and autonomous execution of complex procedures.

The main contributions designed to achieve these objectives include:
- A kinematics-aware keyframe extraction mechanism for automatically identifying critical operational states from non-expert demonstrations.
- A latent similarity-based, stage-wise reward formulation that converts extracted keyframes into progress-aware dense rewards for RL.
- The integration of multi-view perception, keyframe-guided rewards, and human-in-the-loop fine-tuning on a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) backbone for real-world laboratory manipulation.
- Validation of the framework through extensive real-world experiments across four laboratory automation tasks, aiming to demonstrate improvements in convergence speed and success rate.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, termed the Keyframe-Guided Reward Generation Framework, integrates online interaction with multi-stage reward signals to accelerate policy convergence and enable high-precision manipulation. The framework comprises three main modules: Latent Dynamics-Based Keyframe Extraction, Generative Diffusion-Based Online Reward System, and Human-in-the-Loop (HiL) Policy Optimization.

**A. Latent Dynamics-Based Keyframe Extraction**
This module is responsible for automatically identifying structurally critical states, or "keyframes," from demonstration sequences.
1. **Multi-View Latent Encoding:** Given multi-view observations $o_t = \{o_{front,t}, o_{wrist,t}\}$, a discrete vector-quantized encoder $E$, pre-trained on offline demonstrations, generates compact latent representations. A composite feature $f_t$ is constructed by concatenating the latents from the front camera and a weighted latent from the wrist camera, specifically $f_t = concat(E(o_{front,t}), \lambda E(o_{wrist,t}))$ with a view weight $\lambda = 1.5$.
2. **Kinematics-Aware Dynamics Analysis:** To detect significant transitions, 1D Gaussian smoothing is applied to $f_t$ along the temporal axis to obtain $\tilde{f}_t$. Latent velocity $v_t = \|\tilde{f}_t - \tilde{f}_{t-1}\|_2$ and acceleration $a_t = v_t - v_{t-1}$ are then computed.
3. **Turning-Point Mining (Stage 1):** Candidate turning points are identified by detecting peaks in both the latent velocity ($v_t$) and the absolute latent acceleration ($|a_t|$). This process aims to capture moments of significant change in the robot's state and motion.
4. **Fixed-Budget Selection (Stage 2):** From the set of candidate turning points, a fixed number of keyframes (set to $K=6$) are selected. The selection is based on a transition score $s(k) = max(v_k, |a_k|)$, prioritizing points with pronounced kinematic changes. Each selected keyframe generates view-specific latents $\bar{z}_v^h = E(o_v^{k_h})$.

**B. Generative Diffusion-Based Online Reward System**
This module constructs stage-wise rewards during online execution to guide the reinforcement learning process.
1. **Target Sequence Generation:** A diffusion-based model is used to generate a stage-wise target sequence $\{\hat{z}_v^h\}_{H=1}$ (with $H=8$ states) conditioned on the task instruction and initial observation. These generated targets serve as generalized references for the reward module.
2. **Anchor-Centric Sequence Construction:** To ensure the generated sequence for reward calculation aligns with key operational stages and has a consistent length, the $K$ extracted keyframes serve as fixed temporal anchors. The remaining slots in the sequence (up to $H=8$) are filled by uniformly sampling from non-keyframe indices in the demonstration, ensuring the final task state is included. This creates a subset of observations prioritized for reward calculation.
3. **Multi-View Fusion Similarity:** The similarity between the current robot state and the generated target keyframes is evaluated using cosine similarity in the latent space. The similarity $S_t(h)$ for a given target stage $h$ is a weighted sum of similarities from different camera views ($V = \{front, wrist\}$), where $w_v$ are view weights (e.g., $w_{wrist}=0.7, w_{front}=0.3$ for Precision Liquid Transfer, as detailed in the Appendix).
4. **Progress Tracking and Reward Calculation:** A progress state $M_t$ (ranging from 1 to $H$) is maintained. It advances to $M_t+1$ when the current state's similarity to the current target $S_t(M_t)$ exceeds a predefined threshold $\theta$ (e.g., $0.6-0.8$ depending on the task) and $M_t$ is not the final stage. The guided reward $R_{total}$ is formulated as a small step penalty $\epsilon_{step} = -0.05$ plus a positive stage-specific reward $G_{stage}(M_{t+1})$ if progress is made. $G_{stage}(n)$ is a geometric progression $r_1 q^{n-1}$ (with $q=1.3$) ensuring higher rewards for later stages, with a fixed total reward budget of 10 across all $H$ stages.

**C. Online Policy Evolution and Q-Learning Update**
The policy is refined through online interaction and human intervention.
1. **Policy Backbone:** The system uses Octo, a pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model, as its policy backbone. The action head is based on a consistency policy, distilling a diffusion policy's iterative denoising into a single-step inference for real-time control.
2. **Exploration:** Stochastic exploration is maintained by sampling noise $w \sim N(0, \sigma^2 I)$, which generates diverse actions.
3. **Mixed-Buffer Strategy:** Policy updates utilize a mixed-buffer approach, sampling equally from an online replay buffer ($D_{online}$) and a demonstration buffer ($D_{demo}$).
4. **Human-in-the-Loop (HiL) Mechanism:** Operators can intervene during unsafe or suboptimal behaviors. These corrective actions are stored in $D_{demo}$, providing additional expert guidance. The overall optimization follows the HiL-ConRFT framework.
5. **Critic Network Update:** The critic network $Q_{\theta}$ is optimized using Bellman regression. The target value $y = r + \gamma \cdot \min_i Q_{\bar{\theta}_i}(s', a')$ uses a target ensemble to reduce overestimation, where $\gamma$ is the discount factor. The critic loss $L_{critic}$ is the mean squared error between the predicted Q-value and the target.
6. **Actor Network Update:** The actor policy $\pi_{\phi}$ is optimized by minimizing a weighted combination of a behavior cloning (BC) loss $L_{BC}$ (to stay close to demonstrated actions) and a Q-loss $L_Q$ (to maximize expected critic value). The weights are $\lambda_{bc}=0.5$ and $\lambda_q=1$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated its framework on four real-world laboratory manipulation tasks using a Jaka Minicobo robot, with observation space consisting of dual-view RGB images and proprioceptive states, and a 6-DoF incremental end-effector pose action space augmented with task-specific dimensions. Initial object positions were randomized to promote robustness.

**A. Comparison with Baseline Methods:**
The proposed method ("Ours") was compared against three baselines:
- **HG-DAgger:** A supervised learning method for policy fine-tuning with manual online corrections.
- **Hil-ConRFT:** An advanced framework combining human interventions with reinforcement fine-tuning.
- **Hil-SERL:** A reinforcement learning method integrating human interventions and utilizing offline data for accelerated online exploration.

All baselines used a penalty-based dense reward structure ($-0.05$ step penalty, $+10$ terminal reward) to align with the total potential reward magnitude of the proposed framework. Replay buffer initialization and mixed sampling were synchronized.

The main findings from the comparison were:
- **Overall Success Rate:** After 40-60 minutes of online fine-tuning, the proposed keyframe-guided method achieved an average success rate of 82% across the four tasks. In contrast, HG-DAgger achieved 42%, Hil-ConRFT achieved 47%, and Hil-SERL achieved 0%, indicating its inability to solve these long-horizon, high-precision tasks under conventional reward schemes.
- **Task-Specific Performance:** The proposed method achieved a 100% success rate on "Petri Dish De-lidding" and "Precision Liquid Transfer" tasks, and 80% on "Pipette Tip Attachment." Notably, for "Precision Liquid Transfer," baselines HG-DAgger and Hil-SERL had 0% success, while Hil-ConRFT only reached 10%. This highlights the effectiveness of the keyframe-guided approach in tasks with strict physical logic and sequence constraints.
- **Learning Efficiency and Human Intervention:** The learning curves demonstrated that the proposed method exhibited the steepest upward trend in success rate and a rapid decrease in human intervention rate, approaching near zero in a short period. This indicates high sample efficiency and the agent's ability to quickly leverage the intermediate guidance signals for correct state-action mappings.

**B. Ablation Studies:**

1. **Effectiveness of Keyframe Extraction Mechanism (KE):**
 - An ablation study compared the kinematics-heuristic KE with a standard time-based uniform sampling strategy (w/o KE).
 - **Quantitative Results (Table II):** KE significantly improved "Step-wise Coverage (All)" (0.933 vs. 0.742 mean) and "Core Milestone Recall (Core)" (0.938 vs. 0.525 mean). The advantage was most pronounced in "Precision Liquid Transfer," where KE achieved a perfect 1.000 recall of core milestones, compared to 0.400 for uniform sampling.
 - **Qualitative Analysis (Fig. 4):** Uniform sampling failed to capture critical physical bottleneck states (e.g., pipette insertion, aspiration), leading to erroneous spatial guidance and execution failures like collisions. KE, by contrast, anchored these intermediate states, preserving the underlying logic of complex procedures.

2. **Impact of Keyframe-Guided Reward:**
 - Comparison between the full proposed pipeline and HIL-ConRFT (which shares the same VLA backbone, consistency policy, and HiL paradigm but relies on sparse terminal rewards) demonstrated the impact of the keyframe-guided dense reward.
 - **Performance Drop:** Removing the keyframe-guided reward led to a severe performance drop. In "Precision Liquid Transfer," the success rate plummeted from 100% (Ours) to 10% (HIL-ConRFT).
 - **Failure Modes:** Without intermediate state guidance, the baseline policy frequently fell into local optima, exhibiting erroneous behaviors such as premature spraying or insufficient lifting height.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a structured approach to addressing significant challenges in robotic manipulation for laboratory automation. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Mitigation of Reward Sparsity and Credit Assignment:** By automatically extracting kinematics-aware keyframes and transforming them into dense, stage-wise reward signals, the framework effectively mitigates the reward sparsity problem inherent in long-horizon, multi-stage tasks. This direct, continuous feedback addresses the temporal credit assignment challenge in RL.
- **Leveraging Intrinsic Procedural Logic:** The method explicitly utilizes the inherent stepwise structure of laboratory protocols, which is often overlooked by conventional RL approaches. Aligning policy optimization with this procedural logic can lead to more robust and compliant robot behaviors in precision-critical applications.
- **Reduced Reliance on Manual Effort:** The framework reduces the need for manually engineered reward functions, which are often task-specific and labor-intensive to design for complex laboratory procedures. It also lessens the dependency on meticulously perfect expert demonstrations by extracting meaningful supervision from potentially non-expert data.
- **Enhanced Learning Efficiency and Robustness:** Experimental results demonstrate substantially improved convergence speed and higher task success rates compared to baseline methods. The rapid reduction in human intervention during training indicates a more sample-efficient learning process and an accelerated path to autonomous operation.
- **Scalability for Laboratory Automation:** By providing a generalizable method for generating structured rewards, the framework offers a scalable solution for deploying reinforcement learning in diverse and evolving laboratory automation scenarios, beyond rigid preprogrammed pipelines. This can accelerate scientific discovery by enabling more flexible and adaptive robotic systems.
- **Addressing Common Failure Modes:** The method's ability to capture critical intermediate steps (bottleneck states) effectively prevents common failure modes in sequential precision tasks, such as misalignments or collisions, as demonstrated in complex liquid handling.

While the current implementation relies on RGB-based latent similarity, which may be sensitive to visual artifacts, the authors propose future work to explore multimodal representations (tactile, depth, 3D geometric features) and automated environment reset mechanisms. Overall, this research provides a pathway for advancing autonomous, high-precision robotics in scientific laboratories, potentially impacting fields such as drug discovery, synthetic biology, and material science by making complex experiments more reliable and automated.
