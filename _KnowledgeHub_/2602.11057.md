# Divide, Harmonize, Then Conquer It: Shooting Multi-Commodity Flow Problems with Multimodal Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> PRAM, a framework from Zhejiang University and Hefei University of Technology, addresses large-scale Multi-Commodity Flow (MCF) problems by integrating multimodal language models (MLMs) and problem decomposition. This approach achieves near-optimal allocations while significantly reducing computation time, demonstrating up to 100x speedup over traditional LP solvers on large topologies and robust performance against network failures and demand fluctuations.

## Problem

- Traditional Linear Programming (LP) solvers for Multi-Commodity Flow (MCF) problems suffer from poor scalability and high computational cost (O(d^(2.3729))) for large networks, making them impractical for real-time applications.
- Existing machine learning (ML)-based MCF solutions often require extensive retraining, specialized Deep Neural Network (DNN) architectures, and struggle with generalization to unseen conditions due to the 'curse of dimensionality' in representing flow allocations.
- Service providers face a trade-off between obtaining computationally prohibitive optimal solutions and fast heuristic solutions that compromise significantly on quality for large-scale allocation systems.

## Method

- **PRAM** decomposes large MCF problems into smaller, source-node-specific ==subproblems==, reducing model complexity and enabling parallel processing.
- A shared Multimodal Language Model (MLM) backbone acts as the core 'agent,' processing subgraphs (as visual inputs) and demand data (as textual prompts) to determine commodity flow configurations.
- A lightweight multi-agent adaptation framework uses LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) for internal communication and learnable global context embeddings for external guidance, fine-tuning the MLM with Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) and counterfactual reasoning to achieve coordinated decision-making.

## Results

- PRAM achieved near-optimal allocation quality, often ranking second only to ideal LP solvers with perfect foresight, and occasionally outperforming predictive LP by **21%** (CERNET) to **45%** (GÉANT) in minimizing MLU.
- The framework demonstrated strong scalability on large datasets, completing flow allocation in under 25 seconds for the largest Kdl topology (754 nodes), achieving **5x** to **100x** speedups over POP, LP-top, and LP respectively, while retaining over **90%** of LP's performance.
- PRAM exhibited robustness to unseen situations, showing less than **10%** performance degradation during multiple link failures and less than **15%** degradation under high demand fluctuations (α=2) without re-adaptation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - MLMs possess emergent reasoning and generalization abilities, allowing them to effectively process both visual network topologies and textual demand data, reducing the need for extensive manual feature engineering in combinatorial optimization.
> - Decomposing the MCF problem into smaller subproblems and employing a shared MLM significantly improves scalability, transforming the complexity from O(|V|^2) to O(|V|).
> - PRAM's MARL adaptation, supported by theoretical proofs of convergence, effectively guides the MLM to simulate gradient descent, enabling it to learn near-optimal solutions for convex/concave MCF objectives.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The Multi-Commodity Flow (MCF) problem is a foundational challenge in network flow and combinatorial optimization, with widespread applications across domains such as transportation, communication, logistics, energy, and cloud computing. Traditionally, MCF problems have been addressed using optimization-based algorithms, primarily Linear Programming (LP). These LP-based methods offer theoretical guarantees for computing near-optimal solutions. However, their practical application faces significant limitations:
- **Scalability:** LP solvers become computationally expensive and time-consuming as the problem size increases, particularly in modern systems involving thousands of nodes and links or millions of commodities with unpredictable demands. The worst-case complexity of LP is approximately O(d^(2.3729)) for 'd' variables, making it impractical for large-scale scenarios where solution times can extend to hours.
- **Real-time Constraints:** Optimal LP solutions often require complete, real-time knowledge of all commodities, which is frequently unattainable in dynamic environments. Practical implementations typically rely on historical data or predictions, leading to suboptimal performance due to the uncertain nature of real-time data.

In response to these limitations, machine learning (ML) based solutions have emerged, aiming to bypass iterative optimization processes by directly determining routing decisions using deep neural networks (DNNs). While generally faster, existing ML-based methods also exhibit shortcomings:
- **High Engineering Costs:** They often require extensive retraining and specialized DNN architectures, such as Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), which are labor-intensive to design and optimize for complex, target-specific scenarios.
- **Poor Generalization:** DNNs trained on specific environments may not perform effectively when faced with unseen or dynamic conditions.
- **Curse of Dimensionality:** Representing flow allocations still requires a large number of variables (e.g., O(|V|^2) path weights), limiting scalability for topologies with many nodes.

This paper introduces PRAM (Partitioned Resource Allocation with Multimodal Language Models) as an ML-based method that aims to overcome the dual challenges of optimality and tractability. PRAM departs from monolithic optimization by decomposing the MCF problem into smaller subproblems, a strategy previously explored by methods like NCFlow and POP. Its distinguishing feature is the integration of Multimodal Language Models (MLMs) as the core "agent" for solving these subproblems. This approach seeks to leverage the emergent reasoning and generalization abilities of MLMs, reducing engineering costs and improving robustness compared to earlier ML methods, while offering theoretical guarantees for convergence not commonly found in previous ML-based solutions for MCF.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a machine learning-based method capable of efficiently solving large-scale Multi-Commodity Flow (MCF) problems while achieving high-quality, near-optimal allocations. This work is motivated by the growing challenges faced by existing optimization engines in balancing optimality and tractability in modern, rapidly expanding allocation systems. Service providers frequently encounter a trade-off dilemma where obtaining optimal solutions is computationally prohibitive, while fast heuristic solutions often compromise significantly on quality.

The key motivations and objectives for proposing PRAM are:

- **Addressing Scalability and Tractability:** Traditional Linear Programming (LP) solvers, while accurate, become impractically slow for large-scale networks with thousands of nodes and millions of potential flow variables. Existing ML-based methods, despite being faster, often struggle with the "curse of dimensionality" and high engineering overheads, making them difficult to deploy at scale. PRAM aims to provide a solution that scales efficiently by decomposing the problem.
- **Leveraging Multimodal Language Models (MLMs):** The paper posits that MLMs possess "emergent abilities" (such as mathematical reasoning and generalization to unforeseen conditions) acquired during pre-training on vast datasets. These capabilities are seen as crucial for:
 - Quickly computing high-quality allocations for subproblems.
 - Mitigating the need for extensive retraining and manual design of specialized deep neural networks for complex inputs, as MLMs can process both topology (as images) and demand data (as text tokens).
- **Achieving Near-Optimal Performance:** The goal is to develop a method that not only accelerates the allocation process but also yields solutions comparable to, and in some cases surpassing, traditional LP solvers. This includes optimizing for various objectives like minimizing maximum link utilization (MLU), maximizing total flow (MTF), and maximizing concurrent flow (MCF).
- **Ensuring Robustness and Generalization:** Modern networks are dynamic and prone to unforeseen events like link failures or sudden demand bursts. PRAM is motivated by the need for a solution that can adapt to such changes without significant performance degradation or requiring re-adaptation. The emergent generalization abilities of MLMs are hypothesized to contribute to this robustness.
- **Providing Theoretical Foundations:** Many ML-based optimization methods lack strong theoretical guarantees. A key objective of PRAM is to demonstrate theoretical convergence properties, specifically showing that it can provably converge to the optimum within the family of MCF problems by learning to perform gradient descent in context.
- **Practical Integration:** The solution is designed to be objective-agnostic and seamlessly integrable with mainstream allocation systems, providing a practical and scalable tool for future network management.

The specific contributions outlined in the paper reflect these objectives:
- Proposing a distributed approach (PRAM) to divide and optimize MCF problems, enabling efficiency at scale, a departure from centralized consensus methods.
- Developing the first end-to-end MCF solver built on off-the-shelf MLMs, minimizing manual design overhead.
- Introducing a lightweight and interpretable adaptation framework that addresses partition effects and utilizes the MLM's reasoning for diverse flow allocation tasks.
- Establishing theoretical results demonstrating that PRAM can approximate near-optimal solutions, providing performance guarantees.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The PRAM framework is designed to solve Multi-Commodity Flow (MCF) problems by integrating problem decomposition with the reasoning capabilities of Multimodal Language Models (MLMs) and multi-agent reinforcement learning.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The MCF problem is defined on a directed graph G(V, E, c), where V represents nodes, E represents directed links, and c: E → R+ specifies link capacities. Each source-destination pair (s, t) has a predefined set of candidate paths P_s,t and historical demand D_s,t. The objective is to periodically determine an optimal configuration π, which distributes commodity flow using path weights r_p, to optimize various objectives such as minimizing maximum link utilization (MLU), maximizing total flow (MTF), or maximizing concurrent flow (MCF). The problem is known to be NP-complete.

#### 3.2. PRAM Architecture and Components

PRAM operates through three core components: a partition module, an MLM-based agent module, and an adaptation module (Figure 3).

##### 3.2.1. Multimodal Problem Partition

- **Rationale:** To address the scalability bottleneck, PRAM avoids monolithic optimization by decomposing the large MCF problem into smaller, tractable subproblems.
- **Mechanism:** The decomposition occurs at the node level, grouping commodities originating from the same source node into a single subset. This reduces model complexity from O(|V|^2) to O(|V|), enabling parallel processing.
- **Input Representation:**
 - **Visual Input:** Subgraphs (representing all routing links from a specific source node to other nodes) are plotted as images. These images are then fed into the MLM through a vision encoder (e.g., CLIP). Link capacities are explicitly indicated on these visual representations.
 - **Textual Input:** Subtask-aware prompting is used, where recent demands for each commodity subset are explicitly paired with key descriptions (e.g., source node specification) and statistics (e.g., historical rolling average demand). This text is tokenized and processed by the MLM.
- **Agent:** A shared MLM backbone acts as the "agent" for all subproblems, jointly processing both visual and textual modalities to generate MCF configurations.

##### 3.2.2. Lightweight Multi-Agent Adaptation

PRAM employs a novel adaptation framework to fine-tune the shared MLM backbone for multi-agent coordination, enabling agents (each handling a source-specific subproblem) to interact and learn effective global strategies.

- **Communication:**
 - **Inside the Model (LoRA):** Low-rank matrices (using the LoRA technique) are introduced into the MLM's attention weights. These trainable global parameters approximate necessary changes in the backbone parameters, enabling inter-agent communication and coordinated decision-making without significant overhead.
 - **Outside the Model (Reprogramming Context):** Learnable "global context" embeddings are created as a prefix to input prompts. These context parameters act as queries in a multi-head cross-attention layer, extracting global information and guidance from text prototypes (linearly aggregated original token embeddings acting as keys and values). This mechanism is inspired by in-context learning.
- **Adaptation (MARL):** PRAM utilizes a Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) algorithm, specifically leveraging counterfactual reasoning for fine-tuning.
 - **Policy:** Each agent (representing a source node) receives token embeddings of its subset as a local state (s_i) and outputs an action (a_i) from a shared policy head network (π_θ). An action is a vector of path weights for its managed demands. Actions are sampled from a Gaussian distribution during training, with the mean used for deployment.
 - **Reward and Advantage:** Since MCF actions do not affect future states in a single time step, the expected return simplifies to the immediate reward R(s, a). The "advantage" for agent *i* (A_i(s, a)) is computed via counterfactual reasoning: A_i(s, a) = R(s, a) - Σ_a'_i π_θ(a'_i|s_i) R(s, (a_-i, a'_i)). This quantifies an agent's contribution by comparing the actual global reward to a hypothetical scenario where only agent *i*'s action was changed. The counterfactual baseline is approximated via Monte Carlo sampling.
 - **Training:** The model is fine-tuned end-to-end using policy gradients (g = E_π [Σ_i A_i(s, a)∇_θ log π_θ(a_i|s_i)]), with gradients backpropagated seamlessly from the policy head network.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Understanding

The paper provides theoretical results to support PRAM's effectiveness:
- **Case Study (Figure 5):** An illustrative example on a toy network demonstrates that typical MCF objective functions (MLU, MTF, MCF) exhibit convexity or quasi-concavity properties with respect to path weights. This property is crucial as it ensures that first-order methods, like gradient descent (GD), can effectively solve the problem by iteratively updating configurations to guarantee convergence to a global optimum.
- **Theorem 1 (Solving MCF with GD):** Formally proves that a GD algorithm, with an appropriate step size and a finite number of iterations, can achieve an arbitrarily small error from the optimum for convex/concave MCF objectives.
- **Lemma 1 (PRAM's MARL Convergence):** Establishes that PRAM's multi-agent adaptation converges to a locally optimal agent, meaning that after fine-tuning, the model acts as a global mapping from observed demands to allocation schemes.
- **Theorem 2 (PRAM Learns to Implement GD):** Argues that MLMs are capable of internally simulating the gradient descent procedure through their in-context learning mechanism. By constructing specific weight matrices for attention heads, the paper shows that an adapted MLM with constant depth and width can simulate multiple steps of GD updates on a problem objective formulated as linear regression in the token space. This connects the convexity of MCF problems with the MLM's ability to learn and approximate optimal configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of PRAM was conducted on two groups of datasets: five real-world network topologies (Meta DB, Meta WEB, Abilene, CERNET, GÉANT, all with <30 nodes) and five large-scale synthetic topologies (GtsCe, Colt, UsCarrier, Cogentco, Kdl, ranging from 100 to 800 nodes). The performance metrics included normalized maximum link utilization (MLU, lower better), total flow (higher better), concurrent flow (higher better), and computation time. Baselines included LP, POP, LP-top (heuristic), DRL (RL-based), HARP (GNN-based), and Aether (GNN-based MARL), with variants using demand prediction ("pred").

#### 4.1. Experiment on Real-World Datasets (Figure 6)

- PRAM achieved near-optimal performance, consistently ranking second only to the ideal LP solver with perfect future demand knowledge.
- It significantly outperformed prior ML-based approaches like DRL, which showed substantial room for improvement due to training challenges.
- PRAM also surpassed partition-based optimization (POP) and heuristic (LP-top) methods.
- A notable finding was PRAM's ability to sometimes outperform LP (pred) in minimizing MLU, specifically achieving 21% lower MLU in CERNET and 45% lower in GÉANT. This was attributed to MLU's strong convexity, which PRAM's MLM fine-tuning handles effectively.
- Predictive variants of classical methods (LP, POP, LP-top) experienced at least 10% performance degradation compared to their exact-demand counterparts, highlighting the challenge of demand forecasting.
- HARP, specialized for MLU, performed well on that objective but struggled with total and concurrent flow objectives, showing lack of generalization across objectives. Aether improved over DRL but still lagged PRAM and classical methods.

#### 4.2. Experiment on Large-Scale Datasets (Figure 7)

- PRAM demonstrated strong scalability, delivering high-quality allocations with significantly reduced computation time as network size increased.
- On the largest Kdl topology (754 nodes, 1,790 links), PRAM completed flow allocation in under 25 seconds, outperforming POP (5x faster), LP-top (7x faster), and LP (100x faster).
- PRAM maintained over 90% of the performance of the best-performing LP scheme.
- It consistently outperformed HARP and Aether across all objectives (e.g., 6.1% and 17.2% better on average MLU, respectively).
- DRL was fastest but yielded suboptimal allocation quality, indicating a trade-off between speed and expressiveness.
- The results showcased PRAM's ability to balance efficiency and accuracy, making it suitable for large-scale production MCF systems.

#### 4.3. Generalization to Unseen Situations (Figure 8)

- **Coping with Link Failures:** PRAM exhibited strong robustness to link failures. With simple proportional reassignment heuristics and updated visual sub-images, PRAM showed less than 10% performance degradation on GÉANT with multiple link failures. It consistently outperformed all baselines without re-adaptation, demonstrating its generalization ability to transient failures.
- **Reacting to Flow Fluctuations:** PRAM's performance degradation was less than 15% even with high demand fluctuations (α = 2) at the 90th percentile. Most ML-based baselines performed well for small fluctuations but declined significantly for α > 0.5. Classical methods struggled more, with over twice the degradation of PRAM, indicating PRAM's resilience to demand variability.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study and Visualization (Figure 9, 10, 11)

- **Impact of Problem Partition (Figure 9a):** Partitioning was shown to be critical for scalability. PRAM's trainable parameters (LoRA and context modules) remained lightweight and scaled minimally with network size. In contrast, a non-partitioned variant (PRAM w/o partition) had an uncontrollably large number of parameters (e.g., 31,600 MB for Kdl), rendering it impractical.
- **Effect of Adaptation Choices (Figure 9b, 10):**
 - The MLM backbone was crucial; removing it (PRAM w/o mlm) severely degraded performance.
 - All adaptation components (LoRA, global context, MARL) contributed positively to optimizing the objective function.
 - PRAM's use of pre-trained MLM knowledge improved its ability to handle unseen topology changes (e.g., link failures), outperforming PRAM w/o mlm across various single-link failure scenarios (Figure 10).
- **Visualization of Learned Representation (Figure 11):** Analysis of the learned context module revealed that context embeddings developed strong relevance to task-specific words (e.g., "Flow," "Demand," "Capacity") and aligned successfully with task-relevant word embeddings. This indicates that PRAM effectively extracts and utilizes global information and guidance relevant to MCF problems.
- **Additional Results (Appendix E):**
 - **MLM Backbone:** Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct outperformed both smaller (3B) and larger but less mathematically specialized (Llama-3.2-11B-Vision-Instruct) MLMs, suggesting that architecture and training focus matter more than raw parameter count for this task.
 - **Number of Layers:** Performance generally improved with more MLM layers up to 8, but diminished returns were observed beyond this point, supporting the default 8-layer choice for efficiency and performance balance.
 - **Prediction Algorithms:** A moving average predictor for baselines proved effective, performing comparably to ARIMA and better than simpler heuristics.
 - **Synthetic Data Distributions:** PRAM maintained robustness and adaptability across different synthetic demand distributions (Gravity, Poisson, Bimodal), showing less sensitivity than LP-based methods.
 - **Visual Understanding:** The off-the-shelf MLM demonstrated strong visual comprehension of sub-topologies, accurately extracting link and capacity information even with occlusions or reduced image quality, indicating the viability of visual encoding.
 - **Subgraph Processing Schemes:** Vision-based encoding (used by PRAM) generally outperformed text-based and GNN-based encoding schemes, especially for larger topologies, due to better compression ratio and lower inference latency compared to lengthy textual descriptions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

PRAM represents a notable advancement in solving Multi-Commodity Flow (MCF) problems, a fundamental challenge in network optimization. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Scalable and Efficient Solution:** PRAM addresses the critical trade-off dilemma between optimality and tractability in large-scale MCF problems. By employing problem partitioning and leveraging the efficient inference capabilities of Multimodal Language Models (MLMs), it provides a solution that is significantly faster (10-100x faster than LP solvers on large topologies) while maintaining near-optimal allocation quality. This breakthrough is crucial for modern allocation systems that require rapid decision-making in dynamic environments.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** The research demonstrates PRAM's strong robustness to unforeseen events such as link failures and demand fluctuations. Its ability to maintain high performance with minimal degradation under these conditions, often without re-adaptation, highlights the generalization power of MLMs. This makes PRAM a more reliable and practical solution for real-world network deployments where conditions are inherently unpredictable.
- **Novel Application of Multimodal Language Models:** PRAM is presented as the first ML-based method to leverage the reasoning power of off-the-shelf MLMs for MCF problems. This innovative approach opens a new paradigm for solving complex combinatorial optimization tasks by treating network topologies as visual inputs and demands as textual contexts, thereby reducing the need for extensive manual feature engineering and specialized DNN architectures.
- **Theoretical Grounding:** The theoretical results, demonstrating that PRAM learns to perform gradient descent and converges to near-optimal solutions, provide crucial performance guarantees. This helps to bridge the gap between empirical success and theoretical understanding in ML-based optimization, a common challenge in the field.
- **Practicality and Integrability:** PRAM is designed to be objective-agnostic and easily integrable with existing allocation systems. Its lightweight adaptation framework and ability to process diverse input modalities make it a versatile tool for various MCF objectives, offering a practical and deployable solution for future networks in telecommunications, logistics, and cloud computing.
- **Future Research Directions:** This work establishes a foundation for further research at the intersection of foundation models and optimization. It highlights opportunities for exploring more sophisticated fine-tuning strategies, autoregressive generation for temporal dynamics, and improving computational efficiency in MLM-powered optimization systems.

In essence, PRAM offers a compelling blend of efficiency, accuracy, and robustness, positioning it as a potentially transformative solution for managing complex resource allocation challenges in an increasingly interconnected and dynamic world.
