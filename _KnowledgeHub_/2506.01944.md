# Feel the Force: Contact-Driven Learning from Humans

> [!summary] Summary
> FEELTHEFORCE (FTF) learns force-sensitive manipulation from human demonstrations collected with an AnySkin-based tactile glove and MediaPipe hand-pose triangulation. A transformer policy over key-point tracks predicts future robot points, gripper state, and desired contact force; at inference a PD controller modulates gripper closure to track that predicted force. Without any robot training data, FTF reaches 77% success across 5 force-sensitive tasks and 67% under adversarial disturbance.

## Problem

- Tactile imitation learning depends on teleoperation, which scales poorly to diverse real environments and needs costly haptic rigs or visual-cue-based manual gripper-closure adjustment.
- Glove-based mocap collection systems lack force sensing, so they fall back on task-specific priors like manually offsetting actions to approximate higher target forces.
- Feeding tactile signals passively into a policy ties it to the human force distribution, so embodiment gap causes failure off-distribution, and demands far more data.

## Method

- Tactile glove with ==magnetometer-based AnySkin sensors== under the thumb, transparent to preserve hand appearance, streaming 200 fps force aggregated to camera-matched 30 fps; matching sensor on one Franka jaw.
- ==Embodiment-agnostic key-point representation==: MediaPipe hand key points triangulated from two calibrated cameras into robot pose, plus ==DIFT==-propagated object annotations tracked by ==Co-Tracker==.
- **FTF** ==transformer policy== (per-point MLP-encoded tokens, ==action chunking== with exponential temporal averaging, ==MSE loss==) predicts future robot tracks, gripper state, and force; an outer-loop ==PD controller== drives gripper closure until measured force matches.

## Results

- Across 5 tasks FTF reached **77%** success (e.g. 13/15 bread, 13/15 egg, 9/15 cup unstacking) while binary and continuous-gripper human-data baselines scored **0%** on four of five.
- Trained on teleoperated robot data instead, force prediction still generally beat binary and continuous baselines, but degraded on egg (**0%**) and bottle cap, indicating noisier teleoperation force streams.
- Under adversarial disturbances (holding, pressing, reorienting the chip bag) FTF still succeeded **67%** of the time (10/15); masking force input entirely caused no degradation on lift-and-hold bread (**100%**, 10/10).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Actively predicting a target force and closing the loop on it at execution transfers across embodiments far better than passively feeding measured tactile signals into the policy.
> - Force is a more transferable interface than kinematics: retargeting continuous finger separation to gripper closure oscillates and fails, especially on deformable objects.
> - Human-worn sensing can yield cleaner force supervision than teleoperation, and the policy predicted forces well even with tactile input masked, suggesting visual state largely determines force.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics has made significant strides in manipulation, particularly with advancements in vision-based and proprioceptive imitation learning. However, a persistent "core unsolved challenge" remains: controlling fine-grained contact forces during manipulation. Tasks requiring delicate touch, such as handling deformable objects or separating tightly packed items, often prove difficult for robots relying solely on visual or joint-level feedback. Humans, in contrast, master these tasks effortlessly due to their highly developed sense of touch.

Prior work has attempted to integrate force feedback into robot control through various avenues:
- **Tactile Sensing and Haptic Learning:** Researchers have developed advanced tactile sensors (e.g., GelSight, BioTac) and explored learning policies directly from tactile inputs for tasks like slip detection and grasp stability. However, these methods typically rely on robot-collected data, which is costly and may not generalize well across diverse tasks or hardware.
- **Imitation Learning from Human Demonstrations:** Learning from human demonstrations has a long history, traditionally focusing on visual or kinematic supervision. While wearable devices and teleoperation rigs exist, they can be expensive, intrusive, or require precise action-space correspondence. Crucially, visual demonstrations alone often lack the necessary information to infer precise contact forces.
- **Human-to-Robot Embodiment Transfer:** Bridging the morphological gap between humans and robots is a significant hurdle. Approaches involve kinematic retargeting, extracting affordances, or designing specialized data collection tools that mimic robot grippers.

The paper identifies several critical limitations in existing approaches:
1. **Scalability of Tactile Data Collection:** Robot-collected tactile data is expensive and specific to robot embodiments. Teleoperation, while useful, is hard to scale to diverse real-world environments and often requires expensive haptic feedback setups.
2. **Lack of Force Information in Human Demos:** Purely visual human demonstrations cannot convey the precise contact forces.
3. **Passive Use of Tactile Information:** Many methods simply feed observed tactile information into a policy. This "passive" use suffers from:
 - **Embodiment Gap:** The learned model becomes highly reliant on the observed force distribution, which may differ significantly between human and robot embodiments, leading to generalization failures during deployment.
 - **Data Inefficiency:** More precise actions often require more data when tactile information is used passively.

"Feel the Force" (FTF) positions itself as a critical step forward by addressing these limitations. It directly leverages human tactile behavior, enabling scalable data collection in natural environments. Instead of passively using force input, FTF learns to *predict* desired forces, which are then actively reproduced by a low-level PD controller on the robot. This decoupling of learning and execution, combined with a robust human-to-robot embodiment transfer mechanism, allows FTF to achieve zero-shot transfer of force-sensitive skills from humans to robots without needing any robot-specific training data. This approach shifts the paradigm towards truly human-driven, tactile-aware robot learning, aiming for greater robustness and generalization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of the "Feel the Force" (FTF) project is to **endow robots with robust, force-aware control by learning efficiently and scalably from human tactile experiences.**

The core motivations driving this research stem from the limitations of current robot manipulation systems, particularly in handling tasks that require precise force modulation:

- **Bridging the Human-Robot Tactile Gap:** Humans possess an unparalleled ability to manipulate objects with fine-grained force control, relying heavily on their sense of touch. Replicating this capability in robots remains a grand challenge. Existing robot policies, primarily vision-based or proprioceptive, frequently fail when subtle force adjustments are necessary, especially under uncertainty or partial observability.
- **Addressing Scalability of Tactile Data Collection:** Current methods for acquiring tactile data for robots often involve costly robot-in-the-loop data collection or complex teleoperation setups, which are difficult to scale to a wide variety of tasks and environments. The authors seek a more natural and scalable data source.
- **Overcoming Limitations of Passive Tactile Integration:** Simply feeding tactile sensor readings into a robot's policy (passive use) can lead to issues. The policy becomes overly dependent on the specific force distributions observed during training. This creates an "embodiment gap" problem: if the robot's interaction forces deviate from the human training data, the policy may fail to generalize. Moreover, this approach can be data-inefficient.
- **Enabling Generalization Across Embodiments:** A significant hurdle is transferring skills learned from human demonstrations to a robot with a different morphology. The goal is to allow humans to demonstrate tasks in their natural embodiment, without requiring them to act as a direct teleoperator for a robot.

To achieve its overarching goal and address these motivations, FTF sets out to accomplish several key objectives:

1. **Develop a system for capturing human tactile demonstrations:** This involves designing a non-intrusive, force-sensing glove that allows humans to perform manipulation tasks naturally while collecting precise contact force data alongside visual observations.
2. **Model human tactile-proprioceptive signals:** The system must learn a policy that effectively combines visual information (hand pose, object keypoints) with tactile force data.
3. **Predict desired contact forces:** Crucially, the policy should not just observe forces but *predict* the forces needed for manipulation, allowing for active force reproduction.
4. **Decouple learning from execution for robust force control:** The system aims to learn the *intent* of force application from human demonstrations and then use a separate, low-level controller to achieve that desired force on the robot, making the execution robust to morphological mismatches or sensor noise.
5. **Enable zero-shot human-to-robot transfer:** The learned policy should be directly transferable to a robot without requiring additional robot-specific training data or fine-tuning, demonstrating the effectiveness of learning from human embodiment alone.

By pursuing these objectives, FTF aims to provide a more scalable, robust, and intuitive way for robots to learn complex, force-sensitive manipulation skills, ultimately expanding their capabilities in real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FEELTHEFORCE (FTF) is a comprehensive robot learning system designed to learn force-sensitive manipulation directly from human demonstrations. Its methodology comprises four key components: data acquisition, embodiment-agnostic scene representation, policy learning, and inference with active force control.

#### 3.1. Data Acquisition for Human-to-Robot Force Transfer

The core idea is to collect rich multimodal data from humans performing tasks naturally.
- **Human Hardware Design:** A custom, ergonomic tactile glove, inspired by the AnySkin sensor, is used. This glove incorporates 3D-printed magnetometer-based sensors on the thumb's underside. Its transparent design minimizes visual obstruction. The glove streams force data (3D vectors, aggregated into a single norm) at 200 fps, downsampled to 30 fps to match camera frame rates. Two calibrated Intel RealSense D435 cameras record visual observations of the human hand and environment.
- **Robot Hardware Design:** For robot deployment, custom 3D-printed gripper tips are designed for a Franka Panda robot's end-effector. These tips mount AnySkin tactile sensors on one gripper jaw, creating a one-to-one correspondence with the human glove's sensor setup. The same camera setup is used to monitor the robot.

#### 3.2. Embodiment Agnostic Scene Representation

To bridge the human and robot embodiments and provide a unified representation for policy learning, FTF converts human hand motion and scene information into a point-based representation.
- **Human-to-Robot Embodiment Transfer:**
 - For each timestep, Mediapipe extracts 2D image keypoints on the human hand.
 - These 2D keypoints from two camera views are triangulated to obtain 3D hand keypoints ($\text{P}_{\text{t}}^{\text{h}}$), preferred over depth sensors for accuracy.
 - The robot's end-effector position ($\text{R}_{\text{t}}^{\text{pos}}$) is derived from the midpoint of the human index finger and thumb tips.
 - Robot orientation ($\text{R}_{\text{t}}^{\text{ori}}$) is computed by finding the rigid transformation between the initial human hand keypoints ($\text{P}_{0}^{\text{h}}$) and current human hand keypoints ($\text{P}_{\text{t}}^{\text{h}}$), then applying it to the robot's initial orientation.
 - The robot's end-effector pose ($\text{T}_{\text{t}}^{\text{r}}$) is then represented as a set of N robot keypoints ($\text{P}_{\text{t}}^{\text{r}}$) via predefined rigid transformations.
 - The robot's gripper state ($\text{R}_{\text{g}}$) is binarized (open/closed) based on the distance between the index finger and thumb tips.
 - The continuous force value ($\text{R}_{\text{t}}^{\text{f}}$) from the tactile glove is also included as part of the robot state.
- **Scene Key Point Representation:**
 - Task-relevant objects in the scene are represented by sparsely annotated keypoints. A human annotates these keypoints on a single demonstration frame.
 - DIFT (a semantic correspondence model) propagates these annotations to the first frames of all other demonstrations.
 - Co-Tracker (an off-the-shelf point tracker) then tracks these initialized keypoints through each trajectory, handling occlusions and maintaining temporal consistency to derive 3D object keypoints. This approach leverages large pre-trained models for generalization across novel instances and scenes.

#### 3.3. Policy Learning

FTF employs a transformer policy architecture, building on prior work in multi-task policy learning.
- **Inputs:** The policy takes as input the current robot keypoints ($\text{P}_{\text{r}}$), object keypoints ($\text{P}_{\text{o}}$), the binarized gripper state ($\text{R}_{\text{g}}$), and the continuous force value ($\text{R}_{\text{f}}$). These inputs are processed as sequences of tokens.
- **Encoding:** A history of observations for each keypoint is flattened into a vector and encoded using a Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) encoder. The history of gripper state and force values are fed as separate tokens.
- **Outputs:** The transformer policy predicts future tracks for each robot point ($\hat{\text{P}}_{\text{r}}$), the robot's gripper state ($\hat{\text{G}}_{\text{r}}$), and crucially, future gripper force predictions ($\hat{\text{F}}_{\text{r}}$). This is a deterministic action head.
- **Training:** The policy is trained using a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss, applied specifically to the robot point tracks. Action chunking with exponential temporal averaging is used to ensure temporal smoothness of predicted trajectories. The transformer is non-causal during training.

#### 3.4. Inference

During inference, FTF translates the policy's predictions into real-time robot actions, with a novel active force control mechanism.
- **Robot Pose from Predicted Keypoints:** The predicted robot points ($\hat{\text{P}}_{\text{r}}$) are mapped back to a robot pose ($\hat{\text{R}}_{\text{pose}}$) by considering the wrist keypoint as the position and computing orientation using the rigid transformation from the initial robot orientation.
- **Inference-time PD Force Controller (Key Contribution):** This is where FTF actively *reproduces* predicted forces.
 - When the policy predicts a desired force ($\hat{\text{F}}_{\text{t}}$) and the gripper should be closed, an outer-loop PD controller is activated.
 - The PD controller continuously adjusts the robot's gripper closure setpoints based on the error between the predicted force ($\hat{\text{F}}_{\text{t}}$) and the force currently measured by the robot's AnySkin sensor ($\text{F}_{\tau}^{\text{t}}$).
 - The gripper closure is updated iteratively until the measured force converges to the desired force within a small error threshold ($\varepsilon$).
 - This inner-loop PD control allows for precise force reproduction, stabilizing the robot's behavior around the desired tactile feedback, making it robust to morphological mismatches or sensor noise, and critically, *decoupling the learning phase from the execution phase*.
- **Execution:** The combined action (robot pose, gripper state, and achieved force) is executed on the Franka Panda robot using end-effector position control at 6Hz. Object keypoints are tracked using Co-Tracker during rollout.

By combining detailed human tactile data acquisition, an embodiment-agnostic representation, a predictive policy that outputs desired forces, and an active PD force controller at inference, FTF provides a robust and generalizable framework for learning force-sensitive manipulation from human demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to validate FTF's effectiveness in force-sensitive manipulation, compare it against various baselines, and assess its robustness to disturbances.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Robot:** Franka Panda arm in a real-world tabletop manipulation environment.
- **Cameras:** Two Intel RealSense D435 cameras providing third-person RGB images.
- **Data:** 30 human demonstrations collected per task using the custom tactile glove. For baselines, 30 robot teleoperated demonstrations were collected using a VR-based teleoperation framework. All data was recorded at 20Hz and downsampled to ~6Hz for policy learning.
- **Robot Actions:** Absolute actions with 6D rotation representation for orientation.

#### 4.2. Task Descriptions

Five distinct force-sensitive manipulation tasks were chosen to evaluate FTF's capabilities, representing varying degrees of delicacy, compliance, and contact complexity:
1. **Place soft bread on plate:** Picking up a highly deformable object without crushing it.
2. **Unstack single plastic cup from stack:** Requires isolating and lifting one cup from a stack of three without disturbing others.
3. **Place egg in pot:** Gently picking up and placing a fragile egg without breaking it.
4. **Place bag of chips on plate:** Picking up a transparent, deformable bag without crushing chips inside.
5. **Twist and lift bottle cap:** Applying specific twisting and lifting forces to remove a cap.

#### 4.3. Baselines

FTF was compared against five baselines, categorized by data source (human or robot teleoperation) and gripper action space (binary or continuous), all using tactile information passively unless noted:
- **From Human Data (passive force):**
 - **Tactile Point Policy:** Behavior cloning from human point tracks and *passive* tactile glove force readings, predicting robot actions (binary gripper).
 - **Continuous-Gripper Tactile Point Policy:** Similar to above, but predicts continuous gripper closure.
- **From Robot Teleoperation Data (passive/predictive force):**
 - **FTF + Tactile P3-PO:** Trained on teleoperated robot data, *predicts* force alongside actions (binary gripper). (This is the only baseline that also predicts force, like FTF).
 - **Tactile P3-PO:** Learns from robot teleoperated data, using *passive* force readings from the robot gripper (binary gripper).
 - **Continuous-Gripper Tactile P3-PO:** Similar to above, but predicts continuous gripper closure from teleoperated data.

#### 4.4. Main Findings

The experimental results strongly support FTF's effectiveness and its advantages over passive force integration and robot teleoperation baselines.

1. **FTF excels at low-level force control from human demonstrations (Table 1):**
 - FTF achieved a high overall success rate of **77% across all 5 force-sensitive tasks**, demonstrating its ability to reliably solve tasks requiring precise force application.
 - In contrast, **baselines using passive force inputs from human demonstrations (Tactile Point Policy, Continuous-Gripper Tactile Point Policy) largely failed.**
 - For delicate tasks (bread, egg, chips), passive baselines almost universally failed by crushing the objects (0/15 successes).
 - For "unstack single plastic cup," passive baselines either failed completely or picked up multiple cups (e.g., binary gripper lifted all three cups), indicating a lack of fine-grained force control.
 - Even for the "twist and lift bottle cap" task, where the binary gripper could achieve 11/15 success by firmly grasping, it often applied excessive force, leading to failures like lifting the bottle or damaging gripper pads. FTF achieved 13/15 successes with more controlled force.
 - The authors highlight that the continuous gripper baselines struggled significantly due to the imprecision of mapping finger separation to robot closure and the sample inefficiency of learning precise continuous control, leading to oscillations.

2. **FTF generally outperforms teleoperation baselines (Table 2):**
 - FTF performed better than most robot teleoperation baselines (Tactile P3-PO variants) across tasks, showing that natural human data collection can be more effective for tactile learning.
 - While FTF + Tactile P3-PO (which also predicts force but is trained on robot teleop) performed better than purely passive robot teleop baselines, the force signals collected during human demonstrations proved more reliable than those from teleoperation. For instance, FTF did not outperform the binary gripper for "place egg in pot" or "twist and lift bottle cap" when trained on robot teleop, suggesting inconsistencies or noise in the teleoperated force stream.
 - The continuous gripper baseline from robot teleop also struggled with sample inefficiency and inconsistency for deformable objects, only performing marginally better on rigid objects like the bottle cap.

3. **FTF is robust to test-time disturbances (Figure 5):**
 - In the "place bag of chips on plate" task, when adversarial disturbances were introduced (physically holding down the bag, pressing on it, reorienting it), FTF maintained a respectable **67% success rate**.
 - This demonstrates FTF's ability to adapt to shifts in the observed force profiles and successfully reproduce the desired forces, highlighting the robustness of its active force control mechanism.

4. **Force input not strictly required for FTF's prediction (Appendix Table 3):**
 - A variant of FTF that masked force data during training (forcing the model to infer desired forces solely from visual and robot state) showed no degradation in performance on a "Lift and hold bread" task. This suggests that FTF can effectively infer and predict desired forces based on the scene and robot state, even without explicit force input during learning, likely due to the strong correlation between visual cues (e.g., object deformation) and force application in human demonstrations.

In summary, FTF's active force prediction and reproduction mechanism, trained on scalable human tactile demonstrations, significantly surpasses traditional passive force integration and robot teleoperation methods for complex, force-sensitive manipulation tasks, demonstrating notable robustness and generalizability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Feel the Force" (FTF) framework represents a significant advancement in robot manipulation, particularly for tasks requiring fine-grained force control and delicate interaction. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several key aspects:

#### 5.1. Overcoming the Force Control Bottleneck

The most profound impact of FTF is its ability to enable robots to reliably perform force-sensitive manipulation. By actively predicting and reproducing desired contact forces, FTF addresses a critical limitation of current robot learning systems, which often struggle with tasks involving deformable objects, fragile items, or precise contact interactions. This capability opens doors for robots to operate effectively in environments where such tasks are common, from manufacturing to domestic settings.

#### 5.2. Scalable Learning from Human Expertise

FTF offers a highly scalable solution for data collection. Instead of relying on expensive, time-consuming robot-in-the-loop data collection or complex teleoperation setups, FTF leverages natural human demonstrations in everyday environments. Humans are inherently experts in force-sensitive manipulation, and FTF provides a method to efficiently capture this expertise using a simple, ergonomic tactile glove. This dramatically reduces the barrier to acquiring diverse, rich manipulation data, accelerating the development of more capable robots.

#### 5.3. Novel Paradigm for Tactile Imitation Learning

Unlike prior work that passively feeds observed tactile information into a policy, FTF pioneers an active force *prediction* and *reproduction* paradigm. By learning to predict *desired* forces and using a low-level PD controller to track these predictions, FTF decouples the learning of human intent from the robot's physical execution. This makes the system more robust to the "embodiment gap" between human and robot morphology and resilient to sensor noise or test-time disturbances, as demonstrated by the adversarial disturbance experiments. This approach provides a blueprint for how robots can infer and act upon tacit human knowledge about physical interaction.

#### 5.4. Enhanced Generalization and Zero-Shot Transfer

FTF effectively bridges the human-to-robot embodiment gap. By using a unified, keypoint-based representation of both human and robot actions, and by employing a controller that actively modulates force rather than simply mimicking joint trajectories or gripper closures, FTF achieves impressive zero-shot transfer from human demonstrations to a Franka Panda robot. This means skills learned from human hands can be directly applied to a robot, without needing extensive robot-specific fine-tuning or additional robot data, which is a major step towards truly generalizable manipulation.

#### 5.5. Foundations for Future Robotic Capabilities

The success of FTF lays groundwork for several future advancements:
- **Dexterous Manipulation:** The ability to handle subtle forces is foundational for true dexterous manipulation, allowing robots to perform tasks like assembling delicate components, manipulating soft tissues in surgery, or performing complex in-hand reorientation.
- **Human-Robot Collaboration:** Robots capable of understanding and reproducing fine-grained forces can interact more safely and intuitively with humans and perform collaborative tasks that require shared manipulation of objects.
- **Autonomous Skill Acquisition:** By learning from human "feel," robots can potentially adapt to novel objects and materials without explicit programming, making them more adaptable and autonomous.

#### 5.6. Limitations and Future Directions

While significant, the authors also acknowledge limitations:
- **Loss of Shear Force Information:** The current system aggregates 3D force vectors into a single norm, losing directional information (normal vs. shear forces). For highly dexterous tasks, independently collecting, learning, and stabilizing these force components would be beneficial.
- **Fixed Camera Setup:** The data collection relies on a fixed, calibrated camera setup, limiting "in-the-wild" data collection. Future work could explore egocentric cameras and stereo triangulation for broader applicability.

Despite these limitations, FTF represents a substantial leap in enabling robots to learn and perform complex, force-sensitive manipulation skills directly from human demonstrations. By providing a scalable, robust, and generalizable framework, this work paves the way for a new generation of robots capable of interacting with the physical world with a human-like sense of touch.
