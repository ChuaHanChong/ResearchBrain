# HUSKY: Humanoid Skateboarding System via Physics-Aware Whole-Body Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at TeleAI and the University of Hong Kong developed HUSKY, a physics-aware whole-body control framework that enables a Unitree G1 humanoid robot to perform stable and agile skateboarding, including pushing, steering, and smooth phase transitions, in real-world environments. The system demonstrates robust lean-to-steer control and generalizes to various terrains and skateboard platforms.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle to learn complex, highly dynamic skills involving intricate interactions with underactuated and unstable platforms like skateboards due to their high degrees of freedom and elevated center of mass.
- Traditional model-based control approaches for dynamic tasks are computationally expensive and rely on simplified models, leading to limited real-time responsiveness and robustness to unmodeled effects, especially during sim-to-real transfer.
- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) often faces significant challenges in humanoid skateboarding, including amplified sim-to-real discrepancies and difficulties in maintaining robust coordination due to highly nonlinear dynamics and complex leg reorientation requirements.

## Method

- The **HUSKY** framework models the humanoid-skateboard system, explicitly incorporating kinematic constraints such as the lean-to-steer coupling of the skateboard's trucks and non-holonomic wheel-ground contacts.
- It employs an asymmetric actor-critic DRL setup with phase-specific learning strategies, including an ==Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP)== for human-like pushing, kinematics-guided steering for directional control, and trajectory planning for smooth phase transitions.
- Robust sim-to-real transfer is achieved through precise physical identification of the skateboard's passive tilt dynamics and extensive domain randomization of physical parameters during training.

## Results

- HUSKY successfully enabled the Unitree G1 humanoid robot to perform complete skateboarding behaviors, including pushing, steering, and continuous phase transitions, with robust stability in both indoor and outdoor real-world environments.
- Ablation studies in simulation demonstrated the individual contributions of HUSKY's components: AMP for pushing achieved **100%** task success, physics-guided steering provided superior heading accuracy, and trajectory-guided transitions led to **100%** success in phase switching.
- The system showed strong zero-shot generalization to unseen terrains (e.g., inclined slopes, steps) and different skateboard platforms, attributed to extensive domain randomization and accurate physical system identification, confirming robustness to environmental variations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating explicit physics-aware modeling of underactuated platforms (e.g., lean-to-steer coupling) directly into the DRL framework significantly enhances the learning and robustness of complex interaction tasks.
> - Trajectory-guided transition mechanisms are crucial for enabling stable and smooth switching between distinct dynamic phases in multi-contact, long-horizon robotic tasks, preventing policy failures and promoting consistent behavior.
> - Adversarial Motion Prior, combined with task-specific rewards, can effectively guide DRL policies to produce natural, human-like motions while maintaining high task performance and robustness to disturbances.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in robotic hardware and control algorithms have enabled humanoid robots to perform a variety of whole-body control tasks, including locomotion over challenging terrains, dancing, manipulation, and fall recovery. However, humanoid robots learning complex, highly dynamic skills that involve intricate interactions with underactuated, unstable platforms, such as skateboarding, remains a significant challenge.

Existing research in robot-object interaction primarily focuses on static objects or less complex dynamic platforms. Traditional model-based control approaches for legged robots performing skateboarding maneuvers often rely on simplified dynamic models and model predictive control (MPC). While these methods can generate controlled motions, they typically incur high computational costs, limiting real-time responsiveness required for dynamic tasks. Furthermore, simplified models may not adequately capture the complex non-holonomic and underactuated dynamics inherent in skateboarding, affecting robustness to unmodeled physical effects and environmental variations, especially in sim-to-real transfer.

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as an alternative for synthesizing complex behaviors, demonstrating effectiveness in tasks such as force-controlled interaction, end-effector control, quadrupedal skateboarding, and humanoid roller skating. However, humanoid skateboarding presents increased complexity compared to these platforms. Humanoids possess a higher number of degrees of freedom (DoF) and an elevated Center of Mass (CoM), coupled with a smaller and less stable support polygon compared to quadrupeds. The task also requires complex leg reorientation and side-on steering maneuvers, leading to highly nonlinear dynamics and amplified sim-to-real discrepancies. Consequently, existing learning-based controllers that may succeed on simpler platforms often struggle to maintain robust coordination in the context of humanoid skateboarding. This paper addresses this gap by developing a framework specifically designed to tackle the unique structural and task-specific difficulties of humanoid skateboarding in real-world environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable a humanoid robot to perform stable and agile skateboarding maneuvers in real-world scenarios, encompassing pushing, steering, and smooth phase transitions. This goal is driven by several key motivations and challenges:

1. **Addressing high dynamism and complex interactions:** Current humanoid whole-body control frameworks predominantly assume static environments. Skateboarding, however, is characterized by high dynamism and complex interactions with an underactuated wheeled platform, which presents a significant challenge to existing control paradigms.
2. **Mastering non-holonomic constraints and coupled dynamics:** The humanoid-skateboard system is governed by non-holonomic constraints and tightly coupled robot-object interactions. Successful execution of skateboarding requires simultaneous mastery of hybrid contact dynamics and robust balance control on a mechanically coupled, dynamically unstable skateboard.
3. **Overcoming limitations of traditional approaches:** Traditional model-based methods, despite their ability to generate controlled motions, are often computationally intensive and rely on simplified models that are insufficient to capture the full complexity of skateboarding dynamics. This results in limited real-time responsiveness and robustness to unmodeled effects, making real-world deployment challenging.
4. **Extending Deep Reinforcement Learning to higher complexity:** While DRL has shown promise in robot control, applying it to humanoid skateboarding introduces substantial challenges. The high DoF and elevated CoM of humanoids, combined with a smaller support polygon and the necessity for complex leg reorientation and side-on steering, make the task inherently more unstable and prone to sim-to-real gaps compared to quadrupedal or roller skating tasks.
5. **Achieving natural and robust behaviors:** The motivation extends to not only enabling the robot to perform the task but also to do so with natural, human-like motions, particularly during the pushing phase, and with robust stability against external disturbances.
6. **Ensuring smooth phase transitions:** Skateboarding involves distinct phases (pushing and steering) with different contact dynamics and control objectives. A crucial motivation is to develop mechanisms that facilitate smooth and stable transitions between these phases, enabling continuous and coordinated skateboarding behaviors.

To address these motivations, the authors propose HUSKY, a learning-based framework that integrates physics-aware modeling of the humanoid-skateboard system with whole-body control strategies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The HUSKY framework is designed around a three-pronged approach: explicit system modeling, phase-specific learning strategies, and robust sim-to-real transfer mechanisms.

**A. Humanoid-Skateboard System Modeling:**
The framework begins with modeling the humanoid robot (Unitree G1 with 23 controllable DoF) interacting with a passive articulated skateboard. The skateboard is composed of a deck, trucks, and wheels. A simplified kinematic model is adopted for the trucks, which converts board tilt into truck steering. The key insight is an equality constraint derived from the truck geometry: `tan σ = tan λ sin γ`, where `σ` is the truck steering angle, `γ` is the board tilt angle, and `λ` is the constant rake angle. This constraint explicitly links board tilt to steering, forming a physics-informed basis for control. Wheel-ground contacts are modeled as non-holonomic rolling constraints.

**B. Humanoid Skateboarding Task Formulation:**
The skateboarding task is formulated as a hybrid control problem, encompassing two distinct, periodically recurring phases: pushing and steering.
- **Pushing Phase:** One foot maintains contact with the skateboard for balance, while the other intermittently pushes off the ground to generate propulsion.
- **Steering Phase:** Both feet remain on the skateboard, and steering is achieved by modulating the board's tilt through body leaning, which, via the derived kinematic coupling, induces truck rotation for directional control.
- **Phase Transitions:** Explicit strategies are developed to ensure smooth and stable transitions between these distinct dynamic phases.

**C. Problem Formulation and DRL Setup:**
The task is posed as a Reinforcement Learning (RL) problem where a policy `π` maximizes cumulative reward. The policy takes a proprioceptive state (`s_t`) and outputs a 23-DoF action (joint position targets), which are then mapped to motor torques via a PD controller. An asymmetric actor-critic framework is employed:
- **Actor Observation:** Proprioceptive state including base angular velocity, projected gravity, joint angles and velocities, previous action, desired forward velocity, target heading, and a normalized phase cycle variable.
- **Critic Observation (Privileged Information):** Includes robot base linear velocity, skateboard base position/orientation/linear and angular velocities, skateboard tilt/truck joint angles, and humanoid feet contact forces with ground and skateboard.
The overall reward function is a sum of phase-specific reward terms (pushing, steering, transition) and a global regularization term.

**D. Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP) for Pushing Style:**
To achieve human-like and robust pushing motions, HUSKY incorporates the AMP framework. A discriminator `D_ϕ` is trained to differentiate between policy-generated motions and reference human pushing motions sampled from a dataset `M`. The discriminator uses a least-squares loss with a gradient penalty. Its output is then converted into a bounded style reward `r_style`, which, combined with a task-specific velocity tracking reward `r_task`, encourages the humanoid to exhibit natural and effective propulsion.

**E. Kinematics-Guided Heading-Oriented Steering:**
In the steering phase, the robot uses body lean to induce board tilt, which translates to truck steering. To facilitate this, a kinematics-guided reference for the desired board tilt angle (`γ_ref`) is provided. This reference is derived by combining a bicycle-model approximation of skateboard yaw dynamics (`˙ψ = (v/L) tan σ`) with the lean-to-steer constraint (`tan σ = tan λ sin γ`). Given a desired heading change over a steering horizon, `γ_ref` is calculated to minimize heading error. A tilt reward `r_tilt` guides the policy towards `γ_ref`, alongside a heading-tracking reward `r_heading`. Virtual markers above the skateboard trucks guide stable foot placement.

**F. Trajectory Planning for Phase Transition:**
To overcome challenges associated with direct phase switching, HUSKY introduces a trajectory-guided transition mechanism. This generates intermediate reference states by planning smooth trajectories for key body parts (e.g., pelvis, torso, hips, knees, ankles, shoulders, elbows, wrists) relative to the skateboard. Trajectories connect the online terminal pose of the current phase to a predefined canonical stable pose for the next phase. B´ezier curves are used for Cartesian positions, and spherical linear interpolation (slerp) for orientations. A tracking reward encourages the policy to follow these planned trajectories, shaping exploration and enabling stable phase switching.

**G. Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
To bridge the sim-to-real gap, two key strategies are employed:
- **Skateboard Physical Identification:** An equivalent PD model for the skateboard's passive tilt dynamics (truck suspension) is identified through sequential analytical procedures based on its free-decay roll response. This involves measuring logarithmic decrement, damping ratio, torsional stiffness, and damping coefficient from observed oscillations. These identified parameters are then embedded into the simulation.
- **Domain Randomization (DR):** Various physical parameters, including robot and skateboard Center of Mass, default joint positions, robot base pushing velocity, and body/deck friction, are randomized during training. This enhances policy robustness to unmodeled variations in the real world.

The policies are trained using the PPO algorithm in the mjlab framework, integrating MuJoCo physics with the IsaacLab API. Real-world deployment is on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the HUSKY framework's capability to enable stable and agile humanoid skateboarding in real-world scenarios.

**Overall Performance:**
HUSKY successfully executes complete skateboarding behaviors, including pushing, steering, and phase transitions, in both indoor and outdoor environments. The system exhibits robust lean-to-steer control, natural body tilting for heading regulation, smooth and coordinated foot placements during transitions, and resilience against external disturbances, sustaining multiple continuous skateboarding cycles. The framework shows generalization to different skateboard platforms.

**Ablation Studies (Simulation):**
Comparative evaluations against ablated baselines in simulation highlight the contributions of HUSKY’s key design choices:
- **AMP for Pushing Style:** HUSKY achieved a 100% task success rate with low velocity tracking and contact errors. Baselines (Tracking-based and Gait-based) showed significantly lower success rates and higher errors, demonstrating AMP's role in generating flexible, natural, and coordinated human-like pushing behaviors.
- **Physics-guided Steering Strategy:** HUSKY maintained superior heading tracking accuracy compared to the "w/o Tilt Guidance" baseline, indicating that explicitly incorporating the lean-to-steer coupling is crucial for precise directional control.
- **Trajectory Planning for Phase Transition:** HUSKY achieved a 100% success rate and minimal contact errors during transitions. Baselines relying on phase-specific reference motions (AMP Transition), translation-only guidance, or mixed initialization for phase exploration struggled with significantly higher contact errors and lower success rates. This indicates the essentiality of trajectory guidance for stable phase switching and avoiding suboptimal behaviors.

**Skateboard Modeling Validation:**
Visualizations confirmed that without the explicit lean-to-steer coupling, the skateboard could only glide straight, lacking effective turning. Incorporating physics-guided tilt guidance enabled the humanoid to achieve a substantially wider range of headings with higher precision, validating the model's importance.

**Phase Exploration Analysis:**
Policies trained without trajectory-guided transitions, even with mixed phase initialization, failed to learn proper phase transitions, collapsing into pushing-only behaviors and exhibiting persistent incorrect foot-board contact patterns. HUSKY, in contrast, successfully learned foot-mounting transitions and correct contact patterns, leading to higher steering contact rewards.

**Trajectory Analysis:**
Analysis of collected trajectories during transitions showed that the robot maintained smooth, coordinated whole-body motions with consistent foot placement and gradual body pose adjustments, demonstrating temporal coherence and physical plausibility.

**Real-World Experiment Details:**
Detailed analysis of real-world transitions from pushing to steering revealed human-like behaviors: the pushing foot lifts, places onto the skateboard, followed by in-place adjustments to align the torso perpendicularly to the deck for stable steering. Domain randomization was noted as crucial for handling the high friction of real skateboard decks during sim-to-real transfer.

**Role of System Identification:**
Accurate physical identification of board tilt compliance was critical for sim-to-real transfer. Mismatched parameters (e.g., using compliant board parameters on a stiff board or vice versa) led to failures in mounting or stability during steering, respectively. The board-tilt response curves in simulation closely matched those observed in hardware, confirming the fidelity of the identified model.

**Evaluation on Diverse Terrains and Boards:**
HUSKY demonstrated robust zero-shot performance on unseen terrains, including inclined slopes and steps, and generalized across multiple skateboard platforms. This adaptability is attributed to the heavy domain randomization strategy employed during training.

**Sensitivity Analysis of Transition Design:**
The policy's ability to maintain high performance despite perturbations in canonical poses, variations in transition durations, and different interpolation methods, along with its capacity to deviate from planned trajectories under external forces, indicates that the policy has learned the underlying balancing physics rather than rigidly following heuristics.

**Simulation Framework Comparison:**
The study noted that using mjlab for fine-grained simulation was crucial for resolving issues of excessive foot-skateboard slippage encountered in other frameworks, suggesting mjlab's physics engine provided a more accurate representation of the non-linear friction and contact dynamics essential for skating.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HUSKY framework represents a step in enabling humanoid robots to perform complex and highly dynamic tasks involving interaction with underactuated, unstable platforms. Its significance lies in several aspects:

1. **Advancement in Humanoid Control:** The work demonstrates that humanoid robots can achieve agile and stable skateboarding in the real world, a task previously considered largely unexplored and challenging due to the inherent instabilities and complex dynamics. This pushes the boundaries of whole-body control beyond static environment assumptions.
2. **Integration of Physics-Aware Modeling and DRL:** HUSKY's methodology effectively combines explicit physics modeling (lean-to-steer coupling, hybrid dynamics) with advanced DRL techniques (AMP for natural motion, kinematics-guided steering). This integrated approach provides a robust strategy for complex robot-object interaction.
3. **Generalizable Transition Mechanism:** The trajectory-guided transition mechanism offers a principled solution for enabling smooth and reliable phase switching in multi-phase, contact-rich robotic tasks. This mechanism is crucial for long-horizon stability and is not limited to skateboarding.
4. **Emphasis on Sim-to-Real Fidelity:** The research highlights the critical role of accurate physical system identification and extensive domain randomization for successful sim-to-real transfer in tasks involving complex physical interactions. This contributes to best practices for deploying learning-based policies on real hardware.
5. **Broader Applicability:** The authors suggest that HUSKY's core mechanisms—specifically, kinematic coupling for underactuated tools and efficient transitions between contact topologies—have utility beyond skateboarding. These principles could be extended to other challenging tasks involving robot interaction with various dynamic or underactuated objects and environments.

Despite its contributions, the paper identifies areas for future investigation, including the integration of perception for vision-aware control (especially for complex terrains like skateparks) and the development of richer motion priors and terrain-adaptive control strategies for more extreme scenarios. The successful deployment on the Unitree G1 humanoid platform, along with comprehensive simulation and real-world validations, indicates HUSKY's potential to influence future research in dynamic humanoid locomotion and human-robot interaction.
