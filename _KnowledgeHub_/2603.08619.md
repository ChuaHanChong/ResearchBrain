# Embedding Classical Balance Control Principles in Reinforcement Learning for Humanoid Recovery

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for humanoid robot fall recovery embeds classical balance control principles, such as capture point and CoM dynamics, directly into a reinforcement learning architecture. This approach trains a single policy that achieves 93.4% recovery success from diverse configurations in simulation and demonstrates zero-shot transfer for various recovery behaviors on the Unitree H1-2 hardware.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots are highly susceptible to falls, which hinders their deployment in dynamic, unstructured environments and often necessitates human intervention.
- Current fall recovery methods, whether classical or RL-based, often lack generalizability to a full spectrum of unplanned disturbances and diverse contact configurations.
- Reinforcement learning approaches for recovery often treat stability implicitly, without directly leveraging well-established classical balance metrics to guide learning efficiently.

## Method

- A reinforcement learning framework is introduced, explicitly integrating classical balance metrics (capture point, CoM state, centroidal momentum) into both the reward function and the asymmetric critic's privileged observations.
- A single, unified policy is trained via a multi-stage curriculum, enabling it to autonomously select diverse recovery actions, from minor ankle/hip adjustments to corrective stepping and multi-contact stand-up.
- Extensive domain randomization and noise injection are applied during training to enhance robustness and facilitate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- The policy demonstrated a 93.4% success rate in recovering to an upright stance from diverse initial configurations in simulation, with an average recovery time of 5 seconds.
- On the Unitree H1-2 hardware, the policy achieved zero-shot upright recovery in all 10 trials from various lying and seated positions and successfully resisted manual pushes.
- An ablation study confirmed that the balance-informed structure, including privileged critic inputs and capture-point reward terms, was necessary for the policy to discover multi-contact stand-up behaviors, as the ablated policy failed to lift off the ground.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly embedding classical balance metrics, such as capture point and CoM dynamics, into the RL reward function and critic's inputs is crucial for efficiently learning robust and generalizable humanoid recovery.
> - A single reinforcement learning policy can effectively learn the entire spectrum of fall recovery behaviors, including multi-contact stand-up, when trained with a structured curriculum and balance-aware signals.
> - The balance-informed architecture is essential for the policy to discover and execute complex multi-contact stand-up maneuvers, rather than simply being an incidental learning aid.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The reliable operation of humanoid robots in unstructured environments is a significant challenge, with fall recovery remaining a critical barrier to widespread deployment. While humanoids have demonstrated advanced locomotion and manipulation capabilities in controlled settings, their susceptibility to falls and unrecoverable states limits their practical utility. This work positions itself within the field of humanoid robotics control, specifically addressing the problem of robust and generalizable fall recovery.

Prior research on humanoid stability and recovery has explored various approaches. Classical methods, such as the Zero Moment Point (ZMP) framework, Capture Point (CP), and Divergent Component of Motion (DCM), offer analytical criteria for balance under simplifying assumptions. These methods are effective for structured locomotion but become complex in contact-rich, non-periodic fall scenarios. Optimization-based approaches formulate recovery as constrained trajectory optimization, enabling dynamically consistent multi-contact behaviors. However, these are often computationally demanding for real-time, unplanned events.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a promising avenue for more general robot control, including fall recovery. Existing RL-based recovery methods have demonstrated stand-up behaviors and whole-body control under disturbances. However, many of these formulations implicitly treat stability as a consequence of task completion or survival rewards, rather than explicitly integrating balance-aware signals. This can limit their generalization across a broad spectrum of disturbances or require reliance on reference motions, keyframes, or scripted contact schedules, which reduces adaptability to novel situations.

This paper addresses a perceived gap: the underutilization of well-established classical balance metrics (capture point, center-of-mass state, centroidal momentum) within RL frameworks for humanoid recovery. The authors argue that these metrics, which compactly describe a robot's recoverability, can be explicitly embedded into RL to enhance both the efficiency and generality of learned recovery policies. By doing so, the work aims to bridge classical control theory's explicit stability guarantees with the adaptability and generalization capabilities of reinforcement learning, moving towards more autonomous and robust humanoid operation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the vulnerability of humanoid robots to falls, which impedes their practical application in dynamic and unstructured environments. Falls result in operational interruptions, potential hardware damage, and often necessitate human intervention. Current methods, whether classical or based on reinforcement learning, are identified as having limitations in addressing the full spectrum of fall recovery scenarios within a single, unified policy.

Specifically, the authors identify several motivations:
- **Need for Generalizable Recovery:** Existing approaches typically address isolated subproblems (e.g., predefined stand-up sequences, scripted contacts, or motion tracking) which require significant engineering effort and generalize poorly to unplanned disturbances or contact configurations.
- **Limitations of Implicit Stability in RL:** Many RL formulations for recovery treat stability as an implicit outcome of task rewards. This design choice means the policy lacks an explicit representation of its balance state or proximity to an unrecoverable configuration, potentially limiting its ability to generalize across a wide range of disturbances.
- **Underutilization of Classical Balance Principles:** Classical robotics provides robust and interpretable metrics (e.g., capture point, CoM state, centroidal momentum) that precisely characterize a robot's balance and recoverability. These quantities are readily available in simulation and can be estimated on hardware, yet they are rarely used to directly structure learned recovery policies. The authors hypothesize that embedding these metrics could significantly improve RL efficiency and generality without sacrificing adaptability.

In response to these motivations, the key objectives and contributions of this work are articulated as follows:

- **Development of a Balance-Informed RL Formulation:** To embed classical stability metrics—capture point, center-of-mass (CoM) state, and centroidal momentum—directly into both the reward function and an asymmetric critic's privileged inputs. This aims to provide an explicit understanding of recoverability to the learning agent without relying on reference trajectories or scripted contacts.
- **Creation of a Unified Policy for Full Recovery Spectrum:** To train a single RL policy capable of executing diverse recovery behaviors on the Unitree H1-2 humanoid robot. This includes ankle and hip strategies for minor disturbances, corrective stepping for larger perturbations, compliant falling, and multi-contact stand-up utilizing hands, elbows, and knees. The policy is to be trained via a curriculum that explicitly integrates fall-induction and stand-up cycles to cover the complete recovery sequence.
- **Demonstration of Criticality of Balance-Informed Structure:** To perform an ablation study illustrating that the proposed balance-informed structure is essential for the policy to discover and successfully execute multi-contact stand-up behaviors. This objective aims to confirm that the embedded metrics provide a necessary learning signal rather than an incidental one.
- **Validation of Cross-Environment Generalization:** To demonstrate the policy's robustness and transferability through zero-shot hardware deployment on the Unitree H1-2 and sim-to-sim transfer experiments in MuJoCo, without policy modification.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a reinforcement learning framework to develop a fall recovery policy for humanoid robots, integrating classical balance principles into the learning process.

**A. Problem Formulation:**
The fall recovery task is formulated as a continuous-state, continuous-action Markov Decision Process (MDP). A stochastic policy, parameterized by neural network weights, maps proprioceptive observations to joint-level actions, aiming to maximize expected discounted return. The full system state is available during training, while the deployed policy uses only partial, proprioceptive observations. Actions are continuous joint position commands.

**B. Learning Architecture:**
An on-policy actor-critic framework based on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is utilized. The actor generates relative joint position targets, which are then executed by low-level PD controllers on the robot's actuated degrees of freedom. The critic estimates the state-value function, incorporating privileged information only available during training, adhering to an asymmetric actor-critic design. Both actor and critic are structured as multilayer perceptrons with specific hidden dimensions and ELU activations. Training involves 50,000 iterations with 24 rollout steps per environment per update.

**C. Observation Design:**
To facilitate sim-to-real transfer, observations are divided into policy observations (for the actor) and privileged critic observations.
- **Policy Observations:** The actor receives hardware-measurable quantities: base angular velocity and projected gravity, relative joint positions and velocities, and the previous action. Additive uniform noise is applied during training to enhance robustness to sensor uncertainty.
- **Privileged Critic Observations:** The critic augments the actor's observations with simulator-derived quantities unavailable or difficult to reliably estimate on hardware during dynamic, contact-rich fall recovery. These include CoM position, velocity, and acceleration; whole-body linear and angular momentum; and capture point location. These inputs are intended to provide the critic with an explicit understanding of the robot's instantaneous balance state, improving value estimation.

**D. Action Space:**
Actions consist of relative joint position commands for all actuated joints of the Unitree H1-2. Policy outputs are scaled and executed via low-level PD controllers. A randomized per-episode delay (10–40 ms) is introduced to joint commands to model realistic latencies and improve sim-to-real robustness.

**E. Physics-Guided Reward Design:**
The total reward function is decomposed into three groups, aligning with a physical recovery sequence: vertical elevation, dynamic balance, and motion refinement.
- **Group I: Vertical Recovery (R_I):** Rewards progress toward a target CoM height (r_height) using a dense Gaussian. It includes an asymmetric vertical momentum shaping (r_rise rewards upward velocity below target, r_fall penalizes downward velocity quadratically) and a stationary stability term (r_stab) that bonuses proximity to target height while penalizing residual vertical velocity.
- **Group II: Balance and Capturability (R_II):** Embeds classical balance theory.
 - **Capture Point (ξ):** Calculated using the Linear Inverted Pendulum model.
 - **Static Stability (r_CoM):** Rewards CoM projection within the support hull using a smooth Gaussian.
 - **Dynamic Capturability (r_CP):** Penalizes the capture point falling outside the foot support hull, indicating a need for contact change. This term also uses a smooth Gaussian decay.
 - **Momentum Regularization (r_mom):** Penalizes large net centroidal forces and moments quadratically to encourage smooth, non-impulsive reactions.
- **Group III: Regularization and Motion Shaping (R_III):** Includes terms for hardware safety (torque, joint limits, action changes), upright torso orientation (r_orient), height-gated rewards for hand and elbow support during multi-contact stand-up (r_contact), and a survival bonus with early termination penalty (r_alive). These terms guide the policy towards physically consistent and safe multi-contact transitions.

**F. Curriculum and Training Strategy:**
A three-stage curriculum is implemented to encourage broad exploration and progressive realism:
1. **Exploration Phase:** High joint torque limits (10x hardware) allow the policy to discover diverse recovery strategies, including multi-contact behaviors.
2. **Difficulty Expansion:** Introduces external disturbances, randomized initial configurations (standing, squatting, kneeling, sitting, supine), and domain randomization of physical parameters. Large destabilizing impulses are applied once upright stance is achieved to induce falls and ensure training on the full fall-and-recovery cycle.
3. **Hardware Constraint Annealing:** Joint torque and position limits are gradually reduced to hardware-safe values (1x torque, 0.9x position limit factor) to refine behavior for deployment.

Episodes terminate on timeout or unsafe torso-ground contact under high force conditions.

**G. Domain Randomization and Noise Injection:**
To enhance sim-to-real robustness, randomization is applied across several aspects:
- Actuation and joint dynamics (stiffness, damping, friction, armature).
- Contact properties (static/dynamic friction, restitution).
- Initialization noise (base pose, joint offsets).
- Observation noise (additive uniform noise on policy inputs).
- External disturbances (random torso impulses for push recovery and fall induction).
Rigid-body inertias are randomized without recomputing a dynamically consistent model, compelling the policy to infer balance from observed state.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Simulation**
All policies were trained and evaluated in Isaac Lab on the Unitree H1-2, with additional validation in MuJoCo. The policies consistently maintained stable standing under nominal conditions.

1. **Recovery from Diverse Configurations:**
 The policy demonstrated recovery to an upright stance from a variety of initial configurations, including squatting, kneeling, sitting, and supine, without relying on predefined trajectories. Across 10,000 trials, a 93.4% recovery success rate was achieved, with an average recovery time of 5 seconds. Failures predominantly occurred when the robot became stuck in specific contact configurations that prevented upright recovery within the episode timeframe.

2. **Disturbance Rejection:**
 The policy exhibited a graduated hierarchy of responses to external perturbations. Small pushes (50–100 N) were absorbed using ankle strategies. Moderate pushes (100–200 N) elicited hip-based adjustments. Larger pushes (200–300 N) triggered stepping responses. For disturbances exceeding 300 N, the robot transitioned to multi-contact recovery, bracing with its hands before regaining an upright stance. This observed hierarchy is consistent with the balance structure embedded in the reward function and classical push-recovery analysis. The capture point evolution during recovery showed the policy actively driving the capture point back within the foot support hull.

3. **Sim-to-Sim Validation (MuJoCo):**
 The policy was evaluated in MuJoCo for cross-simulator generalization, using five initial configurations and varying forces (0–500 N) from four lateral directions. MuJoCo's contact model is described as more conservative, presenting a robust test of transferability. Success was defined by the CoM exceeding 0.85 m, remaining stable for at least 1 second with low vertical velocity, and torso tilt within 72 degrees of vertical.
 Without perturbation, a 100% recovery rate was observed across all poses. Under perturbation, robustness degraded gracefully. Standing and squatting poses maintained recovery rates above 50% at 200 N. Supine and sitting poses showed lower robustness, dropping below 25% beyond 100 N, which is attributed to the greater centroidal momentum requiring regulation from near-floor orientations. Recovery time increased with force magnitude, reaching up to 9 seconds for supine trials at high forces. Push application height consistently affected recovery, with low pushes (−0.2 m) yielding higher recovery rates and high pushes (0.6 m) resulting in the lowest. Forces applied above the CoM induced greater whole-body angular momentum, leading to larger displacements of the capture point outside the support polygon, a failure mode penalized by the reward structure.

**B. Ablation Study**
An ablation study was conducted by training a reduced policy that excluded privileged critic inputs (CoM dynamics, capture point, centroidal momentum), capture-point reward terms, and the progressive curriculum. All other hyperparameters and reward terms remained constant. The results indicated that the reduced policy failed to learn stand-up behavior. Episodes consistently terminated due to the "stuck-low" condition (no CoM height increase for 2 consecutive seconds) at a rate of 1.0, compared to 0.067 for the full policy, indicating the robot never successfully lifted off the ground. Mean reward decreased significantly from +379.2 to −115.3, and all recovery metrics collapsed. These findings suggest that the balance-informed structure is necessary within this training setup for the policy to discover multi-contact stand-up behaviors. The authors noted that this was a combined ablation, and the individual contributions of each removed component were not disentangled.

**C. Hardware Deployment**
Policies trained in simulation were deployed on the physical Unitree H1-2 hardware using ONNX networks and an OpenCV DNN Java API at 50 Hz. The joint-level control used a PD controller with policy-outputted joint targets. No modifications were made to the policy or control structure during deployment.
Across 10 trials initiated from supine, seated, kneeling, and crouched configurations, the robot achieved upright recovery in all attempts without any parameter tuning between simulation and hardware. Transient high-frequency joint oscillations were observed in 3 trials following recovery, prompting safety intervention. These oscillations were attributed to residual sim-to-real mismatch in damping characteristics. During disturbance trials, the robot successfully resisted manual pushes and regained balance. Qualitatively, observable increases in ankle and hip motor torques following perturbations were consistent with the ankle and hip stabilization strategies learned in simulation, suggesting the learned balance hierarchy transferred to hardware.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents a framework for humanoid fall recovery that integrates classical stability metrics into a reinforcement learning architecture. The proposed methodology explicitly incorporates capture point, CoM state, and whole-body momentum as privileged critic inputs and reward terms, providing the learning agent with an explicit representation of recoverability. This approach enables a single, unified policy to autonomously decide on appropriate recovery actions—ranging from ankle and hip strategies to stepping, compliant falling, and multi-contact stand-up—without relying on predefined reference trajectories or scripted contact schedules.

### 6. Significance

- **Unified and Autonomous Recovery:** The developed policy demonstrates the capacity to span a complete recovery spectrum, indicating a significant step towards more autonomous humanoid operation in unpredictable environments.
- **Validation of Balance-Informed Structure:** The ablation study provides empirical evidence that embedding classical balance metrics is not merely an incidental cue but a critical learning signal for the discovery of complex, multi-contact stand-up behaviors within the proposed RL framework.
- **Improved Generalization:** The successful sim-to-sim transfer to MuJoCo and preliminary zero-shot hardware deployment on the Unitree H1-2 suggest that the learned balance structure is robust and generalizes across different simulation environments and to physical hardware without modification.
- **Bridging Classical and Learning-Based Control:** The research highlights a broader insight: classical balance metrics offer a compact, interpretable, and transferable representation for contact-rich humanoid control. This complements purely task-driven reward design in RL, potentially leading to more efficient and reliable learning processes.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Humanoid Utility:** By improving fall recovery capabilities, the research contributes to making humanoid robots more robust and practical for deployment in unstructured, dynamic, and potentially hazardous real-world environments, reducing the risk of hardware damage and the need for human intervention.
- **Foundational for Complex Behaviors:** The principles demonstrated here, particularly the use of balance-aware control, may serve as prerequisites for developing more robust manipulation under external loads or agile locomotion over complex terrain. Maintaining capturability and regulating momentum could allow robots to react to unforeseen external forces without requiring explicit payload estimation, potentially simplifying downstream control tasks.
- **Advancement in Robot Autonomy:** The ability of a single policy to handle diverse recovery scenarios without external guidance moves closer to truly autonomous humanoid operation, where robots can self-recover from a wide range of disturbances.

Future work outlined includes extending the framework to perception-driven contact selection in cluttered environments, multi-surface recovery, and integrating with long-horizon locomotion and manipulation tasks. Adapting the convex hull support polygon metric for non-planar surfaces, possibly using wrench-space feasibility or planar segmentation for contact normal inference, is identified as a necessary next step to expand the policy's applicability to more complex terrains.
