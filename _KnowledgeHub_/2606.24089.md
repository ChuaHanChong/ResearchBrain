# DynaWM: Dynamics-Aware Distillation with World Model and Momentum Targets for Smooth Locomotion over Continuous Stairs

> [!summary] Summary
> DynaWM enhances bipedal-wheeled robot locomotion over continuous stairs by integrating a dynamics-aware world model to regularize the teacher encoder and a momentum target encoder for stable knowledge distillation. This approach leads to improved terrain adaptability and motion smoothness, achieving high success rates on challenging stair configurations in both simulation and real-world environments.

## Problem

- Existing teacher-student distillation frameworks suffer from weakened dynamics-aware representations, as teacher encoders often disregard broader geometric information beyond immediate rewards.
- During concurrent training, student encoders frequently experience dimensional collapse due to non-stationary teacher updates, leading to instability and loss of essential terrain features.
- The learned latent space in current methods is often opaque, limiting interpretability and preventing clear verification of how critical terrain properties like height are encoded.

## Method

- A world model is introduced to regularize the teacher encoder by enforcing forward-dynamics awareness through a state prediction loss, compelling it to capture comprehensive geometric and dynamical terrain information.
- A ==momentum target encoder==, inspired by BYOL, provides stable distillation targets for the student encoder through exponential moving average updates, preventing dimensional collapse during knowledge transfer.
- An augmented reward function incorporates terms for joint range, wheelbase distance, base position bounds, and leg balance to promote more stable and energy-efficient locomotion on stairs.

## Results

- DynaWM's teacher encoder achieved a PC-Correlation of **0.96** and Linear Probing R^2 of **0.94** for terrain height, significantly outperforming baselines and ablated versions in representation quality.
- The framework demonstrated superior motion smoothness (lower EsmoothE_{smooth}Esmooth and EaccE_{acc}Eacc) compared to the CTS baseline across various stair configurations, indicating more fluent and less jerky movement.
- In real-world experiments, DynaWM achieved **100%** success rates on previously unseen irregular stairs (5/5 trials) and continuous stairs (13 steps of 20cm height, 30cm width), exhibiting robust adaptability to out-of-distribution terrains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The world model successfully compels the teacher encoder to learn structured, dynamics-aware representations of terrain, as evidenced by clear stratification of terrain height in PCA visualizations.
> - Utilizing a momentum target encoder effectively stabilizes knowledge transfer, allowing the student encoder to robustly acquire and replicate the teacher's terrain-aware manifold without dimensional collapse.
> - The proposed framework provides a transparent latent space, enabling both qualitative and quantitative verification of how terrain geometry is hierarchically encoded through metrics like PC-Correlation and Linear Probing R^2.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Bipedal-wheeled robots are recognized platforms in mobile robotics due to their hybrid design, which combines the energy efficiency of wheeled locomotion with the terrain adaptability of legged systems. Prior research, often leveraging model predictive control or deep reinforcement learning, has enabled these robots to traverse diverse terrains such as slopes, grasslands, and single-step obstacles. However, locomotion over more complex continuous terrains, specifically long staircases, remains a significant challenge.

Current approaches, particularly privileged learning paradigms like teacher-student distillation frameworks, address the difficulties of integrating exteroceptive perception due to the sim-to-real gap and hardware unreliability. These frameworks typically involve training a privileged teacher policy with access to exteroceptive information (e.g., terrain elevation maps) and subsequently distilling this knowledge to a student policy that relies solely on proprioceptive observations. Recent extensions have introduced environment encoders to enhance information retention in latent representations and concurrent teacher-student (CTS) training schemes for improved efficiency. While these frameworks have shown effectiveness in traversing various challenging terrains for quadrupedal and humanoid platforms, they exhibit specific limitations when applied to continuous stair traversal.

Three primary issues are identified in current teacher-student approaches:
1. **Weakened dynamics-aware representations:** Teacher encoders, optimized primarily through policy gradients, tend to encode only terrain features directly relevant to immediate rewards, potentially disregarding broader geometric information necessary for comprehensive ground dynamics modeling. This can lead to a partial loss of crucial terrain characteristics.
2. **Dimensional collapse in student encoders:** During concurrent training, rapid updates in the teacher's dynamics-aware representations can cause instability in the student encoder. The student may converge to fitting the teacher's mean predictions, resulting in dimensional collapse and failure to capture essential terrain features.
3. **Opaque latent space:** The interpretability of the learned latent space is often limited, making it difficult to verify the true modeling capability of terrain height or other critical properties. This lack of transparency hinders understanding and improvement of the terrain encoding process.

These limitations collectively hinder the ability of bipedal-wheeled robots to achieve dependable traversal over lengthy consecutive staircases, leading to issues with motion smoothness and terrain adaptability. The proposed DynaWM framework aims to address these specific shortcomings by enhancing dynamics-aware representation learning within the teacher-student paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the terrain encoding capability of reinforcement learning frameworks for bipedal-wheeled robots, specifically to enhance their adaptability and motion smoothness during continuous stair traversal. The motivation stems directly from the identified limitations of existing teacher-student distillation methods, which struggle with continuous stairs due to insufficient dynamics-aware representations, instability during knowledge transfer, and a lack of transparency in the learned latent space.

The specific objectives addressed by the DynaWM framework are:

1. **To enhance dynamics-aware representation learning in the teacher encoder:** The paper aims to prevent the teacher encoder from merely focusing on immediate rewards and instead compel it to capture comprehensive geometric and dynamical information about the terrain. This is motivated by the observation that policy gradient-driven optimization often leads to encoders that disregard broader terrain characteristics. The goal is to ensure the learned representations are predictive of future terrain states, thereby enriching their dynamics awareness.
2. **To stabilize knowledge transfer to the student encoder:** The research seeks to overcome the problem of dimensional collapse in the student encoder, which arises from the non-stationary nature of teacher updates during concurrent training. By providing consistent and stable distillation targets, the objective is to enable the student to acquire robust, terrain-aware representations from proprioceptive observations without suffering from instability or loss of feature richness.
3. **To provide transparent assessment of learned representations:** A key motivation is to move beyond an opaque latent space and enable a verifiable understanding of how terrain features, particularly height, are encoded. This objective aims to confirm that the encoder hierarchically captures terrain geometry, allowing for qualitative and quantitative analysis of its effectiveness.
4. **To achieve robust and smooth locomotion on continuous stairs:** Ultimately, the framework is designed to translate improved terrain encoding into practical benefits: enhanced terrain adaptability and smoother, more dependable motion for bipedal-wheeled robots traversing long and varied continuous staircases in both simulated and real-world environments. This directly addresses the performance gaps observed in existing methods for this challenging locomotion task.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The DynaWM framework integrates three primary components: a world model for dynamics-aware regularization, a momentum target encoder for stable knowledge distillation, and an augmented reward design to promote robust stair traversal. The framework builds upon the Concurrent Teacher-Student (CTS) paradigm and reinforcement learning principles.

**Overall Framework Structure:**
DynaWM operates through three interconnected modules:
- **World Model Learning:** This module regularizes the teacher encoder by enforcing forward-dynamics awareness through a state prediction loss.
- **Concurrent Teacher-Student:** This module facilitates policy learning for both teacher (privileged observations) and student (proprioceptive history) encoders using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and distillation.
- **BYOL Learning:** This module employs a momentum target encoder with soft updates to provide stable distillation targets for the student, aiming to prevent dimensional collapse.

**A. World Model for Dynamics-Aware Representation**
To address the issue of weakened dynamics-aware representations, a world model $W_\omega$ is introduced as a regularizer for the teacher encoder $E_{\theta_t}$. This world model is designed to learn how terrain properties influence future robot states.

1. **World Model Training:** The world model takes the teacher encoder's output $z_t^t = E_{\theta_t}(s_t^t)$ and the current privileged observation $s_t^t$ to predict the next privileged state $\hat{s}_{t+1}^t$. The training objective is to minimize the prediction residual (Mean Squared Error) between the predicted next state and the actual next state. This prediction loss, $L_{pred}$, generates dynamics-aware gradients that flow back to the teacher encoder, compelling it to extract essential dynamical variables relevant for state evolution while filtering out irrelevant features. This process aims to ensure the latent space encodes terrain dynamics rather than solely optimizing for immediate rewards.
2. **World Model Architecture:** The world model is implemented as a deep residual network. Each residual block incorporates a sequence of Dense-LayerNorm-Swish units. This architecture is intended to model complex terrain dynamics through hierarchical feature extraction and facilitate stable gradient flow across deep layers, thereby enabling the capture of long-range temporal dependencies in terrain sequences for improved state prediction accuracy.

**B. Momentum Target Encoder for Stable Distillation**
To counter the issue of non-stationary teacher updates leading to dimensional collapse in the student encoder, a momentum target encoder is introduced, inspired by the Bootstrap Your Own Latent (BYOL) approach.

1. **Mechanism:** The framework maintains two sets of student encoder parameters: an online network $E_{\theta_s}$ updated by gradient descent, and a momentum target network $E_{\hat{\theta}_s}$ that is an exponential moving average (EMA) of the online network parameters. The momentum target network's parameters $\hat{\theta}_s$ are updated smoothly using a decay coefficient $\tau$. This EMA approach provides stable distillation targets that are less susceptible to the rapid fluctuations of the online teacher encoder.
2. **Distillation Objective:** A predictor head $q_\xi$ is applied to the output of the online student network, $z_s^t = E_{\theta_s}(o_{t:t-H}^s)$, to introduce asymmetry. The distillation objective $L_{moment}$ is a normalized Mean Squared Error loss that pulls the online student's predicted representation towards the normalized output of the momentum target network $\hat{z}_s^t = E_{\hat{\theta}_s}(o_{t:t-H}^s)$. This alignment with stable targets, rather than directly with the fluctuating teacher, prevents dimensional collapse. An optional slow-updating alignment loss $L_{mse}$ (between $z_s^t$ and $z_t^t$) can also be added to ensure the target encoder tracks the teacher's representation space. The total student objective combines these as $L_s = L_{pred} + \lambda L_{mse}$.

**C. Reward Design**
The reward function incorporates foundational terms for basic balance and velocity tracking, as defined in previous work. To mitigate observed issues like excessive reliance on hip joints and promote stable stair climbing, additional reward terms are introduced:
- **Joint range constraint reward ($r_{hr}$):** Penalizes hip joint angles exceeding certain limits.
- **Wheelbase distance constraint reward ($r_{fd}$):** Encourages a specific distance between wheels.
- **Base position bound reward ($r_{pb}$):** Prevents large deviations from initial joint states.
- **Leg balance reward ($r_{lb}$):** Promotes coordinated motion between the left and right legs.
These terms are designed to guide the robot towards a more stable and energy-efficient locomotion strategy on stairs.

**Training Details:**
- **Simulation Environment:** Training is conducted in IsaacGym using an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, with 4,000 parallel environments (3,000 teacher, 1,000 student agents).
- **Control Frequencies:** Policy trained at 200 Hz, updated at 50 Hz; hardware operated at 500 Hz.
- **Terrain Diversity:** Training utilizes five terrain types: flat ground, slopes, pyramid steps, discrete uneven terrain, and rough surfaces, to enhance robustness and sim-to-real transfer.
- **Domain Randomization:** Key parameters (PD gains, CoM offset, friction, restitution, motor strength, initial joint angles, command velocities) are randomized during training to bridge the sim-to-real gap. Initial base pose randomization is also applied.

**Experiment Setup:**
- **Baselines:** The DynaWM framework is compared against several baselines, including ablations (w/o WM, MLP-WM, Res-WM, w/o MT), RMA Teacher, Concurrent Teacher-Student (CTS), and a Robust domain randomization baseline.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Qualitative Visualization:** Principal Component Analysis (PCA) visualization of latent variables, color-coded by terrain height, to assess organization of the latent space.
 - **Quantitative Metrics:**
 - **PC-Correlation:** Pearson correlation coefficient between the scores of the first principal component and target terrain properties (height and friction).
 - **Linear Probing R^2:** Coefficient of determination from a linear regressor predicting terrain height or friction from frozen latent variables.
 - **Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA):** Measures student-teacher alignment by quantifying maximum correlation between projected representation spaces.
 - **Locomotion Performance:** Success rates and motion smoothness metrics (e.g., $E_{smooth}$, $E_{acc}$, $E_{engy}$) are evaluated across diverse stair configurations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of DynaWM was conducted through training analysis, quantitative assessment of learned representations, and real-world experiments on a bipedal-wheeled robot.

**Training Results and Representation Quality:**
- **World Model's Impact:** Figure 4 illustrates that the Dense-LayerNorm-Swish architecture used for the world model (Ours) achieves lower prediction loss (Fig. 4a) compared to ResNet (Ours w/ Res-WM) and MLP (Ours w/ MLP-WM) variants. This lower prediction loss correlates with higher episode rewards (Fig. 4b) and more consistent terrain level progression during training (Fig. 4c), indicating that the proposed world model architecture facilitates more accurate future state prediction and effective gradient propagation to the teacher encoder.
- **Dynamics-Aware Encoding:** PCA visualizations (Fig. 3) demonstrate that the teacher encoder with world model regularization (Fig. 3a) exhibits clear terrain height stratification, indicating a structured, hierarchical encoding of terrain geometry. In contrast, the teacher encoder without the world model (Fig. 3b) shows entangled representations, suggesting a less organized latent space where height is not as distinctly encoded.
- **Momentum Target's Impact:** The student encoder trained with the complete DynaWM framework successfully replicates the teacher's structured manifold (Fig. 3c). The PC-Correlation metric (Fig. 4d) shows that the student with momentum target stabilization (Stu. (Ours)) closely approaches the teacher's performance, while direct distillation without the momentum target (Stu.  - **Height Encoding:** The teacher encoder in DynaWM (Tea.  - **Friction Encoding:** Similar trends are observed for friction encoding, with DynaWM's teacher and student encoders demonstrating superior PC-Correlation and Linear Probing R^2 compared to ablated versions.

**Locomotion Performance and Adaptability:**
- **Motion Smoothness:** Comparative analysis of motion quality (Fig. 5) shows that DynaWM (Ours) achieves superior smoothness (lower $E_{smooth}$) and significantly lower base acceleration ($E_{acc}$) compared to the CTS baseline across different step heights. This indicates more fluent motion with reduced jerk. Joint power consumption ($E_{engy}$) remains comparable, suggesting that smoother locomotion is achieved without increased energy expenditure.
- **Success Rates on Diverse Stairs:** Table IV presents the success rates across varying step widths and heights. DynaWM consistently demonstrates high success rates, particularly in challenging configurations. For example, on 20 cm height stairs, DynaWM achieves 89.3% success rate on 26 cm width steps, outperforming CTS (80.6%), RMA Teacher (82.0%), and ablations (e.g., Ours (w/o MT) at 78.1%). The method shows robust performance even for steps up to 20 cm in height, which exceeds the training maximum by 2 cm in some configurations. Ablations confirm the importance of both the world model and momentum target for maintaining high success rates, especially with increased stair complexity.

**Real-World Experimentation:**
- **Robustness on Unseen Terrains:** Deployment on the JiaRan bipedal-wheeled robot platform demonstrates strong robustness and adaptability to previously unseen environments. On irregular stairs with a 1 cm protrusion (never encountered during training), DynaWM achieved a 100% success rate (5/5 trials) with an $E_{smooth}$ of 0.72. On continuous stairs with 13 steps of 20 cm height and 30 cm width (exceeding training height), it also achieved a 100% success rate with an $E_{smooth}$ of 0.58 (Table V). In contrast, the CTS method showed lower success rates and higher $E_{smooth}$ values on both terrains.
- **Consistent Motion:** Figures 6 and 7 illustrate the robot traversing these real-world staircases. Joint angle trajectories confirm highly repeatable step-ascent motions, rapid recovery from disturbances, and smooth joint movements without noticeable discontinuities, which supports the quantitative smoothness metrics.

In summary, the results indicate that the DynaWM framework successfully addresses the identified limitations by learning dynamics-aware representations, stabilizing knowledge transfer, and providing a more interpretable latent space. This translates into improved motion smoothness, higher success rates, and enhanced adaptability for bipedal-wheeled robots navigating continuous staircases in both simulated and real-world environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DynaWM framework contributes to the field of robotic locomotion by addressing several critical limitations in applying teacher-student distillation for bipedal-wheeled robots on complex terrains, particularly continuous stairs.

The introduction of a world model as a regularizer significantly enhances the dynamics-awareness of the teacher encoder's representations. This prevents the encoder from disregarding broader geometric information in favor of immediate rewards, leading to a more comprehensive understanding of terrain properties. The ability of the world model to enforce forward-dynamics awareness ensures that the learned representations are not merely static features but capture the dynamic interplay between the robot and its environment. This approach could be significant for developing more robust and predictive control policies in various dynamic environments beyond stairs.

The employment of a momentum target encoder for student distillation directly tackles the problem of dimensional collapse caused by non-stationary teacher updates. By providing stable distillation targets, the framework enables the student encoder to consistently acquire rich, terrain-aware representations from proprioceptive observations. This stabilization of the knowledge transfer process has implications for the reliability and effectiveness of sim-to-real transfer methods, where student policies must function robustly without access to privileged information. This approach could be generalized to other domains requiring stable representation learning under evolving teacher signals.

The transparent assessment of learned representations through PCA visualization and quantitative metrics (PC-Correlation, Linear Probing R^2, CCA) provides a method for verifying the quality and structure of the latent space. This interpretability moves beyond black-box models, offering insights into how terrain geometry is encoded hierarchically. Such transparency is valuable for debugging, improving, and trusting reinforcement learning policies for complex robotic tasks.

The empirical findings, particularly the superior terrain adaptability, motion smoothness, and high success rates on challenging continuous stair configurations in both simulation and real-world deployment, demonstrate the practical utility of DynaWM. The ability of a bipedal-wheeled robot to reliably traverse previously unseen irregular and high continuous stairs with smoother motion and lower base acceleration suggests enhanced control and robustness. This advancement could accelerate the deployment of bipedal-wheeled robots in practical applications such as logistics, inspection, and service robotics, where navigating multi-story environments is often a requirement.

Future work, as suggested by the authors, may involve direct integration of privileged terrain information as explicit input for real-world control using exteroceptive sensing. This could further enhance terrain adaptability, allowing robots to adapt to even more diverse and highly complex unseen environments, building upon the dynamics-aware representations established by DynaWM.
