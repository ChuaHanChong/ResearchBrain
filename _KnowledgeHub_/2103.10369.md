# Combining Pessimism with Optimism for Robust and Efficient Model-Based Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Robust Hallucinated Upper-Confidence Reinforcement Learning (RH-UCRL) algorithm enables deep reinforcement learning agents to learn policies robust to worst-case disturbances through a model-based approach that combines optimistic exploration for the agent and pessimistic exploration for the adversary. This method provides sublinear robust cumulative regret guarantees and demonstrates superior worst-case performance and sample efficiency across adversarial, action-robust, and parameter-robust control tasks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents often lack robustness when deployed in real-world environments with unknown conditions or unpredictable disturbances, leading to unreliable performance.
- Existing theoretical Robust RL approaches provide sample complexity guarantees but are often computationally impractical for complex, deep RL problems due to restrictive assumptions.
- Current empirical deep Robust RL methods scale to complex tasks but often lack provable robustness guarantees and can be sample-inefficient by not explicitly exploring the environment's adversarial spaces.

## Method

- Introduces **RH-UCRL**, a model-based deep RL algorithm that explicitly models the environment as a stochastic system with actions for both an agent and an adversary.
- Employs a 'calibrated' statistical model of dynamics, using neural networks, to provide mean estimates and confidence measures, differentiating between epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty.
- Combines optimistic policy evaluation for the agent (maximizing return within confidence bounds) and pessimistic policy evaluation for the adversary (minimizing return within confidence bounds) through 'hallucinated' control inputs to guide exploration.

## Results

- Achieved sublinear robust cumulative regret, RT=O(LrCHβTHH3/2TΓT)R_T = O(L_r C^H \beta_T^H H^{**3/2**} √(T \Gamma_T))RT=O(LrCHβTHH3/2TΓT), and provides sample complexity guarantees for learning ϵ-optimal robust policies.
- Empirically outperformed other robust deep RL algorithms (e.g., RARL, RAP) and non-robust baselines in worst-case return across adversarial, action-robust, and parameter-robust Mujoco environments.
- Demonstrated higher sample efficiency compared to on-policy robust methods, showing strong robust performance even after fewer episodes, and occasionally yielded better average performance than non-robust baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Differentiating between epistemic uncertainty (reducible through exploration) and aleatoric uncertainty (inherent noise) is critical for effective, uncertainty-guided exploration in robust settings.
> - Simultaneously applying optimism for the agent's policy search and pessimism for the adversary's policy search within a model-based framework enables efficient discovery of robust policies.
> - Leveraging 'hallucinated' control inputs based on confidence bounds allows both agent and adversary to actively explore worst-case dynamics and disturbances in a principled, theoretically grounded manner.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) agents are increasingly applied to real-world tasks, where they often encounter conditions not present during training. This variability necessitates robustness to ensure reliable performance. The broader research landscape addresses this through Robust Reinforcement Learning (Robust RL), typically formulated as a worst-case optimization problem involving an agent and an adversary (e.g., Zero-Sum Markov Games, H∞ control).

Existing Robust RL approaches can be broadly categorized into two groups:
1. **Theoretical approaches**: These offer sample complexity guarantees but are often impractical for complex, non-tabular, or continuous-action environments due to computational intractability or assumptions like access to a generative model/simulator. Examples include early works on robust dynamic programming (Iyengar, 2005; Nilim & El Ghaoui, 2005) and recent advancements for tabular settings (Bai & Jin, 2020).
2. **Empirical/Heuristic approaches**: These scale to complex deep RL problems but often lack provable robustness guarantees and can be sample-inefficient due to insufficient exploration. Examples include methods using stochastic gradient descent for minimax optimization (Pinto et al., 2017), policy/value iteration (Tessler et al., 2019), and ensemble-based methods (Rajeswaran et al., 2017).

Model-Based Reinforcement Learning (MBRL) has demonstrated improved sample efficiency compared to model-free variants (Deisenroth & Rasmussen, 2011; Chua et al., 2018). The UCRL algorithm (Auer et al., 2009) provides provable sample-efficiency for tabular settings, and its continuous control extension, H-UCRL (Curi et al., 2020a), uses "hallucinated control" for exploration.

This work aims to bridge the gap between theoretical guarantees and practical applicability in Robust RL. It combines principles from provable model-based RL, specifically building upon H-UCRL, with the robust optimization framework. The concept of combining optimism and pessimism for robust decision-making has precedents in related areas, such as robust Bayesian optimization with Gaussian Processes (Bogunovic et al., 2018). The paper extends these concepts to the more complex setting of deep reinforcement learning with adversarial components.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a practical, provably robust, and sample-efficient reinforcement learning algorithm that can handle complex, real-world scenarios. The core motivations stem from the limitations of existing Robust RL methods:

- **Addressing real-world deployment challenges**: RL agents in real environments frequently encounter novel situations or unpredictable disturbances not adequately covered during training. This lack of robustness can lead to unreliable and unsafe performance in critical applications (e.g., autonomous driving braking systems). The authors explicitly model this variability through an adversarial framework where an "adversary" performs worst-case disturbances against the agent's policy.
- **Bridging the theory-practice gap**: Previous theoretical Robust RL algorithms typically offer strong sample complexity guarantees but are often computationally impractical or restricted to simplified environments (e.g., tabular settings or requiring a simulator). Conversely, empirically motivated deep Robust RL approaches scale to complex tasks but often lack provable robustness guarantees and can be sample-inefficient because they do not explicitly explore the environment or adversarial spaces. The authors aim to create an algorithm that simultaneously achieves rigorous theoretical guarantees and practical applicability with deep models.
- **Efficient exploration in adversarial environments**: Learning robust policies in unknown environments necessitates efficient exploration. The agent must not only learn the nominal system dynamics but also identify and adapt to worst-case adversarial conditions. Existing methods either lack explicit exploration strategies for robust settings or do not effectively distinguish between uncertainties.
- **Distinguishing uncertainty types**: The authors emphasize the importance of differentiating between epistemic uncertainty (due to lack of data, reducible through exploration) and aleatoric uncertainty (inherent stochastic noise, irreducible). A probabilistic model that can effectively capture both is crucial for guiding intelligent exploration and robust decision-making.
- **Generalizing Robust RL settings**: Many Robust RL works focus on specific types of robustness (e.g., adversarial, action, parameter). A key objective is to provide a unified algorithmic framework that can be instantiated across these different settings, demonstrating its versatility and broad applicability.

In summary, the motivation is to deliver an algorithm, RH-UCRL, that offers a theoretically grounded, sample-efficient, and practically scalable solution for learning robust policies in environments with unknown dynamics and adversarial influences, thereby advancing the deployment of RL in safety-critical applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper proposes the **Robust Hallucinated Upper-Confidence Reinforcement Learning (RH-UCRL)** algorithm, a model-based deep RL approach designed to learn robust policies by combining optimistic exploration for the agent and pessimistic exploration for the adversary.

**Problem Formulation:**
The environment is modeled as a stochastic system with states $s \in \mathcal{S}$, agent actions $a \in \mathcal{A}$, and adversary actions $\bar{a} \in \bar{\mathcal{A}}$. The dynamics are given by $s_{h+1} = f(s_h, a_h, \bar{a}_h) + \omega_h$, where $f$ is an unknown Lipschitz-continuous function and $\omega_h$ is i.i.d. sub-Gaussian noise. Rewards $r(s_h, a_h, \bar{a}_h)$ are deterministic and known. The goal is to find an agent policy $\pi$ that maximizes the worst-case expected episodic return against an adversary policy $\bar{\pi}$, i.e., $\max_{\pi \in \Pi} \min_{\bar{\pi} \in \bar{\Pi}} J(f, \pi, \bar{\pi})$.

**Learning Protocol (Algorithm 1):**
1. At each episode $t$, the learning algorithm selects an agent policy $\pi_t$ and an adversary policy $\bar{\pi}_t$.
2. The pair $(\pi_t, \bar{\pi}_t)$ is deployed on the true system $f$, observing a trajectory.
3. The collected observations are used to update an underlying statistical model of the dynamics.
4. The system resets to an initial state.

**Statistical Model and Calibration:**
RH-UCRL employs a model-based approach where the dynamics $f$ are learned from data. The agent maintains a statistical model that provides a mean estimate $\mu_t(s, a, \bar{a})$ and a confidence measure $\Sigma_t(s, a, \bar{a})$ (representing epistemic uncertainty). A critical **Assumption 2** is that this model is "calibrated," meaning the true dynamics $f$ remain within a confidence interval defined by $\mu_t$ and $\beta_t \sigma_t$ with high probability, where $\beta_t$ is a non-decreasing parameter and $\sigma_t( \cdot) = \text{diag}(\Sigma_t(\cdot))$.

**Core Algorithmic Principle: Optimism and Pessimism with Hallucination:**
The key idea of RH-UCRL (applied at Line 3 of Algorithm 1) is to use the probabilistic model to guide exploration for both players:
1. **Optimistic Policy Evaluation for Agent**: For any given policies $(\pi, \bar{\pi})$, the optimistic estimate of the return, $J^{(o)}_t(\pi, \bar{\pi})$, is computed by finding the dynamics $f^{(o)}$ that maximize the return, within the confidence bounds of the statistical model. This involves "hallucinating" an additional control input $\eta^{(o)}: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \times \bar{\mathcal{A}} \to [-1, 1]^p$ that perturbs the mean dynamics $\mu_{t-1}$ towards higher returns, bounded by $\beta_t \Sigma^{1/2}_{t-1}$.
2. **Pessimistic Policy Evaluation for Adversary**: Similarly, the pessimistic estimate $J^{(p)}_t(\pi, \bar{\pi})$ is computed by finding the dynamics $f^{(p)}$ that minimize the return, again within the model's confidence bounds. This involves "hallucinating" an additional control input $\eta^{(p)}$ that perturbs $\mu_{t-1}$ towards lower returns, bounded by $\beta_t \Sigma^{1/2}_{t-1}$.

**Policy Selection in RH-UCRL:**
At each episode $t$, RH-UCRL selects policies $(\pi_t, \bar{\pi}_t)$ as follows:
- The **agent policy $\pi_t$** is chosen to maximize its *optimistic* robust performance:
 $\pi_t \in \arg \max_{\pi \in \Pi} \min_{\bar{\pi} \in \bar{\Pi}} J^{(o)}_t(\pi, \bar{\pi})$.
- The **adversary policy $\bar{\pi}_t$** is chosen to minimize the *pessimistic* performance of the agent's selected policy $\pi_t$:
 $\bar{\pi}_t \in \arg \min_{\bar{\pi} \in \bar{\Pi}} J^{(p)}_t(\pi_t, \bar{\pi})$.

This selection strategy means the agent is optimistically exploring to find policies that perform well even against the worst plausible adversary, while the adversary is pessimistically exploring to find the worst-case disturbance for the agent's current optimistic choice. The amount of "hallucination" (i.e., the perturbation allowed by $\eta$) decreases as more data is collected and epistemic uncertainty reduces.

**Output Policy:**
After $T$ episodes, the algorithm outputs $\hat{\pi}_T$, the agent policy $\pi_{t^*}$ that yielded the maximum *pessimistic* robust performance over all episodes: $t^* \in \arg \max_{t \in \{1...,T\}} J^{(p)}_t(\pi_t, \bar{\pi}_t)$.

**Practical Implementation:**
To scale RH-UCRL to complex tasks, neural networks are used to parameterize $\pi$, $\bar{\pi}$, and the hallucination policies $\eta$. The optimization of these policies is performed using actor-critic algorithms, specifically fitted Q-iteration for the critic and pathwise gradients for the actors (agent, adversary, and hallucination policies).

**Theoretical Analysis:**
The paper provides theoretical guarantees for RH-UCRL in terms of:
- **Robust Cumulative Regret ($R_T$)**: Measures the difference between the optimal robust policy's performance and the sequence of agent policies chosen by RH-UCRL. Theorem 1 establishes a sublinear regret bound, $R_T = O(L_r C^H \beta_T^H H^{3/2} \sqrt{T \Gamma_T})$, implying asymptotic optimality.
- **Sample Complexity**: Corollary 1 determines the number of episodes $T$ required to achieve an $\epsilon$-optimal robust policy, where $\epsilon$-optimality is defined as achieving performance within $\epsilon$ of the optimal robust policy.
These bounds depend on factors like the Lipschitz constants of the dynamics and policies, the horizon $H$, and a model-based complexity measure $\Gamma_T$, which quantifies the difficulty of learning the statistical model. The analysis is specialized for Gaussian Process (GP) models, showing that $\Gamma_T$ can be bounded by the Maximum Information Gain (MIG), leading to concrete sublinear regret rates for common kernel functions.

**Robust RL Applications:**
The paper demonstrates RH-UCRL's applicability across three important robust RL scenarios:
1. **Adversarial-robust RL**: Both agent and adversary have distinct action spaces. The adversary injects state-dependent disturbances.
2. **Action-robust RL**: Agent and adversary share the action space, jointly executing a single action, modeling actuator uncertainty.
3. **Parameter-robust RL**: Robustness to changes in environment parameters (e.g., mass, friction) between training and test time, modeled as a stateless adversary selecting a parameter.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research introduces RH-UCRL and evaluates its performance through theoretical analysis and empirical studies across various Mujoco environments and Robust RL settings.

**Theoretical Findings:**
- **Sublinear Robust Cumulative Regret**: Theorem 1 demonstrates that RH-UCRL achieves a sublinear robust cumulative regret, $R_T = O(L_r C^H \beta_T^H H^{3/2} \sqrt{T \Gamma_T})$. This implies that the average instantaneous regret approaches zero as the number of episodes $T$ increases, indicating that the algorithm asymptotically learns near-optimal robust policies. The bound depends on problem characteristics such as Lipschitz constants ($L_r, L_f, L_\sigma, L_\pi, L_{\bar{\pi}}$), the planning horizon ($H$), the confidence parameter ($\beta_T$), and a model-based complexity measure ($\Gamma_T$).
- **Sample Complexity Guarantees**: Corollary 1 establishes the number of episodes $T$ required for RH-UCRL to output an $\epsilon$-optimal robust policy (i.e., achieving a performance within $\epsilon$ of the optimal robust policy). This provides a quantifiable measure of the algorithm's sample efficiency.
- **Specialization to Gaussian Process Models**: For environments where dynamics can be modeled by Gaussian Processes (GPs), the complexity measure $\Gamma_T$ is bounded by the Maximum Information Gain (MIG). This leads to specific sublinear regret bounds for commonly used kernels like linear and squared-exponential, confirming the theoretical sample efficiency of RH-UCRL in these settings.

**Empirical Findings:**
RH-UCRL was experimentally evaluated against existing robust and non-robust deep RL algorithms, as well as ablation baselines, across Mujoco tasks in three Robust RL settings.

1. **Adversarial-Robust Reinforcement Learning (Figure 2)**:
 - **Superior Worst-case Performance**: RH-UCRL consistently outperforms other robust deep RL algorithms (RARL, RAP) and non-robust baselines (H-UCRL) in terms of worst-case return. For example, in the Inverted Pendulum stabilization task, RH-UCRL successfully learns a robust policy, whereas other algorithms, including those trained with an adversary (BestResponse, RAP), fail when faced with a true worst-case adversary.
 - **Comparable Average Performance**: RH-UCRL's average performance is often comparable to or better than non-robust H-UCRL, suggesting that adversarial training can also aid general exploration and lead to better nominal performance.
 - **Importance of Adversarial Exploration**: Ablation studies (comparing RH-UCRL with MiniMax and BestResponse) indicate that active exploration by both the agent (optimism) and the adversary (pessimism) during training is crucial for achieving high robust performance. MiniMax algorithms, which lack explicit exploration for either player, generally perform worse.
 - **Sample Efficiency**: RARL and RAP, which are on-policy algorithms, showed poor robust performance even after 1000 episodes, highlighting their sample inefficiency compared to RH-UCRL.

2. **Action-Robust Reinforcement Learning (Figure 3)**:
 - **Strong Performance**: In the noisy action-robust setting, RH-UCRL again demonstrated comparable or superior worst-case and average returns against AR-DDPG and other baselines.
 - **Ablation Performance Improvement**: The ablations (MiniMax, BestResponse) performed relatively better here than in the adversarial-robust setting. The authors suggest this might be because the shared action space between agent and adversary "injects enough" exploration to successfully learn both policies.

3. **Parameter-Robust Reinforcement Learning (Figure 4)**:
 - **Robustness to Parameter Changes**: RH-UCRL, despite optimizing for the worst-case parameter, exhibited robust and nearly constant performance across different evaluation mass parameters.
 - **Outperforming Non-robust and Other Robust Baselines**: H-UCRL (trained with nominal mass) suffered significant performance drops when parameters varied. Other robust variants like Domain Randomization and EPOpt, while better than H-UCRL, were often outperformed by RH-UCRL, especially in maintaining performance stability across the parameter range.
 - **Greedy Adversary Pitfalls**: The BestResponse ablation in the Inverted Pendulum task highlighted a potential issue: a greedy adversary might consistently choose a single worst-case parameter during training, leading the agent to overfit to that specific parameter and perform poorly under other parameter variations.

**Overall Conclusion from Findings:**
RH-UCRL effectively combines optimistic exploration for the agent with pessimistic exploration for the adversary within a model-based deep RL framework. This strategy leads to policies that are provably robust with theoretical sample complexity guarantees and empirically outperform state-of-the-art deep robust RL algorithms across diverse adversarial environments, often achieving better nominal performance as well.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces RH-UCRL, an algorithm that makes several contributions to the field of Reinforcement Learning, particularly in the domain of robustness. The significance and potential impact can be delineated as follows:

- **Bridging the Gap between Theory and Practice in Robust RL**: A major contribution is the successful integration of rigorous theoretical guarantees with practical applicability in complex, deep RL settings. Previous robust RL algorithms were often either theoretically sound but intractable for large problems, or empirically successful but lacked provable guarantees. RH-UCRL offers a framework that addresses both, making robust deep RL more accessible for real-world deployment.
- **Enhanced Reliability and Safety of RL Agents**: By enabling RL agents to learn policies that are robust against worst-case disturbances, the work directly contributes to improving the reliability and safety of autonomous systems. This is particularly critical in applications such as autonomous driving (braking systems), robotics, and industrial control, where unexpected conditions can lead to catastrophic failures.
- **Sample-Efficient Robust Learning**: The model-based nature of RH-UCRL, combined with its principled exploration strategy, leads to demonstrably higher sample efficiency compared to many existing deep robust RL algorithms (e.g., RARL, RAP). This is crucial for real-world applications where data collection can be expensive, time-consuming, or even dangerous. The sublinear regret and sample complexity guarantees provide a strong theoretical backing for this efficiency.
- **Effective Exploration in Adversarial Spaces**: The core mechanism of combining optimistic policy search for the agent with pessimistic policy search for the adversary effectively guides exploration in both the environment's state-action space and the adversary's perturbation space. This ensures that the agent actively seeks out and learns to handle worst-case scenarios, rather than passively reacting to them. The "hallucination" mechanism provides a concrete way to operationalize this exploration driven by epistemic uncertainty.
- **Unified Framework for Diverse Robustness Settings**: The algorithm's flexibility to be instantiated across adversarial-robust, action-robust, and parameter-robust settings demonstrates its generality. This unified approach simplifies the development of robust policies for various types of uncertainties encountered in real-world systems.
- **Potential for Improved Generalization**: The finding that RH-UCRL's robust policies sometimes achieve better nominal performance than non-robust algorithms suggests that training with an adversary can act as a beneficial form of regularization or enhanced exploration. This could lead to policies that are not only robust but also generalize better to unseen nominal conditions.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The theoretical framework and empirical success of RH-UCRL can serve as a foundation for future research in several directions, including:
 - **Extending to richer adversarial models**: Exploring more complex adversary capabilities or different game-theoretic formulations.
 - **Non-stationary environments**: Adapting the robust learning framework to environments where dynamics or adversarial characteristics change over time.
 - **Scalability to even larger systems**: Further optimizing the computational aspects for extremely high-dimensional state and action spaces.
 - **Integration with other uncertainty quantification methods**: Exploring alternative or hybrid approaches for modeling and distinguishing epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty in deep models.

In essence, RH-UCRL represents a significant step towards practical and theoretically sound robust deep reinforcement learning, offering a pathway to deploy more reliable and safe AI agents in complex and uncertain real-world applications.
