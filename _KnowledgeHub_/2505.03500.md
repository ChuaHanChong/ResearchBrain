# Task Reconstruction and Extrapolation for $π_0$ using Text Latent

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces Text Latent Interpolation (TLI), a method for manipulating internal representations of Vision-Language-Action models, which improved the π0 model's extrapolation ability on a new skill recombination benchmark from 9% to 83% success rate. The research further identified "spatial overfitting" as a prevalent limitation where VLAs associate object names with fixed spatial locations rather than abstract identities.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models exhibit limited out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, struggling to recombine learned sub-skills for novel tasks.
- It was unclear if VLAs possess truly composable internal representations of skills or primarily overfit to specific demonstration trajectories.
- The absence of a dedicated benchmark for evaluating VLA capabilities in composing known motion primitives into novel task sequences hindered progress.

## Method

- The concept of "Text Latent" was introduced, defined as task-specific vectors derived by averaging hidden states of text tokens across ==transformer layers== during task execution.
- ==Text Latent Interpolation (TLI)== was developed to enable skill recombination, smoothly transitioning the model's internal "intent" by ==linearly interpolating text latents== in the ==residual stream==.
- A new benchmark, `libero-ood`, was created featuring tasks that demand the recombination of previously observed motion primitives into novel, extrapolated sequences.

## Results

- Injecting extracted text latent into the π0 model's residual stream restored task success rates from 11-**28%** (with blank prompts) to 81-**94%** for known tasks.
- Text Latent Interpolation (TLI) increased the π0 model's success rate on the `libero-ood` benchmark from a baseline of **9%** to **83%**.
- Experiments revealed that "spatial overfitting" is a common VLA failure mode, causing models to prioritize an object's training-time location over its current position in new environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models internalize individual, composable skill representations, but typically fail to autonomously combine them for extrapolated tasks.
> - The identified "text latent" vectors effectively encapsulate task-specific knowledge, capable of reconstructing complex robot behaviors even without explicit language prompts.
> - A pervasive limitation, termed "spatial overfitting," was discovered, indicating that VLAs often map object names to fixed training locations instead of achieving true object-centric understanding.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language-action models (VLAs) represent a significant area of research aimed at developing generalist robotic agents capable of performing diverse manipulation tasks. These models are typically trained on large-scale multimodal datasets and subsequently fine-tuned on specific target tasks using demonstrations. While this approach has facilitated strong in-distribution generalizability, enabling adaptation to variations in lighting conditions or minor scene layout perturbations, a notable limitation persists: VLAs often struggle with out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks, particularly those requiring the recombination of previously learned skills in novel configurations. For instance, a VLA might execute "put the cream cheese in the bowl" and "put the bowl on top of the cabinet" successfully but fail to perform the extrapolated task "put the cream cheese on top of the cabinet," despite possessing the individual motion primitives. This observation has raised questions regarding whether VLAs merely overfit to training demonstrations or if they possess latent, composable internal representations that could support broader generalization.

This study positions itself within this context by investigating the internal mechanisms of VLAs, particularly their capacity for skill extrapolation. It draws upon principles from mechanistic interpretability (MI), a subfield focused on reverse-engineering the internal computations of models to understand their functioning. While MI has seen applications in analyzing image classification models and large language models (LLMs) to identify feature detectors or functional circuits, its application to neural policies and VLAs remains less explored. Prior work in VLA interpretability has touched upon adversarial attacks, symbolic representation uncovering, and motion-relevant neuron identification. This research contributes to this nascent area by identifying and manipulating causal internal representations within VLAs to activate and combine specific behaviors, thus addressing the gap in understanding OOD generalization capabilities beyond simple fine-tuning. The work specifically builds upon existing state-of-the-art VLA models like π0, UniVLA, openvla-oft, and π0-fast, which have demonstrated high performance on in-distribution tasks, by challenging their generalization abilities with a novel OOD benchmark.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to investigate the nature of skill representations within Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and their capacity for out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. This overarching goal was motivated by the observed limitation of current VLAs, which, despite achieving high performance on demonstrated tasks, frequently fail when required to extrapolate by recombining learned skills in novel ways. This struggle raises a fundamental question: do VLAs simply memorize and overfit to demonstrated trajectories, or do they develop deeper, composable internal representations that could support more general and flexible task execution?

To address this, the study pursued several specific objectives:

1. **Identify and Characterize Task-Specific Internal Representations:** The researchers aimed to determine if distinct, task-specific "skill representations" are embedded within the internal states of a VLA, specifically focusing on the hidden states associated with text (task description) tokens. This involved defining and extracting "text latent" vectors from the model's transformer layers during task execution.

2. **Demonstrate Reconstructive Power of Text Latent:** A key objective was to empirically validate that these identified text latent vectors encapsulate sufficient semantic information to reconstruct the associated task behaviors without requiring explicit task prompts. This would confirm that text latent indeed captures essential task knowledge.

3. **Develop a Method for Skill Recombination:** The study sought to develop a technique, termed Text Latent Interpolation (TLI), capable of blending the skill representations from two distinct tasks. The motivation was to enable the VLA to combine sub-behaviors or motion primitives learned in separate contexts to perform entirely new, extrapolated tasks that were not explicitly demonstrated during training.

4. **Introduce a Tailored OOD Benchmark:** Recognizing the lack of suitable benchmarks for evaluating meaningful OOD generalization in VLAs, a significant objective was to create a new task suite, `libero-ood`. This benchmark was designed to comprise tasks that VLAs *should* inherently be capable of solving by recombining learned representations, rather than tasks that are impossible or entirely unrelated to their training domain. This design choice was motivated by the need to isolate and test the models' intrinsic extrapolation capabilities.

5. **Evaluate OOD Generalization Across SOTA VLAs:** Beyond analyzing the primary model (π0), the research aimed to assess the OOD generalization performance of several other state-of-the-art VLAs on the newly proposed `libero-ood` benchmark. This comparative analysis was intended to provide a broader understanding of the limitations and common failure modes across different VLA architectures.

6. **Uncover and Analyze Failure Modes:** A specific motivation was to conduct further qualitative and quantitative analysis to identify and characterize the underlying reasons for VLA failures on extrapolated and slightly modified in-distribution tasks, particularly focusing on phenomena like "spatial overfitting." This aimed to provide insights into whether VLAs achieve true object and goal understanding or rely on mechanistic memorization of spatial configurations.

Ultimately, the motivation behind these objectives was to advance the understanding of how VLAs learn and represent skills, to unlock their latent potential for OOD generalization, and to guide the development of future generalist robotic policies that can autonomously adapt to novel instructions without relying solely on extensive fine-tuning or post-training interventions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research systematically investigated the internal representations of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, focusing on the state-of-the-art model π0, to understand and enhance its out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization capabilities.

**VLA Formulation and Text Latent Definition:**
The methodology began by outlining the general structure of transformer-based VLAs. These models typically utilize pre-trained vision encoders (e.g., CLIP, SigLIP) and text tokenizers to generate d-dimensional embeddings from images, task descriptions, and proprioceptive states. These embeddings then pass through L transformer layers, producing hidden states. Action generation is a function of these embeddings and hidden states, often using KV-cache mechanisms.
The core concept introduced is "text latent," a task-specific vector derived from the model's internal states. Specifically, text latent (T) for a given task is calculated by averaging the hidden states of text tokens across all L-1 transformer layers over multiple timesteps and demonstrations for that task. This procedure results in a tensor T ∈ R^(L-1)×|T|×d, where |T| is the number of text tokens. The hypothesis is that T encapsulates the essential skill representations for completing the associated task.

**Task Reconstruction using Text Latent:**
To validate that text latent captures task-specific knowledge, two experimental approaches were used for task reconstruction on standard LIBERO benchmarks:
1. **Direct Injection:** The text prompt was either masked or filled with blank characters to remove explicit task instructions. The calculated text latent (T) was then directly added to the text tokens' residual stream (hT(i) = hT(i) + T) during policy execution, effectively "reminding" the model of the task.
2. **Unembedding to Prompts:** The early layer vectors of the text latent (T_l) were unembedded into a sequence of token IDs by calculating cosine similarity with the language embedding matrix. These resulting "alternative prompts" were then fed directly to the model.

**Text Latent Interpolation (TLI) for Skill Recombination:**
To enable VLAs to perform extrapolated tasks by combining learned skills, Text Latent Interpolation (TLI) was developed. For a task conceptually derived from two base tasks (Task 1 and Task 2), TLI blends their respective text latents (T1, T2) over time. The intervention modifies the text hidden states hT(i) as follows: hT(i) = hT(i) + (1 - α)(T1 - T2) + α(T2 - T1), where α = i/λ (timestep i divided by interpolation steps λ) linearly adjusts the mixing ratio from 0 to 1. This causes the model's behavior to transition from being primarily guided by Task 1's context to Task 2's context as the episode progresses.
An alternative approach, Text Embedding Interpolation (TEI), was also considered, which interpolates the initial text embeddings (eT(i) = (1 - α)eT1 + αeT2). Both TLI and TEI can be applied independently or jointly. The parameter λ controls the transition speed, set to the average number of policy-execution steps for LIBERO tasks.

**Benchmark Design: libero-ood:**
A novel task suite, `libero-ood`, was introduced to specifically evaluate OOD generalization. It consists of 20 extrapolated tasks, divided into `libero-goal-ood` and `libero-spatial-ood`, derived from the three standard LIBERO suites (`libero-goal`, `libero-spatial`, `libero-object`). The key design principle for `libero-ood` tasks is that the individual grasping and placement sub-skills (e.g., picking a specific object, placing on a specific location) have appeared separately in the training data, but their combination into a single novel trajectory has not. This ensures the tasks are within the model's *potential* capabilities, testing its ability to recombine learned representations.

**Experimental Setup and Evaluation:**
Experiments were conducted using the LIBERO simulation environment. The primary model investigated was π0, and its performance with TLI and TEI was compared against other state-of-the-art VLAs: π0-fast, openvla-oft, and UniVLA.
For each task, 10 independent runs with different random seeds were performed. The final success rate for a task suite was calculated as the proportion of successful episodes across all 100 runs (10 tasks × 10 runs/task). The identification of text latent for base tasks utilized 20 demonstrations. Experiments were performed on Nvidia RTX 4090 GPUs.

**Analysis of Spatial Overfitting:**
Qualitative analysis of `π0-TLI` behaviors on `libero-ood` tasks, particularly those involving swapped objects, was conducted to identify common failure patterns. Further experiments on a modified `libero-object` suite, involving incorrect prompts and relocated target objects, were designed to confirm "spatial overfitting" across multiple VLAs. The logit lens technique was also employed to analyze which parts of the image observation contribute most to the formation of text latent in π0, shedding light on its attention mechanisms.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the internal representations and generalization capabilities of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models.

**Task Reconstruction with Text Latent:**
The study demonstrated that "text latent" effectively encapsulates task-specific knowledge within π0. When text prompts were masked or blanked, π0's success rate on standard LIBERO tasks dropped significantly (e.g., to 11-28%). However, by injecting the corresponding text latent into the model's residual stream (Blank Prompt+T), the success rate was largely restored to over 80% (e.g., 94% on libero-object, 82% on libero-goal, 81% on libero-spatial). This confirmed that text latent carries essential task information.
Furthermore, unembedding text latent from earlier layers (T1, T2, T3) produced "alternative prompts." While often unreadable, these prompts could still instruct π0 to complete tasks with a notable success rate (e.g., around 70% for libero-object and libero-spatial with T2 or T3). This suggests a potential for "obscure prompting" for private instruction or backdoor attacks.

**Enhanced Task Extrapolation with Text Latent Interpolation (TLI):**
The primary finding was the significant improvement in π0's OOD generalization on the newly introduced `libero-ood` benchmark. Without intervention, π0 achieved only a 9% success rate on these extrapolated tasks. By applying Text Latent Interpolation (TLI), which blends text latents from two base tasks, π0's success rate increased substantially to 83% across the `libero-ood` suite (85% on `libero-goal-ood` and 81% on `libero-spatial-ood`). This result indicates that π0 possesses separate yet composable skill representations, although it fails to autonomously combine them for novel tasks.

**Limitations of Other SOTA VLAs on OOD Tasks:**
A comparative analysis on `libero-ood` revealed that other state-of-the-art VLAs also struggled with extrapolation. UniVLA, openvla-oft, and π0-fast achieved success rates no higher than 21% (21%, 1%, and 15% respectively) on the overall `libero-ood` benchmark, despite their high performance on in-distribution LIBERO tasks after fine-tuning. This highlights a common limitation in OOD generalization across current VLA architectures.

**Comparison of Interpolation and Prompt Switching:**
While TLI proved effective, other approaches were also tested. Applying both Text Embedding Interpolation (TEI) and TLI (π0-TLI+) showed marginal or slightly detrimental effects compared to TLI alone (77% vs. 83% overall). Using TLI with blank prompts (π0-TLI*) resulted in a significant drop in performance (25% overall), indicating the importance of the target task prompt for guiding extrapolated behaviors. Explicit prompt switching (πS0), where the prompt changes mid-episode, achieved a 69% success rate, suggesting it's less effective than TLI's continuous internal representation blending.

**Spatial Overfitting as a Common Failure Mode:**
Detailed analysis revealed a prevalent issue termed "spatial overfitting." π0-TLI+ experiments on `libero-goal-ood` tasks involving swapped objects demonstrated that the model often associates object names with their fixed spatial locations from demonstrated scenes, rather than understanding true object identity. For example, when instructed to "put the orange juice on the stove," if orange juice was placed where a wine bottle typically appeared in training, π0 would attempt to pick the orange juice by moving to the *wine bottle's original location* rather than the orange juice's current location.
This spatial overfitting was confirmed to be common across other VLAs. On a modified `libero-object` suite, VLAs maintained high performance with *incorrect prompts* (e.g., "pick up the cream cheese" for an alphabet soup) if the object was at its "correct" demonstrated location. However, they uniformly failed (0% success) when target objects were relocated to a *novel position* outside of their demonstrated central or top-right locations. This behavior suggests that VLAs often map object names to fixed spatial coordinates rather than performing true object recognition or goal understanding. Logit lens analysis supported this, showing π0 primarily attends to destination and robot arm state, with less focused attention on the object to grasp.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact on the development and understanding of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotics.

**Unlocking Latent Generalization Capabilities:** The most prominent impact is the demonstration that VLAs, specifically π0, possess inherent, "locked" capabilities for out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. The ability of Text Latent Interpolation (TLI) to dramatically increase π0's success rate on novel, extrapolated tasks (from 9% to 83%) confirms that the model has learned composable skill representations. This finding shifts the perception of VLA limitations from a fundamental inability to a challenge of effectively accessing and combining existing knowledge. This has profound implications for developing more robust and versatile robotic agents, suggesting that their generalization is not merely superficial but can be unlocked with appropriate internal interventions.

**Introduction of a Targeted OOD Benchmark:** The creation of `libero-ood` is a crucial contribution. Existing OOD evaluations often lack a principled design, sometimes featuring tasks that are inherently impossible given the model's training domain. `libero-ood` addresses this by designing tasks where the constituent sub-skills (grasping objects, placing at locations) are known to exist in the training data, but their novel combination requires extrapolation. This benchmark provides a standardized and validated tool for the community to rigorously assess the true OOD generalization capabilities of VLAs, encouraging the development of training methodologies that foster autonomous skill recombination rather than reliance on post-training tricks.

**Identification of Common VLA Limitations and Failure Modes:** The study's detailed analysis, particularly the identification of "spatial overfitting," uncovers a fundamental limitation common across multiple state-of-the-art VLAs. The finding that VLAs tend to associate object names with fixed spatial locations from training demonstrations, rather than achieving true object identity or understanding, indicates a gap in their conceptual learning. This suggests that despite impressive in-distribution performance, VLAs may lack a deep semantic understanding of objects and goals. This insight is critical for guiding future research toward developing architectures and training paradigms that foster more abstract and generalizable object-centric and goal-oriented reasoning.

**Advancing Mechanistic Interpretability for Robotics:** By identifying "text latent" as a causal internal representation that can be manipulated to control and combine complex robot behaviors, this work contributes to the nascent field of mechanistic interpretability in robotics. Understanding how specific internal states correspond to high-level skills provides a pathway for reverse-engineering VLA decision-making processes, leading to more transparent, debuggable, and controllable robotic systems. The potential for "obscure prompting" using unembedded text latents also raises considerations for private instruction and security in VLA deployment.

**Future Research Directions:** The results highlight a clear direction for future research: developing "training recipes" that enable VLAs to autonomously combine learned representations for extrapolation, without requiring inference-time interventions like TLI or prompt switching. This could involve exploring novel architectural designs, loss functions, or data augmentation strategies that explicitly encourage the emergence of composable and abstract skill representations. The identified limitation regarding action decoding in models like π0-fast when pushed to generalization limits also points to the need for more robust action generation mechanisms.

In summary, this research not only demonstrates a method to enhance VLA generalization but also provides a framework for understanding their intrinsic capabilities and fundamental limitations. By offering both a solution and a diagnostic tool, it serves as a valuable catalyst for the progression towards truly generalist and robust embodied AI agents.
