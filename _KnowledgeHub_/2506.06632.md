# Curriculum Reinforcement Learning from Easy to Hard Tasks Improves LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> The E2H Reasoner framework enhances Large Language Model reasoning by employing curriculum reinforcement learning that progresses systematically from easy to hard tasks using probabilistic schedulers. This method enables LLMs to build complex problem-solving strategies, achieving state-of-the-art performance on five reasoning benchmarks and demonstrating 2.5-3 times greater sample efficiency than direct training on hard tasks.

## Problem

- Applying reinforcement learning (RL) directly to inherently difficult reasoning tasks for LLMs often suffers from sparse reward signals, hindering effective strategy acquisition.
- Existing curriculum learning approaches for LLM post-training often use rigid strategies that can lead to task forgetting or overfitting to trivial tasks, limiting generalization.
- The definition of LLM 'reasoning' necessitates training methods that promote genuine generalization beyond surface-level pattern recognition to new, complex problems.
- Designing fine-grained, step-by-step reward functions for complex multi-skill reasoning tasks is labor-intensive and not easily scalable.

## Method

- Tasks are decomposed into ==difficulty levels== (trivial, easy, medium, hard) to create a smoother learning trajectory, using both human annotations and automatic zero-shot error rate estimation.
- Reinforcement learning is employed where the LLM is optimized to maximize a sparse reward signal for correct final answers, formalized as a discounted Markov Decision Process.
- Probabilistic curriculum schedulers (Cosine and Gaussian) are introduced to adaptively transition the training focus from easier to harder tasks, mitigating issues like task forgetting and overfitting.

## Results

- E2H Reasoner, especially with Gaussian scheduling (E2H-G), consistently achieved state-of-the-art performance across five reasoning tasks (Blocksworld, Countdown, MATH, GSM8K, AQuA), particularly on hard and out-of-distribution subsets.
- Curriculum reinforcement learning (CRL) methods demonstrated **2.5**-3 times greater sample efficiency compared to non-CRL approaches that train exclusively on hard samples, while also achieving superior performance.
- Direct training of LLMs solely on hard or out-of-distribution examples proved largely ineffective, often underperforming Chain of Thought baselines and yielding negligible performance gains on challenging categories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Systematic progression from easy to hard tasks via curriculum reinforcement learning is crucial for LLMs to acquire robust reasoning skills and generalize to complex, out-of-distribution problems.
> - Probabilistic schedulers, particularly Gaussian (E2H-G), effectively prevent overfitting to easy tasks while ensuring sufficient exposure to harder ones, thereby improving generalization in sparse-reward environments.
> - The findings suggest that even smaller LLMs can develop strong reasoning capabilities when trained with effective curriculum-based methods, challenging previous assumptions about their inherent limitations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated capabilities in various reasoning-intensive tasks, including multi-step arithmetic, planning, and code generation. However, the conceptualization of "reasoning" in LLMs remains an area of active discussion, with some interpretations blurring the distinction between genuine reasoning and surface-level pattern recognition. This work adopts a definition of reasoning that emphasizes generalization: the capacity to extract underlying principles from simpler tasks and apply them to more complex, novel scenarios.

Current approaches to enhance LLM capabilities often involve post-training methods, primarily Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL). SFT, which involves imitating curated examples, has been observed to lead to overfitting to surface patterns, potentially limiting generalization. In contrast, RL-based post-training, utilizing task-specific rewards, has shown promise in improving reasoning performance, as evidenced by models such as DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI o1. Nevertheless, RL alone faces challenges when applied to inherently difficult tasks where pre-trained models exhibit low zero-shot performance. This difficulty is attributed to sparse reward signals on hard tasks, where rewards are granted only for correct final answers.

Curriculum Learning (CL) offers a strategy to mitigate the sparse reward problem in RL by structuring training from easier to harder tasks, thereby promoting more stable and effective skill acquisition. While CL has been applied to general RL domains, its extension to LLM post-training has largely relied on simplified strategies, such as fixed-iteration switching between easy and hard tasks. This paper positions itself within this context by introducing a curriculum reinforcement learning (CRL) approach that utilizes a probabilistic scheduler to gradually transition the training focus from easy to hard tasks. The research aims to advance LLM reasoning and generalization beyond the limitations of prior RL and CL methodologies by providing a more nuanced and adaptive training schedule.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models through reinforcement learning (RL) post-training. The authors aim to enable LLMs to develop robust problem-solving strategies that generalize effectively to complex problems, including those they initially fail to solve in a zero-shot setting.

This objective is motivated by several identified challenges and observations within the current LLM training landscape:

- **Limitations of Pure RL on Hard Tasks**: While recent RL post-trained models have demonstrated improved reasoning on specific tasks, prior studies indicate that applying RL solely to inherently difficult tasks is less effective. This is due to sparse learning signals, as rewards are typically only given for correct final answers, making it challenging for models with low initial performance to explore and learn effective strategies.
- **Ambiguity in LLM Reasoning and Need for Generalization**: The definition of "reasoning" in LLMs often conflates genuine understanding with surface-level pattern recognition. The authors motivate their work by adopting a definition of reasoning as the ability to extract core principles from simpler tasks and apply them to harder, potentially out-of-distribution (OOD) problems. This necessitates training methods that move beyond mere imitation.
- **Addressing the Distribution Gap**: Learning tasks that significantly exceed the base LLM's initial reasoning capabilities often introduces a substantial distribution gap between the model's pre-training data and the target task data. This gap contributes to sparse rewards and can lead to overfitting or memorization, hindering generalization. The research aims to bridge this gap through structured task exposure.
- **Simplifying Reward Design for Complex Skills**: Complex reasoning tasks frequently require LLMs to synthesize multiple skills. Designing fine-grained, step-by-step reward functions for such tasks is generally task-specific and labor-intensive, limiting scalability and generalizability. The motivation is to implicitly guide skill acquisition through task decomposition and scheduling, obviating the need for extensive handcrafted reward shaping.
- **Improving upon Existing Curriculum Learning Approaches**: Previous applications of curriculum learning in LLM post-training have often used rudimentary strategies, such as abrupt switching between easy and hard tasks. These rigid approaches can lead to issues like task forgetting (degradation of performance on earlier tasks) or overfitting to trivial tasks (reward hacking), thereby undermining the acquisition of generalized reasoning skills. The authors are motivated to develop a more sophisticated, probabilistic scheduling mechanism to address these limitations.

In summary, the motivation is to develop a theoretically grounded and empirically effective curriculum-based RL framework, named E2H Reasoner, that systematically guides LLMs from easy to hard tasks, thereby fostering genuine reasoning capabilities, enhancing generalization, and overcoming the inherent challenges of sparse rewards and complex skill acquisition in RL.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The E2H Reasoner (E2H) methodology is built upon a reinforcement learning (RL) framework for LLM reasoning, enhanced by task decomposition and a probabilistic curriculum scheduler.

#### 3.1. RL Formulation for LLM Reasoning

The LLM reasoning process is formalized as a discounted Markov Decision Process (MDP) $M = (S, A, P, r, \gamma)$.
- **State Space (S)**: Consists of all valid token prefixes, where $s_t = (x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_t)$ represents a sequence of tokens.
- **Action Space (A)**: The vocabulary $\Sigma$, meaning each action corresponds to generating the next token.
- **Policy ($\pi_\theta$)**: The pre-trained LLM, which outputs a probability distribution over the next token conditioned on the current prefix, $p_\theta(x_{t+1}|x_0, \ldots, x_t)$.
- **Reward Function (r)**: Designed to be sparse. Intermediate states and actions receive a reward of 0. A non-zero reward $r(y)$ is granted only when the final predicted answer, enclosed by `<answer>` tags, is completed and correct.
- **Goal**: Optimize the policy to maximize the expected cumulative reward, thereby encouraging correct and well-formatted answers derived through effective reasoning. The use of `<think>` and `<answer>` tags, adopted from prior work, distinguishes intermediate reasoning steps from the final answer.

#### 3.2. Task Decomposition for RL Post-Training

To address the challenges of the distribution gap between pre-training and target tasks, as well as the complexity of reward design for multi-skill reasoning, the authors propose task decomposition.
- **Mechanism**: Training data is divided into subsets of increasing difficulty: trivial, easy, medium, and hard. This creates a sequence of intermediate task distributions ($d_k$) that interpolate between the pre-training distribution ($d_0$) and the final target task distribution ($d_K$), thus reducing distribution shift and improving training stability.
- **Skill Acquisition**: This decomposition breaks down the acquisition of complex skills into simpler, manageable steps. For instance, a complex Countdown problem requiring multiple operations is preceded by simpler 2-number problems focusing solely on arithmetic, allowing the model to build foundational competence. This approach aims to reduce the need for handcrafted, step-by-step reward shaping.
- **Difficulty Signals**:
 - **Human-Annotated**: For Blocksworld (plan length), Countdown (number of operations), and MATH (problem level), existing human annotations are utilized to define difficulty levels, including an out-of-distribution (OOD) split for generalization assessment.
 - **Automatic Estimation**: For datasets lacking explicit labels, such as GSM8K and AQuA, difficulty is estimated based on the base model's zero-shot error rates under Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting. The model generates 20 responses per question, and questions are grouped into difficulty quartiles based on their error rates.

#### 3.3. Training Schedulers for LLM Reasoning Ability

The method explores different scheduling strategies to navigate the task decomposition, aiming to mitigate task forgetting and overfitting (reward hacking) commonly associated with rigid curriculum learning.
- **Traditional Scheduling ($S_{trad}$)**: A sequential curriculum where tasks are presented in a fixed order, transitioning from one difficulty stage to the next after a set number of training steps.
- **Balanced Scheduling ($S_{balanced}$)**: All tasks of varying difficulties are mixed and sampled randomly. This represents a baseline where task difficulty is not explicitly considered in scheduling, akin to the default behavior of standard policy optimization algorithms (e.g., GRPO).
- **Cosine Scheduling (E2H-C)**: A non-parametric strategy where the sampling probability for tasks is determined by a cosine function that gradually shifts focus. The easiest tasks have the highest sampling probability initially, which then decreases, while harder tasks' probabilities increase over training steps. This aims to smoothly interpolate between tasks.
- **Gaussian Scheduling (E2H-G)**: A probabilistic scheduling strategy inspired by Gaussian mixture models. It assumes that task data distributions follow Gaussian distributions. The sampling probability for a task at a given training step ($x_t$) is proportional to its likelihood under a Gaussian distribution whose mean moves along the training timeline. This scheduler has two hyperparameters: $\sigma$ (controls sampling concentration) and $\beta$ (controls the speed of movement of the sampling position $x_t$). It offers more flexible control to prevent trivial task overfitting by quickly decaying the sampling probability of easy tasks after initial exposure.

#### 3.4. Theoretical Analysis

The framework for curriculum reinforcement learning (CRL) for LLMs is analyzed using the lens of Approximate Policy Iteration (API).
- **API Assumptions**: The analysis incorporates standard API assumptions including approximate policy evaluation (bounded estimation error $\delta_k$), approximate greedy policy improvement (policy is $\epsilon_k$-greedy), distribution mismatch (bounded concentrability $C_k$), and bounded curriculum drift (deviation between successive optimal Q-functions $\Vert Q^*_K - Q^*_k \Vert_{d_K}$ is bounded).
- **Convergence Guarantee (Theorem 3.1)**: The authors establish a bound on the final performance gap ($E_K = \Vert Q^*_K - Q^{\pi_K}_K \Vert_{d_K}$) of the learned policy $\pi_K$ compared to the optimal policy $Q^*_K$. This bound decomposes into terms related to actor convergence bias, accumulated evaluation errors (stochastic and function approximation), policy update rule errors, and a "curriculum approximation error" representing the cumulative gap between intermediate curriculum-optimal values and the final optimal value. This decomposition highlights how CRL improves sample efficiency (small $\delta_k$, $\epsilon_k$) and ensures smooth interpolation.
- **Finite Sample Approximation Error Analysis (Theorem 3.2)**: This section derives finite-sample complexity bounds, building on linear function approximation within TD-learning. It demonstrates that under specific conditions—namely, a well-designed curriculum with smoother trajectories and smaller approximation errors under finite samples—CRL requires fewer total samples than direct learning on the hardest task. This is further supported by an inequality (Eq. 2) showing that CRL is more sample-efficient when curriculum error allocation and complexity progression follow a geometric scheme.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments address three research questions: the role of task decomposition, the impact of task scheduling, and the ability of LLMs to learn directly from hard tasks.

#### 4.1. Impact of Task Decomposition (RQ1)

- **Observation**: Including trivial and easy examples during RL post-training is crucial for developing core skills in LLMs.
- **Evidence**: Table 1 shows that models trained with a balanced scheduler on a curriculum including trivial, easy, medium, and hard tasks significantly outperform models trained only on hard or medium+hard tasks across Blocksworld, Countdown, and MATH datasets. This indicates that foundational learning on simpler tasks facilitates transfer to more complex problems and improves out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization.

#### 4.2. Impact of Task Scheduling (RQ2)

- **Scheduler Performance**:
 - **Balanced Scheduling**: Serves as a strong baseline but lacks structured progression, leading to suboptimal learning compared to more sophisticated curriculum methods.
 - **Traditional Curriculum Learning (CL)**: Fixed-order progression can result in task forgetting (degradation on earlier tasks) or overfitting to easier tasks, especially in sparse-reward settings. For example, CL on Blocksworld shows lower performance on medium and hard tasks compared to balanced or E2H-G.
 - **E2H-C (Cosine Scheduling)**: Effective for tasks where the base model exhibits reasonable performance across difficulty levels (e.g., MATH), as it gradually shifts focus to harder tasks. However, it can lead to overfitting on tasks with very sparse rewards for harder examples (e.g., Blocksworld), where it shows degraded performance.
 - **E2H-G (Gaussian Scheduling)**: Addresses the overfitting issue by rapidly decaying the sampling probability of trivial and easy tasks after initial exposure. This allows sufficient early learning while quickly transitioning focus to harder examples, improving generalization in sparse-reward environments (Tables 2, 3, 13).
- **Adaptability to Difficulty Estimation**: E2H Reasoner remains effective even when difficulty labels are not human-annotated, instead relying on error rates as a proxy for difficulty (GSM8K, AQuA). Table 4 demonstrates comparable or superior performance across these datasets, and robustness to the number of difficulty splits is shown in Table 15.

#### 4.3. Learning from Difficult Examples Directly (RQ3)

- **Observation**: Training LLMs directly on challenging examples (hard or OOD subsets) is largely ineffective.
- **Evidence**: Table 3 shows that Qwen-2.5 1.5B, when trained directly on Level 5 MATH, underperforms even the Chain of Thought (CoT) baseline. Similarly, training on only "Hard" or "OOD" tasks in Blocksworld and Countdown yields negligible or zero performance on these categories. This finding reinforces the necessity of curriculum-based learning for LLM reasoning.

#### 4.4. Comparison with Baselines and Advanced Insights

- **Self-Evolve**: This adaptive curriculum baseline, which samples problems at a 50% solve rate, shows variable reasoning performance across models and tasks (Table 3). The authors suggest that being only halfway proficient is often insufficient for robust reasoning, and its implementation requires careful hyperparameter tuning.
- **E2H Reasoner Superiority**: E2H Reasoner, particularly E2H-G, consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance across five reasoning tasks (Blocksworld, Countdown, MATH, GSM8K, AQuA), especially on hard and OOD tasks.
- **Complementarity with DAPO**: Combining E2H with DAPO (an RL method filtering problems that are too easy or too hard) further improves performance. Figure 7 illustrates that E2H-G and E2H-C, when combined with DAPO, reduce the fraction of batches with zero advantage, suggesting better alignment of problem difficulty with the model's competence. This indicates that E2H shapes the difficulty-conditioned sampling distribution that DAPO resamples from, leading to synergistic gains (Tables 5, 6).
- **Generalization to External Benchmarks**: Models post-trained using E2H on the MATH dataset demonstrate stronger generalization capabilities on challenging external benchmarks like AIME24 and OlympiadBench compared to GRPO and Self-Evolve (Table 7).
- **Sample Efficiency**: Empirical analysis on Blocksworld (Table 8) supports the theoretical finding (Theorem 3.2) that CRL methods (E2H-C, E2H-G) are 2.5-3 times more sample efficient than non-CRL methods that train exclusively on hard samples, while also achieving superior performance.
- **SFT vs. RL**: A comparison with SFT (Table 11) highlights that SFT's performance is inconsistent across tasks, performing well when training data aligns closely with downstream tasks (e.g., Blocksworld) but poorly on tasks with broader distributions or OOD examples (e.g., Countdown), unlike the more consistent performance of RL-based methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The E2H Reasoner introduced in this paper represents an advancement in enhancing the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models through curriculum reinforcement learning (CRL).

- **Theoretical Grounding and Empirical Validation**: The work provides theoretical convergence guarantees within an approximate policy iteration framework and derives finite-sample complexity bounds, demonstrating that CRL can require fewer total samples than direct learning on hard tasks. This theoretical basis is supported by empirical results showing improved performance and sample efficiency, particularly on challenging reasoning benchmarks.
- **Addressing Limitations of Prior RL and CL**: E2H Reasoner directly addresses known limitations of applying reinforcement learning to complex tasks (sparse rewards, distribution gaps) and the rigidities of previous curriculum learning approaches (task forgetting, overfitting). By scheduling tasks from easy to hard with probabilistic schedulers (Cosine and Gaussian), the method enables LLMs to progressively build reasoning skills, including on tasks they initially fail.
- **Challenging Assumptions about Small LLMs**: The consistent improvements demonstrated by E2H Reasoner on smaller LLMs (e.g., Qwen 1.5B, LLaMA 3.2 3B) challenge the assumption that such models inherently lack robust reasoning capabilities, suggesting that effective training methodologies can unlock significant potential.
- **Practical and Scalable Method**: E2H Reasoner offers a practical and scalable approach for LLM post-training. Its ability to function effectively even without human-annotated difficulty labels, by using model error rates as a proxy, enhances its applicability across diverse datasets. The complementarity with existing RL methods like DAPO suggests its potential for integration into broader training pipelines.

The potential impact of this research is substantial:
- **Improved LLM Reasoning and Generalization**: By enabling LLMs to acquire fundamental problem-solving principles and generalize them to more complex, out-of-distribution problems, this work contributes to the development of more robust and capable AI systems.
- **Broader Accessibility of Advanced AI**: Enhancing the reasoning abilities of smaller-scale LLMs can reduce the computational resources required for advanced AI capabilities, potentially making sophisticated reasoning more accessible to a wider range of researchers and applications.
- **Applications in Critical Domains**: Improved LLM reasoning could have positive implications across various fields, including:
 - **Education**: Facilitating more effective personalized learning experiences and automated tutoring systems.
 - **Scientific Research**: Assisting with complex problem-solving, hypothesis generation, and data interpretation.
 - **Software Engineering**: Enhancing code generation, debugging, and automated program synthesis.
 - **Decision-Making Systems**: Supporting more reliable and nuanced decision-making in areas like healthcare and legal analysis.
- **Future Research Directions**: The findings encourage further exploration into adaptive curriculum strategies and more sophisticated probabilistic schedulers that dynamically adjust to model competence, thereby pushing the boundaries of what LLMs can achieve in reasoning and generalization.
