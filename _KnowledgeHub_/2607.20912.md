# URF: A Unified Robot Control-Policy Framework for Stable Contact Aware Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The Unified Robot Control-Policy Framework (URF) enables robots to predict compliant actions and adapt their low-level control behavior between admittance and impedance modes. This integration leads to significantly higher success rates and enhanced stability in rigid contact manipulation tasks compared to fixed-mode controllers.

## Problem

- Learning-based robot manipulation policies often struggle with stability and failure in tasks involving rigid contact due to rapid force buildup or large oscillations.
- A common decoupling exists between learned policy action prediction and its execution by a static low-level controller, leading to inconsistent and potentially unstable outcomes.
- It is challenging to effectively integrate adaptive impedance-admittance control behaviors into policy learning frameworks due to the difficulty of obtaining ground-truth environment stiffness for supervision.

## Method

- **URF** introduces a unified framework where a high-level policy predicts not only virtual target poses and stiffness but also an ==impedance-admittance switch ratio== (ntn_tnt).
- A low-level ==unified impedance-admittance controller== dynamically adjusts its behavior from admittance-dominant to impedance-dominant based on the policy-predicted switch ratio.
- The switch ratio (ntn_tnt) labels are supervised by ==measured force magnitude== from demonstrations, transitioning from admittance (low force) to impedance (high force) behavior without requiring environment stiffness ground truth.

## Results

- On the rigid box-flipping task, URF achieved a **90%** success rate with **0%** critical failures, drastically outperforming admittance-only control (**25%** success, **86.7%** critical failures).
- For the line-pressing task, URF demonstrated **100%** success and **100%** contact-maintenance with significantly reduced force oscillations (**0.96 N** ORMS), while admittance-only control failed entirely (**0%** success, **3.22 N** ORMS).
- URF's adaptive control strategy mitigated rapid force growth (**23.1 N/s** PFGR vs. **48.5 N/s** for admittance-only) and maintained precise trajectory tracking while ensuring stable contact, a balance not achieved by fixed-mode baselines.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Unifying learned compliant action prediction with an adaptive low-level controller behavior is crucial for achieving stable and successful manipulation in rigid contact environments.
> - Dynamically adjusting the impedance-admittance balance allows the robot to seamlessly prioritize accurate motion tracking in free space and robust force regulation during stiff contact.
> - Supervising the impedance-admittance switch ratio using measured force magnitude provides a practical and effective way to learn adaptive controller behavior from standard demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of learning-based robot manipulation has increasingly moved towards end-to-end policies that map sensory observations directly to robot actions, departing from traditional hierarchical modular pipelines. These policies, often utilizing expressive architectures like generative models and large demonstration datasets, have demonstrated capabilities across a range of manipulation problems.

However, contact-aware manipulation, particularly involving rigid interactions, continues to present challenges. Task success in these scenarios depends not only on motion generation but also on maintaining stable contact, regulating force, and adapting to changes in physical interaction. Standard visual and proprioceptive sensors may not provide sufficient information regarding contact state, such as onset, force buildup, or loss of contact. To address this, recent research has integrated additional sensory data, including contact force/torque or tactile measurements, into observations to allow policies to adapt during contact. Furthermore, some methods have expanded the policy action space beyond a single reference pose to include interaction quantities like desired forces or 6D wrenches for admittance or impedance control, or compliance-related quantities such as varying stiffness and virtual targets.

Despite these advancements, a separation often persists between the prediction of compliant actions by a learned policy and their physical execution by a low-level controller. This decoupling can lead to varied and potentially unstable outcomes for the same predicted command, including unstable contact, tracking errors, or excessive loading, depending on the specific low-level controller used. This issue is particularly relevant to the classical trade-off between admittance control and impedance control. Admittance control offers precise motion tracking in free space and soft contact but can be susceptible to instability in stiff contact environments due. Impedance control, conversely, provides robustness in stiff contact by regulating the dynamic relationship between motion and force in a dissipative manner, though it may be less precise in free-space tracking. While unified impedance-admittance controllers have been proposed to switch between these behaviors, their adaptive utilization has not been widely integrated into policy learning frameworks. The importance of the low-level controller is often understated in compliant settings, where soft materials or deformable objects can passively absorb contact forces, thereby masking potential instabilities. This paper aims to bridge this gap by treating action prediction and low-level control as an integrated system, allowing the policy to predict not only compliant action parameters but also the controller behavior for their execution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a unified robot control-policy framework (URF) that integrates learned compliant action prediction with the adaptive behavior of a low-level interaction controller. The central motivation stems from the observed limitations of conventional learning-based manipulation policies, particularly in rigid contact scenarios, where a rigid separation between policy-predicted actions and their execution by a fixed low-level controller can lead to instability, tracking errors, and physical damage.

Specific objectives include:

- **Unifying Action Prediction and Low-Level Control:** To propose a framework where the robot's policy predicts not only the desired compliant actions (virtual targets and stiffness) but also the appropriate low-level controller behavior, specifically an impedance-admittance switch ratio. This aims to overcome the problems arising from a static low-level controller executing dynamically varying compliant commands.
- **Enabling Adaptive Controller Behavior:** To allow the low-level controller to dynamically adjust its mode—ranging from admittance-dominant (for accurate motion tracking) to impedance-dominant (for stable rigid contact)—based on the policy's prediction of the switch ratio. This adaptive capability is intended to leverage the strengths of both control paradigms according to the task phase and contact conditions.
- **Addressing Data Scarcity for Controller-Mode Prediction:** To develop a practical method for supervising the prediction of the impedance-admittance switch ratio without requiring ground-truth environment stiffness data, which is typically unavailable in demonstrations. The motivation here is to enable learning of controller-aware actions directly from standard demonstration datasets.
- **Improving Stability and Reducing Failures in Rigid Contact:** To demonstrate that by integrating policy prediction with adaptive low-level control, the robot can achieve higher task success rates while mitigating common failure modes observed with admittance-only execution. These failures include rapid force buildup, large force oscillations, tool breakage, and robot safety stops, which are particularly prevalent in rigid contact.
- **Maintaining Task-Level Motion Tracking:** While prioritizing contact stability, the framework also aims to ensure that accurate motion tracking, essential for task completion in free-space or tangential movements during contact, is preserved or enhanced by the adaptive controller behavior.

Ultimately, the motivation is to advance the reliability and robustness of learning-based robot manipulation in complex, contact-rich environments, moving towards a paradigm where the learned policy intelligently dictates not just *what* action to take, but also *how* that action should be physically implemented.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Unified Robot Control-Policy Framework (URF) integrates a low-level unified interaction controller with a high-level policy designed to predict controller-aware actions.

**A. Low-level Controller**
URF employs a modified version of the unified impedance-admittance controller. This controller receives three time-varying inputs from the high-level policy:
- $x_{vt_t}$: The virtual target pose, representing the equilibrium point for the desired interaction dynamics.
- $K_t$: The stiffness matrix, which defines the desired interaction stiffness.
- $n_t$: An impedance-admittance switch ratio, where $n_t \in$, determining the proportion of the control period spent in each mode.

1. **Impedance Phase:** In this phase, the controller computes a force command such that the end-effector follows desired interaction dynamics around the virtual target. The desired impedance behavior is defined by $M_d \ddot{e}_t + D_d \dot{e}_t + K_t e_t = F_{ext_t}$, where $e_t$ is the virtual-target error, $M_d$ and $D_d$ are desired inertia and damping, and $F_{ext_t}$ is the external force. This yields an impedance force command, $F_{imp_t}$, which is then converted into joint torques. This mode enhances robustness in stiff contact.
2. **Admittance Phase:** Here, the measured external force is converted into a desired motion command through outer admittance dynamics, $M_d \ddot{x}_{d_t} + D_d \dot{x}_{d_t} + K_t (x_{d_t} - x_{vt_t}) = F_{ext_t}$. This desired position $x_{d_t}$ is then tracked by an inner position controller using proportional and derivative gains to generate a force command, $F_{adm_t}$, which is also converted into joint torques. This mode is suitable for accurate motion tracking in free space or weak contact.

The switch ratio $n_t$ dictates the duty cycle: $n_t=1$ for admittance-only, $n_t=0$ for impedance-only. Intermediate values of $n_t$ enable switching between phases within each control period. State mapping ensures continuity of robot state variables during transitions, providing stable switching behavior.

**B. High-level Policy**
The high-level policy utilizes a conditional diffusion model to predict a sequence of controller-aware actions from multimodal observations.

1. **Observation Encoding:** At each query, the policy processes visual, proprioceptive, and force observations. The input history includes two RGB frames, three end-effector poses, and one second of force/torque measurements. RGB images are encoded by a pretrained CLIP ViT-B/32 backbone. Force history is converted to a log-spectrogram and encoded by a ViT encoder. These features are fused using a Transformer encoder and concatenated with the proprioceptive state to form a global observation condition $c$.
2. **Controller-Aware Action Representation:** The policy predicts an action $a_t = [x_{vt_t}, K_t, n_t]$ for each time step. $x_{vt_t}$ is the virtual target pose defining the equilibrium of interaction. $K_t$ is the stiffness matrix describing the force-displacement relationship. $n_t \in$ is the impedance-admittance switch ratio specifying the interaction control mode.
3. **Demonstration Label Construction:**
 - **Data Collection:** Demonstrations are collected via direct human teleoperation, recording visual, proprioceptive, and force/torque data.
 - **Virtual Target and Stiffness Labels:** These are generated using the adaptive compliance labeling procedure, which derives an approximate stiffness matrix $K_t$ and reconstructs the virtual target trajectory $x_{vt_t}$ from measured motion and force data.
 - **Switch Ratio ($n_t$) Labels:** Since environment stiffness is challenging to estimate from demonstrations, the measured force magnitude $|F_{ext_t}|$ is used as a proxy for contact loading. User-defined force bounds, $f_{min}$ and $f_{max}$, establish the range over which $n_t$ transitions:
 - If $|F_{ext_t}| \leq f_{min}$, $n_t = 1$ (admittance-dominant, for motion tracking).
 - If $|F_{ext_t}| \geq f_{max}$, $n_t = 0$ (impedance-dominant, for stable stiff contact).
 - For $f_{min} < |F_{ext_t}| < f_{max}$, $n_t$ transitions linearly between 1 and 0.
 This labeling strategy ensures that large contact forces, which could be unstable under admittance control, shift the controller towards a more conservative impedance-dominant behavior, while low-force regions retain admittance-dominant behavior for tracking accuracy.
4. **Policy Training and Execution:** A conditional 1D U-Net is trained to predict future action chunks (16 action steps) conditioned on the observation condition $c$. Training uses the diffusion noise-prediction loss, optimizing $x_{vt}$, $K$, and $n$ jointly. During inference, DDIM sampling generates an action chunk, and its per-step actions are executed in a receding-horizon loop, with re-prediction from the latest observations.

**C. Experimental Setup**
Experiments are conducted on a Franka Research 3 manipulator equipped with an Intel RealSense D405 camera and an AIDIN Robotics AFT200-D80 force/torque sensor. Both the end tool and objects are 3D-printed from PLA to ensure rigid contact conditions.
- **Tasks:** Box-flipping (100 demonstrations) and line-pressing (50 demonstrations).
- **Evaluation:** 20 trials per method for each task, with varied initial object poses.
- **Baselines:**
 - **DP w/ force:** A standard diffusion policy with force encoding, executed by a critically damped PD position controller.
 - **ACP:** Adaptive Compliance Policy, which predicts virtual targets and adaptive stiffness, executed with an admittance controller.
 - **URF w/ n=0.5:** Ablation with a fixed impedance-admittance switch ratio of 0.5.
 - **URF w/ n=0:** Ablation with a fixed switch ratio of 0, corresponding to impedance-dominant execution.
 - **URF (Ours):** The full proposed method with adaptive prediction of $x_{vt}$, $K$, and $n$.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of URF on box-flipping and line-pressing tasks, designed for rigid contact, yielded the following results:

**A. Box-Flipping Task**
This task involved lifting and rotating a stiff box with a 3D-printed tool. Metrics included success rate (SR), critical failure rate (CFR), peak force growth rate (PFGR), and free-space tracking error (FSTE).

- **Baseline Performance:**
 - **DP w/ force:** Achieved 0% success rate, failing to establish sufficient contact or apply necessary load.
 - **ACP:** Achieved a 25% success rate but suffered from a high critical failure rate of 86.7%. Failures were predominantly due to tool breakage or robot safety stops. ACP exhibited a PFGR of 48.5 N/s, which was approximately double that of URF variants, indicating rapid force buildup leading to damage. While ACP had the lowest FSTE, this accuracy in free-space did not translate to stable contact.
- **URF Variants Performance:**
 - **URF w/ n=0.5** and **URF w/ n=0:** Achieved 60% and 50% success rates, respectively, with 0% critical failure rates. Their PFGR values (24.4 N/s and 21.7 N/s, respectively) were significantly lower than ACP, suggesting that impedance-like behavior near contact effectively mitigates rapid force increases. However, fixed switch ratios compromised either approach accuracy (n=0) or overall task stability.
 - **URF (Proposed):** Achieved the highest success rate of 90% and a 0% critical failure rate, with a PFGR of 23.1 N/s. URF demonstrated adaptive behavior by lowering the switch ratio (n ≈ 0) and contact-direction stiffness (k_x ≈ 200 N/m) before contact, preventing rapid force buildup. It then adjusted stiffness and switch ratio throughout the task, restoring admittance-dominant behavior (n ≈ 1) for post-contact tracking. This adaptive switching balanced stable force management with accurate contact point placement.

**B. Line-Pressing Task**
This task required the robot to follow a line on a rigid surface while maintaining a contact force above 5 N. Metrics included success rate (SR), critical failure rate (CFR), contact-maintenance rate (CMR), and oscillation root-mean-square (ORMS).

- **Baseline Performance:**
 - **DP w/ force:** Achieved 0% success.
 - **ACP:** Achieved 0% success rate, with a 100% critical failure rate, always ending in robot safety stops. The force trace revealed repeated drops to near 0 N and rises towards 50 N, indicating unstable contact. ACP exhibited the highest ORMS (3.22 N), demonstrating large force oscillations due to its admittance-only execution interacting with a rigid surface.
- **URF Variants Performance:**
 - **URF w/ n=0.5** and **URF w/ n=0:** Both achieved a 70% success rate and a 100% contact-maintenance rate with 0% critical failures. They showed significantly lower ORMS values (1.20 N and 0.92 N, respectively) compared to ACP, indicating that impedance-like execution successfully suppressed large force oscillations. However, due to reduced tracking accuracy associated with fixed impedance-dominant behavior, some trials deviated from the line and failed to meet the completion threshold.
 - **URF (Proposed):** Achieved a 100% success rate and a 100% contact-maintenance rate with 0% critical failures, and an ORMS of 0.96 N. URF adapted its switch ratio (n) during pressing, balancing impedance-like behavior for stable contact with sufficient admittance-like behavior to accurately follow the line. Trajectory comparisons showed that URF kept the robot's path tightly clustered around the reference line, indicating improved path accuracy while maintaining stable contact.

These results indicate that URF's ability to predict and adapt the low-level controller's impedance-admittance behavior is critical for stable and successful rigid contact-aware manipulation. Fixed-mode controllers, whether admittance-only or fixed impedance/admittance ratios, exhibited significant limitations in either stability or tracking accuracy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The proposed Unified Robot Control-Policy Framework (URF) presents a significant advancement in learning-based robot manipulation, particularly for tasks involving rigid contact. By unifying the learned action prediction with the adaptive behavior of a low-level interaction controller, URF addresses a fundamental limitation in many existing approaches where policy-generated actions are executed by a fixed or independently designed low-level controller.

The core significance of URF lies in its ability to empower the policy to predict not only compliant action parameters, such as virtual targets and stiffness matrices, but also the crucial impedance-admittance switch ratio that dictates *how* these actions are executed. This integration allows the robot to dynamically adapt its interaction control mode based on the immediate task phase and contact conditions, seamlessly transitioning between admittance-dominant behavior for accurate motion tracking in free space or weak contact, and impedance-dominant behavior for stable interaction in stiff contact.

The method's practical utility is further enhanced by its innovative approach to generating switch-ratio labels. By using measured force magnitude as a proxy for environment stiffness, URF overcomes the challenge of requiring unobservable ground-truth environment stiffness data in demonstrations. This force-supervised labeling strategy provides a robust and learnable signal for adapting controller behavior, making the framework applicable to real-world scenarios with less expert knowledge.

The experimental results demonstrate a tangible impact on task performance. In rigid box-flipping, URF significantly increased success rates and reduced critical failures by mitigating rapid force buildup and preventing tool breakage, which plagued admittance-only baselines. In the line-pressing task, URF enabled continuous, stable contact while maintaining accurate path following, in contrast to admittance-only control which led to large force oscillations and safety stops. The ablations confirmed that neither stable contact nor accurate tracking alone is sufficient; successful rigid contact manipulation requires an adaptive balance of both, which URF provides.

This work suggests a broader paradigm shift: for reliable robot manipulation in diverse interaction conditions, the learned policy and the low-level interaction controller should be designed as a coupled, rather than separate, system. This could lead to more robust, safer, and versatile robotic systems capable of handling complex, contact-rich tasks in unstructured environments.

However, the framework has identified limitations. The current impedance-admittance switch ratio is applied uniformly across all control axes, which may be suboptimal for complex tasks like insertion that demand differential compliance along specific axes. Additionally, the reliance on user-defined force bounds ($f_{min}$ and $f_{max}$) for switch-ratio labeling introduces a manual tuning aspect that may need adjustment across different tasks, tools, and contact materials. Future work aims to integrate URF with vision-language-action models (VLA) or robotic foundation models (RFM) to leverage richer scene understanding. This could enable the inference of task- and scene-dependent contact conditions, potentially reducing the need for manual specification of force bounds and supporting even more intricate contact-rich manipulation.
