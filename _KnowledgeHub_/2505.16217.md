# Reward-Aware Proto-Representations in Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This research formalizes the Default Representation (DR) as a reward-aware proto-representation in reinforcement learning, developing efficient learning algorithms and theoretically characterizing its relationship to the Successor Representation. The work demonstrates that DR can lead to superior performance in reward shaping, option discovery, and transfer learning, particularly in environments requiring navigation of complex reward landscapes.

## Problem

- Existing proto-representations, such as the Successor Representation (SR), are reward-agnostic, primarily encoding environmental transition dynamics without considering the desirability of future states.
- Reinforcement learning agents require representations that can differentiate pathways based on associated rewards to effectively navigate complex reward landscapes and maximize cumulative returns.
- The Default Representation (DR), despite its initial proposal in neuroscience, lacked comprehensive theoretical development and practical algorithms for its application within computational reinforcement learning.

## Method

- The authors developed and proved convergence for Dynamic Programming (**DP**) ==and Temporal-Difference== (TD) learning algorithms for the tabular Default Representation (DR), making it practical for computational RL.
- They extended the DR formulation to handle state-action dependent rewards and introduced 'Default Features' (DFs) to enable function approximation for use in larger state spaces.
- Theoretical analysis characterized the DR's eigenspectrum and established a formal relationship with the SR's eigenvectors under specific reward conditions, clarifying when reward-awareness differentiates the representations.

## Results

- DR-based reward shaping significantly outperformed SR-based methods and no shaping in grid-world environments with low-reward regions, enabling agents to learn optimal, reward-aware paths.
- Reward-Aware Covering Eigenoptions (RACE) demonstrated a risk-averse exploration strategy, avoiding low-reward regions, which led to faster learning and superior final returns when combined with Q-learning compared to SR-based methods.
- Default Features (DFs) proved highly effective for transfer learning, directly computing optimal policies for new reward configurations when only terminal state rewards changed, outperforming Successor Features (SFs) in this specific scenario.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The DR uniquely encodes the expected exponentiated sum of rewards along all trajectories between states, directly integrating reward information into its representation, unlike SR's focus on expected discounted visits.
> - DR and SR share the same eigenvectors when non-terminal rewards are constant and negative, indicating a fundamental link but also precisely when DR's reward-awareness starts to provide distinct features.
> - Reward-aware proto-representations can intrinsically guide agents towards optimal, risk-averse behavior by encoding information about undesirable regions, leading to more effective exploration and policy learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Learning effective representations is a foundational and critical challenge in reinforcement learning. Such representations aim to distill complex environmental information into a more compact and useful form for an agent to learn policies, plan, and generalize. Among these, the **Successor Representation (SR)**, introduced by Dayan in 1993, has gained significant traction. The SR represents states by the expected discounted future occupancy of other states, effectively encoding the *transition dynamics* of an environment under a given policy. Its appeal lies in its ability to facilitate generalization, aid in credit assignment, and improve exploration, and it has even found parallels in neuroscience as a model for spatial cognition. Concepts like proto-value functions and slow feature analysis share a similar underlying philosophy of capturing temporal structure.

However, a well-recognized limitation of the SR and many other related "proto-representations" (a term coined in this paper to refer to such temporal dynamics-capturing representations) is their **reward-agnostic nature**. They primarily encode *where* an agent expects to go, not *how rewarding* those future states or trajectories might be. This poses a challenge in environments where the optimal path is not necessarily the shortest path in terms of transitions, but rather the path that maximizes reward, potentially requiring detours around undesirable (low-reward) regions.

This paper directly addresses this gap by introducing and thoroughly analyzing the **Default Representation (DR)** as a "reward-aware proto-representation." The DR was initially proposed in a neuroscience context by Piray and Daw (2021) within the framework of linearly solvable Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), primarily to explain aspects of replanning and cognitive control. However, its theoretical foundations and practical applications within computational RL have remained largely underexplored.

This work significantly expands upon the nascent understanding of DR by:
- **Formalizing its learning mechanisms:** Moving beyond its initial definition to establish efficient dynamic programming and temporal-difference learning algorithms.
- **Characterizing its properties:** Delving into its eigenspectrum and its relationship to the SR, revealing when and how it differs.
- **Extending its applicability:** Generalizing it to handle state-action dependent rewards and incorporating function approximation through "default features."
- **Demonstrating its utility:** Empirically showing its benefits in classic RL challenges where SR has been successful, but where reward-awareness provides an advantage.

By doing so, the paper positions the DR as a strong contender for environments where the reward landscape is complex and necessitates a representation that integrates both transition dynamics and reward information. It offers a principled alternative or complement to the SR, enriching the toolbox for representation learning in RL and fostering a more nuanced understanding of how temporal and reward signals can be jointly encoded for more effective agent behavior. While bisimulation metrics also aim to capture behavioral similarity by considering both immediate rewards and next-state distributions, this paper emphasizes that DR (like SR) directly encodes environment dynamics in a semantically meaningful way that supports planning and other direct applications, unlike distance-preserving mappings from bisimulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance the understanding and practical utility of the Default Representation (DR) as a reward-aware proto-representation in reinforcement learning. This overarching goal is driven by the recognized limitation of existing proto-representations, most notably the Successor Representation (SR), which are inherently reward-agnostic. The authors aim to demonstrate that explicitly incorporating reward dynamics into a state representation can lead to qualitatively different and quantitatively superior performance in various RL tasks, especially those where navigating complex reward landscapes is crucial.

The motivation for this work stems from several specific gaps and opportunities:

1. **Addressing Reward-Agnosticism:** The fundamental motivation is to overcome the reward-agnostic nature of the SR. While SR is excellent at capturing temporal proximity, it treats all transitions equally in terms of desirability. The authors seek a representation that differentiates between pathways based on their associated rewards, guiding agents not just to reachable states, but to *rewarding* reachable states.

2. **Developing Theoretical Foundations for DR:** Despite its initial introduction in neuroscience, the DR lacked comprehensive theoretical development for computational RL. Key theoretical objectives were:
 - To derive **efficient and general algorithms** for learning the DR, including dynamic programming (DP) and temporal-difference (TD) methods, analogous to how SR is learned. This is crucial for practical applicability beyond the tabular case.
 - To **characterize the basis (eigenspectrum)** of the DR's vector space, including its relationship to the SR's eigenvectors, particularly under specific reward conditions. This provides insight into what features DR truly captures.
 - To **extend the DR to handle state-action dependent rewards**, a common scenario in RL, which was not previously fully explored for DR.
 - To formalize the use of DR with **function approximation** through the introduction of "default features" (DFs), enabling its application in larger, non-tabular state spaces.

3. **Empirical Validation and Comparison:** The authors were motivated to empirically compare the DR's performance against the SR in various established RL benchmarks where proto-representations have proven useful. This includes:
 - **Reward Shaping:** Assessing if DR's reward-awareness leads to better shaping signals for optimal policy learning.
 - **Option Discovery:** Investigating if DR can lead to more effective, reward-aware temporally-extended exploration.
 - **Count-Based Exploration:** Exploring if the DR's norm can serve as a meaningful density model for intrinsic motivation, despite its reward-sensitive nature.
 - **Transfer Learning:** Evaluating the efficiency of default features (DFs) in adapting to new reward configurations, especially when terminal rewards change.

By addressing these theoretical and empirical objectives, the paper aims to firmly establish the DR as a valuable, principled tool in the reinforcement learning community, offering a more complete picture of environment dynamics for intelligent agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of this paper is bifurcated into rigorous theoretical development and comprehensive empirical validation.

**Theoretical Development:**
The authors begin by reviewing the Successor Representation (SR) and its definition within the standard MDP framework. They then introduce the Default Representation (DR) within the context of Linearly Solvable MDPs, which typically assumes no discounting (γ=1) and a cost for deviating from a "default policy" (π_d). This framework is crucial for DR's definition, involving element-wise exponentiation of negative rewards.

1. **Closed-Form DR Interpretation:** The authors re-derive the closed-form solution of DR, demonstrating that its entries represent the expected *exponentiated sum of rewards* along all trajectories between two states, weighted by trajectory probabilities under the default policy. This directly contrasts with SR's focus on the *discounted number of visits*.
2. **Eigenspectrum Analysis and SR Relationship:** A significant theoretical contribution is Theorem 3.1, which proves that when the reward function is constant and negative across non-terminal states, the SR and DR share the *same eigenvectors*. Furthermore, it establishes a functional relationship between their eigenvalues. This provides a crucial theoretical link and clarifies when the "reward-awareness" of DR starts to diverge from SR's purely temporal understanding.
3. **General Learning Algorithms:** To make DR practically usable, the paper derives:
 - **Dynamic Programming (DP) Method:** An iterative update rule for the tabular DR and proves its convergence, under the assumption of negative non-terminal rewards.
 - **Temporal-Difference (TD) Learning Method:** An online, incremental update rule (similar to TD(0) for value functions) that estimates DR from sampled transitions, without requiring full knowledge of environment dynamics. This addresses a major gap in previous DR literature.
4. **Extension to State-Action Rewards:** Recognizing that many RL problems involve state-action dependent rewards, the authors extend the DR formulation to this setting, deriving an analogous matrix inversion form for state-action pairs and showing how optimal Q-values and policies can be retrieved.
5. **Function Approximation via Default Features (DFs):** Inspired by Successor Features (SFs), the paper introduces Default Features (DFs). This involves decomposing the terminal reward exponentiation into a product of state features and reward weights. DFs allow for computing optimal value functions (and policies for state-action DR) in the function approximation setting, especially useful for transfer learning when only terminal rewards change. A TD learning rule for DFs is also provided.
6. **Maximum Entropy Representation (MER):** Briefly, the paper also introduces a "Maximum Entropy Representation" (MER) for MaxEnt RL, showing its connection to DR, highlighting its versatility.

**Empirical Validation:**
The empirical methodology focuses on demonstrating the DR's advantages over the SR in settings where reward-awareness is critical.

1. **Environments:** The experiments primarily utilize classical grid-world environments (Grid Task, Four Rooms, Grid Room, Grid Maze), which are explicitly modified to include **low-reward regions**. These negative reward areas are strategically placed to create scenarios where the shortest path (in terms of transitions) is *sub-optimal* in terms of cumulative reward, thereby highlighting the need for reward-aware navigation.
2. **Baselines:** Comparisons are made against SR-based methods, no shaping baselines, and random policies, where appropriate.
3. **Applications:**
 - **Reward Shaping:** The top eigenvector of the DR is used to construct a potential-based reward shaping signal (DR-pot), which is compared to SR-based shaping methods (SR-pot, SR-prior) and no shaping (ns).
 - **Option Discovery:** A DR-based iterative online eigenoption discovery method, **Reward-Aware Covering Eigenoptions (RACE)**, is introduced. It learns the DR and uses its top eigenvector to define intrinsic rewards for option generation, iteratively refining options and representations. This is compared with SR-based Covering Eigenoptions (CEO) and random walks, evaluating state visitation and cumulative reward. The utility of RACE is further tested by combining it with Q-learning.
 - **Count-Based Exploration:** The ℓ2 norm of the DR (specifically, the state-action DR) is used as an intrinsic exploration bonus for Sarsa agents in Riverswim and SixArms environments, comparing its effectiveness against Sarsa + SR.
 - **Transfer Learning:** Default Features (DFs) are compared against Successor Features (SFs) in a Four Rooms environment with multiple terminal states, evaluating their ability to efficiently compute optimal policies when only terminal state rewards change.
4. **Numerical Stability:** The authors explicitly address numerical challenges related to exponentiating rewards in DR computations and discuss solutions like using high-precision libraries (`python-flint`) and analyzing conditions for positive eigenvectors.
5. **Hyperparameter Tuning & Statistical Rigor:** A systematic approach to hyperparameter search is employed, followed by re-running experiments with a large number of seeds (N ≥ 50) for the best settings to mitigate maximization bias and ensure statistical significance, reporting 95% confidence intervals.

This comprehensive approach, spanning theoretical derivation and empirical validation across diverse RL applications, provides a robust foundation for the DR.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents significant theoretical and empirical findings that collectively establish the Default Representation (DR) as a valuable reward-aware proto-representation in reinforcement learning.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

- **Efficient Learning Algorithms:** The authors successfully derived and proved the convergence of a **Dynamic Programming (DP)** method for the tabular DR. Crucially, they also formulated an **incremental Temporal-Difference (TD) learning method** for the DR, making it amenable to online and model-free learning, much like the SR. These algorithms fill a critical gap in the existing literature on DR.
- **Relationship to Successor Representation (SR):** Theorem 3.1 is a key theoretical result, demonstrating that under conditions of constant negative rewards across non-terminal states, the DR and SR **share the same set of eigenvectors**. It further provides a formal, monotonic relationship between their respective eigenvalues. This characterization precisely defines when and how the reward-awareness of the DR aligns with or diverges from the purely temporal aspects captured by the SR.
- **State-Action Generalization:** The DR was formally extended to handle **state-action dependent rewards**, a common scenario in RL, enhancing its practical applicability. This extension maintains the core structure, allowing for the computation of optimal Q-values.
- **Function Approximation with Default Features (DFs):** The concept of **Default Features (DFs)** was introduced, enabling the DR to be used in settings with large state spaces via function approximation. DFs decompose the optimal value function similarly to Successor Features (SFs) but incorporate reward dynamics, allowing for efficient transfer when terminal rewards change.
- **Semantic Interpretation:** The re-derivation of the DR's closed form (Equation 7) clearly shows that DR encodes the *expected exponentiated sum of rewards* along trajectories, directly contrasting with SR's encoding of *expected discounted visits*. This provides a clear semantic meaning to the DR's reward-awareness.

#### 4.2. Empirical Findings

The empirical results consistently highlight the benefits of DR, particularly in environments designed to expose the limitations of reward-agnostic representations.

- **Superior Reward Shaping:** In grid-world environments with **low-reward regions**, DR-based potential-based reward shaping (**DR-pot**) significantly outperformed SR-based shaping methods (SR-pot, SR-prior) and no shaping. This is attributed to DR's top eigenvector effectively capturing the locations of low-reward regions, enabling the agent to learn *optimal, reward-aware paths* that avoid these undesirable areas, rather than merely the shortest paths. In contrast, in environments without low-reward regions, DR-pot and SR-pot performed similarly, aligning with theoretical expectations.
- **Reward-Aware Option Discovery:** The proposed **Reward-Aware Covering Eigenoptions (RACE)**, utilizing DR's top eigenvector for intrinsic motivation, demonstrated qualitatively different exploratory behavior compared to SR-based Covering Eigenoptions (CEO). RACE exhibited a **risk-averse approach**, avoiding low-reward regions during exploration, which sometimes led to a slightly lower overall state visitation but resulted in **higher cumulative rewards**. When combined with Q-learning (RACE+Q), this reward-aware exploration led to **faster learning and superior final returns** compared to CEO+Q, as RACE's initial exploration already biased learning towards more rewarding, albeit potentially longer, paths.
- **Effective Count-Based Exploration:** The ℓ2 norm of the DR was successfully utilized as a density model for pseudo-count-based exploration, yielding **performance orders of magnitude better than random exploration**. Notably, in the Riverswim environment, Sarsa + DR statistically **outperformed Sarsa + SR**, suggesting that even in exploration, the DR's reward-awareness can be beneficial by guiding the agent to overcome initial aversion to negative rewards to find optimal paths.
- **Efficient Transfer Learning:** Default Features (DFs) proved **"extremely effective"** for transfer learning when only the rewards at terminal states changed. In this specific setting, DFs directly compute the optimal policy for new reward configurations, outperforming Successor Features (SFs), which typically compute a policy at least as good as the policies used to compute them. This demonstrates DFs' power in a constrained but common transfer scenario.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the DR successfully addresses the limitations of reward-agnostic proto-representations, providing a robust theoretical framework and empirical evidence for its utility in various challenging RL tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes a substantial contribution to the field of reinforcement learning by advancing the theoretical and practical understanding of reward-aware representations, specifically the Default Representation (DR). Its significance and potential impact can be viewed from several angles:

1. **Fundamental Advancement in Representation Learning:** The paper addresses a critical gap in representation learning for RL. While representations like the Successor Representation (SR) have been highly influential, their reward-agnostic nature limits their effectiveness in environments with complex reward landscapes. By providing a principled, reward-aware alternative, the DR enriches the foundational theory of RL representations, enabling agents to form a more complete and useful model of their environment. This moves the field closer to developing truly intelligent agents that can understand both "what leads to what" and "what is good or bad about what."

2. **Bridging Theory and Practice for DR:** By deriving dynamic programming and temporal-difference learning methods for the DR, the authors transform it from a theoretical concept (primarily in neuroscience) into a practical tool for computational RL practitioners. This broadens its accessibility and usability, paving the way for its adoption in a wider range of applications and research. The extension to state-action rewards and the introduction of Default Features further enhance its versatility for real-world scenarios and scalability with function approximation.

3. **Improved Performance in Key RL Challenges:** The empirical results clearly demonstrate that DR can lead to superior performance in several core RL challenges:
 - **Reward Shaping:** By providing a reward-aware shaping signal, DR can guide agents more effectively towards optimal, rather than merely shortest, paths, which is crucial for safety and efficiency in many applications.
 - **Option Discovery and Exploration:** RACE's ability to discover reward-aware options enhances exploration, allowing agents to avoid dangerous or low-reward regions, leading to more intelligent and safer exploration strategies. This has implications for complex, real-world control problems where poor exploration can lead to catastrophic outcomes.
 - **Transfer Learning:** Default Features offer a powerful mechanism for efficient policy transfer in changing reward conditions, particularly when terminal rewards are modified. This can significantly reduce retraining time and computational costs in dynamic environments.

4. **A Nuanced Understanding of Proto-Representations:** The theoretical analysis, especially Theorem 3.1 linking DR and SR eigenvectors, provides a deeper understanding of when and why different proto-representations are suitable. It clarifies that while SR excels at temporal geometry, DR adds the critical dimension of reward desirability. This informs future research in designing hybrid representations or adaptively choosing between them based on environmental characteristics. The paper positions DR as a "potential middle ground between reward-agnostic and risk-sensitive approaches," suggesting a new avenue for research into agent behavior under uncertainty and varying reward structures.

5. **Future Research Directions:** The work opens several promising avenues for future research:
 - **Scalability to Deep RL:** Adapting DR and DFs for use with deep neural networks, similar to how deep SR and SFs have been developed, is a natural next step to tackle high-dimensional, complex environments.
 - **Numerical Stability in Complex Settings:** Further research into robust numerical methods for DR computation in environments with extremely long horizons or widely varying rewards is necessary.
 - **Unified Frameworks:** Exploring how DR can be integrated with other representation learning techniques, or combined with risk-sensitive RL approaches, could lead to more robust and versatile agents.
 - **Broader Impact on AI Safety and Alignment:** Representations that inherently understand "good" and "bad" outcomes could contribute to developing more aligned and safe AI systems, as they might naturally avoid undesirable states.

In conclusion, this paper significantly advances the state of the art in reinforcement learning by formalizing and demonstrating the utility of reward-aware proto-representations. It provides both the theoretical groundwork and empirical evidence to establish the DR as a powerful tool, fostering more intelligent, efficient, and potentially safer decision-making in autonomous agents.
