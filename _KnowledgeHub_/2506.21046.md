# Boosting Generative Adversarial Transferability with Self-supervised Vision Transformer Features

> [!summary] Summary
> The Beijing Institute of Technology research presents dSVA, a generative adversarial attack leveraging self-supervised Vision Transformer (ViT) features for enhanced black-box transferability. This approach, which exploits ViT facet-level features, self-attention saliency maps, and jointly disrupts both global structural and local textural representations, achieves superior performance, outperforming state-of-the-art attacks by an average of 13.70% and robust defenses by 32.98% against diverse models.

## Problem

- Generating highly transferable adversarial examples for black-box attacks, where the adversary has no access to the target model's internal parameters.
- Existing black-box adversarial attacks predominantly relied on features from supervised Convolutional Neural Networks (ConvNets).
- The rich, semantically meaningful representations learned by self-supervised Vision Transformers (ViTs) remained largely unexplored for boosting adversarial transferability.

## Method

- Developed ==dSVA==, a generative adversarial attack that exploits specific ==facet-level features== within self-supervised Vision Transformers (ViTs).
- Utilized ==self-attention saliency maps== from ViTs as 'dense semantic guides' to focus adversarial perturbations on semantically important image regions.
- Jointly optimized the attack by disrupting complementary features from both ==Contrastive Learning (DINO)== and ==Masked Image Modeling (MAE)== self-supervised ViT models.

## Results

- The dSVA attack achieved state-of-the-art black-box transferability, outperforming existing generative and gradient-based attacks on ImageNet.
- dSVA (Joint) improved attack success rates by an average of **13.70%** over single-model variants and by **32.98%** against adversarially trained defense models.
- Facet-level exploitation (keys for DINO, queries for MAE) and self-attention saliency maps in the penultimate layer (l=10) were found to be most effective for maximizing transferability across diverse target architectures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Exploiting granular 'facet-level' features (queries and keys) within ViT attention blocks yields more generalizable adversarial perturbations than aggregated layer outputs.
> - Self-attention saliency maps from self-supervised ViTs provide effective semantic guidance for regularizing attacks, focusing on perceptually important regions.
> - Features learned by Contrastive Learning (global structure) and Masked Image Modeling (local texture) are complementary, and jointly disrupting both leads to superior adversarial transferability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research operates at the intersection of several critical and rapidly evolving fields: adversarial machine learning, deep neural networks (DNNs), Vision Transformers (ViTs), and self-supervised learning.

**Adversarial Machine Learning and Transferability:** The core problem addressed is the generation of transferable adversarial examples, particularly in black-box attack scenarios. Adversarial examples are inputs crafted to mislead a machine learning model, and "transferability" refers to an adversarial example generated for one model successfully fooling a different, unseen model. Black-box attacks, where the adversary has no access to the target model's internal structure or parameters, are highly practical and challenging. Prior work has shown that exploiting intermediate features of DNNs, rather than just output labels, can significantly boost transferability. This paper builds directly on this premise.

**Generative Adversarial Attacks:** The paper employs a generative approach for crafting adversarial perturbations, a strategy initially introduced to produce both sample-agnostic and sample-specific examples. Generative methods have proven effective for improving transferability, as seen in works like CDA for cross-domain attacks and BIA which generates cross-domain perturbations with limited knowledge. This paper positions its methodology within this successful generative paradigm.

**Evolution of Deep Learning Architectures (ConvNets to ViTs):** Historically, Convolutional Neural Networks (ConvNets) dominated computer vision. Adversarial attacks often focused on exploiting features learned by supervised ConvNets. However, the advent of Vision Transformers (ViTs) has fundamentally shifted the landscape. ViTs, drawing inspiration from natural language processing, have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in vision tasks, often outperforming ConvNets. This paper is one of the pioneering works to systematically explore the unique features of ViTs for adversarial attacks.

**Self-supervised Learning (SSL) and its Synergy with ViTs:** Self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for training DNNs without human-annotated labels, by creating pretext tasks from the data itself. While SSL techniques exist for ConvNets, their success has been particularly pronounced with ViTs. SSL-trained ViTs (e.g., DINO, MAE) are known to learn exceptionally rich, robust, and semantically meaningful representations that capture detailed positional and semantic relationships. This contrasts with supervised learning, which can reduce complex image semantics to a single category label. The paper identifies a significant gap: previous adversarial transferability work largely relied on supervised ConvNet features, leaving the rich representations of self-supervised ViTs largely unexplored for this purpose.

In summary, this work bridges the gap by being among the first to systematically investigate how the powerful, generalizable features distilled by self-supervised Vision Transformers can be leveraged to create highly transferable adversarial examples. It pushes the boundaries of black-box adversarial attack methodologies by moving beyond traditional supervised ConvNet features to embrace the sophisticated representations of modern, self-supervised ViT models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation behind this research stems from the exceptional synergy between self-supervised learning (SSL) and the Transformer architecture, which yields robust and semantically rich visual representations. The authors observe that while prior adversarial attack research primarily exploited features from supervised ConvNets, the unique capabilities of self-supervised Vision Transformers (ViTs) for capturing intricate image semantics remain largely untapped for boosting adversarial transferability.

The overarching objective is to answer the research question: **"How can we fully utilize the rich representations distilled by the harmonious coalition between self-supervision and the Transformer architecture, to boost adversarial transferability?"**

To address this, the authors delineate three specific, interlinked objectives:

1. **Facet-level Feature Exploitation:** Instead of treating the entire output of intermediate layers (tokens) as feature representations, the paper aims to exploit the internal components, or "facets," of ViT's multi-head self-attention (MSA) blocks. Specifically, they propose targeting the **queries, keys, and values** as these are more directly involved in capturing semantic context and are hypothesized to be less noisy and more generalizable than aggregated layer outputs. This aims to leverage the unique architectural design of Transformers more effectively for adversarial purposes.

2. **Self-attention Exploitation as Semantic Guides:** Recognizing that self-attention mechanisms in ViTs inherently capture salient foreground regions and common semantic grounds, the authors propose to systematically extract **saliency maps** from these mechanisms. These maps serve as "dense semantic guides" to identify and prioritize valuable feature targets during the adversarial optimization process, thereby regularizing the attack and improving generalizability.

3. **Joint Self-supervision Feature Discrimination:** The paper identifies two dominant paradigms in self-supervised learning for ViTs: Contrastive Learning (CL), exemplified by DINO, which captures global structural shapes and semantics; and Masked Image Modeling (MIM), exemplified by MAE, which focuses on local textural details. The key hypothesis here is that these two types of features are **complementary**. By jointly exploiting both global structural and local textural features, the attack can achieve superior adversarial generalizability by disrupting a more comprehensive set of image representations critical for DNNs.

Ultimately, the motivation is to develop a novel generative adversarial attack, termed **dSVA (generative dual self-supervised ViT features attack)**, that integrates these three objectives to craft highly transferable black-box adversarial examples. The goal is to create perturbations that are effective against a diverse range of target models, including ConvNets, ViTs, and MLPs, and capable of evading adversarial defenses, by precisely disrupting the rich and complementary features learned by self-supervised ViTs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, dSVA (generative dual self-supervised ViT features attack), is a generative approach designed to craft highly transferable black-box adversarial examples by exploiting specific features from self-supervised Vision Transformers.

#### 3.1. Threat Model

The research considers a standard ℓ∞ threat model, where an adversary aims to create a perturbed image x_adv = x + δ such that a target classifier F(·) misclassifies x_adv, and the perturbation δ is bounded by a small ℓ∞ norm (ε). The attack is black-box, meaning the adversary does not have access to the internal parameters of the target model, relying instead on a surrogate model (in this case, self-supervised ViTs) to generate transferable perturbations. The core idea is to train a generator Gθ to create x_adv such that the feature distance D(F(x), F(x_adv)) between the benign and adversarial examples' features, extracted by the surrogate ViTs, is maximized.

#### 3.2. Facet-level Feature Exploitation

Traditional feature-space attacks on ConvNets typically target the direct output of intermediate layers. For ViTs, this would mean using the aggregated token sequences. dSVA, however, proposes a more granular approach, exploiting **facet-level features** within the Multi-Head Self-Attention (MSA) blocks of ViTs. In a Transformer encoder block, tokens from the previous layer (T^(l-1)) are transformed into queries (q), keys (k), and values (v).
The authors argue that queries and keys, being more directly associated with the input token relationships, provide higher quality, less noisy features that favor generalizability compared to the aggregated output tokens (t) or values (v).
The discriminative loss at this stage aims to minimize the cosine similarity between the chosen facet features of the benign image x and the adversarial example x_adv:
L = arg min_θ D_cos(F^l(x), F^l(x_adv))
where F^l(·) extracts a specific facet (q, k, v, or t) at layer l of the ViT.

#### 3.3. Self-attention Exploitation

Building on the observation that self-supervised ViTs' attention heads strongly focus on salient foreground regions, dSVA incorporates self-attention saliency maps as "feature landmarks" to guide the adversarial optimization.
1. **Saliency Map Extraction:** For a benign sample x, the self-attention maps A^l at layer l are extracted. The attention weights from the [CLS] token to all other tokens across all heads (A^(l)[CLS]) are selected.
2. **Saliency Map Calculation:** The attention saliency map S^l is computed as the mean attention from the [CLS] token to all other tokens over each attention head.
3. **Regularization:** This saliency map S^l is then integrated into the loss function as a regularization term, providing a dense semantic guide. The feature distance is calculated on the features weighted by the saliency map, effectively focusing the perturbation on semantically important regions:
L = arg min_θ D_cos(F^l(x) ⊙ (γ · S^l), F^l(x_adv) ⊙ (γ · S^l))
where ⊙ denotes element-wise multiplication and γ is a scaling factor. This ensures the attack targets features within regions the ViT itself deems important.

#### 3.4. Joint Self-supervision Feature Discrimination

The methodology capitalizes on the complementary nature of features learned by the two major self-supervised learning (SSL) paradigms for ViTs:
- **Contrastive Learning (CL):** Represented by DINO, which captures global structural shapes and high-level semantics.
- **Masked Image Modeling (MIM):** Represented by MAE, which focuses on local textural details and fine-grained information through reconstruction tasks.

The hypothesis is that an attack that disrupts both types of features will be more generalizable. Therefore, dSVA jointly trains its generator Gθ against both DINO and MAE models. The final adversarial loss function (L_adv) is a weighted sum of the losses derived from each self-supervised ViT:
L_adv = λ · L_I + (1 − λ) · L_II
where L_I is the loss from DINO and L_II is the loss from MAE (both incorporating facet-level and self-attention exploitation), and λ is a balancing parameter (empirically set to 0.5).

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

- **Generator:** A ResNet-based generator (Gθ) is used, similar to prior generative attack works.
- **Surrogate Models:** ViT-B/16 architectures are used for DINO and MAE, pre-trained on ImageNet.
- **Training:** The generator is trained for a single epoch on the ImageNet training set using the Adam optimizer.
- **Parameter Choices:** Based on empirical analysis, the penultimate layer (l=10) is chosen for feature extraction. The 'key' facet is selected for DINO, and the 'query' facet for MAE. The attention saliency map scaling factor γ is 100.
- **Perturbation Budget:** ε = 10 for ImageNet evaluations, and ε = 16 for additional defense evasion evaluations (Appendix C).

By combining these innovative strategies, dSVA aims to systematically exploit the distinct and complementary feature representations learned by self-supervised ViTs, thereby generating highly potent and transferable adversarial examples.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by Wu et al. demonstrate the superior performance of dSVA in generating highly transferable black-box adversarial examples, particularly when exploiting features from self-supervised Vision Transformers.

#### 4.1. Superior Black-box Transferability

- **ImageNet Domain:** dSVA consistently outperforms state-of-the-art generative (CDA, BIA) and gradient-based (MI, PNA, TGR, ATT) attacks on ImageNet.
- **Architectural Diversity:** dSVA (Joint) shows exceptional transferability to a wide range of target models, including various ConvNets (VGG-16, ResNet-50, DenseNet-121, EfficientNet-B0, Inception-v3/v4), ViTs (Swin-B, MaxViT, PiT-B, VisFormer, LeViT), and even an MLP-Mixer.
- **Joint Exploitation Benefit:** dSVA (Joint) – using both DINO (CL) and MAE (MIM) – outperforms its single-model variants (dSVA (DINO) and dSVA (MAE)) by an average of 13.70%, confirming the complementary nature of global structural and local textural features.

#### 4.2. Enhanced Transferability to Defense Models

- dSVA demonstrates superior evasion capabilities against adversarially trained models (e.g., Inc-v3_adv, IncRes-v2_adv, EfficientNet-B0_ap).
- dSVA (Joint) surpasses all compared attacks by an average margin of 32.98% against these robust models. This suggests that by attacking fundamental, generalized features learned by self-supervised ViTs, dSVA can bypass even robust features developed through adversarial training.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Relevant Parameters

- **Facet Choice (Query, Key, Value, Token):** Facet-level exploitation significantly outperforms using the entire layer output (token). For DINO, the 'key' facet performs best, aligning with DINO's contrastive mechanism where the teacher provides strong key signals. For MAE, the 'query' facet (representing the input with masked patches) is most effective, consistent with its reconstruction task.
- **Layer Choice (l):** Transferability generally increases with layer depth, peaking at the penultimate layer (l=10). Deeper layers encode richer semantic information. The performance drops at the very last layer (l=11), as it tends to be more specialized for the training task and less generalizable.
- **Joint Training Parameter (λ):** The optimal balance for λ is found at 0.5, meaning equal weighting between DINO and MAE features. This substantiates the hypothesis that both global structural (CL) and local textural (MIM) features are crucial and complementary for maximizing adversarial effectiveness. The analysis also suggests that textural details from MIM might be slightly more impactful for fooling DNNs, which are often sensitive to fine-grained patterns.

#### 4.4. Feature Visualization

- PCA visualizations confirm that self-supervised ViT features (DINO, MAE) are richer and less noisy compared to supervised ViT features.
- The visualizations also illustrate how dSVA's perturbations effectively disrupt meaningful semantics within images, underscoring the attack's efficacy in altering critical model decision factors at the feature level.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study

- **Self-supervision Impact:** Attacks exploiting self-supervised features (DINO, MAE variants) consistently outperform those relying on supervised ViT features, even for single-model variants. This strongly validates the core premise that the rich representations from SSL are superior for adversarial transferability.
- **Self-attention Exploitation Impact:** Self-attention saliency map regularization generally boosts transferability for self-supervised ViTs, especially against stronger models. Conversely, it impairs adversarial effectiveness when applied to supervised ViTs, highlighting the difference in saliency map quality between SSL and supervised models.

#### 4.6. Cross-domain Transferability (Appendix B)

- dSVA (Joint) demonstrates strong cross-domain transferability to both coarse-grained (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SVHN, STL-10) and fine-grained (CUB-200-2011, Stanford Cars, FGVC Aircraft) classification domains.
- It often outperforms baselines, with an average boost of approximately 6%, solidifying its robustness across diverse datasets and tasks.

In conclusion, the findings overwhelmingly support the hypothesis that carefully exploiting self-supervised Vision Transformer features, particularly by targeting specific facets, leveraging attention saliency, and jointly disrupting both structural and textural representations, leads to state-of-the-art black-box adversarial transferability and defense evasion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Boosting Generative Adversarial Transferability with Self-supervised Vision Transformer Features" carries significant implications for both adversarial machine learning and the broader field of AI security.

#### 5.1. Novelty and Methodological Advance

This paper makes a crucial methodological contribution by being the first to systematically investigate and leverage the distinctive features of self-supervised Vision Transformers for adversarial transferability. Previous works predominantly focused on supervised ConvNets. The introduction of **facet-level feature exploitation** (targeting queries, keys, values) moves beyond generic layer outputs, providing a more precise and architecture-aware approach to attacking Transformers. The innovative use of **self-attention saliency maps** as semantic guides offers a novel regularization technique, ensuring attacks focus on perceptually and semantically important regions. Finally, the **joint disruption of Contrastive Learning (CL) and Masked Image Modeling (MIM) features** recognizes and exploits the complementary nature of global structural and local textural representations, leading to more robust and generalizable attacks. These combined strategies represent a significant step forward in understanding how to effectively manipulate modern, advanced deep learning models.

#### 5.2. Breakthrough in Black-box Transferability

The demonstrated state-of-the-art black-box transferability against a diverse array of target models (ConvNets, ViTs, MLPs) and robust, adversarially-trained defenses is a major achievement. This significantly raises the bar for adversarial attacks, indicating that highly potent adversarial examples can be crafted without internal knowledge of complex target systems. The ability to fool models of various architectures and to bypass robust defenses suggests that dSVA exploits very fundamental and universal vulnerabilities in how DNNs process visual information.

#### 5.3. Implications for AI Robustness and Security

The findings highlight a critical security vulnerability in the deployment of self-supervised ViTs. While self-supervised learning is celebrated for its ability to produce robust and generalizable features, this work demonstrates that these very features can be effectively targeted for highly transferable adversarial attacks. This calls into question the perceived inherent robustness of SSL-trained models and necessitates a re-evaluation of current defense strategies. As ViTs and self-supervised learning become more prevalent in real-world applications, understanding and mitigating these vulnerabilities is paramount for building secure and trustworthy AI systems.

#### 5.4. Guidance for Future Research

The paper opens several promising avenues for future research:
- **Robustness of SSL:** It strongly encourages further exploration into the inherent robustness properties of self-supervised learning models and developing defenses specifically tailored to counter attacks exploiting SSL features.
- **Physical World Attacks:** While dSVA shows strong digital transferability, extending its capabilities to physical-world adversarial examples presents a complex yet vital direction.
- **Advanced SSL Models:** Investigating the use of more advanced self-supervised models like DINOv2 (trained on larger datasets) or ViTs with architectural enhancements (e.g., registers) could further enhance attack effectiveness.
- **Multi-layer Optimization:** Exploring the disruption of features across multiple layers, rather than just a single layer, could lead to even more potent attacks.
- **Understanding Feature Vulnerabilities:** This work deepens the understanding of which specific features (global vs. local, specific facets) are most critical and vulnerable in modern vision models.

#### 5.5. Ethical Considerations

The authors explicitly acknowledge the ethical implications of their work. While developing powerful adversarial attacks can expose vulnerabilities, it also serves as a crucial impetus for developing more robust and secure AI systems. This research contributes to the "arms race" in adversarial machine learning, providing valuable insights that can inform the design of next-generation defenses. The potential societal impact underscores the importance of responsible research in this domain.

In conclusion, dSVA represents a significant contribution to adversarial machine learning, pushing the boundaries of black-box transferability by intelligently exploiting the rich, complementary features of self-supervised Vision Transformers. Its findings are vital for advancing our understanding of AI vulnerabilities and for spurring the development of more resilient AI technologies.
