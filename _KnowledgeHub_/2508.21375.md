# Dynamics-Compliant Trajectory Diffusion for Super-Nominal Payload Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Colorado Boulder developed a denoising diffusion model to generate dynamics-compliant trajectories, allowing articulated robots to manipulate payloads up to three times their nominal capacity. The system achieves rapid trajectory generation in approximately 10 milliseconds, making it suitable for real-time applications and expanding robot operational limits through software optimization.

## Problem

- Robots are often underutilized due to conservative, worst-case nominal payload ratings, leading to hardware over-provisioning.
- Applications slightly exceeding nominal payload capacity frequently necessitate procuring larger, more expensive robots.
- Traditional motion planning methods struggle with trade-offs in complexity, computational efficiency, and comprehensive constraint handling, especially for high-dimensional and dynamic constraints.

## Method

- A ==1D U-Net denoising diffusion model== was employed to generate dynamics-compliant, joint-space trajectories conditioned on payload mass.
- The model was trained on a large dataset of time-parameterized, collision-free trajectories rigorously validated for dynamic feasibility, with each trajectory's maximum supported payload mass explicitly determined.
- Payload mass information was integrated as a ==global conditioning vector== (specifically, ==one-hot encoding==) within the diffusion model's noise prediction network, allowing implicit learning of payload-dependent dynamics.

## Results

- The DDIM (One-Hot) method consistently generated dynamically feasible trajectories in approximately **0.01 seconds (10ms)** across all payload conditions, demonstrating constant-time generation far superior to other baselines.
- The system achieved high success rates, enabling a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda robot to manipulate payloads up to **9kg (3x** its nominal 3kg capacity) while retaining **67.6%** of its nominal workspace.
- DDIM (One-Hot) outperformed kinodynamic RRT, plan-and-filter methods (VAMP, cuRobo), and trajectory optimization (SLSQP) in terms of both planning speed and success rate, particularly for super-nominal payloads.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning-based generative models, such as denoising diffusion models, can implicitly capture complex kinematic and dynamic constraints, including payload-induced torques, without requiring explicit runtime checking.
> - By encoding payload information as a global condition during training, the diffusion model learns to generate dynamically feasible trajectories across a continuous range of payload masses.
> - Software-based trajectory optimization allows articulated robots to safely operate with payloads significantly exceeding their conservative nominal ratings, expanding their functional capabilities.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The operational capabilities of articulated robots, particularly regarding payload capacity, are typically defined by conservative nominal ratings. These ratings are often derived from worst-case scenarios, leading to uniform payload constraints across an entire workspace and frequently resulting in the underutilization of a robot's inherent capabilities. This research addresses the disparity between conservative manufacturer specifications and a robot's actual, configuration-dependent dynamic potential.

Existing approaches to robot motion planning that incorporate dynamic constraints include geometric planners with dynamics post-processing, kinodynamic planning, optimization-based methods, and control-based frameworks. However, these methods often present trade-offs concerning design complexity, constraint handling, and computational efficiency. Geometric planners decouple kinematic and dynamic reasoning, requiring post-processing that can invalidate a path. Kinodynamic planners directly incorporate dynamics but struggle with the "curse of dimensionality" in high-dimensional state spaces, leading to high planning times and variability. Optimization-based methods can accommodate dynamics but often require good initialization and can be computationally slow, especially for complex, nonlinear constraints.

Learning-based approaches, such as autoregressive models and more recently, denoising diffusion models, have emerged as promising avenues for efficient trajectory generation in high-dimensional, constrained spaces. Diffusion models, in particular, offer the ability to learn complex, multimodal distributions and handle high-dimensional data, making them suitable for capturing the intricacies of robot dynamics. Prior diffusion-based methods in robotics have primarily focused on position-controlled systems, leaving the integration of both kinematic and dynamic constraints in a unified planning framework largely unaddressed. This work positions itself by extending diffusion models to directly learn and generate dynamically feasible trajectories that explicitly account for joint angles, velocities, accelerations, and payload-induced torques, thereby moving beyond position-only denoising. It aims to offer a computationally efficient and generalizable solution to expand the operational envelope of robots beyond their nominal payload limits through software-based optimization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel trajectory generation approach that enables articulated robots to safely manipulate payloads exceeding their nominal capacity across broad regions of their workspace. This objective is driven by a critical motivation: the significant underutilization of robotic hardware due to conservative, worst-case nominal payload ratings.

The authors identify that standard industrial practice forces system integrators and end-users to select robots based on conservative nominal specifications, rather than on the robot's actual, configuration-dependent capabilities. This leads to several issues:
- **Hardware Over-provisioning:** Applications requiring a payload capacity slightly above a robot's nominal rating often necessitate procuring a larger, more expensive robot, even if the existing robot could handle the task in specific parts of its workspace. This cascade effect results in larger actuators, power supplies, mounting structures, and facility requirements.
- **Reduced Operational Envelope:** The conservative approach constrains robot operation to a fraction of its true physical capabilities, limiting the types of tasks it can perform or the efficiency with which it can perform them.
- **Computational Inefficiency of Traditional Methods:** Existing planning methods, while theoretically capable of handling dynamic constraints, face practical limitations. Sampling-based methods can be inefficient, optimization-based methods are often too slow for real-time applications, and kinodynamic planners struggle with the high dimensionality of combined state spaces (position, velocity, acceleration). These limitations become particularly acute when operating near system limits where the feasible solution space is highly constrained.

To address these challenges, the research proposes a method to:
- **Explicitly Incorporate Payload Constraints:** Design a trajectory generation system that inherently accounts for payload characteristics and their impact on joint torques from the outset of the planning process.
- **Generate Dynamically Feasible Trajectories in Constant Time:** Overcome the computational bottlenecks of traditional methods by developing a system that can produce executable trajectories, including joint angles, velocities, and accelerations, rapidly and reliably without post-processing.
- **Expand Safe Operational Limits:** Demonstrate that robots can safely handle payloads significantly above nominal capacity, thereby expanding their accessible workspace and functional capabilities through software-based optimization rather than hardware upgrades.
- **Leverage Learning-Based Models:** Utilize denoising diffusion models, known for their ability to model high-dimensional distributions and generate diverse samples, to learn complex relationships between robot states, payloads, and dynamic constraints.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to facilitate a more nuanced consideration of payload dynamics in motion planning, ultimately enabling more efficient and capable robotic deployments without necessitating additional hardware investment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on using denoising diffusion models to generate dynamics-compliant, joint-space trajectories conditioned on payload mass.

#### 3.1. Diffusion Model Architecture

The proposed system employs a denoising diffusion model, specifically a 1D U-Net architecture, to generate trajectories. A joint space trajectory $\pi = [X_0, X_1, \ldots, X_{t-1}]^T$ is defined as a sequence of complete robot states, where $X_i = (q_i, \dot{q}_i, \ddot{q}_i) \in R^{3n}$ represents joint angles, velocities, and accelerations for an $n$-DoF robot at timestep $i$.
The model learns to approximate the conditional distribution $p(\pi | P)$, where $P$ is a representation of the target payload. During training, noise is gradually added to ground-truth trajectories (forward process), and a noise prediction network $\epsilon_\theta$ learns to reverse this corruption. The network is trained by minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted noise and the actual noise added to the trajectory.
During inference, the model starts from a pure noise trajectory and iteratively denoises it over $K$ steps to generate a dynamically feasible trajectory $\pi_0$.

#### 3.2. Constraint Handling

- **Start and Goal States:** Enforced via inpainting, where the initial and final waypoints of the trajectory are set to the specified start and goal states (with zero velocities and accelerations).
- **Collisions:** Avoided during inference through gradient guidance, where a collision cost function $J$ is used to steer the denoising process away from obstacles.
- **Joint Limits:** Enforced by clamping joint angles, velocities, and accelerations during both training and inference.
- **Dynamics and Payload Constraints:** These are implicitly modeled. The payload information is integrated as a global conditioning mechanism that influences the entire trajectory generation process.

#### 3.3. Training Data Generation

A critical component of the methodology is the creation of a high-quality training dataset of 25,000 time-parameterized, collision-free joint space trajectories for tabletop pick-and-place motions using a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda robot. This dataset is generated using a "plan-and-filter" pipeline:
1. **Geometric Planning and Trajectory Optimization:** Initial trajectories are generated for a 0kg payload using cuRobo, a GPU-accelerated trajectory optimization framework.
2. **Dynamics Validation:** Each trajectory is then rigorously verified for dynamic feasibility using an analytically-derived robot dynamics model, parameterized on real-world data. This model captures joint inertia $M(q)$, Coriolis/centrifugal effects $C(q, \dot{q})$, gravity compensation $g(q)$, and joint friction $f(\dot{q})$.
 The dynamics equation used is:
 $\tau = M(q)\ddot{q} + C(q, \dot{q})\dot{q} + g(q) + f(\dot{q}) + J^{-1}(q)F_{ext}$
 where $\tau$ is the joint torque vector and $F_{ext}$ is the external task space force.
3. **Payload-Induced Torques:** To account for external payloads, the gravitational effects of a simplified point mass payload $m$ are modeled as a 6D Cartesian wrench $F_g = mg \cdot [0, 0, -1, 0, 0, 0]^T$ applied at the end-effector. This wrench is transformed to joint torques via the manipulator Jacobian $J(q)$ and added to the total joint torque calculation.
4. **Maximum Supported Payload:** For each generated trajectory $\pi_i$, the maximum supported payload mass $m_i$ is determined such that the absolute value of all joint torques $|\tau(\pi_i, m_i)|$ remains below the robot's hardware-specified maximum torque limits $\tau_{max}$ throughout the entire trajectory.
The training dataset $D$ thus consists of tuples $(\pi_i, m_i)$, providing trajectories and their corresponding maximum supported payload masses.

#### 3.4. Payload-Conditional Trajectory Generation

To integrate payload information into the diffusion model, the payload mass $p_i$ (a random supported mass $p_i \sim U(0, m_i)$ from the training trajectory's maximum supported mass $m_i$) is encoded into a vector $P_i$. This payload encoding $P$ is then concatenated with a diffusion timestep embedding to form a global conditioning vector. This vector is integrated into the noise prediction network $\epsilon_\theta$ using either additive or FiLM (Feature-wise Linear Modulation) conditioning.

Four encoding strategies for $P$ are investigated:
1. **Numeric Input:** The continuous scalar value of the payload mass $p_i$ is directly fed to the network.
2. **One-Hot Encoding:** For a discretized payload range (e.g., 0-18kg in 1kg increments), a random supported mass $p_i$ is encoded as a binary vector with a single '1' at the index corresponding to $\lceil p_i \rceil$.
3. **Less-Than Encoding:** A binary vector where entries are '1' for indices $j \le \lceil p_i \rceil$ and '0' otherwise, directly encoding the logical constraint that a trajectory supporting $p_i$ also supports all lighter payloads.
4. **Supported-Range Encoding:** During training, this encoding uses a binary vector with '1's for indices $j \le \lceil m_i \rceil$ (the maximum supported mass for the trajectory). During inference, it can be interpreted as either a one-hot or less-than encoding for the target payload $p$.

These payload encodings are crucial for the diffusion model to implicitly learn the complex relationships between joint states and payload-dependent dynamics, enabling the generation of feasible trajectories without explicit runtime constraint checking.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of the proposed method focused on pick-and-place motions using a 7-DoF Franka Emika Panda robot. Payloads were rigidly attached and had a geometrically centered, uniform mass distribution. Invalid trajectories were not executed, and robot safety stops were triggered for unmodeled dynamics.

#### 4.1. Comparison of Payload Encodings

An evaluation of the four payload encoding schemes (Numeric, One-Hot, Less-Than, Supported-Range), combined with FiLM conditioning, was performed to assess planning success rates.
- **One-Hot and Numeric:** These approaches demonstrated comparable effectiveness, particularly for payloads within the robot's nominal capacity (0-3kg).
- **Underestimation at Higher Payloads:** All methods showed a tendency to underestimate success rates for heavier payloads, which the authors attribute to potential data imbalance in the training set (likely fewer trajectories supporting very high payloads).
- **Supported-Range Encoding:** When interpreted as a "less-than" encoding during inference, it achieved competitive performance. However, its "one-hot" interpretation resulted in significantly lower success rates, suggesting the network struggled to fully utilize the richer payload-trajectory information encoded.
- **Limitations of Other Encodings:** The Numeric, Supported One-Hot, and Supported Less-Than encodings did not achieve 100% success rates even in the nominal operating regime (0-3kg), indicating fundamental limitations in how these schemes enabled the diffusion model to fully satisfy kinematic and dynamic constraints.
- **Chosen Encoding:** The one-hot encoding scheme was selected for subsequent experiments due to its superior performance in terms of success rate.

#### 4.2. Comparison across Planner Types

The DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) variant, conditioned with one-hot encoding (DDIM (One-Hot)), was compared against several baseline trajectory planning methods. The primary evaluation metrics were mean planning time to first solution and success rate over 500 tabletop manipulation tasks, across payloads of 3kg, 6kg, and 9kg (nominal capacity for Panda is 3kg, so 6kg and 9kg are super-nominal at 2x and 3x capacity, respectively).

- **DDIM (One-Hot) Performance:**
 - **Planning Time:** DDIM (One-Hot) generated dynamically feasible trajectories in approximately 0.01 seconds (10ms) consistently across all payload conditions, demonstrating constant-time generation. This was significantly faster than all other baseline methods.
 - **Success Rate:** It achieved high success rates across all payload conditions. For instance, at 9kg (3x nominal), 67.6% of the workspace remained accessible.
 - **Comparison to DDPM:** DDIM achieved identical success rates to DDPM (Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models) but required significantly less planning time (5 denoising steps vs. 25), validating its suitability for real-time applications.

- **Plan-and-Filter Methods (VAMP with RRTConnect, cuRobo + Validation):**
 - **Process:** These methods first plan a geometric path, then time-parameterize it, and finally validate dynamics, iterating until a feasible trajectory is found.
 - **Performance:** They exhibited inferior performance compared to DDIM (One-Hot). VAMP variants, even with deterministic sampling, showed higher standard deviations in planning times and lower overall success rates, particularly with heavier payloads. The decoupling of kinematic and dynamic reasoning, along with separate time parameterization, contributed to these limitations.
 - **cuRobo:** While effective, cuRobo with validation often required multiple attempts. DDIM (One-Hot) leveraged successful trajectories from imperfect planners like cuRobo during training to achieve higher success rates, especially in super-nominal conditions.

- **Kinodynamic Motion Planning (Steering-Based Kinodynamic RRT):**
 - **Process:** This method plans directly in joint angle, velocity, and acceleration space.
 - **Performance:** It suffered from extremely high planning times (over 1000x slower than DDIM) due to the "curse of dimensionality" in the high-dimensional state space. It also showed high standard deviations and a clustering of success rates across payloads, indicating insufficient exploration and inherent planning bias. This approach also required extensive, difficult tuning of hyperparameters.

- **Trajectory Optimization with Torque Constraints (SLSQP):**
 - **Process:** Implemented as Sequential Least SQuares Programming (SLSQP) to minimize jerk, incorporating linearized dynamic payload and collision avoidance constraints.
 - **Performance:** This method exhibited significantly higher planning times compared to DDIM. The success rate disparity was particularly pronounced with higher payloads as the optimization landscape became more constrained. The authors note that their basic SLSQP implementation did not include sophisticated warm-starting or acceleration techniques, acknowledging that more advanced optimization methods could achieve better performance, but highlighting the computational challenge of direct optimization in high-dimensional, non-linear dynamic systems.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Motions

Qualitative examples showed the robot successfully carrying super-nominal payloads of 5.4kg (1.8x nominal capacity) and 6.8kg (2.3x nominal capacity). Visualizations of joint torques indicated that joints 1, 3, and 5 exhibited higher torques, with joint 1 bearing the structural load of the arm and joints 3 and 5 being critical for positioning and orienting the payload. These visualizations confirmed that the generated trajectories stayed within joint torque limits.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work demonstrates that articulated manipulators possess capabilities significantly beyond their nominal payload ratings, challenging the conventional industrial approach of conservative hardware over-provisioning. The key significance lies in the development of a diffusion-based trajectory generation method that enables "super-nominal payload manipulation" through software optimization rather than expensive hardware upgrades.

The potential impacts are substantial:
- **Enhanced Robot Utilization:** The research shows that up to 67.6% of the nominal workspace can remain accessible even with payloads exceeding three times the robot's nominal capacity. This expands the practical operational envelope of existing robotic systems, allowing them to perform tasks previously considered beyond their reach without physical modifications.
- **Economic Benefits:** By allowing robots to handle heavier payloads, industries can potentially avoid investing in larger, more expensive robots for certain applications, leading to cost savings in procurement, power consumption, and facility infrastructure.
- **Computational Efficiency for Real-Time Applications:** The proposed method generates dynamically feasible trajectories in constant time (approximately 10ms). This real-time capability is crucial for industrial automation and dynamic environments where planning speed directly impacts throughput and responsiveness. Unlike traditional methods, it avoids explicit constraint checking at runtime, simplifying deployment.
- **Generalizability of Learning-Based Planning:** By training on a diverse dataset derived from successful (though sometimes imperfect) planning methods and validated against a comprehensive dynamics model, the diffusion model learns generalizable motion policies. This capacity to distill effective solutions across varying payload conditions improves robustness and adaptability.
- **Foundation for Advanced Manipulation:** The ability to explicitly learn and generate trajectories in joint angle, velocity, and acceleration space, conditioned on dynamic properties like payload mass, opens avenues for more complex manipulation tasks. This could include handling objects with internal dynamics (e.g., liquids, articulated parts) or incorporating whole-body contact forces and advanced gripper dynamics, moving beyond rigid-body assumptions.

**Limitations Acknowledged by Authors:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that warrant further research:
- **Rigid Attachment Assumption:** The current experimental setup assumes rigid payload attachment with a centered mass. Real-world scenarios involving flexible materials, non-rigid grasps, or significant center-of-mass offsets can introduce unmodeled dynamics (e.g., payload oscillations, rotational inertia, slippage), potentially leading to tracking errors or failures.
- **Test-Time Robustness and Long-Term Operation:** The long-term effects of operating robots consistently at or near maximum capacity on wear-and-tear and component lifespan require further study, ideally with OEM data. While trajectories respect torque limits, increased joint temperatures under high loads indicate the need for evaluating safety margins under sustained operation.
- **Embodiment Generalization:** The current approach is tailored to a specific robot embodiment. Further work is needed to directly incorporate embodiment understanding into the training process to enable generalization across different robotic platforms.
- **Theoretical Guarantees:** As a learning-based method, formal completeness and optimality guarantees remain open challenges, although the model is trained on optimal trajectories. Traditional optimization methods currently offer clearer constraint satisfaction guarantees and flexibility for new constraints without retraining.
- **Benchmarking and Data Selection:** Rigorous comparisons across different hardware and software implementations (CPU vs. GPU, Python vs. C++) for baseline methods could provide more direct insights. Additionally, optimal training data composition, distinguishing data imbalance from model generalization, and leveraging continual learning capabilities are areas for future exploration.

Despite these limitations, the work represents a significant step towards enabling robots to operate closer to their true physical limits, fostering greater efficiency and capability in industrial and research applications through dynamics-informed generative models.
