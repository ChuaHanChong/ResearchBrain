# Towards Torque-Driven Reinforcement Learning for Quadruped Locomotion

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Louisville developed a torque-driven reinforcement learning framework for the heavyweight Unitree B1 quadruped robot. The policy demonstrated stable locomotion and accurate tracking of desired linear speeds up to 3.5 m/s and angular speeds up to 1.5 rad/s in simulation, while exhibiting robustness against strong external disturbances and rough terrain without explicit linear velocity observations.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning frameworks for quadruped locomotion often rely on position-based control, limiting adaptability and requiring external state estimation, particularly for linear velocity.
- Most RL research has focused on smaller, lightweight quadrupeds (e.g., Unitree GO1/A1), which are not suitable for real-world tasks requiring heavy payloads or traversal of extremely rugged terrain.
- Heavyweight, high-torque quadrupeds (e.g., Unitree B1) introduce significantly more complex dynamics, including higher inertia and larger contact forces, posing challenges for traditional learning-based control.

## Method

- A **torque-driven whole-body reinforcement learning** control policy was developed, leveraging NVIDIA's ==Isaac Sim== for physics simulation and ==Isaac Lab== for the RL workflow, specifically tailored for the Unitree B1 quadruped.
- The policy's ==action space== consists of 12 target joint torques, scaled to facilitate stable exploration, while the ==observation space== explicitly omits linear velocity, relying on other cues like linear acceleration, angular velocity, and joint states.
- A comprehensive ==reward function== was designed to encourage stable periodic gait, accurate velocity tracking, and robustness, complemented by extensive ==domain randomization== and disturbance injection (e.g., random pushing events) during training.

## Results

- The trained policy enabled the Unitree B1 to achieve linear speeds up to **3.5 m/s** and angular speeds up to **1.5 rad/s** in simulation, demonstrating close tracking of randomly sampled desired velocities.
- The robot successfully resisted and recovered from significant external disturbances, such as instantaneous **±5 m/s** velocity pushes, and traversed rough terrain, including stairs, without exteroceptive sensors.
- Visual inspection revealed a behavioral artifact of extended leg swing duration at low velocities, which was attributed to potential overfitting between the 'feet airtime' reward and 'airtime variance' penalty.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Torque-driven control policies can provide enhanced dynamic stability and adaptability for high-inertia quadrupeds, surpassing the capabilities of many position-based control methods.
> - Robust locomotion and accurate linear velocity tracking can be achieved without direct linear velocity input to the policy, indicating an implicit learning of necessary state relationships from other observational cues.
> - A relatively simple multilayer perceptron network architecture, when combined with careful reward and observation shaping, is sufficient for robust locomotion in complex, heavyweight quadruped robots, promoting modularity and adaptability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Quadrupedal locomotion is a significant area within robotics research, recognized for its potential in traversing complex and challenging terrains, offering advantages over wheeled robots. This capability is particularly relevant for applications such as search and rescue, construction, and agriculture, where navigating rugged environments and carrying payloads are fundamental requirements.

Traditionally, quadruped locomotion relies on hybrid model control strategies, which combine high-level controllers for gait generation and stability maintenance (e.g., using Zero-Moment Point (ZMP) and Model Predictive Control (MPC)) with low-level actuator control. While these classical methods are effective for basic locomotion, they often encounter limitations when confronted with high-dimensional, nonlinear dynamics and rugged terrain due to difficulties in handling complex environments.

In recent years, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as an alternative approach for developing locomotion controllers for legged robots. RL-based methods, particularly when trained in simulation, have demonstrated robustness and adaptability, frequently exhibiting capabilities that exceed those of traditional MPC for certain high-level tasks. A common challenge in simulation-trained policies is the "sim-to-real gap," where policies developed in simulated environments may not transfer effectively to physical robots due to unmodeled environmental factors. This gap is often mitigated through techniques such as domain randomization, noise injection, and system disturbances during training.

Much of the existing RL research in quadruped locomotion has concentrated on smaller, lightweight robots, such as Unitree's GO1/A1 or MIT's Mini-Cheetah. These smaller platforms offer benefits in terms of cost, compact size, and reduced state-space complexity, which simplifies the application of learning-based control. However, their utility is limited in tasks requiring heavy payloads or traversal of extremely adverse terrain. In contrast, larger quadrupeds, such as the Unitree B-series or Boston Dynamics' Spot, possess high-torque actuators and robust frames, making them more suitable for demanding real-world applications. The increased size and weight of these robots introduce higher inertia, greater contact forces, and complex system dynamics, which pose significant challenges for model training and policy design in learning-based control.

Within RL for quadruped locomotion, policies have typically utilized an action space consisting of joint positions. However, more recent advancements have shown improved locomotion quality and enhanced dynamic stability and adaptability when using joint torques as the action space, allowing for direct actuator interaction and modeling of actuator dynamics during training.

This work specifically addresses the challenges associated with developing RL-based locomotion for larger, high-inertia quadrupeds, like the Unitree B1, focusing on torque-driven control. It distinguishes itself by aiming to achieve robust locomotion in adverse terrain while minimizing network architecture complexity, in contrast to some research that explores more complex network architectures for larger robots. By using a torque-based control policy and carefully shaping rewards and observations, the authors seek to provide a robust solution that is also modular and adaptable within existing RL simulation frameworks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, torque-driven reinforcement learning control framework specifically tailored for heavyweight, high-torque quadruped robots. This objective is motivated by several identified limitations and challenges within the current landscape of legged robot locomotion research:

1. **Addressing Limitations of Position-Based RL Frameworks:** Traditional RL locomotion frameworks frequently rely on position-based control, which can result in policies that exhibit less adaptability to diverse terrain types. Furthermore, these frameworks often necessitate external state estimation techniques, such as linear velocity estimation, to provide complete observation space information to the policy. A key motivation for this work is to eliminate the dependence on such state estimation, aiming for a policy that can infer necessary state relationships internally.

2. **Bridging the Gap for Heavyweight Quadrupeds:** The majority of existing RL locomotion research focuses on smaller, lightweight quadrupeds (e.g., Unitree GO1/A1, MIT Mini-Cheetah). While these robots are valuable for initial research due to their simplicity and cost-effectiveness, their hardware constraints limit their viability for high-complexity tasks such as carrying significant payloads or navigating extremely rugged terrain. Larger quadrupeds (e.g., Unitree B1, Boston Dynamics Spot) are better suited for these real-world applications but introduce considerably greater system complexities, including higher inertia, larger contact forces, and more intricate nonlinear dynamics. This work is motivated by the need to develop RL solutions that can effectively manage these challenges associated with larger, heavier robots.

3. **Enhancing Adaptability and Dynamic Stability through Torque Control:** Prior research has indicated that torque-controlled RL frameworks can offer enhanced dynamic stability and adaptability compared to their position-based counterparts, primarily by allowing direct interaction with actuator dynamics during training. This provides a strong motivation to implement a torque-driven control policy for a heavyweight quadruped.

4. **Minimizing Network Architecture Complexity:** The authors aim to achieve robust locomotion with a relatively simple network architecture. This approach contrasts with some efforts that pursue more complex actor-critic models or advanced optimization frameworks for larger quadrupeds. The motivation behind this choice is to demonstrate that effective locomotion can be achieved primarily through careful reward and observation shaping, thereby promoting modularity and ease of integration within existing simulation environments like Isaac Lab.

Based on these motivations, the specific contributions outlined by the authors are:
- Development of a torque-driven whole-body reinforcement learning control policy.
- Integration of the Unitree B1 quadruped into NVIDIA's Isaac Sim environment.
- A detailed discussion of the RL policy's architecture, observations, and reward design.
- Presentation of preliminary policy results and experimental findings obtained through simulation.

The overarching goal is to enable heavyweight, high-torque quadrupeds to traverse rough terrain and accurately track desired linear velocities without explicit knowledge of the robot's current linear velocity, thereby increasing their practical applicability in demanding industrial and rescue scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology for training the Unitree B1 quadruped involves a simulation-based reinforcement learning framework, leveraging specific tools and design choices for the agent's interaction with its environment.

**A. Simulation Environment**
- **Platform:** NVIDIA's Isaac Sim was utilized for environment and physics simulation, powered by the PhysX engine. Isaac Lab provided the reinforcement learning workflow.
- **Hardware:** An NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU was employed to run 4096 parallel environments concurrently, facilitating efficient policy training.
- **Domain Randomization:** To mitigate the sim-to-real gap and ensure policy robustness, various parameters were randomized during training. These included foot friction, center of mass, link mass, initial joint positions, and initial velocities, with variations ranging from ±5% to 20% per term. Body velocity, body position, and body orientation were also randomized within specified ranges.
- **Disturbances:** Terrain difficulty was progressively increased throughout training. Additionally, random "pushing events" were introduced every 10-15 seconds by instantaneously setting the robot's linear velocity to a random value in the range (-0.5, 0.5) m/s, serving as external disturbances.
- **Command Velocities:** The policy was trained to track desired command velocities sampled from specific ranges: (-2.0, 3.0) m/s for linear X-velocity, (-1.5, 1.5) m/s for linear Y-velocity, and (-2.0, 2.0) rad/s for angular Z-velocity.

**B. Problem Definition (Markov Decision Process)**
The interaction between the Unitree B1 agent and its environment is modeled as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), characterized by the tuple (S, A, P, R, γ).
- **S (State Space):** Represents the current state of the agent and its environment.
- **A (Action Space):** Denotes the set of possible actions the agent can take.
- **P (Transition Probability):** Describes the probability of transitioning from state S_t to S_(t+1) after taking action A_t.
- **R (Reward Function):** Quantifies the reward received by the agent for taking action A_t in state S_t.
- **γ (Discount Factor):** Determines the relative importance of immediate versus future rewards.
The agent's objective is to learn a policy π(A|S) that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted reward (return) over time.

**C. Action Space**
- **Structure:** The action space is a 12x1 vector, where each element corresponds to the target joint torque for one of the Unitree B1's 12 actuators.
- **Scaling:** Policy actions are multiplied by a constant scalar of 30.0. This scaling is implemented to facilitate exploration within the quadruped's larger actuator torque range (approximately ±140 Nm, in contrast to ±3.14 rad for position-control frameworks). By applying a lower scale factor, the agent is guided towards exploring smaller, more stable, and task-relevant torque values early in the training process, which contributes to initial stability and more efficient exploration.

**D. Observation Space**
The observation space is designed to provide the policy with a comprehensive understanding of the system dynamics, notably *omitting* linear velocity and incorporating specific additional states. It comprises nine terms:
- **Linear Acceleration (a_base ∈ R^3):** Scaled by 0.7 with 0.02 injected noise. Included to help the policy infer tipping or collision events.
- **Angular Velocity (ω_yaw ∈ R^3):** Scaled by 0.7 with 0.01 injected noise.
- **Orientation (O_base ∈ R^4):** Represented as a unit quaternion in the world frame (scaled by 0.7, 0.05 noise), replacing the projected gravity term often used in other RL frameworks.
- **User Command (u*_base ∈ R^3):** Represents the desired linear (X, Y) and angular (Z) velocities of the robot's base (scaled by 1, no noise).
- **Joint Positions (||θ_i - θ|| ∈ R^12):** Relative joint positions, where θ_i denotes a desired stance (scaled by 1.0, 0.05 noise).
- **Joint Velocity (˙θ ∈ R^12):** Scaled by 1 with 0.1 injected noise.
- **Previous Actuator Output Torque (τ_(t-1) ∈ R^12):** Scaled by 0.5 with 0.05 injected noise. Included with linear acceleration to enable the policy to learn actuator dynamics and more accurately capture system events.
- **Previous Action (A_(t-1) ∈ R^12):** Provided in its unscaled form (scaled by 1.0, no noise) to help the policy learn how its actions influence subsequent states.
- **Feet Contact (C_foot ∈ R^4):** A binary signal (scaled by 2.0, no noise) indicating foot collisions, serving as a proxy for ground reaction forces, which were not directly accessible in the simulation.

**E. Rewards**
The reward function is formulated to achieve three primary objectives: stable periodic gait, accurate tracking of desired command velocities, and maintaining an "alive" state (defined as avoiding contact with the robot's base, hips, and thighs).
- **Reward Terms:**
 - **Alive:** Positive reward (weight 0.75) for maintaining an upright, non-contact state.
 - **Feet Air Time:** Reward (weight 5.0) for optimal foot air time (deviation from 0.3 seconds).
 - **Linear Velocity Error:** Exponentially decaying reward (weight 7.0) based on the error between desired and current linear velocities in X and Y.
 - **Angular Velocity Error:** Exponentially decaying reward (weight 5.0) based on the error between desired and current angular Z-velocities.
 - **Gait:** Reward (weight 10.0) encouraging synchronized and asynchronous foot movements for a stable gait pattern.
- **Penalty Terms:** Penalties are applied for:
 - **Action Smoothness:** Penalizes large changes in consecutive actions (weight -0.6).
 - **Air Time Variance:** Penalizes inconsistencies in foot air times across different legs (weight -1.0).
 - **Base Motion:** Penalizes excessive roll and pitch (sum of |ω_x| + |ω_y|, weight -2.0).
 - **Base Orientation:** Penalizes deviation from desired upright orientation (weight -3.0).
 - **Foot Slippage:** Penalizes feet sliding while in contact (weight -0.5).
 - **Joint Position Deviation:** Penalizes deviations from desired joint positions (weight -0.7).
 - **Joint Torque, Acceleration, Velocity:** Small penalties for high torque (weight -0.0005), acceleration (weight -0.000025), and velocity (weight -0.01) to encourage efficient and smooth movements.
 - **Thigh/Calf Contact:** Penalties for contact with the robot's thighs (weight -1.0) and calves (weight -0.2).

**F. Network Architecture and Training**
- **Architecture:** A multilayer perceptron (MLP) serves as the policy's network architecture. It consists of three hidden layers with 512, 256, and 128 neurons, respectively, utilizing exponential linear units (ELUs) as activation functions.
- **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) was used for training.
- **Hyperparameters:** Key hyperparameters included an adaptive learning rate, an entropy coefficient of 0.0025, a target KL-Divergence of 0.01, a discount factor (γ) of 0.99, an advantage estimator discount factor (λ) of 0.95, and an initial learning rate of 0.001.
- **Training Duration:** The policy was trained over 20,000 iterations, which required approximately 16 hours.
- **Design Rationale:** The network architecture was kept intentionally simple, prioritizing reward and observation shaping to achieve robust locomotion. This design choice aims to maintain modularity and adaptability, allowing for easier integration and modification within Isaac Lab for various quadrupedal tasks without necessitating significant overhauls of the underlying RL framework.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The simulation results obtained from the trained torque-driven RL policy on the Unitree B1 quadruped demonstrate its capabilities in velocity tracking and stability, along with an observed behavioral artifact.

**A. Velocity Tracking Performance**
- **Achieved Speeds:** The policy successfully enabled the Unitree B1 to achieve linear speeds of 3.5 m/s and angular speeds of 1.5 rad/s in simulation.
- **Accuracy:** The policy exhibited close tracking of both randomly sampled desired linear (X and Y) and angular (Z) velocities.
- **Adaptability to Changes:** When large instantaneous changes occurred in the desired angular velocity, the policy demonstrated recovery, bringing the robot's angular velocity within ±0.2 rad/s of the new command within approximately 0.5 seconds.
- **Linear Velocity Inference:** The tracking performance, particularly without direct linear velocity in the observation space, indicates that the policy was able to capture the necessary state relationships to effectively estimate and control its linear velocity. After 30 seconds of combined linear and angular velocity commands, the policy maintained accurate linear velocity tracking even when the angular velocity command was set to zero.

**B. Robustness and Stability**
- **Disturbance Rejection and Recovery:** The policy demonstrated robustness by effectively resisting and recovering from significant external disturbances. This was tested by instantaneously setting the robot's base linear velocity to random values of ±5 m/s in both X and Y directions, simulating strong external pushes. The robot's base height remained consistent, indicating that the policy prevented the robot from falling and enabled it to regain stability after such events.
- **Rough Terrain Traversal:** Visual inspection during simulation confirmed the policy's ability to traverse rough terrain, including walking up and down stairs, without the aid of exteroceptive sensors (e.g., cameras or lidar). This highlights the policy's capacity to handle complex ground interactions through its internal state representations.

**C. Efficiency of Architecture**
- The results suggest that a robust locomotion policy for a complex, high-inertia quadruped can be achieved using a relatively simple multilayer perceptron architecture. This outcome is attributed by the authors to the deliberate design and shaping of the observation space and reward function, rather than relying on more complex network models or advanced optimization techniques.

**D. Identified Artifacts**
- **Overfitting Manifestation:** Visual inspection of the policy during simulation revealed certain behavioral artifacts, particularly at low angular and linear velocities. This manifested as an extended swing duration for each leg. The authors attributed this to potential overfitting, where the policy might be attempting to concurrently maximize the "feet airtime" reward while minimizing the "airtime variance" penalty during slower movements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of robotic locomotion by addressing several critical aspects, particularly concerning the application of reinforcement learning to larger, more capable quadruped robots.

**A. Advancing Locomotion for Heavyweight Quadrupeds**
The primary significance lies in demonstrating a viable torque-driven RL framework for heavyweight, high-torque quadrupeds, specifically the Unitree B1. Much existing RL research has focused on smaller, lighter robots; this work addresses the increased complexities (higher inertia, greater contact forces, more intricate dynamics) associated with larger platforms. This advancement is crucial because larger quadrupeds offer greater practical utility in real-world scenarios such as search and rescue, construction, and agriculture, where carrying payloads and traversing significantly challenging terrain are requirements.

**B. Efficacy of Torque-Driven Control**
By successfully implementing a torque-driven whole-body RL control policy, the research reinforces the growing evidence that torque control can provide enhanced dynamic stability and adaptability in legged locomotion compared to traditional position-based methods. Direct control over actuator torques allows for more nuanced interaction with the environment and potentially greater robustness against disturbances.

**C. Reduced Reliance on State Estimation**
A notable finding is the policy's ability to achieve accurate linear velocity tracking without explicitly including linear velocity in its observation space. This demonstrates that the policy can implicitly learn the necessary state relationships from other observational cues (e.g., linear acceleration, angular velocity, joint states). Eliminating the need for dedicated state estimation algorithms simplifies the control architecture, potentially reduces sensor requirements, and could increase robustness in real-world deployments where accurate velocity estimation might be challenging or prone to error.

**D. Promotion of Modular and Adaptable RL Frameworks**
The authors' decision to utilize a relatively simple network architecture, emphasizing careful reward and observation shaping, suggests that robust locomotion policies do not always necessitate highly complex deep learning models. This "plug and play" approach within simulation environments like Isaac Lab facilitates modularity and adaptability. It allows researchers to more easily modify and experiment with policy states for various quadrupedal tasks without overhauling the core RL framework, thereby accelerating future development and exploration of complex behaviors.

**E. Foundation for Sim-to-Real Transfer**
The extensive use of domain randomization and random disturbances during training is a foundational step toward addressing the sim-to-real gap. While this paper presents only simulation results, the methodology is designed to create a robust policy that is amenable to transfer onto physical robots. This lays the groundwork for future work involving real-robot experiments with the Unitree B1, which would be a critical validation of the framework's practical viability.

In conclusion, this work offers a validated approach for torque-driven reinforcement learning in high-inertia quadruped robots, addressing a critical gap in the field. Its success in simulation, particularly in velocity tracking and robustness without explicit linear velocity estimation, and its emphasis on architectural simplicity, provide a significant step towards deploying more capable and autonomous legged robots in demanding real-world applications.
