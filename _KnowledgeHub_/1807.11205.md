# Highly Scalable Deep Learning Training System with Mixed-Precision: Training ImageNet in Four Minutes

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Tencent Inc. and Hong Kong Baptist University developed a highly scalable deep learning training system that trained ResNet-50 on ImageNet to 76.2% top-1 accuracy in 8.7 minutes using 1024 Tesla P40 GPUs, and AlexNet to 58.7% top-1 accuracy in 4 minutes, both utilizing a 64K mini-batch size.

## Problem

- Maintaining model generalization when employing extremely large mini-batch sizes, as increased batch sizes often lead to a degradation in test accuracy.
- Achieving near-linear scalability on large GPU clusters due to communication overhead becoming a bottleneck during gradient aggregation, especially with hundreds or thousands of devices.
- Naively combining FP16 mixed-precision with the LARS optimizer risks vanishing gradients, since LARS-computed local learning rates can fall below FP16's representable dynamic range.

## Method

- Implemented ==mixed-precision training== with **LARS**, performing forward/backward propagation in FP16 while casting to FP32 for LARS computations and master weights to prevent gradient vanishing.
- Introduced architectural improvements, including eliminating weight decay on bias and batch normalization parameters, and adding a ==Batch Normalization== layer after AlexNet's Pool5 to stabilize training with large batches.
- Designed advanced communication optimizations such as ==tensor fusion==, a ==three-phase hierarchical all-reduce==, and a ==hybrid all-reduce== strategy to efficiently aggregate gradients across thousands of GPUs.

## Results

- ResNet-50 was trained on ImageNet to **76.2%** top-1 accuracy in **8.7 minutes** using **1024** Tesla P40 GPUs with a **64K** mini-batch size, and in **6.6 minutes** using **2048** GPUs.
- AlexNet was trained on ImageNet to **58.7%** top-1 accuracy in **4 minutes** using **1024** Tesla P40 GPUs with a **64K** mini-batch size, matching baseline accuracy.
- Achieved **99.2%** scaling efficiency for ResNet-50 on **1024** GPUs compared to 8 GPUs, and **91.4%** scaling efficiency for AlexNet by combining FP16, tensor fusion, and hybrid all-reduce.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Properly integrating mixed-precision training with optimizers like LARS requires careful management of data types (e.g., FP32 for LARS calculations) to maintain accuracy and prevent numerical issues like vanishing gradients.
> - Minor architectural modifications and careful regularization strategies (e.g., selective weight decay, additional BN layers) are crucial for stabilizing training and improving generalization when scaling to exceptionally large mini-batch sizes.
> - A combination of communication strategies, including fusing small tensors and adaptively switching between hierarchical and ring-based all-reduce algorithms, is essential for achieving near-linear scaling efficiency on large-scale GPU clusters.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of deep learning has seen a rapid increase in the size and complexity of neural networks and datasets. Consequently, training these models often requires extensive computational resources and prolonged training times, sometimes spanning days or weeks. This extended duration can impede the iterative process of research and development. To address this, distributed training systems employing synchronized Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with data parallelism have become a prevalent solution, allowing workloads to be distributed across clusters of computational devices.

A key strategy in distributed deep learning for improving system scalability and throughput is the use of larger mini-batch sizes. By processing more data samples per iteration, the communication-to-computation ratio can be reduced, as gradient aggregation occurs less frequently relative to the total computation. This approach, often referred to as "large-batch training," has been explored by several research groups. For instance, Goyal et al. (2017) demonstrated training ResNet-50 on ImageNet in one hour using an 8K mini-batch size across 256 GPUs, employing a linear scaling rule for the learning rate and a warmup scheme. You et al. (2017) further scaled ResNet-50 training to a 32K mini-batch with 2048 KNL chips, achieving a 20-minute training time using the LARS optimizer. Akiba et al. (2017) achieved 15-minute ResNet-50 training with a 32K mini-batch on 1024 Tesla P100 GPUs, though with a reported top-1 accuracy slightly below the baseline. Codreanu et al. (2017) also explored large batches with aggressive learning rate schedules, achieving competitive accuracies but with longer training times.

However, existing large-batch training methods face two primary challenges:
1. **Generalization Gap:** Increasing the mini-batch size beyond a certain point has been observed to lead to a degradation in the model's test accuracy, often referred to as the "generalization gap" (Keskar et al., 2016). While larger batches provide more accurate gradient estimates, they can lead the optimization towards flatter minima, which may not generalize as well as sharper minima found with smaller batches. Previous works often either accept some accuracy loss or require complex adjustments to maintain accuracy at very large batch sizes (e.g., 64K).
2. **Scalability Limitations:** Achieving near-linear scalability with a growing number of machines in large clusters remains difficult, particularly for models with a high communication-to-computation ratio. The gradient aggregation step, typically performed using collective communication primitives like all-reduce, can become a bottleneck as the number of GPUs increases, limiting the overall system throughput and efficiency. Standard all-reduce implementations, such as ring-based algorithms, can suffer from under-utilized bandwidth when handling numerous small tensors or scaling to thousands of nodes.

Another area of relevant research is **low-precision training**, which aims to reduce memory footprint and increase arithmetic throughput by using fewer bits (e.g., FP16) for computations. Micikevicius et al. (2017) proposed techniques like maintaining a single-precision master copy of weights, loss-scaling, and accumulating into single-precision outputs to mitigate accuracy loss in mixed-precision training. However, the integration of low-precision training with very large batch sizes and advanced optimizers like LARS presented a novel challenge.

Finally, **distributed communication optimization** is a critical component. Frameworks like TensorFlow and Baidu's Ring All-reduce (Gibiansky, 2017) have made strides, with NCCL (NVIDIA Collective Communication Library) becoming a standard for multi-GPU communication. IBM's PowerAI DDL (Cho et al., 2017) and Horovod (Sergeev and Del Balso, 2018) also introduced advanced all-reduce algorithms and gradient fusion strategies. However, further optimizations are needed for extreme scalability and efficiency in heterogeneous clusters with thousands of GPUs, especially when dealing with varying tensor sizes.

This work contributes to this landscape by developing a comprehensive system that integrates mixed-precision training, architectural improvements, and communication optimizations to address the generalization gap and scalability challenges simultaneously, enabling faster training of large CNNs on ImageNet without sacrificing accuracy, even at exceptionally large mini-batch sizes (up to 64K).

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is to significantly reduce the training time for large-scale deep neural networks, such as AlexNet and ResNet-50, on extensive datasets like ImageNet, while preserving or improving model accuracy. The authors identify two core challenges that hinder the achievement of this goal in existing distributed deep learning systems:

1. **Maintaining Model Generalization with Large Mini-batch Sizes:** The use of large mini-batch sizes is crucial for improving the scalability of distributed training systems by decreasing the communication-to-computation ratio. However, a well-documented issue is that increasing the mini-batch size often leads to a degradation in the model's generalization ability, resulting in lower test accuracy. For example, previous work reported a drop in ResNet-50 accuracy from 75.4% to 73.2% when the mini-batch size increased to 64K. The objective here is to enable the use of extremely large mini-batch sizes (up to 64K) without compromising the model's final test accuracy.
2. **Achieving Near-Linear Scalability on Large GPU Clusters:** As the number of GPUs in a distributed training system increases, particularly in clusters with hundreds or thousands of devices, it becomes progressively difficult to achieve near-linear scaling efficiency. The communication overhead associated with gradient aggregation after each training step often becomes the bottleneck, especially for models where communication cost is high relative to computation. The motivation is to overcome this communication bottleneck and improve both single-GPU performance (S) and system scaling efficiency (e) to maximize the overall system throughput (T = S * N * e), where N is the number of GPUs.

To address these challenges, the authors set out to develop a highly scalable deep learning training system based on three main contributions:
- **Proposing a mixed-precision training method:** This aims to significantly boost the training throughput of individual GPUs without any loss in accuracy. This addresses the 'S' component of throughput (single GPU performance).
- **Developing an optimization approach for extremely large mini-batch sizes (up to 64K):** This focuses on enabling training of CNN models on ImageNet at these large batch sizes while maintaining baseline accuracy, directly tackling the generalization gap problem.
- **Designing highly optimized all-reduce algorithms:** These algorithms are intended to enhance communication efficiency and system scaling, particularly on dense GPU clusters with up to 1024 Tesla P40 GPUs. This addresses the 'e' component of throughput (scaling efficiency) by reducing the communication bottleneck.

Ultimately, the overarching objective is to set new benchmarks for training speed on standard datasets and models, as evidenced by their goal to train ImageNet models like ResNet-50 and AlexNet in minutes rather than hours, while achieving competitive or superior accuracy.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research presents "Jizhi," a distributed deep learning training system designed to achieve high scalability and fast training times while maintaining model accuracy. The system comprises three main modules: an input pipeline, a training module, and a communication module. Several optimization strategies are integrated across these modules.

#### 3.1. Input Pipeline Module

This module utilizes pipelining to pre-fetch data for the next training step concurrently with the current step's execution. This minimizes CPU and GPU idle times, ensuring a continuous flow of data to the training module.

#### 3.2. Training Module Optimizations

The training module incorporates methods to enhance single-GPU performance and improve model convergence with large batches.

- **Mixed-Precision Training with LARS:**
 - **Motivation:** Half-precision (FP16) computation offers advantages in memory bandwidth and arithmetic throughput. The LARS (Layer-wise Adaptive Rate Scaling) optimizer is effective for enabling large mini-batch training.
 - **Challenge:** Naively combining FP16 with LARS can lead to vanishing gradients because LARS-computed local learning rates might fall outside the dynamic range of FP16, causing values smaller than 2^-24 to become zero.
 - **Approach:** The proposed strategy performs forward and backward propagation operations in FP16. However, before applying LARS, weights and gradients are cast to single-precision (FP32). After LARS computes the updated gradients in FP32, they are cast back to FP16 for the next forward/backward pass. Additionally, for mini-batch sizes of 32K and above, the LARS coefficient is set to a small value (0.001), necessitating FP32 computation for LARS. To prevent overfitting with a 64K mini-batch, the weight decay is increased from 0.0001 to 0.0005.

- **Improvements on Model Architecture:**
 - **Eliminated Weight Decay on Bias and Batch Normalization Parameters:**
 - **Motivation:** Weight decay is a regularization technique. Conventional practice often applies it only to weights, not biases. The authors observed that also excluding the trainable parameters (β and γ) in batch normalization layers from weight decay could improve convergence.
 - **Approach:** The system modifies the regularization scheme to specifically exclude bias terms and batch normalization parameters (β and γ) from weight decay. The rationale is that these parameters constitute a small fraction of the total model parameters, and regularizing them might introduce underfitting without significantly reducing variance.
 - **Adding Proper Batch Normalization Layers for AlexNet:**
 - **Motivation:** While replacing Local Response Normalization (LRN) layers with Batch Normalization (BN) generally improves AlexNet accuracy, the standard AlexNet-BN model still struggled to reach baseline accuracy with a 64K mini-batch size. Analysis showed that the feature map distribution after the Pool5 layer exhibited large variance as training progressed, hindering training.
 - **Approach:** An additional Batch Normalization layer is inserted after the Pool5 layer in the AlexNet architecture (referred to as refined-AlexNet-BN). This aims to rescale the feature map and stabilize its distribution, thereby facilitating better convergence and accuracy.

#### 3.3. Communication Module Optimizations

This module focuses on optimizing the gradient aggregation step, a common bottleneck in large-scale distributed training.

- **Tensor Fusion:**
 - **Motivation:** In deep neural networks, gradient tensors vary significantly in size. Sending numerous small tensors individually over the network can lead to under-utilization of bandwidth and increased latency.
 - **Approach:** During the backward propagation phase, as gradient tensors from different layers are computed, the system fuses multiple small tensors into a larger buffer. An adjustable parameter, θ, defines the threshold for the total size of fused tensors. Once the cumulative size of fused tensors exceeds θ, the combined (fused) tensor is sent for all-reduce communication. This reduces the number of communication operations and better utilizes network bandwidth.
- **Hierarchical All-reduce:**
 - **Motivation:** Traditional ring-based all-reduce algorithms, while efficient for large messages, become less efficient for small tensor sizes and very large numbers of GPUs (k), as messages passed between nodes become smaller, failing to utilize full network bandwidth.
 - **Approach:** A three-phase hierarchical all-reduce algorithm is introduced. First, GPUs within the same physical node or a defined group (size k) perform a reduce operation, storing partial results on a designated master GPU within that group. Second, these master GPUs (total p/k groups, where p is total GPUs) perform a ring-based all-reduce operation across the groups. Finally, after each master GPU receives the final aggregated result, it broadcasts this result to all other GPUs within its group. This approach reduces the number of communication steps from 2(p-1) to 4(k-1) + 2(p/k-1), offering latency benefits for small tensors. The group size 'k' is a tunable parameter; the paper found k=16 optimal for their 1024 GPU cluster.
- **Hybrid All-reduce:**
 - **Motivation:** While hierarchical all-reduce is advantageous for small tensors (e.g., from convolution layers), ring-based all-reduce can still outperform it for very large tensors (e.g., from fully-connected layers) due to its inherent bandwidth efficiency for large data transfers.
 - **Approach:** The system employs a hybrid strategy that dynamically switches between the hierarchical all-reduce and the traditional ring-based all-reduce. A parameter η defines a tensor size threshold: if a tensor's size is below η, the hierarchical all-reduce is used; otherwise, the ring-based all-reduce is employed. This combines the benefits of both approaches, achieving optimal performance across varying tensor sizes.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Models:** AlexNet (62M parameters, 727M FLOPs, communication-bound) and ResNet-50 (25M parameters, 4G FLOPs, computation-bound) are used to represent typical CNN architectures.
- **Dataset:** ImageNet (1.28 million training images, 50,000 validation images for 1000 classes) is the standard benchmark.
- **Software:** TensorFlow is used as the deep learning framework. Gradient aggregation leverages NVIDIA Collective Communication Library (NCCL) and OpenMPI.
- **Hardware:** A cluster of 256 nodes, each with 8 NVIDIA Tesla P40 GPUs interconnected via PCIe. Mellanox ConnectX-4 100Gbit Ethernet network cards are used for inter-node communication, utilizing RoCEv2 (RDMA over Converged Ethernet) and GPUDirect RDMA (GDR) for low-latency, high-bandwidth data exchange.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research achieved accelerated training times for AlexNet and ResNet-50 on the ImageNet dataset while maintaining or exceeding baseline accuracy, employing various optimization strategies.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance Benchmarks

- **ResNet-50 Training:**
 - The system achieved a 76.2% top-1 accuracy in 8.7 minutes using 1024 Tesla P40 GPUs with a 64K mini-batch size.
 - Further, it achieved 75.8% top-1 accuracy in 6.6 minutes using 2048 Tesla P40 GPUs (64K mini-batch).
 - These results represent a notable improvement over prior art:
 - Compared to Akiba et al. (2017) (32K batch, 1024 P100 GPUs, 74.9% accuracy, 15 minutes), this work is 1.61 times faster and achieves higher accuracy with a larger batch size.
 - Compared to You et al. (2017) (32K batch, 2048 KNLs, 75.4% accuracy, 20 minutes), this work is significantly faster and achieves comparable accuracy on a different hardware architecture.
 - Compared to He et al. (2016) (8 P100 GPUs, 29 hours), this work achieves over 248x speedup.
- **AlexNet Training:**
 - The system completed AlexNet training with 58.7% top-1 accuracy in 4 minutes using 1024 Tesla P40 GPUs (64K mini-batch, 95 epochs).
 - This surpasses You et al. (2017) who trained AlexNet (32K batch, 1024 Intel CPUs) in 11 minutes with 58.6% accuracy.

#### 4.2. Convergence Analysis

- **Mixed-Precision Training:**
 - Comparison with single-precision (FP32) training for ResNet-50 demonstrated that the mixed-precision strategy (FP16 for FWD/BWD, FP32 for LARS/weights master copy) achieved a similar top-1 accuracy (76.2% for mixed-precision vs. 76.3% for FP32 at 90 epochs). This indicates that the proposed strategy successfully mitigates precision loss.
 - Loss-scaling was identified as necessary for mini-batch sizes of 16K or greater to prevent vanishing gradients, while LARS was crucial for 32K mini-batch sizes and above.
- **Effect of LARS:**
 - For ResNet-50 with a 64K mini-batch, applying LARS significantly improved top-1 accuracy from 60.6% to 71.9% compared to training without LARS. The training curve also exhibited smoother convergence.
- **Effect of Model Architecture Improvements:**
 - **ResNet-50:** Eliminating weight decay on batch normalization parameters (β and γ) further improved ResNet-50's top-1 accuracy from 71.9% (with LARS but no BN decay elimination) to 76.2% (with LARS and BN decay elimination), reaching the baseline accuracy.
 - **AlexNet:** A series of architectural improvements, including no weight decay on bias, no weight decay on batch normalization parameters, and adding a batch normalization layer after Pool5, cumulatively increased AlexNet's top-1 accuracy for a 64K mini-batch from 55.8% to 58.8%, matching the baseline accuracy for AlexNet. The added BN layer after Pool5 stabilized feature map distributions, preventing an increase in variance and maximum values.

#### 4.3. Training Speed and Scalability

- **Mixed-Precision Speedup:** For ResNet-50 on a single node, mixed-precision training increased throughput from 172 images/second (FP32) to 218 images/second. This gain is attributed to faster FP16 computation and reduced communication parameter size.
- **Scalability for ResNet-50:**
 - With a fixed per-GPU mini-batch size of 64, the system achieved a scaling efficiency of 99.2% on 1024 GPUs compared to a single node (8 GPUs). This is a substantial improvement from 9.0% efficiency observed with the original communication setup.
 - With a fixed per-GPU mini-batch size of 32 (a more challenging scenario for scaling due to higher communication-to-computation ratio), the system achieved 87.9% scaling efficiency on 1024 GPUs, outperforming previous state-of-the-art systems that reported 80.0% efficiency.
- **Scalability for AlexNet:**
 - For AlexNet with a mini-batch size of 128 per GPU, the baseline FP32 all-reduce achieved 70% scaling efficiency when comparing 8 GPUs to 512 GPUs.
 - Introducing tensor fusion improved efficiency to 81%.
 - Using FP16 all-reduce yielded 82.7% efficiency.
 - Combining FP16, tensor fusion, and hybrid all-reduce resulted in 91.4% scaling efficiency, demonstrating the cumulative benefits of the proposed communication optimizations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a highly optimized deep learning training system that addresses critical challenges in large-scale distributed training, yielding notable advancements in training speed while preserving or enhancing model accuracy.

The primary significance lies in the **demonstration of training ImageNet models (AlexNet and ResNet-50) in unprecedentedly short times**—4 minutes and 6.6 minutes respectively—while operating at extremely large mini-batch sizes (64K) and achieving competitive or superior top-1 test accuracies. This establishes new performance benchmarks and indicates that previous trade-offs between training speed, batch size, and accuracy can be mitigated through comprehensive system-level optimizations.

The specific technical contributions offer several impacts:
- **Mixed-Precision Training Integration:** The successful integration of mixed-precision training with LARS, specifically managing the dynamic range limitations of FP16 for critical operations, provides a blueprint for leveraging hardware acceleration without sacrificing accuracy. This can lead to broader adoption of mixed-precision techniques in other large-scale deep learning applications.
- **Architectural Fine-tuning:** The identification and implementation of subtle model architectural improvements, such as eliminating weight decay on batch normalization parameters and strategically adding BN layers to AlexNet, demonstrate that even minor modifications can have a significant impact on convergence and generalization when scaling to very large batch sizes. This highlights the importance of re-evaluating standard practices under extreme scaling conditions.
- **Advanced Communication Strategies:** The development of tensor fusion, hierarchical all-reduce, and hybrid all-reduce algorithms directly tackles the communication bottleneck, which is a fundamental challenge in highly distributed systems. These algorithms enable near-linear scaling efficiency across hundreds and thousands of GPUs, especially for communication-bound models or scenarios with varying tensor sizes. This provides valuable insights and potential open-source contributions for future distributed training frameworks.

The potential impact on the broader field of deep learning is substantial:
- **Accelerated Research and Development:** Drastically reduced training times allow researchers and developers to iterate faster on model designs, hyperparameter tuning, and exploration of new architectures. This can significantly speed up the pace of innovation in areas requiring large-scale model training.
- **Enablement of Larger Models and Datasets:** Faster training means that even larger, more complex models or datasets, which were previously impractical to train due to time constraints, can now be explored more readily.
- **Efficient Resource Utilization:** The high scaling efficiency achieved by the system suggests that large GPU clusters can be utilized more effectively, translating into better return on investment for high-performance computing infrastructure.
- **Practical Framework Development:** The methodologies presented, particularly in mixed-precision and communication optimization, are directly applicable to improving existing or developing new distributed deep learning frameworks, providing practical guidance for engineers and system architects.

In conclusion, this work pushes the boundaries of distributed deep learning training performance, demonstrating that highly scalable systems can deliver record-breaking speeds while maintaining or improving accuracy, thus facilitating faster scientific discovery and application development in artificial intelligence.
