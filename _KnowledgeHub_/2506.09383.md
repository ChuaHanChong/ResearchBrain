# Bipedal Balance Control with Whole-body Musculoskeletal Standing and Falling Simulations

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Tsinghua University developed a training-free Hierarchical Balance Control (HBC) framework for a detailed 700-muscle human musculoskeletal model to simulate bipedal standing and falling dynamics. This system enabled the characterization of muscle-level balance strategies, fall impact patterns matching clinical data, and demonstrated the efficacy of simulated exoskeleton assistance in reducing muscle effort and improving stability.

## Problem

- Lack of systematic investigation into full-body static standing control using detailed musculoskeletal models to gain muscle-level insights.
- Limited understanding of human falling dynamics due to data scarcity and ethical constraints in real-world experiments, making injury-prone impact site analysis challenging.
- Absence of work utilizing musculoskeletal simulations to test and validate balance-targeted exoskeletons for efficacy and optimization.

## Method

- A full-body ==MS-Human-700== model, comprising 90 segments, 206 joints, and 700 Hill-type muscle-tendon units, was used for biomechanically plausible simulations in the MuJoCo physics engine.
- A **Hierarchical Balance Control (HBC)** algorithm was developed, featuring a high-level ==Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI)== planner to optimize major joint coordinates and a low-level muscle-based PD-like controller to generate muscle activation signals.
- A comprehensive cost function with components for height, rotation, CoM position/velocity, and imitation was designed to guide stable standing, and ==Bayesian optimization== was used to tune exoskeleton control parameters.

## Results

- **HBC** achieved stable static balance in the MS-Human-700 model, consistently outperforming DRL baselines (DynSyn, SAC) in maintaining standing duration.
- Analysis of **2,895** simulated falls revealed collision patterns consistent with clinical data, with the upper extremities, lower leg, and pelvis being the most frequent impact sites.
- A simulated hip exoskeleton, optimized via Bayesian optimization, increased the standing success rate under perturbation and reduced activation in gluteal muscles, indicating decreased muscular effort.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The training-free HBC algorithm efficiently generates high-fidelity muscle-level control for static balance, outperforming DRL methods and showing biomechanical consistency with human EMG data.
> - Stable standing involves dynamic CoM trajectory adjustments converging to specific balance regions, and simulated muscle injuries alter these regions, leading to compensatory postural and muscle activation strategies.
> - Musculoskeletal simulations can accurately predict human fall contact patterns, with peak collision frequencies at the upper extremities, lower leg, and pelvis, aligning with clinical injury data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The study addresses challenges in understanding and controlling bipedal balance, specifically focusing on static standing and falling, areas that have received comparatively less attention than dynamic locomotion in both robotics and human biomechanics.

Previous research in bipedal robotics has extensively explored stability using simplified models such as the single or double inverted pendulum, focusing on metrics like the Center of Mass (CoM) and Zero Moment Point (ZMP). While these models have demonstrated the inherent difficulties of stabilizing underactuated systems, they often fail to capture the complexity of human balance, which involves intricate sensorimotor integration through high-dimensional muscular systems. Recent advancements in controlling full-body human musculoskeletal models, particularly using deep reinforcement learning (DRL) for locomotion tasks, have been made. However, the systematic control of full-body static standing balance, especially to gain muscle-level insights, remains largely unexplored.

Furthermore, the understanding of human falling dynamics is limited due to data scarcity and ethical constraints in real-world experiments. Existing fall datasets primarily comprise voluntary falls, which may not accurately represent real-world impact dynamics and protective responses. Similarly, while exoskeletons have been developed to assist balance, most research has focused on locomotion, with limited and often experimentally constrained studies on balance-specific scenarios. Musculoskeletal simulations have been leveraged for exoskeleton design by estimating joint torques or serving as testbeds, but there is a stated absence of work utilizing such simulations to test and validate balance-targeted exoskeletons.

This work aims to bridge these gaps by introducing a comprehensive, muscle-level simulation framework for bipedal balance, offering insights that are challenging to obtain through conventional experimental methods and providing a platform for the design and evaluation of assistive technologies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the recognized importance of balance control for both human well-being and the development of advanced bipedal robotic systems, coupled with existing limitations in quantitative understanding and modeling capabilities.

Humans exhibit obligate bipedalism, necessitating precise coordination between neuromuscular control and biomechanical dynamics to maintain an upright stance. This skill is acquired over years, integrating multisensory inputs and fine-grained muscle coordination, presenting a control challenge distinct from typical robot locomotion. Despite remarkable human adaptability in balance, the underlying principles of its robustness are not fully understood, particularly at the detailed muscle level due to the musculoskeletal system's high degrees of freedom and nonlinearity. A critical gap identified is the lack of systematic investigation into bipedal standing control using full-body musculoskeletal models capable of decoding fine-grained postural dynamics.

Beyond maintaining balance, falling is a significant public health concern, especially among aging populations, leading to injuries and psychological trauma. However, the dynamics of human falls are poorly understood due to the ethical and practical difficulties of collecting real-world fall data.

In response to these motivations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a Hierarchical Control Pipeline:** Create a training-free hierarchical control method (Hierarchical Balance Control, HBC) for full-body bipedal balance using a comprehensive musculoskeletal system (MS-Human-700 model) to enable biomechanically plausible simulations of standing balance and falls.
2. **Characterize Stable Standing Dynamics:** Identify and quantify the spatiotemporal dynamics of balancing during stable standing, including analyzing Center of Mass (CoM) trajectories and balance regions.
3. **Investigate Impact of Muscle Injury:** Reveal how simulated muscle injuries affect balancing behavior and the adaptive strategies employed by the musculoskeletal system.
4. **Analyze Falling Dynamics:** Generate and analyze fall contact patterns to align with clinical data, providing insights into injury-prone collision locations that are difficult to study experimentally.
5. **Evaluate Exoskeleton Assistance:** Demonstrate how simulated hip exoskeleton assistance can enhance balance maintenance and reduce muscle effort under perturbation, and establish a methodology for optimizing such assistive devices.
6. **Provide Muscle-Level Insights:** Offer unique, detailed muscle-level insights into human balance dynamics that are generally inaccessible through traditional experimental techniques.
7. **Lay Foundation for Interventions and Robotics:** Establish a foundation for developing targeted interventions for individuals with balance impairments and support the advancement of humanoid robotic systems by offering a detailed simulation platform.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a simulation-based approach utilizing a detailed human musculoskeletal model and a novel hierarchical control algorithm to study bipedal standing and falling dynamics.

**Musculoskeletal Model Dynamics:**
The study utilized the full-body MS-Human-700 model, which consists of 90 rigid body segments, 206 joints, and 700 Hill-type muscle-tendon units. This model is implemented within the MuJoCo physics engine. Muscle forces are generated based on muscle activation, muscle length, and velocity, governed by specific force-length and force-velocity functions, as well as passive force-length functions. Muscle activation itself follows a first-order nonlinear system, relating the input control signal to the activation state.

**Standing Task Design and Balance-Related Scenarios:**
The human standing control problem was formulated as a finite horizon Markov Decision Process. The objective was to find a control sequence that minimizes a predefined cost function over time, successfully maintaining a standing posture. The control goals for stable standing were defined as achieving near-zero CoM horizontal velocity and ensuring the CoM remained within the support region, thereby preventing flipping. The model was permitted to take steps for balance, but its initial position was consistent across experiments.

**Hierarchical Balance Control (HBC) Algorithm:**
A hierarchical control framework was proposed to manage the high dimensionality of the musculoskeletal system.
- **High-Level Planner:** This component uses Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) as a sampling-based planner. It optimizes a set of major joint coordinates (target pose) over a short planning horizon. MPPI samples multiple sets of target joint angles from a Gaussian distribution, evaluates the cumulative cost for trajectories generated with these targets, and updates the mean and covariance of the distribution based on the best-performing targets. This approach reduces the parameter space from the large number of muscles to a more manageable number of joints, facilitating effective planning and control. The method is described as training-free and capable of generating controls efficiently.
- **Low-Level Controller:** This controller translates the high-level target joint coordinates into muscle activation signals. It employs a normalized PD control-like formulation based on muscle lengths to derive desired muscle forces. These desired forces are then used, through inverse dynamics, to calculate the input control signals for each of the 700 muscle-tendon units.

**Cost Function Design:**
The control objective for standing balance was represented by a cost function comprising multiple weighted components:
1. **Height (`C_H`):** Penalizes deviations from the initial upright height, indicating an upright, non-flipping posture.
2. **Rotation (`C_R`):** Penalizes large rotational angles of the upper body.
3. **CoM Position (`C_Pc`):** Penalizes the horizontal CoM deviation from the center of the support polygon.
4. **CoM Velocity (`C_Vc`):** Penalizes large horizontal CoM velocities.
5. **Imitation (`C_I`):** Penalizes the mismatch between current joint angles and those of a natural standing posture.
The weights for these components were empirically tuned to (300, 300, 300, 10, 1) respectively.

**Standing and Fall under Different Physiological Conditions:**
The HBC algorithm was applied to the MS-Human-700 model, initialized in a natural standing posture for 5-second simulations.
- **Balance Monitoring:** Stable standing was defined by two criteria: no body parts other than feet contacting the ground, and the CoM remaining within the real-time support polygon at the 5-second mark. CoM positions, muscle forces, and body posture data were collected at 500 Hz.
- **Fall Detection and Recording:** A fall was defined as occurring between an "initialization event" (CoM moving outside the support polygon) and a "contact event" (peak body-ground contact force).
- **Muscle Injury Simulation:** The effects of injury were simulated by restricting the force-generating capacity of the left rectus femoris (RF) muscle, mimicking reported clinical conditions.

**Exoskeleton-Assisted Standing:**
The high-level planner of HBC was extended to concurrently optimize control strategies for balance-assistive devices. A hip exoskeleton was simulated by applying torque actuation at the hip joints. The exoskeleton's control policy involved a weighted combination of hip flexion joint angle PD control and postural PD control based on pelvis tilt angle. The design parameters for this exoskeleton policy (control gains and weights) were optimized using Bayesian optimization, formulated as a black-box optimization problem, to enhance assisted balance performance.

**Experimental Setup:**
Simulations were carried out using MuJoCo MPC. The efficiency of HBC was highlighted by its ability to generate extensive dynamic datasets for analysis much faster than DRL-based methods (e.g., 2000 trajectories within 1 day compared to >1 day training for DRL to fail at stable standing). HBC performance was compared against DRL baselines, specifically Dynamic Synergy Representation (DynSyn) and Soft Actor Critic (SAC).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings across the evaluation of the HBC algorithm, balance dynamics under various conditions, and the simulation of assisted balance.

**Performance Evaluation of the HBC Algorithm:**
The Hierarchical Balance Control (HBC) algorithm successfully achieved stable static balance control on the full-body musculoskeletal system. In comparative evaluations against DRL-based methods, DynSyn and SAC, HBC demonstrated significantly superior performance in maintaining static balance, achieving the maximum standing duration in most trials (Figure 2). A fidelity check of the simulated control results indicated consistency between the calculated muscle activations by HBC and experimentally measured human electromyography (EMG) signals for key muscles (tibialis anterior, peroneus longus, gastrocnemius medialis, gastrocnemius lateralis), suggesting biomechanically plausible simulations (Figure 3).

**Study of Balance Dynamics:**
Extensive simulations allowed for the collection of a large dataset, including 2,800 falling trajectories and 640,000 frames of successful standing trials.
- **CoM Trajectories During Balancing:** Analysis of horizontal CoM positions revealed that during the initial phase of balancing, CoM trajectories are often irregular, subsequently converging to a smaller area. This observation indicates the dynamic and responsive nature of balance control in a high-dimensional, muscle-controlled human body (Figure 4b).
- **Balance Region in Stable Standing:** A spatial density plot of CoM coordinates during successful balance trials identified a concentrated "balance region" where CoM positions were most frequently found. This region represents the areas associated with stable balancing dynamics (Figure 4c).
- **Distribution of Collision Positions in Falls:** Statistical analysis of 2,895 simulated falls indicated that the most frequent collision regions were the upper extremities (wrist and forearm), lower leg, and pelvis (Figure 5). These simulated patterns aligned with known clinical data regarding frequently injured sites in human falls, highlighting the framework's capability to analyze injury-prone impact sites.

**Study of Injury Effect on Balance:**
Simulations with a model featuring a unilaterally injured left rectus femoris muscle were conducted, generating 1,280 falling trajectories.
- **Balance Region Alteration:** The balance region of the injured model was observed to shrink and become more concentrated compared to the healthy model (Figure 6b). This suggested a more conservative balancing strategy and increased predictability under injury conditions.
- **Postural Adjustments:** An asymmetric expansion of the low-density CoM region in the posterior direction was noted, indicating an increased tendency for forward-leaning postural adjustments in the injured state.
- **Muscle Compensation:** A significant increase in the activation and force generated by the contralateral (right) rectus femoris muscle was observed (Figure 6c), suggesting a compensatory weight shift to the right leg to maintain stability.

**Simulation of Assisted Balance:**
A hip exoskeleton was simulated with optimized control parameters determined via Bayesian optimization.
- **Perturbation Test Performance:** In a perturbation test where models were pushed in random directions, the exoskeleton-assisted model demonstrated a higher success rate in maintaining standing posture compared to the unassisted model (Figure 7b).
- **Reduced Muscle Effort:** An ablation study showed that exoskeleton assistance led to reduced activation levels in gluteal muscles (gluteus maximus, medius, and minimus) during standing simulation (Figure 7c). This finding suggests that assistive devices can potentially reduce muscular effort and metabolic cost, providing muscle-level data that is challenging to acquire experimentally for deep muscles.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of bipedal balance control through its comprehensive simulation framework and analysis, with implications for both fundamental scientific understanding and applied engineering.

**Enhanced Biomechanical Understanding:** The work provides detailed, muscle-level insights into human balance dynamics during stable standing and falling. Such insights are difficult to obtain through experimental methods alone, offering a complementary tool for human movement science. The ability to observe and quantify the specific muscle activations and compensatory strategies under various conditions (e.g., injury) contributes to a more granular understanding of neuromuscular coordination during balance.

**Methodological Advancement in Control:** The introduction of Hierarchical Balance Control (HBC) offers a training-free, efficient method for controlling high-dimensional musculoskeletal systems for static balance. By effectively reducing the parameter space from muscle-level to joint-level control, HBC outperforms existing DRL-based methods in efficiency and control performance for this specific task. This approach could be adopted for other complex biomechanical control problems.

**Foundational for Clinical Interventions and Assistive Technology:**
- **Targeted Interventions:** The detailed analysis of balance behaviors under muscle injury conditions can inform the development of more targeted rehabilitation strategies and interventions for individuals with balance impairments. Understanding compensatory mechanisms could lead to customized training protocols.
- **Fall Prevention and Mitigation:** The generation and analysis of biomechanically accurate fall contact patterns, validated against clinical data, provides a valuable testbed for developing and evaluating fall prevention strategies and protective devices. This simulation capability can reduce the reliance on risky and ethically challenging real-world fall experiments.
- **Exoskeleton Design and Optimization:** The framework allows for the efficient simulation, validation, and optimization of assistive devices like exoskeletons before physical prototyping or human trials. The demonstration of reduced muscle effort with exoskeleton assistance offers a quantitative metric for assessing device efficacy and designing more effective, energy-saving systems.

**Advancement in Humanoid Robotics:** The insights gained into complex human balance control strategies at a muscle-actuated level can directly inform the design and control algorithms for more robust and agile humanoid robotic systems, enabling them to achieve more human-like balance capabilities in diverse environments.

In summary, this work offers a valuable computational platform for investigating intricate questions in human movement science, particularly those constrained by practical experimental limitations, and provides a foundation for both therapeutic and technological innovations in balance control.
