# Online Estimation and Manipulation of Articulated Objects

> [!summary] Summary
> A factor-graph framework for opening unknown articulated objects (drawers, cabinets, doors) that fuses a screw-theory kinematic model with three sensing streams: an uncertainty-aware vision network's initial articulation prior, force sensing during grasp, and proprioceptive kinematic tracking during interaction, updating the estimate online as the robot manipulates the object; achieves 75% real-hardware success on unseen objects.

## Problem

- Vision-only affordance networks predict articulation priors before contact but cannot correct errors once the robot starts interacting, so wrong initial guesses persist through manipulation.
- Interactive-perception methods that infer articulation from motion require the robot to already grasp and move the object correctly, creating a bootstrapping problem for unknown objects.
- Prior factor-graph articulation trackers (e.g. category-independent tracking) assume a prescribed opening motion or hand-guided demonstration, giving the estimator information a fully autonomous robot would not have.

## Method

- ==Uncertainty-aware articulation network==: a PointNet++-based affordance predictor (extending Flowbot3D-style motion-flow representations) that outputs a screw-parameter prediction with a learned covariance instead of a fixed one.
- **Screw-theory factor graph** in GTSAM with four factor types, Affordance, Articulation, Kinematic, and Force, jointly optimizing joint pose variables and the articulation screw via MAP estimation.
- ==Online force-triggered update rule==: a wrist force/torque threshold during initial pulling injects a Force factor into the graph, forcing re-optimization toward the next most likely joint type (e.g. revolute vs prismatic) before continuous kinematic refinement takes over.

## Results

- In simulation on PartNet-Mobility objects, the method with 1000 articulation-point factors outperforms Flowbot3D affordance prediction by **11.2%**, and by **8.7%** with 500 factors.
- Real hardware trials on previously unseen drawers and cabinet doors achieved a **75%** autonomous opening success rate end-to-end.
- Hand-guided comparison against Heppert et al.'s factor-graph tracker, using the authors' own code, was run on revolute and prismatic joints with motion-capture ground truth to validate kinematic-factor accuracy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Fusing a vision prior with physics-based interaction sensing lets a system recover from a wrong initial guess instead of committing to it, a general remedy for perception-before-contact bootstrapping problems.
> - Representing uncertainty explicitly (learned covariance, not hard-coded) lets downstream fusion know which prediction axes to trust and which to override with contact data.
> - Adding more articulation-point factors improves accuracy but increases optimization latency, an accuracy-versus-runtime tradeoff worth budgeting for single-shot inference at interaction onset.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of service robots into human environments necessitates their capability to interact with and manipulate common articulated objects such as doors, drawers, and appliances. This area of robotics research typically involves two main approaches: vision-based prediction and interactive perception.

Vision-based methods, often leveraging deep neural networks, have focused on predicting object affordances (how an object can be interacted with) from visual data, such as point clouds. These methods frequently rely on large simulated datasets of common household objects, assuming that articulations are often predictable from visual inspection alone. However, a significant limitation of these approaches is their unreliability when visual cues are ambiguous or identical for different articulation types. For instance, a cabinet might have multiple doors that appear identical when closed but open in distinct ways (e.g., hinged, sliding). Furthermore, these methods generally provide a single, static prediction, lacking mechanisms for online updates during interaction. This reliance on a single inference means that any initial error can only be compensated by highly compliant robot controllers, rather than through improved understanding of the object.

Conversely, interactive perception methods focus on estimating object articulations through observations of motion during physical interaction. These approaches often employ analytical models of articulation (e.g., revolute, prismatic, screw joints) and rely on proprioceptive sensing (kinematic and force feedback). A primary challenge for these methods is the requirement for a good initial guess of the articulation to initiate interaction, which can be problematic if the robot does not already possess prior knowledge of the object.

This paper addresses the limitations of both paradigms by proposing a framework that combines the strengths of learned visual priors with the robustness of online proprioceptive sensing. It seeks to bridge the gap where vision-based methods are insufficient and interactive perception methods lack an initial manipulation strategy. Building upon prior work, the authors specifically aim to enhance the robustness of articulation prediction by incorporating uncertainty-aware visual priors and by introducing a novel force sensing factor, which allows for the successful manipulation of objects that were previously challenging, such as sliding doors where initial pulling attempts may yield no motion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable service robots to autonomously and robustly manipulate a wide range of articulated objects, including those that are visually ambiguous or previously unseen. To achieve this, the authors identify and address several specific challenges that motivate their proposed approach:

1. **Addressing Unreliable Vision-Only Predictions:** A primary motivation stems from the inherent unreliability of predicting articulation solely from visual data. Many real-world articulated objects, such as cabinet doors that look identical but open differently (e.g., hinged versus sliding), provide insufficient visual cues for a robot to reliably infer their articulation. The existing deep learning methods often fail in such scenarios, necessitating a more robust estimation mechanism.

2. **Enabling Online Articulation Estimation and Adaptation:** Current vision-based deep learning methods typically generate a single articulation prediction. If this initial prediction is incorrect, the robot has no means to update its understanding during interaction. This often leads to failures or requires complex, highly compliant controllers to manage unexpected object behavior. The authors aim to develop a system that can continuously refine its articulation estimate throughout the manipulation process.

3. **Providing Robust Initialization for Interactive Perception:** While interactive perception methods excel at estimating articulation from motion, they typically require the robot to already be interacting with the object in a way that generates informative motion. This necessitates an initial guess of how to manipulate the object, which can be a significant hurdle for novel or complex objects. The research seeks to leverage learned visual priors to provide this critical initial estimate, even if it is uncertain or partially incorrect.

4. **Overcoming Limitations in Handling Diverse Articulation Types:** Previous attempts to combine visual and proprioceptive sensing, including the authors' earlier work, encountered limitations with specific articulation types, such as sliding doors. If an initial manipulation attempt based on a visual prediction (e.g., pulling a sliding door backwards) results in no movement, the robot cannot gather kinematic data to update its estimate. This motivated the development of a mechanism to infer articulation parameters even when no motion is observed.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a novel approach that fuses learned visual priors with proprioceptive sensing (kinematic and force feedback) within a factor graph framework, utilizing analytical models of articulation based on Screw Theory. This framework is designed to provide an initial visual prediction, incorporate its uncertainty, and then quickly update the estimate using sensory feedback collected during active manipulation, thereby improving robustness and enabling the manipulation of a broader range of articulated objects.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is a factor graph formulation that integrates information from three distinct sensing modalities: visual point cloud data, force measurements, and kinematic readings from the robot's arm. This multi-modal fusion allows for online estimation of articulation parameters.

**Factor Graph Formulation:**
The system models the state `x(t)` at time `t` as consisting of constant screw parameters `ξ` and the articulation configuration `θ(t)`. The articulation is assumed to involve two parts: a static base `T_WB` and an articulated part `T_WA`. The factor graph aims to find the Maximum-A-Posteriori (MAP) estimate of the full history of states `X = {x_k, T_A_k, T_B_k}` given a set of measurements `Z`. Assuming conditionally independent measurements corrupted by zero-mean Gaussian noise, this is formulated as a least squares minimization problem (Eq. 10), where each term represents a residual `r` weighted by its inverse covariance `Σ`.

**Four Main Factors:**

1. **Uncertainty-aware Articulation Prediction from Vision (Affordance Factor `r_P`):**
 - A deep neural network, based on PointNet++ architecture (similar to Flowbot3D), is utilized.
 - Input: A point cloud `P` of `N` points, where each point `p_n` includes 3D position and a mask indicating if it belongs to the articulated part.
 - Output: For each point on the articulated part, the network predicts a 3D flow vector `ˆf_n` (instantaneous velocity for a small articulation change) and its associated aleatoric uncertainty `ˆΣ_n`.
 - Training: The network is trained using a Gaussian Maximum Likelihood (ML) loss function (Eq. 12), which enables it to learn both the articulation flow and its per-point uncertainty in an unsupervised manner from labels `f`. The uncertainty `ˆΣ_n` is parameterized as a diagonal covariance matrix (Eq. 14).
 - This provides a learned prior for the articulation, critically including a measure of confidence in the prediction.

2. **New Affordance Factor (`r_P`):**
 - Unlike previous approaches that used a single articulation factor with a hand-tuned uncertainty, this work introduces a sum of per-point factors (Eq. 16).
 - Each factor `r_P_i` for a point `p_i` compares the predicted post-articulation point `ˆp_i^+ = ˆf_i + p_i` with the point `p_i^+ = T_BA(ˆξ, θ)p_i` derived from the Screw Theory model, assuming a small articulation `θ` (e.g., 0.05 rad).
 - The residual `r_P_i = T_BA(ˆξ, θ)p_i - ˆf_i - p_i` (Eq. 19) is conditioned on the learned covariance `ˆΣ_i` from the neural network. This directly integrates the learned uncertainty into the factor graph, making the visual prior more robust.

3. **Force Factor (`r_F`):**
 - This factor addresses scenarios where initial visual predictions might lead to an applied motion that yields no object movement (e.g., pulling a sliding door backwards).
 - If the robot attempts to open an object, and a wrist-mounted force/torque sensor detects a reaction force above a threshold without any object motion, it is inferred that the applied direction of motion is orthogonal to the valid direction of motion.
 - The relationship `v_valid · ˆF = 0` (Eq. 20) is exploited, where `ˆF` is the measured reaction force and `v_valid` is the true instantaneous motion direction at the grasp point.
 - The method calculates a rotated velocity `v_rot` by projecting the estimated velocity `v_est` onto a plane orthogonal to `ˆF`. The residual `r_F_f = v_est - v_rot` (Eq. 24) is then added to the factor graph.
 - This factor is given a very low uncertainty (`Σ_F = 1e-6`) to strongly constrain the articulation estimate, guiding it towards plausible motions.

4. **Kinematic Factor (`r_K`):**
 - This factor leverages the robot's proprioceptive sensing. Once the articulated part begins to move, the robot's forward kinematics provide measurements of the end-effector's pose `T_A` (assuming a rigid grasp).
 - These measurements `T_A_k` are added to the factor graph as unary factors at predefined intervals (e.g., every 2mm or 0.5 degrees of movement).
 - The base part `T_B` is assumed static, using the initial grasp pose. A manual uncertainty (`σ_K = 1e-3`) is assigned to account for potential small slippage.

**Articulation Factor (`r_A`):**
- This factor connects the estimated screw parameters `ξ` and configuration `θ_k` with the estimated part poses `T_A_k` and `T_B_k` using the Screw Theory model (Eq. 7).
- The residual `r_A_k = T_BA(ˆξ, θ_k) ⊟ T_B_k^-1 T_A_k` (Eq. 25) quantifies the discrepancy between the observed relative pose `T_B_k^-1 T_A_k` and the pose predicted by the screw model `T_BA(ˆξ, θ_k)`. The `⊟` operator denotes pose differencing on the SE(3) manifold using the logarithm map.

**System Implementation:**
The full system operates in three modules:
- **Initialization:** A human user provides a grasp point via an RGB-D camera feed. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) segments the articulated part, and this information, along with depth, is fed into the uncertainty-aware articulation network to predict initial affordance `P`.
- **Estimation:** The factor graph (implemented using GTSAM) is optimized. Initially, only `P` is used. If a force threshold is exceeded without motion, `r_F` is added. Once motion occurs, `r_K` measurements are added. The `r_A` factor continuously enforces the screw model. The graph is re-optimized periodically or upon significant changes.
- **Motion Generation:** The latest articulation estimate `ξ` from the Estimation module is used by a quadratic programming (QP) solver (leveraging Kineverse and CasADi) to generate robot joint trajectories `q` that open the object by incrementing the articulation configuration `θ`. The robot operates in compliant mode, allowing it to adapt to minor inaccuracies, with actual end-effector poses fed back as `T_WA` measurements.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents an extensive evaluation across simulation and real-world robot experiments, detailing the performance of the proposed online estimation and manipulation framework.

**Simulation Experiments:**
- **Comparison of Inference Modes:** Experiments comparing "Single" (initial visual prediction only) and "Multi" (continuous visual updates) inference modes for articulation prediction showed that "Multi" generally yielded higher success rates. However, the authors emphasize that "Single" inference is more practical for real robots due to issues like camera occlusion and computational latency during interaction.
- **Impact of Articulation Factors:** The performance of the proposed method increased with the number of articulation factors (points) incorporated into the factor graph. Using 500 and 1000 points, the method outperformed Flowbot3D by 8.7% and 11.2% respectively, suggesting that integrating information from multiple points provides a more robust overall solution than relying on a single dominant flow point. Performance plateaued after 1000 points.
- **Latency:** The time required for inference increased with more factors, but this was deemed acceptable for the "Single" inference approach intended for initial prediction.

**Hand Guiding Experiments:**
- **Accuracy of Kinematics-based Estimation:** These experiments on a real KUKA LBR iiwa robot demonstrated the high accuracy of the kinematics-based articulation estimation. The estimator achieved an average tangent similarity of 0.90 after only 0.5 degrees of rotation and improved to 0.97 after 1.0 degrees. This indicated that even small movements during interaction could quickly lead to accurate articulation estimates.
- **Efficiency with Limited Data:** The method showed robust performance with a small number of measurements, with as few as 3 equally spaced kinematic measurements sufficient for accurate joint estimation over the configuration range.
- **Comparison with Prior Work:** The proposed method exhibited similar performance to Heppert et al. (2022) for revolute joints but demonstrated superior ability in distinguishing prismatic joints, which Heppert et al.'s method sometimes confused with large revolute articulations.

**Shared-autonomy Robot Experiments:**
- **Full System Validation:** The complete pipeline, including initialization, online estimation, and motion generation, was tested on a real KUKA iiwa robot interacting with a visually ambiguous cabinet.
- **Handling Visually Ambiguous Objects:** The system successfully opened all four doors of the test cabinet (shown in Fig. 1), including a challenging sliding door. The initial visual prediction for these doors often suggested a prismatic pulling motion, which was frequently incorrect.
- **Effectiveness of Force Factor:** For cases like the sliding door, where initial pulling led to no motion, the force factor played a critical role. When a reaction force threshold was met, the force factor updated the articulation estimate, guiding the robot to attempt a different motion (e.g., sliding sideways) that ultimately led to successful opening. This allowed kinematic measurements to then be collected, further refining the estimate.
- **Success Rate and Failure Analysis:** The robot achieved a 75% success rate (15 out of 20 trials) in autonomously opening unknown articulated objects. The failures (4 trials) were attributed to gripper slippage, indicating a limitation in differentiating between gripper slip and actual object immobility.
- **Online Parameter Convergence:** Plots of the estimated screw parameters `ξ` during online experiments (Fig. 11) showed convergence towards stable values, with decreasing 3σ ranges computed from marginal covariances, illustrating the continuous refinement of the articulation model.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of robotic manipulation of articulated objects, offering several implications for future robotic systems.

1. **Enhanced Robustness in Manipulation:** The primary impact is the development of a more robust and adaptive robotic system capable of interacting with a wider variety of articulated objects, particularly those with visually ambiguous or unknown articulation types. By combining learned visual priors with online proprioceptive feedback (kinematic and force), the system reduces its reliance on perfect initial visual predictions, thereby improving its operational reliability in unstructured environments.

2. **Bridging the Perception-Action Gap:** The work effectively bridges the gap between static visual perception and dynamic physical interaction. It demonstrates a practical method for initializing robot interaction with learned visual information and then continuously refining that understanding through physical engagement, a critical step towards more intelligent and autonomous manipulation capabilities.

3. **Improved Generalization:** The ability to infer and adapt to unknown articulations, even when visual cues are misleading, contributes to the generalization capabilities of robots. This is particularly relevant for service robots operating in diverse human environments where exact object models may not be available.

4. **Foundation for Advanced Haptic Perception:** The introduction of a force factor for articulation estimation highlights the utility of haptic sensing beyond compliant control. This opens avenues for more sophisticated haptic perception strategies in robotics, where reaction forces can be directly used to infer underlying physical properties and constraints of objects.

5. **Directions for Future Research:** The paper identifies several promising areas for future investigation, including:
 - **Improved Slippage Detection:** Addressing the gripper slippage issue, potentially through advanced fingertip sensors, could further enhance success rates.
 - **Adaptive Stiffness Control:** Integrating adaptive robot arm stiffness based on online articulation estimates could optimize interaction forces, leading to safer and more effective manipulation of objects with varying physical properties.
 - **Learning Interaction Priors:** Exploring learning-based methods to acquire priors for safe interaction forces, potentially from human demonstrations or language-aligned visual features, could reduce the need for manual hyperparameter tuning.
 - **Learning from Force Sensing:** Further research into learning-based methods that directly estimate articulation parameters from force sensing, leveraging simulation-to-real transfer, could unlock even more powerful interactive perception capabilities.

In conclusion, this work contributes to the development of autonomous robots that can move beyond pre-programmed tasks to intelligently perceive and manipulate objects in dynamic, uncertain real-world settings. Its practical demonstration on real hardware, tackling a challenging problem like visually ambiguous articulation, underscores its relevance and potential for broader application in service robotics.
