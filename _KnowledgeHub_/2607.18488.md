# The Open Ant: A Robot Platform for Reinforcement Learning Research

> [!summary] Summary
> An open-source, affordable quadrupedal robot platform, the Open Ant, was developed to facilitate reinforcement learning research on physical hardware. The platform enabled the learning of competent walking policies on the robot in approximately one hour using SARSA(λ) and SAC, and demonstrated successful transfer of policies trained in simulation.

## Problem

- Reinforcement Learning (RL) research predominantly relies on simulations, causing discrepancies when transferring algorithms to physical robots and limiting studies on learning directly from real-world experience.
- High costs, complex assembly, and demanding maintenance of existing physical robot platforms create a significant barrier for RL researchers, particularly those without extensive robotics expertise.
- A need exists for an accessible, robust, and easily maintainable experimental platform to facilitate rapid iteration and widespread adoption of physical robot RL research.

## Method

- An open-source quadrupedal robot platform, the **Open Ant**, was developed, featuring a low-cost design (approx. USD 2200 for full version) with COTS components for ease of assembly and maintenance.
- The platform includes a non-episodic ==back-and-forth== walking task and an external tracking system to minimize manual resets and support continuous online learning.
- The system integrates with standard RL software tools and supports both discrete (==SARSA(λ)==) and continuous (==SAC==) action reinforcement learning algorithms.

## Results

- Both SARSA(λ) and SAC successfully learned competent walking policies directly on the Physical Ant within **80** minutes, with SAC achieving up to ~**15 cm/s** average reward.
- All **ten** policies trained in simulation with SAC transferred successfully to the Physical Ant, demonstrating the robot's ability to walk, although their performance ranking varied between simulation and reality.
- The platform was assembled by inexperienced users within **2-5** hours and successfully utilized by **20** participants in a winter school, confirming its ease of use and low barrier to entry for RL experimentation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Developing an affordable, open-source physical robot platform significantly reduces the barrier to entry for reinforcement learning researchers, enabling broader engagement with embodied intelligence.
> - Competent walking policies can be learned directly on physical hardware within practical timescales (approximately one hour) using established RL algorithms, fostering rapid experimental iteration.
> - While policies can transfer from simulation to reality, their performance ranking may shift, emphasizing that high-fidelity simulators do not perfectly predict real-world efficacy and physical validation remains crucial.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in both simulated and physical domains. However, the majority of contemporary RL research is conducted in simulations, which can present challenges in translating algorithms and learned policies to physical reality. This predominance of simulation-based research often means that fundamental questions regarding learning directly from physical experience, where real-world dynamics, observational limitations, and environmental changes are present, are not extensively explored.

Existing efforts to validate RL algorithms on embodied systems have utilized various robot platforms, but these often entail high overhead costs, complex setup procedures, and difficulties in maintenance or adaptation, particularly for researchers without extensive robotics engineering backgrounds. Some platforms have been developed to mirror simulated domains (e.g., NoodleBot, RealAnt), while others utilize commercial robots. However, many current platforms may not address all the practical considerations for widespread adoption by the broader RL community, such as affordability, ease of assembly and maintenance, and seamless integration with standard RL software tools.

This work addresses these gaps by proposing an open-source, physical robot platform, the "Open Ant," specifically designed to bridge the divide between simulated and physical RL experimentation. It aims to lower the barrier for RL researchers to incorporate physical robot experiments into their research pipelines, thereby facilitating studies on continuous online learning and adaptation in real-world settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and validate a physical robot platform that simplifies the transition for reinforcement learning researchers from simulation-based studies to experiments involving physical robots. The authors aim to enable a broader segment of the RL community to engage with embodied intelligence, even if they lack extensive prior experience in robotics.

The motivation for this platform stems from several observations within the RL research landscape:
- **Uncertainty in Sim-to-Real Translation:** The reliance on simulations introduces uncertainty in how well algorithms and policies will perform when transferred to physical hardware. Discrepancies between simulated and real-world physics, sensor noise, and unmodeled dynamics can lead to suboptimal or unstable performance in reality.
- **High Barrier to Entry for Physical Robotics:** Researchers accustomed to simulations often face significant challenges when working with physical robots. These challenges include the substantial financial cost of hardware, the time and expertise required for assembly and troubleshooting, and the difficulties associated with repairing and maintaining complex robotic systems. These factors contribute to slower experimental iteration and reduced reusability of research platforms.
- **Limited Focus on Learning Directly from Physical Experience:** Despite historical motivation from animal learning and early computational RL successes on hardware, a relatively small proportion of current RL research focuses on algorithms that learn directly from physical experience in an online, continual manner. Such real-time learning in deployment environments is distinct from sim-to-real transfer or repeated offline retraining and presents unique research questions related to adaptation to unforeseen phenomena.
- **Need for a "Nimble Experimental Ecosystem":** There is a recognized need for research platforms that facilitate rapid user onboarding and allow for quick modifications and repairs. Current platforms often require specialized expertise or can become obsolete, hindering long-term research.

To address these motivations, the Open Ant platform was designed with specific goals:
- To provide an affordable and easily buildable physical robot.
- To mirror a popular simulated environment (the Gymnasium Ant) to allow for familiar experimental setups.
- To integrate with standard RL software tools and support diverse RL methods.
- To enable learning competent policies directly on hardware within practical timescales (e.g., approximately one hour).
- To facilitate policy transfer from simulation to reality.
- To support a robust experimental ecosystem through open-source design, ease of repair, and rapid user onboarding.

Ultimately, the research seeks to foster the study of fundamental questions in RL concerning "run-time" knowledge acquisition and adaptation in dynamic physical environments, complementing "design-time" considerations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research involved the development of the Open Ant platform, its accompanying simulation, and a series of experiments to evaluate its suitability for RL research.

#### 3.1. Platform Design and Construction

The Open Ant is an open-source quadrupedal robot and includes both a physical (Physical Ant) and a simulated (Simulated Ant) version. The physical robot's design is inspired by the Gymnasium Ant, a benchmark in continuous-control research, but scaled down to one-third the size (1.2 kg mass vs. 0.91 kg for Gymnasium Ant).

**Design Principles:**
- **Simplicity and Ease of Assembly:** The design prioritizes the use of commercial off-the-shelf (COTS) components, avoiding the need for soldering.
- **Long-term Operation:** The robot is powered directly from an AC wall outlet, eliminating battery management overhead.
- **Accessibility:** It connects to a personal computer via USB, removing the need for specialized embedded systems or complex network configurations.
- **Durability and Repairability:** The torso and legs are 3D printed, allowing for rapid component revision and localized repairs. Feet are equipped with 3D-printed thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) "socks" to increase ground friction and mitigate wear.

**Physical Attributes:** The Physical Ant features a spherical torso housing onboard electronics (IMU, USB communication converter, USB hub) and four articulated legs. Each leg has two metal-geared Dynamixel actuators (XC430-W240 at the hip, XM430-W210 at the knee). An onboard camera is included for future vision tasks. The total cost is approximately USD 2200. A lower-cost variant, the Physical Ant Lite (approx. USD 500), uses plastic-geared motors (XL430-W250) but is more susceptible to wear and overheating.

#### 3.2. Differences and Similarities with Gymnasium Ant

The Physical Ant maintains the Gymnasium Ant's software interface but differs in sensing, actuation, and reward structure:
- **Observations:** The Physical Ant has a compact 24-dimensional observation space (joint angles, joint angular velocities, torso angular velocity, linear acceleration from IMU, and a 2D unit vector for goal direction). This contrasts with the 105-dimensional observation space of the Gymnasium Ant, which includes hard-to-obtain physical quantities.
- **Actions:** The Physical Ant uses 8 continuously-valued, bounded dimensions representing position commands for hip and knee joints, employing position control. The Gymnasium Ant typically uses torque control.
- **Rewards and Tasks:** Instead of the Gymnasium Ant's default episodic forward-walking task with combined reward terms and resets, the Physical Ant uses a non-episodic "back-and-forth" walking task. The instantaneous reward is based on the robot's planar progress in a specified direction, which flips when the robot exits a defined circular arena and crosses a half-plane. This design minimizes manual intervention for resets.
- **Learning Arena:** An overhead webcam tracks fiducial markers on the robot and ground to compute position for reward signals and heading.

#### 3.3. Performance Measurement

Policy performance is measured as the average reward per second, computed over a sliding window of a specified duration (e.g., 120 seconds).

#### 3.4. Reinforcement Learning Methods

Two distinct RL algorithms were used to demonstrate the platform's compatibility:
1. **SARSA($\lambda$) (Discrete Action RL):** An on-policy action-value method with linear function approximation using tile coding. The robot's continuous action space was abstracted into a discrete set of 8 long-duration (0.5 s) motion primitives. The learning agent operated at a slower rate (0.5 s) than robot communication (0.05 s). Parameter optimization for SARSA($\lambda$) was conducted using Optuna in simulation.
2. **Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) (Continuous Action RL):** An off-policy, entropy-regularized deep RL algorithm. The CleanRL implementation was modified for the Physical Ant, including reduced random interaction steps, Layer Normalization in policy and critic networks for stability, reward scaling for training stability, and adjustments to the return definition to decouple it from time-discretization. SAC was evaluated on both Physical Ant and Physical Ant Lite.

#### 3.5. Common Methodology for Learning Experiments

All learning experiments utilized the back-and-forth task within a 0.3 m radius circle. Cables were positioned to minimize entanglement, but manual interventions were performed to disentangle the robot and reset it to the start configuration when necessary. Experiments for each algorithm ran for 80 minutes over five independent trials.

#### 3.6. Sim-to-Real Policy Transfer

To evaluate sim-to-real transfer, 2,304 policies were learned in simulation using SAC with various parameter configurations over 400,000 timesteps (approx. 13 hours) without domain randomization. Ten policies with varying simulated performance (13-23 cm/s) were selected and evaluated on the Physical Ant for 5,000 steps (10 minutes) without further learning or interventions.

#### 3.7. Platform Suitability Assessment

The platform's suitability for RL researchers was assessed by observing:
- **Ease of Use:** Time taken for assembly (2-5 hours after component purchase), and success rate of inexperienced users. This was tested through independent assembly across multiple locations and a winter school where 20 participants with limited robotics/RL experience successfully used and modified the platform within three days.
- **Reliability and Repairability:** Identification and mitigation of mechanical failure modes (e.g., foot-tip abrasion, joint fractures, motor contamination) through iterative hardware design improvements (e.g., TPU socks, reinforced interfaces, 3D printing for rapid revisions) and the use of COTS components.
- **Thermal and Electrical Analysis (Supplementary):** Stress tests were conducted to monitor motor temperatures, revealing overheating issues in the Physical Ant Lite. Electrical analysis identified intermittent communication dropouts in the Physical Ant due to power connector issues, which were resolved by direct wiring.

#### 3.8. System Identification (Supplementary)

The fidelity of the MuJoCo simulation model was improved through system identification for various parameters:
- **Motor Damping:** Calculated from datasheet values (e.g., 0.235 Nms/rad for XL430-W250-T).
- **Motor Stiffness (Kp):** Empirically measured by deflecting a leg and recording reaction forces (e.g., 19.499 Nm/rad for XL430-W250-T). These values were used as Kp gains in MuJoCo's PD control.
- **Velocity Gain (Kv) and Motor Delay:** Tuned to match physical hardware response; a one-timestep delay was noted for XL430-W250-T motors.
- **Foot-Ground Friction:** Empirically measured (μ = 0.6) on the experimental floor material.
- **Integrator Choice:** MuJoCo's `implicitfast` integrator was selected.
- **Contact Model Adjustments:** `solref` and `solimp` parameters were tuned to prevent simulation exploits where high-frequency jittering in simulation led to unrealizable forward motion. A time constant of 0.12 seconds was added to the motor model to compensate for aggressive closed-loop behavior.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Learning from Physical Experience with Open Ant

- **SARSA($\lambda$) Performance:** All five independent trials demonstrated that SARSA($\lambda$) could learn competent walking policies directly on the Physical Ant. Agents achieved average rewards of 2-4 cm/s within approximately one hour of on-hardware learning. Learning was reliably observed across trials, with only one interruption due to a leg failure that was repaired within 10 minutes.
- **SAC Performance:** SAC reliably learned stable, visually plausible walking behaviors on both the Physical Ant and Physical Ant Lite within 80 minutes across five trials. The SAC policies exhibited higher average rewards (up to ~15 cm/s) compared to SARSA($\lambda$), but also required more frequent interventions due to cable entanglement, likely due to increased robot movement. Ablation studies in simulation confirmed that Layer Normalization, earlier learning starts, and reward scaling contributed to faster and more stable learning with SAC.
- **Reward Drops:** Occasional temporary drops in learning performance, where the average reward decreased to near zero, were observed in both simulation and physical robot experiments. The agent typically recovered from these drops.

#### 4.2. Sim-to-Real Policy Transfer

- All ten policies learned in simulation using SAC, spanning a range of simulated performance (13-23 cm/s), successfully transferred to the Physical Ant, enabling the robot to walk in the correct direction. This indicates that the Simulated Ant model captures key dynamics of the Physical Ant.
- A change in the ranking of policies was observed when transferred to the physical robot. For instance, the policy with the highest reward in simulation performed as the sixth best on the Physical Ant. This suggests that simulation performance may not perfectly correlate with real-world performance, posing challenges for selecting optimal policies for hardware deployment based solely on simulation metrics. Less proficient policies in simulation also led to more cable entanglement on the physical robot.

#### 4.3. Platform Suitability for RL Researchers

- **Ease of Assembly and Use:** The Open Ant was independently assembled and tested across multiple locations within 2 to 5 hours. A winter school demonstrated that 20 participants, many with limited prior experience in robotics or RL, successfully used and modified the platform for RL experimentation (including sim-to-real transfer in episodic settings) within three days. This indicates a low barrier to entry for building and using the platform.
- **Platform Reliability and Repairability:** Iterative design improvements, including reinforced interfaces and TPU foot socks, addressed mechanical failures caused by ground impacts, joint loadings, and stress in 3D-printed components. The open-source nature and use of COTS components facilitated rapid repairs and revisions.
- **Thermal and Electrical Considerations:**
 - **Thermal:** Stress tests showed that the Physical Ant Lite (XL-series motors) frequently reached thermal cutoff (72°C) and shut down after approximately 30 minutes, due to underpowered motors operating beyond continuous torque capability. The Physical Ant (XM-series motors) tolerated higher temperatures (80°C limit), and integrating heat sinks further reduced temperature spikes, preventing shutdowns during sustained operation.
 - **Electrical:** Intermittent communication dropouts in the Physical Ant were diagnosed using an oscilloscope, revealing brief supply voltage drops caused by the barrel power connector under vibration. Replacing this with a direct connection to the board's power terminal eliminated the issue.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Open Ant platform addresses a recognized need within the reinforcement learning community by providing an accessible and robust tool for conducting experiments on physical robots. Its open-source design, affordability, ease of assembly, and integration with standard RL software are intended to lower the historical barriers to entry for RL researchers without specialized robotics backgrounds.

The successful demonstration of learning competent walking policies directly on the physical robot, using both SARSA($\lambda$) and SAC, within practical timescales (approximately one hour), indicates that the platform can facilitate rapid experimental iteration. This capability is significant for enabling researchers to move beyond purely simulated environments and engage with the complexities of real-world learning.

The findings regarding sim-to-real policy transfer, particularly the observed shift in policy ranking between simulation and reality, highlight the importance of physical validation. This discrepancy underscores that a high-fidelity simulator, while beneficial for initial policy development, may not perfectly predict real-world performance. The platform offers a means to investigate these sim-to-real gaps more systematically and develop algorithms that are robust to such differences.

The design features, such as the non-episodic back-and-forth task, mitigate common practical challenges in robot experimentation by reducing the need for frequent manual resets. Furthermore, the iterative improvements to hardware design based on observed failure modes (mechanical, thermal, electrical) contribute to the platform's long-term reliability and maintainability, which are critical for sustained research. The documented experiences with user onboarding, particularly at the winter school, suggest that the Open Ant can indeed support a nimble experimental ecosystem.

Potential impacts include:
- **Democratization of Robot RL Research:** By making physical robot experimentation more accessible, the Open Ant may enable a broader range of RL researchers to explore embodied intelligence, fostering innovation in areas currently dominated by simulation.
- **Advancement in Online/Continual Learning:** The platform is suitable for studying algorithms that learn continuously in deployment, adapting to dynamic and imperfectly modeled environments, which is a key area for advancing robust and autonomous intelligent agents.
- **Improved Sim-to-Real Methodologies:** It provides a testbed for developing and validating techniques for more effective sim-to-real transfer, and for understanding the limitations of simulators.
- **Contribution to Research Infrastructure:** As an open-source project, the Open Ant encourages community contributions, potentially leading to further improvements and broader adoption as a standardized platform, similar to benchmark environments in other areas of AI.

The research also identifies several practical concerns and future directions, such as managing random exploration safely, designing tasks to minimize unrecoverable states, investigating the impact of compute speed and timing on learning, and understanding how environment changes over time affect continual learning. These insights contribute to the practical considerations for future research in physical robot reinforcement learning.
