# RLAC: Reinforcement Learning with Adversarial Critic for Free-Form Generation Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> RLAC (Reinforcement Learning with Adversarial Critic) introduces a framework to fine-tune large language models for open-ended generation tasks by employing a dynamically adapting adversarial critic that identifies failure modes. The method improved factuality in text generation and achieved higher Pass@1 scores in code generation, notably reducing verification costs by up to 97.5% compared to prior approaches.

## Problem

- Existing LLM post-training paradigms like SFT, RLHF, and reward models struggle with the scalability and cost of human data, or are susceptible to 'reward hacking' for complex, open-ended generation.
- Defining a singular, clear reward function for free-form outputs with numerous, often implicit, evaluation rubrics is challenging and prone to misalignment.
- Exhaustive verification of all possible criteria for generated outputs (e.g., test cases for code) is computationally intractable, creating a significant bottleneck for practical application.

## Method

- ==RLAC== reformulates the training objective as an ==adversarial min-max game== between a generator (the LLM being fine-tuned) and a critic (an LLM that learns to propose failure-mode rubrics).
- The critic adversarially generates ==natural language rubrics== that represent properties the generated output is likely to violate, focusing verification efforts on weaknesses.
- An objective, external ==validator== provides binary ground-truth feedback on these proposed rubrics, which is then used to update both the generator and critic via ==Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)==.

## Results

- RLAC achieved the highest FactScore (e.g., **0.889**) in factual text generation tasks, simultaneously reducing the number of verification calls by up to **5.7 times** compared to alternative methods.
- In code generation, RLAC consistently produced superior average Pass@1 scores (e.g., **53.2** for Base models) while reducing verification costs (total test cases executed) by **97.5%** compared to enumerative RL baselines.
- Ablation studies demonstrated that an adversarial critic is crucial; static critics led to generator exploitation and diminished performance, validating the need for dynamic adaptation to maintain effective supervision.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The dynamic, adversarial nature of the critic enables it to continuously adapt to the generator's evolving capabilities, providing 'on-policy' feedback that mitigates reward hacking and drives genuine quality improvement.
> - Framing the problem as an adversarial game with targeted, verifiable feedback from an external validator allows for scalable reinforcement learning post-training in domains where exhaustive verification is impractical.
> - DPO provides a stable and effective mechanism for policy optimization for both the generator and critic, directly leveraging binary preference signals derived from validator checks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research on RLAC addresses a critical and growing challenge in the field of large language models (LLMs): the effective post-training of models for **open-ended and free-form generation tasks**. While LLMs have achieved remarkable success, their application to tasks requiring diverse, often implicit, and potentially uncountable evaluation rubrics remains a significant hurdle.

Current post-training paradigms face distinct limitations:
- **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** This traditional method relies heavily on manually curated data, which becomes prohibitively expensive and unscalable for complex, open-ended generation where defining "correctness" exhaustively is difficult.
- **Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Reward Models:** Approaches like RLHF (e.g., Christiano et al., 2017; Ouyang et al., 2022) train a separate reward model from human preferences. While efficient, this paradigm is susceptible to **reward hacking**. The learned reward model, being a proxy, can misalign with true task objectives when the generator explores beyond its training distribution, leading the LLM to optimize for the proxy score rather than genuine quality. Furthermore, effectively combining multiple, potentially conflicting rubrics into a single scalar reward is challenging and often prompt-specific.
- **Enumerative Verification:** This strategy involves explicitly listing and checking every possible evaluation criterion (rubric) for a generated output (e.g., Min et al., 2023). While providing high fidelity to the underlying task, it suffers from severe scalability issues. For tasks like complex code generation, the number of edge cases or test conditions can be infinite or computationally intractable to enumerate and verify exhaustively. This becomes an "intractable verification bottleneck," making optimization impractical.
- **LLM-as-a-Judge:** Using LLMs themselves to evaluate outputs (Zheng et al., 2023) also faces similar issues to reward models, being prone to reward hacking and struggling with nuanced, implicit rubrics without external ground truth.

RLAC positions itself as a novel solution that bridges the gap between the efficiency of reward models and the accuracy of enumerative verification, while mitigating their respective drawbacks. It moves beyond static reward functions and exhaustive checks by introducing a dynamic, adversarial approach to verification, making RL post-training more scalable and robust for free-form generation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of the RLAC paper is to develop a **scalable and effective reinforcement learning post-training framework for LLMs that produce open-ended, free-form outputs**. This framework aims to overcome the inherent difficulties of defining and applying a reward signal when outputs must satisfy a multitude of diverse, often implicit, and potentially uncountable task-specific evaluation rubrics.

The key motivations driving this research stem from the limitations of existing methods:
1. **Addressing the Reward Function Challenge:** For many free-form tasks, a single, clear-cut reward function is elusive. Outputs are judged by numerous criteria (rubrics), and how these rubrics should be combined into a scalar reward is often context-dependent and difficult to pre-define.
2. **Overcoming Verification Scalability Issues:** Manually enumerating and verifying all potential rubrics for every generated output is computationally prohibitive or even impossible for complex tasks (e.g., all test cases for a piece of code). This makes fine-tuning with RL on such tasks intractable.
3. **Mitigating Reward Hacking and Misalignment:** Existing reward model approaches often lead to generators "gaming" the reward signal without actual improvement in output quality, especially as the generator explores new output spaces. There is a need for a training signal that is grounded in verifiable facts and adapts to the generator's evolving weaknesses.
4. **Enabling RL for Underserved Domains:** The paper aims to extend the benefits of RL post-training, which has been highly successful in domains with easily verifiable outcomes (e.g., math or specific coding problems), to broader domains where success criteria are more ambiguous and complex.

In essence, RLAC seeks to provide a principled way to obtain prompt-specific, verifiable, and scalable supervision for LLMs in free-form generation, thereby making RL post-training applicable to a much wider range of real-world applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

RLAC (Reinforcement Learning with Adversarial Critic) proposes a novel adversarial game formulation to address the challenges of reward design and verification scalability in free-form generation tasks. The core idea is to establish a dynamic interaction between a generator and a learned critic, with an external validator providing ground-truth feedback.

The problem is reformulated from the original objective (Equation 1), which aims to maximize the probability of outputs satisfying *all* rubrics in `C(s)`, into an equivalent min-max game (Equation 4):
$\pi_g = \arg \max_{\pi} \min_{\pi_c} E_{s \sim S} [E_{a \sim \pi(\cdot|s)} E_{c \sim \pi_c(\cdot|s,a)} [R(s, a, c)]]$
This reformulation signifies that the generator ($\pi_g$) aims to produce outputs that are robust even against the "worst-case" rubric proposed by an adversarial critic ($\pi_c$). The critic, in turn, learns to identify the most likely failure mode (`c`) for a given instruction-generation pair (`s, a`).

RLAC consists of three interacting components:

1. **Generator ($\pi_g$):** This is the LLM being fine-tuned. Given an instruction `s`, it produces a free-form output `a`. The generator's goal is to improve its ability to satisfy all task-specific rubrics.
2. **Critic ($\pi_c$):** This is also a pre-trained LLM, but it is fine-tuned to act as an adversarial judge. For a given instruction `s` and a generated output `a`, the critic is prompted to generate a natural language rubric `c` that represents a verifiable property the output is likely to violate. The critic's role is to pinpoint potential weaknesses.
3. **Validator:** This is an external, domain-specific tool or process that provides a binary (0 or 1) ground-truth verification for a proposed rubric `c` against a generated output `a` for instruction `s`. Unlike reward models, the validator is objective and not learned. Examples include rule-based checkers for factual claims or a code executor for programming tests.

The training process (Algorithm 1) involves an iterative online RL loop that alternates between policy evaluation and improvement for both the generator and the critic:

- **Policy Evaluation (Generator):** For each instruction `s`, the generator $\pi_g$ produces `K` candidate outputs `a_i`. For each `(s, a_i)`, the adversarial critic $\pi_c$ proposes a criterion `c_i`. An external validator then checks `R(s, a_i, c_i)`, yielding a binary reward `r_i`.
- **Policy Improvement (Generator):** The generator is updated using the **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) objective (Equation 5)**. Outputs `a_i` with `r_i = 1` are treated as preferred (`a+`), and those with `r_i = 0` are treated as dispreferred (`a-`). DPO allows direct policy optimization from these binary preference signals, providing stability without explicit reward scaling or KL-penalty tuning.
- **Policy Evaluation (Critic - Optional but implemented):** For each `(s, a)` pair, the critic samples `N` criteria `c_j`. The validator checks these.
- **Policy Improvement (Critic - Optional but implemented):** The critic is updated using its own **DPO objective (Equation 6)**. Criteria `c_j` that are valid and *not* satisfied by the generator are treated as preferred (`c+`), encouraging the critic to identify genuine errors. Criteria that are invalid or already satisfied are treated as dispreferred (`c-`).

This joint, adversarial training ensures that the critic continuously learns to find the generator's weakest points, while the generator learns to overcome those weaknesses. The dynamic nature of the critic avoids reward hacking by ensuring that the reward signal is always "on-policy" and tailored to the generator's current behavior, rather than being static and prone to exploitation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

RLAC was evaluated on two distinct free-form generation tasks: factual text generation (an enumerable-but-expensive verification scenario) and code generation (a non-enumerable, intractable verification scenario). The results consistently demonstrate RLAC's superior performance and efficiency compared to existing paradigms.

#### 4.1. Factual Text Generation (Biography Generation)

- **Task:** Generate 4- or 8-sentence biographies using Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B models.
- **Metrics:** FactScore (factual precision), number of correct/incorrect facts, and number of validator calls.
- **Key Results:**
 - **Highest Factuality:** RLAC achieved the highest FactScore across all settings. For instance, on Qwen3-8B with 8-sentence generation, RLAC reached a FactScore of 0.889, surpassing FactTune-FS (0.867) and ArmoRM (0.723).
 - **Significant Verification Efficiency:** RLAC dramatically reduced the number of verification calls. In the Qwen3-8B, 8-sentence setting, it required only 76,800 calls compared to 438,949 for FactTune-FS, representing a 5.7x reduction. This efficiency gain scaled with task complexity.
 - **Productive Training Dynamics:** Initial training rounds showed a temporary dip in FactScore as the critic was still learning, followed by a sharp improvement. RLAC exhibited productive exploration (KL divergence from base model increased alongside FactScore gains), whereas ArmoRM (a reward model method) showed increasing KL divergence without corresponding factuality improvements, indicative of reward hacking.
- **Ablation Study:**
 - **Noisy Validator:** Demonstrated the crucial role of reliable external verification; random correctness labels destabilized training and degraded performance below the baseline.
 - **Static Critic:** A non-adaptive critic led to superficially high FactScore by generating fewer facts rather than genuinely improving accuracy. The generator quickly learned to evade the static critic's patterns, leading to weak and diminishing supervision. In contrast, the adversarial critic maintained consistent pressure for true quality improvement.

#### 4.2. Code Generation

- **Task:** Solve programming problems from HumanEval, MBPP, BigCodeBench, and LiveCodeBench using Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Base and Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct.
- **Metrics:** Pass@1 score and total number of test cases executed (validator calls).
- **Key Results:**
 - **Superior Performance with Less Data:** Despite using only 9% of the training data (2,000 problems vs. 22,000 for baselines), RLAC achieved the highest average Pass@1 scores (53.2 for Base and 56.6 for Instruct models), consistently outperforming AceCoder-Rule (enumerative RL) and AceCoder-RM (reward model).
 - **Exceptional Verification Efficiency:** RLAC achieved higher performance with a 97.5% reduction in verification costs compared to AceCoder-Rule (192,000 test cases vs. 7.86 million).
 - **Robustness to Noisy Validation:** While AceCoder-RM showed performance degradation due to reward hacking under noisy validation, RLAC consistently improved, demonstrating its robustness. The adaptive critic maintained alignment even in imperfect validation environments.
- **Ablation Study:**
 - **Static Critic vs. Adversarial Critic:** The static critic's error detection rate dropped significantly as the generator exploited its fixed patterns, resulting in minimal performance gains (+0.6% Pass@1). The adversarial critic, by continuously adapting, maintained a stable error detection rate and drove substantial performance improvements (+1.8% Pass@1), underscoring the necessity of dynamic adaptation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RLAC framework represents a significant advancement in the field of large language model post-training, particularly for complex, open-ended generation tasks. Its key contributions and potential impacts are:

1. **Scalable and Aligned RL Post-Training:** RLAC successfully addresses the long-standing challenge of applying reinforcement learning to free-form generation where exhaustive verification is infeasible and fixed reward functions are prone to misalignment. By framing the problem as an adversarial game with dynamic rubric verification, it enables scalable and effective training for LLMs in complex domains.
2. **Mitigation of Reward Hacking:** The dynamic, adversarial critic is a powerful mechanism to prevent reward hacking. Unlike static reward models or fixed sets of verification rules, the critic continuously adapts to the generator's evolving capabilities, pushing it to overcome genuine weaknesses rather than exploit superficial patterns in the reward signal. This leads to more robust and higher-quality generated outputs.
3. **Enhanced Verification Efficiency:** RLAC demonstrates remarkable efficiency gains by intelligently focusing verification efforts. Instead of exhaustively checking all possible rubrics, the critic pinpoints the most likely failure modes, drastically reducing the computational cost of feedback while maintaining or even improving accuracy. This makes RL post-training more practical for resource-intensive tasks.
4. **Broad Applicability to Diverse Domains:** The empirical success on both factual text generation (enumerable-but-expensive) and code generation (non-enumerable) highlights RLAC's versatility. This suggests that the approach can generalize to a wide array of other open-ended tasks characterized by multiple, often implicit, or intractable evaluation criteria, such as story generation, scientific paper writing, creative content generation, or sophisticated agent decision-making.
5. **New Paradigm for LLM Evaluation and Improvement:** RLAC introduces a novel paradigm where the "judge" or "verifier" is not a static tool but an active, adaptive participant in the learning process. This transforms LLM-as-a-judge concepts from passive evaluators into active agents that strategically allocate verification resources, leading to more targeted and efficient learning.
6. **Future Research Directions:** RLAC opens avenues for further research into more sophisticated adversarial training strategies, diverse critic architectures, and its application to even more challenging, multi-modal, or long-horizon generation tasks.

In conclusion, RLAC offers a robust and efficient solution for optimizing LLMs on free-form generation, pushing the boundaries of what is achievable with RL post-training and paving the way for more capable and reliable AI agents in complex, real-world applications.
