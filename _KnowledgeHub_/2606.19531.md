# ImageWAM: Do World Action Models Really Need Video Generation, or Just Image Editing?

> [!summary] Summary
> ImageWAM reconfigures pretrained image editing models to act as efficient visual backbones for robot action prediction, demonstrating that compact, instruction-conditioned visual transformation representations can replace full video generation in World Action Models. This approach achieved up to 93.56% success on RoboTwin 2.0 and 83.1% on LIBERO-Plus, while reducing inference latency by approximately 75% and computational FLOPs by about 83% compared to video-generation WAMs.

## Problem

- Existing World Action Models (WAMs) often rely on computationally expensive video generation as a generative prior for robot action prediction.
- Full video prediction requires modeling irrelevant details like appearance and background changes, which diverts model capacity from action-relevant information.
- The inherent difficulty of generating physically consistent future videos can lead to accumulated errors that mislead downstream action prediction modules.

## Method

- Repurposes pretrained image editing models as visual backbones, extracting compact "==editing-aware representations==" from their intermediate key-value caches.
- Attaches an "action expert" module to the image editing backbone, which predicts action chunks conditioned on these instruction-guided visual transformation features.
- Utilizes a joint flow-matching objective to train the image editing branch to predict a task-relevant future endpoint frame and the action expert to generate actions.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **93.56%** on RoboTwin **2.0** (randomized setting) and **98.4%** on the standard LIBERO benchmark.
- Demonstrated superior performance on the challenging LIBERO-Plus benchmark, reaching an **83.1%** success rate with the FLUX.2 4B backbone, significantly outperforming FastWAM (**51.5%**) and OpenVLA-OFT (**69.6%**).
- Reduced inference latency by approximately **75%** and computational FLOPs by about **83%** compared to video-generation WAMs, enabling real-time operation at 69-77ms with optimizations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Image editing models provide a more suitable generative prior for language-conditioned robot manipulation by focusing on instruction-guided visual modifications rather than comprehensive future video prediction.
> - Extracting compact "editing-aware representations" during the image editing process enables a significantly more efficient inference path compared to decoding dense spatio-temporal tokens.
> - The task of predicting a single future endpoint frame that summarizes the task-specified visual transformation is a more effective and less error-prone intermediate reasoning step for robot control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot policy learning has increasingly explored the use of video generation models as backbones for what are termed World Action Models (WAMs). This approach is motivated by the ability of video pretraining to expose models to rich visual dynamics (e.g., object motion, physical interaction) and to support a "reason-before-act" paradigm, where a policy first imagines future scene changes to guide action prediction. Generative pretraining on large video datasets offers scalability and a connection between visual world modeling and robot control.

However, the paper identifies several limitations inherent in video-based WAMs. Firstly, generating dense, multi-frame future tokens for a complete video is computationally expensive, leading to high inference costs that can hinder real-time robot control. Secondly, full video prediction requires modeling various factors such as appearance details, background changes, and camera motion, many of which may be irrelevant to the robot's next action. This can divert model capacity to extraneous details. Thirdly, generating physically consistent videos is a challenging proxy task, especially for fine-grained manipulation where minor interactions are crucial. Errors in imagined videos can propagate and mislead the downstream action predictor. These limitations suggest a potential mismatch between the objective of generating photorealistic future videos and the actual requirements for effective robot action prediction.

ImageWAM proposes an alternative by leveraging image editing models. It argues that for many manipulation tasks, the essential visual information required for action prediction is not a complete future video, but rather an instruction-guided transformation from the current observation to a desired visual state. Image editing models are specifically trained to transform a source image based on a language instruction, thereby aligning well with this requirement. This work positions itself as a re-evaluation of the foundational generative prior for WAMs, questioning the necessity of full video generation and proposing image editing as a more direct and efficient alternative for visual world modeling in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether World Action Models (WAMs) truly necessitate video generation, or if image editing can serve as a more effective and efficient visual generative prior for robot action prediction. The authors argue that conventional video-based WAMs, while conceptually appealing for their "reason-before-act" paradigm, suffer from three main drawbacks:

1. **High Inference Cost:** Video generation involves predicting dense spatio-temporal tokens across multiple future frames, leading to substantial computational expense and latency, which are critical issues for real-time robot control.
2. **Allocation of Capacity to Irrelevant Details:** The objective of generating a complete, photorealistic future video compels models to learn and predict details (e.g., background changes, subtle temporal smoothness) that may not be directly relevant to the robot's specific action. This can dilute the model's capacity and focus away from critical action-relevant information.
3. **Risk of Misleading Action Prediction:** Predicting physically consistent and accurate future videos, especially for long horizons or fine-grained manipulation, is an inherently difficult task. Errors or artifacts in the imagined future video can accumulate and subsequently mislead the action prediction module, leading to task failures.

Motivated by these limitations, ImageWAM proposes that image editing models offer a more suitable generative prior for language-conditioned robot manipulation. The key motivations for this alternative approach are:

1. **Strong Instruction-to-Change Alignment:** Image editing models are specifically pretrained to execute visual modifications based on language instructions. This objective inherently encourages the model to focus on *what* should change, *where* it should change, and *how* the change is specified by the instruction, which is directly relevant to robot task execution.
2. **Easier and More Action-Relevant Proxy Task:** Instead of modeling entire temporal trajectories, an image editing model concentrates on the visual difference between a current state and an instruction-consistent target state. This simplifies the generative task by focusing on the essential transformation rather than complete temporal dynamics, thus avoiding wasted capacity on irrelevant temporal details and reducing the likelihood of generating inaccurate visual futures.
3. **Compact Inference Path:** Image editing models can provide "editing-aware representations" – internal features that encode the intended visual transformation – without requiring the explicit decoding of dense, multi-frame videos or even a full edited image. These compact representations can directly serve as context for action prediction, enabling a more efficient inference process suitable for real-time robotic applications.

Therefore, the primary objective of ImageWAM is to repurpose pretrained image editing models as instruction-conditioned visual backbones for robot action prediction, specifically by utilizing their intermediate features as a compact and transformation-aware representation for direct policy learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ImageWAM proposes a framework that reconfigures pretrained image editing models to serve as backbones for robot action prediction. The methodology focuses on extracting and utilizing "editing-aware representations" from these models rather than relying on explicit video or image generation during inference.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is defined as language-conditioned robot manipulation, where at each timestep $t$, the robot receives an image observation $o_t$ and a task instruction $l$, then predicts an action chunk $a_{t:t+H}$ (actions over a horizon $H$).
Traditional video-generation-based WAMs typically follow a sequence: $(o_t, l) \rightarrow \hat{o}_{t+1:t+H+1} \rightarrow a_{t:t+H}$, where $\hat{o}_{t+1:t+H+1}$ represents a predicted future visual trajectory.
ImageWAM simplifies this intermediate visual reasoning by predicting only a single future endpoint frame: $(o_t, l) \rightarrow \hat{o}_{\text{edit}} \equiv \hat{o}_{t+H+1} \rightarrow a_{t:t+H}$. This $\hat{o}_{\text{edit}}$ is a source-conditioned frame summarizing the task-specified visual transformation, serving as a compact world-action intermediate.

#### 3.2. ImageWAM Architecture

ImageWAM is built upon a variant of existing image editing models, such as OmniGen2, Ovis-U1, or Flux2. The core idea is to attach an "action expert" to the image editing model's branch.
Given the current observation $o_t$ and task instruction $l$, the image editing backbone processes these inputs. During the denoising process of the image editing backbone, intermediate transformer key-value (KV) caches are produced. ImageWAM reuses these caches, denoted as $C_{\tau}^{\text{edit}} = \{(K_{\tau}^{\ell}, V_{\tau}^{\ell})\}_{L=1}^{L}$, as a conditioning context for the action expert. $L$ is the number of transformer layers, and $\tau$ is a sampled denoising timestep. These caches inherently contain task-conditioned visual transformation information derived from the interaction between the visual latent and the task instruction within the editing backbone.

Key architectural design choices:
- **Frozen Components:** The Vision-Language Model (VLM) and multimodal understanding components of the editing model, including those for encoding task instructions and visual context, are kept frozen. This ensures stable language-vision conditioning.
- **Trainable Components:** Only the diffusion-based image generation branch and the action expert are updated during training. The diffusion branch learns to predict task-relevant future frames and produce useful editing caches, while the action expert learns to translate these into actions.
- **Action Expert Initialization:** The Action DiT (Diffusion Transformer) within the action expert uses the same architecture as the image editing model. Its weights are initialized by copying and interpolating weights from the image editing model, with additional projection layers for action inputs/outputs. Cross-modal attention is enabled while maintaining a manageable size for the Action DiT.

#### 3.3. Action Prediction and Training

**Image Editing Objective:** The editing branch is trained to predict a task-relevant future endpoint frame $o_{t+H+1}$. Using a flow-matching objective, the diffusion image branch predicts the velocity field based on an interpolated image latent $z_r$ (derived from the target latent $z_{t+H+1}^*$ and noise $\epsilon_z$). The loss $L_{\text{img}}$ enforces this prediction.

**Action Flow Matching:** The action expert generates an action chunk $a_{t:t+H}$ using a separate flow-matching objective. It predicts a velocity field $v_\theta(a_s, s | o_t, l, C_{\tau}^{\text{edit}})$ for an interpolated action sample $a_s$, conditioned on the current observation, task instruction, and the sampled editing context cache $C_{\tau}^{\text{edit}}$. The action loss is $L_{\text{act}}$.
The joint training objective $L = L_{\text{act}} + L_{\text{img}}$ optimizes both the diffusion image branch and the action expert. Sampling $\tau$ (image editing denoising timestep) during training exposes the action expert to varied editing contexts.

#### 3.4. Efficient Inference

A critical aspect of ImageWAM is its efficient inference procedure. Unlike video-generation WAMs that denoise and decode dense spatio-temporal tokens across multiple future frames, ImageWAM simplifies this process:
1. It selects a fixed editing denoising timestep $\tau^{\star}$.
2. Only a single forward step of the editing branch is performed to obtain $C_{\tau^{\star}}^{\text{edit}} = f_{\tau^{\star}}^{\text{edit}}(o_t, l)$.
3. The action expert then denoises action samples, conditioned directly on this compact $C_{\tau^{\star}}^{\text{edit}}$, to generate the action chunk $\hat{a}_{t:t+H}$.

This design avoids full future-video generation and the decoding of a complete edited image, making inference significantly more compact and efficient. For comparison, a Fast-WAM-style baseline is implemented where a video-WAM backbone also avoids future video token denoising at test time, conditioning its action expert only on current-context KV caches.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ImageWAM was evaluated across multiple benchmarks and real-world setups, demonstrating its effectiveness and efficiency compared to existing methods.

#### 4.1. Performance on RoboTwin 2.0

On the RoboTwin 2.0 benchmark, designed for bimanual robot manipulation with diverse scenes, ImageWAM achieved an average success rate of 93.20% in the clean setting and 93.56% in the randomized setting. These results indicate an improvement over VLA baselines and comparable performance to video-generation-based WAMs, without the need for dense future-video token prediction. The Flux.2 4B variant showed stronger performance (93.20% Clean, 93.56% Rand.) than the OmniGen2 variant (92.48% Clean, 92.80% Rand.).

#### 4.2. Performance on LIBERO & LIBERO-Plus

On the standard LIBERO benchmark, ImageWAM demonstrated strong performance across Spatial, Object, Goal, and Long suites, achieving an average success rate of 98.4%. This performance is competitive with video-generation-based WAMs and pretrained VLA models, despite ImageWAM not using additional policy pretraining.

For the more challenging LIBERO-Plus benchmark, which introduces increased visual and layout variations, ImageWAM maintained an average success rate of 83.1% when using the FLUX.2 4B backbone. This suggests that the source-conditioned editing context helps the policy generalize by focusing on task-relevant visual changes rather than specific visual configurations. ImageWAM (FLUX.2 4B) significantly outperformed FastWAM (51.5%) and other VLA baselines like UniVLA (42.9%) and OpenVLA-OFT (69.6%) on this benchmark.

#### 4.3. Performance on Real-world Experiments

Evaluations on a real-world dual-arm Dobot XTrainer robot platform across four complex tasks (Stack Three Bowls, Fold Towel, Open Drawer & Store Marker, Hang Cup On Rack) showed ImageWAM achieving an average success rate of 84.5%. This surpassed $\pi_0$ (55.8%), $\pi_{0.5}$ (72.3%), and FastWAM (79.0%). ImageWAM performed best on all four tasks. Notably, it showed the largest gain on "Fold Towel" (9 points over FastWAM), indicating the utility of editing-based context for deformable-object manipulation. For "Open Drawer & Store Marker," both WAM-style methods significantly outperformed $\pi_0$, suggesting that world-action reasoning can mitigate visual occlusion challenges.

#### 4.4. Analysis

- **Attention Visualization:** Attention maps revealed that ImageWAM concentrates its attention on task-relevant change regions, such as manipulated objects, target receptacles, and contact areas, while largely ignoring irrelevant background. This supports the hypothesis that editing caches encode source-grounded and change-centric visual information.
- **Latency and FLOPs:** ImageWAM significantly reduced inference latency and FLOPs compared to video-generation WAMs. It achieved a latency of 263 ms and FLOPs of 9.7 (on A6000 GPU) for a single editing-branch forward step, compared to 1081 ms and 63.65 FLOPs for a video-generation WAM that denoises dense spatio-temporal tokens. This represents a latency reduction to approximately 1/4 and FLOPs reduction to 1/6. Further optimization with `torch.compile` and CUDA graphs reduced latency to 69-77ms.
- **Qualitative Analysis of Future-Video Artifacts:** The paper highlighted that video-generation-based WAMs can produce distorted future frames with artifacts (e.g., distorted geometry, inconsistent spatial layout), which can mislead the action expert. ImageWAM, by avoiding explicit future-video token instantiation and decoding, naturally bypasses this issue.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study

- **Effect of Different Editing Models (Q1):** ImageWAM's performance was evaluated with different editing backbones: OmniGen2, Ovis-U1, and FLUX.2 4B. All variants outperformed FastWAM and most VLA baselines on LIBERO-Plus without policy pretraining. OmniGen2 and Ovis-U1 yielded similar average success rates (71.8% and 71.2%), while FLUX.2 4B achieved 83.1%, demonstrating that ImageWAM is not dependent on a specific editing model and benefits from stronger editing backbones.
- **Unified vs. Decoupled Models (Q2):** ImageWAM, with its decoupled approach (frozen VLM for understanding, trainable diffusion for generation/action), outperformed unified understanding-and-generation models like UniVLA and BagelVLA under comparable non-keyframe future prediction settings on LIBERO and RoboTwin2.0. This suggests that jointly optimizing understanding and generation in a single, fully shared model can introduce interference.
- **Effect of Editing Backbone Size (Q3):** Increasing the editing backbone capacity from FLUX.2 4B to FLUX.2 9B on LIBERO-Plus improved the average success rate from 83.1% to 85.21%. Gains were observed across Robot, Language, Background, and Layout perturbations, indicating that larger editing models provide stronger instruction-conditioned visual context. However, the improvements were not uniform across all perturbation types (e.g., Camera, Light, Noise showed less consistent gains).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in ImageWAM offers a substantial contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by re-evaluating the foundational components of World Action Models (WAMs). Its primary significance lies in demonstrating that complex robot control tasks, which have traditionally relied on costly and often inaccurate video generation for world modeling, can be effectively addressed by repurposing image editing models.

The potential impacts are multi-faceted:

1. **Increased Efficiency for Real-time Robotics:** By replacing dense, multi-frame video prediction with the extraction of compact, instruction-conditioned image editing caches, ImageWAM achieves significant reductions in inference latency (up to 4x) and computational FLOPs (up to 6x). This efficiency is critical for deploying robot policies in real-world scenarios that demand real-time responsiveness, such as industrial automation or assistive robotics.
2. **Enhanced Task Relevance and Accuracy:** Image editing models are inherently trained to focus on instruction-guided visual transformations. This aligns more directly with the core requirements of many manipulation tasks, where the objective is to achieve a specific visual change. By leveraging this prior, ImageWAM mitigates the issue of model capacity being diverted to action-irrelevant temporal or appearance details, and avoids the accumulation of visual artifacts that can arise in video generation, leading to more reliable action prediction.
3. **Improved Robustness and Generalization:** The empirical results on LIBERO-Plus demonstrate that ImageWAM maintains strong performance under various visual perturbations and domain shifts. This suggests that the source-conditioned and change-centric representations learned by image editing models facilitate better generalization across diverse environments, a key challenge in robotics.
4. **Broader Utility of Image Models in Robotics:** This research broadens the application spectrum of large pretrained image models beyond their traditional generative or discriminative tasks. It establishes image editing as a viable, and in some cases superior, generative prior for embodied AI, potentially opening new avenues for incorporating other types of image-centric generative models into robot learning pipelines.
5. **A New "Reason-Before-Act" Paradigm:** ImageWAM proposes an alternative formulation of the "reason-before-act" paradigm, shifting from imagining a full future trajectory to reasoning about the essential visual transformation. This conceptual shift could inspire new architectural designs and training methodologies for robot policies, emphasizing targeted visual change understanding over comprehensive scene dynamics.
6. **Reduced Training Data Requirements:** The authors highlight that ImageWAM achieves competitive performance without requiring additional embodied policy pretraining data, relying only on downstream benchmark demonstrations. This could lower the barrier to entry for developing capable robot policies by reducing the need for extensive, task-specific pretraining datasets.

In conclusion, ImageWAM presents a compelling argument and practical framework for leveraging image editing models in robot control, offering a more efficient, robust, and task-relevant approach to world action modeling. Its success could lead to a re-evaluation of current dominant paradigms in robot policy learning and accelerate the development of more capable and deployable robotic systems.
