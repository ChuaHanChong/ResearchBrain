# A Simple "Motivation" Can Enhance Reinforcement Finetuning of Large Reasoning Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Nanyang Technological University and Alibaba Group developed Motivation-enhanced Reinforcement Finetuning (MeRF), a method that injects natural language descriptions of reward functions into LLM prompts during finetuning. MeRF improved reasoning performance by an average of 3.40% in pass@1 on MATH benchmarks and showed substantial gains on logic puzzles and numerical reasoning tasks.

## Problem

- Standard Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for large language models (LLMs) suffers from inefficient trial-and-error exploration.
- LLMs lack explicit awareness of the optimization objective during the reasoning process, often learning from sparse and fragmented reward signals.
- This leads to high computational costs, extensive data requirements, and potential convergence to suboptimal solutions.

## Method

- Introduction of ==Motivation-enhanced== Reinforcement Finetuning (**MeRF**), which augments the system prompt with a natural language description of the reward function.
- MeRF enables LLMs to be explicitly aware of the optimization objective during reinforcement finetuning, leveraging their in-context learning capabilities.
- The approach aims to guide the model's generation process more directly, incentivizing desired outputs from both explicit motivation and external rewards.

## Results

- MeRF consistently outperformed RLVR baselines, demonstrating significant performance gains across K&K Logic Puzzles, MATH Benchmarks (AIME24&25, AMC23, MATH500), and CountDown tasks.
- On MATH benchmarks, MeRF achieved average improvements of **3.40%** in pass@1, **1.13%** in pass@2, **3.50%** in pass@4, and **4.50%** in pass@8.
- The method fosters more effective exploration and maintains higher entropy during training, guiding models toward globally optimal behaviors and showing robustness to various motivation qualities, even adverse ones.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The primary performance gains from MeRF stem from the motivation-enhanced finetuning process itself, rather than solely from in-context motivation during inference.
> - Explicit in-context motivation leads to improved exploration capabilities, evidenced by higher pass@k performance and maintained entropy during training, preventing collapse to suboptimal solutions.
> - Large language models demonstrate a sophisticated ability to adapt to varying qualities of motivation, learning effectively even from suboptimal or initially misleading objective descriptions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated capabilities across various natural language processing and generation tasks, including instruction following, code generation, and specialized applications like medical diagnosis. To further enhance their complex reasoning abilities, Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a method, distinguishing itself from conventional supervised fine-tuning. RLVR treats reasoning as a sequential decision-making process, optimizing models through objective reward signals that can be automatically verified against explicit rules. Examples of this include verifying mathematical answers against ground truth or checking code correctness via unit tests, as evidenced by models like DeepSeek-R1 and OpenAI-o1.

Despite its advantages in allowing LLMs to iteratively refine reasoning towards clearly defined success criteria, the current RLVR paradigm operates primarily on a trial-and-error basis. This approach often leads to inefficiencies, as models must extensively explore the reward space by generating numerous responses and learning from sparse, fragmented reward signals. This "blind" exploration can be computationally intensive and may struggle when optimal behaviors are difficult to discover or reward functions are complex.

The presented work addresses this inefficiency by leveraging two key characteristics of LLMs: the natural language descriptiveness of verifiable reward functions and the strong in-context learning (ICL) abilities of LLMs. Previous research has explored RL for LLMs, including Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) for alignment with human preferences and RLVR for direct reasoning enhancement. Similarly, in-context learning has been a cornerstone of LLM success, enabling models to learn from examples and instructions within the prompt without gradient updates. This paper aims to bridge the gap between these paradigms, proposing to integrate explicit task motivation (awareness of the reward function) into the RLVR finetuning process itself, thereby seeking to guide the model's exploration more directly and efficiently. This situates the research as an effort to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of RL-based finetuning for reasoning tasks by better integrating the inherent capabilities of LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the efficiency and effectiveness of Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) for large reasoning models. The authors propose a method to achieve this by enabling LLMs to be explicitly aware of the optimization objective during the reinforcement finetuning process.

The motivation for this work stems from several observations regarding the current RLVR paradigm:
- **Inefficient Trial-and-Error Learning**: Standard RLVR, similar to conventional reinforcement learning, relies on a trial-and-error mechanism. Models generate responses, receive feedback in the form of rewards, and then adjust their parameters. This process can be inefficient, particularly when the reward space is sparse, meaning positive rewards are infrequent or difficult to obtain. Models may require extensive exploration to understand the patterns of the task and the reward function, leading to substantial computational costs and data requirements.
- **Lack of Explicit Objective Awareness**: In typical RLVR, the model receives reward signals as feedback on its outputs but lacks explicit awareness of the overall optimization objective during the generation process. It learns from "fragments" of reward signals, which might be localized and insufficient to guide it towards globally optimal behaviors, potentially leading to reward hacking or convergence to suboptimal solutions. The model is essentially "blind" to the "rules of the game" while it plays.
- **Opportunity from Verifiable Rewards and In-Context Learning**: The authors identify two factors that present an opportunity to address the aforementioned issues:
 - **Verifiable Rewards**: Since RLVR uses verifiable rewards with explicit rules, these rules can often be described accurately in natural language.
 - **LLM In-Context Learning (ICL)**: Large language models have demonstrated a strong ability to learn from context provided directly within the prompt, without requiring parameter updates.

Based on these points, the core motivation is to investigate whether explicitly "telling" the LLM the rules of the game – specifically, the reward function – as an "in-context motivation" during finetuning can make the learning process more efficient and effective. This approach is hypothesized to align the model's generation process with the ultimate optimization objective, leading to faster learning and improved performance, analogous to how humans benefit from understanding task objectives before attempting them. The goal is to incentivize the model to generate desired outputs not only through external reward signals but also through an "inner motivation" derived from the explicit objective provided in the prompt.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **Motivation-enhanced Reinforcement Finetuning (MeRF)**, a method designed to improve the efficiency of RLVR by integrating in-context motivation into the training process.

**Core Mechanism:**
MeRF's central idea is to directly inject the natural language description of the reward function into the system prompt provided to the Large Language Model during reinforcement finetuning. This injected text serves as an "in-context motivation," explicitly informing the model about the task's optimization objective and what constitutes a desirable response. By doing so, MeRF leverages the in-context learning ability of LLMs to guide their generation process, aiming to align generated outputs with the external reward function more directly than traditional trial-and-error RLVR. The model is thus incentivized to produce desired outputs from both the explicit "motivation" provided in the prompt and the subsequent "external reward" signal received from the environment.

**Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:**
The reinforcement finetuning is implemented using **Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)**. GRPO is a variant of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) that is effective and efficient for LLM finetuning. It operates by sampling a group of `G` outputs from the policy for each question, calculating an advantage `A_i` for each output `y_i` based on its outcome reward `r_i`. The advantage is computed by normalizing the reward `r_i` against the mean and standard deviation of rewards within its group. This approach allows GRPO to learn from relative preferences among outputs in a group without requiring a separate critic model, contributing to training efficiency. For the experiments, the group size (`G`) of GRPO was set to 8, the clip range to 0.2, and the KL penalty to 0.001.

**Reward Function Design:**
For verifiable reward tasks, the reward function is typically rule-based and can be described in natural language. For the K&K Logic Puzzle dataset, the reward function comprises two components:
- **Correctness Score**: Assigns points based on the correctness of the final answer (e.g., +2 for correct, -1.5 for understandable but wrong, -2 for unparsable/incomplete).
- **Format Score**: Assigns points based on adherence to a specified output format (e.g., +1 for correct format, -1 otherwise).
The total score is the sum of these components.

**Prompt Design for In-Context Motivation:**
The system prompt is augmented to include a detailed explanation of these evaluation scoring rules. For example, in the K&K Puzzle, the prompt explicitly states: "You will get evaluated following Evaluation Scoring Rules: - Correctness Score: ... - Format Score: ... You will get the final score as their sum." This provides the LLM with direct guidance on how its responses will be assessed. Similar motivations are designed for Math and CountDown tasks, tailored to their respective evaluation criteria.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Models Evaluated**: A range of models from the Qwen2.5 series (Qwen2.5-7B-Base, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct-1M, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct) and DeepSeek-R1-Distill series (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B) were used. These selections aimed to cover different model sizes, families, and instruction-tuning stages for comprehensive evaluation.
- **Datasets**:
 - **K&K Logic Puzzles**: Used for logical reasoning. Training involved puzzles with 3 to 7 people, while evaluation included in-domain test sets and out-of-distribution (OOD) sets with 2 and 8 people. Each difficulty level had 900 training samples and 100 evaluation samples.
 - **MATH Benchmarks**: Included AIME24&25, AMC23, and MATH500. Training data was a subset of the DAPO-Math-17K dataset.
 - **CountDown**: A numerical game requiring arithmetic operations to reach a target number using a given set of numbers (3 or 4 numbers).
- **Training Details**: Finetuning was conducted with a batch size of 16, a learning rate of 1e-6, and 1 to 2 epochs of training. The Vllm framework was employed for efficient output sampling during rollouts.

**Analysis on Mechanism:**
The study further investigated several aspects to understand MeRF's mechanism:
- **Impact of In-Context Inference**: Compared performance with and without motivation during inference time to isolate the effect of training-time motivation.
- **Role of Exploration**: Analyzed pass@k performance and model entropy during training to understand how motivation affects the model's exploration capabilities.
- **Training and Validation Gap**: Assessed if the discrepancy between training with motivation and validating without it impacts performance.
- **Robustness to Motivation Quality**: Tested MeRF with "Ground-Truth," "Suboptimal," and "Adverse" motivations to evaluate its sensitivity and adaptability to different levels of alignment with the actual reward function.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated that MeRF consistently enhances the performance of reinforcement finetuning for Large Language Models across various reasoning tasks.

**Overall Performance and Training Efficiency:**
- **K&K Logic Puzzles**: MeRF showed substantial performance gains over the RLVR baseline, achieving higher validation accuracy and faster improvements during the training process across different model sizes and families (Figure 1). For example, MeRF on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct improved average accuracy from 0.51 (RLVR baseline) to 0.65, demonstrating a significant uplift (Table 1).
- **MATH Benchmarks**: On AIME24&25, AMC23, and MATH500 datasets, MeRF consistently improved pass@k performance (for k={1,2,4,8}) compared to the RLVR baseline. Across these datasets, MeRF achieved average gains of 3.40% in pass@1, 1.13% in pass@2, 3.50% in pass@4, and 4.50% in pass@8 (Table 2).
- **CountDown Dataset**: MeRF outperformed the RLVR baseline in validation accuracy and pass@4 performance on the CountDown numerical reasoning task (Figure 9).

**Analysis of MeRF's Mechanism:**

- **Impact of In-Context Motivation in Training vs. Inference (Q1)**:
 - While providing in-context motivation during *inference* led to a slight performance improvement (2-4%) for both RLVR and MeRF models, the primary performance gain of MeRF (25-27%) was attributed to the *motivation-enhanced reinforcement finetuning process itself*. This indicates that MeRF's benefits largely stem from how motivation guides learning during training, rather than simply improving in-context inference (Figure 2, 7).

- **How In-Context Motivation Enhances Finetuning (Q2)**:
 - **Improved Exploration**: MeRF consistently achieved higher pass@k performance and continued to improve these metrics throughout training, especially pass@8 (group size for GRPO). This suggests that in-context motivation encourages better exploration of diverse reasoning paths, making it more likely for the model to reach positive rewards (Figure 3, 4).
 - **Higher Entropy**: MeRF maintained a relatively higher entropy during training compared to the RLVR baseline. Initially, MeRF showed lower entropy, indicating structured exploration towards promising areas. As training progressed, RLVR's entropy decreased more rapidly, suggesting a loss of exploration ability and potential collapse to suboptimal solutions. MeRF's sustained higher entropy points to more effective exploration for correct answers rather than rapid convergence to easy, but suboptimal, rewards like format correctness (Figure 5, 6).

- **Training and Validation Gap (Q3)**:
 - Experiments showed comparable performance between models validated with and without in-context motivation after MeRF training (Figure 7). This suggests that the training-validation gap (where motivation is included in training but omitted in validation) has a negligible impact on models with sufficient generalization capabilities. Models trained with MeRF appear to generalize well to inference scenarios lacking explicit motivation.

- **Impact of Different Motivations (Q4)**:
 - **Ground-Truth Motivation**: Providing a motivation perfectly aligned with the reward function yielded the best performance.
 - **Suboptimal Motivation**: Even a suboptimal motivation, only describing the correctness score, still led to better performance than the RLVR baseline, highlighting the partial benefit of explicit guidance.
 - **Adverse Motivation**: When provided with a misleading motivation (e.g., assigning negative scores to correct answers), the model initially showed unstable learning dynamics and a performance drop. However, after several training steps, the model adapted. It learned to either discount the misleading motivational signal or interpret the adverse scores in reverse, eventually outperforming the RLVR baseline and even the suboptimal motivation (Figure 8, 10). This indicates a sophisticated ability of LLMs to reconcile conflicting signals and adapt during reinforcement finetuning.

**Additional Observations:**
- MeRF's performance also benefited from denser reward functions, which provide more informative signals both for backward optimization and forward in-context learning (Figure 12a).
- MeRF demonstrated robustness to different prompt variants, consistently outperforming RLVR regardless of minor variations in prompt formulation (Figure 12b).
- The method showed generalizability to more complex agentic tasks, such as using a search engine for reasoning on the Natural Questions dataset (Figure 13).
- Attention heatmaps revealed that models trained with MeRF paid significant attention to motivation tokens when generating answers, and notably learned to distinguish useful from misleading information in adverse motivations (Figure 14).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Motivation-enhanced Reinforcement Finetuning (MeRF), a method that addresses a notable challenge in the current Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) paradigm: its inefficiency due to reliance on blind trial-and-error exploration and sparse reward signals.

The primary significance of MeRF lies in its demonstration that simply providing large language models (LLMs) with an explicit, natural language description of the reward function during finetuning can substantially improve learning efficiency and task performance. This represents a more direct and intuitive way to guide LLM optimization compared to purely relying on indirect gradient updates from external rewards.

Potential impacts include:
- **Accelerated RL Finetuning**: By leveraging LLMs' in-context learning abilities, MeRF can reduce the computational resources and data required for effective RLVR, making the finetuning process faster and more accessible for complex reasoning tasks.
- **Improved Reasoning Capabilities**: The consistent performance gains across diverse reasoning benchmarks (logic puzzles, math, numerical games) indicate that MeRF can lead to more capable and robust reasoning LLMs. This is particularly relevant for tasks where establishing dense, non-sparse reward signals is challenging.
- **Enhanced Exploration Strategies**: The analysis suggests that in-context motivation facilitates more structured and effective exploration, preventing models from collapsing into suboptimal solutions and encouraging the discovery of better reasoning paths. This could inform future designs of exploration strategies in RL for LLMs.
- **Adaptability and Robustness of LLMs**: The finding that models can adapt to and even overcome adverse or misleading motivations during finetuning highlights a potentially sophisticated learning mechanism in LLMs. This suggests that LLMs are not merely memorizing prompts but engaging in a form of meta-learning or conflict resolution, which could have implications for building more resilient and generalizable AI systems.
- **Bridging In-Context Learning and Reinforcement Learning**: MeRF provides a concrete example of effectively integrating the strengths of in-context learning—a hallmark of modern LLMs—with reinforcement learning paradigms. This integration opens avenues for further research into hybrid learning approaches that capitalize on both implicit and explicit knowledge sources within LLMs.

While the current work uses static motivation, future research could explore dynamic motivation strategies and investigate the applicability of MeRF to models with weaker generalization capabilities. Overall, MeRF represents a step towards more efficient and effective training of large reasoning models, contributing to the development of more intelligent and adaptable AI.
