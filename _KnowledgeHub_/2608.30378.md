# PAVE: Predictive Alignment and Value-Guided Evolution for World-Action Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> PAVE is a direct world-action policy that adds two training-only signals to a flow-matching VLA: JEPA alignment of current-future transition embeddings at a fixed local offset plus trajectory-relative anchors at 25/50/75/100% of the remaining episode, and a distributional value critic that turns deployment rollouts into positive/negative/null advantage text conditions. It averages 96.6% on LIBERO, 73.2% on LIBERO-Plus, and 60.0% on RoboTwin 2.0 at unchanged online cost.

## Problem

- Behavior cloning only asks policies to reproduce demonstrated actions, never requiring internal representations to encode which environmental transition an action produces.
- Prior latent world-action policies supervise a single fixed future offset, capturing one temporal scale while under-specifying intermediate progress and trajectory-level consequences.
- Deployment rollouts of mixed quality get reused indiscriminately; value relabeling alone stays behavior-focused, and prediction alone models bad actions without discouraging them.

## Method

- ==Trajectory-relative multi-horizon alignment==: a shared ==MLP== with horizon embeddings predicts frozen two-frame ==V-JEPA 2.1== paired transition targets at fractions 0.25/0.50/0.75/1.00 of the remaining episode, plus the fixed offset-31 local target.
- ==Distributional token-fusion value critic== over 201 bins on [-1,0], scoring cumulative rollouts and computing ==N-step advantages== aligned to the 20-step action chunk.
- ==Advantage-conditioned policy evolution==: top-30% chunks per task get positive text, others negative, with 0.3 label dropout; the actor retrains from one fixed base checkpoint each round at a 3:1 demo-to-evolution ratio.

## Results

- Beats JEPA-WAM by **2.6** points on LIBERO (**96.6** vs **94.0**), **6.0** on LIBERO-Plus (**73.2** vs **67.2**), and **6.6** on RoboTwin 2.0 (**60.0** vs **53.4**), with **3.4** points on LIBERO-Long.
- Ablations: local target adds **8.1** LIBERO-Plus points over flow matching alone, four horizons add **3.5** more; evolution rounds lift LIBERO-Plus **67.2** to **73.2** across 288 episodes.
- Positive test conditioning reaches **66.6** macro-average versus **64.2** null, **47.6** negative, **61.7** unlabeled multi-round BC; online cost is **1.29B** params, **62.2ms** p50, only **0.2ms** above the baseline.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Predictive correctness and behavioral desirability are separable objectives: every valid trajectory can teach dynamics while only advantaged chunks shape the executed policy.
> - Normalizing future anchors to the remaining episode covers multiple timescales despite variable lengths, but returns peak at four anchors and degrade at six or eight.
> - A 0.69M-parameter distributional critic outperformed a 682M pooled-concat critic, so relabeling quality depends on token-level fusion far more than critic capacity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot manipulation is increasingly focused on Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which aim to map visual observations and natural language instructions directly to robot actions. This direct mapping offers a scalable and intuitive interface for developing generalist manipulation systems. Recent advancements in VLA models often leverage large pre-trained vision-language backbones and utilize advanced action decoders, such as diffusion policies or flow-matching experts, to generate continuous robot actions. A key advantage of these direct execution paths is their suitability for closed-loop control in dynamic environments.

However, a prevalent limitation in many VLA systems is their reliance on action imitation objectives, typically through behavior cloning. While effective for reproducing demonstrated actions, this approach often overlooks two complementary aspects: first, the internal representations of these policies may not be explicitly structured to describe how the environment evolves over multiple time scales beyond a single action chunk; second, when policies are deployed, the resulting trajectories often vary in quality (e.g., successful, failed, inefficient), and standard behavior cloning may not adequately distinguish or leverage these differences, potentially imitating undesirable behaviors alongside useful dynamics.

To address the first limitation, "world-action models" have emerged, integrating future prediction into direct policies. Early approaches involved pixel-generative models or latent world models for planning. More recent methods, such as JEPA-VLA, VLA-JEPA, Fast-WAM, and JEPA-WAM, incorporate predictive supervision within the policy's training, often by learning leakage-free future latent predictions or shaping action representations with current–future joint embeddings. These models aim to improve control by making the policy "future-aware" during training, without necessarily requiring online generation of future observations. A recognized limitation of these methods is that they often use a single, fixed future target, which may capture local motion but might not sufficiently represent intermediate progress or long-range consequences across an entire trajectory.

Concurrently, a separate line of research focuses on improving VLA policies through deployment experience. Methods like RECAP (introduced in π*0.6) use value models to convert diverse deployment data (demonstrations, rollouts, corrections) into advantage conditions for policy refinement. This approach acknowledges and utilizes the mixed quality of autonomous deployment data. However, value-guided relabeling primarily focuses on behavioral desirability and does not explicitly enhance the policy's multi-timescale understanding of environmental transitions. Furthermore, future prediction methods, while capturing environmental dynamics, are inherently behavior-agnostic; they can accurately predict the outcome of a poor action without guiding the actor to avoid it.

PAVE (Predictive Alignment and Value-Guided Evolution) integrates these two complementary paradigms. It proposes a framework that learns comprehensive environmental transitions from all valid trajectories, regardless of their success, while simultaneously leveraging value-guided feedback to select and execute only the relatively more desirable actions. The design principle is to maintain a direct, efficient online execution path for the actor by restricting the multi-horizon predictor and value critic to training-time or offline roles. This work seeks to advance VLA models by providing a more robust and self-improving policy that benefits from both predictive understanding and preferential learning from experience.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of the PAVE research are to enhance the capabilities of direct Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies by addressing two core limitations: improving the policy's understanding of multi-timescale environmental evolution and enabling effective learning from mixed-quality deployment experiences.

The motivations for these objectives are detailed as follows:

1. **Improving Multi-Timescale Scene Evolution Representation:**
 - **Motivation:** Standard behavior cloning, and even some predictive VLA models, often provide supervision only for immediate or single fixed-offset future states. This can result in internal representations that accurately describe local physical changes but may under-specify intermediate task progress or long-range trajectory-level consequences. Robot tasks frequently involve sequences of actions over varying durations, requiring an understanding of how the scene evolves across different temporal granularities.
 - **Objective:** To explicitly require the policy's internal representation to preserve information about physical changes and task progress over multiple time scales. PAVE aims to achieve this without introducing explicit future tokens directly into the action head during online execution.

2. **Leveraging Mixed-Quality Deployment Trajectories for Policy Improvement:**
 - **Motivation:** In real-world robotic deployment, policies inevitably generate trajectories of varying quality—some successful, some failed, some efficient, and others inefficient. Simply replaying all deployment data via behavior cloning can lead to the policy learning and reproducing undesirable behaviors alongside effective ones. Existing value-guided methods address behavior selection but may not simultaneously improve the policy's understanding of environmental dynamics. Conversely, future prediction alone is behavior-agnostic, meaning a predictor could accurately foresee the consequences of a poor action without teaching the actor to avoid it.
 - **Objective:** To develop a mechanism that can separate useful dynamics from undesirable behavior within deployment trajectories. This involves allowing all valid trajectories to contribute information about "what physically happened" (predictive learning), while simultaneously guiding the actor to learn "which actions are desirable" (value-guided improvement).

The central motivating principle for PAVE is to **"learn transitions from every valid trajectory, but execute actions from the better ones."** This principle informs the separation of predictive learning from preferential learning.

The specific contributions outlined by the authors further clarify these objectives:

- **Trajectory-relative multi-horizon transition alignment:** This aims to combine the stability of a local, fixed-offset predictive target with the temporal breadth of normalized future anchors spanning the remaining trajectory. This provides a richer, multi-scale understanding of environmental dynamics.
- **Predictive-preferential decomposition:** This objective seeks to formalize the separation where all valid trajectories supervise the policy's understanding of physical occurrences, while a distributional critic independently identifies and labels relatively better or worse action chunks through advantage-conditioned behavior learning.
- **Self-improving direct policy:** The goal here is to integrate these auxiliary mechanisms (multi-horizon predictor and value critic) purely into the training or offline annotation phases. This design ensures that the online execution path of the direct actor remains lean, avoiding computational overhead from online future rollout, candidate-action evaluation, or Model Predictive Control (MPC) search.

In summary, PAVE's motivation stems from the need for VLA policies to possess a sophisticated understanding of temporal dynamics and to effectively learn from mixed-quality experiences, all while preserving the efficiency of direct action generation during deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

PAVE proposes a multi-component system designed for self-improving direct Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. The methodology integrates outcome-agnostic predictive learning with outcome-aware policy improvement, ensuring that auxiliary training modules are removed during online execution. The system operates in an iterative loop involving expert demonstrations ($D_{demo}$) and cumulative deployment trajectories ($D_k$).

The PAVE architecture comprises three main parts:
1. **Multi-horizon Predictive World-Action Learning:** The actor is trained to predict actions while simultaneously aligning its internal representations with local and multi-horizon current–future environmental transitions.
2. **Distributional Value-Guided Experience Annotation:** An independent distributional critic evaluates cumulative deployment trajectories and converts them into action-chunk-level advantage labels.
3. **Advantage-Conditioned Policy Evolution (ACP):** The actor is retrained using a combination of expert demonstrations and the advantage-labeled deployment data.

#### 3.1. Direct Actor

The core of the system is a direct continuous policy, $\pi_\theta(a_{t:t+H-1} | o_t, l, q_t, c_t)$.
- **Inputs:** Current global and wrist observations ($o_t$), task instruction ($l$), proprioception ($q_t$), and a behavior-quality text condition ($c_t \in \{\text{positive, negative, null}\}$).
- **Visual Encoder:** A frozen V-JEPA 2.1 ViT-L/16 encoder processes two 384x384 camera views, yielding 1152 visual tokens of dimension 1024. A frozen projector maps these to the 896-dimensional hidden space of Qwen2.5-0.5B.
- **Language Model:** The frozen Qwen2.5-0.5B base is adapted using LoRA. The model sequence includes an initial text token, all visual tokens, the remaining task-plus-advantage text, and 64 learned action placeholders.
- **Action Expert:** A trainable flow action head decodes the 64 placeholder states ($M_t$) and proprioception ($q_t$) into a normalized 20-step action chunk ($a \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times 7}$, with $H=20$).
- **Flow-Matching Objective:** The actor predicts a velocity field $u_\theta(x_\tau, \tau, M_t, q_t)$ using a flow-matching objective $L_{FM}$, which aims to match the target velocity field $a - \epsilon$ derived from Gaussian noise $\epsilon$ and flow time $\tau$. This loss is masked ($m_a$) to exclude padded or inconsistent action chunks.

#### 3.2. Multi-Horizon Predictive World-Action Learning

This component trains the actor's representation to understand environmental transitions.
- **Paired Transition Targets:** A frozen V-JEPA 2.1 target encoder, $E_{tgt}$, is used to create joint embeddings of current and future observations. For each camera view, $E_{pair}(o_t, o_h)$ concatenates the two-frame clip embeddings of current ($I_v^t$) and future ($I_v^h$) images, resulting in a 1152x1024 token representation.
- **Local Transition Alignment:** A fixed-offset JEPA target, $Y^\delta_t$, is used for stable short-range supervision. The future observation $o_{h^\delta_t}$ is taken at a fixed offset $\delta=31$ frames. A predictor $P_\delta$ maps the current visual states ($V_t$) from Qwen to a prediction $\hat{Y}^\delta_t$. A token-wise masked cosine loss, $L_{local}$, measures the similarity between $\hat{Y}^\delta_t$ and the target $Y^\delta_t$.
- **Trajectory-Relative Multi-Horizon Alignment:** To capture longer-range progress, future anchors are defined at normalized fractions of the remaining episode length: $\rho = \{0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00\}$. For each fraction $\rho_k$, a target index $h_{t,k}$ is computed dynamically. The joint transition target $Y_{t,k}$ is constructed using $E_{pair}(o_t, o_{h_{t,k}})$. A shared MLP, $P_{MH}$, augmented with a learned horizon embedding $e_k$, predicts $\hat{Y}_{t,k}$ from $V_t$. A masked cosine loss, $L_{MH}$, is applied across all horizons. Importantly, $\hat{Y}_{t,k}$ is not passed to the action expert; this branch solely shapes the shared Qwen LoRA representation during training.
- **Actor Objective:** The overall actor objective is $L_{actor} = L_{FM} + \lambda_{local}L_{local} + \lambda_{MH}L_{MH}$, with $\lambda_{local} = \lambda_{MH} = 0.5$.

#### 3.3. Distributional Value-Guided Experience Annotation

This component provides outcome-aware feedback by evaluating action desirability.
- **Distributional Critic Architecture:** An independent state-value critic, $V_\phi(s_t)$, is trained with $s_t = (o_t, l, q_t)$. It reuses frozen V-JEPA, projector, and Qwen modules, then appends proprioceptive tokens and a learned value-query token. A lightweight trainable head outputs 201 logits over equally spaced supports $z_i \in [-1, 0]$, representing a distribution over possible normalized returns. The expectation of this distribution yields $V_\phi(s_t)$. The critic estimates $V(s_t)$ rather than $Q(s_t, a_t)$.
- **Reward and Target Definition:** Nonterminal rewards $r_t = -1$. Terminal reward is 0 for success and $-C_{fail}$ for failure, where $C_{fail} = M_{task}$ (fixed task horizon). The undiscounted return $G_t$ is normalized to $y_t \in [-1, 0]$.
- **Distributional Objective:** The scalar $y_t$ is projected to a two-hot categorical target $p^*_t$ on the 201 supports. The critic is trained using a cross-entropy loss $L_{value}$.
- **Advantage Calculation:** An N-step advantage, $A^{(N)}_t$, is computed for complete 20-step action chunks (N=H=20). This uses the sum of normalized rewards over N steps and the critic's value prediction for the state N steps later.
- **Labeling:** Within each task, eligible action chunks are ranked by their $A^{(N)}_t$. The top 30% are labeled "positive," and the remainder "negative." Valid human corrections are also designated "positive." These labels are relative to the current cumulative data.

#### 3.4. Advantage-Conditioned Policy Evolution (ACP)

This phase integrates the advantage labels into actor training.
- **Text Conditioning:** The advantage label is injected as text, concatenated with the task instruction (e.g., "Advantage: positive"). During training, labeled conditions are dropped to "null" with probability 0.3 for robustness. Expert demonstrations are always assigned "positive" labels.
- **Evolution Schedule:** PAVE employs an iterative training process over three rounds. In each round $k$:
 1. A fresh critic is trained on all states in the cumulative experience pool $D_{\leq k}$.
 2. This critic scores the cumulative pool to compute advantage labels for all eligible chunks, forming a labeled evolution dataset $\tilde{D}_{\leq k}$.
 3. The actor is re-initialized from a fixed base checkpoint ($\pi_{base}$) to minimize weight drift.
 4. The actor is optimized on a combined dataset $D_{train}^k = D_{demo} \cup \tilde{D}_{\leq k}$, using a 3:1 demonstration-to-evolution sampling ratio.
- **Deployment:** During online execution, the policy sets $c_t = \text{positive}$ and directly samples a 20-step action chunk. Crucially, the paired-future encoder, local and multi-horizon predictors, critic, and advantage computation are not active online, preserving the direct actor's efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative results across three simulation benchmarks: LIBERO, LIBERO-Plus, and RoboTwin 2.0, along with qualitative real-robot demonstrations and ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Overall Performance on Benchmarks

PAVE demonstrates improved average success rates compared to existing methods, including other world-action models and value-guided VLA policies.

- **LIBERO Benchmark (Table 2):**
 - PAVE achieved an average success rate of **96.6%**, surpassing the previous best, $\pi^*0.6$ (94.6%), by 2.0 percentage points, and JEPA-WAM (94.0%) by 2.6 percentage points.
 - Notably, for long-horizon tasks, PAVE reached **95.0%** success, an improvement of 3.4 points over JEPA-WAM (91.6%) and 2.6 points over $\pi^*0.6$ (92.4%).

- **LIBERO-Plus Benchmark (Table 3):** This benchmark evaluates robustness under seven controlled perturbations.
 - PAVE achieved an average success rate of **73.2%**, outperforming $\pi^*0.6$ (68.5%) by 4.7 points and JEPA-WAM (67.2%) by 6.0 points.
 - PAVE demonstrated particular robustness under specific perturbations: a 7.9-point gain over JEPA-WAM under background changes and a 7.4-point gain under sensor noise.

- **RoboTwin 2.0 Benchmark (Table 4):** This benchmark focuses on complex bimanual tasks with structured randomization.
 - PAVE achieved an average success rate of **60.0%**, exceeding $\pi^*0.6$ (55.2%) by 4.8 points and JEPA-WAM (53.4%) by 6.6 points.
 - Significant margins were observed over JEPA-WAM in challenging conditions: 7.0 points under clutter and 7.5 points under background changes.

#### 4.2. Online Efficiency

PAVE's design prioritizes maintaining a lean online execution path.
- **Parameters and Memory (Table 5):** PAVE's online component matched JEPA-WAM's online parameters (1.29 billion) and GPU memory footprint (10.4 GB). The auxiliary predictor and critic modules added 4.63 million parameters, which are used only during training or offline annotation.
- **Latency (Table 5):** PAVE's p50 and p95 latencies (62.2 ms and 66.8 ms, respectively) were minimally higher than JEPA-WAM (62.0 ms and 66.6 ms), indicating that its training-time enhancements do not incur substantial online computational cost.

#### 4.3. Core Ablations (Tables 6, 7, 8)

Ablation studies provided insights into the contributions of PAVE's key components:

- **Effect of Transition Supervision (Table 6):**
 - Without any future transition target (i.e., only flow matching), LIBERO-Plus achieved 59.1% and RoboTwin 2.0 achieved 45.9%.
 - Adding only the one local fixed-offset JEPA target improved success rates significantly, to 67.2% on LIBERO-Plus and 53.4% on RoboTwin 2.0. This represents an 8.1-point and 7.5-point improvement, respectively, over no future target.
 - Further incorporating the shared four-horizon objective yielded additional gains, reaching 70.7% on LIBERO-Plus (3.5 points further improvement) and 56.8% on RoboTwin 2.0 (3.4 points further improvement). This suggests that multi-horizon predictive alignment enhances robustness.

- **Policy Evolution and Advantage Conditioning (Tables 7, 8):**
 - **Evolution Rounds (Table 7):** Success rates consistently improved across the three evolution rounds. On LIBERO-Plus, the base actor started at 68.1% and reached 73.2% after Round 3. On RoboTwin 2.0, it improved from 54.5% to 60.0%. This demonstrates the effectiveness of the self-improving mechanism using deployment experience.
 - **Advantage Conditioning (Table 8):** When testing the policy under different advantage conditions, the "Positive" condition achieved the highest average success (66.6%). The "Null" condition yielded 64.2%, and the "Negative" condition resulted in significantly lower performance (47.6%). Unlabeled multi-round behavior cloning (without ACP) achieved 61.7%. This indicates that explicit advantage conditioning guides the policy towards better actions.

#### 4.4. Additional Ablations (Appendix)

- **Visual Encoder Choice (Table 9):** V-JEPA 2.1 (default) consistently outperformed DINOv2 and LingBot-Vision across all benchmarks, indicating its superior ability to provide representations suitable for PAVE's learning objectives.
- **Number of Future Anchors (Table 10):** Four trajectory-relative future anchors ({0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 1.00}) yielded the highest success rates. Increasing the number of anchors to six or eight led to a slight decrease in performance, suggesting an optimal balance.
- **Loss Components (Table 11):** The full PAVE configuration, including flow matching ($L_{FM}$), local transition alignment ($L_{local}$), multi-horizon alignment ($L_{MH}$), and ACP evolution, delivered the best results. Each component contributed positively, with ACP providing a significant boost beyond predictive losses alone.
- **Value Critic Architecture (Table 12):** The proposed distributional token-fusion critic, with only 0.69M trainable parameters, achieved higher final policy success (73.2% on LIBERO-Plus) compared to a scalar-regression token critic (71.1%) and a much larger $\pi^*0.6$-style critic (70.4%). This highlights the efficiency and effectiveness of the distributional critic design.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The PAVE framework contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing fundamental challenges in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy learning, offering several significant implications:

1. **Enhanced Policy Learning from Mixed-Quality Data:** A key contribution of PAVE is its ability to learn from heterogeneous deployment experiences. By separating predictive alignment (what happened) from value-guided evolution (what should be preferred), it ensures that all valid trajectories can inform the policy's understanding of environmental dynamics, while only desirable behaviors are reinforced for execution. This mechanism is crucial for the development of truly autonomous and self-improving robot agents that can continually refine their skills in open-ended environments, reducing the reliance on solely expert demonstrations.

2. **Improved Robustness and Generalization:** The introduction of trajectory-relative multi-horizon transition alignment allows the policy to develop a more comprehensive understanding of environmental evolution across different temporal scales. This multi-scale predictive capability contributes directly to the observed improvements in robustness under various perturbations (e.g., camera viewpoint, background changes, sensor noise) as demonstrated on the LIBERO-Plus benchmark. Such robustness is vital for deploying robots in complex and variable real-world settings.

3. **Efficient Online Execution for Real-Time Control:** Despite incorporating sophisticated predictive and value-based learning components, PAVE maintains an efficient online execution path. By restricting the multi-horizon predictor and the value critic to training or offline annotation phases, the online actor operates directly from current observations, language instructions, and proprioception, without the computational overhead of online planning, candidate action evaluation, or future rollouts. This makes PAVE suitable for real-time robotic control applications where low latency is critical.

4. **Advancement of World-Action Models:** PAVE refines the concept of world-action models by moving beyond single fixed-offset future predictions. The trajectory-relative anchors provide a richer form of predictive supervision that better captures task progress and long-range consequences, offering a more nuanced "world model" implicitly embedded within the policy's representation.

5. **A Principled Framework for Self-Improvement:** The framework's iterative policy evolution, where a critic evaluates cumulative deployment data to provide advantage-conditioned labels, establishes a principled mechanism for continuous self-improvement. Reinitializing the actor from a base checkpoint in each round helps mitigate issues like catastrophic forgetting or compounding errors, ensuring stable learning from growing datasets.

**Potential Impact:**
PAVE's advancements have the potential to accelerate the development of more capable and adaptable generalist robot manipulation systems. Robots utilizing this approach could learn more efficiently from their own experiences, exhibit greater resilience to environmental variations, and require less manual intervention or expert data over their operational lifetime. This could lead to broader applications of robotics in manufacturing, logistics, service industries, and even domestic environments.

**Limitations (as acknowledged by the authors):**
Despite its strengths, the paper identifies several limitations:
- **Temporal Anchors vs. Semantic Subgoals:** Trajectory-relative anchors serve as temporal progress markers for representation supervision, not as semantic subgoals or guaranteed plans. They may encode unsuccessful endpoints.
- **Chunk-Level vs. Action-Level Correction:** The state-value critic provides chunk-level relative labels, which cannot precisely identify failing actions within a chunk. Denser, action-level correction methods could offer more granular supervision.
- **Critic Sensitivity:** The use of in-sample critic predictions for labeling may make the system sensitive to critic overfitting, calibration issues, and data coverage. Trajectory-level cross-fitting could be an important future extension.
- **Offline Computation Cost:** Retraining the actor from a base checkpoint in every round incurs substantial offline computation, despite efficient online inference.
- **Real-World Safety and Robustness:** While simulation results are strong and a qualitative real-robot demonstration is provided, the work does not establish safety or robustness in unconstrained real-world environments. Broader deployment would necessitate comprehensive safety measures, intervention logging, and failure auditing.

Overall, PAVE represents a significant step towards developing intelligent robotic agents that can robustly learn from their environment and experiences, fostering practical applications in increasingly complex and dynamic settings.
