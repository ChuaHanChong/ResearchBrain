# DiffCoTune: Differentiable Co-Tuning for Cross-domain Robot Control

> [!summary] Summary
> The DiffCoTune framework, developed by researchers from USC and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, introduces an automated, gradient-based method for simultaneously tuning robot controller parameters and simulator parameters. This co-tuning approach addresses the sim-to-real gap, improving controller performance in deployment domains by up to 60% in some tasks, with only a few real-world rollouts.

## Problem

- Robot controllers designed in simulators often exhibit performance degradation when transferred to physical robots due to unmodeled dynamics and discrepancies, known as the "sim-to-real gap."
- Bridging this gap traditionally requires time-consuming and inefficient manual or ad-hoc tuning of controller parameters.
- Existing methods like system identification or controller adaptation in isolation often have limitations, such as leading to conservative performance or struggling with high-dimensional parameter spaces and biased gradients.

## Method

- **DiffCoTune** proposes an iterative, ==gradient-based co-tuning== framework that simultaneously adapts parameters of a ==differentiable simulator== and a ==differentiable controller== in an intermediate tuning domain.
- It defines two objective functions: a ==system identification objective== to match closed-loop dynamics of the model to the target system, and a ==task objective== for controller performance in the model.
- Two update strategies are offered: a ==Combined== strategy for joint optimization and a more robust ==Split-Alternate== strategy that iteratively refines simulator parameters based on real-world rollouts, then tunes controller parameters in the refined simulator.

## Results

- DiffCoTune improved cartpole stabilization performance, transforming an under-damped response to critically damped, and reduced steady-state velocity error and overshoot for DNN controllers with extreme mass discrepancies.
- For quadruped velocity tracking, DiffCoTune Split-Alternate prevented falls and improved performance by **60%** in sim-to-sim transfer and enabled a Unitree Go1 robot to travel **1.04m** further in 5 seconds during high-speed real-world motion.
- The framework enabled a bipedal robot to achieve a **34 cm** jump on hardware, correcting torso pitch and foot placement for stable landing, where an untuned controller failed and controller-only tuning resulted in a fall.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simultaneous co-tuning of both simulator and controller parameters is essential for robust cross-domain transfer, especially in the presence of significant model uncertainty, outperforming controller-only tuning.
> - Gradient-based tuning methods, leveraging differentiable simulators, are significantly more efficient than gradient-free alternatives like Bayesian Optimization, achieving comparable solutions with substantially fewer real-world rollouts.
> - An iterative data collection and tuning scheme, where real-world rollouts from a progressively tuned controller inform subsequent updates, leads to greater performance improvements than a one-shot "sysId-then-tune" approach.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of robot controllers from a synthesis domain (e.g., a simulator or dynamics model) to a deployment domain (e.g., a real robot) frequently encounters challenges due to discrepancies between the model and the real system. These discrepancies, often termed the "sim-to-real gap," arise from unmodeled dynamics, noise, and perturbations inherent in real-world systems. Addressing this gap typically requires manual or ad-hoc tuning of controllers to achieve desired performance, which is often time-consuming and inefficient.

Existing approaches to mitigate the sim-to-real gap can be broadly categorized. Model-based control synthesis utilizes techniques such as robust or stochastic control, adaptive control, and system identification (sysId). Robust and stochastic control account for uncertainties by considering worst-case scenarios or probability distributions. Adaptive control dynamically adjusts parameters based on uncertainty estimations. System identification aims to calibrate the synthesis domain's parameters to accurately reflect the deployment domain using physics-based models.

Model-free learning-based control paradigms employ conceptually similar techniques, including domain randomization, domain adaptation, and system identification using deep neural networks (DNNs). Domain randomization samples from an uncertainty distribution, while domain adaptation seeks invariant features or parameters. Each of these methods, when applied in isolation, has limitations. For instance, robust control and domain randomization can lead to conservative performance if uncertainty bounds are poorly chosen. Adaptive control and domain adaptation require observable states for uncertainty estimation. Consequently, successful empirical applications often involve combinations of these methods, such as robust adaptive control or domain randomization with DNN-based sysId.

This research positions itself at the intersection of domain adaptation and system identification. It moves beyond conventional approaches that treat system identification and controller adaptation as separate problems. Instead, it proposes a *co-tuning* framework that simultaneously adapts a nominal controller and calibrates model parameters in an intermediate tuning domain. This approach leverages recent advancements in differentiable simulators and automatic differentiation (AD) tools, which enable the computation of gradients for high-dimensional systems and complex dynamics. While prior work has utilized differentiable simulators for either system identification or nominal control synthesis, this paper focuses on coupling both processes to directly enhance performance in the deployment domain, distinguishing it from methods that first identify a model and then synthesize a controller, or those that learn residual policies. The work also contrasts with purely model-free auto-tuning methods (e.g., Bayesian optimization) which struggle with high-dimensional parameter spaces and gradient-based model-based auto-tuning methods that suffer from biased gradients due to model mismatch.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an automated, gradient-based framework for tuning robot controllers to improve their performance in the deployment domain, specifically addressing challenges posed by modeling discrepancies between synthesis and real-world environments.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in robot control:
1. **Modeling Discrepancies:** Robot controllers are often designed and optimized in simplified simulators or dynamics models. These models inherently contain inaccuracies and simplifications for computational tractability, leading to a "sim-to-real gap" when transferring controllers to physical robots. Factors like unmodeled dynamics, noise, and external perturbations contribute to this gap.
2. **Ad-hoc Tuning:** Traditionally, bridging the sim-to-real gap requires significant manual and ad-hoc tuning efforts to adjust controller parameters, which is labor-intensive, time-consuming, and lacks a systematic approach. This process can be particularly challenging for complex robotic systems and high-dimensional controllers.
3. **Limitations of Existing Methods:**
 - Pure system identification aims for an accurate model of the target system, which might be overly demanding or yield models that are accurate for data collection but not necessarily optimal for a specific control task.
 - Controller synthesis from scratch for the identified model can be computationally expensive and may not fully leverage existing, empirically compelling nominal controllers.
 - Single-stage optimization methods for controller tuning can struggle with model mismatch, leading to biased gradients.
 - Model-free auto-tuning methods, while not relying on model knowledge, often scale poorly to high-dimensional parameter spaces and require numerous costly rollouts in the deployment domain.
 - Multi-stage optimization approaches, such as learning residual policies and then fine-tuning, often require task-specific reward shaping and may not generalize across different types of controllers or systems.

To address these motivations, the key objectives are:
- To formulate the cross-domain transfer problem as a *co-tuning* problem, where both the differentiable simulator and the differentiable controller parameters are simultaneously adapted.
- To leverage the capabilities of differentiable simulators to enable gradient-based updates for both model (simulator) and controller parameters.
- To achieve performance improvement in the deployment domain with a limited number of trials (e.g., a few rollouts) on the target system, making the approach practical for real-world robotic applications where physical interactions are costly or time-consuming.
- To develop scalable co-tuning techniques applicable to diverse robotic systems (from low-dimensional to high-dimensional) and various types of controllers (model-based like LQR, MPC, Trajectory Optimization (TO), and learning-based like DNN policies).
- To move beyond requiring an accurate recreation of the target system or synthesizing a controller from scratch, focusing instead on adapting an existing nominal controller to perform better in the deployment domain. This aims to find an improved local solution within the neighborhood of the nominal controller.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, DiffCoTune, addresses the co-tuning problem by iteratively refining both simulator and controller parameters using gradient-based optimization. The methodology relies on the availability of a differentiable simulator and a differentiable controller.

**System and Controller Representation:**
- A **simulator** is represented by discrete-time dynamics: $x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t; \beta)$, where $\beta$ denotes model parameters (e.g., mass, friction, or residual dynamics parameters). The simulator is assumed to be *differentiable*, providing gradients $\nabla_{x_t}f$, $\nabla_{u_t}f$, and $\nabla_{\beta}f$. This model exists in the "tuning domain."
- A **controller** (or state-feedback policy) is represented as $u_t = \pi(x_t; \theta)$, where $\theta$ are the controller parameters. It is also assumed to be *differentiable*, providing gradients $\nabla_{x_t}\pi$ and $\nabla_{\theta}\pi$.

**Objective Functions:**
Two distinct objective functions are defined:
1. **System Identification Objective ($J_{sysId}$):** This measures the discrepancy between the closed-loop dynamics of the model and the target system.
 $J_{sysId}(\theta, \beta, \{x_{sys_t}\}) := \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T \|x_t - x_{sys_t}\|_2^2$
 Here, $x_{sys_t}$ are observable states collected from a rollout of the controller $\pi(\cdot; \theta)$ on the actual target system (sys). The term $x_t$ represents states rolled out using the same controller in the differentiable model $f(\cdot; \beta)$. This formulation compares closed-loop behaviors, contrasting with conventional sysId which often compares open-loop dynamics.

2. **Task Objective ($J_{task}$):** This quantifies the desired performance of the controller.
 $J_{task}(\theta, \beta) := \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^T c(x_t, u_t)$
 where $c(\cdot)$ is a differentiable loss term (e.g., squared error for stabilization or tracking) and $x_t$ are states rolled out in the differentiable model $f(\cdot; \beta)$ using controller $\pi(\cdot; \theta)$. The true task objective $J_{task}(\theta, sys)$ on the unknown target system is non-differentiable, so $J_{task}(\theta, \beta)$ serves as a differentiable surrogate in the tuning domain.

**Problem Formulation:**
Given an initial nominal model $\tilde{\beta}$ and controller $\tilde{\theta}$, the goal is to find a fine-tuned controller $\theta^*$ such that its performance on the real system is improved, i.e., $J_{task}(\theta^*, sys) \leq J_{task}(\tilde{\theta}, sys)$.

**Iterative Co-Tuning Algorithm (Algorithm 1):**
The core of DiffCoTune is an iterative algorithm that leverages a "handful" of rollouts in the deployment domain.
1. Initialize $\beta_0 = \tilde{\beta}$ and $\theta_0 = \tilde{\theta}$.
2. For $l = 0$ to $L$ (maximum tuning iterations):
 - Execute a rollout of the current controller $\pi(\cdot; \theta_l)$ on the target system to collect a trajectory $\{x_{sys_t}\}_l$.
 - Apply an `UpdateStrategy` to compute new parameters $(\theta_{l+1}, \beta_{l+1})$ based on the collected rollout and current parameters.
 - Evaluate the performance of $\theta_{l+1}$ on the target system and update $\theta_{best}$ if performance improves.
3. Return $\theta_{best}$.

**Update Strategies:**
Two `UpdateStrategy` variants are proposed to update $\beta$ and $\theta$ in each tuning iteration ($l$):

1. **Combined Strategy (Algorithm 2):**
 This approach optimizes a weighted sum of the two objectives simultaneously:
 $(\beta_{l+1}, \theta_{l+1}) = \arg\min_{[\theta, \beta]} (w_{task} J_{task}(\theta, \beta) + w_{sysId} J_{sysId}(\theta, \beta, \{x_{sys_t}\}_l))$
 This joint optimization is performed numerically using a gradient-descent optimizer (e.g., Adam) over a maximum number of epochs $K$, using simulated rollouts in the differentiable model to compute gradients.

2. **Split-Alternate Strategy (Algorithm 3):**
 This strategy employs alternating optimization:
 - **Simulator Update:** First, $\beta_{l+1}$ is updated by minimizing $J_{sysId}$ with the controller parameters fixed at $\theta_l$:
 $\beta_{l+1} = \arg\min_{\beta} J_{sysId}(\theta_l, \beta, \{x_{sys_t}\}_l)$
 This step refines the simulator to better match the real system's closed-loop behavior given the current controller.
 - **Controller Update:** Second, $\theta_{l+1}$ is updated by minimizing $J_{task}$ with the simulator parameters fixed at the newly updated $\beta_{l+1}$:
 $\theta_{l+1} = \arg\min_{\theta} J_{task}(\theta, \beta_{l+1})$
 This step tunes the controller to perform better in the refined simulator.
 Each sub-problem is solved using gradient descent over a specified number of epochs ($K/2$ for each, to keep total gradient updates consistent with the combined variant).

**Implementation Details:**
The framework is built using the JAX ecosystem, including MuJoCo XLA (MJX) for differentiable simulation, Flax for DNN controllers, and Optax for the Adam optimizer. A single rollout in the target system per iteration ($L=5$) and a maximum of $K=100$ epochs for inner optimization are used across all experiments. For the Combined strategy, equal weights ($w_{task}=w_{sysId}=1$) are applied. The task objective $c(x_t, x_t^*)$ is typically chosen as a squared error norm.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The proposed DiffCoTune framework was evaluated on a range of robot systems and controller types, demonstrating performance improvements across sim-to-sim and sim-to-real transfer tasks.

**A. Tuning Performance:**

1. **Cartpole Stabilization ({cartpole, LQR} and {cartpole, DNN}):**
 - For LQR, with a 30% mass discrepancy, DiffCoTune Split-Alternate improved the stabilizing performance from an under-damped, high-error response to a critically damped response with lower error. Phase portraits showed the system approaching desired critically damped behavior over successive tuning iterations (Figure 2a, 2b).
 - For DNN controllers, with an extreme 300% mass discrepancy, the nominal controller exhibited steady-state velocity error and overshoot. Tuning with DiffCoTune radically altered the phase portrait, yielding an over-damped response with improved convergence to the target (Figure 2c, 2d).

2. **Quadruped Tracking ({quadruped, DNN}):**
 - **Sim-to-Sim Transfer (Barkour robot to Pybullet):** A DNN controller was used for velocity tracking. With a 15% mass discrepancy and a contrasting contact model (LCP-based hard contact in deployment vs. soft contact in synthesis/tuning), the untuned controller failed immediately. DiffCoTune Split-Alternate prevented falls and improved performance by 60%, achieving near-perfect tracking of desired velocities and base height (Figure 3).
 - **Sim-to-Real Transfer (Unitree Go1 robot):** For high-speed forward motion, the untuned controller transferred successfully but showed subpar tracking. Co-tuning improved tracking performance, and the tuned controller advanced 1.04 m further than the untuned one over a 5-second trial, closer to the 5m target (Figure 4).

3. **Biped Tracking ({biped, MPC} and {biped, TO}):**
 - **MPC for HECTOR Bipedal Robot:** A quadratic tracking objective with a linearized Single Rigid Body Model (SRBM) was used. Co-tuning improved height-tracking performance by 29.29% in the MuJoCo deployment domain, reducing jitters, despite the synthesis model's simplification of leg inertia.
 - **Trajectory Optimization (TO) for Bipedal Jumping (Real Robot):** A PD controller tracked a pre-synthesized reference. The untuned controller failed on the real robot. DiffTune (controller-only tuning) improved jump length but resulted in a fall upon touchdown. DiffCoTune achieved a 34 cm jump, closer to the synthesized 40 cm, by correcting torso pitch mid-flight and adjusting foot placement for stable landing (Figure 5).

**B. Analysis and Comparisons:**

1. **Necessity of Co-Tuning:**
 - In the absence of model mismatch (e.g., {cartpole, LQR} without uncertainty), DiffCoTune and DiffTune (controller-only tuning) performed similarly.
 - With model uncertainty (mass discrepancy in cartpole, DNN-quadruped), DiffCoTune Split-Alternate consistently outperformed DiffTune (which tunes only controller parameters on a mismatched model) in subsequent iterations, indicating the importance of co-tuning the model (Figure 7a-f).

2. **Gradient-based vs. Gradient-free Tuning:**
 - Compared with Bayesian Optimization (BO) on {cartpole, LQR} for 100 iterations, DiffCoTune (gradient-based) showed a rapid reduction in loss during the initial phase (first 10 trials) and then a slower asymptotic decrease. In contrast, BO, a model-agnostic method, required more rollouts (over 90 trials) to find a comparable solution (Figure 6, right). This highlights the efficiency of gradient-based methods when target-system rollouts are costly.

3. **Iterative Data Collection and Tuning:**
 - The iterative scheme of DiffCoTune (analogous to on-policy learning) was compared against a "sysId-then-tune" baseline, which performs all model updates upfront before controller tuning. For {quadruped, DNN}, DiffCoTune achieved a greater loss reduction in early iterations by using rollouts collected from progressively tuned controllers, while the baseline, after initial deviation, still improved but did not reach the same level of performance (Figure 6, left). This suggests that iterative data collection with an adapting controller is beneficial.

4. **Controller Tuning with System Rollouts vs. Model Rollouts:**
 - `DiffTune w/ System rollout update` (propagating sensitivity using model Jacobians along a single real-system rollout) improved performance but was limited by the number of gradient updates (K=1).
 - `DiffTune w/ Model rollout update` (purely simulated tuning with many gradient updates, K>>1) performed better due to more updates.
 - DiffCoTune Split-Alternate leveraged both multiple gradient updates (like model rollouts) and target system rollouts (via $\beta$-tuning), enabling it to outperform both DiffTune variants under model uncertainty (Figure 7b,d,e,f).

5. **Combined vs. Split-Alternate Strategy:**
 - The Combined strategy was found to be fragile and prone to divergence in the presence of model uncertainty, especially with naive weight choices ($w_{task}=w_{sysId}=1$). This was attributed to its sensitivity to the curvature of the objective landscape (Figure 7b,d,e,f).
 - The Split-Alternate strategy, without requiring weight tuning, demonstrated robustness and scalability across various systems and controllers, consistently yielding performance improvements.

6. **Varying Source and Magnitude of Uncertainty:**
 - DiffCoTune's performance improvement relative to DiffTune increased with higher mass uncertainty (30% and 45% vs. 15%) for {quadruped, DNN}. At extreme 60% mismatch, both methods degraded, indicating a shift from tuning to a synthesis problem (Figure 8, left).
 - DiffCoTune Split-Alternate consistently improved performance under different types of mismatches, including joint damping and gear ratio, and an amalgamation of all mismatches (dim($\beta$)=5) for {cartpole, DNN}, highlighting its robustness (Figure 8, right).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The DiffCoTune framework represents a systematic approach to addressing the pervasive challenge of transferring robot controllers from simulation to real-world deployment. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Automated and Gradient-based Tuning:** The method provides an automated, gradient-based solution to the sim-to-real gap, moving beyond manual or ad-hoc tuning. By leveraging differentiable simulators and controllers, it enables efficient computation of gradients for high-dimensional parameter spaces.

2. **Enhanced Transferability and Performance:** The co-tuning strategy, which simultaneously adapts both controller and simulator parameters, has been demonstrated to consistently improve controller performance in the deployment domain. This leads to more robust and higher-performing robot behaviors, as evidenced by better stabilization, tracking, and more dynamic tasks like bipedal jumping.

3. **Efficiency with Limited Real-World Interaction:** A crucial aspect of DiffCoTune is its ability to achieve significant performance improvements with a minimal number of rollouts in the target system (e.g., L=5). This is particularly valuable in robotics where real-world experiments are often costly, time-consuming, and potentially damaging to hardware. The rapid convergence of gradient-based methods further contributes to this efficiency.

4. **Scalability and Generality:** The framework's applicability across diverse robotic systems, from low-dimensional cart-poles to high-dimensional quadrupedal and bipedal robots, and various controller types (LQR, MPC, TO, and DNN policies), suggests its broad utility. This generality means it can be applied to a wide range of existing and future robotic control problems without needing task-specific redesigns.

5. **Robustness to Model Discrepancies:** The empirical results indicate that DiffCoTune, particularly its Split-Alternate variant, is robust to varying sources and magnitudes of model uncertainty. This capability to adapt to unknown or complex mismatches in real systems is a key advantage for practical robotics.

6. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work lays a foundation for future advancements in adaptive control and sim-to-real transfer. Potential extensions mentioned include tuning controllers over their entire region of attraction and co-tuning DNN-based residual dynamics models to capture more complex unmodeled effects, further enhancing the fidelity and adaptability of robotic systems.

In summary, DiffCoTune offers a practical, efficient, and robust solution for robot control deployment, potentially accelerating the development and widespread adoption of autonomous robotic systems in complex real-world environments.
