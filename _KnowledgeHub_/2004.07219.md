# D4RL: Datasets for Deep Data-Driven Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> This work introduces D4RL, a benchmark suite of tasks and datasets designed to evaluate offline reinforcement learning algorithms, addressing the need for more realistic and challenging data. The benchmark reveals that current offline RL methods show significant deficiencies when faced with real-world data characteristics like undirected trajectories and mixed policy distributions, despite performing well on simpler RL-generated data.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning benchmarks primarily focus on online data collection, making them unsuitable for evaluating offline (batch) reinforcement learning algorithms.
- There was a lack of standardized, comprehensive datasets for offline RL that accurately reflect the complexities and challenges of real-world data (e.g., narrow distributions, suboptimal behavior, sparse rewards).
- Current evaluation protocols for offline RL often failed to reveal the true limitations of algorithms, hindering progress toward robust and generalizable solutions.

## Method

- Introduces **D4RL**, a new suite of benchmark tasks and datasets specifically designed for ==offline reinforcement learning==.
- Incorporates diverse data properties relevant to real-world applications, including ==narrow/biased distributions==, ==undirected/multitask data==, ==sparse rewards==, and data from human/hand-designed controllers.
- Provides a standardized evaluation protocol with ==normalized scores==, training/evaluation splits, and reference implementations of prominent offline RL algorithms across various domains (MuJoCo, Adroit, FrankaKitchen, Flow, CARLA).

## Results

- Offline RL algorithms generally match or outperform the behavior policy when trained on expert or suboptimal data generated by RL agents (e.g., MuJoCo, Adroit expert data).
- Algorithms demonstrated notable ability to handle sparse reward tasks, sometimes outperforming online baselines.
- Existing algorithms performed poorly on tasks requiring 'stitching' from undirected data (Maze2D, AntMaze, FrankaKitchen, CARLA) and on high-dimensional, perceptually complex domains like CARLA, often yielding near-random performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Offline RL algorithms, while performing well on data generated by online RL agents, struggle significantly with real-world data characteristics such as undirected data, mixed policy distributions, and limited human demonstrations.
> - The ability to 'stitch' together sub-trajectories from diverse, undirected data sources is a major unmet challenge for current offline RL methods.
> - Perceptually complex and partially observable environments (e.g., high-fidelity driving simulators) present extreme difficulties for existing offline RL algorithms, often leading to performance close to random.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of machine learning has witnessed transformative progress driven by the availability of large, diverse datasets, particularly in supervised learning. Deep learning models, leveraging these datasets, have achieved remarkable results across various applications. While deep reinforcement learning (RL) has also benefited from these advancements, its success has largely been predicated on the ability to actively collect data through extensive interaction with an environment. This necessity for active data collection presents a significant bottleneck, limiting the application of RL to domains where data acquisition is costly, dangerous, slow, or simply impossible (e.g., historical medical records, autonomous driving scenarios where errors are catastrophic).

This paper addresses the growing subfield of **offline reinforcement learning (offline RL)**, also known as batch RL or full batch RL. In this setting, an agent learns a policy solely from a pre-collected, static dataset of transitions, without any further interaction with the environment. The promise of offline RL is to bridge the gap between RL and supervised learning, allowing RL methods to leverage vast quantities of previously collected, real-world data—much like how large datasets revolutionized supervised learning. This could unlock profound implications for domains such as robotics, autonomous driving, healthcare, and recommender systems.

However, the authors point out a critical deficiency in the existing research landscape: the lack of suitable and standardized benchmarks for offline RL. Prior works attempting to evaluate offline RL algorithms largely relied on:
1. **Existing Online RL Benchmarks:** These benchmarks, like OpenAI Gym's MuJoCo tasks, were not designed with the unique challenges of offline RL in mind. The data typically used for these tasks in an offline setting is often generated by partially-trained online RL agents, which may not adequately reflect the complexities of real-world data distributions (e.g., narrow, biased, or multi-modal data from human operators or diverse policies).
2. **Limited Offline RL Benchmarks:** While some benchmarks emerged concurrently or previously, they often suffered from similar shortcomings, primarily using data collected from online RL training runs (e.g., discrete Atari domains, or specific locomotion/manipulation tasks with perceptually challenging inputs). As Wu et al. (2019) noted, such limited datasets can fail to differentiate between simple baselines and more sophisticated algorithms, giving a potentially misleading sense of progress.
3. **Proprietary Real-World Datasets:** While offline RL has seen successful real-world applications (e.g., in robotics, recommender systems, dialogue systems), these often rely on private systems or expensive human labeling for evaluation, making them non-scalable and inaccessible for broader research benchmarking. Evaluating candidate policies offline is also challenging due to the need for reliable off-policy evaluation (OPE) methods, which are themselves an active area of research and not yet robust enough for widespread standardization.

D4RL directly addresses this critical gap by providing a **standardized, comprehensive, and widely accessible benchmark suite** that moves beyond the limitations of prior evaluation protocols. It aims to accelerate progress by confronting algorithms with data characteristics more representative of real-world scenarios, thereby revealing true algorithmic deficiencies and guiding future research directions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **introduce D4RL (Datasets for Deep Data-Driven Reinforcement Learning)**, a new suite of benchmark tasks and datasets specifically designed for the offline reinforcement learning setting. This objective is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Address the Lack of Tailored Benchmarks:** The primary motivation stems from the recognition that existing online RL benchmarks and most previous offline RL evaluation protocols are not suitable for rigorously assessing offline RL algorithms. They fail to capture the nuances and complexities of real-world data collection processes.
2. **Reflect Real-World Data Challenges:** The authors are motivated to create a benchmark that incorporates "key properties of datasets relevant to real-world applications." This includes data generated by diverse sources such as hand-designed controllers and human demonstrators, datasets with narrow and biased distributions, multitask datasets where an agent performs different tasks in the same environment, and datasets collected with mixtures of policies. These properties are crucial because they frequently appear in practical scenarios (e.g., passively logged user interactions, expert demonstrations, data from legacy control systems).
3. **Reveal Algorithmic Deficiencies:** A significant motivation is to expose the "important and unappreciated deficiencies of existing algorithms." The authors hypothesize, and later demonstrate, that current algorithms might appear to perform well on simplified benchmarks but struggle significantly when faced with more realistic data characteristics. By uncovering these shortcomings, the benchmark aims to steer future research towards more robust and generalizable offline RL methods.
4. **Standardize Evaluation and Ensure Reproducibility:** To facilitate meaningful comparisons across different research efforts, the authors aim to provide a clear evaluation protocol. This includes a designated split between "training" (for hyperparameter tuning) and "evaluation" tasks, along with a consistent normalization scheme for scores. The goal is to establish a common playing field that ensures reproducibility and allows researchers to accurately gauge true progress.
5. **Accelerate Research Progress:** By offering an open-source, easy-to-use API for tasks and datasets, along with reference implementations of existing algorithms, the authors seek to lower the barrier to entry for researchers in this emerging field. This collaborative approach is intended to foster rapid iteration, identification of algorithmic limitations, and a collective push towards more effective offline RL solutions.
6. **Bridge to Real-World Applications:** Ultimately, the motivation is to accelerate the development of offline RL algorithms that can realistically leverage large, pre-existing datasets in sequential decision-making, thereby enabling real-world applications in domains where active data collection is impractical.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of D4RL involves designing a suite of tasks and datasets based on properties critical to real-world offline RL applications, and then providing a standardized protocol for evaluating algorithms.

#### 3.1. Task Design Factors

The authors systematically identified and incorporated several key properties into their benchmark design:
- **Narrow and Biased Data Distributions:** Common in human demonstrations or hand-crafted policies, these challenge algorithms that assume broad data coverage.
- **Undirected and Multitask Data:** Reflects passively logged data (e.g., user interactions, surveillance footage) where the data may not be specifically directed at the target task, requiring "stitching" of sub-trajectories.
- **Sparse Rewards:** Isolates the credit assignment problem from exploration, a common challenge in real-world RL.
- **Suboptimal Data:** Acknowledges that real-world datasets often contain non-expert behavior, challenging methods that rely solely on optimal demonstrations (e.g., imitation learning).
- **Non-Representable Behavior Policies, Non-Markovian Policies, and Partial Observability:** Addresses situations where the data-generating policy is not within the agent's model class, or where partial observations lead to state ambiguity, introducing additional bias or modeling errors.
- **Realistic Domains:** While acknowledging that real-world evaluation is ideal but impractical for a benchmark, D4RL uses high-quality simulated environments (MuJoCo, Flow, CARLA) that have been battle-tested in prior domain-specific research to strike a balance between realism and reproducibility.

#### 3.2. Tasks and Datasets

The benchmark includes a diverse range of tasks, each designed to embody one or more of the above challenges:
- **Maze2D:** Simple 2D navigation tasks to test "stitching" with non-Markovian planner-generated data. Three maze layouts (umaze, medium, large) are provided.
- **AntMaze:** Similar to Maze2D but with the complex 8-DoF "Ant" robot, adding morphological complexity, sparse rewards, and diverse data generation strategies ("play" and "diverse" from trained policies guided by waypoints).
- **Gym-MuJoCo (Hopper, HalfCheetah, Walker2d):** Standard locomotion tasks but with varied data qualities: "random" (randomly initialized policy), "medium" (partially-trained SAC policy), "medium-replay" (replay buffer data from "medium" training), and crucially, "medium-expert" (mixture of expert and suboptimal data) to test handling of heterogeneous policy mixtures.
- **Adroit:** High-dimensional dexterous manipulation tasks with a 24-DoF Shadow Hand robot (e.g., hammering, door opening, pen twirling). Datasets include "human" demonstrations (small, narrow, sparse reward), "expert" (RL-trained), and "cloned" (imitation policy on human data mixed with demonstrations). This tests learning from limited human demonstrations and narrow, non-representable policy data.
- **FrankaKitchen:** Multitask manipulation with a 9-DoF Franka robot in a kitchen environment. Datasets include "complete" (full task trajectories), "partial," and "mixed" (undirected sub-task demonstrations, requiring significant stitching and generalization).
- **Flow:** Traffic control tasks (ring, merge configurations) involving autonomous vehicles. Data includes "random" and "controller" (from Intelligent Driver Model (IDM), a hand-designed human driving model), challenging algorithms with non-representable human-like behavior.
- **Offline CARLA:** High-fidelity autonomous driving simulator tasks (lane following, town navigation). Observations are 48x48 RGB images, introducing partial observability and visual complexity. Data is generated by hand-designed controllers emulating human driving behavior, designed to test stitching in a perceptually challenging domain.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Protocol

- **Simulated Evaluation:** The use of high-quality simulators allows for accurate and reproducible policy evaluation, overcoming the challenges of real-world deployment or unreliable off-policy evaluation.
- **Training/Evaluation Split:** To mitigate hyperparameter tuning bias, tasks are split into "training" (for tuning) and "evaluation" (for final performance reporting), encouraging robust hyperparameter choices that generalize.
- **Normalized Scores:** Scores are normalized to a 0-100 scale, where 0 represents a random policy and 100 represents a domain-specific expert or the data-collecting controller, facilitating cross-task comparison.
- **Benchmarked Algorithms:** A comprehensive set of prominent offline RL algorithms were evaluated: Behavioral Cloning (BC), SAC (online and offline), Bootstrapping Error Reduction (BEAR), Behavior-Regularized Actor-Critic (BRAC), Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR), Batch-Constrained Q-learning (BCQ), Continuous Action Random Ensemble Mixtures (cREM), and AlgaeDICE. This provides a strong set of baselines for future work.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive benchmarking conducted by the authors revealed several critical insights into the capabilities and limitations of existing offline RL algorithms when faced with D4RL's diverse and challenging datasets.

#### 4.1. Successes on Traditional Settings

- **RL-Generated Data:** Algorithms achieved the most success on datasets generated from an RL-trained policy, particularly in the Gym-MuJoCo and Adroit domains. In these cases, offline RL algorithms were generally able to match the performance of the behavior policy when given expert data, and even outperform it when given suboptimal data. This aligns with and validates previous benchmarks that primarily used such data.
- **Sparse Rewards:** Many methods demonstrated a notable ability to handle sparse reward tasks, specifically in the Adroit and AntMaze domains. Several offline algorithms even outperformed the online SAC baseline in these challenging environments, suggesting that offline RL holds significant promise for scenarios where exploration is difficult and high-reward states are rare.
- **Conservative Methods:** Algorithms that employ conservative approaches, aiming to keep the learned policy close to the data distribution (e.g., BEAR, AWR, CQL, BCQ), showed strong performance on tasks with biased and narrow data distributions, such as those found in Flow and some Gym-MuJoCo datasets.

#### 4.2. Deficiencies Revealed by Realistic Data

The core contribution of D4RL lies in its ability to expose significant limitations of current methods, which were often masked by simpler benchmarks:

- **Undirected and Multitask Data:** Tasks requiring "stitching" of sub-trajectories from undirected data proved highly challenging. The Maze2D, AntMaze, FrankaKitchen, and CARLA domains, designed with this property, severely tested existing algorithms. Even in the relatively simple Maze2D domain, the "large" maze presented a substantial hurdle for most methods. This indicates a major gap in algorithms' ability to generalize and synthesize novel trajectories from disparate experiences.
- **Mixture Distributions and Non-Representable Policies:** Datasets containing mixtures of policies (e.g., "medium-expert" in MuJoCo) or generated by non-representable policies (e.g., human demonstrations in Adroit, IDM controllers in Flow) were particularly difficult. For instance, in MuJoCo's "medium-expert" data (which contains expert trajectories), algorithms often performed only on par with "medium" datasets, failing to effectively leverage the expert data when mixed with suboptimal behavior. Similarly, in Adroit's "cloned" datasets, the algorithms struggled to significantly outperform performance achieved on the limited "human" demonstration data, despite access to additional data.
- **Limited Data (Human Demonstrations):** Tasks relying on small datasets of human demonstrations, like in Adroit and FrankaKitchen, remained highly challenging for most methods. This highlights a need for more sample-efficient offline RL algorithms capable of learning effectively from scarce, high-quality data.
- **High-Dimensional, Perceptually Complex Domains (CARLA):** The CARLA autonomous driving tasks, characterized by partial observability and complex visual observations, presented an extreme challenge. Almost all benchmarked algorithms performed poorly, often failing to significantly exceed random performance. This indicates that current offline RL methods are far from being able to handle realistic, sensor-rich, partially observable environments.

#### 4.3. Overall Observation

The most crucial finding is that as the benchmark transitioned from simple tasks and RL-trained data to those incorporating real-world complexities—such as undirected data, narrow distributions, human demonstrations, and challenging perceptual inputs—the performance disparities between algorithms became starker, and "deficiencies in all algorithms are revealed." This underscores the necessity of D4RL's design philosophy to provide a more accurate measure of progress.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

D4RL represents a highly significant contribution to the field of reinforcement learning, particularly to the burgeoning area of offline RL. Its potential impact spans several key dimensions:

1. **Standardization and Reproducibility:** By providing a comprehensive, open-source suite of tasks and datasets with a clear evaluation protocol, D4RL establishes a much-needed common ground for researchers. This standardization enables direct and fair comparisons between different offline RL algorithms, reducing the current fragmentation in benchmarking and accelerating cumulative progress. Researchers no longer need to spend extensive effort curating their own datasets, freeing them to focus on algorithmic innovation.
2. **Driving Research Towards Real-World Relevance:** The benchmark's deliberate design, incorporating properties like narrow data distributions, undirected/multitask data, sparse rewards, and non-representable policies, forces the research community to confront the complexities inherent in real-world data. By exposing "unappreciated deficiencies" in existing algorithms, D4RL highlights critical areas for future development, such as:
 - Algorithms capable of effectively "stitching" together disparate trajectories to solve long-horizon tasks.
 - Methods robust to diverse and mixed data distributions, including suboptimal and expert behaviors.
 - Sample-efficient algorithms that can learn from limited amounts of high-quality data, like human demonstrations.
 - Approaches that can handle partial observability and learn from high-dimensional, perceptually rich inputs in an offline manner.
 The benchmark effectively shifts the focus of offline RL research from idealized settings to problems with higher practical utility.
3. **Unlocking New Application Domains:** The successful development of robust offline RL algorithms, facilitated by D4RL, has the potential to unlock a vast array of real-world applications where active data collection is prohibitive. These include:
 - **Robotics:** Learning complex manipulation skills from human demonstrations or logged robot failures, without needing costly real-world interaction during training.
 - **Autonomous Driving:** Training driving policies from vast archives of logged vehicle data, including human driving logs, without risking safety during exploration.
 - **Healthcare:** Developing personalized treatment strategies from patient medical records.
 - **Recommender Systems:** Optimizing recommendation policies from historical user interaction logs.
 - **Industrial Control and Finance:** Learning optimal control policies from passively logged operational data.
 By making RL applicable to "big data" settings, D4RL could enable a revolution similar to what large datasets brought to supervised learning.
4. **Bridging the Academia-Industry Gap:** The involvement of researchers from both UC Berkeley and Google Brain ensures that the benchmark is not only theoretically sound but also practically relevant, aligning academic advancements with industrial challenges.
5. **Foundation for Future Work:** D4RL serves as a foundational resource. Beyond providing datasets and tasks, it offers reference implementations and a clear evaluation protocol, lowering the barrier for new researchers to enter the field. The discussion also thoughtfully points to further unexplored challenges, such as environmental stochasticity, large action spaces, and the ultimate need for reliable off-policy evaluation methods and real-world benchmarks, thus guiding the next generation of research.

In summary, D4RL is a pivotal contribution that sets a new standard for evaluating and advancing offline reinforcement learning. By confronting algorithms with realistic data challenges, it promises to accelerate the development of more robust, generalizable, and deployable RL systems for a wide range of real-world applications.
