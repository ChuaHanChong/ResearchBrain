# No Free Lunch: Rethinking Internal Feedback for LLM Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> This study reveals a "no free lunch" principle for Reinforcement Learning from Internal Feedback (RLIF) in LLM reasoning, showing it initially benefits base models by reducing underconfidence but degrades performance with prolonged training by inducing overconfidence. RLIF effectively improves instruction following in models with high initial policy entropy but offers little benefit and can be detrimental to already instruction-tuned models.

## Problem

- Existing RL approaches for LLMs (RLHF, RLVR) are heavily reliant on costly and labor-intensive external supervision.
- RLVR, while improving single-answer accuracy (pass@1), often struggles to fundamentally enhance broader multi-step reasoning capabilities (pass@N).
- A systematic understanding of Reinforcement Learning from Internal Feedback (RLIF) was lacking, specifically regarding its conditions for effectiveness, optimal internal signals, and long-term training dynamics.

## Method

- Conducted theoretical analysis demonstrating that diverse ==internal feedback signals== are partially equivalent and fundamentally ==minimize policy entropy==.
- Empirically evaluated ==Reinforcement Learning from Internal Feedback (RLIF)== using various Qwen models on challenging math reasoning benchmarks (AIME2025, MATH500, GSM8K) within a reinforcement learning framework.
- Employed diagnostic techniques including ==policy entropy tracking==, =="transitional word" analysis==, and ==model merging== experiments to understand the training dynamics and the relationship between initial policy entropy and RLIF effectiveness.

## Results

- RLIF initially boosts reasoning performance for base LLMs, but performance consistently declines with prolonged training, sometimes leading to "training collapse".
- RLIF provides minimal to no improvement and often degrades performance for already instruction-tuned models.
- All three investigated RLIF methods (self-certainty, token-level entropy, trajectory-level entropy) consistently reduce policy entropy during training and decrease the frequency of "transitional words," confirming a shift from mitigating underconfidence to inducing overconfidence.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RLIF's effectiveness is inversely related to a model's initial policy entropy: base models with high entropy benefit, while low-entropy instruction-tuned models do not and may degrade.
> - Internal feedback signals fundamentally operate by minimizing policy entropy, making the model more confident and deterministic in its outputs.
> - The performance dynamics of RLIF are characterized by a two-stage process: initial gains from mitigating underconfidence (reducing excessive deliberation) followed by degradation due to inducing overconfidence (suppressing beneficial deliberation).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper is situated at the intersection of large language models (LLMs), reinforcement learning (RL), and the rapidly evolving field of LLM reasoning. Recent advancements in LLMs have been significantly boosted by sophisticated RL techniques, primarily Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR).

#### 1.1. Evolution of LLM Post-Training

Initial LLM capabilities were primarily driven by massive pre-training. However, as the effectiveness of pre-training approached a plateau, post-training methods became crucial. RLHF (Bai et al., 2022) emerged as a dominant paradigm to align LLMs with human preferences and improve instruction following. More recently, RLVR techniques (e.g., GRPO by Shao et al., 2024, used in DeepSeek-R1 and Qwen3) have shown promise in enhancing specific capabilities like mathematical and code reasoning by leveraging automatically verifiable ground truth signals as rewards.

#### 1.2. Limitations of Existing RL Approaches

Despite their success, RLHF and RLVR face significant challenges. They are heavily reliant on extensive external supervision—human annotations for RLHF or ground truth labels for RLVR. This dependency on external data can be costly, labor-intensive, and difficult to scale. Furthermore, as highlighted by Yue et al. (2025a), RLVR approaches, while improving pass@1 metrics, often struggle to surpass the inherent reasoning capacity limits of large models, sometimes merely shifting the output distribution rather than genuinely enhancing reasoning boundaries. This suggests that while models might become better at producing a single correct answer (pass@1), their broader multi-step reasoning capabilities (pass@N) might not fundamentally improve, or their scope might even narrow.

#### 1.3. Emergence of Reinforcement Learning from Internal Feedback (RLIF)

In response to these limitations, the concept of Reinforcement Learning from Internal Feedback (RLIF) has emerged (Liu, 2025; Zhao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). RLIF represents a paradigm shift by leveraging intrinsic model-derived signals (such as self-certainty, token-level entropy, or trajectory-level entropy) as reward signals, thereby reducing or eliminating the need for external supervision. This unsupervised approach holds immense promise for more efficient and scalable LLM training.

#### 1.4. Contribution to RLIF Understanding

This paper significantly contributes to the nascent RLIF landscape by moving beyond simply proposing new internal reward signals to systematically *rethinking* their underlying mechanisms and effectiveness. While prior works introduced various RLIF signals, this study provides a unified theoretical framework demonstrating their partial equivalence and showing how they fundamentally lead to policy entropy minimization. Critically, it identifies and empirically validates the conditions under which RLIF is beneficial and, more importantly, when it becomes detrimental. By uncovering the "no free lunch" aspect—that RLIF can degrade performance for already instruction-tuned models or with prolonged training—the paper offers a crucial diagnostic lens for understanding LLM reasoning behavior (e.g., through "transitional words" and shifts between underconfidence and overconfidence). This work provides foundational insights and practical guidelines, informing the development of more principled and robust RLIF strategies for future LLM post-training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper's core objectives are driven by the challenges and opportunities presented by Reinforcement Learning from Internal Feedback (RLIF) for enhancing Large Language Model (LLM) reasoning capabilities.

#### 2.1. Fundamental Questions on RLIF Effectiveness

The primary objective is to systematically investigate the conditions under which RLIF methods are effective and to determine which specific internal feedback signals yield the best performance gains. This addresses a critical gap in the understanding of unsupervised reinforcement learning for LLMs, moving beyond mere empirical application to a deeper causal analysis. The authors seek to answer "Under what conditions does the RLIF method work, and which internal feedback signal provides the best performance gain?"

#### 2.2. Addressing Supervision Dependency

A key motivation stems from the inherent limitations of current RL approaches like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). Both require extensive external supervision (human preferences or ground-truth labels), which is costly, time-consuming, and limits scalability. RLIF offers a compelling alternative by deriving reward signals intrinsically from the model's own output distribution, thereby reducing or eliminating reliance on labeled data and facilitating unsupervised training. The paper is motivated to explore the viability and mechanics of this less supervised paradigm.

#### 2.3. Unpacking RLIF Mechanisms and Training Dynamics

The authors aim to gain a deeper theoretical and empirical understanding of *how* RLIF influences LLM behavior, particularly in the context of reasoning. They hypothesize that internal feedback signals are related to policy entropy and seek to confirm this relationship. A significant motivation is to explain observed performance shifts, such as initial improvements followed by degradation or "training collapse." This involves analyzing changes in policy entropy, the model's confidence levels, and specific linguistic patterns like the frequency of "transitional words" during the training process. The goal is to diagnose why RLIF training leads to certain outcomes (e.g., shifts from underconfidence to overconfidence).

#### 2.4. Providing Practical Guidelines for LLM Post-Training

Ultimately, the research seeks to provide actionable insights for LLM developers and researchers. By delineating the precise conditions under which RLIF is beneficial and identifying its limitations, the paper aims to offer practical guidelines for effectively integrating internal feedback signals into future LLM reinforcement learning setups. This includes advising on which types of models (base vs. instruction-tuned) are suitable for RLIF and how to potentially mitigate the adverse effects of prolonged training or excessive entropy minimization.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper employs a comprehensive methodology combining theoretical analysis with rigorous empirical experimentation and diagnostic techniques to achieve its objectives.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Analysis of RLIF Mechanisms

The theoretical core of the paper focuses on demonstrating the underlying mechanism of various internal feedback signals.
- **Selection of RLIF Signals:** The study concentrates on three representative internal feedback signals: self-certainty (Zhao et al., 2025), token-level entropy (Agarwal et al., 2025), and trajectory-level entropy (Agarwal et al., 2025). These signals are derived directly from the model's output distribution, reflecting its internal confidence or uncertainty.
- **Equivalence and Policy Entropy Minimization:** The paper formally analyzes these signals, proposing that they are partially equivalent and fundamentally optimize a common objective: the minimization of policy entropy.
- **Propositions and Proofs:** It provides three key propositions (Propositions 1, 2, and 3), supported by lemmas (Lemma 1, 2, and 3), which mathematically demonstrate that using these selected internal feedback signals as reward functions in the reinforcement learning objective directly leads to a reduction in the model's policy entropy. This theoretical foundation is crucial for explaining the empirical observations.

#### 3.2. Empirical Evaluation Setup

A robust experimental setup is designed to test the theoretical claims and explore RLIF's practical effectiveness.
- **Benchmarks:** Models are evaluated on challenging math reasoning benchmarks: AIME2025 (an unseen competition dataset), MATH500 (a subset of the MATH dataset), and GSM8K (grade school math word problems). These tasks require multi-step reasoning, making them suitable for evaluating reasoning capabilities.
- **Training Data and Framework:** All experiments are conducted on the training split of the MATH dataset (7,500 problems). The training process utilizes the VeRL framework (Sheng et al., 2024), a reinforcement learning framework, suggesting a sophisticated and scalable setup.
- **Base Models:** The study employs several Qwen models as base LLMs: Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, and Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math. This diverse set allows for observing RLIF's effects across different model sizes and pre-training stages.
- **RLIF Implementation:** The three chosen RLIF methods (Self-certainty, Trajectory-level Entropy, Token-level Entropy) are implemented within a reinforcement learning context, likely adapting a GRPO-like (Shao et al., 2024) approach where internal signals replace external verifiable rewards.

#### 3.3. Diagnostic Analysis Techniques

Beyond standard performance metrics, the paper employs several novel diagnostic approaches to understand *why* certain behaviors occur.
- **Performance Monitoring:** Tracking average accuracy on validation datasets across various training steps to observe performance dynamics (initial gain, subsequent degradation).
- **Policy Entropy Tracking:** Directly monitoring the average policy entropy of the models during training to empirically confirm its reduction as predicted by theory.
- **Transitional Word Analysis:** Quantifying the frequency of "transitional words" (e.g., "but," "wait," "therefore") in model responses. These high-entropy tokens are indicative of multi-step reasoning and deliberation. Their change in frequency provides insight into shifts in the model's reasoning process.
- **Model Merging:** A particularly insightful technique, where models are created by linearly interpolating parameters between a base model (high entropy) and an instruction-tuned model (low entropy) at different "merge ratios." Training these merged models allows the researchers to systematically map the relationship between initial policy entropy and the effectiveness of RLIF, providing strong empirical validation for Claim 1.
- **Response Content Analysis:** Categorizing model outputs into "Right Answer/Right Format" (RA/RF), "Wrong Answer/Right Format" (WA/RF), "Right Answer/Wrong Format" (RA/WF), and "Wrong Answer/Wrong Format" (WA/WF). This fine-grained analysis helps differentiate improvements in instruction-following (format) from genuine reasoning ability (correctness) and diagnose shifts in confidence (underconfidence vs. overconfidence).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several critical findings that shed light on the efficacy and limitations of Reinforcement Learning from Internal Feedback (RLIF) for LLM reasoning.

#### 4.1. Performance Dynamics of RLIF on Base LLMs

- **Initial Improvement:** For base LLMs (e.g., Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B), RLIF training consistently leads to an initial boost in reasoning performance on benchmarks like MATH500 and GSM8K. This early phase (typically before 20 training steps) can see performance matching or even surpassing some RLVR techniques.
- **Subsequent Degradation:** However, this initial gain is not sustained. As training progresses beyond approximately 20 steps, the performance of most base models gradually declines, sometimes falling below their pre-training performance levels. In some cases, "training collapse" is observed, characterized by excessively large gradient norms and drastically shortened, non-sensical responses (Table 4).
- **Exception (In-Domain Knowledge):** The Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math model, which is likely already heavily fine-tuned on mathematical data, exhibits continuous performance improvement throughout RLIF training, indicating a different behavior from general base models.

#### 4.2. RLIF on Instruction-Tuned Models

- **Diminishing Returns/Degradation:** In stark contrast to base models, RLIF yields little to no improvement for instruction-tuned models (e.g., Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct). In many cases, their performance degrades under RLIF training (Table 5). This suggests that once an LLM has undergone human-aligned fine-tuning, intrinsic feedback might become less effective or even detrimental.

#### 4.3. Theoretical Confirmation: Policy Entropy Minimization

- The theoretical analysis is strongly supported by empirical observations. The study confirms that all three investigated RLIF methods (self-certainty, token-level entropy, trajectory-level entropy) consistently drive a reduction in the model's policy entropy during training (Figure 4b). This means the model becomes increasingly "confident" and its output distribution more deterministic.

#### 4.4. Relationship between Initial Policy Entropy and RLIF Effectiveness (Claim 1)

- **High Entropy Benefits:** Models with higher initial policy entropy (typically base models) are more adaptive to RLIF optimization and show greater performance gains.
- **Low Entropy Suffers:** Models with lower initial policy entropy (instruction-tuned models) exhibit minimal or negative performance changes.
- **Model Merging Validation:** The model merging experiments provide compelling evidence for this claim (Figure 3). Interpolating between base and instruct models at different merge ratios directly demonstrates that models with a smaller "merge ratio" (i.e., more similar to the high-entropy base model) achieve higher initial entropy and greater performance improvements under RLIF. Conversely, models with higher merge ratios (closer to the low-entropy instruct model) show negligible gains.

#### 4.5. Role of Transitional Words and Confidence Shift (Claim 2)

- **Reduced Transitional Words:** A consistent finding is that RLIF training leads to a significant decrease in the frequency of "transitional words" (logical connectors and terms indicating deliberation, which tend to have high entropy) in model responses (Table 3).
- **From Underconfidence to Overconfidence:**
 - **Initial Improvement (Mitigating Underconfidence):** The early performance gains are primarily attributed to improved *instruction-following* rather than enhanced reasoning. The reduction in transitional words helps curb "underconfidence" (excessive deliberation, rambling, or off-format outputs), allowing the model to produce more concise, goal-directed responses within length limits and in the correct format (evident from a decrease in "Wrong Format" responses in Table 2).
 - **Subsequent Degradation (Inducing Overconfidence):** As training progresses and the policy entropy continues to drop, the suppression of high-entropy tokens (transitional words) becomes excessive. This impairs the model's ability to explore alternative reasoning paths or self-correct, leading to "overconfidence" – shallow reasoning and premature, often incorrect, conclusions. The total number of right answers decreases significantly in this phase (Table 2), indicating a degradation in actual reasoning capability despite appearing more "confident."
 - **Resilience of Knowledge-Rich Models:** Models with strong prior in-domain knowledge (like Qwen2.5-1.5B-Math) are more resilient to this overconfidence effect. Their established reasoning skills allow them to maintain effective internal deliberation even with fewer transitional words, enabling continuous improvement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers significant contributions to the field of large language models and reinforcement learning, with potential impacts on both theoretical understanding and practical application.

#### 5.1. Deeper Theoretical and Empirical Understanding of RLIF

The paper moves beyond the empirical application of RLIF to provide a unified theoretical framework. By formally demonstrating that diverse internal feedback signals (self-certainty, token-level entropy, trajectory-level entropy) are partially equivalent and fundamentally drive policy entropy minimization, it provides a crucial mechanistic explanation for observed behaviors. This theoretical grounding helps demystify *why* RLIF works and, critically, *why it fails* under certain conditions. This is a substantial step toward building more principled and predictable LLM training paradigms.

#### 5.2. Practical Guidelines for LLM Post-Training

Perhaps the most immediate impact is the provision of clear, actionable guidelines for practitioners fine-tuning LLMs using internal feedback:
- **Target Model Selection:** RLIF is best suited for *base models* with high initial policy entropy. It can provide significant initial boosts in performance, particularly in improving instruction-following and mitigating underconfidence.
- **Avoid Instruction-Tuned Models:** The study clearly shows that RLIF offers little to no benefit, and often causes degradation, for models that have already undergone instruction-tuning. This highlights the diminishing returns of intrinsic feedback once a model has converged to a lower entropy state.
- **Beware of Prolonged Training:** The "no free lunch" aspect means that continuous RLIF training can lead to performance degradation and overconfidence. Practitioners must be wary of over-optimizing for entropy minimization, suggesting the need for careful early stopping or dynamic training strategies.
- **Leverage In-Domain Knowledge:** Models with strong in-domain knowledge (like specialized math models) appear more robust to the negative effects of entropy reduction, indicating that a solid knowledge base can mitigate overconfidence issues.

#### 5.3. Informing Future Research Directions

The findings open several avenues for future research:
- **Balanced RLIF Objectives:** Future RLIF algorithms could aim for a more balanced objective that reduces entropy sufficiently to mitigate underconfidence but avoids excessive minimization that leads to overconfidence and superficial reasoning. This might involve dynamic entropy regularization or multi-objective optimization.
- **Adaptive Feedback Signals:** Research could explore internal feedback signals that adapt to the model's current entropy level or reasoning stage, providing different types of intrinsic rewards as the model matures.
- **Hybrid Approaches:** Combining RLIF with sparse external supervision or other techniques might create more robust and universally applicable post-training methods.
- **Understanding Deliberation:** The analysis of "transitional words" underscores the importance of modeling and preserving the LLM's internal deliberation process. Future work could focus on how to maintain rich, high-entropy reasoning paths without leading to verbose or off-topic responses.

#### 5.4. Broader Implications for LLM Development

This work contributes to the growing understanding of the complex interplay between different LLM training stages and their impact on core capabilities like reasoning. It highlights that optimizing for seemingly beneficial internal signals (like confidence/certainty) can have unintended negative consequences if not carefully managed. It reinforces the idea that true reasoning capacity in LLMs is not merely about producing correct answers, but also about the underlying process of deliberation, exploration, and self-correction, which can be inadvertently suppressed by certain optimization strategies.
