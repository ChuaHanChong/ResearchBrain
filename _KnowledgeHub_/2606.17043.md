# Hierarchical Advantage Weighting for Online RL Fine-Tuning of VLAs from Sparse Episode Outcomes

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ACE Robotics, Tsinghua University, and The Chinese University of Hong Kong developed HABC, a method for online RL fine-tuning of Vision-Language-Action models that decomposes sparse episode outcomes into viability and efficiency signals using a dual-head critic and employs intervention-aware credit assignment. This approach improved success rates on real-robot bimanual manipulation tasks up to 92% from lower baselines and enabled autonomous error recovery.

## Problem

- Existing online RL fine-tuning methods for VLA policies conflate distinct viability (can it succeed?) and efficiency (how fast?) information when using sparse, binary episode outcomes as per-transition supervision.
- Incorrectly assigning credit in mixed-control episodes (containing both autonomous policy execution and human interventions) can corrupt the learning signal by misattributing success/failure to the wrong controller.
- Generating dense, per-transition gradients for generative action policies (like flow matching models) from sparse outcomes is challenging and often sample-inefficient.

## Method

- **HABC** employs a ==dual-head critic== with a viability head (VvV_vVv) estimating success probability and an efficiency head (VeV_eVe) estimating negative steps to success, trained on different subsets of online data.
- A state-adaptive gate dynamically blends the viability and efficiency advantages, prioritizing viability when success is uncertain and efficiency when success is probable, to generate per-transition weights for the actor's flow-matching loss.
- An intervention-aware credit assignment mechanism attributes the final episode outcome only to the post-intervention policy execution suffix, while intervention segments are used as imitation data without outcome labels.

## Results

- HABC achieved the highest success rates across all three real-robot bimanual manipulation tasks (Pencil Pouch: **92%**, Paper Bag: **88%**, Snack Bag: **38%**) compared to SFT and other online RL baselines.
- The full HABC method consistently reduced mean trajectory length on successful trials compared to the viability-only HABC-V variant, demonstrating improved task efficiency through the efficiency head.
- Qualitative analysis showed HABC-trained policies learned autonomous error recovery capabilities, detecting failures and executing corrective motions to complete tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decomposing sparse episode outcomes into distinct viability and efficiency signals provides more granular and effective supervision, enabling VLA policies to learn both task completion and optimal progression.
> - Accurate credit assignment, achieved by segmenting episodes based on control authority (policy vs. human), is crucial for preventing misattribution of outcomes and ensuring cleaner learning signals for the policy.
> - A state-adaptive weighting function that prioritizes viability in uncertain states and efficiency in more confident states allows for robust learning across different stages of policy development.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses challenges in fine-tuning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic control, particularly through online reinforcement learning (RL) from sparse feedback. Pretrained VLA policies, such as RT-1, RT-2, Octo, OpenVLA, and $\pi^0$, have demonstrated generalization capabilities across manipulation tasks. However, relying solely on supervised fine-tuning (SFT) from demonstrations is limited by data coverage and susceptible to covariate shift during deployment. This necessitates online RL fine-tuning to allow policies to learn from their own errors, improve robustness, and adapt to new conditions.

A central problem in online RL fine-tuning for robotics is the conversion of sparse, often binary, episode outcomes (e.g., success or failure) into dense, per-transition supervision required for effective actor updates. Existing online RL fine-tuning methods for VLAs, such as HIL-SERL, ConRFT, RIPT-VLA, iRe-VLA, VLA-RL, and SimpleVLA-RL, improve performance through interaction but often rely on standard RL mechanisms without specifically addressing how sparse outcomes should be processed, especially in mixed-control (autonomous and human intervention) episodes.

This work specifically engages with methods that optimize generative action policies, such as those based on flow matching. Policy-gradient approaches for these models can be sample-inefficient due to the need to differentiate through the generative process. HABC, in contrast, trains critics via TD learning and converts their outputs into per-transition weights for a supervised flow-matching loss, bypassing policy-gradient estimation.

The paper also relates to intervention-based imitation learning techniques like DAgger, HG-DAgger, IWR, Sirius, AIM, RaC, and MILE. These methods leverage human interventions as direct supervision or to improve data efficiency. However, they typically treat intervention windows as data to imitate, without explicitly tackling the problem of correctly attributing episode outcomes across mixed-control segments within an episode.

Furthermore, HABC builds upon research in advantage-weighted and advantage-conditioned actor updates, including AWR, AWAC, IQL, CQL, Decision Transformer, AWM, Recap, and LWD. These methods use critic-derived advantages to weight or condition actor updates. Recap, for instance, converts a reward-derived advantage into a prompt token for the actor. HABC takes a complementary approach by maintaining an unchanged actor input and instead using two outcome-derived critic heads to produce bounded per-transition loss weights, thereby decomposing the sparse outcome into viability and efficiency signals. This decomposition and the intervention-aware credit assignment distinguish HABC from prior work by providing a more structured and granular approach to utilizing sparse, mixed-control online data for VLA fine-tuning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The research is motivated by two primary challenges encountered during the online reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies when learning from sparse episode outcomes:

**Challenge 1: Conflation of Distinct Information in Sparse Outcomes**
In online RL for robot manipulation, each interaction episode typically yields a single, binary outcome (e.g., success or failure). However, effective actor updates require per-transition supervision. The primary issue is that a single scalar reward or advantage signal derived from this sparse outcome conflates two fundamentally different forms of transition-level feedback:
- **Viability:** Whether the current state and subsequent actions can still lead to task completion. This signal is crucial early in training when failures are frequent, guiding the policy away from unrecoverable states.
- **Efficiency:** Given that success is achievable, whether the current transition is making optimal progress towards completion or wasting time/effort. This signal becomes important later in training when success rates are high, differentiating efficient trajectories from slow or circuitous ones.
A single scalar signal, as used in many existing methods (e.g., Recap), cannot adequately distinguish between these two objectives. Once a basic success rate is achieved, the binary success label provides minimal gradient to differentiate efficient completions from slower ones, limiting further policy refinement.

**Challenge 2: Incorrect Credit Assignment in Mixed-Control Episodes**
Real-world robot fine-tuning often involves human intervention, where an operator takes control to correct policy mistakes or guide the robot through difficult parts of a task. When autonomous execution segments and human intervention segments are mixed within a single episode, the final episode outcome reflects the combined performance of both the policy and the human. Naively assigning this outcome label uniformly across all timesteps in such mixed-control episodes can lead to incorrect credit assignment:
- It might inadvertently upweight policy mistakes that led to a state requiring human intervention, effectively reinforcing undesirable behavior.
- Conversely, it could incorrectly penalize the human's corrective actions if the episode ultimately fails, misattributing the failure to the human's efforts.
Prior work acknowledges data quality issues in online rollouts but typically does not explicitly differentiate control authority within an episode. This lack of precise attribution can contaminate the learning signal for both the critic and the actor.

**Key Objectives:**
To address these challenges, the main objectives of this work are:
1. To develop a method for decomposing the sparse binary episode outcome into distinct, separable transition-level signals for viability and efficiency.
2. To design a critic architecture that learns these decomposed signals and combines them adaptively to generate per-transition weights for actor updates.
3. To establish an intervention-aware credit assignment mechanism that correctly attributes episode outcomes to the executing controller (policy or human) within mixed-control rollouts, ensuring cleaner supervision.
4. To empirically demonstrate that this hierarchical approach significantly improves the success rate and efficiency of VLA fine-tuning on complex real-robot manipulation tasks, leading to more robust policies capable of autonomous error recovery.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Hierarchical Advantage-Weighted Behavior Cloning (HABC), fine-tunes a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) actor by converting sparse episode outcomes into per-transition weights for a flow-matching loss. It achieves this through a dual-head critic and intervention-aware credit assignment.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup

The robot observes a state $s_t = (I_t, q_t, \ell)$ comprising multi-view images, proprioception, and a language task prompt, and executes an action chunk $a_t$. Episodes are segmented by controller (policy or human). Training samples are referred to as "windows," and the final policy execution segment after the last intervention is a "post-intervention policy execution suffix." The VLA actor is trained with a weighted flow-matching loss:
$L_\pi = \frac{1}{B} \sum_{i=1}^{B} w_i \| v_\theta(s_i, a_i, \sigma) - u_i \|^2_2$, where $v_\theta$ is the flow-matching velocity, $u_i$ is the ground-truth flow target, and $w_i$ is a scalar weight. Online fine-tuning uses demonstrations ($D_{SFT}$), autonomous rollouts ($D_{auto}$), and human-intervention data ($D_{int}$).

#### 3.2. Dual-Head Critic

HABC addresses the conflation of viability and efficiency signals by employing a dual-head critic built on a shared backbone $\phi(s)$:
- **Viability Head ($V_v(s)$):** Estimates $p_v(s) = P(\text{success} | s)$, the probability of eventual task success under the current policy. It outputs logits $z_v(s) = f_v(\phi(s))$ which are converted to probabilities via sigmoid. $V_v$ is trained using binary cross-entropy (BCE) against the episode outcome $y$ on *all* outcome-labeled policy execution windows (successful and failed). This makes it informative even when the success rate is low, as it learns from both positive and negative outcomes.
- **Efficiency Head ($V_e(s)$):** Estimates the negative number of steps to success from state $s$. It outputs a scalar $\hat{V}_e(s) = f_e(\phi(s))$. $V_e$ is trained only on *successful* trajectories (successful policy-execution and intervention windows) where the steps-to-success target is well-defined. Non-terminal actions receive a one-step cost of -1, and the terminal success action is assigned a target of 0: $y_e(s_t) = 0$ if $s_t$ is a terminal success state, and $-1 + \text{sg}[\hat{V}_e(s_{t+1})]$ otherwise (where sg denotes stop-gradient). It is trained with Huber loss, with target values naturally bounded in $[-T_{max}, 0]$. As the policy improves, $V_e$ becomes the primary signal for distinguishing fast from slow progress.
The joint critic loss ($L_{critic}$) combines BCE for $V_v$ and Huber loss for $V_e$, trained on their respective data subsets.

#### 3.3. Advantage-Weighted Actor Update

The outputs of the dual-head critic are converted into per-transition weights for the actor loss:
- **Viability Advantage ($A_v$):** Measures the improvement in predicted viability in logit space: $A_v = z_v(s_{t+1}) - z_v(s_t)$.
- **Efficiency Advantage ($A_e$):** Measures if an action shortens steps-to-success faster than expected: $A_e = -1 + \hat{V}_e(s_{t+1}) - \hat{V}_e(s_t)$.
These two advantages are combined through a **state-adaptive gate $g_t$**:
$g_t = 1 + \tanh \left( (1 - p_v(s_t)) A_v + p_v(s_t) A_e \right)$.
This gate adaptively blends the signals: when $p_v(s_t)$ is low (success is uncertain), it prioritizes $A_v$ to emphasize viability. When $p_v(s_t)$ is high, it prioritizes $A_e$ to emphasize efficiency. The resulting $g_t$ provides a bounded, per-transition weighting signal for the actor.

#### 3.4. Intervention-Aware Credit Assignment

To prevent incorrect credit assignment in episodes with human interventions, HABC partitions each episode by control authority. The key principle is that the final episode outcome (success/failure) is attributed only to segments unequivocally executed by the current policy.
- For fully autonomous episodes, the entire trajectory is labeled with the episode outcome $y$.
- For episodes with human interventions, *only the post-intervention policy execution suffix* receives the outcome label. This segment is where the current policy takes over from a potentially human-corrected state.
- Pre-intervention policy execution segments and the intervention segments themselves are *never outcome-labeled* for $V_v$. Intervention windows are still used as imitation supervision for the actor and as progress targets for $V_e$, leveraging human corrective actions as demonstrations without erroneously attributing the episode outcome to them.

#### 3.5. Training Procedure

The pre-normalization weight $\tilde{w}_i$ is set as follows:
- $\tilde{w}_i = g_t$ for successful online rollout samples.
- $\tilde{w}_i = 0$ for failed online rollout samples.
- $\tilde{w}_i = 1$ for SFT and intervention samples initially. When "intervention reweighting (IR)" is enabled (after an initial HABC phase), $\tilde{w}_i = g_t$ for intervention samples as well.
These weights are then normalized via unit-mean normalization ($w_i = \tilde{w}_i / \max(c, \epsilon)$) to decouple weighting from the effective learning rate. A warmup phase of $N_{wu}$ steps keeps $g_t = 1$ to allow critic heads to calibrate.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of HABC was evaluated through real-robot experiments on three contact-rich bimanual manipulation tasks involving deformable objects: Pencil Pouch, Paper Bag, and Snack Bag.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Tasks:** Pencil Pouch (insert marker, zip pouch), Paper Bag (open, stand upright, insert bottle), Snack Bag (place items, close drawstring). All involve multi-stage bimanual coordination.
- **Robot:** ARX X5 bimanual robot with three RGB camera streams.
- **Action Space:** End-effector frame, chunk size 50 (training), 25 (inference).
- **Base VLA:** $\pi^{0.5}$, initialized from a pretrained offline checkpoint.
- **Baselines:**
 - **SFT:** Supervised fine-tuning from demonstrations, no online data.
 - **Imit-DAgger:** 50/50 mix of SFT and intervention data, no rollout data, no critic reweighting.
 - **Imit-Recap:** Hard-threshold filtering on a single critic's TD residual (adapted from), no intervention-aware credit assignment.
 - **HABC-V:** HABC variant using only the viability head ($V_v$), with the efficiency head ($V_e$) ablated.
 - **HABC:** The full proposed method.
- **Metrics:** Success rate (over 50 trials, with 95% Wilson confidence intervals) and mean trajectory length (number of action frames on successful trials, with standard deviation).
- **Training Protocol:** Step 1 compares initial online fine-tuning with an equal budget. Step 2 evaluates continued training from the HABC checkpoint, with and without intervention reweighting (HABC+IR).

#### 4.2. Main Results (Quantitative)

- **Initial Online Fine-Tuning (Step 1):**
 - HABC achieved the highest success rates across all three tasks: 60% on Pencil Pouch, 78% on Paper Bag, and 22% on Snack Bag.
 - HABC-V, using only the viability head, surpassed all non-HABC baselines (SFT, Imit-DAgger, Imit-Recap) on every task. This suggests that the soft, viability-based weighting extracts more effective supervision from sparse outcomes than hard-threshold filtering.
 - Imit-DAgger underperformed SFT on Pencil Pouch and Snack Bag, indicating that a simple 50/50 mix of SFT and intervention data can bias training away from general task execution.
 - Comparing HABC to HABC-V, the full HABC consistently reduced mean trajectory length on successful trials across all tasks (by -55, -162, and -32 frames respectively). This indicates that the efficiency head effectively distinguishes and prioritizes more direct and efficient task completions.
- **Continued Training with Intervention Reweighting (Step 2):**
 - Starting from the HABC checkpoint, continued training with HABC+IR (applying $g_t$ to intervention windows) further improved performance.
 - The best observed HABC+IR checkpoints reached significantly higher success rates compared to SFT baselines:
 - **Pencil Pouch:** 92% (from 36% SFT baseline)
 - **Paper Bag:** 88% (from 44% SFT baseline)
 - **Snack Bag:** 38% (from 12% SFT baseline)

#### 4.3. Critic and Weight Analysis

- **Viability Head Generalization:**
 - Evaluation of $p_v(s)$ (viability probability) on initial states of Pencil Pouch episodes showed that the region where $p_v(s) > 0.6$ expanded progressively as online fine-tuning continued (Figure 4, left). This suggests that the viability head generalizes, assigning high viability to an increasing range of observed initial conditions.
 - Trajectory-level traces demonstrated that $p_v(s)$ drops sharply when the policy enters an out-of-distribution state (e.g., a failed grasp) and recovers steadily during a human intervention that re-establishes a viable state (Figure 4, right). This indicates that the viability advantage ($A_v$) provides a useful real-time signal for local changes in viability.
- **Per-Transition Weight Analysis:**
 - Analysis of a Pencil Pouch rollout (Figure 5) illustrated the complementary roles of the two critic heads and the state-adaptive gate:
 - At a successful grasp, $p_v(s)$ rises sharply, leading to a large $A_v$ and upweighting via the viability term.
 - During an inefficient "confused regrasp" segment, $p_v(s)$ remained high but $\hat{V}_e(s)$ signaled stalled progress. This caused $A_e$ to become negative, leading to downweighting of the inefficient segment via the efficiency term.
 - During recovery actions, both signals improved, leading to upweighting through both terms.
 - This dynamic weighting, where $V_v$ reacts to discrete viability changes and $V_e$ resolves gradations within high-viability regions, provides a mechanistic explanation for HABC's superior empirical performance.
- **Weight Statistics (Appendix G):**
 - The mean pre-normalization weight $g_t$ on successful autonomous rollout transitions varied across tasks (0.76 for Pencil Pouch, 0.68 for Paper Bag, 0.86 for Snack Bag), confirming that the weighting rule is non-trivial.
 - Imit-Recap's hard threshold filtered out a significant portion of transitions (e.g., 78% for Pencil Pouch), indicating a more aggressive approach compared to HABC's soft weighting.
 - When intervention reweighting was enabled, the mean $g_t$ on intervention windows was approximately uniform (1.0, 1.1, 0.95), suggesting interventions are generally productive, though individual transitions still received non-uniform weights.

#### 4.4. Recovery Behavior Comparison (Qualitative)

Qualitative comparisons (Figure 6) between SFT and HABC-trained policies revealed that HABC enables autonomous error correction and recovery. When encountering manipulation failures (e.g., failed grasp, misalignment), the SFT policy either repeats the error or enters an unrecoverable state. In contrast, the HABC policy, leveraging viability-weighted transitions that upweight recovery-oriented actions, detected failure states and executed corrective motions to re-establish task progress and complete the task.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

**Significance:**
This research introduces HABC, a method for online RL fine-tuning of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models that addresses critical challenges in learning from sparse episode outcomes. The core contributions lie in its hierarchical signal decomposition and intervention-aware credit assignment. By training separate critic heads for viability and efficiency and adaptively combining their advantages, HABC provides a more granular and informative per-transition supervision signal than previous methods that conflate these objectives into a single scalar. The intervention-aware credit assignment prevents incorrect supervision leakage across autonomous and human-controlled segments, leading to cleaner learning signals. The empirical results on complex, real-robot bimanual tasks involving deformable objects demonstrate substantial improvements in success rates and trajectory efficiency, reaching up to 92% success from SFT baselines as low as 12-44%. The qualitative evidence of learned recovery behavior further highlights the policy's improved robustness and error detection capabilities.

**Potential Impact:**
The HABC framework has several potential impacts on the field of robot learning and deployment:
- **Enhanced VLA Fine-Tuning:** It offers a more robust and efficient approach to fine-tuning pretrained VLA policies in real-world settings, where dense rewards are often unavailable. This can accelerate the deployment of generalist robotic policies by allowing them to adapt to specific task nuances and environmental variations through minimal online interaction.
- **Reduced Reliance on Reward Engineering:** By effectively extracting distinct learning signals (viability and efficiency) from sparse, binary outcomes, HABC reduces the need for complex, hand-engineered reward functions or dense intermediate rewards, simplifying the design and implementation of online RL for robotics.
- **Improved Robustness and Generalization:** The ability to learn from both successes and failures through viability estimation, coupled with efficiency optimization, can lead to more robust policies that can recover from errors and achieve tasks more consistently and efficiently. This is particularly relevant for contact-rich and bimanual manipulation, which are inherently challenging.
- **Better Utilization of Human Data:** The intervention-aware credit assignment mechanism provides a principled way to leverage human interventions not just as imitation data, but also to refine the policy's understanding of task viability and efficient progression without corrupting the learning signal from autonomous execution. This can make human-in-the-loop learning more effective.
- **Framework for Sparse Reward Problems:** The hierarchical decomposition of sparse rewards into distinct components (viability, efficiency) could serve as a foundational framework for tackling other sparse reward problems in RL beyond VLA fine-tuning, potentially inspiring new approaches to credit assignment in various domains.
- **Facilitation of Research:** The planned open-source release of code and data will facilitate reproducibility, allowing other researchers to build upon HABC and further explore adaptive gating, multi-step advantage estimation, and the integration of denser outcome signals for complex recovery scenarios.

**Limitations (as identified by the authors):**
- **Intervention Detection Reliability:** The intervention-aware credit assignment relies on accurately detected intervention boundaries. Noisy or unreliable intervention labels could corrupt the $V_v$ supervision.
- **Efficiency Head Signal Weakness:** The efficiency head ($V_e$) is trained exclusively on successful trajectories. Consequently, its signal is weakest precisely when success is rare early in training, which is when the viability head is most critical.
- **Single-Task Evaluation:** The current evaluation focuses on single-task fine-tuning. Extending the dual-head design and intervention-aware credit assignment to multi-task or cross-embodiment settings remains an open area for future research.
- **Future Directions:** Natural next steps include exploring more sophisticated adaptive gating mechanisms, incorporating multi-step advantage estimation, and developing methods for denser outcome signals, particularly for contact-rich recovery scenarios.
