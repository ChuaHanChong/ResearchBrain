# Slicing and Dicing: Configuring Optimal Mixtures of Experts

> [!summary] Summary
> A systematic study from the University of Washington and New York University exhaustively investigated design choices for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures in large language models. The research found that maximizing total MoE parameters and using a coarse expert granularity are key to performance gains, while complex features like heterogeneous experts or generalists offer no substantial benefit.

## Problem

- A comprehensive understanding of how various Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) design choices interact and contribute to model performance was lacking.
- Existing literature examined individual or limited combinations of MoE parameters, leading to an incomplete picture of optimal configurations.
- Practitioners often rely on limited prior work or heuristic tuning for MoE LLMs, potentially missing optimal designs or introducing unnecessary complexity.

## Method

- Conducted an exhaustive experimental study involving over 2,000 pretraining runs of Transformer-based language models with ==MoE layers==.
- Systematically varied key MoE design choices, including ==expert granularity==, ==total expert count==, ==heterogeneous expert pools==, always-active ==generalist experts==, and different ==load balancing== mechanisms.
- Ensured fair comparisons by ==FLOP-matching== all configurations on a per-timestep basis, aligning active FFN parameters with dense baselines across models ranging from 10M to 300M active parameters.

## Results

- Performance consistently improved with an increase in total MoE parameters across models **50M and larger**, up to extreme active expert parameter ratios (**1:128**).
- MoE models with **10M and 20M** active parameters initially underperformed dense baselines but surpassed them when trained with significantly larger data budgets (**20x and 5x**, respectively).
- Dropless routing consistently outperformed or performed comparably to default routing, especially at lower total expert counts, while load balancing hyperparameters had minimal impact within reasonable ranges.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - MoE performance gains are primarily driven by an increase in total inactive model parameters (sparsity) and sufficient data/compute, rather than just splitting FFNs into smaller components.
> - The optimal MoE configuration involves maximizing the total expert count to leverage sparsity, while maintaining a relatively coarse expert granularity (e.g., 1/4 or 1/8 of dense FFN dimension).
> - Introducing architectural complexities such as heterogeneous expert granularities or dedicated always-active 'generalist' experts does not improve performance and can sometimes degrade it.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have been increasingly adopted in large language models (LLMs) due to their ability to decouple computational cost from model capacity, facilitating training at massive scales. This is achieved by conditionally activating a subset of "expert" submodules via a learned router. Previous research has explored various individual design choices within MoE models, such as total expert count, expert granularity, the inclusion of shared FFN components (generalists), and different routing algorithms. However, these studies have largely investigated these factors in isolation or within limited configurations.

The current research identifies a gap in the understanding of how these core MoE design choices interact with one another and whether they can be independently optimized. The broader landscape thus features a growing adoption of MoE models but lacks a comprehensive, systematic understanding of their complex design space. This paper aims to provide such a systematic analysis, moving beyond isolated studies to explore the joint impact of multiple configuration parameters, thereby addressing an open question regarding optimal MoE design recipes. It directly builds upon and contrasts with prior works that offered specific recommendations on expert granularity, shared experts, or active expert counts by conducting a more extensive and disentangled experimental investigation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to conduct a systematic and exhaustive study of key Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture design choices in large language models (LLMs). The authors identified that despite the widespread adoption of MoE architectures, a comprehensive understanding of how various configuration parameters interact and contribute to overall model performance was lacking. Existing literature tended to examine individual or limited combinations of design choices, leaving an open question about the feasibility of independent optimization.

Specifically, the detailed objectives included:
- **Disentangling Expert Count from Granularity:** To precisely measure the independent impact of the size (granularity) of individual experts and the total number of experts, while controlling for active compute budget. This aimed to understand how to optimally distribute total MoE parameters between a greater number of smaller experts versus fewer, larger experts.
- **Evaluating Expert Flexibility:** To assess whether more flexible configurations, such as heterogeneous expert pools (mixed granularities within a single layer) and always-active "generalist" experts, offer performance advantages over simpler, homogeneously sized MoE layers. This was motivated by the intuition that diverse functions might require different expert dimensions.
- **Simplifying Routing Design Space:** To analyze the impact of different routing algorithm configurations, specifically load-balancing hyperparameters (auxiliary loss weight and loss-free bias) and dropless routing mechanisms, across various expert setups and model scales. This aimed to determine the robustness of MoE quality to these choices and identify simpler, effective routing strategies.

The motivation for these objectives stemmed from the observed complexity in designing MoE LLMs. Without a systematic study, practitioners often rely on limited prior work or heuristic tuning, which may not yield optimal configurations or could introduce unnecessary complexity. A detailed understanding of these interactions would enable the development of simpler, more robust, and higher-performing MoE models, ultimately streamlining their adoption and optimization in large-scale machine learning. The authors highlight the practical need for clear, data-driven recommendations for MoE architecture design.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed an exhaustive experimental methodology involving over 2,000 pretraining runs of Transformer-based language models with Mixture-of-Experts layers. Models ranged in size from approximately 10 million to 300 million active parameters, and up to 6.6 billion total parameters.

**Model Architecture:**
- MoE Transformer LMs were based on architectures loosely defined by Muennighoff et al. (2025).
- Models were categorized by their active parameter count: 10M, 20M, 50M, 80M, 110M, 200M, and 300M.
- To ensure fair comparisons, all configurations were FLOP-matched on a per-timestep basis by ensuring the number of active FFN parameters equaled that of a dense baseline. This was achieved by setting the number of active experts (k) such that k * expert granularity (g) = 1.
- Activation sparsity (s) was defined as the factor by which total FFN parameters exceed active FFN parameters, calculated as n * g (where n is total expert count).

**Varying Design Choices (Systematic Exploration):**
- **Expert Granularity (g):** Explored values from {1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64}. A granularity of 1 signifies experts of the same dimension as a dense FFN.
- **Total Expert Count (n):** Varied from {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024}.
- **Heterogeneous Experts:** Investigated configurations with two pools of experts per layer, each with different granularities (e.g., (1/2, 1/4), (1/4, 1/8), etc.) and corresponding total expert counts, designed to equally divide active and total parameters between pools.
- **Generalists (Shared Experts):** Tested the inclusion of an always-active generalist expert with varying granularities ({1/2, 1/4, 1/8}), in combination with routed experts.
- **Load Balancing Mechanisms:**
 - **Auxiliary Loss (L_LB):** Varied the weighting hyperparameter (α) at {1e-2, 1e-4}.
 - **Loss-Free Load Balancing:** Explored the use of a per-expert bias (γ) adjusted at each training step, with values {0 (no bias), 1e-3}.
- **Dropless Routing:** Compared performance between a "dropless" routing mechanism (Gale et al., 2022) which ensures no tokens are dropped, and a "default" routing which allows token dropping.

**Training Details:**
- **Training Data:** A mixture of web text, code, math, and encyclopedic text, as introduced by Muennighoff et al. (2025).
- **Data Budget:** Models were trained with a token-to-active-parameter ratio of approximately 20, following Hoffmann et al. (2022) recommendations. Additional experiments at 10M and 20M scales were conducted with larger data budgets (20x and 5x, respectively) to investigate performance under increased compute.
- **Hyperparameters:** A consistent set of hyperparameters was used (e.g., Adam optimizer, SwiGLU nonlinearity, Cosine Decay LR schedule), with specific load balancing and routing parameters varied as described above. Preliminary sweeps for learning rate and alternative schedulers were conducted but a fixed setting was chosen for consistency. MoE Z-loss was retained.
- **Hardware:** Training was performed on a mix of A40, L40, L40S, and H200 GPUs, accumulating approximately 32,000 GPU-hours for main experiments and 9,500 for preliminary work.

**Evaluation:**
- **Primary Metric:** Macro-average cross-entropy loss on a diverse set of held-out validation data, encompassing various language modeling tasks (e.g., C4, The Pile, WikiText-103, DOLMA sub-domains).
- **Secondary Metrics:** Performance on downstream tasks (BoolQ, HellaSwag, MMLU) was also reported.
- **Robustness:** Sensitivity to random seeds was evaluated for a subset of configurations, showing low standard deviation for language modeling loss.

This comprehensive approach allowed for the disentanglement and analysis of the individual and joint effects of numerous MoE design choices across different model scales.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The systematic study yielded several key findings regarding the optimal configuration of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures:

- **Impact of Total MoE Parameters:**
 - Performance consistently improved with an increase in total MoE parameters across all active-parameter scales investigated (50M and larger). This improvement was observed even at extreme active expert parameter ratios, up to 1:128.
 - Below a certain "compute minimum" (specifically, at 10M and 20M active parameter scales with a standard data budget), MoE models consistently underperformed dense baselines. However, when these smaller models were trained with significantly larger data budgets (e.g., 20x for 10M, 5x for 20M), their performance improved to surpass dense baselines, exhibiting trends similar to larger models. This suggests that MoE benefits are conditional on sufficient data and compute, not just model size.
 - The improvement in performance from MoEs is primarily attributed to the increased total model parameters and sparsity, rather than just splitting the Feed-Forward Network (FFN) mechanism into smaller, always-active components. Densely activated models with granular FFNs (without sparsity) consistently degraded performance compared to dense baselines.

- **Optimal Expert Count and Granularity:**
 - Both increasing expert size (granularity) at a fixed total number of experts and increasing the total number of inactive experts at a fixed granularity improved performance.
 - When constrained by a fixed total MoE parameter count (memory budget), the optimal combination of total expert count and expert granularity varied. As activation sparsity (`s`) increased, the optimal expert granularity tended to remain relatively stable or increase slowly, suggesting that scaling up sparsity primarily benefits from increasing the *total expert count* (`n`) while maintaining a *coarser expert granularity* (`g`) around 1/4 or 1/8 in the studied settings.
 - Top-1 routing (i.e., activating only one expert, `g=1`) generally underperformed configurations with `k > 1` activated experts, aligning with previous findings that multiple activated experts can be beneficial for router learning.

- **Effect of Heterogeneous Experts and Generalists:**
 - **Heterogeneous Experts:** The inclusion of expert layers with varying granularities within the same layer (heterogeneous experts) did not result in performance gains compared to homogeneously sized MoE models that were matched for active and total parameters. The results for heterogeneous setups generally fell between or near the performance of their closest homogeneous counterparts.
 - **Generalists:** The inclusion of always-active "generalist" experts consistently resulted in comparable or slightly degraded performance across all tested configurations, including both homogeneous and heterogeneous expert setups. This suggests that explicitly separating "generalist" computation does not offer an advantage.

- **Routing Mechanism Impact:**
 - **Dropless Routing:** Dropless routing consistently outperformed or performed comparably to default routing (which allows tokens to be dropped). This benefit was more pronounced at lower total expert counts; at very high expert counts (n=1024), where the average expert load is low and token dropping is less frequent, the performance difference became marginal.
 - **Load Balancing Settings:** Load balancing hyperparameters had a minimal impact on MoE quality within a reasonable range of settings. However, poorly chosen hyperparameters, particularly a high bias (`γ=1e-3`) with a large total number of experts (`n >= 512`), could significantly impair performance by leading to high load imbalance. The setting with the lowest load balancing loss weight (`α_LB=1e-4`) and no loss-free bias (`γ=0`) generally resulted in higher load imbalance and performed worse. This indicates that some form of load balancing is necessary, but its specific tuning can be flexible within appropriate bounds.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this systematic study carry several significant implications for the design and optimization of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, potentially impacting future research and practical implementations of large language models.

**Simplification of MoE Design:** The most prominent impact is the simplification of practical recommendations for configuring MoE models. The research suggests that the complex design space, often perceived as requiring extensive hyperparameter sweeps for multiple interdependent factors, can be reduced. Developers can primarily focus on two key architectural parameters:
 - **Total Expert Count (n):** This should be maximized to leverage the benefits of increased inactive parameters, often dictated by memory allowances.
 - **Expert Granularity (g):** A relatively coarse expert granularity (around 1/4 or 1/8 in the studied settings) appears optimal and is largely invariant to the total parameter count, primarily depending on the active parameter count.
 This simplified recipe allows for more efficient model development and reduces the computational overhead associated with extensive hyperparameter tuning.

**Clarification of Design Trade-offs:** The study clarifies the value of certain MoE design elements that have been topics of ongoing discussion:
 - **Limited Benefit of Heterogeneity and Generalists:** The finding that heterogeneous experts and always-active "generalists" do not offer substantial performance gains, and in the case of generalists can even degrade performance, challenges some intuitions and prior recommendations. This suggests that the complexity introduced by these features might not be justified by performance improvements.
 - **Robustness of Load Balancing:** The observation that MoE quality is robust to load-balancing hyperparameters within reasonable ranges provides reassurance to practitioners. While some load balancing is necessary to prevent severe performance degradation, overly fine-grained or mis-tuned mechanisms (like aggressive loss-free biasing at high expert counts) can be detrimental. This emphasizes the importance of selecting a well-behaved load balancing strategy rather than endlessly optimizing its hyperparameters.
 - **Value of Dropless Routing:** The consistent performance gain from dropless routing, especially at lower sparsity factors, offers a clear recommendation for its adoption where possible to improve model quality by preventing token information loss.

**Reframing Scaling Laws:** The work directly challenges or refines conclusions from some prior scaling law studies, particularly concerning the necessity of shared experts and fixed optimal active expert counts. By demonstrating that MoE benefits are deeply tied to total parameter count and effective compute (data budget), and that optimal granularities can be scale-dependent, it contributes to a more nuanced understanding of MoE scaling. This helps avoid potential "artifacts of overfitting to a specific regime" in scaling law derivations, as noted by the authors.

**Practical Tool for Researchers:** The release of code and data supports reproducibility and provides a valuable resource for other researchers to build upon these findings, further exploring MoE configurations or validating new hypotheses.

In essence, this research provides empirically grounded guidance for configuring MoE systems, suggesting a streamlined approach that prioritizes total expert parameters and appropriate granularity over more intricate or specialized expert configurations, thereby fostering more efficient and effective development of future large-scale sparse models.
