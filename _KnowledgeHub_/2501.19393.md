# s1: Simple test-time scaling

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford, UW, and AI2 developed `s1-32B`, an open-source model that achieves state-of-the-art reasoning performance and clear test-time scaling on challenging benchmarks by fine-tuning on only 1,000 high-quality reasoning samples and employing a simple 'budget forcing' inference technique.

## Problem

- Despite the emergence of "test-time scaling" in closed-source models like OpenAI's o1, there was a lack of open-source models demonstrating clear and accessible test-time scaling behavior.
- Traditional large language model (LLM) performance improvements primarily relied on computationally intensive "train-time scaling," making cutting-edge research inaccessible to many.
- Existing open-source replication attempts for test-time scaling were often complex (e.g., Monte Carlo Tree Search, multi-agent systems) or still required substantial resources (e.g., millions of RL samples).

## Method

- Curated ==`s1K`==, a 1,000-sample, high-quality reasoning dataset, meticulously selected from over 59,000 examples based on quality, difficulty, and diversity.
- Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of the Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct base model on the `s1K` dataset, focusing loss computation only on reasoning traces and solutions.
- Developed =="budget forcing"==, a novel and simple decoding-time intervention that directly controls thinking tokens by forcefully terminating generation or suppressing the end-of-thinking token to append 'Wait' and encourage further reasoning.

## Results

- The `s1-32B` model demonstrated clear test-time scaling, consistently showing performance gains with increased thinking tokens across challenging tasks (e.g., from **50%** to **57%** accuracy on AIME24 with increased budget forcing).
- Achieved competitive reasoning performance, surpassing OpenAI's `o1-preview` by up to **27%** on competition math benchmarks (MATH, AIME24) and nearly matching Google Gemini 2.0 Thinking on AIME24.
- Established a new sample efficiency frontier for open-data reasoning models, achieving state-of-the-art performance with only **1,000** fine-tuning samples and minimal training compute (**26 minutes** on 16 H100 GPUs).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-quality reasoning capabilities can be achieved with remarkably few, carefully curated samples, emphasizing quality over quantity (a "less is more" approach to data).
> - Simple inference-time interventions, like "budget forcing," are effective for controlling and scaling a model's 'thinking' process at test time, providing precise control over compute.
> - Uncontrolled rejection sampling for longer reasoning paths can surprisingly lead to inverse scaling, where longer generations are less accurate, suggesting models might struggle or backtrack on unguided, extended attempts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a burgeoning paradigm shift in large language model (LLM) performance improvement: **test-time scaling**. Traditionally, advancements in LLMs have predominantly relied on "train-time scaling," involving the use of progressively larger models and massive pre-training datasets (e.g., Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). While this approach has yielded remarkably powerful models, it demands immense computational resources, making cutting-edge research inaccessible to many.

The concept of "test-time scaling" proposes an alternative: enhancing model performance by increasing compute *during inference* (test time), rather than solely during training. This paradigm gained significant attention with OpenAI's "o1" model (OpenAI, 2024), which demonstrated impressive reasoning capabilities and consistent performance gains from scaling test-time compute. However, OpenAI's o1 model was a closed-source effort, withholding its precise methodology. This lack of transparency spurred numerous replication attempts within the research community, utilizing diverse techniques such as Monte Carlo Tree Search (Gao et al., 2024b), multi-agent systems (Qin et al., 2024), and large-scale reinforcement learning (RL) (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). While some efforts, like DeepSeek R1, successfully matched o1's performance, they often relied on extensive RL training with millions of samples, still representing a substantial resource commitment.

The critical gap identified by the authors is that despite these replication attempts, *none had openly replicated a clear test-time scaling behavior* in an accessible manner. This paper directly steps into this void. By seeking the "simplest approach," the authors aim to democratize test-time scaling capabilities, providing a fully open-source model, data, and code. This work contributes significantly to the open science movement in AI, countering the trend of closed-source, proprietary advancements by demonstrating that state-of-the-art reasoning and scaling can be achieved with surprisingly minimal resources when focusing on smart data curation and simple test-time interventions. It also builds upon prior work like LIMA (Zhou et al., 2023), which suggested that even small, high-quality datasets can suffice for aligning models to user preferences, extending this "less is more" philosophy to complex reasoning tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to discover the **simplest approach to achieve both test-time scaling and strong reasoning performance** in large language models. This primary goal is driven by the desire to demystify and openly reproduce the capabilities observed in closed-source models like OpenAI's o1, thereby fostering broader research and innovation in AI.

To achieve this, the authors pursue several specific objectives:

- **Curate a Sample-Efficient Reasoning Dataset (s1K)**: A key motivation was to demonstrate that high-quality reasoning capabilities do not necessarily require massive, synthetically generated datasets. They aimed to curate a small, yet highly effective, dataset of 1,000 questions (s1K) paired with reasoning traces, distilled from a powerful proprietary model (Google Gemini Thinking Experimental). The emphasis was on curating samples based on difficulty, diversity, and quality criteria to maximize impact despite the small size.
- **Develop a Simple Test-Time Intervention (Budget Forcing)**: The authors sought an uncomplicated mechanism to control the amount of compute an LM dedicates to a problem during inference. This was motivated by the observation that complex techniques like Monte Carlo Tree Search or multi-agent approaches, while effective, might not be the simplest path to test-time scaling. The goal was a method that directly manipulates the model's generation process to extend or constrain its "thinking" time.
- **Achieve Competitive Reasoning Performance with Minimal Resources**: A core motivation was to prove that a model fine-tuned on a mere 1,000 samples, coupled with a simple test-time technique, could compete with or even surpass closed-source, larger-scale reasoning models (specifically o1-preview) on challenging benchmarks, while consuming significantly less training compute (26 minutes on 16 H100 GPUs).
- **Demonstrate Clear Test-Time Scaling Behavior**: Beyond just achieving high performance, a crucial objective was to unequivocally show that the model's performance monotonically increases with the allocated test-time compute, a characteristic previously highlighted by OpenAI's o1 but not openly replicated.
- **Promote Open Science**: Underlying all these objectives is a strong commitment to open-sourcing their model, data, and code. This transparency is motivated by the belief that open replication and shared methodologies are essential for accelerating scientific progress in AI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a straightforward yet effective methodology combining supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on a meticulously curated small dataset with a novel, simple test-time inference technique.

#### 3.1. Reasoning Data Curation (s1K)

The process for creating `s1K` involves two stages:
- **Initial Collection (59K Samples)**: Over 59,000 questions were gathered from 16 diverse, high-quality, and challenging sources. These sources include large mathematical problem datasets (NuminaMATH, MATH, OmniMath, AIME), science Olympiad questions (OlympicArena), standardized tests (AGIEval), and newly created quantitative reasoning datasets (`s1-prob` from Stanford PhD exams, `s1-teasers` from quant trading interviews). The guiding principles for this collection were **Quality**, **Difficulty**, and **Diversity**. For each question, a reasoning trace and solution were generated using the Google Gemini Flash Thinking API, creating 59K question-trace-solution triplets. Data was then decontaminated against evaluation benchmarks and deduplicated.
- **Final Selection (1K Samples)**: From the 59K pool, a minimal set of 1,000 samples (`s1K`) was carefully chosen through a three-stage filtering process:
 - **Quality**: API errors and obvious formatting issues (e.g., ASCII art, broken image references) were filtered out. A subset of 384 samples from inherently high-quality datasets was directly included.
 - **Difficulty**: Questions that could be correctly solved by either Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct were removed, ensuring the selected problems were genuinely challenging for the target model. Reasoning trace length (assuming longer traces indicate higher difficulty) was also used as an indicator. Grading was performed by Claude 3.5 Sonnet.
 - **Diversity**: Questions were classified into 50 domains using the Mathematics Subject Classification (MSC) system. The final 1,000 samples were selected by uniformly choosing a domain and then sampling a problem from that domain, favoring those with longer reasoning traces. This ensured broad coverage across various reasoning tasks.

#### 3.2. Model Training

- **Base Model**: Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, an off-the-shelf, pre-trained, and instruction-tuned language model, was chosen for its strong baseline performance.
- **Fine-tuning**: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) was performed on the `s1K` dataset. Training hyperparameters were basic: 5 epochs, batch size 16, bfloat16 precision, AdamW optimizer with a cosine learning rate schedule (1e-5 max, 5% warmup).
- **Efficiency**: The entire fine-tuning process was remarkably efficient, taking only 26 minutes on 16 NVIDIA H100 GPUs.
- **Loss Calculation**: Loss was only computed on the reasoning traces and solutions, not the input questions, encouraging the model to generate detailed thought processes.
- **Sequence Length Ablation**: Through ablations, the authors found that using a longer training sequence length (32768 tokens) was crucial. It minimized sample cutoffs, led to better overall performance, and counter-intuitively resulted in *shorter* reasoning traces at inference time (as the model learned to complete its chain more efficiently).

#### 3.3. Test-Time Scaling (Budget Forcing)

The authors focused on **sequential scaling**, where later computations build on earlier ones, believing it to be more effective than parallel scaling.
- **Budget Forcing Mechanism**: This novel decoding-time intervention directly controls thinking tokens:
 - **Maximum Tokens**: If the model generates more thinking tokens than a desired limit, the process is forcefully terminated by appending an end-of-thinking token, prompting the model to output its answer.
 - **Minimum Tokens/Lengthening**: To encourage more thinking, the generation of the end-of-thinking token is suppressed, and the string "Wait" is appended to the current reasoning trace. This nudges the model to self-correct or explore further reasoning steps.
- **Baselines for Comparison**:
 - **Conditional Length-Control Methods**:
 - *Token-conditional control*: Specifying an upper bound of thinking tokens in the prompt.
 - *Step-conditional control*: Specifying an upper bound of thinking "steps" (around 100 tokens each).
 - *Class-conditional control*: Generic prompts telling the model to think for "short" or "long" periods.
 - **Rejection Sampling**: Generating multiple responses and selecting one that fits a predetermined compute budget.
- **Evaluation Metrics for Test-Time Scaling**:
 - **Control**: Measures the extent to which a method allows precise control over test-time compute (percentage of runs meeting specified limits).
 - **Scaling**: The average slope of the performance-vs-compute function (should be positive for useful methods).
 - **Performance**: The maximum accuracy achieved by the method on a benchmark.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings, demonstrating the efficacy and efficiency of the proposed `s1` approach.

#### 4.1. Performance and Test-Time Scaling

- **Clear Test-Time Scaling**: The `s1-32B` model, equipped with budget forcing, consistently exhibited performance gains as test-time compute (measured in thinking tokens) increased across all evaluated reasoning-intensive tasks (MATH500, AIME24, GPQA Diamond). This successfully replicates the test-time scaling behavior previously observed in OpenAI's o1. For instance, on AIME24, budget forcing allowed `s1-32B` to extrapolate from 50% to 57% accuracy.
- **Competitive Performance**: `s1-32B` demonstrated strong reasoning capabilities, surpassing OpenAI's `o1-preview` on competition math questions by up to 27% (MATH and AIME24). It also nearly matched the performance of Google Gemini 2.0 Thinking on AIME24, suggesting the effectiveness of their distillation process.
- **Sample Efficiency Frontier**: A key finding is the remarkable sample efficiency of `s1-32B`. It achieves state-of-the-art performance for an open-data reasoning model while being fine-tuned on only 1,000 samples. This contrasts sharply with other competitive open-weight models like DeepSeek r1, which uses over 800,000 reasoning samples. This positions `s1-32B` squarely on the sample-efficiency frontier (Figure 2, right).

#### 4.2. Ablations on Data Curation

The ablations confirmed the crucial importance of the three guiding principles (Quality, Difficulty, Diversity) for `s1K`'s effectiveness:
- **Suboptimal Alternatives**: Training on `1K-random`, `1K-diverse` (diversity only), or `1K-longest` (difficulty by trace length only) datasets resulted in significantly worse performance (e.g., -26.7% to -40.0% on AIME24 relative to `s1K`), highlighting that combined criteria are essential.
- **Quantity vs. Quality**: Training on the full 59K sample pool (`59K-full`) did not offer substantial gains over `s1K`, despite consuming vastly more resources (394 H100 GPU hours vs. 7 H100 GPU hours for `s1K`). This strongly supports the "less is more" hypothesis for data curation in reasoning fine-tuning.

#### 4.3. Ablations on Test-Time Scaling Methods

- **Budget Forcing Superiority**: Budget forcing emerged as the most effective method, providing "perfect control" over thinking tokens and demonstrating a clear positive scaling slope. The simple act of appending "Wait" was found to be the most effective string for encouraging continued reasoning.
- **Limitations of Conditional Length-Control**:
 - *Token-conditional control* (telling the model to generate a specific number of tokens) largely failed without budget forcing, as the model could not reliably count tokens.
 - *Step-conditional control* was also unreliable; models tended to compensate for fewer steps by generating more tokens per step, hindering precise control and often proving more computationally costly due to verbose delimiters.
 - *Class-conditional control* (generic prompts like "think for a long time") could influence thinking duration but lacked precise control and didn't consistently improve performance.
- **Inverse Scaling with Rejection Sampling**: Surprisingly, simple rejection sampling (generating multiple responses and picking one that fits a budget) led to an *inverse scaling trend*. Longer, more resource-intensive generations were often *less* accurate. The authors hypothesize this is because shorter generations represent instances where the model was on the right track from the start, while longer ones often involve backtracking or self-questioning on incorrect paths.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Simple test-time scaling" paper makes several significant contributions to the field of AI, particularly concerning the development and accessibility of advanced language model capabilities.

#### 5.1. Advancing Open Science and Transparency

The most immediate and profound impact of this work is its commitment to **open science**. By openly replicating OpenAI's previously opaque "o1" test-time scaling behavior with a "simple" and entirely open-source approach (model weights, data, and code), the authors directly address the growing concern about the closed nature of cutting-edge AI research. This transparency fosters innovation, allows for independent verification and builds a foundation for collaborative progress, making advanced reasoning capabilities more accessible to the broader research community and mitigating the risks associated with proprietary control over powerful AI.

#### 5.2. Demonstrating Resource Efficiency and Accessibility

The paper challenges the conventional wisdom that achieving state-of-the-art reasoning requires immense computational resources and massive datasets. By demonstrating competitive performance and clear test-time scaling with a model fine-tuned on just **1,000 carefully curated samples** (s1K) and minimal training compute (26 minutes on 16 H100 GPUs), the research significantly lowers the barrier to entry for developing powerful reasoning LLMs. This efficiency has profound implications for researchers and organizations with limited budgets, democratizing access to capabilities previously thought to be exclusive to large corporations. It underscores that "less is more" when it comes to data, emphasizing quality and strategic curation over sheer quantity, aligning with the "Superficial Alignment Hypothesis" from LIMA (Zhou et al., 2023).

#### 5.3. Validating and Extending the Test-Time Scaling Paradigm

This work provides strong empirical validation for **test-time scaling** as a viable and effective strategy for enhancing LLM performance. It shifts focus from solely pre-training larger models to optimizing inference-time computation. The introduction of "budget forcing" offers a simple yet robust mechanism for controlling and extending a model's "thinking process," effectively translating increased compute into improved reasoning accuracy. This opens up new avenues for research into inference-time algorithms, complementing traditional training-time optimizations.

#### 5.4. Providing Insights into Model Reasoning and Future Directions

The counter-intuitive finding regarding **rejection sampling's inverse scaling** provides valuable insights into how models might "think" or self-correct during generation. It suggests that longer, unguided reasoning traces might sometimes indicate struggling or backtracking rather than deeper, more successful thought. This observation could guide future research in designing more effective inference strategies. Furthermore, the paper explicitly defines quantifiable metrics (Control, Scaling, Performance) for evaluating test-time scaling methods, providing a standardized framework for future research in this domain. While acknowledging the current limitations of sequential scaling (e.g., flattening, context window constraints), the authors suggest that parallel scaling methods like REBASE could complement sequential approaches to push test-time compute extrapolation even further.

In sum, this research not only offers a practical, open-source solution for test-time scaling but also contributes significantly to the theoretical understanding of LLM reasoning and provides a roadmap for more accessible and transparent AI development.
