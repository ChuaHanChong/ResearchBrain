# Representation Fréchet Loss for Visual Generation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces FD-loss, a method that directly optimizes Fréchet Distance as a training objective for generative models by decoupling population statistics from batch-level gradient computation. This approach enhances the visual quality of one-step generators and repurposes multi-step models for efficient single-step generation, while also proposing FDr_k, a new multi-representation metric for comprehensive evaluation.

## Problem

- Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) primarily served as an evaluation metric and was deemed impractical for direct optimization as a training objective due to the large sample populations required for stable estimation.
- High-quality image generation often relies on computationally intensive multi-step denoising processes, lacking efficient one-step alternatives that maintain quality without complex distillation or adversarial training.
- FID, relying on a single feature space like Inception-v3, has shown saturation and can sometimes misrepresent true perceptual quality, leading to a 'metric paradox' where low FID doesn't always correlate with superior human perception.

## Method

- Proposes **FD-loss**, decoupling the large sample population needed for stable ==Fréchet Distance== estimation from the small batch size used for gradient computation.
- Implements an ==EMA-based estimator== maintaining running statistics of generated features, with gradients flowing only through the current batch.
- Applies FD-loss as a ==post-training fine-tuning== method to enhance one-step generators and repurpose multi-step generators for one-step image generation.

## Results

- **FD-loss** improved FID: pMF-B/16 from **3.31 → 0.81** (Inception); pMF-H with FD-SIM achieved **1.89 FDr_6**.
- Repurposed **JiT-L/16** to a one-step generator with **FID 0.85** and **FDr_6 3.29** at 1 NFE.
- Human preference studies consistently preferred FD-loss post-trained models over their base versions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly optimizing Fréchet Distance at scale is practical and effective, provided the estimation of population statistics is decoupled from batch gradient computation.
> - The choice of representation model for FD optimization significantly influences the resulting visual quality, with modern ViT-based representations often yielding perceptually superior results despite sometimes higher FID scores.
> - FID alone is an insufficient and sometimes misleading metric for evaluating generative models, necessitating multi-representation metrics like FDr_k to provide a more comprehensive and perceptually aligned assessment.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) has been a primary metric for evaluating the quality of generated images for approximately a decade. Research in image generation has often aimed to improve FID scores, with advances typically being assessed retrospectively through this metric. However, FID has primarily functioned as an evaluator rather than a direct training objective due to the perceived impracticality of its direct optimization. Computing FID reliably requires a large population of samples (e.g., 50,000) to estimate feature distribution statistics (means and covariances). Directly incorporating such a computation into a training loop, which would necessitate back-propagating gradients through tens of thousands of samples at each step, has been considered computationally prohibitive. Prior attempts to use Fréchet Distance (FD) as a training loss have been limited to restricted settings, such as estimating FD from small batches, which have been shown to degrade generator performance.

This work addresses this long-standing challenge by proposing a method to directly optimize FD at scale. It positions FD not just as an evaluation tool, but also as a practical training objective, particularly for post-training existing generative models. This approach connects to broader themes in deep learning optimization where objectives require larger effective sample sets than a single batch provides, such as memory banks and feature queues in contrastive learning, or Exponential Moving Averages (EMA) for population statistics in Batch Normalization.

Furthermore, the paper engages with the limitations of FID as a sole evaluation metric. Despite state-of-the-art generators achieving FID scores that sometimes surpass real validation images, a perceptible quality gap often remains between generated and real images. This discrepancy has motivated the development of alternative metrics and a recognition that a single feature space (like Inception-v3) may be insufficient to capture the multifaceted aspects of visual quality. This work directly probes this paradox by using FD as a training loss across various representation spaces and by proposing a multi-representation metric, FDr_k, to provide a more comprehensive assessment. The research also contributes to the field of efficient one-step or few-step image generation, offering an alternative to teacher distillation, adversarial training, or per-sample target methods for improving existing one-step generators or repurposing multi-step ones.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate whether Fréchet Distance (FD), conventionally used solely as an evaluation metric, can be effectively optimized directly as a training loss for visual generative models, and to explore the implications of such direct optimization.

The key motivations driving this work are:

1. **Overcoming the Practicality Barrier of Direct FD Optimization:** Historically, the direct optimization of FD has been deemed impractical because its reliable estimation requires a large population of samples (e.g., 50,000), far exceeding typical training batch sizes. Simultaneously, computing gradients through such a large population at every training step is computationally infeasible. The central motivation is to develop a mechanism to decouple the population size required for robust FD estimation from the batch size used for gradient computation, thereby enabling FD to function as a scalable training objective.
2. **Improving Visual Quality via Distribution-Level Optimization:** The hypothesis is that directly optimizing a distributional distance like FD, which measures the distance between feature distributions of real and generated data, could lead to more aligned and higher-quality generated samples. This contrasts with common generative model training paradigms that often rely on sample-level losses (e.g., pixel-wise, latent-space regression, or adversarial objectives) which may not directly optimize for overall distributional similarity as effectively. The objective is to demonstrate that FD, when properly optimized, can consistently enhance the visual quality of various generative models as a post-training objective.
3. **Repurposing Multi-step Generators for One-step Generation:** Modern high-quality image generators often employ multi-step denoising processes, which are computationally intensive. There is a strong motivation to develop efficient one-step or few-step generators. This work aims to determine if FD-loss, as a direct distribution-matching objective, can repurpose pre-trained multi-step generators into effective one-step generators without relying on complex techniques like teacher distillation, adversarial training, or per-sample targets. This would simplify the development of efficient generation models.
4. **Diagnosing and Addressing Limitations of Single-Metric Evaluation (FID):** A significant motivation stems from the observed "metric paradox" where state-of-the-art generators achieve FID scores that nominally surpass real validation images, yet their outputs remain perceptibly distinguishable from real images. This suggests that FID, which relies on a single, often dated, feature space (Inception-v3), may have saturated as a reliable indicator of perceptual quality and can sometimes misrank visual quality. The objective is to use FD-loss as a diagnostic tool to expose these limitations and to motivate and introduce a more robust, multi-representation evaluation metric, FDr_k, that provides a broader assessment of generative quality.
5. **Encouraging Broader Exploration of Distributional Distances:** By demonstrating the practical applicability of FD as a training objective, the research aims to encourage the broader community to reconsider the utility of distributional distances not only for evaluation but also as core training mechanisms across various generative modeling tasks and modalities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology presented in this paper, termed FD-loss, centers on decoupling the population size required for reliable Fréchet Distance (FD) estimation from the batch size used for gradient computation during training. This approach enables the direct optimization of FD at scale, overcoming a long-standing practical challenge.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: Fréchet Distance

The paper defines FD between two multivariate Gaussian distributions, representing the features of real and generated images (ϕ(x) and ϕ(ˆx) respectively), using their means (μ_r, μ_g) and covariances (Σ_r, Σ_g). The formula for FD is given by:
FD_ϕ(R, G) = ∥μ_r − μ_g∥₂² + Tr(Σ_r + Σ_g − 2(Σ_r Σ_g)¹ᐟ²)
The challenge for direct optimization lies in the need for a large population (e.g., 50k samples) to stably estimate μ_g and Σ_g, which is prohibitive for typical training batch sizes (e.g., 64-1024) if gradients are to be computed through all samples.

#### 3.2. FD-loss: Decoupling Population Scale from Optimization Scale

The central innovation is to maintain a large effective population for statistics estimation while restricting gradient computation to the current training batch. Two implementations are explored:

- **Queue-based estimator:** This method maintains an online queue of features from recently generated samples. At each iteration, a batch of `B` new images is generated, their features are extracted using a frozen representation model ϕ, and these features are enqueued while the oldest `B` features are dequeued. FD is computed using the empirical mean and covariance of the full queue (e.g., `N = 100,000` features). Crucially, during back-propagation, gradients are only computed for the features of the current batch; features from previous iterations residing in the queue are treated as constants (detached from the computation graph). This approach provides an "on-policy" estimate of statistics.
- **EMA-based estimator:** This alternative avoids storing a large feature queue. Instead, it maintains Exponential Moving Averages (EMA) of the first and second feature moments (μ_g and M_g). For a given batch of generated features, batch moments (μ_batch, M_batch) are calculated. These batch moments are then used to update the running EMA estimates of μ_g and M_g using a decay rate β. The covariance Σ_g is recovered from M_g and μ_g. Similar to the queue-based method, FD is computed using these EMA statistics, but gradients are only back-propagated through the current batch's contribution to μ_batch and M_batch. This method is noted for being more scalable and providing a more on-policy estimate due to EMA’s natural weighting of recent samples. This variant is adopted as the default for subsequent experiments due to its simplicity and stronger results.

#### 3.3. Multi-representation FD-loss

To leverage information from diverse feature spaces, FD-loss supports minimizing FD across multiple representation models {ϕ_i}. To prevent terms from representations with vastly different scales from dominating the loss, each FD term is normalized by its current value (using a stop-gradient operator and a small constant for stability). This ensures each term contributes approximately unit scale to the combined loss, where each term can also be weighted. For simplicity, equal weights are used in this work.

#### 3.4. Training Setup

FD-loss is applied as a post-training objective, fine-tuning pre-trained base generators.

- **Post-training one-step generators:** The method is applied to existing one-step generators, including pixel-space (pMF) and latent-space (iMF) models, to enhance their visual quality while maintaining fast generation.
- **Repurposing multi-step generators:** Multi-step denoising models (e.g., JiT, SD3.5 Medium) are adapted to perform one-step generation. The model is run only once, treating its output as a single-step prediction. FD-loss then optimizes this modified one-step generator. This process is notable for not requiring teacher distillation, adversarial training, or per-sample targets.

#### 3.5. FDr_k: Normalized Fréchet Distance Ratios under K Models

To address the limitations of FID (which often misrepresents visual quality and can be saturated), the paper introduces FDr_k, a multi-representation evaluation metric:

- **FD ratio (FDr):** For each representation model ϕ_i, a normalized FD ratio is calculated as the FD between generated images (G) and the training set (T), divided by the FD between the validation set (V) and the training set (T) in the same feature space. This normalization makes the metric unitless and interpretable (validation images score 1.0).
- **FDr_k:** This metric is the arithmetic mean of the normalized FD ratios across K diverse representation models. The paper uses K=6 models, encompassing supervised (Inception-v3), self-supervised (ConvNeXt-v2, DINOv2, MAE), and vision-language (SigLIP2, CLIP) objectives, across both CNN and ViT architectures. FDr_k is presented as a more robust automatic metric than FID alone, aiming to reduce single-model bias while acknowledging its own limitations (dependence on chosen representations, Gaussian moment-matching assumption).

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

Experiments focus on class-conditional image generation on ImageNet-1k (256x256 and 512x512 resolutions) and text-conditioned generation. All models are initialized from official pre-trained weights. Evaluation uses FID, IS, and the proposed FDr_k, computed from 50,000 generated images against training set statistics. Post-training typically involves 50 or 100 epochs with AdamW, a cosine learning rate schedule, and a global batch size of 1024.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the effectiveness and properties of FD-loss and its impact on generative model evaluation.

#### 4.1. Properties of Population Size in FD-loss

Experiments with pMF-B/16 demonstrate that a sufficiently large population size is crucial for stable FD optimization.
- Optimizing FD from the current batch only (equivalent to a queue size of 0k or EMA beta of 0.0) led to performance degradation across all metrics (FID: 3.31 -> 3.84; FDr_6: 13.70 -> 17.06).
- For the queue-based estimator, performance improved significantly with queue sizes from 5k to 50k (e.g., FID of 0.89, FDr_6 of 10.91 at 50k). However, performance degraded beyond 100k, indicating that overly stale features can outweigh the benefit of a larger population.
- For the EMA-based estimator, optimal performance was observed with a decay rate (β) of 0.999, achieving 0.81 FID and 10.81 FDr_6. This result surpassed the best queue-based result and required less memory, leading to its adoption as the default.

#### 4.2. Properties of Representation Models in FD-loss

The choice of representation model significantly impacts optimization outcomes and perceived quality.
- **Single Representation:** Optimizing with Inception-v3 consistently yielded the lowest FID scores (e.g., pMF-B/16 from 3.31 to 0.81 FID) and a modest improvement in FDr_6. However, optimizing with modern ViT-based representations (DINOv2, MAE, SigLIP2) often led to higher FID scores but more substantial improvements in FDr_6. This indicated that lower FID does not always correlate with perceptually superior quality, especially when evaluated across diverse representations. Some representations (Inception, ConvNeXt) allowed generated images to become statistically closer to the training set than real validation images (FDr < 1.0), suggesting saturation in these specific feature spaces.
- **Visual Quality and Metric Discrepancy:** Qualitative results (Figure 4) illustrated that the model achieving the lowest Inception FID (0.81) did not necessarily produce the most visually appealing samples. Models optimized with modern representations, despite exhibiting higher FID, often displayed improved object structure and overall perceptual quality, reinforcing the FDr_k metric's utility.
- **Multiple Representations:** Combining multiple representations for FD-loss (e.g., SigLIP2 + Inception-v3 + MAE, termed FD-SIM) generally proved more effective than using a single one. FD-SIM achieved a strong FDr_6 with a negligible trade-off in Inception FID, demonstrating a more balanced improvement across perceptual dimensions.

#### 4.3. Repurposing Multi-Step Generators into One-Step Generators

FD-loss demonstrated the ability to repurpose multi-step models for one-step generation without distillation or adversarial training.
- A pre-trained JiT-L/16 model, which performed poorly in a naive one-step setting (291.59 FID), was post-trained with FD-loss.
- After post-training, all FD-loss variants produced sensible images in one step. FD-SIM achieved 0.85 FID and 3.29 FDr_6, outperforming FD-Inception (0.77 FID, 12.86 FDr_6) in terms of FDr_6, indicating a broader improvement in visual fidelity. Qualitative samples (Figure 5) showed that the repurposed 1-NFE models could generate images visually comparable or superior to the original 50-step base model.

#### 4.4. Scalability and System-Level Comparison

FD-loss showed broad generalizability across various generator families (pMF, iMF, JiT), model sizes, and image resolutions (256x256, 512x512).
- Across configurations, FD-SIM consistently drove FDr_6 to values between 1.81 and 5.56, while FD-Inception achieved FID scores between 0.72 and 0.79. These improvements were obtained using the same hyperparameters.
- In system-level comparisons on ImageNet 256x256 (Table 4), FD-loss post-trained models demonstrated competitive or superior performance to existing state-of-the-art systems. Notably, they achieved very low FID scores (e.g., 0.72 for pMF-H and iMF-XL with Inception optimization) and substantially lower FDr_6 scores (e.g., 1.89 for pMF-H with FD-SIM) with only a single network function evaluation (1 NFE), a significant efficiency gain over multi-step counterparts.

#### 4.5. Human Preference

A pairwise human preference study (Figure 6) corroborated the automatic metric improvements.
- FD-loss post-trained models were consistently preferred over their base models across all tested generator families.
- Despite significant improvements, the strongest FD-loss post-trained generator (pMF-H*) was still preferred less often than real ImageNet validation images (30.1% vs 69.9%). This reinforces the FDr_k metric's indication that the ImageNet generation problem, in terms of human perception, is not yet "solved," contrasting with the potentially misleading signal from FID alone.

#### 4.6. Text-Conditioned Generation

The applicability of FD-loss extends beyond class-conditional generation.
- SD3.5 Medium, a 2.5B-parameter MMDiT model, was successfully repurposed into a 1-NFE text-to-image generator using FD-loss. This demonstrated that FD-loss can scale to large, text-conditioned models and inherit aesthetic characteristics from different reference distributions (e.g., photorealistic vs. stylized) with a significant reduction in network function evaluations (56x NFE reduction).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work carries several significant implications and potential impacts on the field of generative modeling:

1. **Repositioning Fréchet Distance (FD):** The most direct impact is the demonstration that FD, long confined to an evaluative role, can function effectively as a direct training objective. By decoupling the population size needed for reliable estimation from the batch size for gradient computation, the research provides a practical method (FD-loss) to optimize distributional distances at scale. This could lead to a paradigm shift in how generative models are trained, encouraging more direct optimization of distribution-level properties.

2. **Improved Generative Model Quality and Efficiency:** FD-loss consistently improves the visual quality of existing one-step generators and can repurpose multi-step generators into efficient one-step models. This is achieved without relying on complex and resource-intensive techniques like teacher distillation, adversarial training, or per-sample targets. This simplification could accelerate the development of high-quality, efficient generative models across various domains.

3. **Enhanced Evaluation of Generative Models:** The paper highlights and addresses the limitations of sole reliance on metrics like FID, which can become saturated and misrepresent perceptual quality. The introduction of FDr_k, a multi-representation metric, offers a more robust and comprehensive way to assess generative quality. This shift in evaluation methodology could encourage the development of models that perform well across a broader spectrum of perceptual attributes, rather than "hacking" a single, narrow metric.

4. **Diagnostic Tool for Representation Spaces:** By optimizing FD in different representation spaces, the work provides a diagnostic lens into what aspects of visual quality each representation captures. This insight can inform future research into designing or selecting more appropriate feature spaces for both training objectives and evaluation metrics. The observation that FID can misrank visual quality suggests a need for continued exploration of what constitutes a "good" representation for generative tasks.

5. **Broader Applicability and Future Research Avenues:** The simplicity and effectiveness of FD-loss suggest its potential applicability beyond image generation on ImageNet. It could be extended to other modalities (e.g., video, audio), scenarios with scarce or restricted access to real data during post-training, and higher-resolution generation settings. The findings also open up new research questions, such as identifying optimal sets of representation spaces for diverse tasks, developing adaptive weighting schemes for multi-representation losses, and exploring other distribution-level objectives that can be optimized at scale.

In summary, this research offers a practical and effective method for direct distributional distance optimization, significantly enhancing the capabilities and efficiency of visual generators. Concurrently, it provides critical insights into the limitations of current evaluation practices, advocating for more robust, representation-diverse metrics, and thus contributes to a more holistic understanding and development of generative AI.
