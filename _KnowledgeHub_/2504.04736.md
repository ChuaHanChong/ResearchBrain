# Synthetic Data Generation & Multi-Step RL for Reasoning & Tool Use

> [!summary] Summary
> SWiRL is an offline multi-step reinforcement learning methodology designed to enhance Large Language Models' reasoning and tool-use capabilities. It generates and utilizes synthetic data, applying process-based filtering for step-wise optimization, which leads to substantial performance gains and strong cross-domain generalization in complex tasks.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) often struggle with complex queries requiring multi-step reasoning and strategic interaction with external tools.
- Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) methods for LLMs provide sparse, single-step reward signals, which are insufficient for training models on long, sequential reasoning tasks.
- Existing multi-step optimization approaches for LLMs frequently lack integrated tool use or rely on outcome-based filtering of data, limiting their ability to learn robust intermediate processes.

## Method

- ==SWiRL (Step-Wise Reinforcement Learning)== introduces an offline multi-step optimization technique that leverages synthetic data generation with iterative tool execution.
- The methodology employs a ==generative reward model== to provide a granular, ==step-wise reward signal==, evaluating the quality and reasonableness of each individual action within its specific sub-trajectory context.
- It explores and prioritizes ==process-based filtering== of synthetic trajectories, retaining data based on the soundness of intermediate steps rather than solely on the correctness of the final outcome.

## Results

- SWiRL achieved significant relative accuracy improvements of **21.5%** on GSM8K, **12.3%** on HotPotQA, **14.8%** on CofCA, **11.1%** on MuSiQue, and **15.3%** on BeerQA.
- Training SWiRL on HotPotQA improved zero-shot GSM8K performance by a relative **16.9%**, while GSM8K training improved HotPotQA by **9.2%**, demonstrating strong cross-domain and cross-tool transferability.
- SWiRL-finetuned Gemma-2-27b models outperformed their base counterparts and, in several cases, even the stronger Gemini 1.5 Pro reward model on out-of-distribution benchmarks, indicating active learning beyond distillation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Process-based filtering of synthetic data, which evaluates the reasonableness of each intermediate step, is more effective for multi-step reinforcement learning than filtering based on the final outcome.
> - SWiRL demonstrates robust generalization capabilities, improving performance across different multi-step tasks (e.g., question answering to mathematical reasoning) and tool types (e.g., search to calculator) even when trained on a single domain.
> - The method can effectively learn from trajectories with logically sound intermediate steps, even if the final answer is incorrect, showcasing a deeper learning of reasoning processes compared to Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs), such as those highlighted in the introduction (Gemini, Claude, OpenAI models), have showcased remarkable capabilities across various Natural Language Processing tasks. However, the paper identifies a critical frontier: LLMs often struggle with complex queries that necessitate multi-step reasoning and interaction with external tools, a requirement increasingly common in advanced "agentic tasks." This limitation is observed across diverse domains including multi-hop question answering, mathematical problem-solving, and coding.

Traditional reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms, specifically Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), RL from AI Feedback (RLAIF), and RL from Execution Feedback (RLEF), have been instrumental in aligning LLMs with human preferences and improving their performance. However, a significant limitation of these methods, as pointed out by the authors, is their focus on **single-step optimization**. This means the reward signal is typically calculated only at the end of a generated response or trajectory, failing to provide granular feedback on intermediate steps in a multi-step reasoning process. This "sparse reward" problem makes it challenging for models to learn effective strategies for tasks requiring a sequence of interrelated actions, where an incorrect intermediate step can easily derail the final outcome.

Within the nascent landscape of multi-step optimization for LLMs, the paper positions its work in contrast to existing approaches. While methods like DQO (Direct Q-function Optimization) and OREO (Offline Reinforcement Learning for LLM Multi-Step Reasoning) have explored offline RL for multi-step reasoning, they often lack integration with **tool use** or operate at a finer (token) level, which has been shown to be less effective than step-level actions. Other approaches like PRIME are online, which can be computationally expensive, and Tulu-3 relies on golden labels for verification.

Furthermore, the paper situates itself within the evolving domain of **synthetic data generation** for reasoning improvement. Previous works like STaR (Self-Taught Reasoner) and Rejection Fine-Tuning (RFT) leverage synthetic reasoning traces but often rely on filtering data based on correct final outcomes, thus implicitly requiring golden labels. ReST (Reinforced Self-Training) also uses iterative data generation and fine-tuning but does not specifically focus on tool use or the nuanced distinction between process and outcome feedback for RL.

This paper addresses these gaps by proposing a novel **offline multi-step RL methodology** that explicitly tackles tool use and provides **step-wise feedback**, significantly departing from the traditional single-step reward model. Crucially, it explores the impact of *process-based filtering* of synthetic data, challenging the common reliance on outcome-based filtering and golden labels for effective RL training. By enabling LLMs to learn from the "reasonableness" of each intermediate step rather than just the final answer, this work pushes the boundaries of how LLMs can acquire and refine complex reasoning and agentic capabilities, particularly in environments requiring interaction with external tools.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind this research stems from the acknowledged limitation of current LLMs: despite their impressive generative abilities, they frequently falter when faced with complex queries demanding **multi-step reasoning and strategic tool use**. Tasks like multi-hop question answering, advanced mathematical problem-solving, and sophisticated coding require not just generating text, but also decomposing problems, interacting with external environments (tools), and synthesizing information over a sequence of steps. Traditional RL methods, optimized for single-step outputs (e.g., a single response to a prompt), are ill-equipped to handle the compounding challenge where errors in intermediate steps lead to incorrect final results.

The key objectives of the proposed Step-Wise Reinforcement Learning (SWiRL) methodology are:

1. **To develop an effective offline multi-step optimization technique for LLMs.** This aims to overcome the "sparse reward" problem inherent in single-step RL approaches by providing more granular feedback throughout a complex reasoning trajectory.
2. **To enable LLMs to learn strategic tool use.** This involves teaching the model when and how to invoke external tools (like search engines or calculators), how to formulate queries for these tools, how to effectively integrate the tool's output, and ultimately, how to synthesize findings to arrive at a final answer.
3. **To facilitate problem decomposition.** The goal is for the LLM to learn to break down complex problems into a sequence of more manageable subtasks, each addressed by an intermediate action or tool call.
4. **To leverage synthetic data efficiently.** The research aims to explore a methodology for generating large volumes of high-quality, multi-step synthetic training data quickly and in parallel, thereby avoiding slow, online tool execution and ensuring greater reproducibility with fixed datasets.
5. **To investigate the impact of different synthetic data filtering strategies.** Specifically, the authors sought to understand whether filtering by the "reasonableness" of intermediate steps (process-based) is more beneficial than filtering by the correctness of the final answer (outcome-based) for multi-step RL.
6. **To evaluate the generalization capabilities of the learned policies.** A significant objective is to determine if training on one type of multi-step task and tool (e.g., question-answering with a search engine) can transfer and improve performance on disparate tasks and tools (e.g., mathematical reasoning with a calculator), thereby demonstrating a more general improvement in multi-step reasoning.
7. **To demonstrate that the approach does not solely rely on golden labels.** A practical motivation is to show that effective multi-step RL can be achieved using model-based judgments for data generation, filtering, and reward, reducing the dependency on costly human annotations or perfectly correct final answers.

In essence, the motivation is to advance LLM agentic capabilities by providing them with a robust, scalable, and generalizable mechanism for learning complex, sequential reasoning and effective tool interaction.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, **Step-Wise Reinforcement Learning (SWiRL)**, is an offline multi-step optimization technique structured into two main stages: synthetic data generation and filtering, followed by step-wise reinforcement learning optimization.

#### 3.1. Multi-Step Data Collection (Stage 1)

This stage focuses on creating a large dataset of synthetic multi-step trajectories.
- **Base Model and Tool Integration:** An open-source LLM (Gemma 2) is augmented with access to a relevant external tool, such as a search engine (for question answering) or a calculator (for mathematical reasoning).
- **Iterative Trajectory Generation:** The model is iteratively prompted to generate multi-step trajectories. At each step, the LLM is free to generate a "chain of thought" (internal reasoning), decide to make a tool call, or produce a final answer.
- **Tool Execution and Contextualization:** If a tool call is generated (e.g., `<search_query>QUERY</search_query>` or `<math_exp>EXPRESSION</math_exp>`), the query is automatically extracted and executed in its respective environment (e.g., vector database search, SymPy interpreter). The result of this execution is then provided back to the model as part of the context for the *next* step. The trajectory concludes when the model generates a final answer, indicated by specific markers (`<answer>ANSWER</answer>`).
- **Sub-trajectory Decomposition:** A crucial aspect is the decomposition of each complete trajectory, $\tau = (s_1, a_1, \dots, s_K, a_K)$, into *k* sub-trajectories. Each sub-trajectory consists of the context from the beginning up to a specific action $a_i$, allowing for granular evaluation of each step. $s_i$ contains the full context of previous states, actions, and tool responses.
- **Dataset Compilation:** The authors compiled two primary datasets: 50,000 synthetic trajectories from 10,000 HotPotQA training questions (multi-hop QA) and 37,500 trajectories from 7,500 GSM8K questions (mathematical reasoning). To manage length, maximum step counts were set (5 for HotPotQA, 10 for GSM8K).
- **Synthetic Data Filtering Strategies:** Four distinct filtering strategies are explored for the generated data:
 1. **No filtering:** All generated trajectories are used.
 2. **Process filtering:** Trajectories are retained only if *every step* (action $a_i$ given context $s_i$) is judged to be "reasonable" by a separate LLM judge (Gemini 1.5 Pro Thinking). This evaluates the quality of the reasoning process itself. No golden labels are used for this judgment.
 3. **Outcome filtering:** Trajectories are kept only if the final answer ($a_K$) matches the golden label. This focuses solely on the correctness of the final output.
 4. **Process and outcome filtering:** Only trajectories that satisfy both process and outcome criteria are retained.
 - **Insight on Filtering:** The paper foreshadows a key finding here: unlike Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), SWiRL performs best with process-filtered data, *even if the final answer is incorrect*, demonstrating a unique learning capability from process-level feedback.

#### 3.2. Step-Wise Reinforcement Learning Optimization (Stage 2)

This stage fine-tunes the base LLM using the synthetic trajectories generated in Stage 1.
- **Objective Function:** The optimization aims to maximize the expected sum of step-wise rewards, formalized as $J(\theta) = E_{s \sim T, a \sim \pi_\theta(s)} [R(a|s)]$. Here, $\pi_\theta$ is the base model being fine-tuned, $T$ is the set of all states (contexts) from the synthetic trajectories, and $R(a|s)$ is the reward for taking action $a$ in state $s$.
- **Generative Reward Model:** The reward signal $R(a|s)$ is provided by a powerful generative reward model (Gemini 1.5 Pro). This model evaluates the quality of *each individual action* ($a_i$) within its specific sub-trajectory context ($s_i$). Crucially, this reward model operates without access to golden labels, assessing the "reasonableness" or "soundness" of the step.
- **Optimization Algorithm:** The policy gradient algorithm, similar to that used for optimizing human feedback in Gemma 2, is employed to update the base model's parameters ($\theta$).
- **Granular Feedback:** By optimizing per-step, SWiRL enables the model to learn both local decision-making (generating the correct next step or tool call) and global trajectory planning (leading to a correct final response), guided by immediate, context-aware feedback on the soundness of each prediction.

#### 3.3. Step-Wise Inference-time Evaluation

During evaluation, the fine-tuned SWiRL model operates iteratively.
- The model is repeatedly prompted to either make a tool call or produce a final answer.
- Tool calls are parsed and executed in real-time (e.g., search query sent to a vector database, math expression to SymPy). The results are injected back into the model's context for subsequent steps.
- This iterative process continues until the model explicitly generates a final answer or reaches a predefined maximum number of queries (5 for QA, 10 for math). This mimics the multi-step reasoning process the model was trained on.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by the authors demonstrate the substantial effectiveness of SWiRL in improving LLMs' multi-step reasoning and tool-use capabilities.

- **Significant Performance Gains:** SWiRL consistently and significantly outperforms baseline approaches across various multi-step question-answering and mathematical reasoning tasks. It achieved relative accuracy improvements of 21.5% on GSM8K, 12.3% on HotPotQA, 14.8% on CofCA, 11.1% on MuSiQue, and 15.3% on BeerQA. The statistical analysis in Appendix A confirms these improvements are meaningful and outside the margin of error.

- **Robust Generalization Across Tasks and Tools:** One of the most exciting findings is SWiRL's strong generalization. Training the model exclusively on HotPotQA (multi-hop question-answering using a search tool) not only improved its performance on other similar QA datasets (BeerQA, MuSiQue, CofCA) but also remarkably boosted its zero-shot performance on GSM8K (a mathematical reasoning dataset using a calculator) by a relative 16.9%. Conversely, training on GSM8K improved HotPotQA performance by 9.2%. This cross-domain, cross-tool transferability suggests that SWiRL enhances a more fundamental multi-step reasoning and planning capability within the LLM.

- **Impact of Data Filtering:** The study critically analyzed the influence of different synthetic data filtering strategies on SWiRL's performance:
 - **Process-only filtering** consistently yielded the highest accuracy for SWiRL. This indicates that focusing on the *reasonableness and soundness of each intermediate step*, as judged by an AI model, is more crucial for SWiRL's effectiveness than merely ensuring a correct final outcome.
 - Counter-intuitively, for SWiRL, **outcome-based filtering (or combined process and outcome filtering) generally harmed performance** compared to using unfiltered or process-only filtered data. This suggests that SWiRL can learn effectively even from trajectories that ultimately lead to incorrect final answers, as long as the intermediate steps are logically sound. The authors hypothesize that this might be because SWiRL benefits from exposure to both positive and negative examples of outcomes, while learning from correct *processes*.

- **Comparison with Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** SWiRL significantly outperforms SFT for multi-step reasoning. SFT, in fact, often *degraded* performance compared to the base model (as detailed in Appendix B), a finding consistent with other recent works suggesting SFT can hinder reasoning capabilities. While SFT benefited from outcome-filtered data (suggesting memorization), SWiRL's superior performance with process-only filtered data highlights its ability to learn a deeper, more generalizable understanding of problem-solving and planning. Even SFT on trajectories generated by a stronger model (Gemini 1.5 Pro) did not improve performance, further emphasizing SWiRL's unique learning mechanism.

- **Effect of Tool Use:** SWiRL's multi-step inference, which allows iterative tool calls, demonstrably boosts performance for both base and SWiRL-finetuned models. However, the SWiRL-finetuned model benefits substantially more. Interestingly, the SWiRL-trained model shows significant improvements even *without* access to a tool at inference time, suggesting that the training process itself enhances the model's intrinsic ability to decompose complex problems.

- **Scaling Effects (Dataset and Model Size):**
 - **Dataset Size:** SWiRL scales effectively with larger synthetic datasets. Even with just 1,000 process-filtered trajectories, substantial gains were observed, with further improvements up to 10,000 data points. This indicates that the method efficiently leverages data for improved reasoning.
 - **Model Size:** While smaller models (Gemma-2-2b and 9b) benefited from in-domain SWiRL training, the largest model (Gemma-2-27b) exhibited superior generalization across tasks and domains, suggesting that the benefits of SWiRL are more pronounced and transferable with larger foundation models.

- **Performance Relative to Reward Model:** The SWiRL-finetuned Gemma-2-27b model not only surpassed its base counterpart but also outperformed Gemini 1.5 Pro (the generative reward model) on several out-of-distribution benchmarks (CofCA, BeerQA). This suggests that SWiRL is not merely distilling the capabilities of a larger reward model but is actively learning and improving upon them.

- **Improvement in Mean Process Label Accuracy:** An analysis of average process label accuracy showed that SWiRL-finetuned models generated trajectories with higher correctness at each step compared to the baseline, both in- and out-of-distribution. This quantitative evidence supports the conclusion that the improved final accuracies are indeed driven by better underlying multi-step reasoning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SWiRL methodology marks a significant advancement in enhancing the capabilities of Large Language Models, particularly in their ability to tackle complex, real-world problems. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enabling Complex Agentic Behaviors:** By providing an effective framework for multi-step optimization and tool use, SWiRL moves LLMs beyond single-turn responses towards more sophisticated "agentic" behaviors. This is crucial for applications requiring sequential decision-making, iterative problem-solving, and interaction with dynamic environments, such as autonomous agents, advanced conversational AI, and scientific research assistants.

- **Overcoming the Sparse Reward Problem in RL for LLMs:** Traditional RL's limitation of sparse, end-of-episode rewards has been a major bottleneck for complex tasks. SWiRL's step-wise reward mechanism provides dense, context-aware feedback, making the learning process more efficient and effective for long reasoning chains. This paradigm shift could inspire new multi-step RL algorithms tailored for LLM capabilities.

- **Reduced Reliance on Costly Human Annotation and Golden Labels:** The finding that process-filtered synthetic data, judged by another AI model, is highly effective for SWiRL, even without correct final outcomes, is transformative. This significantly reduces the reliance on expensive and labor-intensive human annotation and the need for perfectly correct "golden answers" during training. This makes the development of sophisticated LLM agents more scalable and cost-efficient.

- **Unprecedented Generalization Capabilities:** The demonstrated ability of SWiRL to transfer learning across disparate tasks (e.g., QA to math) and different tools (search to calculator) is highly impactful. This indicates that SWiRL fosters a more fundamental understanding of multi-step reasoning, rather than just task-specific memorization. This strong generalization means that LLMs could be more easily and cheaply adapted to new, unseen domains and tools, accelerating their deployment in diverse applications.

- **Deeper Understanding of Reasoning Mechanisms:** The observation that SWiRL improves mean process label accuracy suggests that the method instills a more robust and sound internal reasoning process within the LLM, not just superficial performance gains. This contributes to a deeper understanding of how LLMs can acquire and exhibit more human-like problem-solving strategies.

- **Practical Implications for LLM Development:** SWiRL offers a practical and reproducible offline training methodology that avoids the computational overhead of online tool interaction during the bulk of training. This efficiency, combined with reduced data annotation costs, could become a standard approach for building highly capable and adaptable LLM agents across various industries and research areas.

In conclusion, SWiRL represents a significant leap forward in empowering LLMs to handle complexity. By refining their ability to reason step-by-step and effectively utilize tools, this research paves the way for more intelligent, autonomous, and versatile AI systems that can tackle a wider array of challenging real-world problems.
