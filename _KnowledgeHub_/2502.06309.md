# Analog In-memory Training on General Non-ideal Resistive Elements: The Impact of Response Functions

> [!summary] Summary
> This research provides a theoretical framework for gradient-based training on analog in-memory computing (AIMC) hardware, accounting for non-ideal resistive elements with asymmetric and non-linear response functions. It introduces Residual Learning, a bilevel optimization algorithm proven to achieve exact convergence, significantly outperforming traditional Analog SGD on various neural network models and datasets, while also addressing practical issues like I/O noise and limited response granularity.

## Problem

- The Von Neumann architecture creates a data movement bottleneck, making training large machine learning models computationally and energy-intensive.
- Analog In-Memory Computing (AIMC) offers energy efficiency but faces challenges in training due to non-ideal resistive elements, particularly their asymmetric and non-linear response functions.
- Existing theoretical analyses of AIMC training were often limited to specific cases or empirical observations, lacking a general framework for generic non-ideal hardware.

## Method

- Developed a discrete-time mathematical model for analog updates, decomposing ==non-ideal response== functions into symmetric and asymmetric components.
- Introduced "Residual Learning," a bilevel optimization framework using a main weight array and an auxiliary residual array to correct for asymmetric updates.
- Extended the framework (Residual Learning v2) with a digital buffer for I/O noise filtering and a threshold-based transfer mechanism for limited response granularity.

## Results

- Analog SGD was theoretically shown to exhibit inexact convergence, with an asymptotic error proportional to noise variance and an amplification factor dependent on the asymmetry.
- Numerical simulations on MNIST and CIFAR10/100 datasets demonstrated that Residual Learning achieved test accuracy comparable to Digital SGD, consistently outperforming Analog SGD, which degraded significantly (e.g., below **15%** accuracy) under pronounced asymmetry.
- Residual Learning v2, incorporating noise filtering and granularity handling, further improved robustness, showing enhanced performance on CIFAR100 over the basic Residual Learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Asymmetric and non-linear response functions in resistive elements introduce an "implicit penalty" in Analog SGD, causing it to optimize a modified objective instead of the true one.
> - This implicit penalty leads to "inexact convergence" for Analog SGD, resulting in a persistent, non-diminishing asymptotic error relative to the true optimal point.
> - The proposed Residual Learning framework achieves "exact convergence" by using a bilevel optimization strategy and a "zero-shifted symmetric point" for the auxiliary residual array.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The escalating computational and energy demands of training large machine learning models, such as large vision and language models, have highlighted limitations of the conventional Von Neumann computing architecture. This architecture's physical separation of memory and processing units creates a data movement bottleneck that incurs significant economic and environmental costs. For instance, the paper cites training costs for models like LLaMA and GPT-3 in the millions of dollars.

Analog In-Memory Computing (AIMC) has emerged as a promising alternative, particularly for accelerating computationally intensive matrix-vector multiplications (MVMs), which are fundamental to neural network operations. AIMC hardware, utilizing resistive crossbar arrays, leverages physical laws (Kirchhoff's and Ohm's laws) to perform MVMs with significantly higher energy efficiency (10x-10,000x compared to GPUs for inference).

While AIMC has shown substantial benefits for model inference, its application to model *training* is considerably more challenging. Training involves frequent weight updates, and in AIMC, these weights are represented by the conductance states of resistive elements, updated via electrical pulses. The primary challenge stems from the non-ideal characteristics of these resistive elements, which introduce complexities not present in digital hardware. Existing research has identified several hardware imperfections, including:
1. **Asymmetric and non-linear response functions:** The change in conductance per pulse is often non-uniform and depends on the current conductance state and pulse polarity.
2. **Limited response granularity ($\Delta w_{min}$):** Updates become imprecise when gradients are small, falling below the minimum measurable conductance change.
3. **Noisy input/output (I/O) operations and analog-digital conversion errors.**

Previous work has explored empirical strategies and heuristic algorithms, such as the Tiki-Taka algorithm family, to mitigate these imperfections. Some theoretical analyses have also been presented, but these have largely been limited to specific cases, such as linear response functions. This paper aims to address a gap in the theoretical understanding by investigating the impact of *generic* non-ideal (asymmetric and non-linear) response functions on gradient-based training in AIMC hardware. It seeks to provide a rigorous theoretical foundation that is not restricted to specific resistive element candidates or simplified linear models. By doing so, it contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of analog training dynamics and offers principled algorithmic solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems directly from the challenges and opportunities presented by analog in-memory computing for AI training:

1. **Mitigating the Von Neumann Bottleneck:** The primary motivation is to address the severe computational and energy costs associated with training increasingly large AI models on traditional Von Neumann architectures. AIMC offers a path to significant energy efficiency by performing computations directly within memory, reducing costly data movement.
2. **Addressing Analog Training Challenges:** While AIMC is efficient for inference, training on analog hardware is more complex due to the inherent non-idealities of resistive elements. The paper explicitly identifies several key challenges:
 - **Asymmetric and Non-linear Response Functions:** The core challenge investigated. Conductance changes, driven by electrical pulses, are not constant. Instead, they are scaled by non-linear and asymmetric response functions, $q_+(w)$ and $q_-(w)$, which depend on the current weight ($w$) and pulse polarity. This leads to biased gradient updates, where gradients are scaled by different magnitudes across different coordinates.
 - **Limited Response Granularity ($\Delta w_{min}$):** This constant minimum change means that when target gradient updates are smaller than $\Delta w_{min}$, accurate weight adjustment is difficult, limiting training precision and potentially hindering convergence.
 - **Noisy I/O:** Imperfections such as noise in MVM operations and analog-digital conversion errors further complicate stable and accurate training.
3. **Filling a Theoretical Gap:** Much of the prior work in AIMC training has been empirical or limited to specific, idealized hardware models (e.g., linear response functions). The authors observe a "gap between theory for special linear and generic response functions," demonstrating that algorithms like Tiki-Taka, which work well under ideal linear conditions, can degrade significantly with more general non-ideal responses. This highlights the need for a more general theoretical framework.

Based on these motivations, the paper defines two key objectives:

- **Q1) What is the impact of non-ideal response functions and how to alleviate it?** This objective focuses on developing a theoretical understanding of how asymmetric and non-linear response functions affect gradient-based training (specifically Analog SGD) and proposing a method to counteract these negative effects.
- **Q2) How to extend the framework to address the limited response granularity and noisy IO issues?** This objective aims to build upon the proposed solution for response function non-idealities and adapt it to incorporate other practical hardware imperfections, making the approach more robust for real-world AIMC implementations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research methodology progresses from a theoretical modeling of analog updates to the development and extension of an algorithmic solution, supported by experimental validation.

1. **Modeling Analog Update Dynamics:**
 - The authors first establish a discrete-time mathematical model for the "Analog Update" at the core of AIMC training. This model approximates the effect of multiple electrical pulses on a resistive element's conductance (weight $w$).
 - For a desired weight change $\Delta w$, the actual update $w'$ is modeled as $U_q(w, \Delta w)$, where $U_q(w, \Delta w) = w + \Delta w \cdot q_+(w)$ for $\Delta w \geq 0$ and $w + \Delta w \cdot q_-(w)$ for $\Delta w < 0$. This abstraction allows analysis to proceed without constant reference to the microscopic pulse-by-pulse updates, which are shown to be negligible in error for small $\Delta w$.
 - The element-wise response functions, $q_+(w)$ and $q_-(w)$, are decomposed into a symmetric component $F(W) = (Q_-(W) + Q_+(W))/2$ and an asymmetric component $G(W) = (Q_-(W) - Q_+(W))/2$. This decomposition is crucial for isolating the effects of asymmetry.
 - The resulting Analog SGD dynamics are formulated as $W_{k+1} = W_k - \alpha\nabla f(W_k; \xi_k) \odot F(W_k) - \alpha|\nabla f(W_k; \xi_k)| \odot G(W_k)$, highlighting the scaling by $F(W_k)$ and the introduction of a bias term related to $G(W_k)$.
 - A generic "Response Function Class" is defined, characterized by positive-definiteness and differentiability of $q_+(w)$ and $q_-(w)$, ensuring broad applicability of the theoretical framework.

2. **Theoretical Analysis of Analog SGD:**
 - The paper theoretically demonstrates that due to the asymmetric response functions (non-zero $G(W)$), Analog SGD does not optimize the original objective function $f(W)$. Instead, it implicitly optimizes a "penalized objective" $f_\Sigma(W) = f(W) + \langle \Sigma, R_c(W) \rangle$, where $R_c(W)$ is related to the integrated asymmetry ratio $G(W)/F(W)$ and $\Sigma$ is the element-wise first moment of stochastic gradients.
 - This implicit penalty attracts weights towards "symmetric points" ($W^\diamond$, where $G(W^\diamond)=0$), which may not coincide with the optimal point $W^*$ of the true objective $f(W)$.
 - This leads to "inexact convergence" for Analog SGD, where it converges to a critical point of the penalized objective, resulting in a non-diminishing asymptotic error relative to the true optimal point. The asymptotic error is shown to be proportional to $\sigma^2 S_{ASGD_K}$, where $S_{ASGD_K}$ is an amplification factor dependent on the asymmetry.

3. **Proposed Solution: Residual Learning:**
 - To address the inexact convergence, the authors propose "Residual Learning," a novel theoretical framework formulated as a bilevel optimization problem:
 $\min_W \|P^*(W)\|^2$, subject to $P^*(W) \in \arg\min_P f(W+\gamma P)$.
 Here, $P^*(W)$ represents a residual array that aims to converge to zero as $W$ approaches the optimal point $W^*$.
 - The algorithm introduces two sequences: a main analog weight array $W_k$ and an auxiliary analog residual array $P_k$.
 - **Update for $P_k$ (Lower-level optimization):** $P_{k+1} = P_k - \alpha\nabla f(\bar{W}_k; \xi_k) \odot F(P_k) - \alpha|\nabla f(\bar{W}_k; \xi_k)| \odot G(P_k)$, where $\bar{W}_k = W_k + \gamma P_k$ is the mixed weight. This aims to drive $P_k$ towards $P^*(W_k)$.
 - **Update for $W_k$ (Upper-level optimization/Transfer Process):** $W_{k+1} = W_k + \beta P_{k+1} \odot F(W_k) - \beta|P_{k+1}| \odot G(W_k)$. This transfers the corrected residual from $P_k$ to $W_k$, driving $W_k$ towards optimality.
 - A critical assumption, "Zero-shifted symmetric point" ($G(0)=0$), is introduced for the residual array $P_k$. This can be achieved through a "zero-shifting technique" in hardware.
 - Under this zero-shift assumption and strong convexity (to ensure uniqueness of $P^*(W)$), Residual Learning is theoretically proven to achieve "exact convergence," meaning the asymptotic error vanishes as training progresses. The paper also highlights how this framework relates to and improves upon the existing Tiki-Taka algorithm.

4. **Extension to Other Hardware Imperfections (Residual Learning v2):**
 - **Noisy I/O:** To filter reading errors from the $P_k$ array, Residual Learning v2 introduces a digital buffer $H_k$ that performs a moving average of the noisy $P_k$ signals: $H_{k+1} = (1-\beta)H_k + \beta(P_{k+1} + \epsilon_{k+1})$. This filtered signal $H_k$ is then used in the transfer process for $W_k$.
 - **Limited Granularity:** A threshold-based transfer mechanism is proposed. Instead of transferring the entire $H_k$ at each step, an update to $W_k$ (by $\Delta w_{min}$) only occurs if a component of $H_k$ exceeds $\Delta w_{min}$. The remainder is stored in $H_k$ for future accumulation.

5. **Numerical Simulations and Experimental Validation:**
 - The proposed algorithms and theoretical insights are validated using the IBM Analog Hardware Acceleration Kit (AIHWKIT), an open-source toolkit.
 - Simulations are conducted on:
 - Synthetic least squares problems to illustrate theoretical concepts (e.g., the degradation of Tiki-Taka when $G(0) \ne 0$).
 - Real-world image classification tasks on MNIST (with FCN and CNN models) and CIFAR10/100 (with ResNet and MobileNet models).
 - Various types of response functions (power and exponential) are tested.
 - Ablation studies are performed to investigate the impact of the mixing coefficient $\gamma$ in Residual Learning and the resilience to cycle variation noise.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings regarding the impact of non-ideal response functions on analog in-memory training and the efficacy of the proposed mitigation strategies:

1. **Formulation of Analog Update Dynamics:** A discrete-time mathematical model for analog updates ($W_{k+1} = W_k - \alpha\nabla f(W_k; \xi_k) \odot F(W_k) - \alpha|\nabla f(W_k; \xi_k)| \odot G(W_k)$) was established. This model effectively captures the behavior of generic non-ideal response functions by decomposing them into symmetric ($F(W)$) and asymmetric ($G(W)$) components. This provides a foundation for theoretical analysis at the algorithmic level, abstracting detailed pulse-level mechanics.

2. **Implicit Penalty in Analog SGD:** The analysis demonstrated that asymmetric response functions, represented by $G(W) \ne 0$, introduce an "implicit penalty" into the Analog SGD optimization process. Instead of minimizing the original objective $f(W)$, Analog SGD implicitly optimizes a penalized objective $f_\Sigma(W) = f(W) + \langle \Sigma, R_c(W) \rangle$. This penalty term $R_c(W)$ (related to the integration of the asymmetry ratio $G(W)/F(W)$) drives the weights towards symmetric points ($W^\diamond$), which may not coincide with the true optimal points of $f(W)$.

3. **Inexact Convergence of Analog SGD:** As a direct consequence of the implicit penalty, Analog SGD exhibits inexact convergence. It converges only to a neighborhood of the true critical point, resulting in a persistent, non-diminishing asymptotic error. This error is quantitatively shown to be $O(\sigma^2 S_{ASGD_K})$, where $\sigma^2$ is the noise variance and $S_{ASGD_K}$ is an amplification factor that depends on the magnitude of the asymmetry ($G(W_k)/\sqrt{F(W_k)}$) near the critical point.

4. **Residual Learning Achieves Exact Convergence:**
 - The proposed Residual Learning framework, formulated as a bilevel optimization problem, was proven to overcome the asymmetric update and implicit penalty issues. By explicitly aligning the algorithmic stationary point with a "zero-shifted" physical symmetric point ($G(0)=0$) for the residual array $P_k$, the asymptotic error is eliminated.
 - The framework offers a theoretical explanation for the success of heuristic algorithms like Tiki-Taka and demonstrates that a non-zero mixing coefficient $\gamma$ in the bilevel formulation (unlike simpler Tiki-Taka variants) can enhance performance.
 - Simulations on MNIST (FCN, CNN) and CIFAR10/100 (ResNet, MobileNet) consistently showed that Residual Learning (or Tiki-Taka with appropriate parameters) significantly outperforms Analog SGD, often achieving test accuracy comparable to Digital SGD, even under strong asymmetry. For instance, Analog SGD sometimes failed completely (accuracy below 15%) when asymmetry was pronounced, while Residual Learning maintained high performance.
 - A counter-example (Figure 2) illustrated that previous theoretical guarantees for Tiki-Taka, limited to specific linear response functions (where $G(0)=0$ implicitly), break down when the symmetric point shifts ($c_{Lin} \ne 0$), leading to degradation. Residual Learning's general theory addresses this.

5. **Robustness with Residual Learning v2:**
 - The extended Residual Learning v2, incorporating a digital buffer to filter I/O noise and a threshold-based transfer rule for limited granularity, further improved robustness.
 - RLv2 demonstrated superior performance over basic Residual Learning (Tiki-Taka) on the CIFAR100 dataset, indicating its effectiveness in mitigating these additional practical hardware imperfections.
 - Ablation studies confirmed that cycle variation (noise in individual pulse updates) had a negligible impact on overall training accuracy, validating the model's high-level abstraction for response functions.
 - The mixing coefficient $\gamma$ was found to be beneficial, with performance improving for non-zero $\gamma$ and showing robustness to its specific value in a reasonable range.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a significant theoretical contribution to the field of analog in-memory computing, with potential broad impacts across AI hardware and applications:

1. **Fundamental Theoretical Understanding:** The paper establishes a rigorous theoretical foundation for gradient-based training on AIMC hardware with *generic* non-ideal resistive elements. This moves beyond empirical observations and specific idealized models, providing a deeper understanding of the complex interactions between hardware imperfections and training dynamics. The identification of the "implicit penalty" and its role in inexact convergence for Analog SGD is a core insight.
2. **Principled Algorithmic Design:** By framing the problem as a bilevel optimization, the Residual Learning framework offers a principled approach to designing robust analog training algorithms. It generalizes and explains the success of heuristic algorithms like Tiki-Taka, while also providing conditions (e.g., the zero-shifted symmetric point) necessary for exact convergence. This shifts algorithm development from trial-and-error to a more theoretically guided process.
3. **Guidance for Hardware Co-design:** The theoretical insights can directly inform the design and optimization of future AIMC hardware. Understanding how parameters like the asymmetry ratio $G(W)/F(W)$ affect convergence (through $S_{ASGD_K}$) can guide material science and device engineering towards resistive elements with more favorable response functions, potentially leading to more accurate and efficient analog training chips. For example, the requirement for a zero-shifted symmetric point for the residual array ($G(0)=0$) provides a clear design target for elements intended for residual learning.
4. **Enabling Energy-Efficient AI Training:** By demonstrating and theoretically guaranteeing the feasibility of accurate gradient-based training on non-ideal AIMC hardware, this work directly contributes to overcoming the critical energy and computational bottlenecks of modern AI. This could lead to a significant reduction in the environmental footprint and financial cost of developing and deploying large-scale AI models, making advanced AI more accessible and sustainable.
5. **Broader Applicability to Physical Neural Networks:** The methodology developed for modeling and analyzing analog updates in resistive memory arrays could potentially be adapted and extended to other forms of physical neural networks (PNNs) that leverage various physical phenomena for computation. This offers a pathway for developing comprehensive theoretical frameworks across emerging computing paradigms.
