# A Stereo Visual SLAM System Using Object-Level Motion Estimation and Geometric Filtering Based on Cross Disparity

> [!summary] Summary
> OCD SLAM introduces a stereo visual SLAM system that improves camera trajectory accuracy in dynamic environments by integrating object-level motion estimation with a novel cross disparity-based geometric filtering mechanism. The system accurately distinguishes between static and dynamic features, enhancing robustness in complex outdoor scenes.

## Problem

- Traditional visual SLAM (vSLAM) systems fundamentally fail or degrade in performance in dynamic environments due to their static-world assumption.
- Existing dynamic SLAM approaches suffer from limitations such as indiscriminately removing features within bounding boxes, being restricted to predefined object categories, or susceptibility to sensor noise in geometric checks.
- There is a need for a robust and comprehensive SLAM solution that can accurately localize and map in real-world scenarios with diverse and unpredictable moving objects.

## Method

- **OCD SLAM** extends the ==ORB-SLAM2== stereo architecture by integrating a deep learning-based 3D object detection module (==SMOKE==) for identifying and tracking objects.
- It employs a ==Kalman filter== to estimate the velocity of tracked objects, classifying them as 'static' or 'dynamic' to guide feature weighting in ==bundle adjustment==.
- A novel **'cross disparity'** geometric filter is introduced to detect dynamic features that are not associated with detected objects, providing a complementary mechanism for motion classification.

## Results

- OCD SLAM achieved superior Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) compared to ORB-SLAM2 and several state-of-the-art dynamic SLAM systems across most sequences in the KITTI Odometry and Raw datasets.
- Ablation studies confirmed the complementary nature of the 3D object detection and cross disparity modules, with the full system consistently outperforming individual components in dynamic scenarios.
- The system operates at an average processing rate of **12–14 frames per second**, demonstrating its capability for real-time applications in dynamic outdoor environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining object-level semantic understanding (3D object detection and tracking) with a novel feature-level geometric constraint provides a more comprehensive and robust approach to dynamic SLAM.
> - The cross disparity-based geometric filter effectively identifies dynamic features from uncategorized or partially detected moving objects by leveraging temporal and stereo inconsistencies from an additional viewpoint.
> - Classifying objects as truly 'static' or 'dynamic' based on velocity estimation allows retention of useful static features on potentially mobile objects, improving overall localization accuracy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) is a fundamental capability for autonomous systems such as robots and self-driving vehicles, enabling them to perceive their environment and determine their own position. Visual SLAM (vSLAM), which utilizes cameras, has gained prominence due to its cost-effectiveness and performance. Established vSLAM algorithms like ORB-SLAM2, DSO-SLAM, and LSD SLAM typically operate under a static-world assumption, meaning they presume the environment around the camera is stationary. This assumption causes these systems to fail or exhibit significant performance degradation when dynamic objects, such as moving vehicles or pedestrians, are present in the scene.

To address this limitation, research in dynamic SLAM has explored various approaches, broadly categorized into geometric methods, deep learning-based methods, and hybrid approaches. Geometric methods analyze the motion or properties of image features (e.g., epipolar constraints, depth consistency, RANSAC-based outlier rejection) to distinguish static from dynamic elements. These methods, however, can be sensitive to sensor noise, may misinterpret static features as dynamic, or fail when objects move along specific geometric lines (e.g., epipolar lines). Deep learning-based methods leverage neural networks for semantic segmentation or object detection to identify predefined dynamic objects. While effective for known categories, these methods can suffer from limitations such as removing static regions within detected dynamic bounding boxes (e.g., a parked car identified as a "car") or failing to detect dynamic objects that do not belong to their predefined training classes. Hybrid approaches combine the strengths of both, using semantic information to guide geometric checks, but they can still encounter issues like misclassifying features or lacking robustness across diverse dynamic scenarios.

This work, OCD SLAM, positions itself within this landscape by building upon the established ORB-SLAM2 framework. It aims to overcome the limitations of existing dynamic SLAM methods by integrating a dual-level approach: object-level motion estimation using 3D object detection and tracking, and a novel feature-level geometric filter based on "cross disparity." This combination is intended to provide a more robust and comprehensive solution for separating static and dynamic scene elements, particularly in outdoor stereo vision environments common in autonomous driving.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust stereo visual SLAM system that can accurately estimate camera pose and build consistent maps in highly dynamic environments. This overarching goal is driven by several specific objectives and motivations, aiming to address the shortcomings of existing SLAM systems.

**Key Objectives:**
1. **Improve Trajectory Accuracy in Dynamic Scenes:** A central objective is to significantly enhance the accuracy of camera trajectory estimation compared to traditional static-environment SLAM systems like ORB-SLAM2, which are known to degrade in the presence of moving objects.
2. **Enable Object-Level Motion Classification:** The system aims to move beyond simple removal of entire semantic regions by actively classifying detected objects as either "static" (e.g., a parked car) or "dynamic" (e.g., a moving car) through velocity estimation. This allows static features on potentially dynamic objects to be retained for pose estimation.
3. **Perform Feature-Level Motion Classification for Undetected Objects:** To complement object-level detection, the system seeks to introduce a geometric mechanism capable of identifying individual dynamic features that belong to moving objects not recognized, categorized, or fully detected by the 3D object detection module.
4. **Integrate and Complement Different Motion Detection Strategies:** A key objective is to combine a deep learning-based 3D object detection and tracking module with a novel geometric filtering technique. This integration is designed to leverage the respective strengths of both approaches while mitigating their individual limitations.
5. **Minimize the Impact of Dynamic Features on SLAM Optimization:** By effectively classifying and weighting features, the system aims to reduce the negative influence of dynamic map points on the bundle adjustment process, thereby leading to more accurate and consistent pose estimation and map construction.

**Motivation:**
The motivation for this research stems from the critical need for reliable environmental perception and self-localization in real-world applications such as autonomous driving and robotic navigation. Traditional visual SLAM systems, despite their performance in static settings, are fundamentally limited by their static-world assumption. Existing efforts to mitigate the effects of dynamic objects suffer from several drawbacks:
- **Limitations of Deep Learning Methods:** These methods often remove features indiscriminately within detected bounding boxes, potentially discarding useful static information (e.g., features on parked vehicles). They are also confined to detecting predefined object classes, failing to identify dynamic motion from uncategorized objects.
- **Limitations of Geometric Methods:** While generalizable, geometric methods can be susceptible to sensor noise and may misclassify static features as dynamic. Furthermore, some geometric constraints (e.g., epipolar geometry) primarily check 2D consistency and can fail when object motion aligns with epipolar lines, a common occurrence in outdoor environments. Depth-based methods also face challenges with sensor noise and the assumption of perfect 3D point tracking.
- **Need for Robustness:** There is a clear need for a SLAM system that can effectively handle the complexities of real-world dynamic environments, distinguishing between objects that are structurally dynamic (e.g., a "car") and their actual motion state (parked vs. moving), and also detecting motion from unexpected or uncategorized sources.
The proposed OCD SLAM aims to address these identified gaps by offering a comprehensive, hybrid approach that is more robust to diverse dynamic scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

OCD SLAM is developed as an extension of the ORB-SLAM2 stereo architecture. Its framework incorporates four parallel threads: Tracking, Local Mapping, Loop Closing, and a dedicated 3D Object Detection thread. The methodology integrates deep learning-based object detection and tracking with a novel geometric filtering mechanism.

**A. 3D Object Detection Thread:**
This thread processes the incoming stereo images (typically the left image) to estimate the 3D properties of objects.
- **Detector**: The system employs SMOKE (Single-Stage Monocular 3D Object Detection via Keypoint Estimation), a deep learning model trained on the KITTI dataset for detecting common dynamic objects like cars, pedestrians, and bicycles.
- **Output**: SMOKE predicts the components of a 3D bounding box, including a projected 3D keypoint (x, y, z), dimensions (height, width, length), and the yaw angle (φ) of the object relative to the camera coordinate system.
- **Mechanism**: It uses a DLA-34 backbone with deformable convolutions. Two prediction heads extract features: one for keypoint classification (generating a heatmap for projected 3D object centers) and another for regression (encoding an 8-dimensional vector comprising depth offset, subpixel offsets, dimension residuals, and local orientation). The yaw angle is computed from the object's location and local orientation.

**B. Assigning Features to Objects (Convex Hull):**
To accurately associate extracted image features with detected objects, a method is used to refine object boundaries.
- **Challenge**: Traditional 2D rectangular bounding boxes often include significant background areas.
- **Solution**: The system computes a convex hull using the eight projected 3D corner points of the estimated 3D bounding box. This generates a polygon that tightly encloses the object in the 2D image plane.
- **Feature Association**: Any ORB feature points located within this generated polygon are considered part of the object. This approach is intended to provide a more precise separation between object features and background features.

**C. Assigning IDs to Objects and Tracking:**
For consistent tracking and motion estimation, objects are identified and tracked across consecutive frames.
- **Multi-Criteria Association**: To overcome limitations of solely feature-based matching, the system associates objects between frames using a combination of criteria:
 1. Matching of ORB descriptors of features associated with the objects.
 2. Relative difference in the estimated volumes (height × width × length) of the objects.
 3. Difference between the estimated 3D locations of the objects.
- **ID Assignment**: A weighted sum of these similarity measures is computed. The object in the current frame with the lowest error (highest similarity) is assigned the ID of an object from the previous frame. New unique IDs are assigned to newly detected objects or those that fail to find a consistent predecessor.

**D. Velocity Estimation of Tracked Objects (Kalman Filter):**
To determine if a tracked object is moving or static, its velocity is estimated using a Kalman filter.
- **Problem**: Direct finite difference calculation of velocity from noisy 3D object positions is unreliable.
- **Solution**: A Linear Kalman filter is employed.
 - **State Vector**: Defined as X_k = [p_k, v_k]^T, where p_k is the 3D position of the object (transformed to the world frame) and v_k is its 3D velocity.
 - **State Transition Model**: Assumes constant velocity between frames.
 - **Observation Model**: Only the 3D position (z_k) is used as a measurement.
 - **Noise Parameters**: Process noise covariance Q_k and measurement noise covariance R_k are empirically set (0.1 * I_6x6 and 0.01 * I_3x3, respectively). Initial velocity covariance is set high, and initial velocity to zero, to reflect uncertainty.

**E. Motion Labeling:**
Based on the Kalman filter's velocity estimation and its associated uncertainty, each object is assigned one of three motion labels:
- **Static (Parked)**: If the estimated speed falls below a predefined threshold.
- **Dynamic (Moving)**: If the estimated speed exceeds the threshold.
- **Unknown**: If the velocity covariance is high (indicating low confidence) or if the object has just been assigned a new ID.
- **Feature Filtering**:
 - Features belonging to "Dynamic" objects are removed from the SLAM process.
 - Features of "Unknown" objects are not utilized in the bundle adjustment (BA) process.
 - Features on "Static" (Parked) objects are considered static and assigned a higher weight in BA. This step also serves to re-label any features that might have been incorrectly identified as dynamic by the geometric module but are part of a clearly parked object.

**F. Cross Disparity-Based Geometric Motion Constraint:**
This novel geometric approach is designed to detect dynamic features that are not associated with objects detected by SMOKE (e.g., uncategorized objects, partially occluded objects, or small moving elements).
- **Disparity (D_t)**: The standard horizontal shift of a feature's projection between the left (p^L_t) and right (p^R_t) images at time t: D_t = (p^L_t)_x - (p^R_t)_x.
- **Cross Disparity (D^cross_{t-1})**: Let p^L_{t-1} be a feature in the previous left image, and q^L_t be its correspondence in the current left image. The 3D point Q_t is triangulated from (q^L_t, q^R_t). This Q_t is then projected back into the *previous right image* (CamR_{t-1}) to obtain q^R_{t-1}. The cross disparity is defined as D^cross_{t-1} = (p^L_{t-1})_x - (q^R_{t-1})_x.
- **Principle**: For a static feature, the projection q^R_{t-1} should align closely with the original feature position p^R_{t-1} in the previous right image. Consequently, the difference |D^cross_{t-1} - D_{t-1}| should be minimal (within a tolerance τ). A difference exceeding τ indicates that the feature lies on a dynamic object.
- **Advantage**: This method combines temporal consistency (tracking features from t-1 to t) with stereo consistency and leverages an "additional viewpoint" (projecting back to CamR_{t-1}) to provide a wider geometric baseline for motion detection compared to conventional depth or epipolar checks.
- **Integration**: This module specifically targets features *not* allocated to objects identified by the 3D object detection thread, ensuring a complementary role.
- **Weighting in BA**: Static points are assigned a weight of 1.0, while dynamic points receive a significantly lower weight of 0.001 in the bundle adjustment process.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The performance of OCD SLAM was quantitatively evaluated on the KITTI Odometry and KITTI Raw datasets using Absolute Trajectory Error (ATE) and Relative Pose Error (RPE) as metrics. All reported results are average values over 25 independent executions.

**A. Evaluation on KITTI Odometry Dataset:**
- **Comparison with ORB-SLAM2 (Table II)**: OCD SLAM exhibited lower ATE values than ORB-SLAM2 in the majority of sequences, notably showing significant improvements in highly dynamic sequences such as 01 (10.96m to 8.68m), 07 (0.53m to 0.46m), and 09 (3.60m to 2.98m). In a few sequences (03, 04, 05), ATE remained comparable or slightly increased. RPE values (rotational and translational) for OCD SLAM were generally comparable or marginally higher than ORB-SLAM2. This behavior is attributed to dynamic SLAM systems down-weighting or discarding features, which can reduce long-term drift (improving ATE) but potentially introduce minor short-term motion noise (affecting RPE).
- **Comparison with State-of-the-Art (Table III)**: OCD SLAM achieved the best ATE in 5 out of 11 sequences (00, 01, 02, 06, 07) when compared against DynaSLAM, Wen et al., Yang et al., and RSO-SLAM. This suggests that the combined approach of cross disparity-based geometric constraint and object velocity-based motion categorization contributes to improved trajectory estimation accuracy over several existing dynamic SLAM methods. The paper notes that approaches simply removing dynamic regions (like DynaSLAM) can be less effective than those preserving static features or explicitly distinguishing object motion states.

**B. Evaluation on KITTI Raw Dataset:**
- **Challenging Scenarios**: This dataset presented more complex environments, including heavy traffic, slow-moving vehicles, and objects not explicitly detected by SMOKE (e.g., buses, trucks).
- **Comparison with State-of-the-Art (Table IV)**: OCD SLAM consistently outperformed ORB-SLAM2 across all 7 evaluated sequences. Compared to DynaSLAM and AirDos, OCD SLAM achieved superior ATE in 5 out of 7 sequences (0926-0009, 0926-0014, 0926-0051, 0926-0101, 0929-0004). However, in Sequence 1003-0047, DynaSLAM recorded the lowest error, though OCD SLAM still improved upon ORB-SLAM2 and AirDos. These results indicate robustness in more challenging, less-structured dynamic environments.

**C. Ablation Experiment (Table V):**
An ablation study was conducted on the KITTI Raw dataset to assess the individual contributions of the SMOKE (3D object detection) and cross disparity modules.
- **SMOKE Only**: Using only the SMOKE module (without cross disparity) resulted in higher ATE values compared to the full system. This configuration could not effectively remove features on uncategorized dynamic objects (e.g., buses) or correctly identify static features on objects that might be detected but were temporarily stationary (e.g., parked cars, which would not be dynamically filtered without velocity estimation).
- **Cross Disparity Only**: This configuration, relying solely on the geometric cross disparity module, demonstrated better performance than SMOKE-only in several sequences (e.g., 0926-0101, 0929-0004). This indicates its effectiveness in detecting dynamic features without needing object category knowledge. However, it showed significantly higher errors in Sequence 1003-0047, suggesting limitations in scenarios with numerous slow-moving dynamic objects where object-level context might be more beneficial.
- **Full OCD SLAM System**: The combination of both SMOKE and cross disparity consistently yielded the best results across all sequences. This finding supports the hypothesis that the two modules are complementary: SMOKE handles known dynamic and static objects effectively, while cross disparity addresses dynamic features from undetected or uncategorized moving objects.

**D. Computational Time (Table VI):**
The average computational time costs for the main components were:
- Feature Extraction: 15.63 ms/frame
- Static Point Detection (Cross Disparity): 38.97 ms/frame (the most time-consuming component)
- 3D Object Detection (SMOKE): 22.54 ms/frame
The overall processing rate for OCD SLAM was observed to be 12–14 frames per second (fps), which is lower than ORB-SLAM2's approximate 23–25 fps. The authors indicate that this performance, while acceptable for some applications, could be enhanced through code optimization and hardware improvements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of OCD SLAM contributes to the field of visual SLAM by offering a robust solution for environments characterized by dynamic elements, addressing a long-standing challenge for traditional SLAM systems.

**Significance:**
- **Enhanced Robustness to Dynamic Environments**: The system demonstrates improved accuracy in camera localization (ATE) in highly dynamic urban and residential outdoor scenes, as evidenced by its performance on KITTI datasets compared to ORB-SLAM2 and several state-of-the-art dynamic SLAM methods. This capability is critical for applications that operate in real-world settings with unpredictable motion.
- **Dual-Layered Motion Detection**: The integration of object-level motion classification (using 3D object detection, tracking, and Kalman filtering) with a feature-level geometric filter (based on cross disparity) represents a comprehensive approach. This dual strategy allows for distinguishing between truly moving and stationary objects (e.g., parked cars vs. moving cars) and also identifying dynamic features from objects not explicitly categorized or detected by the deep learning module.
- **Novel Geometric Constraint**: The introduction of "cross disparity" as a geometric constraint provides a new method for detecting dynamic features. By simultaneously leveraging temporal and stereo inconsistencies from an additional viewpoint, this approach offers a distinct geometric baseline for motion validation, potentially mitigating some limitations of previous geometric methods.
- **Complementary Module Design**: The ablation studies highlight the complementary nature of the object detection and cross disparity modules. This suggests a valuable architectural principle for dynamic SLAM systems, where different techniques can effectively cover each other's weaknesses, leading to a more resilient overall system.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancement in Autonomous Systems**: The improved localization and mapping accuracy in dynamic environments directly impacts the reliability and safety of autonomous vehicles, mobile robots, and drones operating in complex, populated areas. This work brings SLAM systems closer to real-world deployment requirements.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The hybrid approach and the novel cross disparity concept could serve as a foundation for further research in dynamic SLAM, inspiring new ways to combine semantic understanding with geometric reasoning.
- **Real-time Applications**: While the current computational performance is moderate (12-14 fps), potential optimizations could enable its use in a wider range of real-time applications, including augmented reality and human-robot interaction in dynamic settings.
- **Addressing Uncategorized Dynamics**: The cross disparity module's ability to detect dynamic features from uncategorized objects is particularly impactful, as it helps bridge the gap where deep learning models might fall short due to limited training data or novel object classes.

**Limitations and Future Work (as noted by authors):**
The current implementation of the 3D object detection module is limited to a predefined set of objects (cars, bicycles, pedestrians). In environments with a wider variety of dynamic objects (e.g., buses, trucks, trams in KITTI Raw), the effectiveness of the cross disparity module alone might be strained if too many such uncategorized dynamic objects are present. Future work is suggested to expand the detectable object categories, which could further enhance the system's robustness and accuracy in more diverse and complex real-world scenes. Additionally, optimizing the computational performance for higher frame rates remains an area for improvement.
