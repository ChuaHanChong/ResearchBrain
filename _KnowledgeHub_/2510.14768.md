# Leveraging Neural Descriptor Fields for Learning Contact-Aware Dynamic Recovery

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Michigan and Honda Research Institute USA introduce CADRE, a reinforcement learning framework that leverages Neural Descriptor Fields (NDFs) to enable dynamic recovery from object-dropping failures. This method not only successfully catches falling objects but also recovers a grasp suitable for resuming the primary manipulation task, achieving an 80% success rate in real-world screwdriver recovery and outperforming baselines in simulation for generalization and downstream task performance.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation is prone to unexpected object-dropping failures due to unmodeled disturbances, especially in systems lacking tactile feedback.
- Existing object catching methods often prioritize stable power grasps, which may not satisfy the specific contact requirements (e.g., precision grasps) for resuming complex primary manipulation tasks.
- Generalizing dynamic, contact-rich manipulation skills to objects with varied geometries is challenging and typically requires extensive retraining.

## Method

- The **CADRE** (==Contact-Aware Dynamic Recovery==) framework formulates dynamic recovery as a reinforcement learning (RL) problem, optimizing for both object catching and recovery to a task-favorable state.
- ==Neural Descriptor Fields (NDFs)== are pretrained to provide implicit, contact-aware geometric features from key points on the robot hand, augmenting the standard RL policy observations.
- A ==Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)== agent is trained with a ==composite reward function== that encourages smooth actions, object stabilization, reaching desired robot/object poses, and achieving task-relevant contact configurations.

## Results

- **CADRE** achieved faster convergence and higher cumulative rewards than all baselines in simulation, demonstrating superior performance in achieving desired contact configurations and lower position/orientation errors.
- In simulation, **CADRE** maintained high catch success rates (e.g., **96.27%** for OOD screwdrivers, **91.06%** for OOD nuts) and enabled better downstream task performance on unseen objects, significantly outperforming baselines in overall task success.
- On physical hardware, **CADRE** demonstrated an **80.0%** success rate for real screwdriver recovery, substantially surpassing baselines (**0-33.3%**) and successfully acquiring task-specific contact states.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating NDF-derived implicit contact features into the RL state representation significantly enhances training efficiency and enables the learning of higher-quality, task-specific recovery grasps.
> - It is possible to train a dynamic recovery policy that simultaneously prevents an object from falling and repositions it into a configuration suitable for seamlessly resuming a precision manipulation task.
> - NDF representations facilitate zero-shot generalization of contact-aware recovery policies to unseen object geometries, allowing the system to maintain performance across diverse manipulation scenarios without retraining.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation in real-world environments is susceptible to unexpected errors and disturbances, which can lead to failures such as dropping manipulated objects. While previous research has focused on enhancing robustness against uncertainties and disturbances, these efforts often encounter limitations when unmodeled factors or disturbances exceed the system's design tolerance. This is particularly challenging for robotic systems lacking tactile sensing, where direct contact feedback is unavailable to confirm a firm grasp.

This work addresses the problem of *dynamic recovery*, specifically catching falling objects before irreversible failure. It proposes a complementary strategy to existing robustness improvements by incorporating a fallback catching policy. This policy activates upon object drop detection and aims to not only catch the object but also to reset the robot system to a configuration suitable for resuming the primary manipulation task. This latter objective distinguishes the present work from prior research on object catching, which primarily focuses on stable catching without considering the subsequent task requirements or the specific grasp types (e.g., precision grasps) often needed for complex manipulation.

In the domain of representation learning for manipulation, various approaches have been explored, including direct use of point clouds, image observations encoded into latent spaces, and latent scene embeddings derived from Neural Radiance Fields. However, these methods often do not explicitly address contact-rich dexterous manipulation scenarios. Neural Descriptor Fields (NDFs) and similar implicit geometric representations have shown promise in motion planning and implicit grasp feature learning for cross-object generalization. The current paper contributes by applying NDF-inspired representations to reinforcement learning for *dynamic dexterous manipulation*, specifically for enabling contact-aware recovery and facilitating generalization across different object geometries. This fills a recognized gap in the existing literature regarding the application of such implicit geometric representations in dynamic, contact-rich RL tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a method for dynamic recovery from object-dropping failures in dexterous manipulation. This problem formulation extends beyond merely preventing an object from falling, encompassing two specific, integrated goals:
1. **Object Catching:** The robot must successfully prevent the object from being dropped once an unexpected disturbance causes it to slip from the initial grasp.
2. **Task-Favorable State Recovery:** Critically, the system must be reset to a configuration (robot joint angles and object pose) that is favorable for the seamless resumption of the primary manipulation task. This implies that the recovery grasp must meet specific contact requirements of the ongoing task, rather than just a stable, generic power grasp.

The motivation for this dual objective stems from practical challenges in robotics:
- **Limitations of simple catching:** While power grasps can effectively catch a wide range of objects, many primary manipulation tasks (e.g., screwdriver turning, nut mating) require specialized grasp types, such as precision grasps. Merely catching an object with a power grasp would necessitate an additional, complex re-grasping or in-hand manipulation phase to achieve the desired precision grasp, which presents significant challenges. Therefore, the recovery policy needs to directly achieve a grasp configuration that supports the contact requirements of the primary task.
- **Generalization to varied objects:** Real-world applications often involve objects with different geometries. The recovery policy must be capable of adapting to these variations without requiring extensive retraining for each new object. This necessitates a representation that can generalize contact behaviors across diverse geometries.
- **Contact-rich manipulation:** Dexterous manipulation heavily relies on maintaining specific contacts between the robotic hand and the object. Conventional state representations like object pose or raw point clouds may not directly encode the nuanced contact information necessary for complex dexterous tasks and generalization across object geometries.
- **Leveraging Neural Descriptor Fields (NDFs):** NDFs are motivated as a solution to provide a rich, implicit representation of geometric correspondence. By extracting NDF features from predefined key points on the robotic hand, the system can reason about finger-object correspondence and interpret these features as implicit indicators of contact, including both desired contact regions (e.g., fingertips) and regions where contact should be avoided (e.g., palm for precision grasps). This approach is hypothesized to improve training efficiency, enhance convergence for reinforcement learning, and enable zero-shot generalization to unseen object geometries by maintaining consistent contact behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed approach, Contact-Aware Dynamic Recovery (CADRE), employs a reinforcement learning (RL) framework enhanced by implicit contact features derived from Neural Descriptor Fields (NDFs).

**Problem Formulation:**
The dynamic recovery task is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) defined by (S, A, p, r, γ).
- **State (o_t):** At each time step `t`, the policy receives observations including the robot's joint angles (q_t), object pose in SE(3) (x_t), and the object's twist (linear and angular velocities) (v_t).
- **Action (a_t):** The robot action is defined as the desired joint position for a low-level joint position controller at the next time step.
- **Assumptions:** The method assumes knowledge of the object's geometry, represented by a point cloud (P), which can be generated from a scan or CAD model. Desired recovery configurations (ˆq for robot, ˆx for object) are specified, which are considered favorable for the primary manipulation task.
- **Generalization:** The method aims to generalize to unseen objects, with training objects randomly sampled from a predefined distribution, and evaluation on both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) objects.

**Neural Descriptor Fields (NDFs) Integration:**
1. **NDF Pretraining:** A Neural Descriptor Field model (f_NDF) is pretrained offline and kept fixed during RL training. This model takes a 3D query coordinate (p) and an object point cloud (P) to output an n-dimensional geometric feature: f_NDF(p|P): R^3 → R^n. The model uses an MLP-based network, Φ, conditioned on latent features of the object point cloud extracted by a PointNet-based encoder (E). Φ is a double-headed model predicting both occupancy and signed distance, which helps capture features for points both near and away from contact regions.
2. **Contact-Aware Grasp Feature:** To characterize the grasp, K key points are predefined on the robot hand links ({p^k_i}_i=1^K). These are sampled from each hand link (e.g., root of each link) to encompass regions beyond just fingertips (e.g., palm) relevant for distinguishing grasp types. The grasp feature g(q, x|P) is formed by concatenating the NDF features of all these key points: g(q, x|P):= M_i=1^K f_NDF(T(x)f_fk(p^k_i, q, k_i)|P), where T(x) transforms to the object frame and f_fk is the forward kinematics function. This feature encodes implicit contact information for the current grasp configuration.
3. **RL Policy Input:** The RL policy receives the standard observations (o_t) augmented with this contact-aware grasp feature (g_t).

**Reinforcement Learning with PPO:**
- **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), a model-free RL method, is used to optimize the recovery policy due to the complex dynamics.
- **Reward Function (r):** A composite reward function is designed to guide the policy:
 - `r_˙a`: Penalizes large changes in actions (joint positions) to promote smoothness.
 - `r_torque`, `r_energy`: Penalize large joint torques and energy consumption for efficiency and safety.
 - `r_drop`: Penalizes the object falling outside a predefined workspace boundary.
 - `r_obj_v`: Exponentially rewards low linear and angular velocities of the object, encouraging stabilization.
 - `r_q`, `r_obj_pose`: Exponentially reward the robot moving to the desired robot configuration (ˆq) and object pose (ˆx), respectively.
 - `r_contact`: Rewards achieving task-relevant contact configurations and can penalize undesired contacts. Task-relevant contacts are defined based on finger-object distances in the desired recovery configuration.
 - `r_safety`: Penalizes robot-table contact.
- **Domain Randomization:** To improve robustness and bridge the sim-to-real gap, domain randomization is applied during training. This includes adding random external wrenches to the object and random perception noise to object pose and velocities.

**Simulation Experiment Setup:**
- **Simulator:** IsaacSim.
- **Robot:** Allegro Hand mounted on a 7-DoF KUKA iiwa arm.
- **Object Generation:** 50 objects sampled from a geometric distribution for training, with size variations. An NDF model is trained using objects from this distribution. Evaluation uses both in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) objects.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Catch success rate, cumulative reward, number of desired contacts, number of undesired contacts, position error, and orientation error (all for successful trials). Downstream task performance (screwdriver turning, nut mating) is also evaluated.

**Baselines and Ablation Methods:**
1. **Pose (Ablation):** Uses only the robot's joint angles, object pose, and object twist as input. Lacks object geometry or explicit contact information.
2. **Point Cloud (Baseline):** Augments the observation with the object's point cloud. A PointNet encodes the point cloud features. This method has access to geometry but not explicit contact features.
3. **DexPoint (Baseline):** Augments observation with an imagined point cloud of robot fingertips and palm, concatenated with the object point cloud, encoded by PointNet. Aims for contact-aware generalization, but uses a different representation than NDFs.

**Hardware Experiments:**
- **Sim-to-Real Transfer:** An open-loop policy is distilled from the trained closed-loop RL policy to address real-world latency (50 ms) and occlusion issues with motion capture (Vicon system). The open-loop policy, a transformer decoder, takes initial observations and outputs an action sequence.
- **Setup:** Unseen real screwdriver (within training distribution). Manual initiation of drop by temporarily opening the hand. Blended trials to remove experimenter bias. Friction tape on the screwdriver handle to compensate for robot fingertip material properties.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated CADRE against baselines on screwdriver and nut recovery tasks in simulation and on a screwdriver recovery task in hardware.

**Simulation Results (Table I and Figure 3):**
- **RL Training Efficiency:** CADRE consistently achieved higher cumulative rewards and converged faster than all baselines (Pose, Point Cloud, DexPoint) in both screwdriver and nut recovery tasks, indicating improved training efficiency and better final policy performance.
- **Screwdriver Recovery (ID Objects):** All methods achieved nearly 100% catch success rates. However, CADRE significantly outperformed baselines in terms of total episode reward, number of desired contacts (2.54 for CADRE vs. 1.57-2.78 for baselines), lower undesired contacts (0.19 vs. 1.05-1.78), lower position error (4.34 cm vs. 4.89-6.25 cm), and comparable or lower orientation error. This suggested that baselines often resorted to less task-specific power grasps.
- **Screwdriver Recovery (OOD Objects):** CADRE maintained a high success rate (96.27%) on unseen objects and exhibited a smaller performance drop across most metrics compared to the Pose and Point Cloud baselines. DexPoint also maintained a high success rate, but its grasp quality, as measured by downstream tasks, was less optimal.
- **Nut Recovery (ID Objects):** CADRE demonstrated substantially higher catch success rates (98.53% vs. 60.67-75.33% for baselines), higher total reward, more desired contacts (3.85 vs. 1.66-2.88), fewer undesired contacts (0.15 vs. 0.26-0.36), and lower position and orientation errors.
- **Nut Recovery (OOD Objects):** CADRE maintained robust performance with a 91.06% success rate, showing a smaller performance degradation when facing OOD nuts compared to baselines (e.g., Pose 48.40%, Point Cloud 46.00%, DexPoint 38.40%).

**Downstream Task Performance (Table II):**
- **Screwdriver Turning:** CADRE showed superior performance in the subsequent screwdriver turning task. For ID objects, CADRE had a 4% drop rate and a 13.77° distance to goal, which increased to 4% drop rate and 35.25° for OOD objects. Baselines generally had higher drop rates on OOD objects (e.g., Pose 72%, DexPoint 28%) and larger distances to goal, indicating that CADRE's recovered grasps were more suitable for precision manipulation.
- **Nut Mating:** While some baselines achieved higher *valid success rates* (successful mating given successful catching), CADRE's significantly higher *catching success rate* resulted in a superior *overall success rate* (successful mating out of all trials) for both ID (53.6% for CADRE vs. 4.8-46.4% for baselines) and OOD (45.2% for CADRE vs. 8.4-40.8% for baselines) objects. This indicates that CADRE reliably catches the nut in a configuration that enables subsequent mating, even if the mating step itself is challenging.

**Hardware Experiment Results (Table III):**
- CADRE demonstrated a 80.0% success rate in catching a real screwdriver, significantly outperforming baselines (Pose: 6.7%, Point Cloud: 0.0%, DexPoint: 33.3%).
- CADRE achieved a higher average number of desired contacts (2.83) and zero undesired contacts, further indicating its ability to achieve task-specific grasps in the real world.
- A noticeable sim-to-real gap was observed, necessitating the distillation of an open-loop policy due to latency and occlusion challenges.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work makes contributions to the field of robotic manipulation and offers several areas of potential impact:

- **Enhanced Robotic Robustness:** The proposed CADRE framework addresses a critical gap in robotic manipulation by providing a strategy for dynamic recovery from object-dropping failures. This capability directly enhances the robustness and reliability of robotic systems operating in unpredictable real-world environments, where unexpected disturbances are common.
- **Novel Problem Formulation for Recovery:** By defining recovery as not only catching a falling object but also achieving a grasp configuration suitable for *resuming the primary manipulation task*, this research presents a more practical and application-oriented approach to robotic recovery. This nuanced understanding of recovery moves beyond simple object stabilization to enable seamless transitions back to productive work.
- **Efficacy of Implicit Contact Representations:** The research demonstrates the significant benefits of incorporating NDF-derived implicit contact features into reinforcement learning for dexterous manipulation. These features are shown to improve training efficiency, accelerate convergence, and yield higher-quality recovery grasps. This highlights NDFs as a powerful representation for tasks requiring detailed contact reasoning.
- **Improved Generalization Capabilities:** CADRE's ability to achieve zero-shot generalization to unseen objects with varying geometries is a substantial advancement. This reduces the need for extensive data collection and retraining for every new object encountered, leading to more adaptable and scalable robotic deployments.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The successful integration of NDFs into an RL framework for dynamic, contact-rich tasks opens new avenues for research. The paper suggests the broader potential for implicit contact representations in other complex manipulation scenarios, indicating a promising direction for developing more advanced robotic skills.
- **Practical Applicability:** The validation of CADRE through hardware experiments, despite existing sim-to-real challenges, demonstrates the practical feasibility of deploying such dynamic recovery policies on real robotic platforms. This bridge from simulation to hardware is crucial for transferring research outcomes to real-world applications.

**Limitations and Future Directions (as identified by the authors):**
The authors acknowledge limitations, including that the current NDF structure primarily focuses on geometric information for generalization, potentially overlooking dynamic variations associated with different object geometries. They also highlight persistent sim-to-real challenges in highly dynamic systems due to factors like system latency, gravity compensation errors, and policy computation time, suggesting future work on making policies more aware of these discrepancies. Overcoming these limitations would further enhance the precision and robustness of dynamic recovery systems in real-world scenarios.
