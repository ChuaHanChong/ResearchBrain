# iMaC: Translating Actions into Motion and Contact Images for Embodied World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> An embodied world model, iMaC, translates abstract robot actions into dense, image-like controls
—motion and contact images—to improve future video prediction for robot policy evaluation.
This approach achieved strong positive correlations (0.833 to 0.956) between world-model-based
success estimates and real-world policy performance across six out of eight challenging manipulation tasks.

## Problem

- Existing embodied world models struggle to reliably condition future video prediction on robot actions, particularly for fine-grained spatial consequences in manipulation tasks.
- Current action representations are often abstract (compact vectors) or merely visualizations, failing to directly control future robot body states or explicitly describe robot-scene interaction geometry.
- Evaluating robot policies in the real world is resource-intensive, time-consuming, difficult to reproduce consistently, and poses safety risks.

## Method

- **iMaC** integrates a multi-view ==image-to-video diffusion== transformer with novel action-derived control videos - Motion Images (direct pixel-level guidance for future robot appearance via URDF/forward-kinematics rendering), Scene-to-Gripper Contact Images, and Robot-to-Scene Contact Images.
- Contact Images encode contact-relevant spatial relations between the future robot's motion and the observed scene using two streams of pointcloud-derived distance fields.
- A training-time rollout strategy mitigates exposure bias by using the model's own one-step flow clean estimate of the final frame from the previous chunk as the next reference during training.

## Results

- iMaC achieved competitive future-video prediction quality, demonstrating the best task-averaged Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), and Frechet Video Distance (FVD) against baselines.
- For six out of eight challenging real-robot manipulation tasks, world-model evaluations exhibited strong positive correlations (**0.833** to **0.956**) with real-world policy performance, reliably preserving the relative ranking of policies.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both motion images and contact images are crucial for precise grasp and accurate robot body state prediction, with internally predicted RGB-D states yielding more consistent contact geometry than external depth estimation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Spatially explicit, image-like controls derived from robot kinematics and scene geometry can significantly enhance an embodied world model's ability to accurately predict fine-grained contact-rich manipulation.
> - Directly encoding future robot motion and robot-scene contact geometry into the latent space of a diffusion model reduces the model's burden of inferring complex interactions from abstract action representations.
> - Training a world model on its own generated contexts (via training-time rollout) is crucial for mitigating exposure bias and enabling robust, long-horizon closed-loop policy evaluation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

World models, which enable agents to predict the consequences of their actions, are recognized as a foundational component for planning and control in artificial intelligence. Recent advancements in video generation have extended this concept to robotics, allowing future states to be represented as direct visual observations rather than relying on manually engineered simulator states. These "embodied world models" are particularly relevant for robot policy evaluation, offering a scalable, reproducible, and potentially safer alternative to real-world hardware trials, which can be slow, expensive, and unsafe for certain failure scenarios.

A critical challenge for embodied world models, especially in manipulation tasks, is their ability to reliably condition future video prediction on robot actions. Existing approaches often encode future actions as compact vectors or use learned conditioning modules (e.g., cross-attention, AdaLN, FiLM). While convenient for adapting large video generators, these methods require the model to indirectly infer fine-grained spatial consequences. This abstraction is limiting for real-world manipulation, where subtle action differences (e.g., centimeter-level variations) can determine contact events, object motion, and overall task outcomes.

Other works have attempted more explicit action representations, such as projected spheres, projection-based action maps (EVAC, ABot-PhysWorld), or Gaussian mixture maps (Action Images). However, these primarily function as action visualizations and do not directly control the future robot body state within generated frames or explicitly describe the interaction geometry between the future robot and the current scene.

iMaC addresses this specific bottleneck by proposing a method for spatially explicit action representation that directly guides both future robot motion and contact-relevant robot-scene geometry. By translating abstract actions into dense, image-like controls, iMaC aims to enhance the reliability of embodied world models for long-horizon, closed-loop policy evaluation in contact-sensitive manipulation tasks. This work builds upon and contributes to the ongoing research in action-conditioned video generation and its application in robotic policy evaluation, aiming to overcome limitations related to action abstraction and spatial precision.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an embodied world model, named iMaC, capable of reliably translating robot actions into future visual observations, specifically for the purpose of robot policy evaluation and closed-loop rollout.

The motivation for this work stems from several key challenges and limitations observed in the field of robot learning:

1. **Demand for Reliable Policy Evaluation:** Real-world evaluation of robot policies is inherently resource-intensive, time-consuming, difficult to reproduce consistently across policy checkpoints, and carries safety risks, particularly for handling rare failure cases. A learned real-world simulator offers a scalable solution by allowing policies to be rolled out in generated observations, facilitating policy comparison, failure analysis, and iterative improvement without extensive hardware trials or the need for task-specific physical simulators.

2. **Addressing Action-Conditioning Limitations:** A crucial requirement for a useful learned real-world simulator is its responsiveness to robot actions. The model must accurately predict how different future actions impact contact, object motion, and task outcomes, not merely generate realistic videos. Existing action-conditioned video models often represent actions abstractly (e.g., compact vectors), which forces the model to indirectly infer precise spatial consequences. This indirect inference is particularly problematic in manipulation, where small changes in action can significantly alter physical interactions.

3. **Enhancing Spatial Precision in Action Representation:** Previous attempts at more explicit action representations, such as projected maps, have been limited to visualizing actions rather than directly controlling the future robot body state in generated frames or explicitly encoding the geometric interactions between the future robot and the observed scene. The motivation is to provide the world model with direct, spatially explicit guidance about the robot's future configuration and its interaction with the environment.

4. **Supporting Long-Horizon Closed-Loop Rollouts:** For policy evaluation, the world model must sustain long rollouts where its own predicted observations become the input for subsequent action choices. Small visual or geometric errors can accumulate over time in such closed-loop scenarios (exposure bias), leading to unreliable long-term predictions. The research aims to develop mechanisms to reduce this accumulation of errors, enabling minute-level generation.

In summary, the core motivation is to move beyond abstract action conditioning by providing detailed, image-like controls that directly describe future robot motion and contact-relevant geometry. This aims to create a more reliable and spatially precise embodied world model, thereby enabling robust, long-horizon policy evaluation that correlates strongly with real-world performance, ultimately reducing the need for costly and time-consuming real-robot experiments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

iMaC is designed as an embodied world model for policy evaluation, formulated as predicting a future observation chunk $\hat{o}_{t+1:t+H}$ given a current observation $o_t$, a policy-proposed future action sequence $a_{t:t+H-1}$, and an optional language instruction $l$. The model's objective is to enable reliable closed-loop rollouts for comparing policy checkpoints.

The approach integrates a multi-view image-to-video (IT2V) diffusion transformer backbone with novel action-derived control videos and a training-time rollout strategy.

#### 3.1. IT2V World-Model Backbone

- **Foundation:** iMaC builds upon a WAN2.2 image-to-video DiT (Diffusion Transformer) architecture.
- **Observation Representation:** $o_t$ comprises one fixed head-camera view and two wrist-camera views, mosaiced into a single image. For depth prediction, a six-panel mosaic combines RGB and colorized depth views.
- **Chunk-wise Generation:** Rollouts are generated in chunks. The first chunk uses a given initial RGB-D image as a reference. Subsequent chunks use the last generated frame from the previous chunk as the next reference.
- **Diffusion Process:** The VAE encoder $E$ encodes the reference latent $z_r$ and clean future-video latent $x_1$. Noise is applied only to the future latent, generating $x_\tau$. The DiT predicts the flow for future tokens, with an objective to predict $(x_1 - x_0)$.
- **Control Injection:** iMaC constructs three action-derived control videos: Motion Images ($C_m$), Scene-to-Gripper Contact Images ($C_{s \to g}$), and Robot-to-Scene Contact Images ($C_{r \to s}$). These controls are VAE-encoded, patchified by control-specific layers, and then added to the noised future tokens in the latent space, while reference tokens remain clean. An auxiliary depth prediction branch is incorporated to provide geometric state for constructing pointcloud controls in subsequent chunks.

#### 3.2. Motion Images from Robot Kinematics

- **Purpose:** To provide direct, pixel-level guidance for the future robot appearance, reducing the model's burden of inferring robot motion from abstract actions.
- **Mechanism:** Given a future joint action sequence $a_{t:t+H-1}$, the robot's URDF and forward kinematics ($K_{URDF}$) are used to determine future robot configurations ($M_{t+k}$).
- **Rendering:** These posed robot models ($M_{t+k}$) are rendered ($R$) from the same camera views (fixed head camera, two wrist cameras with poses from FK) used by the world model.
- **Output:** A control video $C_m$, which is a mosaic of the three-view rendered robot observations. This process leverages the inherent knowledge of the robot's kinematic model, which is a natural requirement for evaluating policies on a specified robot platform.

#### 3.3. Contact Images from Robot-Scene Geometry

- **Purpose:** To encode contact-relevant spatial relations between the future robot's motion and the observed scene, beyond just robot appearance.
- **RGB-D Prediction:** iMaC predicts depth alongside RGB to enhance spatial understanding. Initial reference depth is estimated using Depth Anything 3 (DA3) from multi-view RGB and camera poses. For subsequent chunks, depth is predicted by the world model itself, making the generated final frame a combined RGB-D reference for the next chunk. Depth maps are colorized to be compatible with the IT2V backbone, and controls are duplicated vertically to align with both RGB and depth rows in the six-panel mosaic.
- **Two-stream Geometry Controls:**
 1. **Scene Pointcloud Construction:** The robot is removed from the current depth map using its rendered mask, and the remaining pixels from all views are lifted into a scene pointcloud ($P_s^t$).
 2. **Robot Pointclouds:** Future full-robot pointclouds ($P_r^{t+k}$) and gripper pointclouds ($P_g^{t+k}$) are obtained from the URDF/FK-predicted robot configurations.
 3. **Robot-to-Scene Stream ($C_{r \to s}$):** For each point in the future robot pointcloud ($r \in P_r^{t+k}$), its nearest distance to the current scene pointcloud ($P_s^t$) is calculated. These distances are then projected into the future robot's render mask and densified, producing robot-centric contact images.
 4. **Scene-to-Gripper Stream ($C_{s \to g}$):** For each point in the current scene pointcloud ($p \in P_s^t$), its nearest distance to the future gripper pointcloud ($P_g^{t+k}$) is calculated. These distances are projected back to the current scene pixels and densified within the scene mask, producing scene-centric contact images.
 5. **Output:** Both distance videos are colorized with a heatmap after sequence-level normalization, providing the model with explicit cues about where the robot approaches the scene and which scene regions are close to the gripper.

#### 3.4. Training-time Rollout for Long Video Generation

- **Motivation:** To mitigate exposure bias, which arises when the model is trained on ground-truth references but tested on its own generated (and thus imperfect) observations during closed-loop inference.
- **Strategy:** During training, a sequence is split into multiple chunks. For the first chunk, the ground-truth initial RGB-D observation is used as a reference. For subsequent chunks, the model's own *one-step flow clean estimate* of the final frame from the previous chunk (decoded from predicted RGB-D latents and detached) becomes the next reference.
- **Control Recomputation:** The action-derived controls ($C_m$, $C_{s \to g}$, $C_{r \to s}$) for each chunk are recomputed based on this *generated* RGB-D reference. This ensures that later chunks are trained under generated contexts while still benefiting from paired supervision from the recorded video-action sequence via standard flow-matching losses for RGB and depth.
- **Warm-up:** Training-time rollout is warmed up; for the initial 40 epochs, chunks use clean ground-truth reference observations before enabling generated references.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details

iMaC training proceeds in two stages:
1. **Stage 1 (General Model):** A shared model is trained on data from all eight tasks, initially using only motion-image controls.
2. **Stage 2 (Task-Specific Finetuning):** The model is finetuned on each individual task, and contact-image controls are introduced at this stage after the model has learned to produce clearer RGB-D predictions. The final evaluation uses one world model per task.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across future-video prediction quality, closed-loop policy evaluation, and ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Future-Video Prediction Quality

Quantitative comparison of future-video prediction quality, presented in Table 1, demonstrates that iMaC achieves competitive performance against baselines (Ctrl-World, ABot-PhysWorld) across standard metrics.
- iMaC obtained the best task-averaged Fréchet Inception Distance (FID), Peak Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM), and Frechet Video Distance (FVD).
- It matched the best Mean Squared Error (MSE) within rounding, indicating high visual fidelity.
- Specifically, the full iMaC model showed improved FID and FVD compared to `iMaC w/o contact images`, suggesting that the contact images contribute to better action-conditioned prediction quality by providing dense robot-scene distance cues.

#### 4.2. Closed-Loop Policy Evaluation

Figure 2 illustrates iMaC's performance as a closed-loop policy evaluator.
- For six out of the eight challenging long-horizon real-robot manipulation tasks, the world-model evaluation scores exhibited strong positive correlation with real-world policy performance. The per-task correlations ranged from 0.833 to 0.956. This indicates that iMaC can reliably preserve the relative ranking of different policy families (π0.5 and GigaBrain-0.5) and their respective training checkpoints. This capability is critical for model-based checkpoint selection before extensive hardware evaluations.
- Two tasks, Task 3 (r = 0.678) and Task 5 (r = 0.428), showed lower correlation. The paper attributes this to missing task-relevant observations from the available camera views. For example, in Task 3 ("stack the box ears"), the model struggles to infer whether a box ear has been lifted sufficiently to clear a side wall before insertion. In Task 5 ("sweep the trash into the dustpan"), it struggles to ascertain whether the dustpan entrance is flush with the tabletop or raised, which affects trash collection. These cases highlight limitations related to camera coverage rather than the world model's internal prediction capability per se.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

Figure 3 and corresponding analysis provide insights into the contributions of iMaC's components:
- **Without Contact Images:** The model generated an interaction-like cloth motion, but the gripper failed to grasp the cloth precisely. This suggests that without the contact-aware guidance, the world model lacks the fine-grained information needed for successful physical interaction.
- **Without Motion Images:** The gripper repeatedly attempted the desired motion but could not achieve a precise grasp. This indicates that direct guidance about the future robot configuration via motion images is crucial for accurate robot body state prediction.
- **Depth Source for Contact Images (DA3 Depth vs. iMaC RGB-D World Model):** When using DA3-estimated depth for contact image construction, action following was partially improved due to the presence of controls. However, the contact geometry was less consistent compared to using iMaC's internally predicted RGB-D state, resulting in the gripper still missing the cloth corner in the example. This implies that the world model's self-generated and more consistent RGB-D state, coupled with training-time rollout, leads to more accurate contact geometry for control images.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The iMaC framework introduces a method for action-conditioned video generation that has several implications for robot learning and policy evaluation:

1. **Spatially Explicit Action Conditioning:** The primary significance of iMaC lies in its novel approach to translating abstract robot actions into dense, image-like controls—motion images and contact images. This provides the world model with direct, spatially explicit guidance regarding the future robot state and its geometric interaction with the scene. This contrasts with previous methods that relied on compact action vectors or sparse visualizations, which required the model to infer fine-grained spatial consequences indirectly. This explicit conditioning can lead to more accurate and reliable predictions of contact-rich manipulation.

2. **Enhanced Policy Evaluation Capabilities:** iMaC enables a more robust form of "in-silico" policy evaluation. By demonstrating a strong positive correlation between world-model-based success estimates and real-world policy performance across a range of challenging tasks, iMaC offers a scalable, reproducible, and potentially safer alternative to traditional real-world hardware trials. This capability can significantly reduce the cost, time, and safety risks associated with evaluating and iterating on robot policies, allowing researchers to quickly rank different policy checkpoints and identify promising candidates.

3. **Improved Long-Horizon Prediction:** The integration of training-time rollout strategies helps mitigate exposure bias, a common challenge in autoregressive generation where errors accumulate over long sequences. By training the model on its own generated contexts, iMaC enhances its ability to sustain long closed-loop rollouts (minute-level generation), which is essential for evaluating complex, multi-step manipulation tasks.

4. **Integration of Multi-Modal Information:** The framework effectively combines robot kinematics (URDF/FK) for motion images, auxiliary depth prediction for geometric understanding, and pointcloud-based two-stream distance fields for contact images. This multi-modal integration provides the world model with a comprehensive understanding of both robot appearance and the intricate geometric relationships driving scene dynamics.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated Robot Development:** By providing a reliable virtual testbed, iMaC can significantly accelerate the development and iteration cycles for robot policies, reducing reliance on expensive and time-consuming physical robot experiments.
- **Safer Policy Testing:** It allows for the testing of policies in scenarios that might be dangerous or cause wear-and-tear on real hardware, enabling exploration of rare failure cases in a safe, simulated environment.
- **Foundational for Embodied AI:** The advancements in spatially explicit action conditioning could be foundational for future embodied AI systems, contributing to more precise and robust interaction with complex physical environments.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges a limitation regarding the reliance on Depth Anything 3 (DA3) for initial depth supervision, which can introduce centimeter-level errors in manipulation scenes. While the heatmap-based contact images are designed to be robust to coarse trends, higher-quality depth sensors or manipulation-adapted depth models could further enhance contact timing, collision localization, and overall long-horizon rollout reliability. This suggests a clear avenue for future research to improve the geometric input to the system.

Overall, iMaC represents a significant step towards developing more reliable and practical embodied world models for robotics, offering a valuable tool to complement, rather than eliminate, real-world evaluation by optimizing the policy development process.
