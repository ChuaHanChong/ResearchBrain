# A Practical Recipe Towards Improving Sim-and-Real Correlation for VLA Evaluation

> [!summary] Summary
> This research investigates how well simulated environments predict real-world performance for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) robot policies, providing a framework to quantify and enhance this correlation. The study shows that targeted fine-tuning with an optimal data amount can significantly improve the alignment between simulated and real-world policy rankings and perturbation sensitivities, with one simulator achieving a policy-ranking Spearman correlation of 0.875 after such adaptation.

## Problem

- Real-world evaluation of generalist robot policies is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and difficult to scale across diverse tasks and embodiments.
- Despite advancements in simulation realism, it is unclear under what conditions simulators reliably act as a measurement proxy for real-world policy behavior.
- Existing simulation efforts often lack systematic validation of their predictive capabilities regarding policy performance and failure patterns in the physical world.

## Method

- A unified ==evaluation platform== was constructed, featuring nine tabletop manipulation tasks with corresponding simulated and real-world settings on the DROID embodiment.
- Controlled perturbations (vision, language, layout, behavior) were systematically introduced to assess policy robustness in both simulated and real environments.
- Three VLA simulation benchmarks (VLA-Arena, SIMPLER, REALM) and five VLA policies were compared, all fine-tuned with identical teleoperated data for a fair comparison of simulator fidelity.

## Results

- REALM consistently exhibited the highest average policy-ranking correlation (Spearman 
ho = **0.700**, Pearson r = **0.785**) and the lowest Mean Maximum Rank Violation (MMRV = **0.030**) among evaluated simulators.
- REALM was the only simulator that preserved the real-world hierarchy of perturbation severity, where behavior perturbations were most damaging, followed by layout, vision, and language.
- Targeted simulator-based post-training in REALM improved policy-ranking Spearman correlation from **0.700** to **0.875** and reduced Sensitivity MAE from **0.110** to **0.041**, enhancing sim-and-real alignment.
- An intermediate amount of simulator fine-tuning data (e.g., 10 demos per task) achieved optimal overall sim-and-real alignment, while more extensive fine-tuning (20 demos) decreased perturbation-sensitivity alignment.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reliable simulators must induce policy failure patterns and perturbation severities that align with real-world observations, not just visual or physical realism.
> - The inherent fidelity of the simulator's core mechanisms (e.g., physics engine, rendering) is a more critical determinant of sim-and-real correlation than minor discrepancies in object assets.
> - Simulator-based post-training, specifically lightweight fine-tuning, is a practical strategy to significantly improve sim-and-real correlation by closing the distribution gap between policies and the simulator.
> - There is an optimal, non-monotonic relationship between the amount of simulator fine-tuning data and proxy reliability; excessive fine-tuning can lead to overfitting and degrade generalization under distribution shifts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Embodied artificial intelligence, particularly in the domain of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies, has experienced substantial advancements, leading to the development of generalist robot policies capable of performing diverse tasks across various scenes, objects, and embodiments. This progress has, however, created a bottleneck in evaluation. Traditional real-world evaluation of robot policies is resource-intensive, slow, and challenging to scale due to the requirement for numerous rollouts across varied task configurations, scene appearances, and object variations.

To mitigate these evaluation challenges, significant effort has been directed towards developing simulation benchmarks. These benchmarks aim to offer scalable, reproducible, and controllable environments that mimic real-world scenarios, thereby providing platforms for assessing robot policy capabilities and limitations. Recent simulation efforts have focused on enhancing task diversity, visual and physical realism, and incorporating structured perturbations. Some approaches have also employed real-to-sim techniques to reduce the fidelity gap between simulated and real-world environments, with the objective of improving the correlation between simulated and real-world evaluation outcomes.

Despite these advancements, a fundamental question persists: under what conditions does simulation reliably act as a measurement proxy for real-world policy behavior? A simulator's visual realism, task diversity, or challenge level does not inherently guarantee that policy-level conclusions drawn from simulation will align with those in the physical world. Prior work has acknowledged that simulated performance does not automatically predict real-world performance and that simulator utility requires validation through paired sim-and-real measurements rather than solely relying on visual realism. Studies such as SIMPLER and REALM have begun to formalize this by focusing on whether simulators can preserve relative policy performance and robustness patterns under controlled visual and control discrepancies.

This paper situates itself within this landscape by conducting a systematic investigation into the sim-and-real correlation of existing simulation platforms. Unlike many prior works that focus on developing new simulators or validating a single simulator's predictive capabilities, this research comparatively evaluates multiple VLA policies across different simulation platforms. The objective is to understand which types of simulation signals are more aligned with real-world deployment outcomes, specifically focusing on policy ranking consistency, performance correlation, and perturbation-wise failure patterns. The work also extends to analyzing how simulation can be leveraged by users for policy improvement, including the benefits and limitations of simulator-based fine-tuning. By doing so, the paper contributes to a unified framework for measuring, interpreting, and enhancing the practical utility of simulation in the VLA policy development pipeline.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate and improve the sim-and-real correlation for VLA policy evaluation. This overarching goal is driven by the observation that, despite substantial progress in simulation benchmark realism and diversity, these platforms are not consistently adopted as reliable proxies for real-world robot policy evaluation. The practical value of simulation for policy developers hinges on its ability to preserve decision-relevant information, such as which policy performs better or which perturbations reveal greater model sensitivity.

To address this, the authors delineate two primary research questions:

1. **For simulator designers**: What specific properties should a simulator possess to reliably preserve real-world policy-level conclusions? This question motivates an analysis of existing simulators to identify characteristics that contribute to or detract from their fidelity as real-world proxies. The paper aims to move beyond generic notions of "realism" to pinpoint attributes that ensure simulated evaluation accurately reflects real-world policy rankings and vulnerability patterns.
2. **For simulator users**: How should an available simulator be utilized or adapted to further enhance its sim-and-real correlation? This question investigates practical strategies for practitioners, such as the effectiveness of simulator-based fine-tuning and the influence of post-training data quantity on alignment between simulated and real-world behavior. The motivation here is to provide actionable guidance for integrating simulation effectively into the policy development and evaluation pipeline.

The motivation for this work stems from the increasing complexity and generality of modern robot policies, which makes real-world evaluation prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and difficult to standardize. Reliable simulation offers a potential solution to this bottleneck, provided its results are genuinely predictive of real-world performance. By systematically studying sim-and-real correlation, the authors aim to:
- Characterize the limitations of current simulators in reflecting real-world policy behavior.
- Identify which types of simulation signals are more aligned with real-world deployment.
- Offer guidance for simulator designers on creating more trustworthy evaluation environments.
- Provide a practical "recipe" for VLA practitioners on how to leverage simulation effectively for policy improvement and evaluation.

Ultimately, the research seeks to advance the utility of simulation as a foundational tool for developing and testing generalist robot policies, making the evaluation process more scalable, reproducible, and reflective of actual robotic capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employs a systematic methodology to investigate sim-and-real correlation across various dimensions.

**Evaluation Platform Construction:**
A unified evaluation platform was developed, encompassing nine tabletop manipulation tasks. These tasks were selected to cover a spectrum of action spaces and interaction patterns, including pick-and-place, object rotation, articulated object manipulation, and fine-grained manipulation. A key aspect of this platform design was to ensure task-level correspondence between simulated and real-world settings. Each simulated task was aligned with the same underlying manipulation skill and success criteria in the real world, aiming to attribute evaluation differences to simulator fidelity rather than task definition discrepancies. The real-world experiments utilized the DROID embodiment for hardware instantiation.

**Perturbation Dimensions:**
Controlled perturbations were introduced along four dimensions to assess policy robustness:
- **Vision perturbations:** Modifications to scene visual appearance, such as blur, contrast, and lighting conditions.
- **Language perturbations:** Variations in the form of task instructions.
- **Layout perturbations:** Alterations to spatial configurations of objects, including the manipulated object (an improvement over original REALM layout perturbations).
- **Behavior perturbations:** Evaluation of generalization to unseen manipulated objects. For simulators not natively supporting specific perturbations, comparable protocols were implemented to maintain fairness, with the exception of unseen-object dimensions where simulator defaults were followed.

**Simulation Platforms and VLA Policies:**
The study compared three representative simulated VLA benchmarks:
- **VLA-Arena:** Utilizing the Mujoco physics engine.
- **SIMPLER:** Utilizing the Sapien physics engine.
- **REALM:** Utilizing the Isaac-Sim physics engine.
Five recent VLA policies were evaluated: $\pi^0$, $\pi^0$-FAST, $\pi^{0.5}$, GR00T N1.6, and GR00T N1.7.

**Experimental Protocol:**
- **Rollouts:** Evaluation involved 20 simulated rollouts and 5 real-world rollouts for each policy, task, and perturbation dimension. This amounted to a total of 11,800 simulated rollouts and 1,115 real-world rollouts.
- **Fair Comparison:** To ensure an equitable comparison across benchmarks, task-specific data was collected via teleoperation (using a Meta Oculus Quest 2) for newly constructed simulation tasks. All evaluated policies were then fine-tuned using this identical collected data. This controlled protocol aimed to ensure that observed differences were attributable to the simulators themselves rather than disparate access to adaptation data. For GR00T models, the DROID embodiment configuration was aligned with the teleoperation data format (joint positions and gripper states).

**Evaluation Metrics for Sim-and-Real Correlation:**
The usefulness of simulation was measured using a set of complementary metrics:

- **Policy-ranking proxy metrics:** Applied to compare simulated and real-world policy performance vectors ($s^e_d$ vs. $s^{real}_d$).
 - **Spearman rank correlation ($\rho$):** Measures the extent to which simulation preserves the relative ordering of policies observed in the real world. A higher value indicates stronger ranking consistency.
 - **Pearson correlation ($r$):** Measures the linear relationship between simulated and real-world scores, indicating if simulated score differences track real-world score differences.
 - **Mean Maximum Rank Violation (MMRV):** Quantifies the severity of high-margin ranking mistakes. It penalizes rank inversions based on the absolute performance difference between mis-ranked policies in the real world. A lower MMRV indicates fewer and less severe ranking errors.

- **Perturbation-sensitivity metrics:** Used to assess whether simulators induce realistic vulnerability patterns in policies.
 - **Normalized perturbation sensitivity ($I_{e,m,d}$):** Calculates the relative performance degradation caused by a specific perturbation for a given policy within an environment. Values range from 0 (least damaging perturbation) to 1 (most damaging).

- **Perturbation-sensitivity alignment metrics:** Applied to compare flattened vectors of simulated and real-world sensitivity values ($i_e$ vs. $i_{real}$).
 - **Sensitivity Spearman correlation ($\rho_{sens}$):** Measures if the relative ordering of perturbation severity is preserved between simulation and real world.
 - **Sensitivity Pearson correlation ($r_{sens}$):** Measures the linear alignment of normalized sensitivity values, indicating if the magnitude of vulnerability across perturbations is matched.
 - **Sensitivity MAE (Mean Absolute Error):** Quantifies the absolute discrepancy between simulated and real-world sensitivity values. A lower MAE indicates more accurate reproduction of real-world perturbation severity.

**Ablation Study (Object-level Task Mismatch):**
An ablation study was conducted in REALM for a task ("pick the banana into the box") exhibiting low cross-simulator alignment. This involved replacing the "banana" with five different manipulated objects (corn, zucchini, hotdog, carrot, cucumber) to determine whether differences in proxy ability were due to object-level task discrepancies or simulator-level fidelity.

**Post-training Study:**
Based on initial findings, REALM was selected for a post-training study. This involved:
- **Targeted Fine-tuning:** Collecting a small amount of simulator data from the target evaluation distribution for each task and fine-tuning policies for a limited number of steps.
- **Data Amount Variation:** Investigating how varying amounts of simulator-based fine-tuning data (zero-shot, 5 demos/task, 10 demos/task, 20 demos/task) affected sim-and-real correlation metrics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several observations regarding the effectiveness of simulation as a proxy for real-world VLA policy evaluation:

**Observation 1: Reliable simulators should make policies fail like the real world.**
- **Policy Ranking and Correlation:** Across the three evaluated simulators (VLA-Arena, SIMPLER, REALM) and five VLA policies, REALM consistently exhibited the highest average policy-ranking correlation with the real world (average Spearman $\rho = 0.700$, Pearson $r = 0.785$) and the lowest Mean Maximum Rank Violation (MMRV = 0.030). This indicates REALM's comparatively stronger ability to preserve real-world policy rankings and avoid significant ranking errors. VLA-Arena performed well on language and layout perturbations but showed zero Spearman correlation on vision. SIMPLER generally showed lower correlations.
- **Perturbation-Sensitivity Alignment:** A positive association was identified between a simulator's ability to predict policy rankings and its alignment with real-world perturbation-induced failures. Simulators that better preserved real-world policy rankings also tended to produce perturbation vulnerabilities that were more consistent with real-world observations (Figure 2).
- **Perturbation Hierarchy:** REALM was the only simulator among those evaluated that preserved the real-world hierarchy of perturbation severity. In the real world, behavior perturbations were the most damaging, followed by layout, while vision and language perturbations were less severe. REALM accurately reflected this pattern, whereas VLA-Arena over-emphasized layout perturbations relative to behavior, and SIMPLER lacked behavior perturbations entirely (Table 3). This suggests that a simulator's perturbations should be designed to induce failure patterns and relative severities that align with real-world deployment conditions.

**Observation 2: Proxy gaps are dominated by simulator-level fidelity rather than object-level task mismatch.**
- An ablation study conducted in REALM, replacing the manipulated object in a "pick the banana into the box" task with different objects (corn, zucchini, hotdog, carrot, cucumber), demonstrated that while absolute success rates varied, the relative policy ordering remained consistent across object variants. This indicates that variations in specific manipulated objects within a simulator environment primarily affect overall task performance but do not significantly alter the comparative ranking of policies. The implication is that the fundamental fidelity of the simulator (e.g., its rendering, physics engine, overall environment consistency) is a more critical determinant of sim-and-real correlation than minor discrepancies in object assets or geometries.

**Observation 3: Simulator-based post-training significantly improves sim-and-real correlation.**
- Targeted post-training, involving fine-tuning policies on a small amount of simulator-specific data within REALM, consistently enhanced sim-and-real alignment. Policy-ranking Spearman correlation improved from 0.700 to 0.875, and Proxy MMRV decreased from 0.030 to 0.015 (Table 4).
- Perturbation-sensitivity alignment also improved, with Sensitivity Spearman correlation rising from 0.924 to 0.955, Pearson correlation from 0.875 to 0.970, and Sensitivity MAE substantially decreasing from 0.110 to 0.041. These improvements indicate that fine-tuning reduces the distribution gap between pretrained policies and the simulator environment, making the simulated evaluation more predictive of real-world behavior, even if it does not always lead to an increase in absolute task performance.

**Observation 4: More simulator fine-tuning data does not monotonically improve proxy reliability.**
- Investigation into the effect of varying fine-tuning data amounts revealed a non-monotonic relationship with sim-and-real correlation (Figure 4). While a small amount of fine-tuning (e.g., "Tune-5" demos per task) improved proxy metrics compared to zero-shot evaluation, "Tune-10" achieved the optimal overall alignment, demonstrating the highest policy-ranking and perturbation-sensitivity correlations.
- However, further increasing the fine-tuning data to "Tune-20" resulted in a notable drop in perturbation-sensitivity alignment, becoming worse than the non-tuned baseline for some metrics. This was attributed to potential overfitting to the limited complexity of the simulated environment, causing models to become less sensitive to certain perturbations (e.g., language variations) and distorting their generalization ability under distribution shifts. This suggests an optimal, intermediate range for simulator-based adaptation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of embodied artificial intelligence by providing a systematic investigation into the practical utility of simulation for evaluating Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. The findings offer implications for both simulator design and the application of simulation in policy development.

**Significance for Simulator Designers:**
The study establishes that for a simulator to be a reliable proxy for real-world VLA evaluation, it must not only accurately rank policies but also realistically reflect how policies fail under various perturbations. This "perturbation realism" goes beyond visual or physical plausibility, emphasizing that the severity of simulated perturbations should align with their real-world impact on policy performance. This suggests that future simulator design should prioritize creating perturbation dimensions that induce vulnerability patterns consistent with real-world deployment, thereby serving as more effective diagnostic tools for understanding policy failure modes. The finding that simulator-level fidelity, rather than minor object-level task mismatches, primarily governs sim-and-real correlation further underscores the importance of robust fundamental simulator properties.

**Significance for Practitioners and Simulator Users:**
The research provides practical, user-oriented guidance for integrating simulation into the VLA policy development pipeline. It demonstrates that simulator-based post-training, specifically lightweight fine-tuning, can significantly improve the correlation between simulated and real-world evaluations. This adaptation acts as a calibration step, reducing the distribution gap between broadly pretrained policies and the specific simulator environment. However, the study also identifies a critical caveat: the benefit of fine-tuning is non-monotonic with data scale. Excessive fine-tuning can lead to overfitting to simulator-specific artifacts, potentially degrading the model's generalization ability under distribution shifts and distorting the proxy signal. Therefore, practitioners are advised to seek an intermediate adaptation regime to achieve the most reliable proxy signals, rather than maximizing fine-tuning data or steps.

**Broader Impact on VLA Evaluation:**
This work contributes to a more rigorous and measurement-oriented approach to VLA policy evaluation. By defining a unified framework for quantifying sim-and-real correlation through metrics like Spearman and Pearson correlation, Mean Maximum Rank Violation, and perturbation-sensitivity alignment, the research provides a standardized way to assess the practical value of simulators. This framework moves beyond subjective assessments of realism towards quantifiable metrics of decision-relevant fidelity.

Overall, the paper offers a practical recipe for improving sim-and-real correlation, which is crucial for scalable and reproducible evaluation of generalist robot policies. By understanding when and how simulation can reliably predict real-world behavior, the field can accelerate the development and deployment of more capable and robust robotic systems, making simulation a more dependable and valuable tool in embodied AI research. While the study's empirical analysis is within a bounded scope (e.g., tabletop manipulation, fixed embodiment), its methodological insights are intended to be broadly applicable, fostering future larger-scale and more comprehensive evaluations.
