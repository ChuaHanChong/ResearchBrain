# Robots Need More than VLA and World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> A position paper argues that achieving generalist robot intelligence necessitates overcoming the "grounding bottleneck," which involves converting the world's abundant unstructured physical data into robot-usable supervision. It proposes four architectural pillars—Physical Data Engines, Task-preserving Retargeting, Physics-Grounded World Models, and Self-Improving Deployment Loops—as essential components for a future integrated robot learning system.

## Problem

- The dominant paradigm in robotics views generalist intelligence as primarily a "policy-scaling problem," relying on large Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and extensive robot-native datasets.
- A fundamental asymmetry exists between the vast amount of unstructured physical data (e.g., human motion, internet videos) and its usability for robots due to a lack of embodiment-specific action labels, task semantics, and reward structures.
- Current robot learning largely depends on expensive and slow 'robot-native' supervision, which is explicitly collected and curated for specific robot embodiments, limiting scalability.

## Method

- Advocates for a shift from a "robot-data-centric" to a "==grounding-centric==" pipeline, where VLAs function as one layer within a broader "physical-intelligence stack."
- Proposes four architectural components: Physical Data Engines, Task-preserving Retargeting, Physics-Grounded World Models, and Self-Improving Deployment Loops, to enable robots to learn from diverse physical experiences.
- Envisions the future of generalist robotics as a compounding system, integrating these four pillars to continuously transform unstructured physical experience into usable, grounded robot supervision.

## Results

- Identified four conceptual architectural pillars essential for future robot learning: Physical Data Engines and Embodied Autolabelling, Task-preserving Retargeting across Embodiments, Physics-Grounded World Models for Consequence Predictions, and Self-Improving Deployment Loops.
- Outlined a research agenda that focuses on fundamental questions concerning grounding, consequence prediction, and continuous learning from diverse physical experience, suggesting new evaluation metrics.
- Redefined the concept of the "next foundation model for robotics" as an integrated *compounding system* of these pillars, designed for continuous, autonomous learning from the physical world.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The core bottleneck for generalist robot intelligence is the absence of mechanisms to convert the world's unstructured behavioral data into grounded, robot-usable supervision, rather than solely a policy-scaling challenge.
> - Existing large-scale robot learning efforts, while showing progress, rely on costly "robot-native" supervision, and methods using weakly grounded observations do not fully resolve the embodiment-specific "correspondence problem."
> - Generated physical experience (e.g., from world models) is only useful for robot control if it accurately preserves and predicts *physical consequences* (like contact and stability), not merely visual plausibility.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This position paper addresses a foundational debate within the rapidly evolving field of robotics, particularly concerning the path towards generalist robot intelligence. The current research landscape in robotics has been significantly influenced by advancements in artificial intelligence, mirroring the successes of large language models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) in other domains. This has led to a dominant paradigm in robotics where generalist intelligence is often conceptualized as a "policy-scaling problem." This paradigm suggests that by accumulating more robot demonstrations, training larger Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, and expanding datasets across various tasks and embodiments, robots will naturally acquire broader generalization capabilities. Efforts such as RT-1, RT-2, Open X-Embodiment, Octo, and RoboCat exemplify this trend, demonstrating improved robot behavior through increased data and model scale.

However, the authors argue that this prevailing framing is incomplete. They highlight a fundamental asymmetry between data availability and usability in physical interaction compared to text and image data. While text and images are naturally digitized and often come with dense human-generated supervision, the vast amount of physical behavioral data (e.g., human motion, internet videos, simulation rollouts) lacks the embodiment-specific action labels, precise task semantics, and explicit reward structures that robot policies require. Current robot learning pipelines, even when scaled, predominantly rely on "robot-native" supervision—data that has already been explicitly collected, manually labeled, or curated for a specific robot's embodiment and task. This dependency makes progress expensive, slow, and limits scalability to the immense volume of physical experience available in the world.

The paper positions itself as a critical analysis of these limitations, advocating for a shift from a "robot-data-centric" to a "grounding-centric" pipeline. It acknowledges the importance of VLAs and large datasets but redefines their role as one layer within a larger "physical-intelligence stack," arguing that VLAs depend on upstream mechanisms for grounding data, embodiment, dynamics, rewards, and deployment feedback. The work thus aims to survey existing progress while simultaneously identifying the missing architectural components necessary to enable robots to learn from the broader, unstructured physical world. It serves as a call to action for the robotics community to focus on the underlying mechanisms that transform diverse physical experience into usable, grounded supervision for robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this position paper is to argue that the current understanding and pursuit of generalist robot intelligence, predominantly framed as a policy-scaling problem, is insufficient and incomplete. The authors articulate a detailed motivation for this stance, underpinned by several key objectives:

- **Identify the Core Bottleneck**: The primary motivation is to pinpoint the fundamental limitation preventing robots from achieving broad general intelligence. The paper posits that this bottleneck is not merely about the size of policies or datasets, but rather the "absence of mechanisms that convert the world’s abundant unstructured behavioural data into grounded robot supervision." This includes human motion, internet videos, simulation rollouts, and interactive demonstrations, which contain rich information but lack the embodiment-specific action labels, task semantics, and reward structures necessary for direct robot policy learning.
- **Critique Existing Robot Learning Paradigms**: The authors are motivated to critically examine the strengths and, more importantly, the scaling limits of current robot learning approaches. They acknowledge significant progress enabled by large-scale robot datasets and generalist VLA models (e.g., RT-2, Open X-Embodiment). However, they emphasize that these successes still predominantly rely on "robot-native supervision"—data where actions, observations, task descriptions, and success signals are already made legible to a robot learning algorithm. The paper highlights the expense and difficulty in scaling such curated data, arguing that the amount of usable robot supervision remains minuscule compared to the total physical behavior in the world.
- **Propose a Shift in Research Focus**: A key objective is to motivate a shift in the field's central question. Instead of asking "which model architecture to train," the authors propose asking "which mechanisms are needed to make the world’s physical experience learnable by robots." This aims to redirect research toward the foundational challenge of "grounding"—how to transform diverse physical experiences into actionable robot-usable quantities like actions, contacts, object states, task phases, goals, and rewards.
- **Outline Four Missing Pillars for Physical Intelligence**: Building on their critique, a major objective is to articulate a concrete research agenda by identifying four specific "missing components" or "pillars" essential for the next generation of robotics. These are:
 1. **Physical Data Engines and Embodied Autolabelling**: To process heterogeneous experiences into structured, robot-relevant signals.
 2. **Task-preserving Retargeting**: To translate human or inferred behavior into robot actions that maintain task-relevant physical effects across different embodiments.
 3. **Physics-Grounded World Models**: To predict not just plausible visual futures, but the physical consequences of actions that are critical for control.
 4. **Self-Improving Deployment Loops**: To convert real-world robot deployment outcomes (successes and failures) into structured, task-conditioned supervision for continuous learning and improvement.
- **Redefine the Role of VLAs and World Models**: The paper seeks to clarify that VLAs are not the ultimate solution but a crucial "policy interface" within a broader system. Similarly, world models, while promising for generating experience, must be "physics-grounded" to be truly useful for robot control, predicting actionable physical consequences rather than just visual plausibility.

In essence, the motivation stems from the belief that while scaling policies has shown promise, it is insufficient to address the fundamental challenge of enabling robots to truly learn from and interact with the complex, unstructured physical world. The paper aims to provide a conceptual framework and a research roadmap for building integrated robotics systems capable of achieving genuine physical intelligence through robust grounding mechanisms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This paper adopts a methodology characteristic of a *position paper*: it primarily involves a critical analysis, a comprehensive literature survey, and the synthesis of a novel conceptual framework and research agenda. It does not present new experimental results or implement novel algorithms but rather evaluates the existing state of the art and proposes a new direction.

The approach can be broken down into the following stages:

- **Problem Statement and Central Thesis Formulation**: The paper begins by stating its core argument: that the prevailing focus on policy-scaling for generalist robot intelligence is incomplete. It identifies the "grounding bottleneck" – the difficulty of converting unstructured physical experience into robot-usable supervision – as the central challenge.
- **Structured Review of Current Robot Learning Paradigms**: The authors conduct a structured survey of contemporary work in robot learning, organized not merely by algorithmic family or data source, but by how each area exposes or attempts to address the "grounding bottleneck."
 - **Robot-Native Supervision**: This section reviews efforts that rely on explicitly collected robot demonstrations, multi-robot datasets (e.g., RoboNet, BridgeData V2, DROID, RH20T), and generalist VLA policies (e.g., BC-Z, RT-1, RT-2, SayCan, PaLM-E, Open X-Embodiment, Octo, RoboCat, Dobb-E). It also covers advancements in policy-learning machinery (e.g., Diffusion Policy, ALOHA, Action Chunking with Transformers) and specialized VLA architectures (e.g., Gato, VIMA, RoboFlamingo, OpenVLA, π0, CogACT, RoboMamba, SpatialVLA, RDT-1B, 3D-VLA, humanoid-focused systems like GR00T N1, Gemini Robotics, Figure's Helix, LeVERB, WholeBodyVLA, HuMI, HEX, Skild Brain). The analysis here highlights that while these methods yield strong results, their effectiveness stems from data that is *already grounded* into robot-native quantities, thereby exposing the limits of this supervision regime.
 - **Learning from Weakly Grounded Physical Observations**: This section surveys methods that attempt to leverage abundant but less structured data like human or internet videos. It categorizes these into:
 - *Representation Learners* (e.g., R3M, VIP, MVP, VC-1) that pretrain visual features.
 - *Imitation from Observation and Cross-Embodiment Learning* (e.g., Time-Contrastive Networks, AVID, XIRL, DVD) addressing appearance and embodiment gaps.
 - *Latent-Action Approaches* (e.g., LAPA, UniVLA) that aim to discover action-like abstractions.
 - *Task-Progress Signals* (e.g., PROGRESSOR, Adapt2Reward, ReWiND, TimeRewarder, SARM) that infer rewards from passive observation. The authors analyze how these methods offer useful signals but do not fully resolve the "correspondence problem" or provide direct robot actionability without further grounding.
 - **Generating Physical Experience**: This part reviews approaches that generate synthetic experience to overcome data scarcity, including:
 - *Simulation Environments* (e.g., RLBench, Meta-World, ManiSkill, CALVIN, LIBERO) that provide structured, controllable worlds.
 - *Data Generation Engines* (e.g., MimicGen, RoboCasa, RoboGen) that synthesize large datasets from limited seeds.
 - *Real-to-Sim-to-Real methods* (e.g., RialTo, RL-GSBridge, Real-is-Sim, RoboGSim, SOUS VIDE, SINGER, GRaD-Nav) that bridge the reality gap.
 - *Learned World Models* (e.g., World Models, PlaNet, Dreamer, DayDreamer, RoboDreamer, UniSim, Genie) that learn predictive dynamics, including 3D, object-centric, and physics-grounded variants (e.g., FOCUS, PointWorld, ParticleFormer, ContactGaussian-WM, Physics-informed WM, JEPA-family models like V-JEPA 2). The analysis emphasizes that the utility of generated experience depends on its ability to preserve *physical consequences* critical for control, not just visual realism, highlighting the need for physics-grounding and uncertainty quantification.
- **Synthesis and Proposal of Missing Components**: Based on the observed limitations and opportunities in the surveyed literature, the authors synthesize their findings into a conceptual framework centered around four "missing components" for achieving physical intelligence. For each component, they provide a detailed description of its function, the challenges it addresses, and its role in a holistic robot learning system.
 - **Physical Data Engines and Embodied Autolabelling**: Proposes a system to automatically infer structured labels (object states, contacts, task phases, latent actions, rewards) from heterogeneous, asynchronous raw physical experiences using various sensing modalities.
 - **Task-preserving Retargeting across Embodiments**: Defines the challenge of translating latent physical actions into robot-specific commands that preserve the *task-relevant physical effect*, rather than merely mimicking kinematic trajectories.
 - **Physics-Grounded World Models for Consequence Predictions**: Advocates for predictive models that forecast the physical consequences of actions (geometry, contact, forces, stability) in a task-conditioned manner, enabling actionable planning and counterfactual reasoning.
 - **Self-Improving Deployment Loops**: Describes a closed-loop system where robot deployments provide task-conditioned reward grounding and feedback, allowing the system to continuously learn from successes, failures, and human corrections, leading to targeted updates across all components.
- **Architectural Vision and Research Agenda**: The paper concludes by presenting an integrated vision for the "next foundation model for robotics" as a compounding system built upon these four pillars, and outlines a corresponding research agenda focusing on questions related to grounding, consequence prediction, and continuous learning from diverse physical experience.

This methodical approach allows the authors to build a cohesive argument, leveraging extensive literature to support their critique of current paradigms and to justify their proposed architectural framework for future generalist robotics.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

As a position paper, "Robots Need More Than VLAs & World Models" does not present empirical results in the traditional sense. Instead, its "findings" are derived from a comprehensive analysis of the current state of robotics research and culminate in a conceptual framework and a set of critical insights into the future direction of the field. The main findings and results are:

- **Identification of the Generalist Robotics Bottleneck**: The primary finding is that the central bottleneck for achieving generalist robot intelligence is not solely a policy-scaling problem (i.e., collecting more robot demonstrations or training larger VLA models). Instead, it is identified as the fundamental absence of robust mechanisms to convert the world's vast, unstructured behavioral data (e.g., human motion, internet video, simulation rollouts) into *grounded, robot-usable supervision*. This unstructured data, though rich, typically lacks the embodiment-specific action labels, task semantics, and reward structures required for effective robot learning.

- **Critique of Current Robot-Native Supervision Limits**: The paper finds that while existing large-scale robot learning efforts have achieved significant progress with "robot-native" supervision (data explicitly collected with robot actions and labels), this approach is inherently expensive and difficult to scale. The amount of usable, pre-grounded robot data remains minuscule compared to the total physical experience available, hindering true generalization beyond curated laboratory settings.

- **Limitations of Weakly Grounded Physical Observations**: An analysis of methods that learn from passive, weakly labeled physical observations (e.g., human videos) reveals that while these can provide valuable visual representations, latent action codes, or task-progress signals, they do not fully solve the "correspondence problem." The inferred signals often lack precise grounding to a specific robot's morphology, action space, and dynamics, meaning a "latent action" is not directly an executable command, and human strategies may not be directly transferable.

- **Necessity of Physics-Grounded Generated Experience**: The review of simulation and learned world models demonstrates their potential for generating abundant experience. However, a key finding is that the utility of this generated experience for robot control is contingent upon it preserving *physical consequences* (e.g., geometry, contact, forces, material properties, stability) rather than merely visual plausibility. A visually realistic but physically inaccurate prediction is insufficient for reliable robot planning and control, emphasizing the need for physics-grounding and uncertainty quantification in world models.

- **Proposal of Four Missing Architectural Pillars**: The paper's most significant result is the conceptual articulation of four interdependent "missing components" or "pillars" deemed essential for unlocking true physical intelligence:
 1. **Physical Data Engines and Embodied Autolabelling**: A system to automatically transform heterogeneous, asynchronous physical experiences into structured, robot-relevant labels (object states, contacts, task phases, latent actions, goals, rewards).
 2. **Task-preserving Retargeting across Embodiments**: Mechanisms to translate inferred physical behaviors or human demonstrations into robot actions that preserve the *task-relevant physical effect* rather than just kinematic similarities, bridging the "embodiment gap."
 3. **Physics-Grounded World Models for Consequence Predictions**: Predictive models that estimate the actionable physical outcomes of robot actions, allowing for robust planning, evaluation of alternatives, and understanding of success/failure modes. These models should prioritize physical variables over mere pixel prediction.
 4. **Self-Improving Deployment Loops**: A closed-loop system where robot deployments generate task-conditioned progress, success, and failure signals. This "reward grounding" enables continuous learning, allowing the system to diagnose errors (e.g., policy, world model, retargeting) and update relevant components with new, structured supervision.

- **Redefinition of the "Foundation Model" for Robotics**: The overarching finding is that the "next foundation model for robotics" will not be a singular, monolithic VLA or world model, but rather a *compounding system* or *pipeline* that integrates these four pillars. This system would be designed to continuously transform diverse physical experience into reusable, grounded robot supervision, fostering incremental and compounding intelligence.

These findings collectively argue that the focus of robotics research must expand beyond scaling policies to fundamentally address the challenge of making the physical world itself a continuous source of structured and actionable supervision for robots.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The significance and potential impact of "Robots Need More Than VLAs & World Models" stem from its critical re-evaluation of current paradigms and its proposal of a structured research agenda.

- **Reframing the Generalist Robotics Challenge**: The paper's primary impact is its potential to reframe the core challenge of generalist robotics. By arguing that the bottleneck is "grounding" rather than solely "policy scaling," it encourages a more holistic approach to robot intelligence. This shift in perspective could guide research away from solely building larger models on increasingly curated datasets and towards developing systems that can inherently understand and interpret the physical world in robot-actionable terms.

- **Unlocking New Data Sources**: The proposed framework, particularly the concept of "Physical Data Engines and Embodied Autolabelling," has the potential to unlock vast, currently underutilized sources of data. Internet videos, human demonstrations without direct robot action labels, and diverse simulation rollouts could become viable learning resources, drastically expanding the experiential base for robots. This could reduce the reliance on expensive and time-consuming robot-native data collection.

- **Enhancing Robot Robustness and Generalization**: The emphasis on "Task-preserving Retargeting" and "Physics-Grounded World Models" directly addresses the limitations of current robots in dealing with real-world physical complexities like contact, friction, and material properties. By ensuring that learned behaviors maintain their intended physical effects and that world models predict actionable consequences, the framework aims to produce robots that are more robust, generalizable, and less prone to "hallucinations" or physical inconsistencies when deployed in new or varied environments.

- **Accelerating Development Through Continuous Learning**: The concept of "Self-Improving Deployment Loops" introduces a mechanism for continuous, autonomous learning from real-world interaction. Instead of treating deployments as mere evaluation, every success and failure becomes structured supervision. This could significantly accelerate the development and refinement of robot skills, allowing robots to adapt more quickly to new tasks and environments, reducing the need for human intervention after initial deployment.

- **Promoting Integrated System Design**: The paper advocates for an integrated, compounding system rather than isolated components. This holistic view encourages researchers to consider how perception, action, planning, world modeling, and reward mechanisms interact and mutually reinforce each other. This could lead to more cohesive and intelligent robot architectures, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration across different subfields of robotics and AI.

- **Guiding Future Research and Benchmarking**: By clearly identifying "four missing pillars" and posing specific evaluation questions (e.g., "Can it infer contacts...?", "Can its world model predict the consequences that matter...?"), the paper provides a concrete roadmap for future research and suggests new metrics for evaluating generalist robot capabilities. This could standardize evaluation around fundamental grounding abilities, complementing existing task-completion benchmarks.

- **Towards True Physical Intelligence**: Ultimately, the framework aims to move robotics closer to true "physical intelligence"—a deep understanding of the physical world and how to interact with it, rather than just pattern recognition or high-level reasoning. This could enable robots to perform complex manipulation tasks, adapt to unforeseen situations, and collaborate more effectively with humans by understanding shared physical semantics.

In summary, this paper offers a significant conceptual contribution by challenging the dominant narrative in generalist robotics and proposing a more foundational, grounding-centric approach. Its impact could be to redirect research efforts, unlock new data sources, and pave the way for a new generation of robots that are more robust, adaptable, and genuinely intelligent in their physical interactions.
