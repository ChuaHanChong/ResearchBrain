# Beyond Monotonic Progress: Retry-Supervised Value Learning for Robot Imitation

> [!summary] Summary
> ReTVL, a framework developed by researchers including those from Tsinghua University and Microsoft Research Asia, learns mistake-sensitive value functions for robot imitation by incorporating sparsely annotated "retry events" from human demonstrations. The framework integrates global progress calibration with local pairwise preference learning, which led to an 80% average success rate on real-robot manipulation tasks, surpassing baseline methods by effectively prioritizing useful corrective behaviors.

## Problem

- Human robot demonstration data often contains imperfections, such as hesitation and repeated attempts, which are referred to as "retry events."
- Traditional value and reward learning methods assume "monotonic progress," treating these imperfections as noise and failing to accurately represent local errors or corrective actions.
- This limitation leads to inaccurate value functions that can assign high values to suboptimal states, degrading the performance of downstream policy learning by causing robots to learn undesirable behaviors.

## Method

- Introduces **ReTVL** (Retry-Supervised Value Learning), a framework to learn mistake-sensitive value functions by integrating global progress calibration with local pairwise preference learning derived from retry events.
- Utilizes sparse annotations of "==retry keypoints==" to construct preference pairs, enforcing value drops as execution approaches degraded states and value rebounds as corrective behaviors restore feasibility.
- Combines an absolute progress loss, applied to segments outside retry neighborhoods, with a soft-weighted pairwise logistic loss from retry events, ensuring both global task advancement and fine-grained mistake-recovery dynamics are captured.

## Results

- ReTVL achieved strong global value estimation, with a Value-Order Correlation (VOC) of **0.987** and a perfect Success-Fail Detection (S/F Det.) of **1.000**, comparable to leading baselines.
- Demonstrated substantial improvements in local mistake-sensitive metrics, increasing Drop AUC to **0.797** and achieving a Pre > Retry score of **0.740**, significantly higher than all baselines.
- Policies trained with ReTVL-guided behavior cloning achieved an **80.00%** average success rate on real-robot tasks, outperforming RECAP-BC (**62.50%**) and Standard BC (**41.25%**), by effectively reducing the average weight of "bad actions" by **80%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Retry events, often considered noise, are valuable local supervisory signals that reveal specific value drops associated with execution errors and value rebounds associated with corrective actions.
> - Integrating both absolute global progress signals and local relative preferences derived from retry events allows for learning value functions that are sensitive to both overall task advancement and fine-grained mistake-recovery dynamics.
> - Explicit preference learning around retry keypoints, rather than just numeric shaping, is crucial for teaching a value model to identify visual cues for relative value ordering during error and recovery states.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning has recently seen significant advancements through Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which integrate visual and language understanding for general-purpose robotic policies. These models often rely heavily on human demonstration data for task acquisition. However, real-world human demonstrations are frequently imperfect, containing elements such as hesitation, imprecise manipulations, and repeated attempts, commonly referred to as "retry events."

Existing approaches for learning value or reward functions from robot data often operate under a "monotonic progress" assumption. This paradigm typically models task advancement as a monotonically increasing scalar signal throughout a trajectory, aiming to capture coarse task completion or success likelihood. Methods like TOPReward, Robometer, and RECAP-Value fall within this category, focusing on estimating global progress, predicting success/failure, or ranking trajectories. While these methods are effective at capturing overall task progression, they often treat imperfect segments—such as mistakes and subsequent corrective behaviors—as noise or suboptimal data to be ignored or discarded. This can lead to learned value functions that are overly smooth and fail to accurately reflect local deviations from a desirable path or the necessary recovery actions.

This research addresses a critical gap by challenging the pervasive monotonic progress assumption. It positions itself by arguing that retry events, rather than being mere noise, are rich sources of information about when execution errors occur and how task feasibility is restored. By explicitly leveraging these specific imperfections, the work seeks to develop value models that are more sensitive to local execution errors and the corrective actions taken, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of task dynamics beyond simple monotonic advancement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that human demonstrations, although crucial for robot imitation learning, are inherently imperfect. They often include segments where an operator makes a mistake, attempts to correct it, or repeats an action until success. Current value and reward modeling techniques, which largely assume monotonic task progress, overlook these critical "retry events." This oversight has several consequences:
- **Inaccurate Value Functions**: By treating all trajectory segments, including those leading to or recovering from mistakes, as monotonically increasing in value, existing models can assign inaccurately high values to suboptimal or harmful states. This misrepresentation hinders the model's ability to distinguish effective from ineffective actions.
- **Degraded Policy Learning**: When these inaccurate value functions are used to guide downstream robot imitation learning (e.g., for data filtering or weighting), policies may inadvertently learn to reproduce the undesirable mistake-prone behaviors present in the demonstrations, leading to lower success rates and less robust performance.
- **Missed Information**: Discarding or ignoring retry events means losing valuable supervisory signals. A retry event typically signals a degradation in task progress, followed by a recovery phase. This local "degradation-and-recovery" structure provides direct evidence about what constitutes a deviation from the desired path and what actions are effective in correcting such deviations.

Given these limitations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **To identify retry events as informative supervisory signals**: The research aims to demonstrate that sparse annotations of retry events can provide valuable local supervision that reveals specific value drops associated with execution errors and value rebounds associated with corrective behaviors, which are often obscured by coarse global progress labels.
2. **To develop a novel value learning framework (ReTVL)**: The goal is to propose a framework, ReTVL (Retry-Supervised Value Learning), that integrates two complementary forms of supervision: global progress calibration and local pairwise preference learning derived from retry events. This framework is designed to enable the learning of value functions that simultaneously reflect overall task advancement and fine-grained mistake-recovery dynamics.
3. **To improve the accuracy of value estimation**: The objective is to produce value models that can assign appropriately lower values to mistake-prone segments and higher values to useful corrective behaviors, thereby yielding more fine-grained and accurate value estimates than models relying solely on monotonic progress.
4. **To enhance downstream robot imitation learning**: Ultimately, the research aims to show that the retry-sensitive value functions learned by ReTVL can be effectively used to reweight demonstration chunks for behavior cloning, leading to improved policy performance by down-weighting harmful execution errors and prioritizing useful corrective actions.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, ReTVL (Retry-Supervised Value Learning), is designed to learn mistake-sensitive value functions from mixed-quality robot demonstrations by integrating global progress signals with local retry-induced preference supervision.

#### 3.1. Value Function Definition

The value model, denoted as $V_\theta(h_t, \ell)$, estimates the current task progress as a scalar value within $$, given a history observation window $h_t$ (of size $H$) and a language instruction $\ell$. For stability, a distributional value function is employed, predicting a categorical distribution over $K$ discretized progress bins. The scalar value is then computed as the expected progress under this predicted distribution.

#### 3.2. Supervision Sources and Training Objectives

ReTVL combines two primary sources of supervision:

- **Global Progress Supervision (Absolute Loss)**:
 - **Target Generation**: For successful trajectories of length $T$, a target progress $v^\star_t = t/T$ is assigned to each timestep $t$. For failed trajectories, $v^\star_t = 0$ is assigned to terminal frames.
 - **Application**: This absolute progress supervision is applied to frames *outside* the defined retry neighborhoods, where monotonic progress assumptions are considered reliable.
 - **Loss Function**: A cross-entropy loss, $L_{abs}$, is used to train the model to predict the discretized target progress bin. This objective ensures a global reference for task advancement.

- **Local Pairwise Value Learning from Retry Events (Preference Loss)**:
 - **Data Annotation**: In addition to standard success/failure labels, trajectories containing retry behaviors are sparsely annotated with "retry keypoints" ($r_{i,j}$). Each keypoint marks the *start* of a corrective behavior. This sparse annotation is chosen for its reliability and ease of acquisition compared to delineating full mistake or recovery segments.
 - **Pair Construction**: For each retry keypoint $r_{i,j}$, the temporal neighborhood is divided into three approximate regions:
 - $W^{pre}_{i,j}$: The window before the retry keypoint, indicating potential degradation.
 - $W^{near}_{i,j}$: A small window centered around the retry keypoint, representing the degraded state requiring correction.
 - $W^{post}_{i,j}$: The window after the retry keypoint, representing the recovery phase.
 - Preference pairs $(h^+, h^-)$ are sampled from these regions based on a "drop-and-rebound" assumption: values should decrease as execution approaches the degraded state ($W^{pre} > W^{near}$), and increase as corrective behavior restores feasibility ($W^{post} > W^{near}$). Additional intra-window pairs (e.g., earlier $W^{pre}$ > later $W^{pre}$) reinforce the value gradient.
 - **Soft-Window Weighting**: To mitigate noise from approximate window boundaries, a soft-window weighting scheme is applied. Each sampled pair $(h^+, h^-)$ is assigned a weight $w(h^+, h^-)$ that decays exponentially with the temporal distance of the preferred history $h^+$ from the retry keypoint. This prioritizes supervision from regions closer to the unambiguously labeled retry keypoint.
 - **Loss Function**: A soft-weighted pairwise logistic loss, $L_{pref}$, is optimized. This loss enforces the local value ordering derived from the retry events, encouraging the model to learn value drops before correction and value recovery afterward.

#### 3.3. Overall Training Objective

The total training objective, $L_{value}$, is a weighted sum of the absolute progress loss and the preference loss: $L_{value} = \lambda_{abs}L_{abs} + \lambda_{pref}L_{pref}$. Absolute progress samples are drawn from frames outside retry neighborhoods, while retry-based preference pairs are sampled from retry regions, with a fixed sampling ratio $\rho_{pref}$.

#### 3.4. Value-Guided Behavior Cloning (Downstream Policy Learning)

To evaluate the practical utility of the learned value model, it is integrated into a weighted behavior cloning (BC) framework.
- **Chunk Weight Calculation**: For each action chunk starting at time $t$ with stride $\Delta_a$, a reward $r_t$ is computed as the predicted value improvement: $r_t = V_\theta(h_{t+\Delta_a}, \ell) - V_\theta(h_t, \ell)$. This reward is then normalized and clipped to produce a weight $\alpha_t \in$ using the mean ($\mu$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$) of value improvements across the offline dataset.
- **Weighted BC Objective**: The policy $\pi_\psi$ is trained using a normalized weighted BC objective, which scales the standard BC loss $l_{BC}$ for each chunk by its corresponding weight $\alpha_t$. This effectively down-weights action chunks associated with low or negative predicted progress improvement (e.g., mistake segments) and emphasizes chunks that contribute to higher predicted task progress (e.g., successful or recovery segments).

#### 3.5. Architecture and Implementation Details

All trainable value models, including ReTVL, utilize Robometer-4B as the backbone, which is a VLM pretrained on large-scale robotic data (based on Qwen/Qwen3-VL-4B-Instruct architecture). A two-layer MLP value head is attached to the VLM's vision-language hidden state of the last frame in the observation history window (8 frames). The backbone is fine-tuned with LoRA, and the value head is trained jointly. Hyperparameters for sampling window sizes, soft-window temperature, preference temperature, and loss weights are specified. For downstream policy learning, a flow-matching Vision-Language-Action policy with a $\pi_0$-style backbone is used, and it takes task instruction, RGB observations (from two cameras), and robot proprioception as input to predict a 6-DoF end-effector pose and gripper command.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to assess ReTVL's ability to capture subtle execution errors and improve downstream policy learning compared to progress-based value models.

#### 4.1. Value Evaluation

The value models were evaluated using both global and local metrics on four real-robot manipulation tasks (Pick up Spoon, Stack Blocks, Fold Towel, Open Drawer).

- **Global Metrics (Table 1)**: These measure overall task progress and success prediction.
 - **Value-Order Correlation (VOC)**: ReTVL achieved a VOC score of 0.987, comparable to the highest baseline (Robometer: 0.994), indicating its ability to model monotonic progress on clean successful trajectories.
 - **Success-Fail Detection (S/F Det.)**: ReTVL achieved a perfect score of 1.000, matching RECAP-Value, demonstrating its capability to distinguish successful from failed trajectories.
 - These results collectively indicate that ReTVL maintains strong performance in global value estimation, on par with or exceeding leading baselines.

- **Local Metrics (Table 1 & Figure 3)**: These assess mistake sensitivity and value changes around retry events.
 - **Drop AUC, Drop Probability, Pre > Retry, Post > Retry**: ReTVL exhibited substantial improvements across all local metrics.
 - **Drop AUC**: Increased significantly from baseline values (e.g., RECAP-Value: 0.372) to 0.797 for ReTVL.
 - **Drop Probability**: Improved to 0.874, indicating more consistent detection of value drops around retries.
 - **Pre > Retry**: ReTVL achieved 0.740, markedly higher than all baselines (e.g., TOPReward: 0.329, Robometer: 0.086, RECAP-Value: 0.296). This indicates that ReTVL more reliably assigns lower values to states immediately preceding a retry (i.e., problematic states).
 - **Post > Retry**: ReTVL also showed a strong score of 0.967, indicating effective value recovery after corrective behaviors.
 - **Visualizations (Figure 3)**: Representative value curves confirmed that ReTVL generated clear local value drops around retry keypoints and subsequent rebounds after correction, a behavior less pronounced or absent in progress-based baselines.
 - These findings demonstrate that ReTVL effectively captures local degradation-and-recovery patterns around execution errors without sacrificing global progress estimation.

#### 4.2. Policy Learning

The learned value functions were used to reweight demonstration chunks for behavior cloning, and policy performance was evaluated in terms of success rate on real-robot tasks.

- **Success Rate (Table 2)**: ReTVL-BC consistently outperformed Standard BC and RECAP-BC across all four tasks.
 - **Average Success Rate**: ReTVL-BC achieved 80.00%, significantly higher than RECAP-BC (62.50%) and Standard BC (41.25%).
 - The improvements suggest that ReTVL's mistake-sensitive values enabled the policy to prioritize more effective actions and suppress harmful ones, leading to more robust policies.
- **Weighted Bad Actions (Figure 4 & Table 7)**:
 - Analysis of weights assigned to manually annotated "bad actions" showed that ReTVL-BC reduced the average weight of bad actions to 0.11, an 80% reduction compared to RECAP-BC's 0.54.
 - ReTVL also demonstrated a much lower Strict-bad Retention rate (0.206) compared to baselines (0.695-0.896), indicating its ability to consistently down-weight harmful segments.
 - Concurrently, ReTVL assigned the highest weights to post-retry recovery chunks (0.636), highlighting its selective weighting that preserves useful corrective behaviors while suppressing mistakes.

#### 4.3. Ablation and Analysis

Ablation studies investigated the contribution of ReTVL's key components.

- **Without Preference Loss (Table 3 & Figure 6)**: Replacing preference loss with direct regression to manually shaped progress targets (with injected value drops) resulted in strong global metrics but significant drops in local mistake-sensitive metrics (e.g., Drop AUC from 0.797 to 0.510). This indicates that direct numeric shaping does not reliably teach the model to learn the visual cues for relative value ordering around mistakes; explicit preference supervision is crucial.
- **Without Soft Window (Table 3 & Figure 6)**: Removing soft-window weighting, which applies uniform retry-related supervision, led to a decrease in VOC (from 0.987 to 0.940) while local metrics remained comparable. This suggests that the soft weighting mechanism helps stabilize the interaction between retry supervision and absolute progress, preventing conflicts at window boundaries.
- **Without Absolute Calibration (Table 3 & Figure 6)**: Removing the absolute progress and success-failure calibration losses weakened the global value structure (VOC dropped from 0.987 to 0.929, S/F Det. from 1.000 to 0.967), although local metrics remained strong. This indicates that absolute calibration provides a necessary anchor for the global value scale, complementing the local relative preferences.

In summary, the results demonstrate that ReTVL effectively leverages retry events to learn nuanced value functions that distinguish between local errors and recovery actions, leading to substantial improvements in imitation learning from imperfect demonstrations. All three proposed components (preference supervision, absolute calibration, and soft windowing) contribute to the overall performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in robot imitation learning, particularly in addressing the challenges posed by imperfect human demonstrations.

### 6. Significance

- **Challenging the Monotonic Progress Assumption**: The work demonstrates that the widely adopted assumption of monotonic progress in value/reward learning is insufficient for robustly interpreting real-world human demonstrations. By explicitly modeling non-monotonic value changes around retry events, ReTVL offers a more realistic and fine-grained representation of task progress.
- **Leveraging "Noisy" Data**: A key contribution is the identification and effective utilization of retry events—often considered noise or suboptimal data—as valuable supervisory signals. This paradigm shift transforms problematic data segments into informative cues for learning.
- **Mistake-Sensitive Value Learning**: ReTVL successfully learns value functions that can accurately identify local execution errors (value drops) and subsequent corrective behaviors (value rebounds). This capability is crucial for distinguishing between harmful and beneficial actions within a demonstration.
- **Improved Imitation Learning**: The learned mistake-sensitive values directly translate into tangible improvements in downstream policy learning. By enabling weighted behavior cloning to selectively suppress erroneous actions while prioritizing effective recovery, ReTVL leads to more robust and higher-performing robot policies. This is a practical advancement for deploying robot learning systems.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Reduced Data Collection Burden**: By making robot learning more resilient to imperfect demonstrations, ReTVL can significantly reduce the stringent requirements for perfectly curated, "expert-only" datasets. This can lower the cost and effort associated with data collection, making robot learning more accessible and scalable.
- **More Robust Robot Policies**: Policies trained with ReTVL's approach may be more capable of self-correction and adaptation in dynamic or uncertain environments, as they have learned to recognize and recover from sub-optimal states. This could lead to robots that are less prone to failure and more reliable in real-world deployments.
- **Foundation for Advanced Feedback Systems**: The framework for incorporating retry events as sparse supervision could be extended to other forms of human feedback or environmental cues that indicate local deviations or corrective actions. This opens avenues for developing more interactive and adaptable robot learning systems.
- **Broader Applicability**: While demonstrated on manipulation tasks, the core idea of learning from non-monotonic progress signals around corrective events could be applicable to other domains where imperfect demonstrations or human interventions are common, such as autonomous driving or human-robot collaboration.

**Limitations and Future Directions (as identified by the authors):**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, including the assumption of a specific degradation-and-recovery pattern around retries (which may not cover all real-world correction types like exploratory or unsuccessful retries). The current application is limited to offline behavior cloning reweighting, with broader closed-loop policy improvement or online reinforcement learning settings yet to be explored. Finally, the evaluation was conducted on a limited set of real-robot tasks, suggesting a need for broader assessment on larger datasets and diverse scenarios to confirm generalization capabilities across different tasks, robot platforms, and demonstration styles. Addressing these limitations could further expand the impact of this work.
