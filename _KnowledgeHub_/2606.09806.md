# Topological Neural Operators

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces Topological Neural Operators (TNOs), a framework that integrates principles from Discrete Exterior Calculus (DEC) into operator learning to model physical systems on cell complexes. TNOs and their hierarchical extension (HTNOs) achieve improved accuracy and generalization on various PDE benchmarks by respecting the geometric typing of physical quantities and encoding conservation laws directly into the architecture.

## Problem

- Existing Neural Operators (NOs) abstract all physical quantities to a point-centric representation, overlooking their distinct geometric types (e.g., scalars on vertices, fluxes on faces).
- Current NOs do not explicitly incorporate the structured interactions between physical quantities governed by differential operators (gradient, curl, divergence), potentially obscuring inherent conservation laws.
- Modeling complex multi-degree PDE systems, where unknowns reside on different ranks of a cell complex and interact topologically, is challenging for traditional NOs.

## Method

- Topological Neural Operators (**TNO**s) represent physical quantities as k-cochains on cell complexes, associating features with their natural geometric supports (vertices, edges, faces).
- TNOs explicitly integrate ==Discrete Exterior Calculus (DEC)== operators (exterior derivative, codifferential, Hodge Laplacian) into their architecture to define fixed, principled topological information flow paths.
- Hierarchical TNOs (HTNOs) extend the framework to a hierarchy of cell complexes, using a learned V-cycle akin to multigrid methods to propagate long-range and topology-dependent information efficiently.

## Results

- TNOs and HTNOs consistently outperformed seven baseline methods, including RIGNO, MeshGraphNet, and Geo-FNO, on steady-state benchmarks like Poisson-Gauss, Elasticity, and compressible airfoil flows.
- On large-scale 3D surface PDEs (EmmiWing dataset), HTNO achieved the lowest aggregate L1 error (**2.41%**) and demonstrated approximately **6x** faster convergence compared to RIGNO-18.
- In a controlled study with anisotropic Darcy flow, TNOs outperformed parameter-matched MPNN baselines by 4-5 percentage points in L1 error, with an additional **0.54** pp improvement when natively ingesting higher-rank (face-supported) tensor orientation data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling the fixed topological flow of information (using DEC operators) from learnable feature transformations embeds conservation laws and compatibility conditions directly into the neural network structure.
> - The TNO framework provides a unified perspective, recovering standard point- and graph-based neural operators as specialized cases within its broader topological context.
> - Processing signals at their appropriate topological rank, particularly by utilizing the harmonic-basis channel and sheaf transport, is crucial for resolving the spectral signature of anisotropic operators and handling non-trivial curl in PDEs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of scientific machine learning has increasingly focused on operator learning, particularly with the advent of Neural Operators (NOs), to model and predict complex physical systems governed by partial differential equations (PDEs). Methods such as Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs) and their geometric extensions have demonstrated substantial speedups and generalization capabilities across different discretizations compared to traditional numerical solvers. These successes establish a scalable paradigm for scientific machine learning applications, including digital twins, design optimization, and real-time simulation.

However, the authors identify a fundamental limitation in existing NO approaches: their inherent "point-centric" nature. Current NOs typically represent all physical quantities as functions defined on nodes or edges, with interactions modeled through learned kernels or graph-based message passing. This abstraction, according to the paper, overlooks a critical structural aspect of continuum physics: physical quantities possess a distinct geometric type. For instance, scalar potentials are associated with vertices, circulations with edges, fluxes with faces, and densities with volumes. Their interactions are intrinsically governed by differential operators (e.g., gradient, curl, divergence) that explicitly couple quantities across different spatial dimensions. In compatible numerical discretizations, these relationships are encoded directly via incidence structures, which inherently ensure the preservation of conservation laws and identities (such as `div curl = 0`).

By collapsing diverse physical quantities to a single representation on nodes, current neural operators may obscure this inherent structure. This can limit their effectiveness in modeling complex multi-physics systems, enforcing conservation and compatibility constraints, and capturing subtle topological phenomena, such as cycles and harmonic modes.

This work introduces Topological Neural Operators (TNOs) as a response to these limitations. TNOs aim to integrate a principled topological framework into operator learning by modeling physical quantities on their natural geometric supports within cell complexes. This approach positions TNOs as an extension that can recover standard point- and graph-based NOs as specialized cases, thereby offering a unified perspective on operator learning that spans various discretization types and respects the underlying physical and geometric structure of the data. The framework builds upon concepts from Discrete Exterior Calculus (DEC) and topological deep learning (TDL), seeking to directly embed the algebraic backbone of discrete physical laws into the neural network architecture.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce Topological Neural Operators (TNOs), a framework designed for operator learning on cell complexes. This framework aims to extend the capabilities of existing neural operators by explicitly incorporating the topological and geometric structure of physical data.

The motivation for this development stems from several observations regarding the limitations of current neural operator paradigms when applied to physical systems:

- **Geometric Typing of Physical Quantities:** Physical quantities are not homogenous in their geometric support. Potentials are naturally defined on points (vertices), circulations along curves (edges), fluxes across surfaces (faces), and densities within volumes (3-cells). Existing point-centric neural operators typically abstract all these quantities to node features, potentially losing critical information about their intrinsic geometric nature.
- **Structured Interactions via Differential Operators:** The interactions between these geometrically distinct quantities are governed by specific differential operators (gradient, curl, divergence). These operators intrinsically couple quantities across different dimensions (e.g., a gradient transforms a scalar field on vertices into a vector field on edges). This cross-dimensional coupling is a fundamental aspect of continuum physics.
- **Inherent Conservation Laws and Compatibility Conditions:** In compatible numerical discretizations, the relationships between different-dimensional cells (e.g., a face being bounded by edges) directly encode conservation laws and algebraic identities (e.g., the boundary of a boundary is zero, which translates to `curl(grad) = 0` and `div(curl) = 0`). By collapsing all quantities to nodes, current NOs obscure these structures, forcing the model to implicitly learn these fundamental physical principles rather than having them built into the architecture.
- **Modeling Multi-Degree PDE Systems:** Many physical systems, such as Maxwell’s equations or Navier-Stokes in vorticity form, inherently couple fields of different geometric types (e.g., a 1-form electric field with a 2-form magnetic flux). Existing NOs struggle to explicitly model such multi-degree PDE systems where unknowns reside on different ranks of the cell complex and interact through differential operators.
- **Need for a Unified Perspective and Generalization:** The authors seek to provide a unified framework that can encompass existing point- and graph-based neural operators as restricted cases while offering enhanced capabilities for generalization across discretizations and for handling topology-dependent effects.

To address these motivations, the key design principle of TNOs is to decouple "where information flows" from "how it is transformed." The "where" is governed by fixed, principled topological operators derived from Discrete Exterior Calculus (DEC), which define the allowed pathways and types of interaction between cells of varying dimensions. The "how" refers to the learnable components of the neural network, which transform and mix the features propagated along these fixed topological pathways. This design is hypothesized to yield models that inherently respect the geometric support of physical quantities, expose conservation and compatibility structures, and enable explicit modeling of multi-degree PDE systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TNO framework is built upon the mathematical principles of cell complexes and Discrete Exterior Calculus (DEC).

**Data Representation and Operators:**
- **Cell Complexes:** TNOs operate on cell complexes, which are discrete domains composed of cells of various dimensions: 0-cells (vertices), 1-cells (edges), 2-cells (faces), and higher-dimensional elements. Physical quantities are represented as *k-cochains*, assigning a feature vector to each k-cell. This respects the geometric support of quantities, distinguishing between scalar potentials on vertices, circulations on edges, fluxes on faces, etc.
- **Discrete Exterior Derivative ($d_k$):** This operator lifts a k-cochain to a (k+1)-cochain by accumulating signed values over incidence relations. For example, $d_0$ is the discrete gradient, $d_1$ is the discrete curl, and $d_2$ is the discrete divergence-of-dual. A key property is $d_{k+1} \circ d_k = 0$, reflecting the continuum identities like `curl(grad) = 0` and `div(curl) = 0`.
- **Hodge Operators ($M_k$):** These are positive-definite matrices that endow each cochain space with a geometric structure, encoding quantities like edge lengths, face areas, and cell volumes.
- **Codifferential ($\delta_k$):** Defined as the $M_k$-adjoint of $d_{k-1}$, this operator maps downward in degree, from a k-cochain to a (k-1)-cochain ($\delta_k = M_{k-1}^{-1} B_k M_k$). It depends on both topology (incidence matrix $B_k$) and geometry ($M_k$).
- **Hodge Laplacian ($\Delta_k$):** This self-adjoint operator, $\Delta_k = \delta_{k+1} \circ d_k + d_{k-1} \circ \delta_k$, couples k-cochains to themselves. It is decomposed into $\Delta_k^\uparrow$ (coupling via (k+1)-cells) and $\Delta_k^\downarrow$ (coupling via (k-1)-cells), which are physically distinct propagation mechanisms.
- **Hodge Decomposition:** The framework leverages the orthogonal decomposition of k-cochain space into exact, harmonic, and coexact components ($C^k = \text{im}(d_{k-1}) \oplus \text{ker}(\Delta_k) \oplus \text{im}(\delta_{k+1})$). These components have physical interpretations (e.g., conservative fields are exact, divergence-free fields are coexact, topological modes are harmonic).

**Topological Neural Operators (TNOs) Architecture:**
A TNO is defined as a parameterized family of neural networks $T_K^\theta$ that adheres to three properties:
1. **Cellular intrinsicness:** It is constructed from the cellular operators of the cell complex K (boundary/coboundary maps, Hodge stars, etc.), ensuring independence from fixed grids or cell orderings.
2. **Multi-degree coupling:** Information can flow between cochain degrees through the DEC operators ($d, \delta, \Delta$) and learned compositions, enabling an output at one degree to depend on inputs at any other degree.
3. **Discretization transferability:** The weights $\theta$ are shared across different discretizations, ensuring consistency under mesh refinement.

**Realization as a Topological Neural Network (TNN):**
The TNO is structured as an encoder-decoder network that operates on hidden cochains, $H = (H^0, \ldots, H^N)$, within a lifted combinatorial complex $\tilde{K}$. The core of the TNO is its *topological layers* $L^{(\ell)}_\theta$:
- **Layer Update Rule:** Each rank-k block update within a layer is formulated as:
 $H_k^\text{out} = \phi_\theta \left( d_{k-1}H_{k-1}W_k^\downarrow, \Delta_k^\uparrow H_k W_k^{\Delta,\uparrow}, \Delta_k^\downarrow H_k W_k^{\Delta,\downarrow}, H_k, \delta_{k+1}H_{k+1}W_k^\uparrow \right)$.
 This equation explicitly incorporates features from adjacent ranks (via $d_{k-1}$ and $\delta_{k+1}$) and within the same rank (via $\Delta_k^\uparrow$, $\Delta_k^\downarrow$, and a self-map $H_k$).
- **Trainable Components:** The trainable elements are the channel-mixing matrices ($W$) and the nonlinear transformation $\phi_\theta$. Crucially, the transport maps (e.g., $d_{k-1}, \Delta_k^\uparrow$) are *fixed* by the topological and geometric structure of the cell complex, not learned. This design choice directly implements the "decoupling" principle.
- **Linear Realization:** A simplified linear TNO layer uses the Hodge decomposition channels directly: $T_{\theta,k}(h) = \delta_{k+1}h_{k+1}W_k^\uparrow + d_{k-1}h_{k-1}W_k^\downarrow + P_{\text{harm}}^k h_k W_{\text{harm}}^k + h_k W_{\text{self}}^k$. This separates coexact, exact, harmonic, and local components.

**Recovery of Existing Neural Operators:**
The framework demonstrates that:
- **Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs):** Can be recovered as a rank-0 spectral TNO layer on uniform periodic cubical complexes, where the same-rank spectral channel is diagonal in the Fourier basis.
- **Graph Neural Operators (GNOs):** Can be recovered as a rank-0 TNO on directed graph complexes, using learned incidence-supported kernel messages followed by target-incidence aggregation.

**Hierarchical TNOs (HTNOs):**
To propagate long-range and topology-dependent information, HTNOs extend the TNO architecture to a hierarchy of cell complexes ($K_0 \leftarrow K_1 \leftarrow \cdots \leftarrow K_L$). It arranges TNO blocks as a learned V-cycle, analogous to multigrid methods for de Rham complexes. Coarsening can be achieved via fixed (k-means) or learned (soft Voronoi) clustering of fine vertices, from which coarse complexes and their DEC operators are derived. Transfer maps between levels are degree-preserving and aim for compatibility with the coboundary operators.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TNOs and HTNOs spanned various PDE benchmarks, including steady-state problems on irregular 2D and 3D meshes, as well as controlled studies.

**Performance on Established Steady-State Benchmarks:**
- **Poisson-Gauss, Airfoil, Elasticity (Table 1):** TNOs and HTNOs generally exhibited improved accuracy compared to seven baselines (RIGNO-18, RIGNO-12, MeshGraphNet, Geo-FNO, FNO DSE, GINO, UPT) on Poisson-Gauss and Elasticity problems. On the Airfoil Flow benchmark, TNO was competitive with RIGNO-18, a result attributed to the relatively limited regime variation in that specific dataset.
- **Compressible Airfoils (NACA0012, NACA2412, RAE2822) and Poisson-with-Sines (PCS) (Table 2a):** In tests involving compressible airfoil flows across a broad range of Mach numbers and angles of attack (encompassing subsonic, transonic, and supersonic regimes), TNOs and HTNOs consistently outperformed RIGNO and GAOT. This performance is hypothesized to be due to the natural alignment of TNO's Hodge/harmonic-basis inductive biases with the elliptic characteristics of steady compressible flow outside shocks. For the Poisson-with-sines problem on a fixed mesh, HTNO achieved a strong performance (0.52% relative L1 error) that surpassed GAOT (0.72%), a method well-suited for such settings.

**Performance on Large-Scale Surface PDEs (EmmiWing):**
- On the EmmiWing dataset, which involves 3D wing surfaces and outputs surface pressure and wall-shear-stress components, HTNO achieved the lowest aggregate L1 error (2.41%). This result surpassed Transolver (2.55%), a matched-budget self-attention Transformer (2.60%), PointNet (2.62%), and RIGNO-18 (2.78%). Additionally, HTNO demonstrated faster convergence, requiring approximately 6 times fewer epochs than RIGNO-18. This indicates the effectiveness of HTNO in modeling long-range interactions without relying on per-sample Hodge decompositions on large, resampled geometries.

**Controlled Study: Anisotropic Darcy with Per-Face Random Tensor Orientation:**
- This experiment investigated the benefits of TNOs natively ingesting higher-rank signals. By designing a diffusivity tensor field with per-face random orientations that were explicitly lossy upon projection to vertices, the study isolated the impact of topology-aware signal processing.
- **Architectural Advantage:** TNOs consistently outperformed parameter-matched MPNN baselines by approximately 4-5 percentage points (pp) in L1 error, regardless of how the orientation information was presented (vertex inputs or projected inputs). This improvement is attributed to the TNO's harmonic-basis channel, which is capable of resolving the spectral signature of the anisotropic operator in a manner that vanilla message-passing cannot.
- **Native Rank-2 Ingestion:** When TNO was allowed to ingest the orientation field at its native rank 2 (as a face-supported 2-cochain), it achieved an additional 0.54 pp improvement (~10% relative) compared to when the same information was provided via vertex-projected channels. This confirms the benefit of processing signals at their appropriate topological rank, particularly when projection to lower ranks leads to information loss.

**Ablation Studies on Synthetic Topologies:**
- This set of studies aimed to isolate the contributions of TNO’s harmonic-basis input and sheaf transport components on scalar PDEs (Darcy with reaction and conservative advection-diffusion) where flux carries non-trivial curl.
- **Harmonic Basis Contribution:** Disabling the harmonic basis led to a substantial decrease in accuracy (6.8 pp loss on Darcy and 7.9 pp loss on Advection-Diffusion).
- **Sheaf Transport and Synergy:** The results indicated a synergistic relationship between sheaf transport and the harmonic basis. Sheaf transport was beneficial when the harmonic basis was enabled, but it actively detrimental when the harmonic basis was disabled (e.g., Darcy error increased from 9.44% to 11.64%). The complete TNO architecture achieved performance approximately 33% better than the parameter-matched MPNN baseline on both PDEs. These findings suggest that the harmonic and sheaf components function as an effective inductive bias, particularly for PDEs characterized by non-trivial curl.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Topological Neural Operators (TNOs) and Hierarchical TNOs (HTNOs) represents a methodological shift in operator learning by integrating explicit topological and geometric structures into neural network architectures.

The significance of this work can be understood through several key aspects:

- **Principled Framework for Physical Systems:** TNOs provide a principled framework for operator learning by directly incorporating Discrete Exterior Calculus (DEC) into the network design. This ensures that the models inherently respect the geometric support of physical quantities (e.g., potentials on vertices, fluxes on faces) and the cross-dimensional coupling governed by differential operators.
- **Enhanced Structural Fidelity and Physical Consistency:** By decoupling the fixed topological flow of information (governed by DEC operators like gradient, curl, divergence) from the learned feature transformations, TNOs embed conservation and compatibility laws (e.g., `div curl = 0`) directly into the architecture. This structural fidelity can lead to improved physical consistency in learned models, rather than requiring these laws to be implicitly learned or enforced through additional penalties.
- **Unified Perspective on Operator Learning:** The framework demonstrates that existing point-based and graph-based neural operators can be recovered as restricted cases of TNOs. This offers a more generalized and unifying perspective on operator learning across diverse discretization schemes.
- **Improved Accuracy and Generalization:** Empirical results across a range of PDE benchmarks, including those with irregular geometries and higher-rank systems, indicate that TNOs and HTNOs achieve improved accuracy and generalization capabilities. This is particularly evident in problems where the underlying physics benefits from explicit topological awareness and multi-degree coupling.
- **Addressing Multi-Degree PDE Systems:** TNOs are explicitly designed to handle multi-degree PDE systems, where physical quantities reside on different topological ranks and interact through differential operators. This capability is crucial for accurately modeling complex multi-physics phenomena.
- **Foundation for Scientific Foundation Models:** The approach paves the way for the development of "scientific foundation models" that are not merely data-driven approximations but are also grounded in the inherent geometry and physics of the systems they model. Such models could accelerate scientific simulations and design optimization processes.

The potential impact of TNOs spans various scientific and engineering disciplines:
- **More Accurate and Efficient Surrogate Models:** TNOs could lead to more faithful and efficient surrogate models for complex simulations in fluid dynamics, electromagnetism, elasticity, and other areas, potentially reducing computational costs associated with traditional numerical solvers.
- **Robustness to Irregular Geometries:** The intrinsic nature of TNOs makes them well-suited for problems on irregular and unstructured meshes, which are common in real-world engineering and scientific applications.
- **Informed Machine Learning for Science:** By integrating deep learning with fundamental mathematical structures from differential geometry and topology, TNOs contribute to the broader effort of developing physics-informed machine learning methodologies.

However, the authors also acknowledge limitations and areas for future work, including scaling to dynamic, adaptive, and very large domains; developing rigorous approximation, stability, and convergence theories; and extending TNOs to multiscale systems, evolving manifolds, inverse problems, and gauge- or symmetry-equivariant operator families. The importance of careful validation against trusted numerical solvers is also highlighted, particularly for safety-critical applications, as TNOs remain learned approximations.
