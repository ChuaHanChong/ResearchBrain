# From World Models to World Action Models: A Concise Tutorial for Robotics

> [!summary] Summary
> This tutorial clarifies the conceptual scope of "world models" in embodied AI and robotics, proposing a unified definition and a structured taxonomy. It distinguishes between "world models" (predicting future observations or states) and "world action models" (linking predictions to executable actions), providing a comprehensive framework for understanding design choices and trade-offs across various approaches.

## Problem

- The term "world model" is ambiguously used across embodied AI, computer vision, and robotics, hindering clear communication and systematic comparison of methods.
- Existing "world models" vary significantly in their modeling objectives, prediction targets, and how they are used for control or planning, leading to a fragmented research landscape.
- A lack of consistent definition and classification impedes a structured understanding and comparison of diverse predictive and control paradigms in robotics.

## Method

- Establishing clear, unified definitions for ==world models== and introducing ==world action models== to explicitly link predicted futures with executable robot actions.
- Developing a ==structured taxonomy== for world models based on their prediction space (==observation-space== vs. ==state-space==) and dimensions like observation explicitness and ==action abstraction==.
- Categorizing world action models into ==four representative paradigms== that illustrate different ways to integrate visual future prediction with action generation.

## Results

- Unified, operational definitions were established for "world," "embodied AI task," "world model," and "world action model," providing a common conceptual framework.
- A comprehensive design-space taxonomy for world models was presented, categorizing models by their prediction space (observation-space based on spatial explicitness and action abstraction; state-space based on latent, point track, neural-symbolic, and physical representations).
- Four distinct paradigms for world action models were identified and detailed: imagine-then-execute, video-feature-conditioned action prediction, joint video-action modeling, and auxiliary video prediction for policy learning, outlining their respective design considerations and trade-offs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The choice between observation-space and state-space prediction, alongside the level of action abstraction, significantly dictates a world model's applicability and trade-offs in visual fidelity, interpretability, and control usability.
> - Integrating predictive capabilities with action generation in robotics involves distinct architectural paradigms, each with specific advantages and disadvantages concerning modularity, computational efficiency, data requirements, and consistency.
> - Fundamental trade-offs, such as visual fidelity versus dynamics learning complexity, and compact representations versus semantic interpretability, are inherent in the design of world and world action models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This tutorial addresses a conceptual challenge within the fields of embodied artificial intelligence (AI) and generative simulation, specifically concerning the term "world model." The ability to predict how the environment evolves in response to actions is a fundamental requirement for embodied AI systems, supporting capabilities such as controller design, planning, decision-making, and policy learning.

Within these domains, the term "world model" has been applied to a diverse range of methods, including latent dynamics models, video prediction models, physics-informed simulators, and action-conditioned generative models. These methods exhibit substantial differences in their modeling objectives, prediction targets, and how their predictions are utilized for control or planning. The prevalent ambiguity surrounding the scope and characteristics of these models creates a challenge for researchers seeking to understand, compare, and develop new systems.

This tutorial positions itself as a clarifying work within this landscape. It aims to provide a unified definition and a structured taxonomy that organizes existing "world models" and introduces the concept of "world action models." By doing so, it seeks to bring conceptual clarity to a rapidly evolving and fragmented research area, facilitating a more systematic understanding and comparison of different predictive and control paradigms in robotics. The work synthesizes approaches from computer vision (e.g., video prediction), reinforcement learning (e.g., latent dynamics), and robotics (e.g., physical simulation, action grounding) into a cohesive framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this tutorial stems from the increasing but often ambiguous use of the term "world model" across various research communities in embodied intelligence and generative simulation. While these models are recognized for their potential to enable complex robot behaviors, the lack of a consistent definition and classification system hinders effective communication, comparison, and advancement of the field. The paper identifies that existing "world models" vary significantly in their underlying mechanisms, what they aim to predict, and their intended applications, leading to potential misunderstandings and unoptimized design choices.

In response to this motivation, the key objectives of this tutorial are:

1. **To Clarify the Conceptual Scope of World (Action) Models:** The tutorial aims to establish a clear and consistent definition for "world models" and introduce the related concept of "world action models," thereby resolving ambiguity in terminology. This involves defining the "world" itself, the nature of "embodied AI tasks" and "policies," and then formalizing what constitutes a predictive "world model."
2. **To Provide a Structured Taxonomy for Embodied Prediction and Control:** A central objective is to offer a systematic categorization of existing world model approaches. This taxonomy is designed to organize the diverse methodologies based on their fundamental design choices, specifically regarding their prediction space and the level of action abstraction.
3. **To Present a Design-Space View of World Models:** The tutorial approaches world models from a design-space perspective, framing them as action-conditioned predictive models that estimate the future evolution of task-relevant observations or states. This framework allows for a structured analysis of the trade-offs inherent in different design decisions.
4. **To Categorize and Compare Existing Methods:** A detailed objective is to classify current world models into distinct categories, primarily "observation-space" and "state-space" models, and to systematically compare their respective advantages and disadvantages. This comparison focuses on factors such as visual fidelity, spatial structure, physical interpretability, and control usability.
5. **To Introduce and Summarize World Action Models:** The tutorial specifically aims to define "world action models" as an extension that links predicted futures with executable robot actions. It seeks to summarize and categorize representative paradigms that implement this coupling, thereby illustrating how predictive capabilities are translated into practical robot control.

Collectively, these objectives are intended to provide a foundational and organized understanding of predictive models in embodied AI, assisting researchers in developing, applying, and evaluating such systems more effectively.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

As a tutorial, the paper's methodology involves defining, categorizing, and analyzing existing concepts and research paradigms rather than presenting new experimental results. The approach is structured into three main parts: establishing definitions, classifying world models, and categorizing world action models.

#### 3.1. Architectural View and Definitions

The tutorial begins by establishing a foundational vocabulary:
- **World:** Defined as the collection of task-relevant entities, encompassing both the robot and its environment, including objects of interest.
- **Embodied AI Task and Policy:** An embodied AI task is characterized as transforming an initial world configuration into a target configuration through robot control. A policy is a function that receives a language instruction and current observation to output an action.
- **World Models:** Formally defined as models that predict the future observation ($o_{t+1}$) or state ($x_{t+1}$) of the world, conditioned on the current observation ($o_t$) and action ($a_t$). The tutorial notes that these models can vary in form (e.g., symbolic equations, neural networks, diffusion models) and the nature of $o_t$, $a_t$, and $x_t$.
- **World Action Models:** Introduced as an extension of world models, these explicitly link predicted future observations or states with executable actions, effectively functioning as a policy for embodied AI tasks.

#### 3.2. Design-Space View of World Models (Section 2)

The core of the methodology for classifying world models revolves around the space in which prediction occurs. A one-step world model is generalized as $y_{t+1} \sim p_\theta(\cdot | o_t, a_t)$, where $y_{t+1}$ is the prediction target.

- **Observation-space World Models:** These models predict future observations directly in their raw, high-dimensional observation space ($o_{t+1} \sim p_\theta(\cdot | o_t, a_t)$). The design space is characterized along two axes:
 - **Observation Spatial Explicitness:** Categorizes observations by their geometric information content, from RGB images (least explicit) to multi-view RGB, RGB-D images, and point clouds (most explicit). The tutorial discusses the trade-off between data availability and the richness of spatial information.
 - **Action Abstraction:** Classifies action representations by their level of detail and control, ranging from low-level robot commands (e.g., joint actions), to interface actions (e.g., camera controls), latent actions (learned from data), and high-level language instructions. The impact of action abstraction on the model's suitability for control, simulation, pretraining, or planning is analyzed.

- **State-space World Models:** These models first abstract raw observations into a more compact and structured state representation ($x_{t+1} \sim p_\theta(\cdot | o_t, a_t)$) and then predict future evolution in this state space. The categorization here is based on the type of state representation:
 - **Latent State Models:** Utilize latent vectors learned by neural networks, either directly from the target domain or extracted from pretrained visual/vision-language models. These are often used for reinforcement learning or model-predictive control.
 - **Point Track Models:** Represent states as trajectories of 2D or 3D points, capturing explicit motion while reducing irrelevant background variations. These are often conditioned on language instructions.
 - **Neural-Symbolic Models:** Employ grounded predicates as state representations, mapping visual observations to logical facts to support reasoning and long-horizon planning.
 - **Physical State Models:** Represent the world using explicit physical variables (e.g., object poses, velocities, contact states), incorporating human priors about physical evolution and often aligning with physics simulators for model-predictive control or synthetic data generation.

For each category, the tutorial discusses the specific design choices, their implications for learning complexity, task relevance, interpretability, scalability, and downstream usability.

#### 3.3. Taxonomy of World Action Models (Section 3)

The tutorial then focuses on how predicted futures are coupled with action generation, particularly for language-conditioned observation-space world models. Four representative paradigms are identified:

- **Imagine-then-execute:** A two-stage approach where a video prediction model generates visual subgoals, and a separate inverse dynamics model or goal-conditioned policy translates these subgoals into robot actions. The modularity, training data flexibility, and potential for intermediate quantity estimation are discussed, alongside the risk of error propagation.
- **Video-feature-conditioned Action Prediction:** This paradigm extracts intermediate spatiotemporal features from a video prediction model and uses these features to condition an action prediction module, avoiding full video decoding at inference time. The computational efficiency versus the reduced interpretability of latent feature interfaces is highlighted.
- **Joint Video-Action Modeling:** A single generative model learns a joint distribution over future observations and corresponding action sequences. This approach aims for consistency between visual futures and actions but requires action-labeled robot data and faces challenges in optimizing disparate objectives.
- **Auxiliary Video Prediction for Policy Learning:** Here, video prediction serves as an auxiliary training objective to encourage the policy backbone to encode spatiotemporal information, without explicit video generation during inference. This offers efficiency but relies on implicit transfer of predictive information and the balance of loss functions.

Throughout these classifications, the tutorial systematically identifies and discusses trade-offs (e.g., visual fidelity vs. computational cost, interpretability vs. efficiency, data requirements vs. model complexity), providing a comparative analysis of the different approaches.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

As a tutorial and review paper, this work's "findings" primarily consist of the structured conceptual framework and comprehensive categorization it presents for world and world action models in robotics. The main results are the clarity and organizational structure provided to a previously ambiguous field.

1. **Unified Definitions:** The tutorial establishes clear, operational definitions for "world," "embodied AI task," "world model," and "world action model." This addresses the initial ambiguity in terminology by providing a common language and conceptual scope for researchers. A world model is defined as an action-conditioned predictive model for future observations or states, while a world action model explicitly links these predictions to executable actions.

2. **Comprehensive Design-Space Taxonomy for World Models:**
 - **Observation-Space World Models:** The paper systematically categorizes these models based on two key dimensions:
 - **Observation Spatial Explicitness:** Ranging from RGB images (common, abundant data) to multi-view RGB, RGB-D, and point clouds (richer geometric information, but scarcer data). This categorization highlights a trade-off between visual fidelity/spatial understanding and data availability.
 - **Action Abstraction:** Spanning low-level robot actions (suitable for control/simulation) to interface actions, learned latent actions (useful for pretraining), and high-level language instructions (beneficial for planning). This demonstrates how action representation dictates the model's application and usability.
 - **State-Space World Models:** These models, which abstract observations into structured states, are categorized by their state representation type:
 - **Latent State Models:** Utilize neural network-encoded vectors, learned or pretrained, for compact simulation and control.
 - **Point Track Models:** Represent states as 2D/3D points, preserving motion information while ignoring background, useful for language-conditioned goal setting.
 - **Neural-Symbolic Models:** Employ grounded predicates for logical reasoning and long-horizon planning with high-level skills.
 - **Physical State Models:** Incorporate human physical priors via explicit physical variables for accurate simulation, control, and synthetic data generation. This section clarifies how different state abstractions manage the balance between compactness, semantic structure, physical interpretability, and scalability.

3. **Taxonomy of World Action Model Paradigms:** The tutorial identifies and elaborates on four distinct paradigms for integrating visual future prediction with action generation:
 - **Imagine-then-execute:** A modular two-stage approach separating visual planning and action grounding, offering flexibility but susceptible to error propagation between stages.
 - **Video-feature-conditioned Action Prediction:** Reduces computational load by using intermediate features from a video model to condition action prediction, balancing efficiency with interpretability.
 - **Joint Video-Action Modeling:** A unified generative model for both visual futures and actions, promoting consistency but requiring specific action-labeled robot data and careful optimization.
 - **Auxiliary Video Prediction for Policy Learning:** Utilizes video prediction as an auxiliary training signal to shape policy representations, improving inference efficiency by removing the prediction branch at test time, but relying on implicit information transfer. This classification articulates the various strategies and their inherent trade-offs regarding modularity, interpretability, computational expense, data requirements, and consistency.

4. **Identification of Key Trade-offs:** Across all categories, the tutorial consistently highlights fundamental trade-offs involved in designing world and world action models. These include, but are not limited to, the trade-off between high-dimensional visual fidelity and the complexity of dynamics learning, the balance between compact representations and semantic interpretability, and the tension between generalizability from large datasets and task-specific performance or physical accuracy.

In summary, the core result of this tutorial is the provision of a structured, comprehensive, and comparative framework that systematically organizes the diverse landscape of world and world action models in robotics. This framework clarifies existing methods and articulates the design considerations and associated trade-offs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This tutorial holds significance for the embodied AI and robotics research communities through several contributions:

1. **Conceptual Clarity and Unification:** By providing a clear and unified definition of "world models" and introducing "world action models," the tutorial addresses a prevalent ambiguity in the field. This clarity can facilitate more precise discussions, reduce misunderstandings, and establish a common vocabulary among researchers from diverse backgrounds (e.g., computer vision, reinforcement learning, robotics).

2. **Structured Understanding and Taxonomy:** The detailed design-space view and taxonomies of both observation-space and state-space world models, along with the categorization of world action model paradigms, offer a structured framework for understanding the vast array of existing methods. This organization can help new researchers navigate the literature and established researchers contextualize their work within a broader landscape.

3. **Guidance for Model Design and Selection:** The systematic analysis of trade-offs associated with different design choices (e.g., observation explicitness, action abstraction, state representation, prediction-action coupling) can serve as a guide for practitioners. It can inform decisions on which type of world or world action model is most appropriate for a given robotic task, considering factors like data availability, computational resources, desired interpretability, and required control fidelity.

4. **Facilitating Research and Development:** By clearly delineating existing approaches and their characteristics, the tutorial can highlight underexplored areas or specific challenges. This can stimulate new research directions, encourage hybrid approaches that combine strengths from different paradigms, and foster innovation in developing more capable and robust embodied AI systems. For instance, understanding the trade-offs in data requirements or the impact of action abstraction can guide efforts in scaling models or developing more generalist robot policies.

5. **Benchmarking and Comparison:** The structured classification provides a basis for more systematic comparisons between different world model and world action model architectures. Researchers can use this framework to identify commonalities and differences, leading to more rigorous benchmarking and evaluation methodologies.

6. **Advancing Embodied Intelligence:** Ultimately, a more organized and comprehensive understanding of world and world action models is foundational for advancing embodied intelligence. These models are critical components for intelligent agents to plan, adapt, and operate effectively in complex, dynamic environments. By clarifying their roles and designs, this tutorial contributes to building more autonomous and capable robotic systems that can perform complex tasks in the real world.
