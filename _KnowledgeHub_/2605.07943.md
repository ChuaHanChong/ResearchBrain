# TAVIS: A Benchmark for Egocentric Active Vision and Anticipatory Gaze in Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> TAVIS introduces a simulation-based benchmark for egocentric active vision and anticipatory gaze in imitation learning, offering a standardized platform to compare active vision policies and quantify their contributions. The benchmark reveals that active vision benefits are task-conditional, multi-task learning improves performance, but policies show limited generalization to out-of-distribution shifts, while implicitly acquiring human-like anticipatory gaze from demonstrations.

## Problem

- The lack of a shared, reproducible evaluation infrastructure for active-vision imitation learning, making systematic comparison of different systems challenging.
- Existing benchmarks primarily rely on fixed cameras, preventing controlled quantification of active vision's demonstrated benefits in visuomotor manipulation.
- A gap exists in current imitation learning benchmarks for metrics to assess anticipatory gaze, a crucial temporal and communicative aspect of human manipulation.

## Method

- Introduces **TAVIS**, a modular, ==simulation-based benchmark== with two task suites (TAVIS-Head for global search, TAVIS-Hands for local occlusion) implemented in IsaacLab.
- Employs a ==paired headcam-vs-fixedcam protocol==, using identical teleoperated trajectories to isolate and quantify active vision's specific impact.
- Develops **GALT** (==Gaze-Action Lead Time==), a novel kinematic metric, to quantify anticipatory gaze in learned policies, inferring lead times from proprioceptive data.

## Results

- Headcam policies achieved higher suite-mean success rates on TAVIS-Head compared to fixedcam policies (e.g., GR1T2: **47.1%** vs. **38.8%**), with up to **45 percentage points** improvement in tasks like `conditional-pick`.
- Multi-task training improved headcam policy success rates on TAVIS-Head from **32.7%** (single-task) to **43.0%**, and on TAVIS-Hands from **53.5%** to **73.4%** in-distribution.
- Learned multi-task headcam policies acquired anticipatory gaze, demonstrating Gaze-Action Lead Times (GALT) of approximately **2-3 seconds** that aligned with human teleoperator references within **7-10%** for most tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The utility of active vision is task-conditional; it yields substantial gains in information-gathering tasks but can sometimes degrade performance in fully observable scenarios by introducing nuisance variance.
> - Multi-task training consistently improves in-distribution performance for active vision policies, particularly on task suites with smaller individual datasets.
> - Imitation learning policies trained on TAVIS tasks exhibit limited extrapolation capabilities, experiencing significant performance degradation under out-of-distribution spatial and initial robot pose shifts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of visuomotor manipulation using imitation learning (IL) has seen significant advancements, with numerous benchmarks developed to facilitate progress. However, most existing benchmarks, such as LIBERO, RLBench, and CALVIN, primarily rely on fixed third-person or wide-angle cameras. Over the past year, an increasing number of independent research systems (at least eight, as cited by the authors) have demonstrated that enabling policies to control their own gaze—referred to as active vision—can substantially enhance manipulation performance. These systems encompass a range of active vision configurations, including high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) active necks, low-DoF stereo or eyeball heads, foveated vision, viewpoint-optimized vision, and bimanual wrist-driven cameras. The consensus from these studies is that active gaze provides unique information inaccessible to fixed cameras, leading to improved task execution.

Despite this convergence on the benefits of active vision, a critical gap exists: there is no shared, reproducible evaluation infrastructure specifically designed for active-vision imitation learning. Current active vision systems are often validated on custom tasks, hardware, and metrics, making meaningful comparison and comprehensive assessment of their contributions challenging. Existing active vision artifacts like EFM-10 are hardware-locked, limiting their reproducibility across different research groups. This absence of a standardized benchmark is identified as a bottleneck for the community to systematically evaluate active vision's utility across different task types and conditions.

Furthermore, active vision in manipulation is inherently a coupled perception-action problem. Gaze serves multiple functions beyond simple visual search, including clutter disambiguation, temporal monitoring, and the communication of intent. Cognitive science research on human manipulation and human-robot interaction (HRI) studies have consistently shown that human gaze proactively leads hand movements by hundreds of milliseconds, contributing to the legibility and predictability of actions. Existing IL benchmarks do not include metrics to quantify these temporal and communicative aspects of gaze behavior, focusing primarily on task completion.

TAVIS addresses these identified gaps by introducing a shared, simulation-based benchmark designed to enable fair comparison of active vision policies, quantify their contributions, and assess the temporal coordination of learned gaze behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to introduce TAVIS, a novel evaluation infrastructure for active-vision imitation learning, aimed at closing the identified gaps in the research landscape. This objective is driven by several detailed motivations:

- **Establish a Shared Benchmark for Active Vision IL:** The proliferation of active vision systems for robotic manipulation, coupled with the lack of a common evaluation platform, motivates the creation of TAVIS. The goal is to provide a standardized environment where different active vision approaches can be meaningfully compared, fostering cumulative research and identifying best practices.
- **Quantify Active Vision's Contribution:** The benefits of active vision are widely acknowledged, but the extent of these benefits, their task-dependency, and the conditions under which they are most pronounced remain underexplored due to the absence of controlled comparisons. TAVIS aims to enable researchers to quantify *what* active vision contributes, on *which task types*, and under *what conditions*.
- **Isolate Active Vision as a Controlled Variable:** Existing benchmarks conflate active vision with other aspects of manipulation. A key motivation for TAVIS is to allow for controlled experiments where active vision can be isolated and its impact precisely measured. This is achieved through a paired headcam-vs-fixedcam protocol, using identical demonstrations.
- **Introduce Metrics for Anticipatory Gaze:** Drawing from cognitive science and HRI literature, the authors note the importance of anticipatory gaze in human manipulation for task efficiency and communicating intent. Existing IL benchmarks lack metrics to assess whether learned policies acquire such sophisticated gaze behaviors. A core motivation for TAVIS is to introduce GALT (Gaze-Action Lead Time), a novel kinematic metric, to quantify anticipatory gaze in learned policies, thereby evaluating the temporal and communicative dimensions of gaze.
- **Assess Policy Generalization under Distribution Shifts:** Concerns about generalization versus memorization in IL benchmarks underscore the need for evaluating policies under varied conditions. TAVIS aims to provide structured in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) splits to robustly assess the extrapolation capabilities of active vision policies under controlled perturbations.
- **Provide Reproducible and Accessible Infrastructure:** To overcome the limitations of hardware-locked benchmarks, TAVIS is implemented purely in simulation using IsaacLab, ensuring that the benchmark is reproducible and accessible to a wide range of researchers without requiring specific physical robot setups.
- **Release Comprehensive Datasets and Baselines:** To facilitate immediate engagement and comparison, the authors aim to release all necessary components: code, evaluation scripts, a large dataset of teleoperated demonstrations (LeRobot v3.0), and trained baseline policies.

In summary, the key objectives are to provide a comprehensive, reproducible, and standardized evaluation platform (TAVIS) that enables controlled comparisons, measures nuanced gaze behaviors, and assesses generalization for active-vision imitation learning in robotic manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The TAVIS benchmark is designed as a modular and composable evaluation platform for egocentric active vision, implemented in simulation using IsaacLab and IsaacLab-Arena for ray-traced rendering.

#### 3.1. Task Suites

TAVIS features two complementary task suites, each designed to probe distinct aspects of active vision in manipulation:

- **TAVIS-Head (Global Active Vision):** This suite comprises five tasks that require active head movement for global search, clutter disambiguation, conditional information gathering, temporal monitoring, and vertical workspace scanning.
 - `conditional-pick`: The robot must look at a colored card to determine which of two objects to pick.
 - `wait-then-act`: The robot must monitor a status light, waiting for it to change color before grasping an object.
 - `clutter-pick-cube`: The robot must visually locate and pick a red cube among four distractor objects.
 - `clutter-pick-lift`: The robot is given a language prompt naming a target object among five, which it must locate and lift.
 - `multi-shelf-scan`: The robot must scan a three-shelf unit to locate and retrieve a target object specified by a language prompt.

- **TAVIS-Hands (Local Occlusion & Peeking):** This suite includes three tasks where head movement alone is insufficient, requiring wrist-mounted cameras for local perception, particularly when targets are occluded. Both arms are used jointly for perception and manipulation.
 - `peeking-box`: A target object is inside a box with one open side (randomized); wrist cameras must identify the open side to guide the correct hand for retrieval.
 - `occluded-reach`: A vertical screen blocks the head camera's view of a target object, necessitating wrist camera use to reach around the screen.
 - `blocked-clutter-pick-cube`: Similar to `clutter-pick-cube`, but the head camera is masked, forcing reliance on wrist cameras to locate and grasp the red cube.

#### 3.2. Robots

The benchmark supports two fixed-base humanoid torsos: GR1T2 (Fourier Intelligence) and Reachy2 (Pollen Robotics). Both robots have two 7-DoF arms and a 3-DoF neck. A unified 19-dimensional canonical action space is used, abstracting away robot-specific details and including per-arm inverse kinematics (IK) targets, 3-DoF neck control, and gripper commands. This standardization enables cross-embodiment evaluation.

#### 3.3. Demonstration Collection and Datasets

Demonstrations were collected by a single teleoperator using a Meta Quest 3 VR headset. The operator's head movements controlled the robot's neck, and controllers drove end-effectors. Crucially, each episode was simultaneously recorded from a head-mounted camera and a second fixed workspace camera. This paired recording yields identical trajectories under both camera views, which is foundational for the headcam-vs-fixedcam comparison.
The datasets are released as four Hugging Face repositories (one per suite × robot combination), totaling approximately 2200 episodes. The dataset format is LeRobot v3.0, including synchronized RGB streams, proprioceptive state, canonical actions, and language instructions.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Protocol

TAVIS provides three controlled evaluation axes:

1. **Paired Headcam vs. Fixedcam Comparison:** This is the central primitive. Policies are trained and evaluated using either the agent-controlled head-mounted camera feed or the static fixed workspace camera feed, derived from the *same* teleoperation trajectories. This allows isolation of active vision as a controlled variable. For TAVIS-Hands tasks, the fixedcam condition is omitted as the head camera and fixed camera are structurally uninformative by design.

2. **ID and OOD Distribution Splits:** To assess generalization, the benchmark includes:
 - `id` (in-distribution): Samples within the same range as training data.
 - `ood-spatial`: Expands the distribution of initial object positions beyond the training range.
 - `ood-init-pose`: Perturbs the robot's reset pose with Gaussian noise on end-effector Cartesian positions and neck yaw/pitch.

3. **GALT (Gaze-Action Lead Time):** This novel kinematic metric quantifies anticipatory gaze.
 - **Definition:** GALT is defined as `t_hand - t_head`, where `t_hand` is the time of grasp completion and `t_head` is the time the head-mounted camera reaches its final pre-grasp fixation. A positive GALT indicates anticipatory gaze. This metric is grounded in cognitive science, where human gaze precedes action, and HRI, where anticipatory gaze contributes to action legibility.
 - **Detection:** Both `t_hand` and `t_head` are inferred from proprioceptive data (commanded action trajectories) alone, making the metric portable to real-robot evaluations. `t_hand` is the latest gripper-command transition, and `t_head` is the arrival of the lowest-velocity neck fixation within a backward search window from `t_hand`.

Evaluation success is reported as a success rate (SR) over 96 evaluation episodes, with GALT specifically reported for successful TAVIS-Head episodes.

#### 3.5. Baselines and Training Details

The authors trained two baseline policies using LeRobot:
- **π0 (Black et al.):** Trained at both single-task and suite-multi-task scopes, fine-tuned from a pretrained model.
- **Diffusion Policy (Chi et al.):** Trained single-task only, and only on non-language-prompted tasks.
Each policy was trained for both headcam and fixedcam observation modes, where applicable.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted using TAVIS investigated four key questions regarding active-vision imitation learning, employing Diffusion Policy and π0 as baselines.

**Q1: How much does active vision help, and on which task types?**
- **TAVIS-Head:** Active vision generally demonstrated benefits, but these were task-conditional. At the suite-mean level, headcam policies outperformed fixedcam policies on both robots (GR1T2: 47.1% vs. 38.8%; Reachy2: 38.8% vs. 13.3%).
 - The largest benefits were observed in tasks requiring explicit information gathering, such as `conditional-pick` (+28 percentage points (pp) for GR1T2, +45pp for Reachy2), where policies use gaze to interpret cues.
 - Conversely, for tasks like `wait-then-act` on GR1T2, performance regressed with active vision (-23pp), suggesting that head motion introduced nuisance variance in an already fully observable workspace.
- **TAVIS-Hands:** For tasks specifically designed for local occlusion, where head/fixed cameras are inherently uninformative, policies leveraging wrist cameras (the native TAVIS-Hands setup) achieved 70-77% in-distribution success rates.
 - An ablation study on `clutter-pick-cube` demonstrated that going from no-active vision (fixed-cam) to full active vision (head + wrist) yielded a +19pp increase in success (45.9% vs. 26.6%). Wrist-only vision (head blocked) achieved 63.0%, sometimes exceeding full-AV, which the authors attribute to demonstration design emphasizing exploratory wrist trajectories under occlusion.

**Q2: To what extent does multi-task training help versus single-task training?**
- Multi-task training, using the π0 baseline, consistently improved performance compared to single-task training for headcam policies on in-distribution splits.
 - On TAVIS-Head, the suite-mean success rate increased from 32.7% (single-task) to 43.0% (multi-task).
 - On TAVIS-Hands, the suite-mean success rate rose from 53.5% (single-task) to 73.4% (multi-task), with larger gains observed on the suite with smaller individual task datasets. These findings align with prior observations regarding the benefits of multi-task learning in imitation learning.

**Q3: What is the impact of distribution shift during evaluation?**
- Policies exhibited substantial degradation under controlled out-of-distribution (OOD) shifts.
 - For TAVIS-Head headcam policies, the suite-mean success rate dropped from 43.0% (ID) to 25.0% (ood-spatial) and 6.6% (ood-init-pose).
 - For TAVIS-Hands, the suite-mean success rate decreased from 73.4% (ID) to 50.0% (ood-spatial) and 26.4% (ood-init-pose).
 - The `ood-init-pose` perturbation led to a more severe collapse in TAVIS-Head headcam performance compared to TAVIS-Head fixedcam and TAVIS-Hands, consistent with head-pose changes primarily affecting policies that rely on head-tracked visual observations. These results suggest that policies trained via imitation learning on TAVIS tasks demonstrate limited extrapolation capabilities to unseen spatial or initial robot configurations.

**Q4: Do policies acquire anticipatory gaze from imitation alone?**
- Multi-task π0 headcam policies acquired anticipatory gaze patterns comparable to the human teleoperator reference in TAVIS-Head tasks.
 - Policies demonstrated Gaze-Action Lead Times (GALT) where gaze preceded grasp by approximately 2–3 seconds.
 - Pooled median GALT values for policies aligned with the dataset reference within approximately 180 milliseconds on a 2.1–2.7 second scale.
 - Per-task median GALT differences (`|∆median|/median_dataset`) remained within 7–10% for four out of five tasks. The `multi-shelf-scan` task showed a larger deviation (approximately 20%), which the authors attribute to variability in per-shelf fixation rather than an absence of GALT.
- This finding indicates that the temporal coupling between gaze and action, including anticipatory behavior, can be implicitly acquired through imitation learning from expert demonstrations.

**Headcam Teleoperation Bias Investigation:**
- An investigation into the potential bias introduced by training fixedcam policies on demonstrations from head-teleoperated trajectories (which inherently include "look-then-reach" pauses) revealed that this bias actually *aided* fixedcam learning.
- For the `wait-then-act` task on GR1T2, a fixedcam-only teleoperation ablation resulted in 39.6% ID success, compared to 52.1% for the standard fixedcam policy (trained on head-teleop trajectories) and 63.5% for the headcam policy. The total 24pp head-vs-fixedcam-only gap decomposed into approximately 11pp from observation-time headcam access and approximately 13pp from the trajectory-time head-teleop bias. This suggests that the reported headcam advantages in Q1 are conservative estimates.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The TAVIS benchmark introduces a standardized, reproducible evaluation infrastructure that addresses several identified bottlenecks in active-vision imitation learning. Its significance and potential impact span multiple areas:

- **Enabling Fair Comparison:** By providing two complementary task suites, two humanoid torso embodiments, and a unified action space within a simulation environment (IsaacLab), TAVIS offers a common platform for researchers to compare different active-vision policies fairly. This can accelerate progress by identifying effective approaches and highlighting areas requiring further research, moving beyond isolated system comparisons.
- **Quantifying Active Vision Contributions:** The paired headcam-vs-fixedcam evaluation protocol is a core contribution that allows for the precise quantification of active vision's benefits across various task types and conditions. This can lead to a deeper understanding of *when* and *how much* active vision helps, guiding the design of more efficient and capable robotic systems.
- **Advancing Gaze-Action Coordination Research:** The introduction of GALT (Gaze-Action Lead Time) is a significant step towards incorporating cognitively-inspired metrics into robot learning. By quantifying anticipatory gaze, TAVIS enables the study of not just task success, but also the human-like legibility and temporal coordination of learned robot behaviors. This can foster research into policies that are not only effective but also safer and more intuitive for human interaction.
- **Stress-Testing Generalization:** The inclusion of ID/OOD distribution splits provides a mechanism to systematically evaluate the robustness and generalization capabilities of active vision policies. The observed sharp degradation under OOD shifts highlights a critical area for future research in developing more robust and adaptable robot learning algorithms.
- **Accessibility and Reproducibility:** Being entirely simulation-based, TAVIS eliminates hardware-specific barriers, making the benchmark accessible to a wider research community. The public release of code, evaluation scripts, comprehensive datasets, and trained baselines ensures high reproducibility of results, promoting transparency and collaborative development.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The modular and compositional design of TAVIS makes it extensible. It explicitly supports the addition of new robot embodiments, tasks, and OOD perturbation axes (e.g., visual textures, lighting, object semantics, language-prompt variations). This provides a robust foundation for exploring advanced topics such as post-hoc gaze relabeling for existing datasets, sim-to-real transfer for active vision tasks, and foveated vision systems.

**Limitations and their implications for impact:**
While significant, the paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Simulation-only:** The current setup remains in simulation, implying a sim-to-real gap that requires future bridging. This limits immediate real-world deployment but enables broad research access.
- **Single operator teleoperation:** All demonstrations were collected by one operator, which ensures consistency but might embed operator-specific biases in gaze and pacing. This suggests future work could explore operator variability or alternative teleoperation strategies.
- **Restricted active-vision configurations:** TAVIS focuses on commodity pan/tilt necks and wrist cameras, not the higher DoF necks or eye-gimbal configurations explored elsewhere. This indicates the benchmark's scope, but its modularity allows for future expansion.
- **GALT scope and proxy for gaze:** GALT is a post-hoc metric reliant on successful episodes and head orientation as a gaze proxy. Its direct optimization requires careful consideration to avoid unnatural motions. This highlights the ongoing need for nuanced metric development in robot learning.
- **Limited OOD axes and scene templates:** Current OOD splits target specific spatial and pose perturbations, but real-world distribution shifts are richer. This means current generalization findings are specific to these shifts, but the framework is designed to incorporate more complex OOD scenarios.

Overall, TAVIS represents a concrete step toward standardizing evaluation in a rapidly growing area of robot learning. By providing a comprehensive, reproducible, and extensible framework with novel metrics, it aims to clarify the benefits of active vision, guide the development of more robust policies, and foster interdisciplinary research between robotics, cognitive science, and human-robot interaction.
