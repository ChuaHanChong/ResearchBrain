# Motion-Acceleration Calibration and Compensation in IMUs without External Equipment for Attitude Estimation Filters

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Julius-Maximilians-Universität Würzburg introduced a method for calibrating the extrinsic displacement of off-centered IMUs and compensating for motion-induced accelerations without external equipment. This technique enhances attitude estimation filter accuracy by providing cleaner gravity vector measurements, reducing reliance on gyroscope integration and improving LiDAR point cloud quality in dynamic robotic systems.

## Problem

- IMUs mounted off the center of rotation in mobile systems experience centripetal and tangential accelerations, contaminating accelerometer readings and distorting the perceived gravity vector.
- Current attitude estimation filters manage motion by increasing reliance on gyroscope data, leading to long-term drift and cumulative errors in orientation estimation.
- Robust calibration of off-centered IMU displacement vectors and explicit motion acceleration compensation are uncommon in robotics, often requiring specialized external equipment.

## Method

- A multi-stage methodology combines ==intrinsic IMU calibration== with an ==equipment-free multi-position method== to estimate biases, scales, misalignments, and the extrinsic displacement vector to the system's center of rotation.
- Online motion compensation numerically subtracts ==centripetal and tangential accelerations== from accelerometer measurements, utilizing gyroscope data and a ==Derivative-of-Gaussian (DoG) kernel== for stable angular acceleration estimation.
- An optimization problem is formulated to find the displacement vector by minimizing the error between the compensated accelerometer magnitude and the known local gravity magnitude.

## Results

- Monte-Carlo simulations showed high calibration accuracy with a median position error of **2.6 cm** and a rotation error of **0.0091 degrees** for the displacement vectors.
- Using synthetic data, motion compensation significantly reduced rotation errors for state-of-the-art attitude estimation filters (Autogain, QEKF, Mahony) compared to uncompensated results.
- Real-world evaluation on a spherical mobile mapping system demonstrated qualitatively improved LiDAR point clouds, which were "more structured and resemble the true geometry of the environment" with compensation versus "significantly distorted" without it.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Actively compensating for motion-induced accelerations provides cleaner gravity vector measurements, allowing attitude filters to continuously trust accelerometer data and significantly mitigate gyroscope drift during dynamic maneuvers.
> - The Derivative-of-Gaussian (DoG) kernel provides a numerically stable and robust method for deriving angular acceleration from noisy gyroscope measurements, essential for accurate compensation without amplifying noise.
> - The extrinsic displacement vector of an off-centered IMU, critical for motion compensation, can be accurately calibrated without specialized external equipment through an optimization approach based on gravity magnitude.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Inertial Measurement Units (IMUs) are foundational sensors for attitude estimation in mobile systems, providing measurements of angular velocity via gyroscopes and acceleration (including gravity) via accelerometers. A persistent challenge in attitude estimation arises when an IMU is in motion: additional accelerations, specifically centripetal and tangential forces, are introduced into the accelerometer readings. This effect becomes more pronounced with increasing distance between the IMU and the system's center of rotation.

Traditional approaches to mitigate this issue often involve modifying attitude estimation filters (e.g., Madgwick or Mahony filters) to rely more heavily on gyroscope integration during periods of high angular velocity. However, this strategy is known to accumulate long-term drift, particularly affecting the estimation around the gravity vector. Historically, the recognition and compensation of motion-induced accelerations have been more prevalent in fields like aerospace and military engineering, as exemplified by NASA's work on V/STOL aircraft in 1969 or military technical reports on cruise missiles in 1979. These efforts involved estimating and subtracting motion-related forces, sometimes utilizing external trajectory data or multiple symmetrically placed IMUs. More recent work in UAVs has also explored calculating centripetal acceleration based on turn radius and airspeed to correct accelerometer data.

Within the robotics community, explicit motion acceleration compensation remains less common. This has been attributed to the comparatively lower precision of micro-electro-mechanical systems (MEMS) gyroscopes, which are often used in robotics, and a perceived lack of motivation for systems where IMU displacement or angular velocity magnitudes are small. However, numerous robotic applications, such as spherical mobile mapping systems, robotic arms, or bio-inspired robots, feature rigidly mounted IMUs at significant distances from their centers of rotation, leading to non-negligible motion acceleration effects.

Existing calibration methods for IMUs often focus on intrinsic parameters (bias, scale, misalignment). For instance, Tedaldi et al. (2014) introduced a robust method for intrinsic calibration without external equipment, relying on a multi-position data collection approach. Some specialized approaches exist for calibrating non-centered IMUs, sometimes requiring external equipment like servos to induce known rotations or precise turntables for lever-arm parameter estimation. More comprehensively, Zhang et al. (2022) proposed a method to optimize 45 intrinsic and extrinsic parameters simultaneously, though this approach can be sensitive to initial guesses and parameter tuning. Notably, the LiDAR-Inertial Odometry (LIO) community also briefly considered centripetal and tangential acceleration compensation in early work like DLIOM (Chen et al., 2023 preprint), but this aspect was subsequently removed in published versions, possibly due to challenges associated with numerically deriving angular acceleration from noisy gyroscope measurements.

This work addresses the gap in the robotics community by proposing a generalized, robust, and accessible method for both calibrating the extrinsic displacement vector of an off-centered IMU and compensating for motion-induced accelerations without requiring external equipment. By providing corrected accelerometer measurements, the aim is to enhance the accuracy of any attitude estimation filter that relies on the local gravity vector, thereby mitigating the drift associated with gyroscope-heavy estimation during dynamic maneuvers.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation for this research stems from the impact of motion-induced accelerations on IMU-based attitude estimation. When a mobile system moves, its rigidly mounted IMU experiences accelerations beyond gravity, including centripetal and tangential components. These additional accelerations contaminate the accelerometer readings, leading to an inaccurate measurement of the local gravity vector. The magnitude of this effect is directly correlated with the angular velocity of the system and the distance of the IMU from the system's center of rotation.

Current state-of-the-art attitude estimation filters typically attempt to manage this issue by reducing their reliance on accelerometer data during dynamic periods, instead increasing the weight given to gyroscope integrations. While this strategy prevents direct corruption from motion accelerations, it introduces a vulnerability to gyroscope drift, leading to cumulative errors over time, particularly in the estimated orientation relative to the gravity vector.

The primary objective of this work is to overcome this limitation by actively compensating for these motion-induced accelerations. Specifically, the authors aim to:

- **Utilize Gyroscope Measurements for Compensation:** Employ the IMU's gyroscope readings to predict and subtract the centripetal and tangential accelerations from the accelerometer measurements. This would effectively isolate the gravity component, providing a cleaner input for attitude filters.
- **Develop an Equipment-Free Calibration Method:** Introduce a robust calibration procedure to accurately estimate crucial intrinsic IMU parameters (e.g., axes misalignment, bias, scale) and, significantly, the extrinsic displacement vector between the IMU and the system's center of rotation. This calibration is designed to be performed without the need for external, specialized equipment, enhancing its practicality and accessibility. For multi-IMU systems, the method also aims to recover relative sensor orientations.
- **Improve Attitude Filter Accuracy:** Demonstrate that the proposed motion compensation significantly enhances the accuracy of various state-of-the-art attitude estimation filters that rely on the direction of gravity. This addresses the long-term drift problem by allowing filters to continuously trust the (corrected) accelerometer measurements.
- **Enable Use in Constrained Systems:** Provide a solution for systems where IMU placement at the center of rotation is impractical or impossible due to design constraints (e.g., spherical mobile mapping systems, robotic end-effectors).

The specific contributions outlined in the paper are:
1. A robust method for calibrating intrinsic scale, misalignment, and bias parameters of IMU accelerometer and gyroscope triads, as well as displacement vectors to the center of rotation and relative sensor orientations in multi-IMU systems, without external equipment.
2. A numerically stable method for online motion acceleration compensation that subtracts centripetal and tangential terms from accelerometer measurements using gyroscope data.
3. An evaluation of the calibration and compensation method using synthetic, semi-synthetic, and real-world datasets, demonstrating improved accuracy for state-of-the-art attitude estimation filters.
4. The release of the calibration and simulation software, alongside calibration datasets, to promote further research and adoption.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a multi-stage approach, combining intrinsic and extrinsic IMU calibration with a numerically stable method for online motion acceleration compensation. The overall goal is to correct accelerometer measurements by removing motion-induced accelerations, leaving primarily the gravity component, which improves downstream attitude estimation.

**Data Collection Strategy:**
The data collection for calibration follows a "multi-position method" inspired by. It involves:
1. Placing the IMU system statically for a moderate duration (approximately 5 seconds).
2. Manually rotating the IMU system into a different orientation.
3. Repeating this process for 36 to 50 distinct orientations, each held for at least 5 seconds.
During this process, the system is expected to primarily experience rotations around a common center point, though the method is noted to be robust against minor deviations.

**IMU Intrinsic Calibration:**
Before motion compensation, the IMU triads (accelerometer and gyroscope) must provide measurements in a shared, consistent coordinate system. This is achieved through intrinsic calibration, which models sensor readings as:
- `a = M_a * S_a * (hat_a - b_a)` for accelerometers.
- `omega = M_omega * S_omega * (hat_omega - b_omega)` for gyroscopes.
Here, `hat_a` and `hat_omega` are raw sensor readings, `b_a` and `b_omega` are biases, `S_a` and `S_omega` are diagonal scale matrices, and `M_a` and `M_omega` are misalignment matrices. The method from is applied to estimate these intrinsic parameters using the multi-orientation dataset.

**Derivation of Motion Compensation Model:**
The fundamental principle for motion compensation is derived from rigid body dynamics. Let `r` be the offset vector from the center of rotation to the IMU sensor, and `omega` be the angular velocity. The linear velocity of the sensor `v(t)` is `omega(t) x r(t)`. The linear acceleration `a_motion` acting on the sensor (excluding gravity) is then:
`a_motion = d(omega)/dt x r + omega x (omega x r)`
An accelerometer measures the sum of external specific forces and the negative of gravity. Therefore, the goal is to obtain `a_g`, the measurement due to gravity alone:
`a_g = a_motion - a_raw = (omega x (omega x r)) + (d(omega)/dt x r) - a_raw`
where `a_raw` is the raw accelerometer measurement. This equation forms the basis for compensation.

The paper also briefly discusses an extended model for spherical systems rolling without slippage, which considers additional linear accelerations due to the ball's center motion. However, this extended model requires an estimate of the surface normal in the local sensor frame and is indicated as a subject for future work. The core methodology primarily utilizes the simpler rigid body model for compensation.

**Motion Compensation Calibration (Extrinsic):**
This step estimates the extrinsic parameter, specifically the displacement vector `r`, from the system's center of rotation to the IMU. The calibration is based on the premise that after correctly subtracting motion-induced accelerations, the magnitude of the compensated accelerometer measurement should equal the known magnitude of local gravity (`||g||`).
An optimization problem is formulated to find `r`:
`r = argmin_r (sum_{n=1 to N} ( ||g|| - ||omega_n x (omega_n x r) + d(omega_n)/dt x r - a_n||_2 )^2)`
where `omega_n` and `a_n` are intrinsically calibrated gyroscope and accelerometer measurements at time `n`. The optimization problem is solved using the Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) algorithm, implemented with automatic differentiation (AD) in Ceres, to find the `r` that minimizes the sum of squared errors in magnitude.

**Implementation of Discrete Derivative:**
A critical component for both calibration and online compensation is the accurate numerical differentiation of noisy gyroscope measurements to obtain angular acceleration `d(omega)/dt`. Naive finite difference methods amplify noise. To address this, the authors employ a Derivative-of-Gaussian (DoG) kernel, which combines smoothing and differentiation through convolution.
- **Kernel Definition:** The DoG kernel `h_1[k]` is derived from the first derivative of a Gaussian function, parameterized by a standard deviation `sigma` related to a cutoff frequency `f_cut`.
- **Offline (Calibration):** A non-causal convolution `(d(x)/dt)[n] = sum_{k=-K to K} (h_1[k] * x[n-k])` is used, providing a smooth, zero-phase, zero-delay derivative.
- **Online (Compensation):** A causal convolution `(d(x)/dt)[n] = sum_{k=-K to K} (h_1[k] * x[n-k-K])` is used for real-time processing. This introduces an inherent delay of `K` samples, which is a trade-off for noise reduction. A cutoff frequency of 20 Hz is selected for online processing, balancing phase shift and noise gain.
The DoG method is compared against finite differences, EMA+FD, and algebraic differentiators, demonstrating its effectiveness in suppressing noise gain.

**Multi-IMU Extrinsic Calibration:**
For systems with multiple IMUs, an additional step ensures all sensor measurements are expressed in a common coordinate system. This involves finding the relative rotation `R_ref_i` from each IMU `i` to a designated reference IMU. Only gyroscope data is used for this, as accelerometers alone cannot resolve rotation around the gravity vector. The optimization problem is:
`R_ref_i = Exp(argmin_theta sum_{n=1 to N} ( ||R(theta) * omega_i_n - omega_ref_n||_2 ))`
where `omega_i_n` and `omega_ref_n` are intrinsically calibrated gyroscope measurements. The rotation `R(theta)` is parameterized using axis-angle representation `theta` and the exponential map `Exp`. Once `R_ref_i` is found, the individual displacement vectors `r_i` (recovered from motion compensation calibration) are transformed into the reference frame: `r_ref_i = R_ref_i * r_i`. This yields a full `SE(3)` transformation `T_i` for each IMU relative to the reference sensor.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed motion-acceleration calibration and compensation method was evaluated using a combination of synthetic, semi-synthetic, and real-world datasets.

**Simulation-Based Calibration Accuracy (Monte-Carlo):**
A Monte-Carlo simulation was conducted to quantitatively assess the calibration accuracy. This involved 2000 simulated IMU calibrations using semi-synthetic data, where real-world gyroscope measurements drove a simulated rigid body system with known IMU placements and orientations.
- **Position Error:** The median error in recovering the ground truth displacement vector was found to be 2.6 cm.
- **Rotation Error:** The median error in recovering the ground truth rotation (relative orientation) was 0.0091 degrees.
These results indicate that the method can accurately recover the rigid body transformations of each sensor, although the authors acknowledge that these estimates might be influenced by the characteristics of the real-world angular velocities used for generating the semi-synthetic data.

**Simulation-Based Attitude Estimation Improvement:**
The impact of motion compensation on downstream attitude estimation was evaluated using fully synthetic data. A trochoidal motion model, mimicking a point inside a rolling ball, generated realistic sensor trajectories. Simulated accelerometer and gyroscope measurements (including bias, noise, drift, and scale errors) were then fed into three state-of-the-art attitude estimation filters:
1. **Autogain filter:** Specialized for spherical systems.
2. **Quaternion-based Extended Kalman Filter (QEKF).**
3. **Mahony filter.**
Each filter processed data both with and without the proposed motion compensation (using the calibrated displacement vector).
- **Result:** The rotation errors of all three attitude estimators were significantly reduced when motion compensation was applied. Without compensation, especially under high angular velocities, the estimators rapidly accumulated errors because both noisy gyroscope integration and uncorrected accelerometer gravity measurements were unreliable. The compensation method provided cleaner accelerometer data, allowing the filters to maintain accuracy.

**Real-World Evaluation (Spherical Mobile Mapping System):**
The method was tested on a prototype spherical mobile mapping system equipped with symmetrically placed Phidget Spatial 3/3/3 IMUs and a Hesai Pandar-XT32 laser scanner.
- **Extrinsic Calibration Results:** The calibrated displacement vectors for the on-board IMUs showed differences of 1.46 cm, 0.63 cm, and 2.03 cm when compared to the CAD model specifications.
- **Accelerometer Measurement Magnitude:** Real-world data showed that the magnitude of compensated accelerometer measurements was consistently closer to the magnitude of local gravity (9.81 m/s²) compared to uncompensated measurements. This was observed across different configurations: a single off-centered IMU, a single approximately centered IMU, and an average of multiple symmetrically placed IMUs. The authors noted that compensated real-world measurements still deviated more from ideal gravity magnitude than in simulation, attributing this possibly to unmodeled non-linearities in the accelerometer sensors.
- **Impact on LiDAR Point Cloud Quality (Qualitative):** The most significant qualitative impact was observed by processing LiDAR point clouds using attitude estimates derived with and without motion compensation. The Autogain filter was used for attitude estimation.
 - **Single Off-Centered IMU:** Without compensation, point clouds were "significantly distorted." With compensation, the point clouds became "more structured and resemble the true geometry of the environment," indicating a more accurate attitude estimate.
 - **Single Approximately Centered IMU:** Similar improvements were observed, with compensated data leading to better-structured point clouds compared to distorted ones from uncompensated data.
 - **Multiple Symmetrically Placed IMUs (Averaged):** While averaging inherently provides some degree of motion acceleration cancellation due to symmetry, applying the proposed compensation method still yielded a "small but still observable" improvement. A detailed comparison highlighted that the compensated point clouds exhibited overall less angular error.

These results collectively demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed calibration and compensation method across different evaluation scenarios, indicating improved IMU measurement quality and, consequently, more accurate attitude estimation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of inertial sensing and attitude estimation, particularly for mobile robotic systems where IMU placement constraints are common. The work addresses a long-standing challenge by providing a robust and practical method for mitigating the impact of motion-induced accelerations on accelerometer measurements.

The key significance and potential impacts include:

- **Enhanced Attitude Estimation Accuracy:** By actively compensating for centripetal and tangential accelerations, the method provides cleaner gravity vector measurements to downstream attitude estimators. This directly reduces the reliance on gyroscope integration during dynamic motions, thereby mitigating the accumulation of long-term drift that often plagues IMU-based systems. The consistent improvement observed across various state-of-the-art filters (Autogain, QEKF, Mahony) underscores its broad applicability and effectiveness.
- **Broader Applicability for Off-Centered IMUs:** The method is particularly beneficial for systems where design constraints necessitate placing IMUs away from the center of rotation. This includes diverse applications such as spherical mobile mapping systems, robotic arms/grippers, bio-inspired mobile robots, rotary-wing UAVs, and in-orbit spacecraft. It removes a significant barrier to achieving accurate attitude estimation in such configurations.
- **Accessibility through Equipment-Free Calibration:** A notable aspect of this work is the ability to calibrate both intrinsic IMU parameters and the extrinsic displacement vector without requiring any specialized external equipment. This lowers the entry barrier for implementation, making the method more accessible to researchers and developers who may not have access to high-precision calibration setups. The use of a simple multi-position data collection approach further enhances its practicality.
- **Improved LiDAR-Inertial Odometry and Mapping:** For systems that integrate IMU data with other sensors, such as LiDAR in mobile mapping, the improved attitude estimates directly translate to better registration of point clouds and reduced geometric distortion in the reconstructed environment. This was qualitatively demonstrated with the spherical mobile mapping system, showing clearer and more structured point clouds.
- **Foundation for Multi-IMU Systems:** The method provides tools for calibrating relative orientations and compensating individual IMU measurements in multi-IMU setups. Even when relying on the inherent self-cancellation effects of symmetrically placed IMUs, the proposed compensation still offers observable improvements, suggesting its value even in redundant sensor architectures.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The commitment to open-sourcing the calibration and simulation software, along with real-world datasets, is a valuable contribution to the research community. This promotes reproducibility, facilitates comparative studies, and encourages further development and adoption of the method.

While the work addresses a critical problem, the authors identify areas for future research. These include investigating unmodeled sensor non-linearities (suggested by discrepancies between real-world and simulated results), developing robust methods for estimating surface normals required by the extended model for spherical systems, and exploring tighter integration of the compensation terms directly within Kalman filter frameworks. Nevertheless, this work provides a robust, practical, and broadly applicable solution for more accurate attitude estimation in mobile systems with off-centered IMUs, which is expected to have a lasting impact across various robotics and navigation domains.
