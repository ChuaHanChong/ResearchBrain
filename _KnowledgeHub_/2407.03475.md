# How JEPA Avoids Noisy Features: The Implicit Bias of Deep Linear Self Distillation Networks

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Apple theoretically analyze why Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) often outperform Masked Autoencoders (MAE) in self-supervised learning for perception tasks, demonstrating that JEPA implicitly prioritizes features predictive of target representations, whereas MAE focuses on features with high covariance. The analysis shows JEPA's beneficial bias strengthens with increasing network depth.

## Problem

- Empirical evidence shows JEPA-based self-supervised learning methods often perform better on downstream perception tasks than Masked Autoencoders (MAE).
- The underlying theoretical reasons for this performance discrepancy have not been clearly understood.
- A fundamental lack of clarity existed regarding the implicit biases of MAE and JEPA paradigms.

## Method

- The researchers at Apple employed a tractable model using ==deep linear== neural networks to analyze the learning dynamics of each paradigm.
- They derived analytical solutions for the gradient flow of both JEPA and MAE objective functions within this simplified model.
- The analysis characterized the learning dynamics in terms of data covariance matrix eigenvalues (λi) and regression coefficients (ρi) between features and target outputs.

## Results

- Analytical solutions show JEPA's learning dynamics for each feature depend on both covariance (λi) and regression coefficient (ρi) with the relationship λi1/Lρi1−1/L\lambda_i^{1/L} \rho_i^{1-1/L}λi1/Lρi1−1/L.
- MAE's learning dynamics are primarily governed solely by feature covariance (λi).
- Numerical simulations and theoretical derivations confirm that as network depth increases, JEPA increasingly prioritizes features with high regression coefficients, while MAE continues to prioritize high-variance features, with JEPA's dynamics almost entirely dependent on ρi in very deep networks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - JEPA objectives are implicitly biased towards learning "influential features" characterized by high regression coefficients (ρᵢ), which indicate predictive power.
> - MAE objectives are implicitly biased towards learning "highly covarying features" based on data covariance (λᵢ), which may include noisy or less informative signals.
> - The preference of JEPA for influential features becomes more pronounced and stronger as the network depth (L) increases, explaining its robustness to noisy details.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Self-supervised learning (SSL) has become a crucial paradigm in machine learning, allowing models to learn meaningful representations from unlabeled data. Two prominent approaches within SSL are Masked Autoencoders (MAE) and Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA). While both approaches have demonstrated success, empirical evidence suggests that JEPA-based methods often perform better on downstream perception tasks, but the theoretical reasons for this difference have remained unclear.

#### 1.1. Self-Supervised Learning Paradigms

Self-supervised learning aims to learn meaningful representations without labeled data. Two dominant approaches have emerged:

1. **Masked Autoencoders (MAE)**: These methods reconstruct masked or missing parts of input data from observed portions. Examples include BERT in natural language processing and various vision models that reconstruct missing image patches. The objective function for MAE typically minimizes the reconstruction error between the original input and the reconstructed output.

2. **Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA)**: These methods learn representations by predicting latent representations of semantically similar views of the data. A key subset of JEPA is self-distillation, where an online encoder learns to predict the output of a target encoder for different views of the same data. Notable implementations include SimCLR, BYOL, and Barlow Twins.

The fundamental distinction between these approaches lies in their objectives: MAE reconstructs raw input directly, while JEPA operates in the latent space, predicting representations rather than raw input.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

This paper by researchers at Apple presents a theoretical analysis that uncovers the implicit biases of these two SSL paradigms, explaining their fundamental differences and why JEPA tends to excel at perception tasks. By examining the learning dynamics of deep linear networks, the authors demonstrate that JEPA prioritizes "influential features" with high regression coefficients, while MAE focuses on features with high covariance, which may include irrelevant or noisy signals.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

To analyze the differences between JEPA and MAE, the authors introduce a tractable model - a deep linear neural network - that enables a complete characterization of training dynamics. The model consists of:

- A data source that produces samples x ∈ ℝⁿ from a multivariate Gaussian distribution
- A target encoder that maps x to y₀ = Wx, where W is a fixed linear transform
- An online (trainable) encoder composed of L linear layers: f(x) = W₁W₂...W₁x

For a deep linear network with L layers, the output is:
```
f(x) = W₁W₂...W₁x
```

The authors define two objective functions:
1. JEPA objective: minimizes the mean squared error between the online encoder's output and the target encoder's output.
2. MAE objective: minimizes the reconstruction error of the input x.

#### 3.1. Learning Dynamics Analysis

The authors derive analytical solutions for the learning dynamics of both JEPA and MAE objectives in the context of deep linear networks. By using gradient flow equations, they describe how network weights evolve during training.

When features are simultaneously diagonalizable (a common simplification for theoretical analysis), the dynamics can be expressed in terms of the eigenvalues (λᵢ) of the data covariance matrix and the regression coefficients (ρᵢ) between features and target outputs.

For the JEPA objective in a network with L layers, the learning dynamics for each feature i are governed by:

$$\frac{d}{dt}W_i(t) \propto \lambda_i^{1/L} \rho_i^{1-1/L}$$

For the MAE objective, the dynamics are:

$$\frac{d}{dt}W_i(t) \propto \lambda_i$$

These equations reveal that JEPA's learning rate for each feature depends on both covariance (λᵢ) and regression coefficient (ρᵢ), while MAE's learning rate depends only on covariance.

#### 3.2. Critical Time Analysis

The authors introduce the concept of "critical time" - a measure of how quickly each feature is learned during training. By comparing the critical times for different features under JEPA and MAE objectives, they quantify how each objective prioritizes features.

For a feature with eigenvalue λᵢ and regression coefficient ρᵢ:

- JEPA's critical time scales as: t_c(i) ∝ -log(λᵢ¹/ᴸρᵢ¹⁻¹/ᴸ)
- MAE's critical time scales as: t_c(i) ∝ -log(λᵢ)

These expressions confirm that:
1. MAE learns features in order of decreasing variance (λ)
2. JEPA considers both variance and regression coefficient, with the influence of regression coefficients increasing with network depth

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The core finding of the paper is that JEPA and MAE exhibit fundamentally different implicit biases:

1. **JEPA prioritizes "influential features"**: JEPA objectives are biased toward learning features with high regression coefficients (ρᵢ), which serve as a proxy for the predictive power of a feature. This bias becomes stronger as network depth increases.

2. **MAE prioritizes "highly covarying features"**: MAE is primarily driven by the covariance of input features (λᵢ), even if these features are noisy or less informative for downstream tasks.

This distinction explains why JEPA may be better suited for perception tasks - it naturally prioritizes features that are most predictive of the target representation, filtering out noisy or irrelevant information that may dominate in terms of variance.

#### 4.1. Depth Dependence

A key insight from the analysis is that the implicit bias of JEPA becomes more pronounced as network depth increases. In a shallow network (L=1), JEPA and MAE have identical learning dynamics. However, as L increases, JEPA's preference for features with high regression coefficients becomes stronger.

For very deep networks (L→∞), JEPA's learning dynamics depend almost entirely on the regression coefficients:

$$\frac{d}{dt}W_i(t) \propto \rho_i$$

The authors validate this theoretical finding through numerical simulations, showing that deeper networks increasingly prioritize features with high regression coefficients, even if they have lower variance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper provides a theoretical foundation for understanding the empirical success of JEPA-based self-supervised learning methods compared to MAE approaches. By analyzing the learning dynamics of deep linear networks, the authors identify distinct implicit biases in these two SSL paradigms:

- JEPA prioritizes features with high regression coefficients (influential features)
- MAE prioritizes features with high covariance (potentially including noisy features)
- The beneficial bias of JEPA becomes stronger as network depth increases

These findings explain why JEPA-based methods often excel in downstream perception tasks - they naturally focus on learning abstract, semantic features while filtering out task-irrelevant details. The mathematical framework presented in this paper not only explains existing empirical observations but also provides guidance for designing more effective self-supervised learning methods in the future.

The work highlights the importance of understanding the implicit biases of learning objectives, as these biases significantly impact what features a model learns, regardless of the specific architecture or optimization algorithm used. By leveraging these insights, researchers can develop self-supervised learning approaches that more efficiently capture semantically meaningful representations from unlabeled data.

#### 5.1. Implications for Self-Supervised Learning

The theoretical findings have several important implications for self-supervised learning:

1. **Feature Prioritization**: JEPA's ability to prioritize influential features explains why it may learn more meaningful representations for downstream tasks compared to MAE, which might waste capacity on high-variance but irrelevant features.

2. **Slow Feature Hypothesis**: The results align with the "slow feature hypothesis" in neuroscience, which suggests that meaningful features tend to vary slowly over time. JEPA prioritizes features with high regression coefficients, which often correspond to slowly varying, semantically meaningful attributes.

3. **Avoiding Noisy Details**: MAE's focus on high-variance features makes it susceptible to learning fine-grained, potentially noisy pixel information, while JEPA naturally avoids this by prioritizing features that are most predictive of the target representation.

4. **Depth Matters**: The analysis suggests that deeper networks amplify JEPA's beneficial bias, which may explain why deep architectures have been particularly successful in self-supervised learning approaches based on JEPA.
