# Benchmarking In-Hand Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at KTH Royal Institute of Technology, University of Utah, Yale University, and Google AI developed a standardized, platform-agnostic benchmarking scheme for robotic in-hand manipulation. This framework defines three levels of task difficulty and comprehensive quantitative error metrics for object pose and contact points, demonstrated with initial performance baselines on multi-fingered hands and parallel grippers.

## Problem

- The lack of standardized evaluation methods hindered systematic comparison of diverse robotic in-hand manipulation techniques and heterogeneous hardware platforms.
- Evaluating contact-rich tasks like in-hand manipulation in physical, real-world scenarios was challenging due to the complexities of real-world interactions and the absence of universal benchmarks.
- Existing manipulation benchmarks were often limited in scope, lacking comprehensive qualitative error metrics or universal applicability across different manipulation platforms (e.g., simple grippers vs. dexterous hands).

## Method

- A ==standardized, platform-agnostic benchmarking protocol== was established for in-hand manipulation, designed to be adaptable to various robotic systems, from simple grippers to multi-fingered hands.
- ==Three distinct levels of task difficulty== were defined: achieving a desired hand pose, achieving a desired contact region, or achieving both, catering to different research foci and system capabilities.
- A comprehensive set of ==quantitative error metrics== was proposed for object pose (position and orientation) and contact regions (==Euclidean and geodesic distances==), alongside a protocol for recording planning and execution times, and failure rates.

## Results

- For Level I tasks (desired hand pose only), the relaxed-rigidity method achieved a **0% object drop rate** and the lowest median position error of **1.32 cm**, outperforming other methods with drop rates up to **9%**.
- Baseline results for Level III tasks (desired hand pose and contact points) using a parallel gripper demonstrated high planning accuracy, with orientation errors as low as **0.016%** and Euclidean contact region errors around **0.663 cm** for certain objects.
- The demonstration highlighted significant variations in planning times among methods (e.g., **0.3s** for relaxed-position-orientation vs. **14.8s** for Point-contact) and differing success rates, providing initial performance indicators for the benchmark.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A formal, platform-agnostic benchmarking scheme can effectively standardize the evaluation of in-hand manipulation, enabling rigorous comparisons across diverse robotic hardware and algorithmic approaches.
> - Detailed metrics for both object pose and contact region errors, including normalized values and geodesic distances, provide a granular and objective assessment of manipulation capabilities.
> - Prioritizing physical robot execution in the benchmark is crucial for validating contact-rich manipulation methods, while still accommodating simulation-based evaluations through object meshes and task definitions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic in-hand manipulation, which involves changing the grasp on an object without releasing it, has seen increasing interest and advancements in recent years. Researchers have demonstrated various manipulation skills on diverse robotic platforms, ranging from simple grippers to anthropomorphic hands. This proliferation of methods and hardware has created a challenge for systematic comparison and evaluation, as individual research laboratories often find it complex to conduct thorough comparisons across different approaches and hardware setups.

Existing benchmarking efforts in robotics include examples for evaluating grasp planning, primarily in simulated environments. However, for contact-rich tasks like grasping and in-hand manipulation, the authors argue for the fundamental importance of evaluating system performance in physical, real-world scenarios. Previous work, such as the manipulation tasks proposed by Rajeswaran et al., primarily focused on simulated dexterous hands, offered tasks that might not be universally practical for all manipulation platforms (e.g., parallel grippers), and did not propose comprehensive qualitative error metrics for system capabilities.

This work addresses these gaps by proposing a standardized benchmarking scheme for in-hand manipulation that is intended to be adaptable to various robotic systems. It emphasizes physical robot execution while also being applicable to simulation and planning algorithm evaluation. By providing a common set of tasks, quantitative metrics, and supporting software, this research aims to establish a consistent framework for researchers to evaluate and compare new methodologies in a more standardized manner, thereby facilitating progress and comparability within the in-hand manipulation community.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to establish a benchmarking scheme to evaluate the planning and control capabilities of robotic in-hand manipulation systems. The overarching goal is to assess a system's proficiency in altering the pose of a hand-held object through the use of fingers, interaction with the environment, or a combination of both.

The motivation for developing this benchmark stems from several identified needs within the robotics research community:

- **Lack of Standardization:** The rapid development of diverse in-hand manipulation methodologies and the use of heterogeneous robotic hardware platforms (e.g., simple grippers, multi-fingered dexterous hands, multi-arm setups) have made it difficult for researchers to conduct direct and standardized comparisons of their proposed techniques. This diversity complicates the assessment of relative performance and progress.
- **Emphasis on Real-World Performance:** For tasks involving intimate physical contact, such as grasping and in-hand manipulation, the authors contend that evaluating performance in a physical, real-world environment is crucial. While simulation provides a valuable testing ground, real-world complexities like friction, compliance, and perception noise often differ significantly. The benchmark therefore prioritizes physical execution but remains adaptable for simulated evaluations.
- **Comprehensive and Platform-Agnostic Tasks:** Existing benchmarks for manipulation often lack the breadth or flexibility to accommodate all types of manipulation platforms or tasks. This work aims to define a series of in-hand manipulation tasks that are platform-agnostic, enabling evaluation across different robot geometries and capabilities without being overly restrictive.
- **Quantitative and Qualitative Metrics:** Beyond defining tasks, a core motivation is to propose robust, quantitative metrics that accurately measure the error in reaching desired goal states. These metrics are designed to assess both object pose and contact point accuracy, providing a detailed understanding of system performance. They also account for the initial setup variation and normalize errors for better comparability.
- **Reproducibility and Accessibility:** To encourage widespread adoption and facilitate comparative analysis, the authors are motivated to provide all necessary supporting materials. This includes software, task examples, and initial evaluation results, all based on widely available resources like the Yale-CMU-Berkeley (YCB) object and model set. This provision aims to lower the barrier for researchers to participate in the benchmarking process.
- **Establishing Baselines:** By demonstrating the application of the benchmark with existing methods, the authors aim to establish initial performance baselines. These baselines can then serve as reference points for future comparisons and advancements in the field.

In summary, the motivation is to create a formal, robust, and accessible framework that enables consistent evaluation and comparison of robotic in-hand manipulation systems, thereby accelerating research and development in this domain.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology establishes a protocol for benchmarking in-hand manipulation capabilities. The objective is to assess how well a system can execute goal-directed in-hand manipulation tasks, defined as altering an object's grasp without releasing it onto a support surface.

**Grasp Definition:**
A grasp (G) is formally defined by two components:
- The hand's pose (H) relative to the object's reference frame.
- The contact points (P) established between the hand and the object.
The protocol allows for adaptation of specific hand poses and contact points to accommodate different robotic hardware, focusing on the relative change between initial and desired states.

**Task Description:**
An in-hand manipulation task is characterized by:
- An initial contact region (C_i) and a desired contact region (C_d), defined as meshes on the object's surface (using YCB object meshes).
- An initial grasp pose (H_i) and a desired grasp pose (H_d) for the hand relative to the object.
Initial (P_i) and final (P_d) contact points must reside within their respective regions, allowing for hardware-specific adjustments. The benchmark emphasizes achieving a specific relative transformation (T = H_d * H_i⁻¹) between the initial and desired hand poses.

**Three Levels of Task Difficulty:**
To cater to varying system capabilities and research foci, three levels of task completion are defined:
- **Level I:** The objective is solely to achieve the desired hand pose (H_d). Contact regions are not constrained.
- **Level II:** The objective is solely to achieve the desired contact region (C_d). Hand poses are not constrained.
- **Level III:** The objective is to achieve both the desired contact region (C_d) and the desired hand pose (H_d).

**Procedure for Each Task:**
1. **Initialization:** The object is manually or autonomously placed into the robot's hand at the initial grasp (P_i within C_i). The actual initial hand pose (ˆH_i) is recorded. A trial is discarded if the position or orientation error between ˆH_i and the specified H_i from the dataset exceeds 10%.
2. **Execution:** The in-hand manipulation method is executed to move the object towards the desired configuration (C_d, P_d, and H_d).
3. **Recording:** The final reached hand pose (H_r) and contacts (P_r) are recorded, along with planning and execution times.

**Setup Description:**
- **Objects:** Tasks are defined for objects from the YCB dataset, which provides both physical objects and 3D meshes for consistency across research groups.
- **Hand Poses:** Example H_i and H_d are provided for typical robot hands. Researchers adapt these to their specific robot's kinematics, ensuring the required relative transformation T is maintained.
- **Contact Regions:** Provided as surface meshes, allowing researchers to define specific contact points within them if their method requires.

**Robot/Hardware Requirements:**
The benchmark targets manipulation systems equipped with grippers, multi-fingered hands, or multiple arms configured for dexterous manipulation. A perception system is necessary to estimate the initial and final poses of the object and the robot hand. Meshes of the robot's hand links are required for computing contact-related error metrics.

**Execution Constraints:**
- **Autonomy:** No human intervention is permitted during the planning and execution phase after initial object placement.
- **Stability:** The object must be stably grasped at both initial and final configurations, meaning the hand pose relative to the object remains constant for 5 seconds without external support.
- **Manipulation Definition:** Regrasping (repeated pick-and-place) is not considered in-hand manipulation. However, utilizing external surfaces for pushing or dynamic manipulation (tossing and catching) is allowed, provided continuous contact with the robot is maintained during external interaction, or no external contact occurs during free flight. A second robot hand can provide support but not for inter-hand regrasping.

**Scoring and Metrics:**
Each grasp set is run five times per object. Error metrics quantify the difference between the reached grasp (G_r = [H_r, P_r]) and the desired grasp (G_d = [H_d, C_d]).
- **Hand Pose Error (for Levels I and III):**
 - `err_pos`: Euclidean distance between desired (s_d) and reached (s_r) hand positions.
 - `err_pos%`: Normalized position error, expressed as a percentage of the distance between initial and desired hand positions.
 - `err_or%`: Orientation error, computed using quaternion distances as a percentage.
- **Contact Region Error (for Levels II and III):**
 - These metrics quantify the largest distance between the robot's contact-making links and the desired contact region (C_d).
 - `G_euc`: Euclidean distance.
 - `G_geo`: Geodesic distance (shortest path on the object surface).
 - **Algorithm 1** is provided to compute these metrics by projecting desired contact regions and robot link meshes onto the object mesh, identifying intersecting vertices, and then calculating the maximum shortest distance from the reached contact vertices to the desired contact region vertices.
 - `G_min`: Euclidean distance between the two closest vertices on the object mesh, providing context for the granularity of contact error.
- **Timing and Failures:** Planning and execution times are recorded. The percentage of unsuccessful executions, along with failure causes, is reported.
- **Visualization:** Box plots are recommended for presenting aggregated error results.

**Reporting and Comparison:**
Researchers are required to report details of their robotic platform, experimental setup, object weights, and perception system characteristics. Submitted data should include initial/desired grasp sets and the computed error metrics. Comparison is primarily made between methods addressing the same task levels and using similar hardware, though trade-offs between hardware complexity and accuracy can also be considered. The benchmark acknowledges the difficulty of identifying an "absolute best" system due to heterogeneity, instead aiming to enable identification of the "relative best" for specific applications.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents demonstration results for Level I and Level III tasks, serving as baselines for future comparisons.

**A. Level I Task Demonstration (Desired Hand Pose Only)**
This demonstration compared five in-hand manipulation methods that aim to repose a grasped object without breaking or making new contacts:
- Relaxed-rigidity
- Relaxed-position
- Relaxed-position-orientation (all three from Sundaralingam and Hermans)
- IK-rigid (assumes a rigid contact model)
- Point-contact (assumes fixed contact position with friction)

**Setup Details:**
- Experiments were conducted using an Allegro multi-fingered hand attached to a box frame.
- Ten objects from the YCB dataset (apple, banana, gelatin box, Lego Dublo, fork, orange, airplane toy, Phillips-head screwdriver, spatula, tuna fish can) were used.
- ARUCO markers and an ASUS XTION camera provided object and hand tracking.
- A human initialized objects, and a rigid transformation accounted for human setup error.
- For each object, two initial grasps and five desired grasps were defined. Each generated trajectory was executed five times, totaling 50 executions per object and 500 trajectories per method.
- Goal positions ranged from 0.8 cm to 8.33 cm (mean 4.87 cm) relative to initial positions. Goal orientations ranged from 1.53% to 30.7% (mean 11.96%).

**Results:**
- **Planning Time (Median):** Point-contact (14.8s), IK-rigid (4.4s), relaxed-position (0.5s), relaxed-position-orientation (0.3s), relaxed-rigidity (0.5s).
- **Execution Time:** Fixed at 1.67s as trajectories were executed without replanning.
- **Offline Planning Error (Fig. 6):** The IK-rigid method exhibited difficulty in reaching the desired object position during planning, attributed to the problem being over-constrained. Consequently, this method was not evaluated on the real robot.
- **Real Robot Execution Errors (Fig. 7, Table II):**
 - **Object Drops:** The relaxed-rigidity method achieved a 0% object drop rate across all 500 trials. In contrast, point-contact, relaxed-position, and relaxed-position-orientation methods had drop rates of 5%, 9%, and 7%, respectively.
 - **Position Error (`err_pos`):** Relaxed-rigidity exhibited the lowest median position error (1.32 cm) across all objects. Point-contact, relaxed-position, and relaxed-position-orientation had median position errors of 1.69 cm, 1.64 cm, and 1.54 cm, respectively. The relaxed-rigidity method also demonstrated lower variance in final position errors.
 - **Normalized Position Error (`err_pos%`):** Relaxed-rigidity had a median of 28.67%. Point-contact, relaxed-position, and relaxed-position-orientation had 36.81%, 30.95%, and 29.19% respectively.
 - **Orientation Error (`err_or%`):** Relaxed-rigidity achieved a median orientation error of 9.86%. Point-contact, relaxed-position, and relaxed-position-orientation had median errors of 9.74%, 10.43%, and 9.84% respectively.
 - **Initial Setup Error:** The median errors between the initial planned pose and the human-set initial pose were consistent across methods, ranging from 0.47 cm to 0.58 cm for position and 3.19% to 3.55% for orientation. Contact point metrics were not applicable for Level I tasks.

**B. Level III Task Demonstration (Desired Hand Pose and Contact Points)**
This demonstration utilized the Dexterous Manipulation Graph (DMG) method, a planner for in-hand manipulation designed for parallel grippers. The focus was on evaluating the planned solution for tasks requiring both desired hand pose and specific contact points.

**Setup Details:**
- An ABB Yumi smart gripper was used.
- Tasks were defined using selected contact regions from the protocol, along with corresponding H_i and H_d.
- Specific initial (P_i) and desired (P_d) contact points were defined to suit the gripper's two-contact-point per hand structure.

**Results (Table III):**
The planned solutions for four different YCB objects (gelatin box, cracker box, spatula, potted meat can) were evaluated:
- **Position Error (`err_pos`):** Ranged from 0.267 cm (cracker box) to 0.610 cm (potted meat can).
- **Normalized Position Error (`err_pos%`):** Ranged from 2.7% (cracker box) to 11.0% (potted meat can).
- **Orientation Error (`err_or%`):** Ranged from 0.016% (gelatin box) to 0.049% (potted meat can).
- **Euclidean Contact Region Error (`G_euc`):** Ranged from 0.663 cm (potted meat can) to 1.125 cm (gelatin box).
- **Geodesic Contact Region Error (`G_geo`):** Ranged from 0.663 cm (potted meat can) to 1.505 cm (spatula).
- **Minimum Vertex Distance (`G_min`):** Ranged from 0.004 cm to 0.057 cm, indicating the precision context for contact error.
- **DMG Offline Computation Time:** Ranged from 10.312s to 18.295s, representing the time to build the graph structure.
- **Planning Time (Online):** Very low, ranging from 6.7e-05s to 0.023s, reflecting the efficiency of path finding once the graph is constructed.

These results illustrate the application of the proposed metrics and provide initial performance indicators for different in-hand manipulation methods under the benchmark's protocol.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This benchmarking scheme for in-hand manipulation offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotics:

- **Standardization of Evaluation:** The primary significance lies in providing a standardized and universally applicable protocol for evaluating in-hand manipulation systems. This addresses the current fragmentation in methodologies and hardware, enabling consistent comparison across diverse research groups and robotic platforms (e.g., grippers, multi-fingered hands, multi-arm systems).
- **Enhanced Comparability and Reproducibility:** By defining common tasks, a shared object dataset (YCB objects), and a comprehensive set of quantitative metrics, the benchmark fosters direct and objective comparisons between different in-hand manipulation techniques. The provision of supporting software and example results further enhances reproducibility, allowing researchers to build upon and validate existing work.
- **Comprehensive Performance Assessment:** The introduction of detailed error metrics, including position, orientation, and both Euclidean and geodesic contact region errors (with context from `G_min`), allows for a more granular and accurate assessment of system performance. The normalized error metrics (`err_pos%`, `err_or%`) provide context independent of the absolute displacement, aiding in meaningful comparisons.
- **Focus on Robustness:** The requirement of multiple trials per task and explicit reporting of failure rates and causes promotes a focus on the robustness and reliability of manipulation methods, which is critical for real-world robotic applications.
- **Facilitation of Research and Development:** By establishing clear baselines and a structured evaluation process, the benchmark can accelerate research progress. New methods can be rigorously tested against established performance levels, highlighting their strengths and weaknesses and guiding future development efforts.
- **Guidance for System Design and Selection:** The framework allows for the evaluation of diverse hardware. By explicitly acknowledging that an "absolute best" system may not exist due to the heterogeneous nature of robotics, the benchmark empowers users to identify the "relative best" system for their specific application requirements, considering factors like hardware complexity versus accuracy. This can inform design choices and practical deployment decisions.
- **Bridge Between Simulation and Reality:** While primarily advocating for physical execution, the benchmark's compatibility with object meshes and common simulators allows for flexible evaluation, serving as a bridge between simulated development and real-world validation for contact-rich tasks.

In conclusion, this benchmarking scheme is a foundational effort that provides the tools and guidelines necessary to bring greater rigor, consistency, and comparability to the study and development of robotic in-hand manipulation, ultimately contributing to the advancement and practical application of dexterous robotics.
