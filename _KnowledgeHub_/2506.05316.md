# Improving Data Efficiency for LLM Reinforcement Fine-tuning Through Difficulty-targeted Online Data Selection and Rollout Replay

> [!summary] Summary
> This research introduces a method to enhance the data efficiency of Large Language Model (LLM) reinforcement learning fine-tuning by combining difficulty-targeted online data selection and rollout replay. The approach reduces total training time by an average of 40.7% while achieving comparable or superior performance on mathematical and scientific reasoning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning for Large Language Models (LLMs) is computationally very expensive, demanding significant GPU resources and time.
- Existing on-policy RL methods for LLMs are data inefficient, often discarding valuable generated rollouts after a single training step.
- The high cost hinders research iteration speed and limits broader accessibility to developing advanced LLM capabilities.

## Method

- A ==Difficulty-targeted Online Data Selection (DOTS)== framework that adaptively predicts the optimal difficulty (failure rate near 0.5) for questions using a lightweight attention-based model and a small reference set.
- A ==Rollout Replay (RR)== mechanism that reuses previously generated rollouts from a FIFO buffer for on-policy GRPO training, using importance sampling to maintain stability.
- The combined DOTS and RR approach is integrated within the ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== framework, commonly used for LLM reasoning tasks.

## Results

- The combined DOTS+RR method reduced total RL fine-tuning time by an average of **40.7%** (ranging from **23%** to **62%**) across various LLMs and datasets, achieving comparable or better performance.
- DOTS alone enabled convergence with **13.33%** to **56.67%** fewer training steps by increasing the ratio of informative "effective questions" in each batch by an average of **25.4%**.
- Rollout Replay reduced per-step training time by **11-13%** by reusing past rollouts, without compromising the stability or final performance of the fine-tuning process.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Training samples with an adaptive difficulty (failure rate) around 0.5 yield the most effective learning signal, maximizing the expected squared norm of the policy gradient.
> - Adaptive difficulty can be accurately and efficiently predicted using an attention-based framework, avoiding costly full rollouts for all questions.
> - Rollout replay, when combined with importance sampling, can stably and effectively reduce the per-step computational cost in on-policy LLM RL fine-tuning without performance degradation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Large Language Models (LLMs) has seen significant advancements, with reinforcement learning (RL) emerging as a prominent paradigm for fine-tuning these models, particularly to enhance their reasoning capabilities. Algorithms such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) have become standard for this purpose. However, a primary challenge in LLM RL fine-tuning is its substantial computational cost, which often involves extensive GPU hours, even for moderately sized models and datasets.

Despite continuous algorithmic improvements in RL fine-tuning, the aspect of *data efficiency* has received comparatively less attention. Existing work has largely focused on enhancing the RL algorithms themselves or scaling models and datasets, rather than optimizing the utility of the training data. This paper positions itself by addressing this identified gap, emphasizing that reducing computational expense requires not only better algorithms but also more efficient use of data.

The paper draws inspiration from two established concepts in general reinforcement learning:
1. **Online Data Selection:** The principle that not all training examples contribute equally to learning, and prioritizing informative samples can accelerate training. In traditional machine learning, this has led to various online methods that adapt data selection to the model's evolving state.
2. **Experience Replay:** A technique in traditional RL that mitigates data inefficiency and instability in on-policy algorithms by storing and reusing past transitions in a buffer, allowing for multiple passes over collected data.

This work specifically adapts and extends these concepts to the context of LLM RL fine-tuning, proposing mechanisms to reduce both the number of training steps required to achieve target performance and the computational cost per training step. By focusing on data efficiency, the paper aims to contribute to making LLM RL fine-tuning more scalable and accessible, particularly for complex reasoning tasks that demand extensive training resources.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to improve the data efficiency of Large Language Model (LLM) reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning. This overarching goal is decomposed into two distinct, but complementary, sub-objectives:

1. **Reduce the number of training steps required to achieve a target performance level.** This objective is motivated by the observation that in RL, not all training samples provide an equally valuable learning signal. Tasks that are either excessively easy or exceedingly difficult for the current policy tend to yield weak or uninformative gradients, thereby slowing down convergence. An adaptive mechanism is necessary to focus on data that maximizes learning progress as the policy evolves throughout training.
2. **Reduce the per-step computational cost of RL fine-tuning.** This objective is driven by the fact that generating rollouts (i.e., sampling responses from the LLM and evaluating them with a reward model) constitutes a significant portion of the computational burden in each RL training step. High-profile studies have quantified this expense, showing that even relatively small LLMs require thousands of GPU hours for fine-tuning on modest datasets. Decreasing the resources needed for each training iteration would directly translate to overall cost and time savings.

To achieve these objectives, the authors propose two main techniques:

- **Difficulty-targeted Online Data Selection (DOTS):** The motivation here is rooted in the curriculum learning principle, where selecting training examples of appropriate difficulty can optimize learning. The core idea is to identify and prioritize questions that are of *moderate adaptive difficulty* for the LLM's current policy. Such questions are hypothesized to offer the most salient learning signals, preventing the model from wasting computation on already mastered tasks or those currently beyond its comprehension. The challenge lies in efficiently estimating this "adaptive difficulty" without incurring prohibitive computational costs, as direct measurement requires costly rollouts for every question.

- **Rollout Replay (RR):** This component addresses the per-step computational cost. Rollout generation involves computationally intensive forward passes through the LLM. By reusing previously generated rollouts, instead of generating a fresh set for every policy update, the system can reduce the demand for new computations. This approach is inspired by experience replay in traditional RL, which has proven effective in improving sample efficiency and stabilizing training by allowing agents to learn from past interactions. The motivation is to adapt this concept to the LLM context, thereby lowering the wall-clock time per training step.

In combination, DOTS aims to accelerate convergence (fewer steps), and RR aims to reduce the time spent per step, collectively leading to a substantial reduction in total RL fine-tuning time and associated resource consumption.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method integrates two techniques: Difficulty-targeted Online Data Selection (DOTS) and Rollout Replay (RR), aiming to reduce both the number of training steps and the per-step computational cost in LLM RL fine-tuning based on the GRPO algorithm.

#### 3.1. Problem Setup: GRPO

The foundational algorithm for fine-tuning in this work is Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which uses verifiable rewards. For each question `q`, a group of `G` responses are sampled from an `old policy` (π_old). The advantage `Âi` for the `i`-th response is calculated by normalizing group-level binary rewards `ri ∈ {0, 1}`. The standard deviation normalization term, present in some GRPO formulations, is removed based on prior work indicating it can introduce bias. The GRPO objective `J_GRPO(θ)` involves a clipped policy update term and a KL penalty `DKL(πθ || πref)` against a fixed reference policy. In standard GRPO, training questions are uniformly sampled for each batch.

#### 3.2. Difficulty-targeted Online Data Selection (DOTS)

DOTS aims to reduce the number of training steps by prioritizing questions that are most informative for learning.

##### 3.2.1. Adaptive Difficulty Definition

The authors define the *adaptive difficulty* `d(t)q` of a question `q` at training step `t` as the average failure rate of the current policy `πt` on that question. Specifically, `d(t)q = (1/G) * Σ(1 - r(t)i)`, where `r(t)i` is the binary reward for the `i`-th response. A higher value indicates greater difficulty for the current policy. This measure is dynamic, evolving with the policy's capabilities.

##### 3.2.2. Challenge of Efficient Difficulty Estimation

Directly computing adaptive difficulty for all questions in the dataset is computationally expensive, as it requires generating multiple rollouts for each question. To circumvent this, an attention-based prediction framework is introduced.

##### 3.2.3. Attention-based Adaptive Difficulty Prediction Framework

- **Reference Set:** At each step `t`, a small subset of `K` questions (e.g., 128 or 256) is uniformly sampled from the full training dataset `D` to form a *reference set* `Dref`.
- **Ground-Truth Difficulty for Reference Set:** For each question in `Dref`, rollouts are executed using the current policy `πt`, and its ground-truth adaptive difficulty `d(t)i` is computed using the definition above.
- **Embedding Model:** A lightweight embedding model `Eθ` (frozen Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct backbone with a 3-layer MLP adapter) is used to encode questions.
- **Similarity-Weighted Prediction:** For the remaining `N − K` unlabeled questions, their embedding `zq` is computed. The adaptive difficulty `ˆd(t)q` is then estimated as a similarity-weighted average of the ground-truth difficulties of the reference questions. The similarity scores `ai` are computed using a softmax over dot products between the unlabeled question's embedding and the reference questions' embeddings.
- **Calibration:** Platt scaling is applied to the predicted difficulty `ˆd(t)q` using mean and standard deviation statistics from the reference set difficulties. A lightweight MLP produces scale and bias parameters `(w(t), b(t))` which are used in a sigmoid transformation to yield the calibrated predicted difficulty `ˆd(t)q,cal`. The MLP is optimized using binary cross-entropy loss.

##### 3.2.4. Selection Strategy

Based on the efficiently estimated adaptive difficulties, DOTS prioritizes questions whose predicted difficulty is closest to 0.5. This strategy is motivated by the insight that questions of moderate difficulty provide the most informative learning signals. A theoretical justification (Theorem 1) states that the expected squared norm of the policy gradient is maximized when the reward success rate (and thus adaptive difficulty) is 0.5. This dynamic selection implicitly promotes diversity by rotating out questions as they become too easy or too hard for the evolving policy.

#### 3.3. Rollout Replay (RR)

RR aims to reduce the per-step computational cost associated with rollout generation.

- **Mechanism:** At each training step, new rollouts are generated for only a fraction `δB` of the batch size `B` (e.g., `δ=0.5`). The remaining `(1 − δ)B` samples are filled with recent rollouts retrieved from a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) replay buffer `Dreplay` of capacity `C`.
- **Off-Policy Correction:** Reusing past rollouts introduces an off-policy issue. To address this, a modified GRPO loss `J_GRPO-RR(θ)` is used, incorporating importance sampling with respect to the `behavior policy` `πθ_behavior` under which the buffered rollouts were originally collected. The probability ratio `˜r_i,t(θ)` includes the behavior policy term in its denominator.
- **Buffer Management:** Newly generated rollouts are stored in the `Dreplay` buffer only if their group average reward is not exactly 0 or 1, meaning they yielded a non-zero gradient signal. When the buffer is full, the oldest samples are discarded.

In summary, the methodology combines an efficient, adaptive data selection strategy to accelerate convergence with a rollout replay mechanism to reduce the computational burden of each training step, thus improving overall data efficiency for LLM RL fine-tuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted across six LLM-dataset combinations, utilizing Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B, Qwen2.5-3B, and Qwen2.5-Math-7B models fine-tuned on MATH, DeepScaleR-40K, Open-Reasoner-Zero-57K (ORZ), and DeepMath-103K datasets. Performance was evaluated using the average accuracy across four mathematical reasoning benchmarks: GSM8K, MATH500, Minerva Math, and OlympiadBench. The baseline for comparison was the original GRPO algorithm with uniform batch selection.

#### 4.1. Overall Training Efficiency

- **Reduction in Training Steps:** The proposed method (DOTS+RR) consistently achieved the same performance level as the original GRPO but required substantially fewer training steps. Reductions ranged from 13.33% to 56.67% across various LLM-dataset combinations. This indicates that DOTS successfully accelerates convergence by focusing on informative samples.
- **Reduction in Per-Step Computational Cost:** Rollout generation was identified as a major component of per-step time (46-54% depending on model size). The Rollout Replay (RR) strategy, by reducing the fraction of fresh rollouts (`δ=0.5`), led to an 11%–13% reduction in per-step training time.
- **Total Training Time Reduction:** Combining these two effects, DOTS+RR reduced the total RL fine-tuning time by an average of 40.7% (ranging from 23% to 62%) compared to the original GRPO. The largest improvement was observed on Qwen2.5-3B trained on DeepMath, with a 61.65% reduction. Performance curves demonstrated that DOTS+RR maintained higher accuracy than GRPO throughout training, reaching GRPO's final accuracy in less wall-clock time.

#### 4.2. Effectiveness of Adaptive Difficulty Prediction Framework

- **Accuracy of Difficulty Prediction:** The attention-based adaptive difficulty prediction framework demonstrated a Pearson correlation (ρ) greater than 0.7 between predicted and ground-truth adaptive difficulties across all experimental settings. This indicates that the framework can reliably track the evolving difficulty of questions relative to the policy's current capabilities.
- **Selection of Informative Samples:** The framework effectively filtered out uninformative samples. DOTS selected an average of 25.4% more "effective questions" (those with adaptive difficulties strictly between 0 and 1, yielding non-zero gradient signals) compared to the original GRPO. This supports the hypothesis that prioritizing moderately difficult questions enhances learning signals.
- **Computational Overhead:** The prediction framework incurred minimal computational overhead, processing 10,000 samples in approximately 1.71 seconds, primarily due to caching question embeddings and using a lightweight encoder.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies and Further Analysis

- **Individual Contributions:**
 - **DOTS (alone):** When DOTS was applied without RR, it yielded a steeper learning curve and achieved faster convergence (fewer training steps) compared to the original GRPO. This confirms its role in accelerating learning.
 - **RR (in conjunction with DOTS):** Adding RR to DOTS further reduced the total training time by approximately 20% without degrading model performance. This indicates that DOTS and RR provide complementary benefits.
- **Comparison to External Difficulty Baseline:** DOTS consistently outperformed an online data selection baseline that used static external difficulty labels (e.g., GPT-4o-mini annotations) and a staged curriculum. This highlights DOTS's adaptability and independence from expensive, manually tuned external labeling.
- **Generalization to Non-Mathematical Domains:** The efficacy of DOTS+RR was demonstrated beyond mathematics. When applied to a science domain (SCP-25K dataset, evaluated on MMLU science subsets with Qwen2.5-3B), the method significantly improved RL data efficiency, indicating its broader applicability.
- **Robustness to Reference Set Size:** DOTS maintained comparable RL performance when using a smaller reference set size (128 vs. 256 questions), suggesting robustness and potential for further efficiency gains in rollout collection.
- **Extended Training Horizon:** The method's effectiveness and stability were confirmed over longer training durations (100 steps), consistently outperforming the GRPO baseline.

In summary, the research demonstrates that the proposed DOTS and RR techniques collectively lead to substantial reductions in LLM RL fine-tuning time by accelerating convergence and lowering per-step costs, with the adaptive difficulty prediction framework accurately guiding selection and incurring minimal overhead.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of Large Language Model (LLM) fine-tuning by directly addressing a critical bottleneck: the high computational cost and data inefficiency of reinforcement learning (RL) methods. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood in several dimensions:

- **Enhanced Accessibility and Scalability of LLM RL Fine-tuning:** By achieving an average reduction of 40.7% (and up to 62%) in total training time, the proposed techniques make LLM RL fine-tuning more resource-efficient. This directly translates to lower GPU hour consumption and reduced financial costs, making advanced RL-based fine-tuning more accessible to a wider range of researchers and organizations that may not have access to vast computational resources. This is particularly crucial for larger LLMs and for tasks requiring extensive training horizons.

- **Improved Training Efficiency for Reasoning Capabilities:** The paper specifically focuses on enhancing LLMs' reasoning capabilities, a domain where RL fine-tuning has shown promise but also incurs significant costs. The demonstrated improvements in efficiency for mathematical and scientific reasoning tasks suggest that these methods can accelerate the development of more capable and robust reasoning LLMs.

- **Novel Data-Centric Approach to LLM RL:** While much prior work in LLM RL has focused on algorithmic improvements, this research highlights the substantial gains achievable through a *data-centric* perspective. It introduces novel mechanisms for dynamically selecting informative data and efficiently managing rollouts tailored for the LLM context. This shift in focus may encourage further exploration of data optimization strategies in LLM training pipelines.

- **Adaptive Learning and Generalization:** The concept of "adaptive difficulty" and its efficient prediction allows the training process to dynamically adjust the curriculum to the model's current capabilities. This enables more targeted learning, moving beyond static data selection methods that fail to account for the evolving policy. The demonstrated generalization of the methods beyond mathematical domains to science suggests broad applicability across various knowledge areas for LLM enhancement.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for future investigation. These include exploring more sophisticated reference set selection strategies, incorporating prioritized experience replay mechanisms, and enriching input embeddings for difficulty prediction. The theoretical justification for maximal gradient signal at moderate difficulty also provides a basis for further theoretical development in adaptive data selection for RL.

In summary, this research offers practical and theoretically grounded solutions to improve the data efficiency of LLM RL fine-tuning. Its potential impact lies in making advanced LLM training techniques more sustainable, democratized, and ultimately, in accelerating the development of more intelligent and capable language models.
