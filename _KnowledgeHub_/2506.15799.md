# Steering Your Diffusion Policy with Latent Space Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A new framework, Diffusion Steering via Reinforcement Learning (DSRL), enables rapid, autonomous adaptation of pre-trained diffusion policies for robotic control by learning to manipulate their latent noise input space. This approach achieves high sample efficiency and black-box compatibility, making it practical for real-world fine-tuning of large generalist robot policies such as π0.

## Problem

- Behavioral cloning policies, particularly those based on diffusion models, struggle to adapt autonomously in novel, open-world environments without collecting new human demonstrations.
- Traditional reinforcement learning is often prohibitively sample-inefficient for complex, high-dimensional robotic domains, making real-world online adaptation impractical.
- Finetuning large, complex diffusion policies directly with standard RL methods is computationally intensive, numerically unstable, and requires white-box access to model weights.

## Method

- **DSRL** reinterprets a pre-trained ==diffusion policy== as a ==deterministic transformation== from a ==latent-noise input space== to the physical action space.
- Frames the problem as learning a separate, smaller ==reinforcement learning policy== that operates on this latent-noise space to ==steer== the fixed, pre-trained diffusion policy.
- Introduces ==Noise-Aliased DSRL (DSRL-NA)==, which uses a ==dual-critic== setup to efficiently propagate value information across aliased noise samples and effectively incorporate ==offline data== for improved sample efficiency.

## Results

- Achieved **5-10x** improvement in sample efficiency compared to existing state-of-the-art RL methods for diffusion policies in simulation.
- Demonstrated rapid real-world adaptation, improving a single-task pick-and-place success rate from **20%** to nearly **100%** with less than **50 episodes** (approx. **3500 online steps**).
- Successfully adapted a **3.3B** parameter generalist robot policy (π0) in both simulation and real-world tasks, marking the first successful real-world RL-based fine-tuning of this model with approximately **10,000 online steps**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The latent noise input space of a diffusion policy offers sufficient expressivity to steer its behavior for robotic control.
> - By treating the diffusion policy as a black-box transformation within the environment, standard RL can be applied efficiently to a smaller latent-noise policy without finetuning the large base model.
> - Leveraging 'noise aliasing' (where different noise inputs can produce similar physical actions) allows for improved sample efficiency by transferring value information between physical and latent-noise action spaces.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work resides at the intersection of several rapidly evolving fields within artificial intelligence and robotics: behavioral cloning (BC), diffusion models, and reinforcement learning (RL).

**Behavioral Cloning and Diffusion Policies in Robotics:**
Behavioral cloning, where a robot policy learns from human demonstrations, has been highly successful in transferring complex skills to robots. Recent advancements have seen diffusion models emerge as a state-of-the-art parameterization for BC policies due to their ability to model complex, multi-modal action distributions effectively. These "diffusion policies" have achieved impressive results in real-world robotic domains, ranging from single-task applications to generalist policies (e.g., Octo, π0). However, a fundamental limitation of BC is its inability to autonomously improve or adapt when deployed in novel, open-world settings where the initial demonstrations might not perfectly cover all scenarios. Improving such policies typically requires collecting more expensive human demonstrations.

**The Promise and Challenge of Reinforcement Learning:**
Reinforcement learning offers the promise of autonomous policy improvement by allowing agents to learn from trial and error and a reward signal. While powerful, RL is notoriously sample-inefficient, especially in complex, high-dimensional robotic domains. Learning policies from scratch with RL often requires an prohibitive amount of real-world interaction, making it impractical for many applications.

**Finetuning Pretrained Models with RL:**
In other domains, notably large language models (LLMs), RL has proven effective as a finetuning tool to adapt models pretrained on vast offline datasets. However, applying this finetuning paradigm directly to robotics, particularly for state-of-the-art diffusion policies, presents unique challenges:
1. **Computational Cost:** Diffusion policies are often large, complex models. Finetuning their weights with RL involves computationally intensive backpropagation through their multi-step denoising process, which can be numerically unstable.
2. **Sample Efficiency:** Even if feasible, direct finetuning still often requires many samples, undermining the goal of efficient adaptation.
3. **Black-Box Access:** Many large foundation models in AI (including some generalist robot policies) are becoming available primarily via API, without direct access to their internal weights, precluding traditional finetuning.

**Existing Approaches to RL with Diffusion Models:**
Prior work has explored various ways to combine RL with diffusion models. These include:
- **Offline RL:** Training diffusion policies on value-weighted objectives or optimizing them directly for reward, often with techniques like rejection sampling.
- **Online RL:** Finetuning the diffusion policy's early denoising steps, matching score functions with Q-values, or using iterative BC to clone high-value actions. Some approaches also learn an *additive residual policy* on top of a pretrained BC policy.

**DSRL's Unique Position:**
DSRL distinguishes itself from these existing methods by taking a fundamentally different approach. Instead of modifying the diffusion policy's weights (direct finetuning) or adding a separate residual policy, DSRL *steers* the pretrained policy by manipulating its *initial input noise distribution*. This strategy draws inspiration from noise optimization techniques in image diffusion (e.g., optimizing initial noise for image quality) and prior work in control using normalizing flows. However, DSRL specifically addresses the critical challenge of applying this to *diffusion models* (which lack the invertibility guarantee of normalizing flows, making expressivity of the noise space less obvious) in *robotic control*, where its practical relevance is much higher given the success of diffusion policies. By re-framing the problem as RL in a "latent-noise space," DSRL offers a highly sample-efficient, black-box compatible, and stable method for adapting complex diffusion policies, avoiding the complexities of direct weight modification.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation behind DSRL stems from the limitations of current state-of-the-art robotic control methods based on behavioral cloning (BC) and the inherent challenges of traditional reinforcement learning (RL) in real-world settings.

**Primary Problem:**
Robotic control policies learned via BC from human demonstrations, particularly those parameterized by diffusion models, achieve impressive initial performance. However, they struggle to adapt autonomously in novel, open-world environments where their initial performance might be suboptimal. Improving their behavior typically necessitates collecting additional human demonstrations, a process that is notoriously expensive, time-consuming, and not scalable. Reinforcement learning, while theoretically capable of autonomous improvement, generally requires an prohibitively large number of samples, making its direct application for real-world robot adaptation impractical. Finetuning large, complex diffusion policies with standard RL methods is further complicated by computational intensity, numerical instability, and the need for white-box access to model weights.

**Key Objectives of the Work:**
Against this backdrop, the authors set forth the following key objectives for DSRL:

1. **Enable Fast Autonomous Adaptation:** Develop a method that allows BC-trained policies, especially diffusion policies, to improve their behavior autonomously and rapidly in real-world deployment.
2. **Achieve High Sample Efficiency:** Design an RL-based approach that significantly reduces the number of samples (real-world interactions) required for policy improvement, making online adaptation feasible.
3. **Black-Box Compatibility:** Ensure the method requires only forward passes of the pretrained BC policy, without needing access to its internal weights or intermediate denoising steps. This would allow for adaptation of proprietary models or those accessed via API.
4. **Avoid Finetuning Base Policy Weights:** Circumvent the challenges associated with modifying the weights of large, complex diffusion policies, such as computational intensity and instability during backpropagation through the diffusion chain.
5. **Leverage Latent-Noise Space:** Propose modifying the policy's sampling process by running RL over its latent-noise input space, hypothesizing that this space offers sufficient expressivity for effective steering.
6. **Demonstrate Real-World Effectiveness:** Empirically validate the approach on real-world robotic tasks, beyond just simulations, showcasing its practical utility.
7. **Adapt Generalist Policies:** Show that DSRL can be used to improve the behavior of existing state-of-the-art large generalist robot policies, further underscoring its broad applicability and power.

In essence, the motivation is to bridge the gap between the strong generalization capabilities of data-driven behavioral cloning and the adaptive power of reinforcement learning, specifically for the increasingly dominant diffusion-based policy architectures, while overcoming the practical hurdles of real-world robot learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core innovation of DSRL lies in its unique strategy for adapting pre-trained diffusion policies: instead of modifying the policy's internal weights, it learns to *steer* its behavior by influencing the initial noise input to its denoising process.

#### 3.1. Diffusion Policies and Their Deterministic Nature

The paper focuses on diffusion policies (or flow-based policies) that, once trained, can generate actions. Specifically, it highlights the use of DDIM (Denoising Diffusion Implicit Models) sampling or flow-based policies. A crucial property of these models is that given a state `s` and an initial noise sample `w`, the output action `a` is *deterministic*. This allows the authors to define the diffusion policy as a deterministic mapping `π_W_dp: S × W → A`, where `S` is the state space, `W` is the latent-noise space (typically `R^d`), and `A` is the action space.

#### 3.2. Reinterpreting Policy as an Action Space Transformation

The central conceptual step is to reinterpret the role of `π_W_dp`. Instead of `π_W_dp` being the policy itself, it is treated as a *transformation* from a "latent-noise action space" `W` to the original "physical action space" `A`.
This allows for the construction of a new Markov Decision Process (MDP), termed the **latent-action MDP** (`M_W`). In `M_W`, the states are still `S`, but the actions are now chosen from `W`. When an agent selects a latent-noise action `w` in state `s`, this `w` is fed into the pretrained `π_W_dp(s, w)` to produce a physical action `a`. This `a` is then executed in the original environment `M`, yielding a reward `r(s, a)` and a next state `s'`. The reward function for `M_W` is simply `r_W(s, w) = r(s, π_W_dp(s, w))`, and the transition kernel `P_W(· | s, w) = P(· | s, π_W_dp(s, w))`.
This re-framing effectively "black-boxes" the diffusion policy `π_W_dp` into the environment, transforming the original RL problem into a standard policy optimization problem over `M_W`. A separate, much smaller RL policy, `π_W: S → △_W`, is then trained to select `w` in this latent-noise space.

#### 3.3. Computational Efficiency and Black-Box Access

This approach offers significant computational advantages and flexibility:
- **No Backpropagation through `π_dp`:** The RL algorithm only needs to perform *forward passes* through `π_W_dp` to obtain the physical action `a` and compute the reward. This avoids the unstable and computationally expensive backpropagation through the multi-step diffusion process, a major hurdle for direct finetuning of diffusion policies.
- **Smaller RL Policy:** The policy `π_W` operating in the latent-noise space is typically a much smaller neural network compared to the large diffusion policy `π_W_dp`. Training and updating this smaller network is far more computationally efficient.
- **Black-Box Compatibility:** DSRL only requires access to `π_W_dp` as a function that maps `(s, w)` to `a`. This means DSRL can adapt policies that are only accessible via an API or where their internal weights are proprietary, a crucial feature for adapting large foundation models.

#### 3.4. Noise-Aliased DSRL (DSRL-NA) for Sample Efficiency

While any RL algorithm can, in principle, be applied to `M_W`, the authors introduce **Noise-Aliased Diffusion Steering via Reinforcement Learning (DSRL-NA)** (Algorithm 1) to specifically exploit the structure of diffusion policies and enhance sample efficiency, particularly when leveraging offline data.
The key insight is that different latent noise samples `w` and `w'` might lead to approximately the same physical action `a = π_W_dp(s, w) ≈ π_W_dp(s, w')`. This "aliasing" behavior can be leveraged. DSRL-NA maintains two critics:
- **`Q_A(s, a)` (Action-Space Critic):** This critic operates on the original physical action space `A`. It is trained using standard temporal-difference learning on observed `(s, a, r, s')` transitions, allowing it to naturally incorporate pre-existing *offline datasets* (which are typically recorded in the physical action space).
- **`Q_W(s, w)` (Latent-Noise Critic):** This critic operates on the latent-noise space `W`. It learns by *distilling* the value from `Q_A`. Specifically, `Q_W(s, w)` is trained to predict `Q_A(s, π_W_dp(s, w))`. This enables `Q_W` to infer the value of latent-noise actions `w` without directly observing them in the environment, by translating them through `π_W_dp` to `A` and querying `Q_A`. This mechanism efficiently propagates value information across aliased noise samples.

The training procedure for DSRL-NA involves iterative updates:
1. `Q_A` is updated using experiences from the replay buffer (which can contain both offline data and online rollouts).
2. `Q_W` is updated by minimizing the difference between its prediction `Q_W(s, w)` and the value from `Q_A(s, π_W_dp(s, w))` for sampled `w`.
3. The latent-noise actor `π_W` is updated to maximize `Q_W(s, π_W(s))`, effectively pushing it towards higher-value latent noise samples.
In the online setting, `π_W` is used to sample `w`, which is then translated by `π_W_dp` into `a` for execution, and the resulting `(s, a, r, s')` is added to the replay buffer.

**Implicit Conservatism:**
When `π_dp` is trained on an offline dataset, it inherently produces "in-distribution" actions. Because DSRL-NA only queries `Q_A` on actions from the dataset or those generated by `π_dp`, it naturally avoids evaluating out-of-distribution actions, thereby achieving a form of conservatism often explicitly enforced in offline RL (e.g., through penalty terms).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

DSRL's performance was rigorously evaluated across a variety of simulated and real-world robotic tasks, demonstrating its effectiveness, sample efficiency, and robustness.

#### 4.1. DSRL Enables Efficient Online Adaptation of Diffusion Policies (Simulation)

- **Benchmarks:** OpenAI Gym (Hopper, Walker2D, HalfCheetah) and Robomimic (Lift, Can, Square, Transport).
- **Key Finding:** DSRL (using both DSRL-NA and DSRL-SAC variants) consistently achieved near-optimal behavior on these tasks, significantly improving the performance of the pre-trained base diffusion policies.
- **Sample Efficiency:** DSRL exhibited a remarkable **5-10x improvement in sample efficiency** compared to existing state-of-the-art RL methods for diffusion policies (e.g., DPPO, IDQL, DQL, DIPO, QSM). This highlights DSRL's ability to achieve high performance with far fewer real-world interactions.

#### 4.2. DSRL Enables Efficient Offline Adaptation of Diffusion Policies (Simulation)

- **Benchmark:** 10 tasks from the OGBench offline RL benchmark.
- **Key Finding:** DSRL-NA demonstrated strong performance, achieving **state-of-the-art results on approximately half of the evaluated tasks**.
- **Improvement over BC:** Critically, DSRL-NA substantially improved performance over the base BC policy (π_dp) from which it was steered, demonstrating that effective policy improvement can be achieved even purely from offline data without online interaction.

#### 4.3. DSRL Enables Efficient Offline-to-Online Adaptation of Diffusion Policies (Simulation)

- **Benchmarks:** Robomimic Can and Square.
- **Key Finding:** Incorporating offline data into DSRL's training **improved its sample complexity by a factor of approximately 2x** during online learning.
- **Baseline Comparison:** In contrast, standard offline-to-online RL approaches like RLPD and CAL-QL were unable to learn effectively on these challenging tasks, underscoring DSRL's robust performance.

#### 4.4. Adapting Real-World Single- and Multi-Task Robot Policies with DSRL

- **Single-Task Robot (Franka Emika Panda):**
 - **Task:** Pick-and-place (cube into bowl).
 - **Key Finding:** DSRL rapidly improved the base diffusion policy's success rate from 20% to **nearly 100% with less than 50 episodes** (approximately 3500 online steps).
 - **Baseline Comparison:** RLPD completely failed to learn, and even RLPD augmented with human interventions was unable to consistently solve the task, highlighting DSRL's superior real-world sample efficiency and efficacy.
- **Multi-Task Robot (WidowX 250 6-DoF, Bridge V2 pretrained):**
 - **Tasks:** Pick-and-Place, Drawer Closing, Block Stacking.
 - **Key Finding:** DSRL significantly improved the pre-trained multi-task policy's performance on all three tasks with a **very small number of online adaptation steps (100-150 episodes)**.
 - **Baseline Comparison:** RLPD again failed to learn, reinforcing DSRL's practical advantage in complex real-world settings.

#### 4.5. Steering Pretrained Generalist Robot Policies with DSRL (π0)

- **Generalist Policy:** π0, a 3.3B parameter vision-language-action flow model, challenging to finetune directly due to its scale and high-dimensional action space (1600 dimensions).
- **Simulation (Libero, AlohaTransferCube):**
 - **Key Finding:** DSRL dramatically improved π0's performance (e.g., Libero success rate from ~20% to **~100% after only ~10,000 online samples**).
 - **Baseline Comparison:** Residual RL methods (RESIP) and value guidance methods (V-GPS) failed to achieve significant improvements, demonstrating DSRL's effectiveness in exploiting the latent-noise space of such complex policies.
- **Real-World (Franka, π0-DROID pretrained):**
 - **Tasks:** Turning on a toaster, picking up a spoon and placing it on a plate.
 - **Key Finding:** DSRL successfully and significantly improved π0's performance in the real world with a relatively small number of samples (~10,000-11,000 total steps per task). This marks **the first successful demonstration of real-world RL-based finetuning of π0**.

#### 4.6. Understanding Diffusion Steering (Ablations)

- **DSRL-NA vs. DSRL-SAC:** DSRL-NA (noise-aliased variant) was approximately **2x more sample efficient** than DSRL-SAC in online settings, demonstrating the importance of the aliasing mechanism.
- **Sensitivity to `π_dp` Size:** DSRL successfully steered base policies of various sizes. Larger `π_dp` capacities could sometimes lead to faster steering, even if initial performance was similar, suggesting more diverse behaviors could be captured.
- **Sensitivity to Data Quality of `π_dp`:** DSRL proved **robust to the quality of the data** `π_dp` was initially trained on. Even when `π_dp` was trained on "Worse" quality data, DSRL still achieved high performance, albeit with a slight initial degradation.
- **Sensitivity to `π_dp` Training Epochs/Denoising Steps:** The number of training epochs or denoising steps for `π_dp` had **almost no effect** on DSRL's performance, indicating its ability to effectively steer even potentially overfit policies.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The proposed Diffusion Steering via Reinforcement Learning (DSRL) represents a significant advancement in robotic learning, with profound implications for the deployment and continuous improvement of intelligent robots.

**1. Enabling Practical Real-World Robotic Adaptation:**
The most critical impact of DSRL is its ability to make online reinforcement learning practical for real-world robots. By achieving **unprecedented sample efficiency (5-10x improvement over baselines)** and demonstrating robust performance on actual robotic hardware (Franka, WidowX), DSRL overcomes a major bottleneck that has historically limited the widespread application of RL in robotics. This means that highly capable BC-trained policies can now be autonomously refined and adapted to new environments or tasks with a feasible amount of robot interaction, drastically reducing the reliance on expensive and time-consuming human demonstrations or interventions.

**2. Bridging the Gap between BC and RL:**
DSRL effectively bridges the gap between the strengths of behavioral cloning (simplicity, scalability, ability to leverage large datasets) and the power of reinforcement learning (autonomous improvement, goal maximization). It allows researchers and practitioners to leverage the best of both worlds: start with a strong, data-driven BC policy, then efficiently fine-tune it to optimize for specific performance metrics via RL, rather than learning from scratch.

**3. Black-Box Policy Adaptation and Foundation Models:**
A groundbreaking aspect of DSRL is its **black-box compatibility**. By operating solely on the latent-noise input space and requiring only forward passes of the base diffusion policy, DSRL can adapt policies without needing access to their internal weights or architecture details. This is especially significant in the era of large foundation models (like π0), where pre-trained models might be proprietary or too massive to fine-tune conventionally. DSRL's successful real-world adaptation of π0, often with significantly less compute than required for traditional fine-tuning, paves the way for rapid specialization of powerful generalist policies to specific downstream tasks.

**4. Addressing Challenges of Diffusion Policy Finetuning:**
DSRL ingeniously sidesteps the well-known difficulties of directly finetuning diffusion policies, such as the computational burden and numerical instability of backpropagating through multi-step denoising processes. By training a separate, much smaller RL policy in the latent space, it provides a stable and efficient mechanism for policy adjustment.

**5. Foundational Implications for Generative Models:**
The underlying principle of "steering" a generative model by optimizing its input noise, as demonstrated for action generation, has broader theoretical implications. It suggests a powerful paradigm for optimizing any complex generative process towards a desired objective, opening up new research avenues for diffusion models in other domains beyond control, such as image generation, drug discovery, or material design. The findings on noise aliasing further deepen our understanding of the latent spaces of diffusion models.

**Potential Future Directions:**
The authors themselves suggest several exciting future directions:
- **Expanding Steering Inputs:** Investigating whether modifying other inputs to the policy, such as observations or language prompts, can also lead to effective steering, potentially extending DSRL-like methods to autoregressive transformer policies.
- **Beyond Control:** Applying DSRL's core principle to other domains where diffusion models excel (e.g., image generation, protein modeling) to optimize various objectives.
- **Theoretical Guarantees:** Deepening the theoretical understanding of diffusion steering, including the expressivity of noise-space optimization and the learnability of policies within this space.

In summary, DSRL offers a highly practical, efficient, and versatile framework for continuous improvement of robotic control policies, especially those built on state-of-the-art diffusion models. Its implications extend from making real-world robot deployment more feasible to advancing our understanding and application of large generative models across various AI domains.
