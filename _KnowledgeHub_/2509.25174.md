# XQC: Well-conditioned Optimization Accelerates Deep Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A study reveals how specific architectural choices, like Batch Normalization, Weight Normalization, and Categorical Cross-Entropy loss, synergistically create a well-conditioned optimization landscape in deep reinforcement learning critic networks. The resulting XQC algorithm achieves state-of-the-art sample efficiency across 70 continuous control tasks with 4.5 times fewer parameters and 5 times lower computational cost than prior methods.

## Problem

- Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) often suffers from poor sample efficiency, making real-world applications costly and time-consuming.
- Existing DRL advancements often rely on scaling model complexity or update ratios, leading to high computational costs without a fundamental understanding of the underlying optimization problem.
- A lack of systematic analysis exists regarding how common architectural components (e.g., normalization layers, loss functions) affect the DRL critic's optimization landscape.

## Method

- Conducted a systematic investigation into DRL critic architectures, analyzing ==Batch Normalization== (BN), Layer Normalization (LN), Weight Normalization (WN), and critic loss functions (MSE vs. Categorical Cross-Entropy (CE)).
- Performed in-depth Hessian analysis, computing the eigenspectrum and condition number of the critic's loss function to quantify optimization landscape properties.
- Developed the **XQC** algorithm, integrating BN, WN, and a C51-style categorical critic with CE loss into a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) framework based on insights from the optimization analysis.

## Results

- XQC achieved sample efficiency on par with or superior to state-of-the-art baselines across 70 diverse continuous control tasks, including both proprioceptive and vision-based environments.
- The algorithm significantly reduced computational requirements, utilizing approximately 4.5 times fewer parameters and 5 times fewer FLOP/S than leading baselines, resulting in roughly **60%** faster wall-clock training times.
- Plasticity analysis indicated that XQC maintained stable learning dynamics with consistently bounded gradient norms and effective learning rates, suggesting sustained network plasticity throughout training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Architectural choices fundamentally alter the DRL critic's optimization landscape; Batch Normalization (BN) consistently produced more compact and stable Hessian eigenspectra than Layer Normalization (LN).
> - Categorical Cross-Entropy (CE) loss, combined with BN and Weight Normalization (WN), synergistically created an exceptionally well-conditioned optimization problem, characterized by orders of magnitude smaller and more stable condition numbers.
> - Theoretical analysis confirmed that CE loss provides bounded gradient norms and, when combined with WN and weight decay, an upper-bounded Hessian condition number, which contrasts with the unbounded nature of MSE loss.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated capabilities in complex domains, but a persistent challenge remains sample efficiency, which refers to the number of interactions an agent requires with its environment to learn an effective policy. This is particularly critical for real-world applications such as robotics, where data collection can be costly and time-consuming.

Recent advancements in model-free DRL have largely adhered to a "scaling" paradigm. This approach involves increasing the complexity of DRL systems through various means, including the use of larger neural networks, higher update-to-data (UTD) ratios, and expanded computational budgets. These strategies primarily aim to enhance performance by facilitating stable scaling, often resulting in empirical improvements in sample efficiency. While effective in practice, this paradigm typically incurs significant computational costs and often prioritizes empirical performance over a fundamental understanding of the underlying optimization problems. The existing literature shows a focus on architectural components like Layer Normalization (LN) to stabilize scaling or specific network architectures for performance gains, with Batch Normalization (BN) historically considered problematic in RL settings until recent work challenged this view. Distributional critics, particularly those employing a categorical cross-entropy (CE) loss, have gained traction, often motivated by their ability to model the full distribution of returns rather than just the mean, or by observations that they are empirically easier to optimize.

This paper positions itself as a departure from the sole focus on complexity scaling. Instead, it adopts a principled approach by investigating the optimization landscape of the DRL critic network. By systematically analyzing the impact of common architectural design decisions—specifically normalization layers (BN vs. LN), weight normalization (WN), and critic loss functions (CE vs. Mean Squared Error, MSE)—through the lens of optimization theory, the work aims to uncover architectural synergies that inherently lead to better-conditioned learning problems. This approach seeks to improve sample efficiency not by adding complexity, but by optimizing the foundational learning process itself, thereby contributing to the development of more efficient and robust DRL algorithms.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance sample efficiency in deep reinforcement learning by focusing on the underlying optimization problem of the critic network, rather than solely increasing model or algorithmic complexity. The authors propose that a "better-conditioned optimization problem" can lead to more efficient learning.

The detailed motivations and specific objectives driving this work are:

1. **Systematic Investigation of Architectural Components:** To conduct a systematic examination of commonly used architectural components within DRL critics. This includes different normalization layers (Batch Normalization (BN) and Layer Normalization (LN)), the application of Weight Normalization (WN), and various critic loss functions (Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Categorical Cross-Entropy (CE)). The motivation here is that the roles of these components in RL are often guided by heuristics, and a principled understanding of their impact on optimization dynamics is lacking.
2. **Principled Explanation via Hessian Analysis:** To provide a principled explanation for observed performance differences among various DRL architectures. This involves an in-depth analysis of the critic's Hessian matrix, specifically its eigenspectrum and condition number. The motivation is to move beyond empirical observations and establish a theoretical basis for architectural choices by quantifying how they affect the curvature and smoothness of the loss landscape, which directly influences the effectiveness of gradient-based optimization.
3. **Identification of Synergistic Architectural Combinations:** To identify combinations of architectural components that synergistically improve the conditioning of the optimization problem. The authors hypothesize that specific interactions between BN, WN, and CE loss could dramatically reduce condition numbers and stabilize training. This is motivated by the potential for these elements to jointly address issues like gradient norm instability and variable effective learning rates, which are critical in non-stationary RL environments.
4. **Development of an Optimization-Aware Algorithm (XQC):** To design and introduce a novel deep actor-critic algorithm, named XQC (eXceptionally well-Conditioned Optimization), based on the insights derived from the optimization landscape analysis. This algorithm is intended to embody the identified principles of well-conditioned optimization, specifically integrating BN, WN, and a distributional CE loss into a Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) framework. The motivation is to translate theoretical understanding into a practical, sample-efficient DRL algorithm that achieves competitive performance while utilizing fewer resources.
5. **Empirical Validation and Performance Evaluation:** To rigorously validate the proposed XQC algorithm across a wide range of continuous control tasks, including both proprioception and vision-based environments. This objective is motivated by the need to demonstrate that the principled approach to optimization conditioning translates into tangible improvements in state-of-the-art sample efficiency and computational resource usage compared to existing, often larger-scale, DRL methods.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to shift the DRL research focus from brute-force scaling to a more fundamental understanding and manipulation of the optimization problem itself, aiming for algorithms that are both performant and efficient.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involved a two-pronged approach: a systematic empirical and theoretical investigation into the optimization landscape of DRL critics, followed by the development and comprehensive evaluation of a novel algorithm, XQC, based on the insights gained.

#### 3.1. Optimization Landscape Investigation

The core of the investigation focused on analyzing the first- and second-order properties of the Bellman error minimization problem in DRL.

- **Architectural Components Under Study:**
 - **Normalization Strategies:** Batch Normalization (BN), Layer Normalization (LN), and a baseline "None" (Dense) without explicit normalization layers.
 - **Weight Projection:** Weight Normalization (WN) (periodically projecting network weights to the unit sphere) versus no WN.
 - **Critic Loss Functions:** Mean Squared Error (MSE) and Categorical Cross-Entropy (CE), the latter used with a distributional critic.
- **Experimental Setup for Analysis:** A total of 12 distinct architectural combinations were tested by combining these components. The evaluation was conducted on the challenging DeepMind Control Suite (DMC) `dog-trot` environment. For each architecture, 5 random seeds were run for 1 million environment steps.
- **Hessian Eigenvalue Analysis:** The Hessian eigenspectrum of the critic's loss function was computed at 20 checkpoints during training. This was performed using an efficient JAX implementation of the stochastic Lanczos quadrature algorithm, adapted from previous work in supervised learning.
- **Optimization Metrics:**
 - **Condition Number (κ):** Defined as the ratio of the largest to the smallest absolute eigenvalue of the Hessian (κ(H) = λ_max / λ_min). A lower condition number indicates a "well-conditioned" optimization problem.
 - **Largest Eigenvalue (λ_max):** Used as a proxy for the β-smoothness constant, quantifying the maximum curvature of the loss landscape.
 - **Kurtosis of Eigenvalues:** Provided a proxy measure for the presence of outliers in the eigenspectrum.
 - **Effective Learning Rate (ELR):** Defined for scale-invariant functions as η / ||θ||₂.
 - **Gradient Norm:** Monitored to understand stability and boundedness of updates.

#### 3.2. Theoretical Analysis of Loss Functions

The paper provided theoretical propositions and a theorem to formally motivate the empirical findings regarding CE and MSE losses:

- **Gradient Norm Boundedness:** Propositions were presented to show that the L2 norm of gradients with respect to predictions for CE loss is bounded, specifically ||t - Softmax(ŷ)||₂ ≤ √2, while for MSE loss, it is unbounded.
- **Effective Gradient Update Bound:** Theorem 1 demonstrated that for CE loss combined with Weight Normalization and a Lipschitz assumption on the function approximator, the effective gradient update can be upper-bounded.
- **Hessian Condition Number Bounds:** Propositions 3 and 4, under an assumption of bounded eigenvalues for the function approximator's Hessian and the presence of weight decay, argued that the condition number for MSE loss is unbounded, whereas for CE loss, it can be upper-bounded and made finite (given suitable weight decay).

#### 3.3. XQC Algorithm Design

Based on the insights from the optimization analysis, the XQC algorithm was developed as an extension of Soft Actor-Critic (SAC).

- **Critic Architecture:** XQC's critic utilizes a four-hidden-layer MLP (512 neurons per layer). Batch Normalization (BN) layers are applied directly on the network input and after each linear layer, preceding the ReLU activation. A "joined forward pass" method is used to calculate BN running statistics on both (s, a) and (s', a') distributions.
- **Loss Function:** A C51-style categorical critic with 101 atoms and a Categorical Cross-Entropy (CE) loss is employed for Bellman error minimization. Reward normalization, based on the running standard deviation of returns, is used to scale Q-values to the critic's support.
- **Weight Normalization (WN):** Applied to the weights of each dense layer after every gradient step. This is enabled by the scale-invariance property of BN.
- **Vision Encoder:** For vision-based tasks, the standard DRQ-V2 image encoder (convolutional layers, ReLU, linear, LN, tanh) is adopted without modification, with XQC's architectural changes confined to the subsequent MLP layers.

#### 3.4. Empirical Validation of XQC

A comprehensive experimental evaluation was conducted to assess XQC's performance.

- **Benchmarks:** 70 continuous control tasks:
 - 55 proprioceptive tasks from HumanoidBench (14), DeepMind Control Suite (25), MyoSuite (10), and MuJoCo (6).
 - 15 vision-based tasks from DeepMind Control Suite.
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Sample Efficiency:** Measured by Area Under the Curve (AUC) of normalized returns over 1 million environment steps (aggregated over 10 seeds for main experiments, 5 for ablations). Normalized scores between 0 and 1 using benchmark-specific protocols.
 - **Plasticity Metrics:** Parameter norms, gradient norms, and Effective Learning Rate (ELR) were tracked.
 - **Computational Efficiency:** Parameter count, FLOP/S, and frames-per-second (FPS) during training and evaluation.
- **Baselines:** XQC was compared against strong, recent model-free baselines including SIMBA-V2, BRO, MRQ, BRC, CROSSQ+WN, SAC, and DRQ-V2 (for vision tasks).
- **Ablation Studies:** Extensive ablations were performed to confirm the necessity and synergistic contribution of XQC's components:
 - Removing WN.
 - Replacing CE loss with MSE loss.
 - Replacing BN with LN.
 - Investigating robustness to action repeat, weight decay, batch size, UTD ratios, network depth, and width.

This comprehensive methodological framework allowed for both a theoretical understanding of optimization dynamics and practical validation of an algorithm designed based on these principles.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the optimization landscape of DRL critics and the performance of the proposed XQC algorithm.

#### 4.1. Optimization Landscape Analysis (Hessian Eigenvalues and Condition Numbers)

- **Batch Normalization (BN) vs. Layer Normalization (LN):** Architectures employing BN consistently produced more compact and stable eigenspectra during training. Their eigenvalues remained within a moderate range without significant outliers. In contrast, LN architectures exhibited large and growing outlier eigenvalues, indicative of sharp curvature in the loss landscape.
- **Cross-Entropy (CE) vs. Mean Squared Error (MSE) Loss:** The CE loss function induced a significantly better-conditioned optimization landscape compared to the MSE loss. This was reflected in the condition numbers, which were orders of magnitude smaller for CE-based critics.
- **Synergistic Effect:** The combination of BN, Weight Normalization (WN), and a categorical CE loss worked synergistically. This specific combination led to the best-conditioned optimization landscape, characterized by condition numbers that were an order of magnitude smaller and more stable than architectures lacking these components.
- **Correlation with Performance:** A clear and strong trend was observed: architectures with lower condition numbers and smaller maximum eigenvalues of the Hessian achieved higher returns at 1 million timesteps. This correlation indicated that a smoother, better-conditioned optimization landscape directly contributed to improved DRL performance. BN-based architectures also showed lower Kurtosis values, suggesting fewer outlier eigenvalues.

#### 4.2. Theoretical Insights (CE Loss Properties)

- **Bounded Gradients:** Theoretical analysis demonstrated that the L2 norm of gradients with respect to predictions is inherently bounded for the categorical CE loss, whereas it is unbounded for the MSE loss.
- **Bounded Effective Learning Rate (ELR):** Combining the bounded gradients of CE loss with WN and a Lipschitz assumption on the function approximator allowed for an upper-bounded effective gradient update.
- **Bounded Hessian Condition Number:** Under an assumption of bounded eigenvalues for the function approximator's Hessian and the inclusion of weight decay, the condition number of the Hessian for the CE loss was shown to be upper-bounded. This contrasts with the MSE loss, for which the condition number cannot be upper-bounded under similar assumptions, providing formal intuition for the empirical observations.

#### 4.3. XQC Algorithm Performance

- **State-of-the-Art Sample Efficiency (Proprioception):** XQC achieved sample efficiency on par with or superior to strong baselines such as SIMBA-V2, MRQ, BRO, and CROSSQ+WN across 55 proprioceptive continuous control tasks from four benchmarks (HumanoidBench, DMC, MyoSuite, MuJoCo). It demonstrated notable performance gains on complex tasks like those in HumanoidBench and DMC-hard environments.
- **Improved Sample Efficiency (Vision-Based):** On 15 vision-based DMC tasks, XQC significantly improved sample efficiency compared to the DRQ-V2 baseline, particularly on the more challenging humanoid tasks. This was achieved using the standard DRQ-V2 encoder, isolating the performance gains to XQC's critic architecture.
- **Stable Learning Dynamics (Plasticity Analysis):** Plasticity metrics aggregated over proprioceptive tasks indicated that XQC's architecture created stable learning dynamics. The combination of BN and WN stabilized the network's parameter norms. BN combined with CE loss maintained gradient norms and the effective learning rate (ELR) near constant and orders of magnitude smaller throughout training, suggesting sustained plasticity. In contrast, an XQC variant with MSE loss showed growing, unbounded gradient norms and ELRs.

#### 4.4. Parameter and Compute Efficiency

- **Reduced Parameter Count:** XQC achieved its performance with approximately 4.5 times fewer parameters than SIMBA-V2.
- **Lower Computational Cost:** XQC exhibited significantly higher computational efficiency, requiring about 5 times fewer FLOP/S than SIMBA-V2 and BRO, and over 100 times fewer FLOP/S than BRC.
- **Faster Wall-Clock Time:** This efficiency translated to approximately 60% higher frames-per-second (FPS) during training, resulting in faster wall-clock times.

#### 4.5. Scaling Behavior and Architecture Ablations

- **Robust Scaling:** XQC demonstrated stable scaling behavior, with performance generally improving or being maintained with increasing UTD ratios, network depths, and widths. This indicated that its well-conditioned design is robust to variations in compute and model capacity.
- **Necessity of Components:** Ablation studies confirmed the synergistic necessity of each of XQC's three main architectural components: BN, WN, and the CE distributional critic. Removal of any component led to a significant performance drop, with replacing BN with LN resulting in the largest performance degradation. This emphasized their combined contribution to the algorithm's performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of deep reinforcement learning by shifting the focus from solely scaling complexity to a more fundamental approach rooted in optimization theory. The work's significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Principled Design Paradigm for DRL:** The study provides a principled framework for designing DRL architectures, moving beyond empirical heuristics often used in the field. By demonstrating that architectural choices can fundamentally alter the optimization landscape, the work advocates for designing DRL components that are inherently "well-conditioned." This approach offers a systematic alternative to purely scaling up models or update ratios, which often leads to increased computational demands without a deeper understanding of efficiency gains.
- **Enhanced Sample and Computational Efficiency:** The XQC algorithm, developed based on these optimization-aware principles, achieves state-of-the-art sample efficiency across a diverse range of continuous control tasks. Crucially, it does so with significantly fewer parameters and lower computational requirements (FLOP/S, FPS) compared to many competing methods. This increased efficiency is vital for applying DRL in real-world scenarios, particularly in domains like robotics where data collection is expensive and computational resources may be constrained.
- **Improved Training Stability and Robustness:** The analysis of plasticity metrics indicates that XQC's architecture leads to exceptionally stable learning dynamics, characterized by bounded gradient norms and stable effective learning rates. This stability contributes to more reliable training, potentially reducing the need for extensive hyperparameter tuning and making DRL algorithms more robust to varying task complexities and environmental conditions.
- **New Insights into Architectural Components:** The systematic Hessian eigenvalue analysis offers novel insights into why specific architectural components, such as Batch Normalization, Weight Normalization, and Cross-Entropy loss in distributional critics, contribute to better optimization. It provides a theoretical basis for understanding their synergistic effects, challenging previous assumptions (e.g., the perceived incompatibility of BN with RL) and offering explanations beyond mere representational benefits (e.g., for CE loss).
- **Guidance for Future DRL Research:** The findings suggest that future research in DRL could benefit from a continued focus on optimizing the underlying learning problem. The insights derived from XQC's design could be applied to accelerate other categories of DRL algorithms, including model-based RL, imitation learning, and algorithms that leverage prior datasets, potentially leading to broader advancements in the field's capabilities and practical applicability.

In summary, this work underscores that a dedicated focus on the fundamentals of optimization can yield substantial improvements in performance and efficiency in deep reinforcement learning, offering a valuable direction for future development and deployment of DRL systems.
