# Bilinear World Models: Learning Representations with Structured Dynamics for Efficient Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Bilinear World Models is a JEPA-style world model that restricts latent dynamics to a normalized bilinear form, so the ViT encoder has to carry the expressivity. This structure lets actions be recovered in closed form, which prevents representation collapse, and it supports a fast Gauss-Newton planner. On TwoRoom, PushT, Reacher and Cube it matches or beats LeWM and SMWM while cutting planning time by roughly three orders of magnitude.

## Problem

- JEPA world models such as LeWM and SMWM use unconstrained neural predictors whose flexibility adds little for control and forces expensive black-box sampling planners like CEM.
- Joint encoder-predictor training admits collapsed constant representations, and prior fixes rely on latent-distribution regularizers or an auxiliary learned inverse network rather than structural guarantees.
- Existing latent world models are evaluated mainly on short-horizon offline planning, because minutes of CEM planning per episode rule out long horizons and real-time replanning.

## Method

- ==Normalized bilinear latent dynamics==: the next latent equals Az plus Q(z)R times the action, where Q is the ==Cholesky-QR factor== of B plus Cz, so the predictor is just four matrices.
- Closed-form action recoverability through the ==pseudo-inverse== of the action map, trained as a weighted loss. Because M(z) transposed times M(z) equals R transposed times R, full rank depends only on R, which prevents collapse structurally.
- A ==damped Gauss-Newton planner== that uses endpoint sensitivities over a reduced coherent action subspace with ==line search==, plus a projected residual model trained after the representation is frozen. A ==Koopman proposition== guarantees expressiveness for control-affine systems.

## Results

- Success rates are **100%** on TwoRoom, **99%** on Cube, **90%** on PushT and **75%** on Reacher at 25-step goal offsets, matching or beating LeWM and SMWM, with Cube **17 points** above the strongest baseline.
- Planning time drops from **7-32 minutes to 0.7-3.1 seconds**. At 75-step offsets LeWorld needs about **1.4 hours** and SMWM **22 minutes**, versus **6.5 seconds**, and TwoRoom success stays above 75% versus roughly 40%.
- In 20 Hz moving-goal control, success is **68, 77, 71 and 93%** versus **46, 47, 45 and 63%** for the best iCEM baseline, about **27 points** higher. Gauss-Newton applied to the unstructured baselines does not match this.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Constraining the dynamics predictor shifts the modeling burden onto the encoder, which then organizes the latents around controllable degrees of freedom, with variance concentrated in about as many components as action dimensions.
> - Structural guarantees can replace auxiliary anti-collapse losses: making actions invertible from transitions by construction prevents representation collapse without a learned inverse model or a distribution regularizer.
> - Planning speed is a property of model design: gradient-based planners only pay off when latent dynamics are structured, so designing the model and controller together unlocks long-horizon and real-time control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A controller asked to reach a moving target must act while the target changes. A planner that takes too long risks acting on stale information. The authors train the representation so that actions have explicit, recoverable effects, then use those effects to adjust a plan. In moving-goal Cube, the method reaches **93% success**, versus **63% for the strongest baseline**, under a 20 Hz control loop. Success means completing the environment's task. The controllers differ in this comparison: Bilinear World Models use Gauss–Newton, and the neural-dynamics baselines use iCEM because CEM is too slow for the prescribed real-time budget.

Success rate on M-TwoRoom, M-PushT, M-Reacher and M-Cube for Bilinear, SMWM, LeWorld and Random. The setup uses a 20 Hz loop, a random-walk target and latency-aware replanning. SMWM and LeWorld use iCEM; Bilinear uses GN.

### 2. Make actions visible in the latent state

A world model encodes observations into a compact latent state and predicts how actions change it. This one is JEPA-style: it predicts future embeddings without reconstructing the original images. Its action map is generated by a bilinear form in the current latent state, then normalized, giving $M(z)=Q(z)R$. Here $Q(z)$ supplies normalized, state-dependent action directions, and the state-independent $R$ controls rank and conditioning.

When the map has full column rank, the action that produced a latent transition is recoverable from the transition. The authors argue that this rules out a constant representation, because a collapsed state could not preserve distinctions between actions. By comparison, [[2603.19312|LeWorldModel]] uses a Gaussian-distribution regularizer to avoid collapse, while [Sensorimotor World Models](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.20104) add a learned inverse-dynamics model.

The practical method has two qualifications:

- Normalization makes the learned dynamics **not strictly bilinear**.
- In training, an inverse-action loss encourages rank in place of a hard constraint. The reported minimum singular value stays bounded away from zero across the four environments. This is an observed training result, not a guarantee.

This structure also changes how a plan is improved. CEM samples many candidate sequences and compares their predicted outcomes. Gauss–Newton (GN) instead uses how changing actions changes the predicted endpoint to iteratively improve a sequence. In the reported implementation, endpoint sensitivities are estimated by batched directional finite differences in a reduced, coherent action subspace, with damping and line search. Planning also adds an auxiliary residual model after representation learning, so the evaluated planner is more than an unmodified bilinear transition.

### 3. Fast planning without a success trade-off

On TwoRoom, PushT, Reacher and Cube, success rate is the percentage of 100 start–goal tasks completed within the allowed action budget. Planning time is the controller computation needed to obtain the action sequence.

In the main comparison, Bilinear uses GN, while LeWM and SMWM use CEM, their original planner. Bilinear matches or improves success across the four tasks, with planning times on the order of seconds. The baselines take several minutes.

Success rate (solid bars) and average total wall-clock planning time per episode (hatched bars, logarithmic seconds) for Bilinear, SMWM and LeWorld. The results cover 100 evaluation episodes, each a start–goal pair with a 25-step goal offset.

The authors tested whether the planner alone explains the speed by applying CEM, iCEM and GN across all three models:

- GN paired with Bilinear performs strongly and quickly. GN on the two unconstrained models does not yield the same combination.
- CEM works across all three models but is much slower on Bilinear than GN.
- iCEM reduces computation but loses performance on harder tasks.

These results support the authors' interpretation that GN benefits from the structured dynamics. They do not show that structure is the only factor.

The moving-goal result from the opening has the same limits. It compares success rates under a 20 Hz, latency-aware protocol, and its tasks exclude episodes that a policy that never acts could solve. It is not a matched-controller proof that the model alone caused the gain.

### 4. One representation, two jobs

The same action-shaped latent transition keeps action effects distinguishable and gives the planner a local route for improving its plan. Full rank comes from a training loss and is observed in the fitted models. It is not imposed as a hard guarantee.
