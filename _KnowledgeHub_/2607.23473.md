# PRISM: Polynomial Representations for Interaction-Structured Motor Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Michigan, Ann Arbor, developed PRISM, a method that explicitly incorporates polynomial representations of physical interactions into robot control policies. This architectural change enhances performance in locomotion and manipulation tasks, and notably enables compliant behaviors in contact-rich scenarios without needing additional sensors.

## Problem

- Standard MLP policies in robot control process first-order observations, implicitly requiring the network to discover higher-order physical interactions (e.g., joint power, kinetic effects).
- This implicit discovery can be inefficient and may not yield optimal or robust control, as physical couplings are not explicitly structured.
- Naively expanding all polynomial terms to make interactions explicit leads to computational inefficiency and dimensional intractability.

## Method

- **PRISM** introduces a compact ==factorized polynomial module== that replaces or augments standard ==linear conditioning pathways== in neural network policies.
- It computes learned ==affine factors== from proprioceptive state and combines them element-wise to form ==polynomial features== up to a specified degree, controlled by learned parameters.
- The resulting ==interaction-structured representation== is integrated into existing reinforcement learning (e.g., ==PPO==) and imitation learning (e.g., ==Diffusion Policy==) frameworks without altering objectives or requiring additional deployment-time sensors.

## Results

- PRISM achieved a **92.50%** survival rate in humanoid locomotion, significantly outperforming the MLP baseline's **51.00%**, and reduced linear velocity tracking error from **0.4607** to **0.2099**.
- The method attained a **91.0%** average task success rate across LIBERO manipulation tasks, surpassing Diffusion Policy (**63.8%**) and even a sensor-rich MCC-Oracle (**64.5%**) while demonstrating implicit force-free compliance.
- Linear probes on frozen PRISM representations showed that mechanics-inspired quantities like slip velocity, joint power, and contact impulse became more linearly recoverable, indicating a better alignment of latent features with physical concepts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly modeling polynomial interactions through a compact, factorized architecture aligns the policy's representation with the underlying physics, which inherently involves multiplicative effects of physical variables.
> - This architectural inductive bias, rather than merely increased model capacity, provides a more efficient way for policies to reason about physically meaningful quantities.
> - The structured proprioceptive input enables emergent compliance-like contact behaviors in manipulation tasks without requiring force/tactile sensors or explicit admittance controllers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

In contemporary robot control, policies are frequently implemented as Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) that map observed states to actions. This approach is widely adopted in both imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks. While MLPs have demonstrated efficacy in tasks such as image recognition, their application in robotics presents a distinct challenge. Robot observations consist of physical variables such as joint positions, velocities, commands, and IMU data. Many critical action-relevant physical phenomena, including joint power, inertial effects, contact, slip, and compliance, are not directly represented as first-order variables within the observation vector. Instead, these phenomena manifest as products or higher-order combinations of observable signals. For example, joint power is the product of torque and velocity, while kinetic and Coriolis effects depend on velocity products.

Standard MLP policies receive these first-order variables and are implicitly tasked with discovering these higher-order relationships through learning. While MLPs are universal approximators and can, in principle, approximate any polynomial structure given sufficient capacity, they do not inherently expose this structure. Increasing network width primarily adds capacity to form weighted sums of the original inputs, but it does not alter the fundamental input basis. Consequently, crucial physical couplings, such as torque-velocity or state-velocity interactions, remain latent, requiring the network to infer them from raw data rather than receiving them as explicitly structured features.

Prior work in robotics has addressed this by incorporating physical priors through various means: auxiliary estimators, constrained objectives, or physics-informed loss functions. These methods often require explicit estimation of specific physical quantities or task-specific consistency constraints during training. Other approaches involve introducing additional sensory modalities, such as force, tactile, or audio inputs, or employing specialized compliance controllers to manage contact-rich scenarios. However, these solutions typically increase deployment-time sensing requirements or introduce additional control complexity.

PRISM proposes an alternative by introducing an architectural inductive bias. It aims to make these polynomial interactions explicit and learnable within the policy's proprioceptive representation itself, without requiring additional physical supervision, modified loss functions, or increased deployment-time sensing. This positions PRISM as a method that structurally enhances the policy's ability to interpret physical observations, addressing a fundamental limitation in how standard neural network architectures process physically grounded sensor data for robotic control.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a perceived disconnect between the nature of physical robot observations and the default representation mechanisms of modern neural network policies. Robot observations are inherently physical variables, and critical action-relevant cues often arise not from individual variables but from their multiplicative interactions. The authors identify that quantities like joint power (torque $\times$ joint velocity), kinetic effects (velocity products), slip, impact, and compliance are fundamentally products or higher-order combinations of observable signals.

The current standard practice of using MLPs, which primarily operate on first-order variables, treats these interaction-dependent physical cues as implicit. This means the policy must internally discover these complex relationships from raw data, a process that can be inefficient and may not always yield optimal or robust control, particularly when the underlying physics heavily relies on such interactions. Simply increasing the capacity (width) of an MLP may allow it to approximate these interactions, but it does not provide the policy with a direct, structured basis for them. This leads to the first key objective:
- **Objective 1: Make polynomial interactions among observable physical variables explicit and learnable.** The goal is to provide the policy with a direct, learnable interaction basis, allowing it to represent and utilize these higher-order physical quantities more efficiently than implicit approximation. This aims to align the policy's representation with the underlying physics of robot control.

A direct approach to making all polynomial terms explicit would be to append all coordinate products to the observation vector. However, this method quickly becomes computationally inefficient and dimensionally intractable as the input dimension and polynomial degree increase. This leads to the second key objective:
- **Objective 2: Ensure the interaction basis is compact and efficient.** The aim is to introduce higher-order interaction features at a manageable computational cost, avoiding the combinatorial explosion of terms associated with a naive polynomial expansion.

By pursuing these objectives, the authors motivate that the proposed representation, PRISM, could lead to several benefits:
- **Improved performance and robustness:** Policies can more effectively leverage physically relevant cues for tasks like locomotion command tracking and stable manipulation.
- **Emergent behaviors without additional sensors:** By structuring the proprioceptive input to inherently capture interaction effects, the policy might exhibit behaviors such as compliance in contact-rich tasks without requiring explicit force/tactile sensors, contact labels, or specialized admittance controllers. This reduces deployment complexity and sensor overhead.
- **Enhanced interpretability:** The explicit interaction features might make mechanics-inspired quantities more accessible within the policy's learned representations.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between physical reality and policy representation by introducing an architectural modification that explicitly models multiplicative interactions among observable physical variables in a compact and learnable manner, thereby enhancing robot control capabilities without increasing sensing requirements.

### 3. Methodology and approach

PRISM (Polynomial Representations for Interaction-Structured Motor Control) introduces a compact proprioceptive conditioning approach designed to represent explicit interactions among deployment-available robot observations. The core idea is to replace or augment the standard linear conditioning pathways in neural network policies with a factorized polynomial module.

**Policy Setting and Input Partitioning:**
The policy is defined as $\pi_{\theta}(a_t | o_t)$, where $a_t$ is the action and $o_t$ is the observation at time $t$. The observation $o_t$ is partitioned into $(x_t, c_t)$, where $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the deployment-available proprioceptive state or history to which polynomial conditioning is applied, and $c_t$ includes other policy inputs such as commands, unmodified state variables, images, or language. PRISM specifically modifies the processing of $x_t$. The policy is designed not to receive latent physical quantities like contact forces or terrain friction during deployment; instead, PRISM aims to encode their observable effects reflected in $x_t$.

**Interaction-Structured Proprioceptive Representation:**
PRISM's central component is a factorized polynomial module. Given an input $x_t$, it first computes two learned affine factors:
$u_t = W_1 x_t + b_1$
$v_t = W_2 x_t + b_2$
where $W_1, W_2$ are weight matrices and $b_1, b_2$ are bias vectors.

For a default second-order representation (degree 2), the polynomial feature $\psi_2(x_t)$ is computed as:
$\psi_2(x_t) = u_t \odot (1 + \alpha_2 \odot v_t)$
This can be expanded to:
$\psi_2(x_t) = u_t + \alpha_2 \odot (u_t \odot v_t)$
Here, $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication, and $\alpha_2$ is a learned parameter vector that controls the strength of the quadratic interaction for each latent feature. The first term, $u_t$, preserves a direct first-order pathway, while the second term introduces factorized quadratic interactions. $\alpha_2$ is initialized near zero, allowing the representation to start close to a standard linear projection and gradually learn the interaction contribution during training. This factorized construction avoids explicitly enumerating all pairwise monomials, providing a compact and efficient way to introduce higher-order interactions.

The approach can be extended recursively to a polynomial degree $K$:
$\psi_1(x_t) = W_1 x_t + b_1$
$\psi_k(x_t) = \psi_{k-1}(x_t) \odot [1 + \alpha_k \odot (W_k x_t + b_k)]$, for $k = 2, \dots, K$.
Each additional factor increases the maximum polynomial degree by one while retaining all lower-order pathways. The resulting representation $\psi_K(x_t)$ contains learned interaction terms up to degree $K$. This $\psi_K(x_t)$ is then mapped to the conditioning dimension $z_t$ used by the downstream policy via a learned projection $g_{\eta}$:
$z_t = g_{\eta}(\psi_K(x_t))$

**Integration into Reinforcement Learning (RL):**
In RL, PRISM encodes the deployment-available proprioceptive state or motion history into $z_t$. This representation is then combined with the remaining policy inputs $c_t$ to predict a continuous action distribution: $\pi_{\theta}(a_t | z_t, c_t)$. The parameters of PRISM and the policy are optimized jointly using the original RL objective (e.g., Proximal Policy Optimization, PPO). PRISM does not alter the action space, reward function, policy objective, or low-level controller. Any privileged simulator information remains restricted to the critic during training and is not available to the deployed actor.

**Integration into Imitation Learning (IL):**
For IL, PRISM replaces the policy’s standard proprioceptive conditioning pathway while maintaining its output dimension and downstream interface. Given robot proprioception $s_{proprio_t}$, the policy computes $z_{proprio_t} = g_{\eta}(\psi(s_{proprio_t}))$. This representation is then combined with the policy’s existing visual and task-conditioning features for action prediction. Examples include Diffusion Policy, where $z_{proprio_t}$ is combined with the visual representation, and SmolVLA, where it replaces the standard proprioceptive state embedding. In both IL instantiations, PRISM parameters are optimized using the backbone’s native imitation-learning objective and training protocol, without auxiliary physical supervision or additional deployment sensing.

**Experimental Setup:**
PRISM was evaluated in two settings:
1. **Humanoid Locomotion:** Using Humanoid-Gym for sensor-minimal humanoid locomotion tasks. Comparisons were made against a standard MLP actor, a larger-capacity MLP actor (with matched parameter count), and polynomial actors of different degrees.
2. **Contact-Rich Manipulation:** Using LIBERO imitation-learning tasks. Comparisons were made against Diffusion Policy and Minimalist Compliance Control (MCC) (both sensorless and oracle versions).

Linear probes were also used to analyze whether PRISM's learned features inherently capture underlying physical quantities by predicting future mechanics-inspired responses from frozen policy representations. Ablation studies investigated the impact of individual latent factors.

### 4. Main findings and results

The experimental results consistently demonstrated the efficacy of PRISM across various robot control tasks and learning paradigms.

**Humanoid Locomotion (Humanoid-Gym):**
- PRISM exhibited improved performance in humanoid locomotion tasks. Specifically, it achieved higher mean episode rewards, significantly reduced linear-velocity tracking error (0.2099 for PRISM vs. 0.4607 for MLP Baseline), lower yaw-velocity tracking error (0.1221 vs. 0.2817), and a higher survival rate (92.50% vs. 51.00%) compared to a standard MLP actor.
- A larger MLP baseline, with a parameter count closely matching that of PRISM, failed to achieve comparable performance, showing only marginal improvements or even slight degradation in some metrics compared to the standard MLP. This outcome indicates that the performance gains from PRISM are attributable to its structured polynomial interaction modeling rather than merely an increase in model capacity.
- Training curves showed that polynomial actors learned more reliably and reached higher performance, and in closed-loop evaluations, PRISM policies tracked target velocities with minimal oscillation, maintaining stability throughout rollouts.
- An ablation study on polynomial degree (Table 18 in Appendix) indicated that quadratic interactions (Degree 2) provided most of the performance gain, with a cubic variant (Degree 3) offering a slight further improvement, but Degree 2 was adopted as the default for its simplicity and effectiveness.

**Contact-Rich Manipulation (LIBERO):**
- PRISM achieved a higher average task success rate across LIBERO tasks (91.0%) compared to the Diffusion Policy baseline (63.8%), MCC-Sensorless (47.8%), and even MCC-Oracle (64.5%) (Table 2). It also showed slightly improved position and orientation errors in successful rollouts.
- PRISM demonstrated compliance-like contact behavior in manipulation tasks without requiring force, wrench, tactile input, contact labels, or an explicit admittance controller. For instance, in a stove-turning task, PRISM maintained a low and stable contact-force profile by exhibiting instantaneous velocity suppression upon contact, which absorbed impact energy. In contrast, Diffusion Policy suffered hard impacts, and MCC-Sensorless showed delayed responses and failed to adapt in time, leading to higher contact forces. This suggests that polynomial proprioceptive conditioning functions as an implicit force-free compliance mechanism.

**Validation on Stronger Policy Backbones:**
- When integrated into more advanced policy backbones, BFM-Zero for humanoid locomotion and SmolVLA for multi-task manipulation, PRISM continued to provide benefits (Table 3).
- For BFM-Zero, PRISM reduced tracking Earth Mover's Distance (EMD) under nominal dynamics, low friction, and payload-mass perturbations.
- For SmolVLA, PRISM improved the average LIBERO success rate.
- In both cases, PRISM outperformed corresponding larger-capacity conditioner controls, often with fewer parameters, reinforcing that the benefit derives from explicit multiplicative interactions rather than merely expanded model capacity.

**Latent Physical Interaction Analysis:**
- Linear probes trained on frozen PRISM policy representations demonstrated that PRISM made mechanics-inspired quantities, such as slip velocity, joint power, contact impulse, and contact work, more linearly recoverable (Table 4). This was indicated by lower Mean Squared Error (MSE) and higher Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC) in predicting these quantities from PRISM's latent features compared to baselines.
- Analysis of latent factors (Table 5) revealed that PRISM discovered emergent task-specific interaction proxies. For humanoid locomotion, high-impact factors involved velocity memory coupling (e.g., $\dot{q}_{Lhippitch}(t) \times \dot{q}_{Lhippitch}(t-1)$), cross-joint velocity coupling, and state-velocity interactions. Ablating these factors directly affected predicted joint-position commands, suggesting that the learned quadratic structures were actively used for control.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The introduction of PRISM carries several implications for the field of embodied motor control:

**Addressing a Fundamental Limitation in Policy Representation:** PRISM directly addresses a fundamental limitation in prevailing robot policy architectures, specifically MLPs, which treat physical observations as abstract feature vectors. By making polynomial interactions among observable physical variables explicit and learnable, PRISM provides a mechanism for policies to inherently reason about physically meaningful quantities such as power, inertial effects, and contact dynamics. This move from implicit discovery to explicit representation can lead to more robust and physically informed control.

**Consistent Performance Gains and Robustness:** The consistent improvements demonstrated across diverse tasks—from humanoid locomotion command tracking to contact-rich manipulation—and different learning paradigms (RL and IL) underscore the general applicability and effectiveness of PRISM. The fact that PRISM outperforms larger-capacity MLP baselines with matched parameter counts indicates that the gains are driven by structural alignment with physics rather than simply increased model complexity. This suggests a more efficient use of model capacity.

**Enabling Sensorless Emergent Behaviors:** A significant impact of PRISM is its ability to induce compliance-like contact behavior in manipulation tasks without requiring additional deployment-time sensing (e.g., force, tactile sensors) or explicit admittance controllers. This "sensorless compliance" feature simplifies robot deployment, reduces hardware complexity and cost, and makes robots potentially more adaptable to unknown contact scenarios. This could accelerate the adoption of robots in environments where complex sensing is prohibitive.

**Improved Interpretability of Learned Policies:** The finding that PRISM representations make mechanics-inspired quantities more linearly predictable suggests that the policy's internal state becomes more aligned with fundamental physical concepts. This enhanced interpretability can be valuable for debugging, analysis, and potentially for transferring learned skills by leveraging a more physically grounded latent space.

**Setting a New Architectural Standard:** The authors propose that polynomial representations should become a standard architectural choice for embodied motor control. Given the demonstrated benefits in performance, robustness, and sensorless capabilities without increasing deployment overhead, PRISM could indeed influence future neural network designs for robotics, shifting away from purely generic architectures towards those that incorporate domain-specific inductive biases based on the physics of interaction.

**Future Research Directions:** While significant, the work also opens avenues for future research. The current focus on low-degree polynomial interactions leaves room to explore adaptive polynomial degrees or more sophisticated temporal interaction modeling for complex, long-horizon dynamics. Expanding to broader robot morphologies and more diverse real-world contact scenarios could further validate and extend the utility of PRISM.

In conclusion, PRISM offers a practical and effective method to infuse physically relevant interaction structures into robot learning policies. Its capacity to yield superior performance and emergent sensorless behaviors suggests a meaningful step towards more capable, robust, and deployable robotic systems.
