# NoiseGate: Learning Per-Latent Timestep Schedules as Information Gating in World Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> NoiseGate introduces a method that learns task-adaptive per-latent timestep schedules for future observation latents in World Action Models (WAMs), allowing for dynamic information gating during action generation. The system achieved an average success rate of 94.28% on the RoboTwin benchmark, outperforming existing WAMs by 1.70 percentage points.

## Problem

- Existing joint video-action World Action Models (WAMs) typically assign a single, scalar timestep to all future predicted latent frames, implying uniform reliability for action generation.
- This assumption imposes a strong prior that every future latent frame contributes equally to the action generation process, which is often not true as reliability is heterogeneous and context-dependent.
- Suboptimal action generation can result from fixed or hand-crafted schedules for revealing future latents, as the optimal information flow for a task is unknown in advance.

## Method

- **NoiseGate** redefines per-latent timestep assignment as a learnable ==information-gating policy== within a ==Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) WAM==, where video and action tokens interact through shared self-attention.
- A ==two-stage training protocol== is used: first, the WAM backbone is trained with independent per-latent timestep sampling for each future latent frame to generalize to arbitrary noise levels. Second, a lightweight ==Gating Policy Network (GPN)== is optimized.
- The GPN learns per-latent timestep decrements using ==Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)== guided by sparse, binary task-success rewards to adaptively control the denoising speed of individual future video latents.

## Results

- NoiseGate achieved an average success rate of **94.28%** across **50** tasks on the RoboTwin benchmark, representing an absolute gain of **1.70 percentage points** over the demonstration-tuned Stage-1 WAM baseline (**92.58%**).
- Ablation studies confirmed that the learned GPN is central to performance gains, with its removal (Shared-t baseline) causing the largest performance drop from **67.5%** (full NoiseGate) to **57.5%**.
- Qualitative analysis revealed task-specific, non-uniform denoising trajectories and distinct residual noise levels at the final step, indicating selective attenuation of information from future frames based on task utility.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The 'noise-as-masking' interpretation is empirically validated; higher noise levels on video tokens monotonically decay action token attention to them, demonstrating continuous reliability gating.
> - The optimal per-latent timestep schedules are non-uniform and task-dependent, allowing the model to adaptively prioritize which future frames to denoise faster or keep partially masked based on task context and utility.
> - Optimizing this information-gating policy with reinforcement learning from task-success rewards significantly improves the WAM's ability to handle uncertainty and make robust decisions in complex manipulation tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of World Action Models (WAMs), an emerging paradigm in robotics that integrates robot action generation with future-observation modeling. WAMs generalize traditional video-language-action (VLA) policies by modeling the joint distribution of future latent frames and action chunks, given a current observation and language instruction. A significant family of WAMs, exemplified by systems such as Motus and Fast-WAM, achieves this by jointly denoising future latents and actions using a diffusion or flow-matching backbone. This approach couples perception, prediction, and control within a single generative process, offering conceptual simplicity and empirical efficiency.

Current joint video-action WAMs typically employ Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architectures where video and action tokens interact through shared self-attention layers. Within this structure, each predicted latent frame *could* theoretically be assigned an independent denoising timestep (t_f). However, existing systems commonly constrain this degree of freedom by forcing all predicted latent frames to share a single scalar timestep (t) at each denoising step. This practice, when viewed through the lens of Diffusion Forcing (which interprets noise levels as partial masking), implies a strong, potentially unjustified, prior: that every predicted latent frame contributes equally reliably to the action generation process.

The paper addresses this limitation by proposing to treat the per-latent timestep as a learnable information-gating policy. This perspective leverages the shared self-attention mechanism in MoT backbones, where the noise level on a latent frame can modulate the reliability of its Key/Value contribution to action tokens, effectively acting as a continuous gate. This approach distinguishes itself from classical diffusion samplers (e.g., DDIM, EDM) that improve global denoising trajectories or learned schedules (e.g., TPDM) that predict scalar timesteps for single-modality generation. Instead, NoiseGate learns a vector of timesteps for future video latents within a multi-modal context, explicitly controlling information flow to action generation.

Furthermore, this work builds upon advancements in reinforcement learning (RL) for VLA policies, which have demonstrated the benefits of optimizing policies with task-success rewards. Unlike these methods that typically optimize the action-producing policy directly, NoiseGate applies RL to a lightweight scheduler that modulates how future latent frames are revealed to the action tokens, thereby learning an internal information-gating policy rather than directly refining the action policy itself.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the performance and robustness of World Action Models (WAMs) by introducing a learned, task-adaptive information-gating mechanism for future observation latents during the action generation process.

The motivation stems from a recognized limitation in existing joint video-action WAMs. These models typically operate under a simplifying assumption: all future predicted latent frames share a uniform noise level (a single scalar timestep, *t*) at each step of the denoising trajectory. The authors argue that this shared scalar timestep imposes an implicit and often unjustified prior, assuming that every predicted latent frame is equally reliable and informative for generating actions.

However, the authors posit that the true influence and reliability of each future latent frame for action prediction are heterogeneous, context-dependent, and inherently unknown in advance. For instance, in complex manipulation tasks, certain critical moments (e.g., grasping, placing) might benefit from a more "blurred" or uncertain representation in the imagined future, allowing the model to integrate evidence more cautiously. Conversely, other frames might provide critical, immediate information that should be made clearer earlier in the denoising process. A fixed or hand-crafted schedule for revealing future latents would merely replace one strong prior with another, potentially leading to suboptimal action generation.

To address this, the authors reframe the per-latent timestep assignment as a learnable information-gating policy. In the Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture commonly used in WAMs, video and action tokens interact through shared self-attention. The "noise-as-masking" interpretation from Diffusion Forcing suggests that a higher noise level on a latent frame effectively makes it less "visible" or reliable, thereby attenuating its Key/Value contribution to the action tokens. By allowing individual future latent frames to possess independent noise levels (t_f), the model gains a continuous mechanism to control how much evidence from each future latent propagates into the action tokens.

The core motivation is that the optimal pattern of this "gating" is not universal but specific to the task at hand and should therefore be learned from data rather than being pre-defined or fixed. The goal is to allow the WAM to adaptively prioritize the denoising of specific frames that are most informative for the current action, leading to more effective and robust policy execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

NoiseGate is proposed as a solution to learn per-latent timestep schedules. Its methodology integrates three primary components: a specialized World Action Model (WAM) backbone, an independent per-latent timestep sampling strategy during training, and a Gating Policy Network (GPN) optimized with reinforcement learning. This is structured into a two-stage training protocol.

#### 3.1. World Action Model and MoT Backbone

NoiseGate is built upon the joint video-action modeling paradigm of WAMs, specifically using a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) architecture. This backbone integrates a chunk-bidirectional video DiT (Wan 2.2-TI2V-5B) with an Action-Expert DiT. In this setup, the model co-generates a chunk of future latent frames (v = {v_1..., v_F}) and an action chunk (a) conditioned on a current observation latent (v_0) and a language instruction (l). The clean observation latent v_0 is always pinned at t_0 = 0. The predicted latent frames {v_f} and action tokens (a) are processed within a joint sequence, sharing self-attention blocks while having modality-specific feed-forward layers.

The crucial aspect of this architecture for NoiseGate is that the shared self-attention layers serve as the physical mechanism for information gating. The individual noise level (t_f) assigned to each predicted latent frame v_f modulates its reliability and thus its contribution to the action tokens' representations. While video latents can have unique t_f values, action tokens (a) share a single global timestep (t_a) at any given denoising step, ensuring a consistent action generation pace.

#### 3.2. Training Substrate: Independent Per-Latent Timestep Sampling (Stage 1)

To enable the WAM backbone to handle and generalize to arbitrary, heterogeneous per-frame schedules during inference, a specific training regime is employed in Stage 1. Inspired by Diffusion Forcing, this stage involves sampling independent timesteps for each future latent frame (t_v = (t_1..., t_F)) and a separate timestep for the action (t_a) for each training sample. The backbone is then optimized with the corresponding diffusion noise or flow-matching velocity targets. This independent sampling across latents during training ensures the model learns to extract features from video contexts with varying levels of uncertainty and detail, preparing it for the GPN's dynamic control. After this stage, the WAM backbone is frozen.

#### 3.3. Gating Policy Network (GPN)

The GPN (π_ϕ) is a lightweight network responsible for learning the per-latent schedule. At each denoising step *k*, the GPN observes the current heterogeneously noised video chunk (v_t^(k)), their corresponding timesteps (t_v^(k)), and the clean initial observation (v_0). Its output consists of per-latent timestep decrements (∆t_f^(k)) exclusively for the predicted video latents. The action schedule (t_a) remains governed by a fixed global schedule and is not directly controlled by the GPN.

The policy is parameterized to predict bounded relative scales (r^(k) ∈ (0, 2)^F) for the decrements. If δt^(k) is the nominal timestep decrement from a standard scalar schedule, each per-latent decrement is calculated as ∆t_f^(k) = δt^(k) * r_f^(k). This relative scaling preserves the overall denoising pace while allowing the GPN to adjust the individual speeds of latent frame denoising. The GPN comprises a spatiotemporal encoder and a squashed-Gaussian actor head, with further architectural details provided in the paper's appendix. The predicted-video timestep vector is updated component-wise: t_f^(k+1) = max{0, t_f^(k) - ∆t_f^(k)}. No explicit monotonicity or fixed shape prior is imposed on these learned schedules.

#### 3.4. Training with GRPO (Stage 2)

In Stage 2, the WAM backbone trained in Stage 1 is frozen. Only the GPN policy (π_ϕ) is optimized using Group-Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). GRPO is selected for its ability to learn from sparse, binary task-success rewards (r ∈ {0, 1}) provided by the simulator. For a group of trajectories, advantages (Â_i) are computed by comparing individual rewards against a group-relative baseline. The policy is updated using a clipped surrogate objective with an entropy bonus to ensure stable learning, leveraging per-latent importance ratios (ρ_f). This reward-based optimization grounds the gating policy in actual task utility.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative results demonstrating the efficacy of NoiseGate, particularly its ability to learn and exploit per-latent timestep schedules for improved robot manipulation.

#### 4.1. Main Quantitative Results (RoboTwin Random-Scene Comparison)

On the RoboTwin benchmark under random-scene conditions, NoiseGate consistently improves performance over baselines.
- **Overall Performance:** NoiseGate achieves an average success rate of 94.28% across 50 tasks, outperforming the demonstration-tuned Stage-1 WAM (92.58%) by an absolute gain of 1.70 percentage points. It also surpasses other strong RoboTwin baselines like Fast-WAM (91.78%), LingBot-VA (91.50%), π0.5 (76.76%), and Motus (87.02%).
- **Task-Specific Gains:** NoiseGate demonstrates notable improvements on tasks where there is "headroom" for better performance. For example, it achieves gains of 6-9 points on tasks such as Hanging Mug (+9), Blocks Ranking Size (+7), Put Object Cabinet (+7), and Place Bread Skillet (+6). Many other medium-difficulty placement and manipulation tasks show improvements of 2-5 points.
- **Sustained Performance:** On tasks where the Stage-1 WAM already performs near the ceiling (e.g., Adjust Bottle, Move Can Pot), NoiseGate maintains the high success rates, indicating it does not degrade already robust behaviors. Only minor regressions were observed on a few tasks like Place Object Scale and Open Microwave.
- **Conclusion:** These results suggest that the learned per-latent schedule selectively aids tasks that benefit from adaptive control over the reliability of predicted video latents during action generation, rather than uniformly boosting performance.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

A controlled ablation study, conducted on a smaller subset of tasks, isolates the contribution of different components of NoiseGate:
- **Impact of Learned Schedule:** Removing both the GPN and independent noise training (the "Shared-t" baseline, representing conventional WAMs) results in the largest performance drop, from 67.5% (full NoiseGate) to 57.5%. This highlights the critical role of the per-latent timestep schedule.
- **Independent Noise Training as Substrate:** Keeping independent per-latent noise training for the backbone but using a shared scalar for inference ("Stage-1 WAM") recovers substantial performance (61.3%). This indicates that preparing the backbone to handle heterogeneous noise is a crucial enabling step.
- **Hand-crafted Schedules:** Replacing the GPN with a hand-crafted monotone timestep schedule yields 63.4%, an improvement over the "Stage-1 WAM" baseline, but still significantly below NoiseGate. This shows that while some per-latent structure is beneficial, an arbitrarily fixed schedule is insufficient; task-adaptive learning is necessary to fully exploit this degree of freedom.
- **GPN's Contribution:** The full NoiseGate system, with its learned GPN, closes the remaining gap to 67.5%, confirming that the GPN's ability to learn task-adaptive schedules is central to the overall performance gains.

#### 4.3. Qualitative Analysis: Gating, Schedules, and Noise-as-Masking

- **Noise as a Reliability Gate (Figure 3 & 6):** Empirical analysis validates the "noise-as-masking" interpretation. As the noise level (t_f) on a video token increases, the mean attention from action tokens to that video token monotonically decays. This demonstrates that t_f indeed functions as a continuous reliability gate, controlling the contribution of future latents to action representations. This gating effect is particularly strong in the early and late layers of the transformer, with intermediate layers showing more uniform behavior.
- **Task-Specific Timestep Schedules (Figure 4):** Visualizations of denoising trajectories show that the GPN learns non-uniform, task-dependent per-latent schedules. Instead of all predicted latents following a single shared curve, they diverge into distinct trajectories. This indicates that the policy adaptively decides which future frames to denoise faster (making them more "visible" earlier) and which to keep partially masked (slower denoising) based on the task context.
- **Cross-task Structure of Final-step Residual Noise (Figure 7):** Further analysis reveals that the GPN does not simply drive all predicted frames to a uniformly clean state. Instead, future frames often exhibit distinct residual noise levels at the final denoising step, and these differences are task-dependent. For instance, in some tasks, the second future frame retains a higher residual noise, implying the policy opts to keep the farther future latent partially masked, especially when uncertainty over future contact or placement outcomes is high. This supports the notion of selective attenuation of information based on utility.

#### 4.4. Case Study (Figure 5)

A representative test case illustrates NoiseGate's practical benefit. While a Stage-1 WAM might generate visually clean future predictions, it can be overconfident about critical events (e.g., a grasp) in early frames, leading to premature failures. NoiseGate, conversely, maintains a higher level of uncertainty (less fully denoised) for the critical grasp frame. This prevents the action tokens from over-relying on potentially unreliable early predictions, allowing the model to integrate evidence from other frames and the current observation, resulting in a successful grasp.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

NoiseGate introduces a conceptual shift in the design of World Action Models (WAMs) by transforming the static, implicit assumption of a shared-scalar timestep into a dynamic, learnable information-gating policy. This work demonstrates that the per-latent timestep schedule is not merely a hyperparameter but a critical design object that can be optimized for task-specific utility.

The potential impact of NoiseGate is multi-faceted:

- **Enhanced Robot Manipulation Capabilities:** By allowing the model to dynamically control the "visibility" and reliability of imagined future observations, NoiseGate contributes to more robust and effective action generation in complex robot manipulation tasks. The observed performance gains on the RoboTwin benchmark suggest that this adaptive gating mechanism enables robots to handle uncertainty more effectively and make better decisions in dynamic environments.
- **Improved Model Interpretability and Adaptability:** The qualitative analyses provide insight into how the learned schedules function, showing that the model intelligently prioritizes and attenuates information from different future time steps based on task requirements. This offers a degree of interpretability into the model's decision-making process concerning future predictions. This adaptive control over information flow is particularly valuable in settings where the importance of future events is highly heterogeneous and context-dependent.
- **Broader Applicability to Generative Models:** The core idea of treating per-token noise levels as learnable information gates is not confined to robotics. The authors suggest that this framework could be broadly applicable to any generative setting where a downstream consumer (e.g., action generation, language generation) reads information from jointly-denoised tokens with varying utility via shared attention. This includes domains such as general video prediction, autonomous driving decision-making, and embodied navigation, where selectively emphasizing or de-emphasizing certain future states could lead to more accurate and robust outputs.
- **Foundational Contribution to Diffusion Models:** The work highlights an under-explored degree of freedom in diffusion-based generative models—the per-latent timestep—and provides a methodology to effectively leverage it. This could inspire further research into dynamic scheduling mechanisms for various generative tasks, moving beyond uniform or fixed denoising trajectories.

The main limitation identified is the data-collection efficiency for training the schedule policy with sparse task-success rewards in simulation, which currently limits scalability to very large, multi-task suites. Addressing this could further unlock the potential of this approach. Nevertheless, NoiseGate presents a novel and effective method for integrating intelligent information gating into WAMs, offering a pathway towards more capable and adaptive robot intelligence.
