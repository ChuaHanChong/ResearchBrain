# Legged Locomotion in Challenging Terrains using Egocentric Vision

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University and UC Berkeley developed an end-to-end system for quadrupedal robots to navigate complex terrains like stairs, gaps, and stepping stones using only egocentric depth vision. This approach, which bypasses explicit elevation mapping, enabled a Unitree A1 robot to achieve 94% success on stepping stones and 100% on stairs and gaps, substantially outperforming traditional map-based and blind locomotion methods.

## Problem

- Traditional legged locomotion relies on elevation maps, which are susceptible to noise from pose estimation errors and demand high computational resources.
- Existing reinforcement learning approaches for legged robots often lack robust vision integration, struggling with terrains requiring precise foot placement.
- Small quadruped robots face unique challenges on high obstacles, as predefined gaits are insufficient for their scale-specific movement requirements.

## Method

- A ==two-phase training scheme==: initial ==reinforcement learning (PPO)== using efficient ==scandots== (teacher policy), followed by ==supervised distillation (DAgger)== to an egocentric depth vision-based student policy.
- An ==end-to-end locomotion system== that directly processes raw egocentric depth images and proprioception to predict robot joint actions.
- ==Recurrent neural network architectures (GRUs)== are incorporated to provide short-term memory, enabling the robot to track terrain features and environmental parameters, with both a ==monolithic== and an ==RMA (Rapid Motor Adaptation)== policy variant explored.

## Results

- The proposed method achieved **60-90%** higher total mean time to fall and average displacement in simulation compared to noisy elevation map-based and blind policies.
- A Unitree A1 robot successfully traversed real-world challenging terrains, demonstrating **94%** success on stepping stones and **100%** on **17cm** high stairs and **26cm** gaps.
- The learning approach enabled the emergence of specialized gaits, such as significant hip abduction for climbing obstacles, and generalized robustly across diverse urban and natural environments.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Direct, end-to-end processing of egocentric depth images, coupled with proprioception, can effectively replace complex and noisy elevation mapping for robust locomotion.
> - A two-phase training strategy, combining reinforcement learning with a simplified terrain representation and subsequent supervised distillation to visual inputs, enables efficient learning and real-time deployment.
> - Allowing gaits to emerge naturally through reinforcement learning, without predefined priors, enables small robots to develop specialized movements (e.g., hip abduction) necessary for navigating obstacles close to their body height.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robotics has long strived to enable robots to traverse challenging and unstructured terrains with the agility and precision observed in animals. This paper directly addresses a significant gap and limitation in current approaches, positioning itself at the forefront of vision-guided legged locomotion.

Traditionally, robotic locomotion on rough terrain has relied on a decomposed approach: first creating an elevation map of the environment, and then planning foot placements and motions based on this map. While effective for certain scenarios, this **elevation mapping and foothold planning** paradigm suffers from several critical drawbacks:
1. **Noise Sensitivity:** Elevation maps are constructed by fusing multiple depth images over time, requiring accurate camera pose estimation (visual or inertial odometry). This process is highly susceptible to noise and drift, leading to inaccurate maps and hindering reliable performance, especially on tricky terrains like gaps and stepping stones.
2. **Hardware Requirements:** Generating and processing high-fidelity elevation maps often demands specialized hardware and significant onboard computational resources.
3. **Biological Implausibility:** This decoupled, map-centric approach diverges significantly from how animals navigate, which often involves tight coupling of vision and motor control with a form of short-term memory rather than explicit metric map building.

More recently, **reinforcement learning (RL)** has revolutionized legged locomotion, enabling robots to learn robust and agile gaits in simulation and transfer them to the real world. However, many successful RL-based locomotion policies are "blind," relying solely on proprioceptive feedback (joint angles, velocities, orientation) and often struggle on terrains that require precise foot placement decisions based on visual information. Some recent works integrate vision by feeding elevation maps into RL policies, but they still grapple with the noise issues inherent in map generation.

This paper distinguishes itself by proposing an **end-to-end locomotion system that leverages egocentric depth vision directly, without constructing explicit elevation maps.** This approach is inspired by biological observations that humans and animals use a continuous stream of egocentric visual information and a form of short-term memory to guide foot placement. By directly predicting joint angles from raw depth and proprioception, the authors bypass the noisy and computationally intensive elevation mapping stage entirely.

Furthermore, the work addresses the specific challenges posed by **smaller quadruped robots** like the Unitree A1. Their diminutive size makes traversing obstacles like high stairs particularly difficult, often requiring specialized, non-standard gaits that predefined motion priors cannot capture. This paper's "tabula rasa" approach, allowing gaits to emerge naturally through RL, is particularly well-suited for such robots.

Compared to other vision-guided methods that might use lidar scans, hierarchical policies, or focus on specific tasks like obstacle avoidance or jumping, this work presents a **unified system capable of traversing a broad variety of challenging terrains (slopes, stairs, curbs, stepping stones, gaps)** using a single, inexpensive front-facing depth camera. It represents a significant step towards creating more autonomous, robust, and biologically plausible legged robots that can operate effectively in complex, unstructured real-world environments with limited onboard resources.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and agile locomotion system for quadrupedal robots that can effectively traverse a diverse range of challenging terrains using only egocentric vision. Specifically, the authors aim to:

1. **Achieve end-to-end locomotion:** Design a single system that directly maps egocentric visual input and proprioception to robot actions (target joint angles), circumventing the traditional, decoupled approach of elevation mapping and foothold planning.
2. **Enable traversal of complex terrains:** Demonstrate the robot's ability to navigate environments featuring stairs, curbs, stepping stones, and gaps, both indoors and outdoors, and in varied lighting conditions.
3. **Utilize minimal sensing hardware:** Accomplish this using a single, front-facing depth camera, contrasting with more complex multi-camera or LiDAR setups often used by larger robots.
4. **Operate on medium-sized, compute-limited robots:** Validate the system on a Unitree A1 quadruped, which is smaller and has limited onboard computational power compared to larger research platforms like ANYmalC or Spot. This necessitates efficient policy computation in real-time.
5. **Develop emergent, specialized gaits:** Allow the robot to discover novel and effective gait patterns through reinforcement learning, rather than relying on predefined or human-designed motion priors, which are often insufficient for the unique challenges faced by smaller robots on high obstacles.
6. **Ensure robustness to real-world perturbations:** Develop a policy that is resilient to pushes, slippery surfaces, and rocky terrains, facilitating successful deployment in natural environments.

The motivations driving these objectives stem from critical limitations of existing robotic locomotion paradigms and insights from natural biological systems:

- **Limitations of Traditional Approaches:** Current methods relying on elevation maps are inherently susceptible to noise from pose estimation errors, demand specialized and often expensive sensor suites, and are computationally intensive. This hinders their reliability and broad applicability, particularly for dynamic and precise maneuvers over discontinuous terrain like stepping stones.
- **Biological Inspiration:** Animals, including humans, exhibit remarkable agility by tightly coupling an egocentric visual stream with motor control. They utilize short-term memory to anticipate terrain features, do not rely on conscious, pre-planned gaits, and learn through trial-and-error. This paper seeks to emulate these principles.
- **Challenges of Small Robots:** Smaller robots face unique challenges; for instance, a stair that is a minor obstacle for a larger robot can be a significant impediment for a small one, requiring substantial hip abduction or other unusual movements. Pre-programmed gaits often fail in such situations, highlighting the need for learned, emergent behaviors.
- **Resource Constraints:** Smaller, more accessible robots typically have limited onboard compute. An end-to-end learned policy that avoids complex optimization or localization offers a more resource-efficient solution, expanding the potential applications for these platforms.
- **Need for Generalization:** A robust vision-based system should generalize across diverse terrains and environmental conditions without extensive re-calibration or specialized policies for each scenario.

By addressing these points, the authors aim to push the boundaries of autonomous legged locomotion, making robots more capable and versatile in complex, unstructured environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is a novel **two-phase training scheme** designed to overcome the computational intractability of direct reinforcement learning with high-resolution depth images. This scheme leverages simulation for both phases, followed by direct transfer to the real world.

#### 3.1. Two-Phase Training Scheme

1. **Phase 1: Reinforcement Learning from Scandots (Teacher Policy)**
 - **Goal:** Train a powerful "teacher" locomotion policy using Reinforcement Learning (RL) in simulation, which can reliably traverse challenging terrains.
 - **Observation Proxy:** Instead of computationally expensive depth images, this phase uses "scandots." Scandots are a cheap-to-compute variant of terrain information, representing a set of (x,y) coordinates in the robot's frame of reference where terrain height is queried. These capture terrain geometry efficiently.
 - **RL Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to learn the policy.
 - **No Gait Priors:** Crucially, the training does not impose any predefined gait patterns or reference motions. This allows optimal, stable, and natural gaits to emerge directly from the reward functions, which primarily minimize energy consumption while adding penalties to prevent hardware damage on complex terrains.
 - **Training Environment:** A diverse set of virtual terrains (slopes, stepping stones, stairs, discrete obstacles) with varying difficulty levels is generated in IsaacGym. A curriculum learning approach promotes the robot to harder terrains as it succeeds and demotes it upon failure, enhancing robustness. Extensive randomization of simulation parameters (e.g., mass, friction, motor strength) and observation noise is applied to aid sim-to-real transfer.

2. **Phase 2: Supervised Learning (Distillation to Egocentric Depth - Student Policy)**
 - **Goal:** Distill the knowledge from the high-performing Phase 1 policy into a "student" policy that operates solely on real-world onboard sensor data: proprioception and egocentric depth images from a front-facing camera.
 - **Supervised Learning:** This phase uses supervised learning (DAgger algorithm) to train the student policy to imitate the actions predicted by the Phase 1 teacher policy. Supervised learning is orders of magnitude more sample-efficient than RL, making the overall training tractable.
 - **Depth Processing:** The raw depth map from the simulated egocentric camera is preprocessed through a ConvNet to extract features before being fed into the policy.
 - **Theoretical Guarantee (Theorem 2.1):** The authors provide a theoretical justification for this two-phase approach, demonstrating that if the Phase 1 policy is near-optimal and the Phase 2 policy closely approximates Phase 1, then the Phase 2 policy will also achieve near-optimal performance. This relies on careful selection of scandot locations and camera field-of-view to ensure the depth input can infer sufficient terrain information.

#### 3.2. Policy Architectures

The authors explore two distinct architectures for the policy, both recurrent to incorporate short-term memory for past visual and proprioceptive information:

1. **Monolithic Architecture:**
 - A conceptually simpler approach where a Convolutional Neural Network (ConvNet) preprocesses the egocentric depth map, and the resulting features (`˜d_t`) are directly fed along with proprioception (`x_t`) and commanded velocities (`u_cmd_t`) into a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU). The GRU then directly predicts the target joint angles (`a_t`). This architecture implicitly learns to track terrain geometry and environmental parameters within its weights.

2. **RMA (Rapid Motor Adaptation) Architecture:**
 - This architecture follows the decoupled design proposed in earlier work by some of the authors ( Kumar et al., 2021). It consists of an MLP base policy that takes proprioception, commanded velocities, and two latent vectors as input:
 - `γ_t`: Encodes local terrain geometry.
 - `z_t`: Encodes environment parameters (e.g., ground friction, motor strength).
 - These latent vectors are estimated by separate RNNs (GRUs): `ˆγ_t` is estimated from the history of depth features (`˜d_t`) and proprioception, while `ˆz_t` is estimated from proprioception history alone.
 - **Advantage:** This modularity allows for disentangling different input modalities, potentially enabling sensor swapping or using one stream to supervise another, and makes the internal representations more interpretable.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Robot Hardware:** Unitree A1 quadruped robot, equipped with a single front-facing Intel RealSense D435 depth camera.
- **Onboard Compute:** Jetson NX (for ConvNet processing) and an UPboard (for the base policy), communicating via UDP socket.
- **Control Frequency:** Policy operates at 50Hz, sending joint position commands to a low-level PD controller running at 400Hz.
- **Simulation Environment:** IsaacGym, providing GPU-accelerated physics simulation for massively parallel RL training.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Blind Policy:** Trained with scandots observations masked with zeros, relying only on proprioception. Used to quantify the benefit of vision.
 - **Noisy Methods (Elevation Map Baseline):** Simulates policies that rely on elevation maps, incorporating large noise during training (similar to Miki et al., 2022) to account for real-world odometry errors. This highlights the challenges of map-based approaches.

#### 3.4. Reward Functions

The reward functions are largely task-agnostic and energy-minimization based, consistent with prior work that showed this leads to emergent, stable, and natural gaits. Key components include:
- Absolute work penalty (`−|τ · q|`).
- Command tracking reward (linear and angular velocity).
- Penalties for foot jerk and drag.
- Collision penalties for contact with calf/thigh (to prevent damage on stairs).
- A constant survival bonus.
Crucially, no explicit gait priors are enforced, allowing the robot to discover optimal movements for its size and the given terrain.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates a significant advancement in vision-guided legged locomotion, both in simulation and on physical hardware. The key findings and results are summarized below:

#### 4.1. Simulation Results (Table 1)

The proposed methods (Monolithic and RMA architectures) consistently and substantially outperform the "Noisy" (elevation map-based) and "Blind" baselines across a variety of challenging terrains in simulation.

- **Overall Performance:** Our methods achieve 60-90% higher total mean time to fall and average displacement compared to baselines.
- **Slopes:** While vision is not strictly necessary for slopes (baselines make progress), our methods still travel up to 27% farther, indicating more efficient and robust traversal.
- **Stepping Stones:** This is where the advantage is most stark. Blind and Noisy baselines barely make any progress (average displacement ~1m), failing due to inability to locate stones reliably or being misled by noise. Our methods travel around 20m, showcasing the necessity and effectiveness of direct vision for such discontinuous terrains.
- **Stairs and Discrete Obstacles:** Our methods travel up to 6.3 times farther than the baselines. The blind policy, though making some progress (by bumping), is less efficient. The noisy elevation map baseline performs poorly here as well, indicating that even a map-based system struggles when the map itself is compromised.

#### 4.2. Real-World Comparisons (Figure 4)

Deployment on the Unitree A1 robot in real-world environments confirms the robustness and efficacy of the vision-based policies.

- **Upstairs (17cm high):** Our method achieves 100% success rate on 13 steps. The blind baseline fails to complete the staircase even once, highlighting the challenge for a small robot without vision.
- **Downstairs (17cm high):** Both our method and the blind baseline achieve 100% success. However, the blind policy learns a "very high impact falling gait" that caused hardware damage (dislocating a leg), whereas our vision-guided policy executes a controlled and safe descent.
- **Gaps (26cm apart):** Our method achieves a 100% success rate. The blind baseline fails completely, falling as soon as a gap is encountered.
- **Stepping Stones (30cm wide, 15cm apart):** Our method achieves a remarkable 94% success rate on these difficult terrains, demonstrating precise foothold placement without explicit mapping or optimization. The blind robot fails entirely.

#### 4.3. Emergent Behaviors and Generalization

- **Adaptive Gaits:** The "tabula rasa" approach (no gait priors) leads to the emergence of specialized gaits. For climbing high stairs (up to 24cm, 89% of its hip height), the robot develops an impressive hip abduction movement, which is essential for its small size and would not typically be pre-programmed.
- **Robustness to Perturbations:** The robot demonstrates robustness to external pushes, slippery surfaces, and rocky terrain. It exhibits impressive recovery behavior even when missing a step on stairs or getting stuck in crevices in natural environments.
- **Diverse Environments:** The policy generalizes well across a variety of settings:
 - **Urban:** Successfully navigates stairs, ramps, and curbs (up to 26cm high).
 - **Natural:** Operates effectively on rugged stairs covered with dirt, pebbles, large rocks, and tree roots on hiking trails. Even on sandy beach terrain, it manages despite slips and unstable footholds.
- **Efficiency:** The two-phase training enables the policy to run in real-time on the limited onboard compute (Jetson NX and UPboard) of the Unitree A1, without requiring expensive hardware or optimization for MPC/localization.

In summary, the results conclusively demonstrate that an end-to-end, egocentric vision-based approach, trained with the proposed two-phase scheme, enables a small quadruped robot to achieve unprecedented agility and robustness on complex, challenging, and varied terrains, significantly outperforming traditional and blind methods.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the field of robotic locomotion, particularly for agile quadrupedal robots operating in complex, unstructured environments. Its significance and potential impact can be understood through several key aspects:

1. **Paradigm Shift in Vision-Guided Locomotion:** The paper successfully demonstrates the first end-to-end locomotion system that directly uses egocentric depth vision for traversing highly challenging terrains (stairs, gaps, stepping stones) without relying on explicit, metric elevation maps. This fundamentally challenges the long-standing "perception-then-planning" paradigm in favor of a more biologically inspired, tightly coupled vision-motor control system. By bypassing the error-prone and computationally expensive elevation mapping step, it offers a more robust and efficient alternative.

2. **Enhanced Capabilities for Small Robots:** The work showcases that even smaller, more resource-constrained robots like the Unitree A1 can achieve high-level agility and robustness on terrains typically considered difficult even for larger, more powerful platforms. The ability to climb obstacles almost as high as its body, utilizing emergent gaits like significant hip abduction, is a testament to the power of learning-based control without predefined priors. This expands the potential applications for compact and cost-effective legged robots.

3. **Improved Robustness and Generalization:** The policy's demonstrated ability to perform reliably across diverse indoor, urban, and natural environments (including rocky trails, slippery surfaces, and sandy beaches) and its resilience to external perturbations (pushes) highlights its impressive generalization capabilities. This is largely attributed to the extensive simulation randomization, curriculum learning, and the memory-driven recurrent architectures that allow the robot to infer terrain properties and adapt its motion.

4. **Computational Efficiency and Real-Time Performance:** The two-phase training methodology (RL with scandots, followed by supervised distillation to depth) is a key contribution to making this complex problem tractable. It allows for efficient training and results in a deployable policy that runs in real-time on limited onboard computation, eliminating the need for heavy-duty processors or constant optimization tasks like MPC or localization.

5. **Biological Plausibility:** By drawing inspiration from how animals use egocentric vision and short-term memory for locomotion, the research offers a step towards more biologically plausible and intuitive robotic control. This alignment with natural systems could pave the way for more adaptive and intelligent autonomous agents.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Advancements in Autonomous Robotics:** This work contributes significantly to the development of highly autonomous and agile legged robots that can operate in dynamic, real-world environments, opening doors for applications in disaster relief, search and rescue, exploration of hazardous terrains, inspection, and logistics in challenging industrial settings.
- **Accessibility of Legged Robotics:** By demonstrating high performance on an inexpensive, medium-sized robot with minimal sensing, this research could make advanced legged locomotion technology more accessible to a wider range of researchers and applications, potentially accelerating innovation in the field.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays a strong foundation for future research in several directions. It encourages further exploration into integrating more complex visual cues (e.g., semantic information), developing more sophisticated memory mechanisms, and enhancing the sim-to-real gap bridging techniques. The authors themselves point to leveraging real-world data collection to continuously improve policies as a future direction, addressing the acknowledged limitation of potential visual or terrain mismatches between simulation and reality.

In conclusion, "Legged Locomotion in Challenging Terrains using Egocentric Vision" represents a landmark achievement in vision-based robot control, pushing the boundaries of what small, agile robots can achieve in complex environments and setting a new standard for robust and biologically inspired locomotion systems.
