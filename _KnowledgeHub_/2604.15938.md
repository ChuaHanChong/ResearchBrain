# VADF: Vision-Adaptive Diffusion Policy Framework for Efficient Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Fudan University introduced VADF, a vision-adaptive diffusion policy framework that dynamically reallocates computational resources during robotic manipulation tasks to enhance efficiency and performance. The system demonstrated up to a 2.46x speedup in inference latency and an average 31.3% increase in success rates on real-world tasks by adaptively managing training optimization and inference scheduling.

## Problem

- Existing diffusion policies in robotic manipulation employ uniform computational allocation, leading to inefficient gradient updates and protracted training convergence due to varying sample difficulty.
- Fixed denoising steps and action horizons during inference cause computational mismatch, resulting in high latency, redundant overhead, and degraded success rates, especially in precision-critical or long-horizon tasks.
- Prior adaptive approaches often require task-specific configurations, architectural modifications, or additional training, hindering their general applicability and ease of integration into existing frameworks.

## Method

- An ==Adaptive Loss Network (ALN)== is introduced to accelerate training convergence by employing a ==learnable timestep sampler== and ==re-weighting mechanism== for ==hard negative mining==, focusing optimization on challenging samples and critical transitions.
- A ==Hierarchical Vision Task Segmenter (HVTS)== enables ==dynamic inference scheduling== by using a Vision-Language Model to decompose high-level tasks into semantic segments and assign adaptive ==denoising steps (Nd)== and ==action horizons (Na)== to each subtask.
- The **VADF** framework is designed to be ==model-agnostic== and ==plug-and-play==, integrating seamlessly into existing diffusion policy architectures without requiring architectural modifications or additional training.

## Results

- Achieved up to a **2.46x speedup** in inference latency on the Push-T task when integrated with DDIM, maintaining high-fidelity execution without performance degradation.
- Increased task success rates by **4.5%** on Push-T and an average of **31.3%** in real-world ARX5 robotic arm experiments compared to baseline diffusion policies.
- Showcased accelerated training convergence, reaching peak success rates with fewer gradient steps and reduced variance in comparison to uniform computational allocation methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically adapting computational resources, such as denoising steps and action horizons, based on real-time visual-language understanding of task complexity enhances both training efficiency and inference performance.
> - Prioritizing optimization on high-loss regions and critical denoising timesteps through hard negative mining and a learnable sampler significantly accelerates training convergence and improves policy robustness.
> - Decomposing long-horizon tasks into semantically meaningful subtasks enables a variable effort paradigm, allowing for precise allocation of computational budgets to precision-critical phases and reducing overall latency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen advancements through the integration of vision-language models (VLMs) and diffusion policies, which enable semantic reasoning and multi-modal trajectory generation. Diffusion policies, initially developed for generative tasks in computer vision, have been adapted for continuous control in robotics, demonstrating improved performance in high-dimensional action generation compared to traditional Gaussian policies. This adaptation, pioneered by works such as Diffusion Policy, treats action sequences as data to be denoised, conditioned on environmental observations.

Despite their capabilities, existing frameworks often employ a uniform computational allocation strategy during both training and inference. This "one-size-fits-all" approach does not account for the inherent spatio-temporal heterogeneity of manipulation tasks, where sample difficulty and stage complexity can vary significantly. Consequently, current diffusion policies frequently experience prolonged training convergence due to inefficient gradient updates and suffer from redundant inference overhead, leading to prohibitive latency and diminished success rates in real-time applications.

Previous research has explored non-uniform timestep sampling and instance-aware schedules in image generation to focus on critical perceptual intervals. However, these methods are often task-specific or handcrafted and have not been widely applied to the dynamic and sample-specific difficulty inherent in robotic trajectories. Acceleration techniques for diffusion policies generally fall into categories such as distillation-based approaches, single-step models requiring architectural modifications, or offline/stochastic scheduling. While some works have investigated improved visuomotor modeling or adaptive denoising, they frequently lack generalization across diverse task complexities. Similarly, hierarchical control architectures and Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning have been effective for long-horizon tasks through semantic planning, but their autoregressive nature can introduce high latency, hindering real-time interaction.

VADF positions itself within this landscape by addressing the aforementioned limitations. It introduces a dual-adaptive framework designed to dynamically reallocate computational resources throughout both the training and inference phases, driven by vision-language understanding. This approach aims to enhance training efficiency by focusing on challenging samples and to improve inference efficiency and robustness by adapting the denoising budget and action horizon to the real-time complexity of subtasks. The framework is presented as model-agnostic and plug-and-play, allowing integration into existing diffusion policy architectures without requiring architectural modifications, additional training stages, or human annotations. This distinguishes it from prior methods that often demand specific architectural changes or incur substantial overhead.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the efficiency and performance of diffusion policies in robotic manipulation by addressing the limitations associated with uniform computational allocation. The authors identify two main problem areas that motivate the development of VADF:

First, during the **training phase**, current diffusion policies typically employ uniform sampling across diffusion timesteps and data instances. This approach fails to differentiate between trivial samples and more informative "hard negatives." Critical factors such as early denoising steps and contact-rich trajectories, which significantly influence policy robustness, often receive insufficient optimization focus under this uniform regime. This results in inefficient use of computational resources, as significant effort is expended on low-information regions, leading to protracted training convergence. The motivation here is to develop a mechanism that can quantify per-step sample difficulty in real-time and prioritize optimization on high-loss regions and critical transitions, thereby accelerating convergence.

Second, during the **inference phase**, the rigid application of fixed denoising steps ($N_d$) and action horizons ($N_a$) irrespective of subtask demands constitutes a "computational mismatch." For instance, a simple preparatory reaching motion in a task may require less precision and fewer computational steps than a complex insertion or striking phase. This uniform allocation strategy leads to resources being over-allocated to simple actions and under-served for complex maneuvers. The consequences include prohibitive latency, redundant computational overhead, and degraded success rates, particularly in long-horizon or precision-critical tasks. The motivation is to design a system that can adaptively segment action sequences into subtasks based on visual input and assign appropriate computational budgets ($N_d$ and $N_a$) dynamically, thereby reducing computational overhead while maintaining or improving success rates.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
- Develop a vision-driven dual-adaptive framework that improves training convergence speed.
- Achieve early success in inference by dynamically adjusting computational resources.
- Design a model-agnostic system that can be seamlessly integrated into various diffusion policy architectures.
- Reduce hard negative class imbalance during training through adaptive loss mechanisms.
- Minimize computational overhead during inference by adapting denoising steps and action horizons based on task complexity.
- Enhance task performance and efficiency across diverse robotic manipulation benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VADF (Vision-Adaptive Diffusion Policy Framework) is presented as a dual-adaptive, model-agnostic, and plug-and-play system that addresses computational inefficiencies in diffusion policies during both training and inference. The framework integrates vision-language understanding to facilitate dynamic resource reallocation.

#### 3.1. Mathematical Foundation and Problem Formulation

The authors first establish the mathematical basis by reviewing the diffusion process, which involves a forward process of gradually adding Gaussian noise to data samples and a reverse process of learning to predict and remove this noise. For robotic control, diffusion policy adapts this by treating action sequences as the generation target, learning to produce smooth, multi-step trajectories conditioned on observations. The standard training loss minimizes the mean squared error between true and predicted noise. The problem is identified as the static configuration of denoising steps ($N_d$) and action horizon ($N_a$) during both training and inference, leading to training suboptimality and inference inefficiency.

#### 3.2. Adaptive Loss Network (ALN) for Training

To improve training efficiency and accelerate convergence, ALN introduces two adaptive mechanisms:

- **Learnable Timestep Sampler:** Standard diffusion training often uses a vanilla Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss, which implicitly suggests certain timesteps are more important. Instead of relying on a fixed theoretical importance weighting, ALN replaces it with a learnable policy, $\pi_\phi(k)$. This policy adapts online to the model's evolving error structure, allowing it to prioritize timesteps that are currently more informative or challenging. The timestep $k$ is encoded using sinusoidal positional embeddings and processed by a 3-layer MLP to produce normalized probabilities $\pi_\phi(k)$, which are then used for sampling. The sampler is trained via REINFORCE with entropy regularization.

- **Re-weighting for Hard Negative Mining:** This mechanism extends importance sampling to the trajectory level. A lightweight weight vector $\mathbf{w}$ (one entry per dataset trajectory) is maintained. After each batch, the weight $w_i$ for trajectory $i$ is updated based on its normalized MSE loss, $r_i$, using a cosine annealing schedule for the update rate $\alpha$. Trajectories associated with higher losses (hard negatives) receive increased sampling probability via PyTorch's `WeightedRandomSampler`. This ensures that the model focuses optimization on difficult trajectories.

Crucially, ALN modifies only the sampling distribution without altering the original diffusion loss formulation, and it introduces no auxiliary objectives or additional training stages.

#### 3.3. Hierarchical Vision Task Segmenter (HVTS) for Inference

HVTS is a zero-shot, training-free plug-in designed to dynamically modulate the ($N_a$, $N_d$) pair during inference, mirroring a variable effort paradigm.

- **Task Decomposition:** HVTS utilizes a lightweight VLM (Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct) to synthesize visual observations and linguistic instructions, decomposing high-level tasks into $K$ semantic segments. Each segment $k$ is assigned a specific computational pair ($N^k_a$, $N^k_d$). This decomposition is performed offline once per task using a single expert demonstration video, where uniformly sampled keyframes and a task-specific prompt are fed to the VLM to generate a structured JSON list of stage templates with names and descriptions.

- **Dynamic Scheduling:** To translate this decomposition into real-time control, HVTS performs online stage identification. A VLM (Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct) analyzes live environmental observations (from a temporal history buffer) against the pre-defined semantic stage library. To ensure smooth transitions, a probabilistic strategy is employed: the VLM outputs a distribution over top-k candidate stages, and the active stage $k$ is sampled from this distribution to update ($N^k_a$, $N^k_d$) dynamically. This allows accelerated schedules for simple transitions and higher-fidelity denoising for precision-critical maneuvers. This parameter assignment is also done offline by the VLM, which receives stage definitions and assigns $N_a$ and $N_d$ values based on perceived difficulty, ensuring at least one precision-critical phase receives higher computation.

- **Model Architecture:** The ALN module uses a 3-layer MLP with 256 hidden dimensions and sinusoidal positional embeddings. The HVTS module uses Qwen2-VL-7B-Instruct for both task decomposition and real-time stage identification. The framework is designed to be compatible with any sufficiently capable VLM.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative and qualitative evaluations of VADF across various robotic manipulation benchmarks, demonstrating its effectiveness in improving training speed, inference efficiency, and task performance.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Comparison

- **Behavior Cloning Benchmark (RoboMimic):** VADF was evaluated on six tasks (Lift, Can, Square, Transport, Push-T, ToolHang) with two proficiency levels of expert data (proficient-human, 'ph'; mixed-human, 'mh'). VADF-C (MLP-based) and VADF-T (Transformer-based) consistently matched or improved task success rates compared to baseline Diffusion Policy (DP) variants. Notably, VADF significantly boosted the "early success rate"—from 85.4% to 91.3% for the MLP variant and from 80.1% to 86.8% for the Transformer variant. This suggests that adaptive inference scheduling accelerates the convergence to successful trajectories.

- **Diffusion Policy Benchmark (Push-T, Kitchen, BlockPush):** The full VADF framework showed absolute improvements over vanilla DP, with a 4.5% increase on Push-T and a 0.6% increase on Kitchen tasks. An ablation study (VADF$^\dagger$, ALN only) indicated foundational improvements of 1.4% (Push-T) and 0.2% (Kitchen), supporting the synergistic effect of combining adaptive training and intelligent inference scheduling.

- **Adroit Simulation:** VADF demonstrated its plug-and-play nature and orthogonality by integrating with the state-of-the-art 3D Diffusion Policy (DP3). DP3-VADF achieved improvements of 10.0, 3.3, and 10.9 percentage points on the Door, Hammer, and Pen tasks, respectively, despite limited expert demonstrations (25 per task) and challenging visual conditions.

- **Efficiency Analysis:** The ALN module was shown to accelerate convergence significantly, enabling VADF to reach peak success rates with fewer gradient steps and reduced variance compared to vanilla DP (Fig. 3). During inference, adaptive scheduling consistently reduced latency without degrading performance. When integrated with DDIM, VADF achieved up to a 2.46$\times$ speedup on the Push-T task (Table 3), balancing high-fidelity execution with real-time computational constraints.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Comparison

- **Push-T Task:** HVTS successfully decomposed the "Push the T-block into the green target area" instruction into five stages: approach, align, push, reach, and complete. During execution, the Dynamic Stage Classifier adaptively allocated a higher denoising budget ($N_d$) for precision-critical stages like "align block" and accelerated inference during simpler "approach block" phases. This prevented common baseline failures like premature pushing during alignment.

- **Adroit-Pen Task:** For the "Rotate the pen tip upward" instruction, HVTS decomposed the task into initial grasp, rotation, and stabilization. The Stage Classifier accurately identified the "rotation in progress" stage and maintained stable finger-level control through synchronized step-level scheduling, even under complex visual conditions and self-occlusion. These qualitative results (Fig. 4) highlight HVTS's ability to enable semantic-level task decomposition and vision-driven stage transitions, supporting efficient and robust control in complex, long-horizon tasks.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

- **Component Contribution:** The ablation study (Table 4) revealed that while the ALN component (VADF$^\dagger$) provided consistent but modest improvements (e.g., 1.4% on Push-T), the HVTS component was crucial for substantial gains, especially in complex manipulation scenarios. The full VADF framework achieved a 4.5% improvement on Push-T and a 0.5% gain on Kitchen, demonstrating the complementary nature and synergistic effect of adaptive training optimization and intelligent inference scheduling.

- **Sensitivity to Scheduling Ranges:** An analysis of $N_a$ (action steps) and $N_d$ (inference steps) ranges showed that a configuration of $N_a \in$ and $N_d \in$ achieved the best trade-off between high success rates and low inference latency (Table 5). The study also indicated that $K=5$ semantic stages provided an optimal balance between semantic precision and scheduling stability.

#### 4.4. Real-world Validation

VADF's cross-domain transferability was validated on a physical ARX5 robotic arm across three tasks: stacking cups, placing an apple in a microwave, and inserting a cup into a paper slot (Fig. 5, Table 7, Appendix A). DP+VADF significantly improved success rates over vanilla DP (from 44.3% to 75.6% average) and DP3+VADF also showed considerable gains over DP3 (from 64.5% to 84.5% average). This confirms that HVTS effectively translates semantic task logic into robust physical execution under real-world conditions by allocating more inference steps to manipulation-critical stages, improving trajectory consistency, and reducing stochastic sampling noise.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

VADF contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by introducing a vision-adaptive framework that addresses computational inefficiencies inherent in current diffusion policies. Its significance lies in several aspects:

Firstly, VADF establishes a paradigm for compute-efficient robotic imitation learning. By dynamically allocating computational resources based on perceived difficulty and task complexity, it mitigates issues of protracted training and redundant inference overhead. This efficiency could enable the deployment of more sophisticated policies in real-world scenarios where computational budgets and latency constraints are critical.

Secondly, the framework's dual-adaptive nature, encompassing both training and inference, offers a comprehensive solution. The Adaptive Loss Network (ALN) accelerates training convergence by prioritizing hard negatives and informative timesteps, while the Hierarchical Vision Task Segmenter (HVTS) enhances inference efficiency and robustness by adaptively scheduling denoising steps and action horizons based on semantic subtasks. This combined approach results in improved overall performance and reliability.

Thirdly, the model-agnostic and plug-and-play design of VADF is a notable contribution. It allows seamless integration into existing diffusion policy architectures without requiring architectural modifications, additional training stages, or human annotations. This generalizability makes VADF broadly applicable and positions it as an enhancement that can readily be incorporated into future diffusion-based robotic control systems. The demonstrated improvements on various benchmarks, including RoboMimic, Adroit, and real-world ARX5 tasks, highlight its effectiveness across diverse manipulation challenges and data scarcity conditions.

Finally, VADF contributes to the interpretability of robotic policies by explicitly decomposing tasks into semantically meaningful stages. This vision-language guided decomposition provides a structured understanding of the robot's high-level planning and low-level execution. This structured approach could facilitate debugging, monitoring, and potentially, human-robot collaboration. The framework's ability to scale diffusion policies to more realistic and complex manipulation scenarios is a significant step towards generalist robot policies.

Future work directions identified by the authors include enhancing the reliability of the VLM for task segmentation, as its performance is intrinsically linked to the underlying perception model. Additionally, extending the "adaptive-compute" paradigm beyond diffusion-based policies to a broader range of generative robotic models is suggested as a promising avenue.
