# Intercepting an Agile Target with Net-Carrying Drones using Competitive Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed a competitive Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning framework for net-carrying drones to intercept an agile target, utilizing low-level control and prioritized self-play. This approach enabled pursuers to achieve catch rates up to 88.4% against a learning evader, with an average time-to-catch of 3-4 seconds, while maintaining low crash rates and exhibiting cooperative tactics.

## Problem

- Autonomous interception of agile aerial targets by drones is challenging due to the target's dynamic maneuvers and active evasion.
- Traditional interception methods often rely on accurate models or predictable target behaviors, which are insufficient against modern, agile quadrotors.
- Existing reinforcement learning approaches for multi-UAV interception frequently use high-level control inputs, limiting agility, or simplify opponent behaviors, neglecting the adversarial element of a learning evader.

## Method

- The interception task is formulated as a ==competitive Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL)== problem using ==MAPPO==, where a team of net-carrying pursuer drones aims to capture an agile evader drone.
- Agents are trained using ==low-level control commands (collective thrust and body rates, CTBR)== within a high-fidelity simulator to enable highly agile and dynamic flight behaviors for both pursuers and the evader.
- **Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play (PFSP)** is implemented to train robust policies against a diverse pool of past opponent strategies, mitigating issues like non-stationarity and catastrophic forgetting in competitive MARL.

## Results

- The proposed PFSP-CTBR pursuer policies achieved catch rates up to **88.4%** against a learning PFSP evader and **93.3%** against a simpler self-play evader, with average time-to-catch around **3-4 seconds**.
- The method maintained low pursuer crash rates (e.g., **7.7%** against the PFSP evader), demonstrating effective learning of boundary adherence and inter-pursuer collision avoidance alongside aggressive interception.
- Ablation studies confirmed that low-level control (CTBR) and Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play (PFSP) are essential components, with PFSP policies showing superior robustness and generalization compared to simpler self-play or no self-play approaches.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Low-level control commands are crucial for enabling the high agility required for robust and safer pursuit and evasion strategies, significantly outperforming higher-level velocity control.
> - Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play effectively combats non-stationarity in competitive MARL, yielding robust and generalizable policies that perform well against diverse adversarial strategies.
> - Complex cooperative interception tactics, such as coordinated pushes towards boundaries and rapid re-engagement after failed attempts, emerge naturally from the reinforcement learning process without explicit programming.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The interception of agile aerial targets by autonomous drones represents a significant challenge within robotics and security. The increasing prevalence of Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) incursions into restricted airspaces necessitates effective solutions for applications such as airspace protection, infrastructure security, and event safety. Fleets of interceptor drones equipped with capture nets are a potential approach, but their efficacy depends on sophisticated control and coordination capabilities when confronting evasive targets.

Traditional interception methods typically rely on accurate models, preplanned strategies, or predictable target behaviors. However, modern quadrotor drones are capable of highly dynamic maneuvers and active evasion, rendering classical approaches less effective. Recent advancements in deep reinforcement learning (RL) have demonstrated the capacity for drones to learn agile flight behaviors directly through environmental interaction, achieving high performance in tasks such as drone racing. Concurrently, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) has shown utility in competitive environments, including complex games like Go, Dota 2, and StarCraft II.

Despite these advances, a gap exists in the application of learning-based methods to multi-UAV interception. Prior RL approaches to pursuit-evasion tasks often employ high-level control inputs and simplified quadrotor dynamics, which can limit the agility of learned behaviors. Some research focuses on RL for low-level control of agile pursuers but assumes fixed evader behaviors. Other studies combine competitive RL frameworks with agile control inputs but restrict their scope to one-versus-one (1v1) pursuit-evasion scenarios.

This work addresses the intersection of these challenges by proposing a solution for multi-pursuer interception against a learning, agile evader. It integrates competitive MARL with low-level control commands for both pursuers and evader, filling a niche where existing research either overlooks the adversarial element of a learning evader or lacks highly dynamic flight capabilities for both parties. The paper contributes to the development of robust, autonomous systems capable of neutralizing agile targets in dynamic, adversarial settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an autonomous system capable of intercepting an agile drone using a team of agile, net-carrying drones. This overarching goal is driven by the growing need for effective anti-drone solutions in various security and protection contexts, where current methods are insufficient against sophisticated, evasive targets.

The specific objectives and motivations underpinning this work are detailed as follows:

1. **Formulate the problem as a competitive Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) task:** The motivation here is to leverage the demonstrated success of MARL in complex competitive environments. By treating the interception as a competition between a team of pursuers and an evader, the system can learn dynamic and adaptive strategies that account for an actively adversarial opponent, unlike methods relying on predictable target models.

2. **Develop a competitive MARL framework for cooperative interception using low-level control:** The paper aims to enable highly agile flight for both pursuers and the evader. This is motivated by the observation that many prior RL approaches for multi-drone tasks use high-level control inputs (e.g., velocity commands) and simplified dynamics, which limit the agents' ability to perform the rapid, complex maneuvers necessary for effective pursuit and evasion against agile targets. By using low-level control commands (collective thrust and body rates), the agents can explore and learn a broader range of dynamic flight behaviors.

3. **Implement a Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play (PFSP) training scheme for robust policy learning:** A key challenge in competitive MARL is non-stationarity, where agents constantly adapt to each other's evolving strategies, leading to potential overfitting to the current opponent and "catastrophic forgetting" of past effective strategies. The motivation for using PFSP is to mitigate these issues. By training against a diverse pool of past opponent policies, prioritized by their difficulty, the system aims to learn robust policies that can generalize effectively against various adversarial behaviors, rather than just the latest or a fixed strategy.

4. **Train agents in a high-fidelity simulator:** The researchers motivate the use of a detailed simulation environment to accurately model quadrotor dynamics, including low-level control responses and collision physics. This allows for the safe and efficient exploration of complex and potentially dangerous maneuvers that would be impractical in a real-world setting during the initial training phases.

5. **Demonstrate performance superiority over standard baselines:** A core objective is to quantitatively show that the proposed MARL-based solution with PFSP and low-level control performs more effectively than conventional heuristic and simpler learning-based methods. This is assessed through metrics such as catch rate, time to catch, and crash rates, providing empirical evidence of the method's advantages.

6. **Conduct ablation studies to validate methodological components:** The paper aims to isolate and evaluate the contribution of PFSP and low-level control commands. This is motivated by the need to confirm that these specific components are indeed crucial for achieving the observed performance improvements, rather than being incidental factors.

7. **Qualitatively analyze emergent cooperative tactics:** Beyond quantitative performance, the research seeks to understand the types of behaviors learned by the agents. The motivation is to demonstrate that complex cooperative strategies among pursuers can emerge from the reward structure and competitive training, without explicit programming of such tactics.

In summary, the motivation stems from the practical demand for effective anti-drone technology, coupled with the recognition that current approaches often fall short in dynamic, adversarial scenarios. The research leverages advanced MARL techniques and high-fidelity simulation to develop agile, adaptive, and cooperative drone interception capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research frames the drone interception task as a competitive Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) problem.

**A. Problem Formulation:**
The scenario involves a team of `N` pursuers, each equipped with a catching net, tasked with intercepting a single evader drone. Both pursuers and the evader are quadrotors with identical maneuvering capabilities operating within an obstacle-free rectangular arena. The pursuers' objective is to capture the evader swiftly, while the evader aims to avoid capture. An episode begins with agents' initial positions randomized within the arena. Capture is defined as the evader's center coming within a specific capture distance of any pursuer's rigid, circular net, which is mounted below the pursuer's body. To prevent the evader from exploiting arena boundaries by "wall-hugging," a buffer zone is established along the walls, where the evader is penalized for entry, effectively constraining its movement to a central volume. The study operates under several simplifying assumptions, including direct observation of target state (no target detection, state estimation, or trajectory prediction), an obstacle-free environment, and a constrained evader area, with relaxation of these assumptions reserved for future work.

**B. Observation and Action Space:**
For each agent `i` (pursuer or evader), its observation `o_i` comprises three components:
- **Self-state (`o_self_i`):** Includes the agent's linear velocity in its body frame and its rotation matrix.
- **Other agents (`o_others_i`):** Contains the relative position and velocity of all other agents (j) with respect to agent `i`, expressed in agent `i`'s body frame.
- **Environment (`o_env_i`):** Consists of the agent's altitude and distances to arena bounds, measured along multiple rays uniformly distributed in the horizontal plane.
All observations are normalized before being processed by the neural networks.
The action space for each agent `a_i` consists of low-level control commands: a collective thrust `a_th_i` and body rates `a_ω_i`. This "collective thrust and body rates" (CTBR) input enables agile flight maneuvers.

**C. Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:**
The control policies are trained using Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO), an Actor-Critic method. This approach utilizes two neural networks per team: a policy network (Actor) and a value network (Critic). The training follows a Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE) paradigm:
- **Decentralized Execution:** Each team (pursuers and evader) has a single, shared policy network. During execution, each agent uses only its local observation to generate its actions (thrust and body rates).
- **Centralized Training:** A single centralized value network for each team is used exclusively during training. This network receives privileged global information, including the exact positions, velocities, and rotation matrices of all agents, as well as the actions taken by the opponent(s) at each time step. This global insight helps address the non-stationarity inherent in multi-agent learning environments.

**D. Rewards:**
The reward function is designed to incentivize desired behaviors while penalizing undesirable ones.
- **Pursuer Reward (`r_P_p`):** Comprises a reward for successful catch (`r_catch`), a penalty for each timestep (`-r_step`), penalties for inter-pursuer body collisions (`-r_coll_p,P`), pursuer-evader body collisions (`-r_coll_p,E`), agent failure (crashing or going out of bounds) (`-r_fail_p`), dynamically infeasible commands (`-r_cmd_p`), and being far from the evader (`-r_dist_p`).
- **Evader Reward (`r_E`):** Includes a penalty for being caught (`-r_catch`), a reward for surviving each timestep (`+r_step`), penalties for evader-pursuer body collisions (`-r_coll_e,P`), evader failure (`-r_fail_e`), infeasible commands (`-r_cmd_e`), and approaching arena boundaries (`-r_bnd`).
 - `r_catch` provides a significant positive reward to all pursuers upon capture and an equal negative reward to the evader, terminating the episode.
 - Pursuer-evader body collisions (`r_coll_p,E`, `r_coll_e,P`) incur a soft, continuous penalty without terminating the episode, encouraging net-based capture rather than body-slamming.
 - Inter-pursuer collisions (`r_coll_p,P`), ground crashes, or going out of bounds (`r_fail_i`) result in significant penalties and episode termination.
 - Crucially, no agent receives a reward when an opponent fails (e.g., crashes), promoting active pursuit/evasion rather than strategies designed to force opponent failure.
 - `r_cmd_i` penalizes high body rates, discouraging dynamically infeasible commands.
 - `r_dist_p` encourages pursuers to stay close to the evader.
 - `r_bnd` imposes an exponentially growing penalty on the evader as it approaches arena boundaries, reinforcing its confinement to the central volume.
All reward terms are balanced by positive hyperparameters (`λ`) tuned during training.

**E. Training Method (Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play - PFSP):**
To address non-stationarity and avoid overfitting, the research employs PFSP.
- Pursuers and the evader train in separate loops, each maintaining a growing pool of past, frozen policies.
- During each training episode, the current training policy of one team plays against a set of frozen opponent policies sampled from the opponent's pool.
- **Sampling Strategy:** 50% of episodes involve playing against the opponent's latest policy to ensure continuous adaptation, while the remaining 50% sample from the opponent pool using a performance-weighted distribution.
- **Performance Metrics:** For a pursuer policy `i` against evader policy `e`, `ρ_P(i, e)` is defined as `1 - (t/τ)` if `i` catches `e` (where `t` is time to catch and `τ` is max episode length), `0` if `i` crashes or `e` evades, and `1` if `e` crashes. The evader's performance `ρ_E(e, i)` is `1 - ρ_P(i, e)`.
- **Prioritization:** A weighting function `f(x) = (1-x)^2` is used, which focuses PFSP on the most challenging opponents by giving higher sampling probability to policies that resulted in lower performance (from the perspective of the current training agent).
- **Policy Update:** When the current policy of a team surpasses a performance threshold against the *full* current pool of opponent strategies, a snapshot of this policy is frozen and added to the team's policy pool. Training then switches to the other team.

**F. Training Setup:**
- **Simulator:** A high-fidelity quadrotor dynamics simulator is used, incorporating low-level control (CTBR inputs), inter-quadrotor collisions, and interactions with the arena and capture nets. It also supports a high-level Incremental Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion (INDI) controller for baseline comparisons.
- **Neural Network Architecture:** Policy networks are two-layer multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) with 256 ReLU units per hidden layer. Their output consists of the mean and standard deviation of a multivariate Gaussian, followed by a tanh activation for bounded continuous actions. Value networks have a similar MLP architecture but with a linear output layer.
- **Hardware and Parallelization:** The entire pipeline, including the simulator, is implemented in Python using JAX, which enables just-in-time (JIT) compilation and massively parallelized execution on accelerated hardware. Rollouts are generated in parallel across 1024 environments. The training was conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 4090, AMD Ryzen 9 7950X3D, and 128 GB RAM, achieving approximately 3.5x10^5 environment steps per second.
- **Training Duration:** Total training amounted to 5 billion environment steps for each team (10 billion total), roughly corresponding to 5 hours and 30 minutes of wall-clock time.
- **Environment Dimensions:** Experiments were conducted in a 32x32x16 meter arena, with the evader constrained to a 12x12x6 meter central volume.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the performance of the trained policies in a 3v1 interception scenario, comparing them against heuristic baselines and various ablations of the learning-based approach. Performance metrics include catch rate, evade rate, crash rates (pursuer, evader, double crash), and a right-censored time-to-catch (set to 10 seconds for crashes or timeouts). Each strategy combination was tested over 10,000 episodes.

**A. Comparison with Baselines:**

- **Heuristic Baselines (FRPN, APF, FRPN+APF):**
 - Heuristic pursuer strategies demonstrated reduced performance against agile, learned evaders.
 - Fast-Response Proportional Navigation (FRPN) exhibited a high crash rate, particularly due to the lack of built-in boundary constraints or inter-pursuer collision avoidance, which was exacerbated in multi-pursuer scenarios.
 - Artificial Potential Field (APF) led to safer flights due to boundary repulsion but often resulted in "Pure-Pursuit behavior," where pursuers chased the evader without effective capture.
 - Combining FRPN with APF repulsion improved catch rates but still proved inconsistent against the learned evader policies.
- **PFSP-CTBR (Proposed Method):**
 - The proposed PFSP-CTBR (Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play with Collective Thrust and Body Rates) pursuer policies demonstrated the highest catch rates and lowest time-to-catch across all evader types, including heuristic and learned evaders. For example, it achieved a 88.4% catch rate against its own PFSP evader and a 93.3% catch rate against the SP evader, with time-to-catch figures around 3-4 seconds.
 - It maintained low crash rates for pursuers (e.g., 7.7% against the PFSP evader), indicating effective learning of boundary adherence and inter-pursuer collision avoidance while executing aggressive interception.
 - The PFSP-CTBR evader policy also proved to be the most robust and agile, achieving high evade rates (e.g., 96.6% against APF pursuers and 77.6% against PFSP-vel pursuers) and very low crash rates (e.g., 0.3% against PFSP pursuers). This suggests it consistently avoided capture or forced pursuers to fail for the full episode duration.

**B. Ablation Studies:**

1. **Impact of Control Level (PFSP-vel vs. PFSP-CTBR):**
 - Policies trained with velocity control (PFSP-vel) exhibited limitations in agility. The PFSP-vel evader showed an increased crash rate when facing pursuers utilizing CTBR or acceleration control.
 - While PFSP-vel pursuers maintained reasonable catch rates against less agile evaders, they were notably ineffective against the highly agile PFSP-CTBR evader.
 - In comparison, PFSP-CTBR agents consistently achieved higher catch and evade rates, coupled with lower crash rates, than PFSP-vel agents across all experimental settings.
 - **Conclusion:** These results indicate that low-level control commands (CTBR) are crucial for enabling the agility required for robust and safer pursuit and evasion strategies, outperforming higher-level velocity control.

2. **Effect of Self-Play (noSP vs. SP vs. PFSP-CTBR):**
 - **noSP (no self-play):** Agents trained against fixed, deterministic opponents showed strong performance against those specific opponents (e.g., noSP pursuers effectively captured the APF evader, noSP evader achieved high evade rates against FRPN-APF pursuers). However, their performance degraded substantially when facing other learned or more agile evaders, with crash rates rising sharply. This demonstrates significant overfitting to the specific training opponent and poor generalization.
 - **SP (self-play against latest opponent):** SP policies showed better robustness than noSP. Their catch rates remained high against various evaders (except the highly challenging PFSP-CTBR evader), and their evade/crash rates were more consistent across different pursuer types (again, with the exception of PFSP-CTBR pursuers). Training against an adapting opponent improved robustness to unpredictable trajectories.
 - **PFSP-CTBR (Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play):** PFSP-CTBR agents demonstrated consistently strong performance across all opponent strategies, maintaining high catch/evade rates and low crash rates. By prioritizing challenging opponents from a pool of past policies, PFSP effectively sustained a strong learning signal, leading to more robust and generalizable strategies than simpler self-play or no self-play approaches.

**C. Qualitative Results:**

- **Evader Behaviors:** The learned evader exhibited diverse, agile evasive maneuvers, effectively utilizing the full 3D space.
- **Pursuer Behaviors:**
 - Pursuers learned to anticipate evader maneuvers and orient their nets to maximize capture probability.
 - Cooperative tactics emerged, including inter-agent collision avoidance and coordinated interception attempts. Pursuers sometimes split, with one initiating a rapid capture attempt while others position themselves for follow-up attempts if the first fails (Fig. 4a, Fig. 5b). They also demonstrated adaptive recovery, quickly re-engaging after failed attempts.
 - A notable strategy was the exploitation of the evader's reluctance to enter its penalized boundary zone. Pursuers would coordinate to push the evader towards these boundaries, blocking escape routes, and then intercept it as it maneuvered to avoid the penalty zone (Fig. 3).
 - While encircling strategies were less frequently observed, likely due to the evader's confined arena promoting direct interception, pursuers did employ encircling when favorable conditions arose (Fig. 4b). Individual capture attempts by a single pursuer were generally unsuccessful.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the fields of robotics, autonomous systems, and artificial intelligence, particularly concerning the interception of agile targets. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Advancement in Anti-Drone Technology:** The work directly addresses the critical need for effective solutions to neutralize unauthorized or rogue drones. By demonstrating the ability of autonomous net-carrying drones to intercept agile targets, it offers a promising pathway for applications in airspace protection, infrastructure security, and event safety, where current methods may be inadequate.

2. **Demonstration of Advanced Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) Capabilities:** The study successfully applies a sophisticated competitive MARL framework (MAPPO with Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play) to a complex, real-world-relevant physical task. This showcases the robustness and adaptability of MARL in dynamic, adversarial environments, moving beyond simpler cooperative or fixed-opponent scenarios.

3. **Validation of Low-Level Control for Agile Robotics:** A key finding is the superior performance of policies trained with low-level control commands (collective thrust and body rates) compared to those using higher-level velocity commands. This highlights the importance of providing agents with fine-grained control over their dynamics to enable truly agile and effective maneuvers in high-speed, dynamic tasks. This insight is crucial for developing real-world agile drone systems.

4. **Generation of Robust and Generalizable Policies:** The implementation of Prioritized Fictitious Self-Play (PFSP) is shown to effectively combat non-stationarity and catastrophic forgetting, common challenges in competitive MARL. The resulting policies are more robust and can generalize well against diverse and unseen adversarial strategies, which is essential for deployable autonomous systems that must operate in unpredictable environments.

5. **Emergence of Complex Cooperative Behaviors:** The qualitative analysis reveals that sophisticated cooperative and adaptive tactics, such as coordinated interception attempts, strategic boundary exploitation, and rapid recovery from failed maneuvers, emerge naturally from the reinforcement learning process without explicit programming. This provides further evidence of the capacity of RL to discover complex strategies for multi-agent coordination.

6. **Enabling Technologies for Scalable RL Training:** The use of the JAX framework for simulator and training pipeline development, enabling massively parallelized execution and just-in-time compilation, significantly reduces training times. This computational efficiency is vital for conducting extensive RL training and paves the way for future research into even more complex and large-scale autonomous systems.

7. **Foundation for Future Real-World Deployment:** While acknowledging limitations such as evaluation purely in simulation and the absence of perception/state estimation, this work establishes a strong methodological foundation. It lays critical groundwork for future efforts in sim-to-real transfer, integration with real-world sensor data, and the development of robust state estimation techniques, bringing the concept closer to practical implementation.

In summary, this research advances the state-of-the-art in autonomous drone interception by demonstrating a robust, learning-based approach. Its findings underscore the importance of competitive MARL, sophisticated self-play mechanisms, and low-level control for developing highly agile and adaptive multi-agent robotic systems with significant potential impact on security and control applications.
