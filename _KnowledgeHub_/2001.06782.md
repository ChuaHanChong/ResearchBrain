# Gradient Surgery for Multi-Task Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces Projection-based Conflicting Gradients (PCGrad), a gradient surgery method that addresses optimization challenges in multi-task learning by explicitly mitigating detrimental gradient interference. It demonstrated improved data efficiency and performance across multi-task supervised learning and reinforcement learning benchmarks, solving all 10 Meta-World tasks and reducing sample requirements by 15 million in MT50 compared to independent training.

## Problem

- Multi-task learning (MTL) often faces severe optimization challenges, leading to worse performance or data efficiency compared to training tasks independently.
- The precise reasons for these optimization difficulties in MTL, such as slower convergence or plateaus, have not been well understood.
- Previous MTL approaches often focused on architectural solutions or distillation, bypassing the core joint optimization problem.

## Method

- Proposes **PCGrad** (Projecting Conflicting Gradients), a model-agnostic ==gradient surgery== method that modifies gradients only when they conflict.
- **PCGrad** iteratively ==projects conflicting task gradients== (where `g_i ⋅ g_j < 0`) onto the ==normal plane== of the conflicting gradient.
- The sum of these modified gradients is then used for the parameter update, allowing beneficial interactions while preventing ==destructive interference==.

## Results

- In multi-task supervised learning, PCGrad improved accuracy (e.g., **2.8%** absolute gain on CIFAR-100 when combined with Routing Networks) and outperformed other optimization methods.
- For multi-task reinforcement learning on Meta-World, PCGrad significantly improved data efficiency, solving all **10 MT10** tasks and **70%** of 50 MT50 tasks while requiring **15 million** fewer samples on MT50 compared to independent training.
- Empirical analysis validated the "tragic triad," showing that conflicting and dominating gradients were prevalent early in training and decreased as PCGrad facilitated learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimization difficulties in multi-task learning stem from a "tragic triad": conflicting gradients, high positive curvature, and large differences in gradient magnitudes.
> - Directly modifying conflicting gradients (gradient surgery) can effectively mitigate this interference, enabling true benefits of multi-task learning.
> - PCGrad is compatible with and enhances existing multi-task learning architectures, showing a complementary approach to MTL research.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep learning and deep reinforcement learning (RL) have achieved remarkable successes in specific domains, from image classification to complex game playing and robotic control. However, a pervasive challenge remains: **data efficiency and the ability to acquire a breadth of capabilities.** Current methods often require vast amounts of data to learn a single task from scratch, which is impractical for real-world systems needing to perform many diverse tasks.

**Multi-task learning (MTL)** has emerged as a promising paradigm to address this. The core idea is to train a single model on multiple tasks simultaneously, encouraging the sharing of representations and knowledge across tasks, thereby ideally leading to more efficient learning and better generalization than training tasks individually.

However, the paper highlights a significant practical bottleneck: **MTL often presents severe optimization challenges.** Counter-intuitively, training multiple tasks jointly can sometimes lead to worse overall performance and data efficiency compared to learning each task independently. This has led to complex multi-task RL algorithms that resort to independent training as a subroutine, followed by distillation, effectively circumventing the joint optimization problem and sacrificing the inherent efficiency gains MTL promises. The fundamental *reasons* for these optimization difficulties in MTL have not been fully understood.

This paper directly addresses this critical gap. It fits into the broader landscape by:

- **Challenging the prevailing narrative:** While MTL theoretically offers benefits, its practical application has been hampered by optimization issues. This work provides a concrete explanation and solution.
- **Differentiating from architectural solutions:** Much prior work in MTL (e.g., Cross-Stitch Networks, MTAN) has focused on designing specific neural network architectures to facilitate knowledge sharing or task isolation. This paper proposes a *model-agnostic* optimization method, meaning it can be seamlessly integrated with existing architectural solutions, often enhancing their performance. This highlights a complementary approach to MTL research.
- **Distinguishing from distillation/decomposition methods:** Some approaches train separate task-specific models and then distill their knowledge into a single multi-task model. This work, in contrast, aims to enable *simultaneous* training of a single, shared model, thereby directly realizing the efficiency benefits of joint learning.
- **Advancing gradient-based optimization for MTL:** Previous optimization-focused MTL methods primarily focused on adaptively re-weighting or normalizing task losses/gradients (e.g., GradNorm, Multi-Task Learning as Multi-Objective Optimization by Sener & Koltun). This paper goes a step further by performing "gradient surgery," directly *altering the direction* and magnitude of conflicting gradients. This is a novel and more aggressive intervention compared to mere scaling.
- **Connecting to continual learning:** There's a tangential connection to continual learning, where methods project gradients to prevent "catastrophic forgetting" of previously learned tasks. While related in technique (gradient projection), the problem setting is different: continual learning focuses on sequential task acquisition, while this paper addresses simultaneous multi-task learning to maximize positive transfer. PCGrad's iterative projection without requiring quadratic programming is also a practical distinction.

In essence, this research aims to unlock the true potential of multi-task learning by tackling a fundamental optimization challenge that has previously limited its effectiveness, offering a general and practical solution that complements existing advancements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to **overcome the significant optimization challenges prevalent in multi-task learning (MTL)**, thereby enabling more data-efficient and higher-performing deep learning and deep reinforcement learning systems that can acquire a wide range of capabilities.

The primary motivations for this objective stem from two key observations:

1. **Disparity between Hypothesis and Reality in MTL:** While MTL is theoretically promising for sharing structure and improving data efficiency and generalization, in practice, it often leads to a difficult optimization problem. This difficulty can manifest as slower convergence, plateaus in the optimization landscape, or even worse final performance compared to training tasks independently. This practical impediment prevents the realization of the full hypothesized benefits of MTL.
2. **Unclear Root Causes of Optimization Difficulties:** Despite significant research into MTL, the precise reasons for these optimization challenges have not been well understood. Prior works pointed to issues like varying learning speeds across tasks or plateaus, but a unifying, actionable explanation was lacking.

To address this, the authors put forth a clear hypothesis: **one of the main optimization issues in multi-task learning arises from detrimental gradient interference.** They further characterize this interference by identifying a "tragic triad" of conditions that, when co-occurring, lead to significantly degraded performance:

- **(a) Conflicting Gradients:** Gradients from different tasks point away from one another (negative cosine similarity). While some conflict is natural and averaging gradients usually works, severe conflict can be problematic.
- **(b) High Positive Curvature:** The optimization landscape exhibits high curvature along the directions of the task gradients. This can lead to overestimation of progress for dominating tasks and underestimation of degradation for dominated tasks.
- **(c) Large Difference in Gradient Magnitudes:** One task's gradient is significantly larger than others, causing it to "dominate" the overall multi-task gradient. This dominance, especially in the presence of conflict and high curvature, can hinder progress on other tasks.

Motivated by this hypothesis, the **key objectives** of the paper are:

- To formally define and empirically validate the "tragic triad" as a significant impediment to multi-task optimization.
- To develop a simple, general, and model-agnostic algorithm—dubbed "gradient surgery"—that directly mitigates gradient interference by altering task gradients.
- To theoretically analyze the proposed method to understand its convergence properties and the conditions under which it improves upon standard multi-task gradient descent.
- To empirically demonstrate the effectiveness of this approach across a diverse set of challenging multi-task supervised learning and multi-task reinforcement learning problems, showcasing improvements in data efficiency, optimization speed, and final performance.
- To show that the proposed method can be readily combined with existing state-of-the-art multi-task learning architectures for enhanced performance, highlighting its complementary nature.

By tackling this fundamental optimization problem, the authors aim to make multi-task learning a more practical and effective paradigm for building truly versatile and efficient AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work lies in its proposed method for "gradient surgery," specifically named **PCGrad (Projecting Conflicting Gradients)**, designed to mitigate the detrimental effects of gradient interference in multi-task learning.

#### 3.1. The "Tragic Triad" Hypothesis and its Empirical Basis

The authors begin by formalizing their hypothesis about the "tragic triad" that causes optimization difficulties:
- **Conflicting Gradients (Definition 1):** Two task gradients `g_i` and `g_j` are conflicting if their cosine similarity is negative (`cos φ_ij < 0`).
- **Dominating Gradients (Definition 2):** Measured by `Φ(g_i, g_j) = 2‖g_i‖₂‖g_j‖₂ / (‖g_i‖₂² + ‖g_j‖₂²)` which goes to zero as magnitude differences increase.
- **High Curvature (Definition 3):** Defined by a large lower bound `C` on the average curvature of the multi-task loss `H(L; θ, θ')`.

They provide an illustrative 2D optimization problem (Figure 1) where a standard optimizer stalls in a region characterized by this triad, and later empirically validate its presence in neural network training. This hypothesis motivates the direct intervention on gradients.

#### 3.2. PCGrad: Projecting Conflicting Gradients (Algorithm 1)

The central methodological contribution is PCGrad. The aim is to modify gradients only when they conflict, preventing destructive interference while allowing constructive interactions.
The procedure for a batch of tasks `B = {T_k}` is as follows:
1. **Compute individual task gradients:** For each task `T_k` in the batch, compute its raw gradient `g_k = ∇_θ L_k(θ)`.
2. **Initialize modified gradients:** Create a copy `g_PC_k = g_k` for all `k`.
3. **Iterative Projection:** For each task `T_i` in the batch:
 - Iterate through other tasks `T_j` (sampled uniformly and in random order from `B \ T_i`).
 - **Check for conflict:** If the current modified gradient `g_PC_i` conflicts with `g_j` (i.e., `g_PC_i ⋅ g_j < 0`), perform a projection:
 - `g_PC_i = g_PC_i - ((g_PC_i ⋅ g_j) / ‖g_j‖₂²) g_j`
 - This operation subtracts the component of `g_PC_i` that points in the direction of `g_j`, effectively projecting `g_PC_i` onto the normal plane of `g_j`.
4. **Final Update:** The optimizer then uses the sum of these `g_PC_k` as the overall gradient for the parameter update (`∆θ = ∑_i g_PC_i`).

**Key aspects of this approach:**
- **Simplicity:** The algorithm is straightforward to implement, requiring only a small modification to the gradient application step.
- **Model-Agnostic:** It operates directly on the gradients, making it compatible with any neural network architecture (e.g., multi-head, shared encoders) and any gradient-based optimizer (SGD, Adam).
- **Iterative Nature:** The sequential projection within a batch (task `i` against `j`, then `k`, etc.) means the order of tasks *within a batch* can matter, although random ordering is used to make it symmetric in expectation.

#### 3.3. Theoretical Analysis

The paper provides theoretical insights into PCGrad's behavior:
- **Convergence (Theorem 1):** In a convex setting with two tasks, PCGrad converges to either the optimal value `L(θ*)` or a point where the two task gradients are perfectly conflicting (`cos φ₁₂ = -1`). The authors note that the latter scenario is unlikely in practice due to noisy SGD estimates, and empirical results confirm practical convergence.
- **Conditions for Loss Improvement (Theorem 2):** For two tasks (possibly non-convex), PCGrad is proven to achieve lower loss after one update than standard gradient descent if:
 - Gradients conflict sufficiently (`cos φ₁₂ ≤ -Φ(g₁, g₂)`).
 - Curvature is high enough (`H(L) ≥ ξ(g₁, g₂)L`).
 - The learning rate is sufficiently large (`t ≥ ...`).
 This theorem directly links PCGrad's effectiveness to the presence of the "tragic triad" conditions.

#### 3.4. Practical Implementations

- **Multi-task Supervised Learning:** Data is sampled to form stratified mini-batches containing examples from multiple tasks. Per-task gradients are computed, and then PCGrad is applied before the optimizer step.
- **Multi-task / Goal-conditioned Reinforcement Learning:** PCGrad is applied to both the actor and critic gradients in SAC. A novel per-task learnable temperature is also introduced to allow task-specific entropy regularization, addressing potential issues with exploration across tasks.

This methodology represents a direct and targeted intervention to address a fundamental problem in multi-task optimization, grounded in a clear hypothesis and supported by theoretical analysis.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents a comprehensive empirical evaluation of PCGrad across diverse multi-task supervised learning and reinforcement learning benchmarks, demonstrating its significant advantages.

#### 4.1. Multi-Task Supervised Learning

PCGrad's effectiveness is shown across several datasets:
- **MultiMNIST:** PCGrad improved accuracy for both left and right digit classification over the baseline Sener & Koltun (96.58% vs 96.45% for left, 95.50% vs 94.95% for right).
- **CelebA (40-task multi-label classification):** PCGrad achieved a lower average classification error (8.69% vs 8.95%) compared to Sener & Koltun, indicating its scalability to a higher number of tasks.
- **CIFAR-100 (20-task classification):** PCGrad alone achieved 71% accuracy, outperforming several prior methods including independent training and Cross-Stitch networks. Crucially, when combined with state-of-the-art architectural methods like Routing Networks, PCGrad further boosted performance to 77.5%, a 2.8% absolute improvement, highlighting its complementary nature.
- **NYUv2 (3 tasks: semantic segmentation, depth estimation, surface normal prediction):** When integrated with the MTAN architecture, PCGrad+MTAN achieved the best scores in 8 out of 9 evaluation metrics compared to MTAN alone and other baselines. This reinforces that PCGrad can enhance existing architectural solutions.
- **CityScapes (semantic segmentation, depth estimation):** PCGrad+MTAN outperformed MTAN in three out of four scores, securing top mIoU and pixel accuracy for segmentation.

Across these supervised learning tasks, the results consistently show that PCGrad either outperforms existing optimization-focused methods or significantly boosts the performance of architectural multi-task learning approaches. The authors suggest that PCGrad may offer a regularization effect in supervised learning, leading to better sharing of representations.

#### 4.2. Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning

PCGrad demonstrates even more dramatic improvements in multi-task RL:
- **Meta-World (MT10 & MT50 benchmarks):**
 - **Data Efficiency & Performance:** PCGrad combined with SAC (Soft Actor-Critic) significantly outperformed baselines (single SAC policy, multi-head policy) in both success rates and data efficiency. It successfully solved all 10 tasks in MT10 and approximately 70% of the 50 tasks in MT50.
 - **Comparison to Independent Training:** While independent SAC agents could eventually solve all MT10 tasks and 70% of MT50, they required substantially more samples (2 million more in MT10, 15 million more in MT50) than PCGrad+SAC. This unequivocally demonstrates PCGrad's ability to leverage shared structure for accelerated learning.
- **Ablation Study (Gradient Direction vs. Magnitude):** An ablation study showed that modifying *both* the direction and magnitude of gradients (as PCGrad does) is critical. Variants that only altered direction or only magnitude performed worse than full PCGrad and also worse than GradNorm (which only scales magnitudes), underscoring the importance of directional "surgery."
- **Comparison to CosReg:** PCGrad significantly outperformed CosReg (which regularizes gradient cosine similarity towards 0), suggesting that while interference should be reduced, positive cosine similarities (indicating beneficial shared directions) should not be entirely eliminated.
- **Goal-Conditioned RL:** Applied to a robotic pushing task, PCGrad+SAC consistently achieved better performance (average distance to goal) and data efficiency than vanilla SAC, extending its efficacy to continuous task distributions.

#### 4.3. Empirical Analysis of the Tragic Triad

The paper provides empirical validation for its central hypothesis by analyzing two Meta-World tasks (reach and press button top):
- **High Curvature:** The multi-task curvature was observed to remain positive and increase during training for both Adam and PCGrad+Adam, supporting the presence of condition (b) in Theorem 2.
- **Conflicting and Dominating Gradients:** Early in training, before tasks were solved, a high percentage of iterations exhibited conflicting gradients and large magnitude differences (conditions (a) and (b) in Theorem 2). As PCGrad facilitated learning for Task 2, the prevalence of these conditions decreased. This direct empirical evidence strongly supports the hypothesis that the "tragic triad" is indeed a major impediment to multi-task learning, and that PCGrad's ability to mitigate these conditions is key to its improved performance.

In summary, the findings robustly support the claim that PCGrad is a highly effective, versatile, and general optimization technique for multi-task learning across both supervised and reinforcement learning domains, enabling the practical realization of multi-task learning's promised benefits.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes a significant contribution to the field of deep learning and reinforcement learning by addressing a fundamental and widely acknowledged bottleneck in multi-task learning (MTL). Its significance and potential impact are multifaceted:

#### 5.1. Fundamental Contribution to Multi-Task Optimization

The paper offers a novel and compelling explanation for the long-standing optimization difficulties in MTL by identifying the "tragic triad" of conflicting gradients, high curvature, and large gradient differences. More importantly, it provides a simple yet effective solution: **gradient surgery (PCGrad)**. By directly altering gradients to mitigate detrimental interference, PCGrad moves beyond adaptive weighting schemes to a more direct, directional manipulation, proving that such interventions can unlock the full potential of shared representations.

#### 5.2. Enabling Practical Multi-Task Learning

The core significance lies in making MTL more practical and effective. Historically, MTL often failed to deliver its hypothesized benefits (data efficiency, better performance through shared structure) compared to learning tasks independently. PCGrad empirically demonstrates that by alleviating these optimization challenges, these benefits *can* indeed be realized, as shown by substantial gains in data efficiency and asymptotic performance across a wide range of tasks (e.g., 15 million fewer samples needed in MT50). This paves the way for building systems that can learn a broader repertoire of skills more efficiently.

#### 5.3. Generality and Complementarity

- **Model-Agnostic:** PCGrad's strength is its universality. It operates at the gradient level, making it compatible with any deep learning architecture (fully shared, multi-head, modular, etc.) and any gradient-based optimizer (Adam, SGD, etc.).
- **Complementary to Existing Methods:** Crucially, the paper shows that PCGrad is not a replacement but a *complement* to prior MTL architectural advancements. When combined with state-of-the-art architectures like Routing Networks or MTAN, PCGrad consistently leads to further performance improvements. This suggests a powerful synergy that can drive future MTL research.
- **Broad Applicability:** Demonstrated success in both multi-task supervised learning (vision tasks like segmentation, depth estimation, classification) and multi-task/goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (complex robotic manipulation) highlights its wide applicability across different learning paradigms.

#### 5.4. Opening New Research Avenues

The concept of "gradient surgery" and the identified "tragic triad" could extend beyond multi-task learning. The authors suggest potential applications in:
- **Meta-learning:** Where a model learns to learn new tasks, often involving optimizing over distributions of tasks.
- **Continual Learning:** Where models learn sequentially and need to avoid catastrophic forgetting while also leveraging positive transfer.
- **Multi-goal Imitation Learning:** Where a policy needs to generalize across various goals.
- **Multi-agent Optimization / Two-player Games (e.g., GANs):** Where conflicting objectives or non-cooperative agents lead to complex, unstable gradient dynamics. PCGrad's principles might offer ways to stabilize training in these settings.

#### 5.5. Real-World Impact

The practical implications of enabling more efficient and effective multi-task learning are vast:
- **Computer Vision:** Development of more versatile vision systems that can simultaneously perform object detection, semantic segmentation, and depth estimation, leading to improved performance in applications like autonomous driving, medical imaging, and augmented reality.
- **Robotics:** Revolutionizing the training of robotic agents. Instead of learning each skill in isolation, robots could learn complex skill repertoires (e.g., surgical tasks like stitching and tumor removal, or kitchen chores like cooking and dishwashing) much more efficiently, making advanced robotic systems more feasible and adaptable for real-world deployment.

#### 5.6. Broader Impact Considerations

The authors acknowledge the general risks associated with deep learning and RL systems, such as safety concerns (e.g., autonomous driving failures), difficulties in reward specification for RL, biases in supervised learning from training data, and the compute/data-intensive nature of training. While PCGrad itself doesn't introduce new ethical risks, it amplifies the capabilities of these systems, making it even more crucial for future research to focus on robustness, safety, bias mitigation, and equitable access to trained models and data.

In conclusion, "Gradient Surgery for Multi-Task Learning" is a highly impactful paper that addresses a core bottleneck in a critical area of AI. By offering a novel, principled, and empirically validated solution, it significantly advances the state-of-the-art in multi-task learning, paving the way for more capable, efficient, and versatile artificial intelligence systems.
