# Learn from What We HAVE: History-Aware VErifier that Reasons about Past Interactions Online

> [!summary] Summary
> The History-Aware VErifier (HAVE) enhances robotic manipulation in visually ambiguous real-world scenarios by explicitly leveraging past interaction history. This method significantly reduces failure rates and interaction steps across various tasks, including articulated objects and multimodal doors, by decoupling action generation from verification.

## Problem

- Robots struggle with visually ambiguous objects where a single observation corresponds to multiple correct manipulation strategies due to unobserved latent states.
- Existing history-conditioned generative policies perform suboptimally because training on heterogeneous expert datasets (where optimal actions vary for the same observation) leads to a 'reward gap'.
- Purely visual policies are insufficient for tasks requiring disambiguation through interaction, as they cannot infer hidden object properties from static observations.

## Method

- **HAVE** proposes a two-stage generation-verification paradigm, where an unconditional ==diffusion-based generator== proposes diverse candidate actions.
- A ==history-aware verifier==, using ==PointNet++== and ==Transformer== layers, evaluates these proposals by explicitly reasoning about the current observation, proposed action, and the entire sequence of past observations and action outcomes (==observation flow==).
- The verifier trains on an offline dataset comprising history sequences and action proposals, labeling each with ground-truth scores to learn to estimate the reward of a proposed action given interaction history.

## Results

- HAVE achieved significantly lower failure rates (e.g., **2.5%** vs. **13.4%** for FlowBot3D) and reduced mean steps to task completion in simulated articulated object and multimodal door tasks.
- The framework successfully transferred to a real-world ambiguous door, demonstrating higher success rates and fewer steps by effectively suppressing actions belonging to previously failed modes.
- HAVE performed robustly in uneven object pick-up tasks and proved more resilient to noisy observation flow estimates compared to alternative architectures, highlighting its practical applicability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling action generation and verification is effective for ambiguous manipulation, as a verifier with even slightly better-than-random accuracy can significantly improve expected action quality by reasoning about heterogeneous optimal solutions.
> - Explicitly encoding and reasoning about interaction history, including past successes and failures via observation flow and dense action fields, enables robots to adapt and disambiguate uncertain scenarios more efficiently.
> - The proposed architecture, incorporating attention mechanisms over action and observation embeddings, provides robustness against noisy perception and effectively learns from negative interactions to suppress suboptimal actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of **robotics, machine learning, and interactive perception**, specifically addressing challenges in **ambiguous object manipulation**. It makes a significant contribution by integrating a **generation-verification paradigm** into a robot learning framework, a concept gaining increasing traction across various AI domains.

Historically, robot manipulation has grappled with the problem of **interactive perception**, where robots actively probe their environment to gather more information about hidden properties or dynamics. HAVE explicitly builds on this tradition by enabling robots to learn from trial-and-error interactions and adapt their strategies. It extends previous work in learning articulation flow (e.g., FlowBot3D, FlowBotHD) by addressing the fundamental problem of visual ambiguity, where objects appear identical but require different manipulation strategies.

In machine learning, the **generation-verification paradigm** has seen remarkable success in areas like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), and, more recently, in improving reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs). These paradigms recognize that explicitly separating the process of *proposing* solutions from *evaluating* them can lead to more robust and higher-quality outcomes. This paper leverages this insight for robotics.

The authors directly motivate their approach by citing **Setlur et al.**, which demonstrates the theoretical and empirical difficulties of training purely generative policies when the ground-truth solutions are heterogeneous for the same input – a situation directly analogous to ambiguous object manipulation where a single visual observation can correspond to multiple correct actions depending on an unobserved latent state. This theoretical grounding highlights that HAVE is not merely an engineering heuristic but a principled solution addressing a known limitation of direct imitation learning in such contexts.

Furthermore, HAVE differentiates itself from conventional **history-conditioned generative policies** by explicitly *decoupling* generation from verification. While many approaches use in-context learning or reinforcement learning to adapt robot behavior based on history, HAVE argues that a standalone verifier can more effectively reason about the outcomes of past interactions, particularly failures, to guide future action selection. This contributes to the growing body of work advocating for explicit verification mechanisms to enhance the robustness and reliability of autonomous systems, including safety-focused approaches in robotics.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robotic systems to **disambiguate uncertain scenarios in online manipulation tasks by effectively leveraging their past interactions**. Robots often encounter visually ambiguous objects whose internal mechanisms or physical properties are not immediately apparent. For instance, a closed door might look the same whether it needs to be pushed or pulled, or two identical-looking boxes might require different lifting strategies due to uneven mass distribution. The goal is for a robot to **explore through trial-and-error, observe the outcomes of its actions, and adapt its future strategy accordingly**, continuously updating its internal model to improve behavior.

The core motivation stems from an identified limitation of existing approaches:
1. **Inherent Visual Ambiguity**: Many real-world objects present visual ambiguities, making it impossible for a robot to determine the correct manipulation strategy from a single observation alone. A purely visual policy, no matter how sophisticated or large, would struggle in such cases.
2. **Suboptimal Performance of History-Conditioned Generators**: While a natural extension is to use history-conditioned generative models to adapt, the authors empirically found these models to perform suboptimally. This aligns with theoretical findings which suggest that training a generative policy to imitate a heterogeneous expert dataset (where the "correct" action varies for the same observation due to unobserved states) leads to a significant "reward gap" and reduced data efficiency. In ambiguous settings, a single observation can correspond to multiple valid ground-truth actions, making the expert dataset inherently heterogeneous.

To overcome these challenges, the authors propose a **decoupling of action generation from action verification**. Their key motivation is that:
- An **unconditional diffusion-based generator** can propose a diverse set of candidate actions, exploring the action space broadly without being burdened by historical context.
- A **history-aware verifier** can then explicitly reason about the observed outcomes of past interactions (both successes and failures) to assess the likelihood of success for each proposed action, thus selecting the most promising one.

The theoretical analysis provided reinforces this motivation, demonstrating that even a verifier with better-than-random-chance accuracy (p_V > 0.5 for binary rewards) can significantly improve the expected action quality by reducing the failure rate. This establishes a strong foundation for why a verifier-based approach is superior in ambiguous, heterogeneous environments compared to relying solely on a history-conditioned generator.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed method, **History-Aware VErifier (HAVE)**, lies in its two-stage process: an **unconditional diffusion-based generator** to propose diverse actions, and a **history-aware verifier** to select the most promising action by reasoning about past interactions.

#### 3.1. Overall Framework

At each timestep *t*:
1. **Action Generation**: An unconditional diffusion-based generator ($G_\phi$) proposes *N* candidate actions $\{a_t^{(0)}, \dots, a_t^{(N-1)}\}$ based on the current observation $o_t$. This generator is trained to produce diverse, potentially viable actions without explicit conditioning on interaction history.
2. **Action Verification**: A history-aware verifier ($V_\theta$) evaluates each proposed action. It takes as input the current observation $o_t$, the action proposal $\hat{a}$, and the entire history of past observations and actions $\{(o_0, a_0, \dots, o_{t-1}, a_{t-1}, o_t)\}$. It outputs a scalar score $s_t^{(i)}$ for each proposal, reflecting its estimated reward.
3. **Action Selection**: The action with the highest estimated score, $a_t^{(\hat{i})} = \arg \max_i s_t^{(i)}$, is selected and executed by the robot.

#### 3.2. Dataset Generation

To train the history-aware verifier to adapt quickly, an offline dataset is generated. Each sample includes a history sequence (of varying lengths) and a set of action proposals. History sequences are constructed using either random actions (likely failures) or ground-truth actions (obtained from privileged simulator knowledge). Action proposals comprise ground-truth actions, random actions, and transformed history actions (adjusted to the current state). Each action proposal and history action is labeled with a ground-truth score based on task success (e.g., amount of door opening, proximity to mass center).

#### 3.3. History-Aware Verifier Architecture

The verifier is designed to explicitly model relationships between proposed actions, historical actions, and their observed outcomes:
- **Action Embeddings ($E_{action}$)**: Both the proposed action $\hat{a}$ and historical actions $\{a_i\}$ are represented as **dense action fields**. This representation encodes a 3D action vector (point and direction) across the observed point cloud, with magnitude decaying from the contact point. These dense action fields are then encoded into feature embeddings ($\hat{a}_{emb}, \{a_{emb,i}\}$) using a **PointNet++** point cloud encoder, which is effective for 3D geometric understanding.
- **Observation Embeddings ($E_{obs}$)**: The outcomes of past actions are encoded by processing pairs of consecutive observations $(o_{i-1}, o_i)$. A crucial step is to compute the **"Observation Flow"**: the 3D point displacement from each point in $o_{i-1}$ to its corresponding location in $o_i$. This flow is then encoded by another **PointNet++** into observation embeddings $\{f_i\}$. Ground-truth flow is used for training, while the DELTA 3D tracker provides estimated flow during inference.
- **Transformer Layers**:
 - **Action Transformer**: Processes the sequence of action embeddings ($\hat{a}_{emb}$ and $\{a_{emb,i}\}$) to produce updated embeddings ($\hat{a}'_{emb}$ and $\{a'_{emb,i}\}$), enabling sequential reasoning.
 - **Observation Transformer**: Processes the observation embeddings $\{f_i\}$ to produce updated embeddings $\{f'_i\}$.
- **Unconditional Action Encoder ($E_{uncond}$)**: Provides an independent evaluation of the proposed action $\hat{a}$ without considering history, yielding an unconditional value $\hat{v}_u$. This is used to handle cases where history is sparse or irrelevant.
- **Explicit Dot-Product Attention**: This is the core reasoning mechanism of the verifier. $\hat{a}'_{emb}$ acts as the query (Q). The updated history action embeddings $\{a'_{emb,i}\}$ serve as keys (K), and the updated history observation embeddings $\{f'_i\}$ serve as values (V). The attention mechanism computes similarities between the proposed action and historical actions, using these similarities to weigh the historical outcomes. An unconditional logit $\mu_{qk}$ and $\hat{v}_u$ are concatenated to the values, providing a fallback when no similar actions are found in the history.
- **Score Decoder**: The final aggregated embedding $\hat{v}$ passes through a small MLP with a tanh activation to output the final scalar score $s \in [-1, 1]$.

#### 3.4. Training Objectives

The verifier is trained with three MSE losses:
1. **Final Score Loss**: Supervises the predicted score $s$ for the action proposal against its ground-truth score.
2. **Unconditional Score Loss**: Supervises the $\hat{v}_u$ embedding (after passing through the score decoder) with the ground-truth reward for the proposed action, encouraging a good baseline evaluation.
3. **History Score Loss**: Supervises the $\{f'_i\}$ embeddings (after passing through the score decoder) with the ground-truth rewards of the corresponding history actions, promoting robust encoding of action outcomes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluations consistently demonstrate HAVE's effectiveness across various challenging manipulation tasks in both simulated and real-world environments.

#### 4.1. General Articulated Objects in Simulation

- **Performance vs. Baselines**: HAVE (with ground-truth observation flow) achieved a significantly lower failure rate (2.5%) compared to all baselines: FlowBot3D (13.4%), FlowBotHD (w/o CC) (21.9%), Generator Only (22.8%), and Conditional Diffusion (19.7%). Even against FlowBotHD (w/ CC) (6.2%), which incorporates a heuristic consistency check, HAVE showed a nearly 3x reduction in failure rate. This robustly validates the benefits of explicitly decoupling generation from verification.
- **Ablation Analysis**:
 - **Sparse Action**: Using a sparse action representation (only a single action vector at the contact point) drastically increased the failure rate, highlighting the importance of the dense action field representation.
 - **Point Cloud as Result**: Directly using raw point cloud sequences for results without explicit observation flow also led to degraded performance, confirming the value of extracting meaningful motion information.
 - **w/o History/Unconditional Score Loss**: Removing these auxiliary losses resulted in slight performance drops, indicating their contribution to better model learning.
- **Oracle Sampler/Verifier**: Oracle experiments revealed that an ideal generator (Oracle Sampler) or an ideal verifier (Oracle Verifier) further reduced the failure rate (1.7% and 1.1% respectively), suggesting remaining potential for improvement within the framework.

#### 4.2. Multimodal Doors in Simulation

For tasks involving doors with the same geometry but different opening directions (push/pull, left/right), HAVE significantly outperformed baselines. It reduced the failure rate and, importantly, decreased the mean steps required to open the doors, demonstrating improved **efficiency in disambiguating different modes through exploration**.

#### 4.3. Real-World Ambiguous Door

HAVE successfully transferred to a real-world custom-made ambiguous door. It exhibited a **greater success rate and a lower number of mean steps to open** across different modes (push left/right, pull left/right). Visual analysis from real-world trials showed that HAVE effectively suppressed actions belonging to previously failed modes, even when the generator continued to propose them, ultimately selecting the correct action to open the door. This confirms HAVE's practical applicability and robustness.

#### 4.4. Uneven Object Pick-up

In the task of lifting objects with unknown, unevenly distributed mass, HAVE significantly reduced the failure rate on both the training "ambiguous rod" dataset and an "unseen object" test dataset. Unlike articulated objects, here HAVE performed well with both unconditional and conditional generators, possibly due to a simpler action space. Visualizations showed HAVE's ability to narrow down the theoretical center of mass range with each interaction, leading to more efficient and successful lifts by learning from failures.

#### 4.5. Analysis of Design Choices

- **Robustness to Noisy Flow Estimates**: Comparing HAVE to a "Vanilla Transformer" architecture, HAVE proved more robust to noisy observation flow estimates (from the DELTA 3D tracker), highlighting the benefits of its explicit attention design in handling real-world sensing imperfections.
- **Number of Samples**: The verifier showed high sample efficiency, with significant performance improvements achieved with as few as 5 samples, and continuous but diminishing returns with more samples. The computational cost for generation and verification scaled linearly with sample count, demonstrating practical viability.
- **Verifier Performance w.r.t. History Type and Length**:
 - **Failure History**: The verifier consistently assigned lower scores to actions that led to past failures, almost always choosing actions from "other modes" and demonstrating a strong ability to learn from negative interactions.
 - **Success History**: Even with highly occluded observations, the verifier could leverage successful histories to reason about optimal grasp points (preferring higher leverage) and correct action directions.
 - **History Length**: Performance improved significantly from 1-step to 5-step histories, with marginal gains or potential instability for very long histories, suggesting an optimal context window.
- **HAVE with Conditional Generators**: HAVE paired with an unconditional generator generally outperformed pairing with a conditional generator in complex environments. This suggests that unconditional generators, being more expressive and diverse, provide richer action proposals for the verifier to select from, while conditional generators might suffer from mode collapse, limiting the verifier's impact.
- **Classifier Guidance**: Direct application of classifier guidance (using verifier gradients to guide the diffusion process) showed only minor improvements, significantly underperforming HAVE. This suggests that direct gradient-based guidance might be less effective due to transformations from noisy flows to action fields or out-of-distribution intermediate states for the verifier.
- **Generalization and Capacity**: HAVE exhibited strong generalization to unseen instances and reasonable generalization to unseen categories. Further analysis on model capacity indicated that larger verifier models could overfit, suggesting an appropriate balance was struck with the chosen architecture size.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "Learn from What We HAVE" paper presents a highly significant advancement in robotic manipulation, particularly for tasks involving inherent ambiguity and multi-modality. Its contributions span both theoretical foundations and practical applications, with broad implications for future robot learning systems.

**Theoretical Significance**:
- The paper provides a compelling **theoretical justification** for adopting a generation-verification paradigm in robotics. By explicitly linking its approach to recent theoretical work, it rigorously demonstrates that employing a verifier with even a slightly better-than-random accuracy can significantly improve the expected quality of selected actions, especially in scenarios where optimal solutions are heterogeneous for the same partial observation. This formal backing strengthens the argument for decoupling generation and verification beyond mere empirical success.

**Practical Impact and Robustness**:
- **Enhanced Disambiguation**: HAVE directly tackles the pervasive problem of visual ambiguity in real-world manipulation. By actively learning from the outcomes of past interactions, it enables robots to adapt and infer hidden object properties (e.g., door opening mechanisms, mass distribution) more effectively and efficiently than purely generative policies.
- **Improved Efficacy and Efficiency**: The empirical results across diverse simulated and real-world environments (articulated objects, multimodal doors, uneven pick-up) consistently show substantial reductions in failure rates and, critically, fewer steps required to achieve task success. This translates directly to more reliable and efficient robotic operation in unstructured environments.
- **Generalizable Framework**: HAVE's architecture, built on dense action fields, observation flow, and explicit attention, provides a generalizable framework for history-aware reasoning. Its superior robustness to noisy perception (e.g., estimated observation flow) further underscores its readiness for real-world deployment.
- **Learning from Failures**: A key strength demonstrated is the verifier's ability to effectively learn from failed interactions, actively suppressing suboptimal actions and guiding the robot towards successful strategies. This is crucial for autonomous exploration and learning in unknown environments.

**Broader Implications for AI and Robotics**:
- **Validation of Generation-Verification Paradigm**: This work serves as a powerful validation of the generation-verification paradigm's utility in a complex, physical domain like robotics. It suggests that this decoupled approach, which has proven successful in LLMs and other generative models, is a promising avenue for improving intelligence and robustness across various AI systems.
- **Inspiration for Adaptive Policies**: HAVE could inspire the development of more adaptive, history-aware manipulation policies. Future robot systems might increasingly rely on explicit mechanisms for evaluating and refining proposed actions based on continuous interaction experience, moving beyond single-shot decision-making.
- **Addressing Data Heterogeneity**: The paper highlights a fundamental challenge in learning from diverse datasets where optimal actions are not unique. Its solution offers a blueprint for handling such heterogeneity, which is common in many real-world robotic tasks.

**Limitations and Future Directions**:
The authors acknowledge that the pipeline's performance is ultimately bounded by the generator's expressiveness. If the generator fails to propose any good actions, the verifier cannot improve outcomes. Future work aims to explore how the verifier can actively guide and improve the generator's proposals. Another critical area is to reduce reliance on privileged knowledge (simulator-derived ground-truth scores) for training the verifier, moving towards more unsupervised or self-supervised score learning in real-world scenarios.

In conclusion, HAVE represents a significant step towards more intelligent and adaptable robotic manipulation. By marrying the power of modern generative models with a principled, history-aware verification mechanism, it offers a robust solution for dealing with the inherent ambiguities of the physical world, setting a promising direction for future research in robot learning.
