# Neuro-Cognitive Reward Modeling for Human-Centered Autonomous Vehicle Control

> [!summary] Summary
> The University of Technology Sydney researchers developed an EEG-guided decision-making framework for autonomous vehicles, which predicts human cognitive states from visual scenes to provide implicit reward signals for reinforcement learning. This approach yielded superior driving performance and collision avoidance in simulated emergency braking and left-turn scenarios compared to baselines, including traditional RLHF, by enabling more human-aligned and safer control policies.

## Problem

- Existing end-to-end autonomous driving models struggle with human-like reasoning, generalization to out-of-distribution scenarios, and robust decision-making in complex interactive situations.
- Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) lack efficient mechanisms to align AI behavior with human expectations and nuanced cognitive responses, with traditional RLHF being time-consuming and potentially indirect.
- Prior work using physiological signals like EEG to guide RL often requires real-time data collection during inference or responds only after an error occurs, limiting practicality and scalability.

## Method

- A novel ==EEG-guided== decision-making framework was developed to leverage neuro-cognitive signals (Event-Related Potentials, ERPs) as implicit feedback for reinforcement learning in autonomous vehicles.
- A human cognitive reward model, a lightweight CNN, was designed and trained to predict ERP occurrence directly from visual scene information, removing the need for real-time EEG during inference.
- The predicted ERP probability, representing cognitive load or surprise, was integrated as a negative component into the reward function of a TD3-based RL algorithm to guide the autonomous agent's policy.

## Results

- The lightweight CNN for ERP prediction achieved an **82%** average accuracy and a real-time inference speed of 204 frames per second, outperforming established image classification baselines.
- The proposed framework consistently achieved the highest scores in Route Completion, Driving Score, and Infraction Penalty across both emergency braking and left-turn scenarios in the CARLA simulator, surpassing several baselines including traditional RLHF.
- Analysis of machine attention revealed that the proposed model developed more focused attention on critical scene elements (e.g., the lead vehicle) compared to baselines, indicating improved internal representations for decision-making.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human cognitive responses, specifically ERPs, exhibit a statistically significant correlation with driver reaction time and cognitive effort during critical driving events, affirming their utility as an implicit feedback signal.
> - It is feasible to accurately predict human cognitive states (ERPs) from visual scene information using a lightweight CNN, which operates at high inference speeds, enabling scalable neuro-cognitive reward signaling without real-time physiological sensors.
> - Incorporating neuro-cognitive reward signals into RL significantly enhances the autonomous agent's ability to focus on critical scene elements, leading to the learning of safer and more human-aligned driving behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Autonomous Intelligent Vehicles (AIVs) has seen significant advancements, particularly with the integration of deep learning techniques. End-to-end autonomous driving (E2E-AD), which directly maps raw sensor inputs to control signals, has shown promising performance in simulated environments like CARLA. However, existing E2E-AD models, often trained via imitation learning (IL), primarily replicate expert trajectories and exhibit limitations in human-like reasoning, generalization to out-of-distribution scenarios, and robust decision-making in complex interactive situations such as emergency braking. These limitations are largely attributed to the distribution shift problem inherent in IL, where models struggle with unforeseen failure cases not present in the training data.

Reinforcement learning (RL) offers an alternative paradigm, learning through trial and error and adapting dynamically to new environments, which has the potential to mitigate the distribution shift problem. However, both IL and RL lack inherent mechanisms to ensure alignment with human expectations and preferences. This gap has led to the emergence of Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF), which trains reward models based on human preferences to guide AI behavior. Traditional RLHF methods typically rely on explicit human feedback, such as manually ranking generated outputs or comparing trajectory segments, a process that is often time-consuming and may not fully capture nuanced cognitive responses.

This research addresses these challenges by exploring a more intuitive and efficient approach to integrate human cognitive insights into RL for autonomous driving. It extends the concept of RLHF by leveraging neuro-cognitive signals, specifically event-related potentials (ERPs) from electroencephalography (EEG), as a form of implicit feedback. While previous work has explored physiological signals like eye-tracking and error-related potentials (ErrP) from EEG to guide RL, these often require real-time data collection during inference or respond only after an error occurs. This paper situates itself within the evolving landscape of brain-computer interfaces (BCIs) and neuro-inspired AI, particularly building upon advancements in predicting brain responses from visual stimuli using deep learning. By moving towards predicting cognitive states from visual input, this work aims to create a more scalable and practical neuro-cognitive reward mechanism for autonomous systems, thereby enhancing human-AI alignment and system safety.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized deficiencies in current autonomous driving systems, particularly their limited capacity for human-like reasoning, generalization beyond training data, and alignment with human expectations. Despite advancements in computer vision and E2E-AD, models struggle in out-of-distribution and interactive scenarios, often due to their reliance on imitation learning which suffers from the distribution shift problem. While reinforcement learning (RL) offers a dynamic learning paradigm, it often lacks explicit mechanisms to align with human preferences. Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) has emerged to bridge this gap, but traditional methods involve time-consuming, explicit, and potentially indirect human labeling or ranking.

The paper identifies the need for a more natural and efficient way to incorporate human cognitive insights into RL for autonomous driving, specifically without interrupting human behavioral responses or requiring real-time human physiological input during operation. Electroencephalography (EEG) and its event-related potentials (ERPs) are presented as a promising, non-invasive avenue for this, offering direct, millisecond-level temporal resolution of natural brain responses to stimuli, including unexpected or surprising events and task difficulty. The P3 component of ERPs, in particular, is highlighted for its correlation with cognitive effort and resource allocation.

Based on these motivations, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a novel methodology for incorporating human cognitive insights into RL for autonomous driving:** This involves moving beyond explicit human feedback to leverage natural neural responses captured by EEG.
2. **Collect a comprehensive multimodal dataset:** This dataset should capture human drivers' behavioral data, eye-tracking data, EEG signals, and corresponding scene images within a realistic driving simulator under challenging scenarios.
3. **Design and train a human cognitive reward model:** This model aims to predict the strength of ERPs (specifically, the occurrence of an ERP-inducing event) directly from visual scene information, thereby eliminating the need for real-time EEG data during inference.
4. **Integrate this cognitive information into the reward signal of an RL algorithm:** The predicted cognitive load or surprise (derived from ERP strength) should serve as a component of the reward function to guide the RL agent's decision-making.
5. **Enhance the collision avoidance ability and overall driving performance of RL algorithms:** Through the integration of neuro-cognitive reward, the goal is to train autonomous agents that exhibit safer and more human-aligned driving behaviors in complex and interactive scenarios.
6. **Demonstrate the effectiveness and scalability of the proposed framework:** Evaluate its performance against established baselines in simulated driving environments, highlighting the potential for neuro-cognitive feedback to improve autonomous driving systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes an EEG-guided decision-making framework to integrate human cognitive insights into reinforcement learning for autonomous driving. The methodology involves data collection, EEG preprocessing, training an EEG feature prediction model, formulating the RL problem with a cognitive reward, and designing a policy network.

#### 3.1. Dataset Collection Setup

A novel multimodal dataset was collected from 20 healthy participants in a realistic driving simulator environment.
- **Participants:** 32 healthy individuals initially recruited (mean age: 25.6 ± 4.6 years), with 20 included in the final analysis after screening for motion sickness or engagement issues. All had normal/corrected vision/audition, no neurological/psychiatric conditions, and average driving experience of 4.9 ± 3.9 years. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained.
- **Equipment:** HTC Vive Pro Eye VR headset (for realistic visual experience), CARLA simulator (for scenario development), Logitech G923 Racing Wheel and Pedal (for active driving data), Synamps2 system with 64 Ag/AgCl EEG channels (following 10-20 system, 1000 Hz sampling rate, impedances < 15kΩ).
- **Data Types:** The dataset captures behavioral data (control inputs), eye-tracking data, EEG signals, and corresponding scene images (RGB, depth, semantic segmentation). This is noted as the first dataset to combine EEG, gaze, and active control data from drivers in a VR simulator.
- **Scenario Design:**
 - **Emergency Braking Scenario:** Inspired by adaptive cruise control (ACC), the ego vehicle follows a lead vehicle that brakes unexpectedly at random intervals (4-7 seconds). Participants were instructed to avoid collisions and maintain speed, with awareness of a trailing vehicle to induce urgency. Two sessions were conducted (day/night conditions), plus two control sessions where AI managed acceleration/braking and participants only steered, providing a baseline for ERP analysis.
 - **Left Turn Scenario:** The ego vehicle executes a left turn at an intersection, avoiding oncoming non-yielding vehicles from the right (3-5 m/s). The task required merging into traffic flow swiftly. This scenario was used to test generalization, with models trained in Town01 and tested in Town05 of CARLA.

#### 3.2. EEG Preprocessing

Raw EEG data underwent a standardized pipeline using EEGLAB v2020.0 in MATLAB v2022a:
1. **Filtering:** Bandpass filter (0.5-100 Hz, zero-phase digital).
2. **Downsampling:** From 1000 Hz to 250 Hz.
3. **Noise Removal:** 50-Hz power line noise removed via CleanLine plugin (multi-tapering).
4. **Artifact Rejection:** Automated rejection via CleanArtifacts plugin, followed by Artifact Subspace Reconstruction (ASR) algorithm. Bad channels were removed based on standard deviation and correlation, then interpolated.
5. **ICA and Component Classification:** Independent Component Analysis (AMICA function) and dipole fitting were performed. Non-brain sources (e.g., muscle, eye, electrical noise) were identified and removed using ICLabel (probability < 50% brain component) and dipole fitting (residual variance > 15%).
6. **ERP Analysis:** Event markers were defined at the onset of the front vehicle's braking. Trials where subjects did not focus on the car (based on eye-tracking) were discarded.

#### 3.3. EEG Feature Prediction Model

To remove the need for real-time EEG data during RL inference, a human cognitive reward model was developed to predict ERPs from visual input.
- **ERP Classification:** ERP-inducing trials were identified using a moving average filter (20 samples) and a peak-to-peak threshold of 1.7 μV. This threshold was selected to achieve a 50/50 class split (high-ERP vs. low-ERP) and aligns with reported minimum ERP peak amplitudes.
- **Model Architecture:** A lightweight Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) with three convolutional layers and an average pooling layer was designed. This architecture aims to reduce parameters and prevent overfitting.
- **Input and Output:** The model processes sequences of segmentation images and outputs a binary classification probability (ˆy_i) indicating the presence of an ERP.
- **Training:** Trained using Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss. Five-fold cross-validation was used to ensure robustness across subjects.

#### 3.4. Reinforcement Learning Problem Formulation

The autonomous driving task was formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) to learn goal-oriented collision avoidance.
- **State Space (S):** Temporal sequences of three consecutive single-channel semantic segmentation frames (I_{t-2}, I_{t-1}, I_t) captured by a front-facing camera.
- **Action Space (A):** Continuous scalar values in [-1, 1], where -1 is maximum braking and 1 is full throttle.
- **Reward Function (R):** A composite reward signal (r_t) comprising environmental and cognitive components:
 - **Cognitive Reward (βr_cog(s_t)):** The predicted ERP probability (ˆy_i from the EEG feature predictor) with a negative weight (β = -1) to penalize states associated with increased cognitive load or perceived difficulty.
 - **Collision Penalty (r_collide):** A severe penalty (-100) for collisions.
 - **Idle Penalty (r_idle):** A mild penalty (-1) if the ego vehicle's speed drops below 0.2 m/s.
 - **Time-Gap Reward (r_gap):** Encourages maintaining an optimal following distance.
- **RL Algorithm:** The TD3 (Twin Delayed DDPG) algorithm was adopted for its robustness and sample efficiency in continuous control tasks.

#### 3.5. Policy Network

A policy network incorporating a self-attention mechanism was designed to determine the vehicle's control policy.
- **Input:** Sequence of three consecutive semantic segmentation images (state representation).
- **Architecture:**
 1. **CNN Encoder:** A shallow CNN extracts features from the input images.
 2. **Self-Attention Layer:** Processes the flattened feature map to enlarge the receptive field and provide contextualized feature representations. This involves projecting features into Query, Key, and Value matrices and computing attention scores.
 3. **MLP Prediction Heads:** Two multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) predict:
 - The action (a_t) representing throttle and brake control.
 - Time-To-Collision (TTC) in the range seconds, used as an auxiliary task for training regularization. TTC is calculated based on distance and relative speeds.
- **Loss Function:** The total loss (L_total) is a combination of the RL policy loss (L_π) and an auxiliary Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss (L_mse) for TTC prediction, weighted by α = 0.1 (L_total = L_π + αL_mse).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. EEG Analysis

- **ERP-Reaction Time Correlation:** A statistically significant positive Pearson correlation (p = 0.0438) was found between ERP peak latency and the driver's reaction time (interval from lead vehicle braking to subject's braking initiation).
- **ERP Amplitude under Active Reaction:** The average ERP waveform showed substantially higher amplitude when participants actively reacted to emergency braking compared to a baseline control condition where no active response was required. A non-parametric permutation test confirmed a significant difference in the 300-500 ms window, aligning with the literature on P3 amplitude increasing with cognitive effort and resource allocation.

#### 4.2. EEG Feature Prediction Model

- **Accuracy and Efficiency:** The proposed lightweight CNN model for predicting ERP occurrence from scene images achieved an average accuracy of 82% across five-fold cross-validation. This performance was comparable to or slightly higher than established image classification baselines such as ResNet-18 (81%), Swin-ViT (79%), and ConvNeXt (80%).
- **Real-time Inference Capability:** Crucially, the model demonstrated a processing speed of 204 frames per second (fps), significantly faster than ResNet-18 (73 fps), Swin-ViT (62 fps), and ConvNeXt (74 fps). This high inference speed makes it suitable for integration as a pretrained reward model within the RLHF framework, saving approximately 2.1 hours of training time for 1M RL steps compared to ResNet-18.

#### 4.3. Driving Performance Analysis

The proposed framework was evaluated against five baseline methods: Vanilla RL (TD3), Behavior Cloning (BC), Prioritized Human In-the-Loop (PHIL) RL, TD3-lag (safe RL), and traditional RLHF (manual preference labeling). Evaluation was conducted in the CARLA simulator across two complex scenarios (Emergency Braking and Left Turn) and assessed using three metrics: Route Completion, Driving Score, and Infraction Penalty. Five models were trained for each method with different random seeds, and generalization was tested by training in one town (Town7 for emergency braking, Town1 for left turn) and evaluating in another (Town4 for emergency braking, Town5 for left turn).

- **Overall Performance Superiority:** The proposed framework ("Ours") consistently achieved the highest scores across all evaluated metrics in both scenarios:
 - **Emergency Braking:** Route Completion (85 ± 43), Driving Score (79 ± 31), Infraction Penalty (0.84 ± 0.14).
 - **Left Turn:** Route Completion (67 ± 8), Driving Score (57 ± 10), Infraction Penalty (0.77 ± 0.05).
- **Comparison with Baselines:**
 - "Ours" significantly outperformed Vanilla RL, BC, PHIL, and TD3-lag.
 - It also surpassed traditional RLHF, which required 10 hours of manual labeling, demonstrating the efficiency and effectiveness of implicit neuro-cognitive feedback.
- **Machine Attention Visualization:** Analysis of the policy network's machine attention revealed that the proposed model consistently focused on the lead vehicle across all time steps in the emergency braking scenario. In contrast, attention in other baseline models appeared more dispersed, suggesting that the cognitive reward model enhances the internal representation of the policy network by guiding it to relevant elements in the scene.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions to the fields of autonomous driving and human-centered AI, with notable potential impacts:

1. **Advancing Human-AI Alignment:** The core contribution is a novel framework that integrates human cognitive insights, specifically EEG-derived ERPs, as implicit feedback for reinforcement learning in autonomous vehicles. This represents a step towards aligning AI decision-making with human cognition in a more natural and direct manner than traditional explicit feedback methods like human preference ranking.
2. **Enhancing Safety and Performance of Autonomous Systems:** By incorporating a neuro-cognitive reward signal, the framework demonstrably improves collision avoidance capabilities and overall driving performance in challenging scenarios (emergency braking, left turns). This suggests a pathway to developing safer autonomous systems that can anticipate and react to situations in a manner consistent with human safety priorities and cognitive processes.
3. **Scalability of Human Feedback in RL:** A key innovation is the development of an EEG feature prediction model that estimates ERP occurrence directly from visual scene images. This eliminates the need for real-time EEG data collection during inference, making the neuro-cognitive feedback approach practical and scalable for real-world autonomous vehicle deployment. This addresses a major limitation of prior RL approaches relying on continuous physiological monitoring.
4. **Novel Multimodal Dataset:** The creation and release of a unique dataset combining active driving control, eye-tracking, EEG signals, and corresponding scene images from a realistic VR driving simulator offers a valuable resource for future research in human factors, neuro-ergonomics, and AI development for autonomous systems.
5. **Improved Decision-Making and Generalization:** The study indicates that incorporating cognitive reward leads to a policy network with more focused attention on critical scene elements (e.g., the lead vehicle). This suggests that the neuro-cognitive feedback helps agents learn more robust and generalizable internal representations, potentially mitigating the "distribution shift" problems faced by imitation learning and enhancing performance in out-of-distribution scenarios.
6. **Foundation for Future Neuro-Inspired AI:** This work lays a foundation for future research in leveraging indirect, inferred human cognitive states to guide complex AI behaviors. While the current EEG feature prediction model is scenario-specific and based on a limited participant pool, the demonstrated efficacy opens avenues for developing more generalized cognitive models and collecting larger, more diverse datasets. Future advancements, such as integrating foveated rendering to mitigate motion sickness, could further enable broader data collection.

In summary, this research presents a paradigm shift from explicit human-in-the-loop feedback to implicit neuro-cognitive guidance, offering a promising direction for building autonomous systems that are not only efficient but also inherently safer and more aligned with human expectations.
