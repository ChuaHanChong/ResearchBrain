# Online, Reachability-Aware, Sampling-Based Motion Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> A sampling-based MPC planner that computes guaranteed reachable-set overapproximations online using interval-based mixed-monotone embedding systems in JAX. Each of 1024 sampled control sequences is rolled out as an interval tube, certified safe under bounded adversarial disturbances, then ranked by an interval-valued cost. Cuts crashes by over 99% in racing simulation and runs a physical racecar for 35 laps collision-free at 60Hz.

## Problem

- Standard sampling-based MPC penalizes risk in the cost or reshapes sampling distributions, mitigating failure probability but giving no deterministic safety guarantee under disturbances.
- Control-barrier-function safety filters are theoretically appealing, but designing a barrier for practical systems is non-obvious and filter interventions degrade performance counter-intuitively.
- Hamilton-Jacobi reachability planners require solving a PDE offline on a dense state-space grid, which suffers the curse of dimensionality and assumes a fully known system and environment.

## Method

- Interval reachability via a ==mixed-monotone embedding system== whose face-wise ==inclusion-function dynamics== propagate a guaranteed state tube at roughly twice the cost of a nominal trajectory.
- A reachability-aware planner replacing nominal-trajectory safety checks with a certified ==interval safety set==, yielding a theorem proving every accepted sampled control is robustly safe over the horizon.
- An ==interval-valued cost functional== ranking certified samples by a guaranteed upper bound on incurred cost, with GPU-parallel ==JAX== rollouts and heuristic interval handling for model switching and Frenet conversion.

## Results

- Across a 540-run-per-controller sweep, the reachability-aware planner crashed **26** times versus **141** for the trajectory-only baseline; excluding unrecoverable initializations, crashes dropped over **99.1%**.
- Against the HJ-reachability baseline it drove **0.98 m/s** versus **1.04 m/s**, a **6%** speed penalty, while avoiding a **38-hour**, **719.1 MB** precomputation; on the 6D model the baseline needed **115 hours**, **2.4 GB**, and still crashed.
- Successful runs rose from **44** to **504** and mean lap times fell from **5.97s** to **5.52s**, with success rates near-constant across disturbance type, centering weight, and velocity scale.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Conservative but cheap interval reachability can substitute for exact value functions online, trading a small performance penalty for dimension-scalable safety without any offline precomputation.
> - Robust safety certification decoupled safety from cost tuning and incidentally improved lap times, suggesting hard constraints can act as beneficial regularizers rather than pure performance taxes.
> - Per-sample safety certification still lacks recursive feasibility and sampling completeness, so residual failures concentrate where the truly safe control set is empty or violations lie beyond the horizon.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses the problem of combining the flexibility of sampling-based Model-Predictive Control (MPC) with the formal safety guarantees associated with reachability analysis.

MPC is widely used in robotics because it repeatedly predicts system behavior over a finite horizon and selects control actions that optimize a performance objective. Sampling-based variants, particularly Model-Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control, are attractive for nonlinear and high-dimensional systems because they do not require the optimization problem to be expressed in a readily solvable analytical form. Instead, they sample candidate control sequences, simulate the resulting trajectories, assign costs, and select or combine the best candidates.

A major limitation of standard sampling-based MPC is that safety is generally represented through penalties in the cost function. Such penalties can reduce the frequency of collisions but do not provide deterministic guarantees. Other methods alter the sampling distribution to favor safer trajectories or apply a safety filter after the nominal controller has selected an action. Control Barrier Functions are one example of a safety-filter approach, although constructing an effective barrier function for complex systems can be difficult.

Reachability analysis provides a different framework. Rather than evaluating one nominal trajectory, it computes an over-approximation of all states that may be reached under specified disturbances and uncertainties. If this entire set remains inside the safe region, the associated control sequence is guaranteed to be safe for all disturbances covered by the model. Historically, online reachability has been limited by computational cost, especially for nonlinear and high-dimensional systems.

The paper builds on recent improvements in interval-based reachability and the JAX-based `immrax` toolbox. It also responds directly to prior work using Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability with sampling-based MPC. HJ approaches can provide rich information about safe controls and intervention times, but they typically require solving a partial differential equation over a discretized state space before deployment. This precomputation becomes increasingly expensive as state dimension increases. The present work instead performs conservative reachability calculations online, avoiding the need for a precomputed global value function.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The central objective is to develop a sampling-based MPC controller that can provide formal safety guarantees while retaining the flexibility and online adaptability of sampling-based planning.

The authors formulate two related problems. First, given the current state, the controller should identify a control signal that maintains the system within a safe set for a finite horizon despite all allowable disturbances. If no such control exists, the method should ideally determine that the safe control set is empty. Second, among the safe controls, the controller should select one that optimizes a performance objective, such as progress, tracking accuracy, or energy use.

The motivation has several components. Safety-critical robotic systems cannot rely solely on probabilistic reductions in failure frequency, since a low-probability collision may still have unacceptable consequences. At the same time, many practical systems are nonlinear, making analytical safety filters or conventional optimization formulations difficult to construct. The authors therefore seek a method that can work directly with nonlinear dynamics and sampled control sequences.

A second motivation is adaptability. Offline HJ reachability assumes that the system model and environment are known in advance and requires a costly precomputation step. An online interval method can potentially accommodate changing track geometry, dynamic environments, or adapted system models, provided that the reachability calculation remains computationally feasible.

The paper evaluates whether the proposed method can reduce safety violations relative to nominal trajectory-based sampling, whether it can achieve performance comparable to an HJ-based planner, and whether it can run fast enough for real-time control on a physical vehicle.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. System and Safety Formulation

The paper considers a nonlinear continuous-time system,

\[
\dot{x}=f(x,u,w),
\]

where \(x\) is the system state, \(u\) is the control input, and \(w\) is a bounded disturbance. The disturbance represents modeling errors and uncertainties between the mathematical model and the real system. It is treated adversarially rather than statistically, with the assumption that every disturbance remains inside a known interval.

Safety is represented by a function \(h(x)\). The safe set is

\[
S_x=\{x:h(x)\geq 0\}.
\]

A control signal is considered safe over a horizon if every trajectory generated by that control, under every allowable disturbance signal, remains in the safe set throughout the horizon.

#### 3.2. Interval-Based Reachability

The main technical tool is an interval over-approximation of the system’s reachable states. An interval \([\underline{x},\overline{x}]\) represents all states whose components lie between corresponding lower and upper bounds.

The method constructs an inclusion function for the dynamics. Given an interval of possible states, controls, and disturbances, the inclusion function returns an interval guaranteed to contain the vector field values for every point in those intervals.

The authors use an embedding system whose state consists of the lower and upper bounds of the reachable interval. Rather than simply evaluating the dynamics over the entire state interval, the embedding system evaluates the outward expansion of each interval face. This exploits the structure of continuous-time dynamics and can reduce conservatism compared with a direct interval evaluation.

For a given sampled control signal, integrating the embedding system produces a time-varying interval trajectory,

\[
[\underline{\Phi},\overline{\Phi}](t).
\]

The reachability result guarantees that every trajectory of the original disturbed system lies inside this interval trajectory. In the implementation, the `immrax` toolbox automatically constructs inclusion functions for suitable compositions of mathematical operations. JAX enables parallel execution of many sampled reachability calculations, particularly on GPUs.

#### 3.3. Sampling-Based Planner

The controller uses a receding-horizon sampling procedure. At each control step, it shifts the previous best control sequence forward in time and appends a terminal input, thereby warm-starting the next planning iteration. A batch of \(N\) candidate piecewise-constant control sequences is sampled around this reference sequence. The sampled inputs are smoothed and clipped to remain compatible with low-level vehicle tracking.

The baseline planner integrates each candidate under zero disturbance and checks whether its nominal trajectory satisfies the safety condition. It then evaluates the cost of the nominally safe trajectories and applies the first control action from the best one.

The proposed planner replaces nominal trajectory rollouts with interval reachable-set rollouts. For each candidate sequence, the embedding system is integrated under the bounded disturbance model. A candidate is accepted only if the entire reachable interval remains within the safe set. The controller then selects the minimum-cost candidate from this formally verified subset.

The safety theorem follows directly from the containment property of the interval trajectory. If the lower-bound evaluation of the safety function remains nonnegative over the complete prediction horizon, then every possible disturbed trajectory also satisfies the safety condition.

#### 3.4. Racecar Model and Objectives

The experimental platform is modeled using a six-dimensional dynamic bicycle model. The state comprises planar position, heading, longitudinal velocity, lateral velocity, and yaw rate. Steering and acceleration are the control inputs. Tire forces are modeled using the Pacejka Magic Formula, and longitudinal acceleration is represented through a motor model.

The model includes bounded disturbances in lateral velocity and heading rate. These terms represent errors in estimating tire slip and lateral motion, which are difficult to infer accurately from noisy position measurements.

At low longitudinal speeds, the dynamic model becomes numerically singular because of terms involving division by longitudinal velocity. The authors therefore switch to a kinematic bicycle model below a threshold. For interval states crossing the threshold, the implementation uses a less conservative heuristic based on the largest velocity in the interval.

Safety and performance are expressed in Frenet coordinates relative to a reference raceline. The safety function requires the lateral position to remain between the left and right track boundaries. The performance cost includes lateral deviation from the track center, a boundary-proximity penalty, deviation from desired speed, a penalty for reverse motion, and a terminal progress term. Track geometry, desired velocity, and boundary information are assumed to be observable over the prediction horizon.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Comparison with Nominal Planning

The authors conducted a parameter sweep involving 30 initial states, nine cost-weight combinations, and two disturbance types, producing 540 runs for each controller. Each controller attempted three consecutive laps. Runs ended after a collision or after the vehicle failed to advance for ten seconds.

The nominal trajectory-based planner crashed in 141 of 540 trials. The reachability-aware planner crashed in only 26 trials. Twenty-five of those 26 failures occurred when the vehicle began very close to a track boundary and did not sample any control sequence that could be formally certified as safe. The authors interpret these cases as likely instances in which no safe control was available over the specified horizon. Excluding these difficult initializations, the reachability-aware method reduced crash frequency by more than 99.1%.

The reachability-aware controller also showed greater robustness to changes in disturbance type, cost weights, and velocity scaling. Its success rate remained high across the parameter sweep, whereas the baseline was successful in only 44 of the 540 runs. Mean lap time for successful reachability-filtered runs was 5.52 seconds, compared with 5.97 seconds for successful baseline runs. The authors attribute this performance improvement partly to the controller’s explicit handling of disturbances, which prevents performance degradation caused by unexpected deviations.

#### 4.2. Comparison with an HJ-Based Planner

The paper compares its method with the HJ-based DualGuard MPPI approach. On a three-dimensional Dubins-vehicle racecar model and the same track geometry, the proposed controller prevented all collisions and achieved an average speed of 0.98 m/s. The HJ-based reference achieved 1.04 m/s, giving the interval method a reported 6% speed disadvantage.

The HJ method benefits from a precomputed value function that provides additional safety information during sampling and improves sample efficiency. However, generating and storing that value function required more than 38 hours and produced a 719.1 MB file for the lower-dimensional example. Applying HJ reachability to the paper’s six-dimensional vehicle model required a coarser grid, more than 115 hours of computation, and 2.4 GB of storage. The resulting interpolation errors contributed to a crash before completion of the corresponding trial.

These results indicate a trade-off: the interval method may sacrifice some performance because it lacks the globally precomputed information of HJ reachability, but it avoids the associated offline computation and scales more readily to the six-dimensional model.

#### 4.3. Hardware Demonstration

The method was deployed on a 1/28-scale BuzzRacer vehicle. The controller sampled 1,024 control sequences and propagated each over 30 steps with a 0.02-second step size. GPU parallelization enabled an execution rate of approximately 60 Hz.

The physical vehicle completed more than 35 consecutive laps without collision. The experiment provides evidence that the interval disturbance model and online reachable-set computations can support closed-loop control on hardware, although the test used precise state estimates from OptiTrack cameras.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

The principal significance of the work is its integration of formal disturbance-aware safety checking into a sampling-based MPC architecture without requiring offline reachability computation. This creates a possible middle ground between nominal sampling methods, which are computationally flexible but lack guarantees, and HJ methods, which provide strong safety information but face substantial dimensionality and precomputation costs.

The approach could be relevant to autonomous vehicles, drones, mobile robots, and other nonlinear systems in which models are available but exact online optimization is difficult. Its online structure may also facilitate use in environments that change over time or in systems whose models are updated adaptively.

The guarantees are conditional rather than absolute. They depend on accurate disturbance bounds, a valid model of how disturbances enter the dynamics, sufficiently accurate numerical integration, and adequate state observation. The finite sampling process may fail to identify a safe control even when one exists. The receding-horizon formulation also lacks recursive feasibility: a currently safe action may lead to a future state from which no safe action can be found. The method can additionally become conservative because interval over-approximations enlarge reachable sets, and some geometric cost and safety calculations use practical heuristics rather than exact interval transformations.

The authors identify backup policies, stronger recursive-feasibility mechanisms, dynamic-environment operation, and adaptive control as directions for further development. Overall, the paper demonstrates that online interval reachability can substantially improve the safety of sampling-based motion planning while maintaining real-time performance on a nonlinear vehicle platform.
