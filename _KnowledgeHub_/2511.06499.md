# SportR: A Benchmark for Multimodal Large Language Model Reasoning in Sports

> [!summary] Summary
> SportR, a new multi-sport benchmark from Rice University and collaborators, evaluates Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) on fine-grained, rule-based visual reasoning in sports. The benchmark introduces human-authored Chain-of-Thought rationales and an explicit visual grounding task, showing that state-of-the-art MLLMs struggle significantly, though training on SportR data yields notable performance improvements in rule application and cross-modal generalization.

## Problem

- Existing MLLMs struggle with nuanced visual details, applying abstract sports rules, and connecting these rules to specific visual evidence within a multi-sport context.
- Current sports benchmarks lack detailed, human-authored Chain-of-Thought (CoT) rationales suitable for training generative reasoning, and often do not include precise visual grounding annotations.
- Many benchmarks rely on multiple-choice questions or single-sport focuses, limiting the assessment of true reasoning capabilities and generalizability across diverse sports.

## Method

- Introduces **SportR**, a human-authored, ==multi-sport benchmark== (SportsImage with 4,789 images and SportsVideo with 2,052 clips) for basketball, soccer, table tennis, badminton, and American football.
- Provides high-quality, human-authored Chain-of-Thought (CoT) rationales and a progressive QA hierarchy (up to seven questions per image, six per video) for granular evaluation and training of MLLMs.
- Incorporates an explicit visual grounding task requiring precise bounding box coordinates for rule infractions in images, directly linking abstract rules to localized visual evidence.

## Results

- Baseline MLLMs achieved low accuracy on SportR, particularly on Foul Classification (Q2) and Explicit Visual Grounding (Q5), with Q5 IoU scores under **7%** across all models in a zero-shot setting.
- Training Qwen-2.5VL-**7B** on SportsImage data via SFT and RL improved performance significantly; for example, Foul Classification (Q2) accuracy increased from **14.4%** (base) to **57.0%** (SFT+RL).
- The SFT+RL model, trained only on images, demonstrated cross-modal generalization to video tasks, improving Video Infraction Identification (Q8) from **25.49%** (base) to **59.52%**, surpassing all other models.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - State-of-the-art MLLMs, including proprietary models, show low accuracy in zero-shot settings on SportR's challenging tasks, with visual grounding IoU consistently below 7% for baselines.
> - Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) on SportR's image data lead to substantial performance gains (e.g., Qwen-2.5VL-7B's Foul Classification accuracy rose from 14.4% to 57.0% with SFT+RL).
> - Error analysis reveals that video tasks are primarily hampered by visual perception errors, while image tasks for stronger models expose a significant 'domain knowledge gap' in mapping visual evidence to abstract rules.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence in sports has expanded beyond traditional applications like game prediction to encompass sophisticated tasks such as automated officiating and tactical analysis. This evolution is increasingly supported by Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), which integrate visual and linguistic understanding to interpret complex sports events. Current MLLMs demonstrate capabilities in generating contextual outputs, leading research to focus on their capacity for deep, multi-step reasoning in sports.

Existing multimodal sports analysis primarily falls into two categories: early benchmarks focusing on action recognition or descriptive questions (e.g., ActionAtlas, Ego-Exo4D, Sports-QA) and more recent efforts to incorporate deeper reasoning. Notable works in the latter category include SPORTU, which introduced multi-level difficulty questions involving game rules and tactics, and single-sport benchmarks such as SoccerNet-XFoul, FSBench, FineBadminton, and SoccerBench, which offer expert-annotated explanations or fine-grained analysis within a specific sport.

However, a significant gap persists in the research landscape. Many existing benchmarks, including SPORTU, primarily use multiple-choice questions or lack fine-grained Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning traces suitable for training models to perform explicit, step-by-step reasoning. Furthermore, a common limitation is the absence of precise visual grounding annotations, making it difficult to verify whether model judgments are directly tied to specific visual evidence. Models have shown proficiency in basic perceptual tasks but struggle with tasks requiring true comprehension and rule application.

This paper addresses these limitations by introducing SportR, a benchmark designed to bridge the gap between basic perception and elite-level sports scenarios. SportR focuses on "fundamental sports reasoning," offering a multi-sport context, explicit human-authored CoT annotations for training, and a novel visual grounding task to connect abstract rules with precise visual evidence. This positions SportR as a resource intended to advance MLLM capabilities in deep, rule-based visual reasoning across multiple sports, moving beyond descriptive understanding towards causal reasoning in complex sports environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for developing SportR stems from the identified challenges in advancing artificial intelligence for sports applications. Core reasoning capabilities are essential for sophisticated AI tools in sports, ranging from automated officiating that requires precise understanding of rule infractions to tactical analysis demanding insight into strategic plays. Deeply understanding sports necessitates a complex integration of fine-grained visual perception, the application of abstract rule knowledge, and the ability to ground this knowledge in specific visual evidence. Current MLLMs face significant bottlenecks in these areas.

Specifically, the authors observe that:
- **Limited Reasoning Capabilities:** Existing MLLMs, despite their advancements, struggle with nuanced visual details, applying abstract sports rules, and connecting these rules to specific visual evidence within a multi-sport context. For example, adjudicating a subtle foul in basketball requires identifying momentary illegal contact and linking it to specific rules.
- **Limitations of Current Benchmarks:**
 - **Lack of Multi-Sport Generalization:** Many specialized benchmarks focus on single sports (e.g., soccer, figure skating), hindering the evaluation of a model's ability to generalize reasoning across diverse sports.
 - **Insufficient Reasoning Traces:** While some benchmarks provide explanations, they often lack the detailed, fine-grained, human-authored Chain-of-Thought (CoT) rationales necessary to explicitly train models in step-by-step reasoning processes. This limits their utility as training resources for generative reasoning.
 - **Absence of Visual Grounding:** A critical limitation is the lack of precise visual grounding annotations. Without these, it is difficult to ascertain whether a model's judgments are based on specific visual evidence or if it is merely performing high-level pattern recognition without true comprehension of the localized event.
 - **Evaluation Format Issues:** Some benchmarks rely heavily on multiple-choice questions, which may not accurately reflect true reasoning capabilities and can be susceptible to guessing or prompt-engineering biases.

To address these limitations, the key objectives of SportR are:
1. **Introduce a Multi-Sport, Large-Scale Benchmark:** To provide a comprehensive dataset for training and evaluating MLLMs on fundamental sports reasoning, covering diverse ball and racket sports (basketball, soccer, table tennis, badminton, American football).
2. **Establish a Progressive QA Hierarchy:** To enable granular evaluation of model capabilities across increasing depths of reasoning, from basic infraction identification to complex penalty prediction and tactical analysis.
3. **Provide High-Quality, Human-Authored Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Annotations:** To serve as a "gold standard" for complex reasoning, offering step-by-step logic for challenging tasks, thereby facilitating the training of generative reasoning capabilities in MLLMs.
4. **Incorporate an Explicit Visual Grounding Task:** To directly test models' ability to connect abstract rule knowledge to precise visual evidence by requiring the output of bounding box coordinates for rule infractions in images.
5. **Enable Cross-Modal Evaluation:** To assess reasoning capabilities across both static images and dynamic video clips, recognizing that some scenarios inherently require temporal understanding.
6. **Demonstrate Utility as a Training Resource:** To show that the benchmark can effectively improve MLLM performance through supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, driving advancements in fine-grained sports reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SportR is designed as a multi-modal benchmark for evaluating and training MLLMs on fine-grained visual reasoning in sports. The approach is structured around a conceptual pyramid of sports understanding, focusing on the "fundamental sports reasoning" layer, which involves common fouls and tactics. The benchmark comprises two main components: SportsImage and SportsVideo.

#### 3.1. Data Collection and Annotation

The benchmark is fully human-authored and annotated to ensure high quality and expert-driven ground truth. A team of 16 experts, including former NCAA Division I student-athletes and individuals with at least three years of dedicated sports engagement, created the annotations. The focus was on fundamental and widely understood fouls and tactics, rather than obscure professional-level scenarios, to ensure clarity and consistency in rationales.

The annotation process involved:
1. **Training Phase:** Annotators collaboratively developed and standardized guidelines for CoT rationales and bounding box annotations.
2. **"Macro-to-Micro" Logical Flow:** CoT reasoning was structured into three sequential steps: (1) Identify the specific court area and involved parties; (2) Describe the action details and dynamics; (3) Pinpoint the precise point of contact or critical visual evidence.
3. **Verification and Filtering:** A strict protocol included self-review by annotators, followed by independent review by a second expert for flagged uncertain instances. Data points where consensus could not be reached were discarded to minimize mislabeling.

#### 3.2. SportR Components: SportsImage

- **Content:** 4,789 images from basketball, soccer, table tennis, badminton, and American football. Each image captures a critical gameplay moment.
- **Annotations:** Each image is paired with a human-authored CoT rationale. This rationale forms the basis for a progressive QA suite of up to seven distinct questions, yielding over 17,000 structured question-answer pairs.
- **QA Suite (Q1-Q7):**
 - Q1: Infraction identification (Yes/No).
 - Q2: Foul classification (specific type).
 - Q3: Penalty prediction (requires multi-step reasoning, uses CoT as ground truth).
 - Q4: Free-form explanation (uses the full human rationale as ground truth).
 - Q5: Explicit visual grounding (requires outputting precise bounding box coordinates of the infraction).
 - Q6: Offensive tactic identification.
 - Q7: Defensive tactic identification.
- **Visual Grounding (Q5):** This is a unique task where annotators manually drew the tightest possible bounding box around the critical visual evidence of a rule infraction. This task is focused on static images due to the discrete, localized nature of fouls in such frames, allowing for unambiguous, high-precision ground truth.

#### 3.2.2. SportsVideo

- **Content:** 2,052 video clips from basketball, soccer, table tennis, and American football (excluding badminton). These videos capture dynamic scenarios requiring temporal understanding.
- **Annotations:** Each video has a comprehensive, human-authored CoT rationale explaining the core foul or tactic. This forms the basis for a progressive QA suite, resulting in over 6,000 structured question-answer pairs.
- **QA Suite (Q8-Q13):** Mirrors the image tasks for temporal scenarios, covering infraction identification, foul classification, penalty prediction, free-form explanation, offensive tactic identification, and defensive tactic identification.
- **Distinction from SportsImage:** This component does not include the explicit, coordinate-based visual grounding task (Q5) due to the inherent complexity of annotating precise spatial coordinates across dynamic frames, which is considered an area for future research.

#### 3.3. Experimental Approach: Baseline Models

- **Proprietary Models:** GPT-5, Claude 4.0, Gemini 2.5 Pro were evaluated via their official APIs.
- **Open-Source Models:** LLaVA-OneVision 7B, LLaVA-Next, QwenVL-2.5 7B, QwenVL-2.5 72B, Deepseek-VL, and Glm-4.5V were evaluated.
- **Setting:** All baseline evaluations were conducted in a zero-shot setting, using a consistent prompt template and a temperature of 0.7.

#### 3.3.2. Model Training on SportR

- **Target Model:** Qwen-2.5VL-7B.
- **Training Data:** Exclusively the SportsImage component was used for training.
- **Two-Stage Process:**
 1. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Initial training on 10% of SportsImage data for 6 epochs. Target outputs varied by question type: concise answers for simple QA, full human-authored CoT for penalty prediction (Q3) as reasoning steps, and full human rationales for free-form explanation (Q4).
 2. **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Employed the Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) algorithm.
- **Reward Function (for RL):** A weighted combination of a correctness reward ($R_{correct}$) and a format incentive ($R_{format}$).
 - $R_{correct}$ for QA tasks (Q1-Q3, Q5-Q7): LLM-as-Judge (DeepSeek-V3.1) provided a binary reward (1 for semantic consistency, 0 otherwise). For visual grounding (Q5), Generalized Intersection over Union (GIoU) was used, transformed to a 0-1 range.
 - $R_{format}$: A score of 1 for correct adherence to output format (e.g., presence of <think> and <answer> blocks) for reasoning tasks. Explanation questions (Q4) were excluded from RL training.

#### 3.4. Evaluation Metrics

- **Visual Grounding (Q5):** Intersection over Union (IoU) was used.
- **Text-Based QA (all other tasks):** An "LLM-as-Judge" framework was employed. Three proprietary models (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude 4.0 Sonnet) served as judges, and the average score was reported. A human verification study on a stratified subset (660 samples) showed a strong Pearson correlation (r > 0.65) between the LLM average score and expert human judgments, confirming reliability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the significant challenge posed by the SportR benchmark and its utility as a training resource for MLLMs.

#### 4.1. Performance on SportsImage (Table 1)

- **Baseline Performance:** State-of-the-art MLLMs, including proprietary models like GPT-5, exhibited low accuracy in a zero-shot setting. Performance was particularly poor on tasks requiring deep, specialized knowledge, such as Foul Classification (Q2) and Explicit Visual Grounding (Q5). IoU scores for Q5 were consistently below 7% across all baselines, indicating that existing models struggle to connect abstract rules to precise visual evidence.
- **Effect of Training on SportsImage:**
 - **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Training the Qwen-2.5VL-7B model on SportR's SportsImage data resulted in substantial performance improvements across nearly all tasks. For instance, Foul Classification (Q2) accuracy increased from 14.4% (base) to 50.7% (SFT).
 - **Reinforcement Learning (SFT+RL):** Further application of RL significantly boosted performance, with the SFT+RL model achieving the highest scores in 5 out of 7 categories. Despite these gains, performance on the Explicit Visual Grounding task (Q5) remained modest, improving from 4.61% IoU (base) to 9.94% IoU (SFT+RL), highlighting the inherent difficulty of this task.

#### 4.2. Performance on SportsVideo (Table 2)

- **General Challenge:** Video tasks were found to be generally more challenging than their image-based counterparts, with overall lower model performance across the board.
- **Proprietary Model Performance:** GPT-5 achieved the highest scores on the majority of video tasks (Q9-Q12), although its scores remained relatively low, further emphasizing the benchmark's difficulty in the temporal domain.
- **Cross-Modal Generalization:** The Qwen-2.5VL-7B (SFT+RL) model, which was trained exclusively on SportsImage data, demonstrated cross-modal generalization. Its performance on Video Infraction Identification (Q8) dramatically improved from 25.49% (base) to 59.52% (SFT+RL), surpassing all other models, including GPT-5. The model also showed performance gains on most other video tasks (except Q12), suggesting that image-based fine-tuning can transfer some reasoning capabilities to video understanding.

#### 4.3. Error Analysis (Figure 3, 18, 19)

A manual error analysis on 1,500 failure cases across representative models revealed modality-specific challenges:

- **SportsVideo (Temporal Perception Bottleneck):** Errors in video tasks were predominantly (60-70%) Visual Perception Errors and Visual Hallucinations. This indicates that the primary barrier in video is the models' inability to parse fine-grained, temporal dynamics, often preventing them from reaching the stage of successful rule application.
- **SportsImage (Knowledge Mapping Gap):** In static image tasks, where visual perception is inherently less ambiguous, the error distribution shifted. Stronger models showed a notable increase in Domain Knowledge Gaps. For instance, GPT-5's Domain Knowledge Gap rose from 20.00% in video to 36.00% in images, and Qwen-2.5VL-72B's gap increased from 10.67% to 43.33%. This suggests that even when models successfully perceive visual evidence, they still struggle significantly to accurately map that evidence to the correct abstract sports rules, exposing an underlying deficiency in reasoning rather than just perception.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SportR contributes to the advancement of multimodal large language models in sports understanding by addressing several critical gaps in existing research and benchmarks.

#### 5.1. Addressing Research Gaps

- **Comprehensive Reasoning:** SportR is the first multi-sport, large-scale benchmark designed specifically to train and evaluate MLLMs on fine-grained, rule-based reasoning, moving beyond descriptive tasks.
- **Actionable Training Data:** By providing 6,841 high-quality, human-authored Chain-of-Thought (CoT) annotations, SportR offers a "gold standard" resource for explicitly training generative reasoning capabilities in MLLMs, a significant improvement over benchmarks primarily focused on evaluation.
- **Explicit Visual Grounding:** The introduction of a novel visual grounding task, requiring precise bounding box coordinates for rule infractions, directly assesses a model's ability to connect abstract sports rules with specific, localized visual evidence. This is a crucial step towards more trustworthy and verifiable AI decisions in sports.
- **Multi-Modal and Progressive Evaluation:** The benchmark integrates both static images and dynamic video clips, along with a progressive QA hierarchy, enabling a holistic assessment of MLLM capabilities across different levels of complexity and modalities.

#### 5.2. Driving Future Research

- **Revealing Model Limitations:** The experiments demonstrate that even state-of-the-art MLLMs perform poorly on SportR's most challenging tasks, particularly visual grounding and complex rule-based reasoning. This highlights significant room for improvement and identifies key research directions.
- **Targeted Model Development:** The error analysis points to specific weaknesses: fine-grained visual perception for dynamic events (in video) and the ability to align visual evidence with abstract domain knowledge (in images). This provides clear targets for developing more robust MLLM architectures and training methodologies.
- **Foundational Resource:** SportR's utility as an effective training resource through supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning is demonstrated, indicating its potential to foster the development of MLLMs with stronger reasoning and grounding abilities.

#### 5.3. Potential Real-World Impact

- **Enhanced Sports Intelligence:** Advancements driven by SportR could lead to more accurate and reliable AI systems for various sports applications, including automated officiating, real-time tactical analysis, intelligent commentary, and player performance evaluation.
- **Improved Trustworthiness:** By pushing models to perform explicit visual grounding, SportR contributes to developing AI systems that can not only make decisions but also justify them with precise visual evidence, which is essential for adoption in sensitive applications like sports officiating.
- **Generalized AI Capabilities:** The multi-sport nature of the benchmark encourages the development of MLLMs with generalized reasoning capabilities, which could translate to improved performance in other complex, real-world visual-linguistic domains requiring intricate rule application and visual grounding.

In conclusion, SportR presents a rigorous challenge for the MLLM community, providing a critical resource that is expected to inspire and guide future research towards more intelligent, explainable, and trustworthy AI systems for sports and beyond.
