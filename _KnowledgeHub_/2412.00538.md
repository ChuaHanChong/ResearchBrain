# Prognostic Framework for Robotic Manipulators Operating Under Dynamic Task Severities

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper is not about manipulation policy learning — it is a prognostics-and-health-management (PHM) framework for predicting a robotic manipulator's **Remaining Useful Life (RUL)** as its own end-effector accuracy degrades from repeated, variable-severity use (e.g., alternating light vs. heavy payloads). The core contribution is a mathematically explicit model of *path-dependent, cumulative* degradation — a stochastic process whose drift depends on the sequence of task severities the robot has actually experienced — as opposed to a fixed wear-out threshold, together with a closed-form RUL distribution validated against Monte Carlo simulation on robot fleets.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulators degrade (end-effector position accuracy declines) under repeated use, and degradation rate depends on the *severity* of each task (e.g., payload weight) — but task severity varies dynamically over the manipulator's operating life rather than staying fixed.
- Existing degradation/prognostics models typically assume a single, static operating condition, so they cannot capture how a *sequence* of alternating high- and low-severity tasks accumulates into an overall degradation trajectory.
- Predicting Remaining Useful Life under this kind of history-dependent, cumulative degradation requires a model whose *state* is explicitly a function of the load path, not just the current instant.

## Method

- Models end-effector position-accuracy degradation as an ==integral (path-dependent) stochastic process==: $A(c_{k+i}) = A(c_k) + \int r(\psi(\nu))\,d\nu + \gamma W(c_{k+i}-c_k)$ — a Brownian-motion-with-drift term whose drift $r(\psi) = \alpha\psi + \beta$ depends linearly on the task-severity state $\psi$, plus stochastic noise $W$.
- Task severity itself is modeled as a **continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC)** switching between severity levels (e.g., 1 kg vs. 5 kg payload), so the *sequence* of severities the robot has been exposed to — not just its current task — determines the accumulated drift.
- Derives a novel **closed-form expression** for the Remaining Lifetime Distribution (an Inverse Gaussian, $T \sim IG(\mu_\phi, (D-A(c_k))^2/\gamma^2)$) and validates it against Monte Carlo simulation, proving equivalence while being orders of magnitude cheaper to compute.

## Results

- Validated on physics-based planar and spatial robot-fleet simulators under a CTMC (1 kg vs 5 kg payload tasks, symmetric transition rates $Q = [-0.005, 0.005; 0.005, -0.005]$, stationary distribution $\pi = [0.5, 0.5]$), confirming fleets with more high-severity tasks have systematically shorter RUL via this cumulative, sequence-dependent effect.
- RUL prediction error shrinks as more of the degradation trajectory is observed: **40.5% ± 19.4%** (planar) / **33.2% ± 11.5%** (spatial) error at 30% of lifetime, tightening to **2.9% ± 2.5%** (planar) / **3.3% ± 2.6%** (spatial) at 90% of lifetime.
- The closed-form RUL expression computes in **~5 seconds** versus **~9,200-11,000 seconds** for the Monte Carlo baseline at equivalent accuracy — a three-to-four-order-of-magnitude speedup.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Path-dependent, cumulative-damage modeling — an integral of a load-dependent rate function over the *history* of operation, not a function of the current instant alone — is already tractable, closed-form, and validated machinery in robotics; it is just aimed at the manipulator's own actuators/joints, never at the object being manipulated.
> - The severity-switching CTMC is a direct structural analog for a *contact-force-severity* state in manipulation: the same "integrate a severity-dependent rate, track a remaining budget" recipe could in principle be re-targeted at an object's accumulated contact damage instead of a robot arm's position-accuracy drift.
> - The prediction-error trajectory (40%+ error early, tightening to ~3% near end-of-life) is a useful calibration reference for any manipulation-side damage-integral model asking "how much of the load history do I need to observe before my cumulative-damage forecast is trustworthy?"

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a challenge in industrial robotics concerning the degradation of manipulators over time, which compromises their ability to perform tasks requiring high accuracy, especially with heavy payloads. While fault diagnosis in robots, often focusing on specific components or end-effector accuracy, has received considerable attention in the literature, systematic prognostic models for predicting a robot's Remaining Useful Life (RUL) are less prevalent. Existing diagnostic methods, such as those by Qiao and Weiss, or Izagirre et al., have explored end-effector accuracy assessment but primarily for current state evaluation rather than future life prediction.

Previous attempts at RUL modeling in robots that consider operating conditions, such as those by Yang et al. and Aivaliotis et al., have either lacked a formal relationship between working regimes and RUL, or assumed deterministic degradation processes, thereby not fully quantifying predictive uncertainty. This paper identifies a research gap in developing a systematic framework for computing robot RUL that accounts for dynamic task severity and quantifies predictive uncertainty.

Within the broader field of prognostics, many models assume constant environmental or operating conditions. However, real-world scenarios often involve stochastic and dynamic conditions that accelerate degradation rates. While some research has incorporated dynamic operating conditions using deterministic profiles or physics-based models, and others have employed data-driven approaches like Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) for non-linear degradation or general additive models, a key identified gap is the lack of closed-form solutions for Remaining Lifetime Distribution (RLD) under dynamic operating conditions. Prior work by Bian et al. (2015) and Kharoufeh and Cox (2005) utilized Brownian motion-based degradation models with operating conditions modeled as Continuous-Time Markov Chains (CTMC), but their RLD approximations typically relied on Laplace transforms or Monte Carlo simulations.

This research distinguishes itself by proposing a novel closed-form expression for RLD for a Brownian motion-based degradation model where the drift is influenced by CTMC-modeled task severity. Additionally, it addresses the practical challenge of intermittent robot utilization by adopting a periodic inspection scheme rather than continuous monitoring, a departure from conventional assumptions in many degradation models. The methodology is designed to be data-driven and agnostic to robot kinematics, increasing its general applicability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed degradation of industrial robots, which often perform repetitive tasks under varying operational loads. This degradation can lead to a decline in performance, particularly for tasks demanding high precision, such as manipulating heavy objects. Understanding and predicting this degradation, especially its dependence on operating conditions like payload weight (referred to as "task severity"), is crucial for effective robot management and maintenance. Traditional continuous monitoring of robot health across diverse tasks is often impractical due to data heterogeneity and collection complexities.

The key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a Prognostic Modeling Framework:** To create a systematic approach for predicting the Remaining Useful Life (RUL) of robotic manipulators.
2. **Incorporate Dynamic Task Severity:** To explicitly account for the influence of varying task severities (e.g., payload weights) on the robot's degradation process, recognizing that heavier payloads can accelerate degradation.
3. **Address Data Homogeneity Challenges:** To propose and implement a data collection scheme that utilizes periodic, pre-specified calibration tasks. This ensures that the degradation data used for modeling is homogeneous, overcoming issues arising from varied operational tasks.
4. **Define Robot Lifetime and Degradation Signal:** To define a robot's lifetime as the point at which its end-effector position accuracy, chosen as the degradation signal, crosses a predefined soft failure threshold. Position accuracy is characterized by the deviation from a desired target.
5. **Develop a Data-Driven and Kinematics-Agnostic Methodology:** To ensure the framework is driven by empirical data and is not reliant on specific kinematic models of the robot, thereby enhancing its applicability across different robot types.
6. **Propose Novel RLD Estimation Approaches:** To introduce two methods for estimating the Remaining Lifetime Distribution (RLD): a novel closed-form expression, which is computationally efficient, and a Monte Carlo simulation-based approach, commonly used in prognostics literature. The equivalence between these approaches is also theoretically established.
7. **Validate the Framework Experimentally:** To validate the proposed framework using physics-based simulators for both planar and spatial robot fleets, demonstrating its robustness to unit-to-unit variability and different robot dynamics/controllers.
8. **Enable "What-If" Analysis:** To demonstrate the practical utility of the closed-form RLD estimation for performing "what-if" analyses, allowing assessment of how variations in future task proportions influence a robot's predicted lifetime. This supports proactive decision-making regarding task planning and maintenance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The prognostic framework developed in this paper integrates models for task severity and robot degradation, coupled with Bayesian updating for parameter estimation and two distinct approaches for RUL prediction.

**Degradation Signal and Data Collection:**
The degradation of the robotic manipulator is quantified by monitoring the position accuracy of its end-effector. Position accuracy is defined as the Euclidean distance between the end-effector's actual and commanded position after completing a task cycle, and is assumed to increase with progressive degradation. To overcome the challenges of collecting heterogeneous data during varied operational tasks, a periodic inspection scheme is proposed. During these inspection epochs, which occur after a fixed number of operational cycles (e.g., 50 operational cycles to 1 inspection epoch in simulations), the robot performs a consistent set of calibration tasks designed to evaluate its degradation.

**Key Modeling Assumptions:**
1. **Task Severity Model:** The sequence of tasks with dynamic severity levels is modeled as a Continuous-Time Markov Chain (CTMC), denoted by $\{\psi(c) : c > 0\}$. The states of the CTMC correspond to distinct task severity levels (e.g., payload weights). The infinitesimal generator matrix $Q = [q_{ij}]$ governs transitions between these states, with transition rates $q_{ij}$ treated as random variables following Gamma prior distributions.
2. **Robot Position Accuracy Model:** The robot's position accuracy degradation is modeled as a Brownian motion process with a random drift. This drift, representing the degradation rate, is influenced by the task severity $\psi(\nu)$. The position accuracy at a future epoch $c_{k+i}$ is given by $A(c_{k+i}) = A(c_k) + \int_{c_k}^{c_{k+i}} r(\psi(\nu))d\nu + \gamma W(c_{k+i} - c_k)$, where $r(\psi(\nu)) = \alpha\psi(\nu) + \beta$ is the degradation rate, and $\gamma W(c_{k+i} - c_k)$ is a Brownian motion term with diffusion parameter $\gamma$. The accuracy model parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are random variables following independent normal prior distributions.

**Bayesian Updating of Model Parameters:**
To capture the robot's latest degradation characteristics, Bayesian inference is used to update model parameters:
- **Severity Model Parameters:** Given observed task severities $P_{c_k}$, the posterior distributions of $q_{ij}$ are updated. These remain Gamma distributions, with shape and scale parameters updated based on the observed number of transitions $n_{ij}(c_k)$ and holding times $h_i(c_k)$ in each state.
- **Accuracy Model Parameters:** Given observed accuracies $A_{c_k}$ and task severities $P_{c_k}$, the posterior distributions of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are updated. Through re-parameterization, their joint posterior distribution is shown to be bivariate normal, with closed-form expressions derived for their posterior means, variances, and correlation coefficient.

**Remaining Lifetime Distribution (RLD) Estimation:**
The robot's lifetime is defined as the first time its position accuracy degradation signal crosses a predefined soft failure threshold, $D$. The RLD, $P(R_k \leq C - c_k | A_{c_k}, P_{c_k})$, representing the probability that the RUL $R_k$ is less than or equal to $C - c_k$, is estimated using two approaches:

1. **Approach 1: Effective Degradation Rate (Closed-Form):**
 This approach leverages the stationary distribution ($\pi$) of the CTMC to calculate an "effective degradation rate" ($\mu_\phi$). Assuming that, for long prediction horizons, the robot experiences tasks according to this stationary distribution, the RUL ($T$) is approximated by an Inverse Gaussian distribution (IG). The parameters of this IG distribution are derived from the current degradation state, the failure threshold, the effective degradation rate, and the diffusion parameter $\gamma$. This provides a computationally efficient closed-form solution.

2. **Approach 2: Monte Carlo Simulation:**
 This numerical method involves generating a large number ($M$) of sample paths for the random accuracy model parameters ($\alpha, \beta$) and severity model parameters ($q_{ij}$). For each sample path, the RLD is computed by numerically evaluating an expectation related to the probability of a Brownian motion crossing a time-dependent boundary. The final RLD is then obtained by averaging the RLDs from all sample paths.

**Theoretical Analysis:**
A theoretical result (Lemma 1) demonstrates that, under the conditions of an ergodic CTMC, an infinite number of Monte Carlo samples ($M \rightarrow \infty$), and a sufficiently long prediction horizon ($(C-c_k) \rightarrow \infty$), the expected RUL computed using Monte Carlo simulations ($E[T_2]$) converges to a value greater than or equal to the expected RUL derived from the effective degradation rate approach ($E[T_1]$). This suggests that the closed-form approach provides a conservative lower bound for the expected lifetime.

**Simulation Studies:**
The framework's validation was conducted using two physics-based robotic simulators:
- **Task Planner Simulation:** A two-state CTMC modeled the task planner, scheduling tasks with 1 kg and 5 kg payloads, with a 50/50 stationary distribution. Inspections occurred every 50 operational cycles.
- **Robot Degradation Simulation:** Degradation was simulated by increasing the joint viscous friction coefficient. The rate of change of this coefficient was linearly dependent on payload weight. The effect of this joint-level degradation was observed at the end-effector's position accuracy.
- **Robot Fleets:** Experiments involved 25 planar (UR5e model) and 25 spatial (Panda robot model) manipulators.
- **Parameter Estimation:** Prior distributions for $\alpha$ and $\beta$ were estimated from 24 training robots using least-squares, then updated via Bayesian methods for a test robot. The diffusion parameter $\gamma^2$ was estimated from the variance of error increments. Failure thresholds were set at 0.27m for planar and 0.021m for spatial robots.

**Comparison Baselines:**
The proposed approaches were compared against two common prognostic models:
1. **Fixed Degradation Rate Model:** A Brownian motion-based model with a constant degradation rate, independent of task severity.
2. **Deterministic Task Severity Profile Model:** A Brownian motion-based model where the future task severity profile (ψ) is assumed to be known deterministically, not stochastically modeled by a CTMC.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation studies demonstrated the performance and characteristics of the proposed prognostic framework.

**Model Parameter Validation:**
Numerical analyses confirmed the underlying assumptions of the models. Shapiro-Wilks normality tests on the least-squares estimates of accuracy model parameters ($\alpha$ and $\beta$) for both planar and spatial robots resulted in p-values above conventional significance levels (e.g., 0.89 and 0.59 for planar; 0.31 and 0.62 for spatial), indicating that the assumption of normally distributed $\alpha$ and $\beta$ was reasonable. Furthermore, analysis of the error increments from the position accuracy model showed they were normally distributed around zero, supporting the Brownian error assumption.

**Remaining Lifetime Distribution (RLD) Prediction Accuracy:**
The framework was evaluated by predicting RUL at various stages of the robot's lifetime (30%, 50%, 70%, and 90% of true lifetime). Both Approach 1 (closed-form) and Approach 2 (Monte Carlo simulation) exhibited decreasing prediction errors as more degradation data was accumulated closer to the robot's failure point. The prediction errors were comparable between the two approaches and across both planar and spatial robot fleets. For instance, at 30% of lifetime, mean errors for planar robots were 40.5% (Approach 1) and 46.3% (Approach 2), reducing to 2.9% and 3.6% respectively by 90% of lifetime. Spatial robots showed similar trends, with mean errors of 33.2% (Approach 1) and 24.4% (Approach 2) at 30% of lifetime, improving to 3.3% and 6.4% at 90%.

**Computational Efficiency:**
A notable difference between the two approaches was their computational time. Approach 1, leveraging its closed-form expression, was significantly more efficient than Approach 2. For training and testing all 25 robots in each fleet, Approach 1 required approximately 5.49 seconds for planar robots and 5.09 seconds for spatial robots. In contrast, Approach 2, which involved generating 1000 sample paths, required substantially more time: 11080.37 seconds for planar robots and 9218.62 seconds for spatial robots. This highlights the practical advantage of the closed-form solution for real-time applications.

**Performance Comparison with Baselines:**
The proposed approaches demonstrated superior performance compared to a baseline model assuming a fixed degradation rate, which failed to account for task severity. This baseline exhibited significantly higher prediction errors (e.g., 228.5% for planar robots at 30% of lifetime). Another baseline model, which assumed a deterministic future task severity profile, showed comparable performance to the proposed methods in most scenarios. However, the reliance of this baseline on perfect foresight of future tasks renders it unrealistic and infeasible in practical applications, thereby underscoring the value of the stochastic CTMC task severity model.

**Impact of Task Proportions on Robot Lifetime ("What-if" Analysis):**
Using Approach 1, "what-if" scenarios were simulated by varying future task proportions (e.g., proportion of 1kg vs. 5kg tasks). The analysis revealed a consistent trend: the predicted lifetime of the robot decreased as the proportion of high-severity tasks (5kg payload) increased. This effect was observable through shifts in the Remaining Lifetime Distributions. The impact of these task proportion variations on predicted lifetime was more pronounced in the early stages of the robot's life and diminished as the robot approached its failure threshold. For instance, for a chosen planar robot at 30% lifetime, the predicted lifetime ranged from 30,000 operational cycles for 100% 1kg tasks to 11,100 cycles for 100% 5kg tasks. By 90% lifetime, this range narrowed significantly. While similar trends were observed for the spatial robot, the magnitude of the distributional shifts was less pronounced, which the authors attributed to higher noise levels in the spatial robot's accuracy measurements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that holds significance for the prognostics and health management of robotic manipulators, with several potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Maintenance and Operational Planning:** By providing robust predictions of a robot's RUL under dynamic task severities, the framework enables more informed and proactive maintenance scheduling. This can reduce unexpected downtime, optimize resource allocation, and extend the operational lifespan of expensive robotic assets.
2. **Improved Fleet Management:** The framework's validation on robot fleets demonstrates its applicability to managing degradation across multiple robots. This is particularly relevant for industrial settings where numerous robots operate under varying conditions, facilitating fleet-wide health monitoring and predictive maintenance strategies.
3. **Data-Driven Decision Support for Task Allocation:** The "what-if" analysis capability allows operators and task planners to assess the impact of different future task compositions on robot lifetime. This insight can guide task assignment strategies, potentially enabling robots to autonomously decide which tasks they can complete based on their predicted RUL and current health state.
4. **Novel Methodological Contribution:** The development of a closed-form expression for Remaining Lifetime Distribution (RLD) under dynamic operating conditions represents a methodological advancement in prognostics. This computationally efficient approach offers a practical alternative to more time-intensive simulation-based methods, especially for real-time applications.
5. **Addressing Real-World Data Challenges:** The proposed periodic inspection scheme, involving calibration tasks, provides a practical solution to the problem of collecting homogeneous degradation data in environments where robot tasks are varied and non-standardized.
6. **Generalizability Across Robot Types:** The data-driven nature of the methodology, being agnostic to specific robot kinematics, suggests broad applicability to various types of robotic manipulators, extending its utility beyond the planar and spatial examples used in the study.
7. **Foundation for Advanced Autonomy:** By linking task severity to degradation and RUL, the framework establishes a foundation for future advancements in robot autonomy. Robots equipped with such prognostic capabilities could adapt their behavior, modify task execution, or signal for maintenance based on their predicted health status, contributing to more resilient and self-aware robotic systems.
