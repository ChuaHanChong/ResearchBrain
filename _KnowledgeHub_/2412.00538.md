# Prognostic Framework for Robotic Manipulators Operating Under Dynamic Task Severities

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper is not about manipulation policy learning — it is a prognostics-and-health-management (PHM) framework for predicting a robotic manipulator's **Remaining Useful Life (RUL)** as its own end-effector accuracy degrades from repeated, variable-severity use (e.g., alternating light vs. heavy payloads). The core contribution is a mathematically explicit model of *path-dependent, cumulative* degradation — a stochastic process whose drift depends on the sequence of task severities the robot has actually experienced — as opposed to a fixed wear-out threshold, together with a closed-form RUL distribution validated against Monte Carlo simulation on robot fleets.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulators degrade (end-effector position accuracy declines) under repeated use, and degradation rate depends on the *severity* of each task (e.g., payload weight) — but task severity varies dynamically over the manipulator's operating life rather than staying fixed.
- Existing degradation/prognostics models typically assume a single, static operating condition, so they cannot capture how a *sequence* of alternating high- and low-severity tasks accumulates into an overall degradation trajectory.
- Predicting Remaining Useful Life under this kind of history-dependent, cumulative degradation requires a model whose *state* is explicitly a function of the load path, not just the current instant.

## Method

- Models end-effector position-accuracy degradation as an ==integral (path-dependent) stochastic process==: $A(c_{k+i}) = A(c_k) + \int r(\psi(\nu))\,d\nu + \gamma W(c_{k+i}-c_k)$ — a Brownian-motion-with-drift term whose drift $r(\psi) = \alpha\psi + \beta$ depends linearly on the task-severity state $\psi$, plus stochastic noise $W$.
- Task severity itself is modeled as a **continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC)** switching between severity levels (e.g., 1 kg vs. 5 kg payload), so the *sequence* of severities the robot has been exposed to — not just its current task — determines the accumulated drift.
- Derives a novel **closed-form expression** for the Remaining Lifetime Distribution (an Inverse Gaussian, $T \sim IG(\mu_\phi, (D-A(c_k))^2/\gamma^2)$) and validates it against Monte Carlo simulation, proving equivalence while being orders of magnitude cheaper to compute.

## Results

- Validated on physics-based simulators of planar and spatial robot fleets under a CTMC with symmetric transition rates (generator $Q = [-0.005, 0.005; 0.005, -0.005]$, stationary distribution $\pi = [0.5, 0.5]$ between 1 kg and 5 kg payload tasks).
- RUL prediction error shrinks as more of the degradation trajectory is observed: **40.5% ± 19.4%** (planar) / **33.2% ± 11.5%** (spatial) error at 30% of lifetime, tightening to **2.9% ± 2.5%** (planar) / **3.3% ± 2.6%** (spatial) at 90% of lifetime.
- The closed-form RUL expression computes in **~5 seconds** versus **~9,200-11,000 seconds** for the Monte Carlo baseline at equivalent accuracy — a three-to-four-order-of-magnitude speedup.
- Confirms that fleets experiencing a higher proportion of high-severity tasks have systematically shorter RUL, and that the framework captures this cumulative, sequence-dependent effect rather than only an instantaneous failure threshold.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Path-dependent, cumulative-damage modeling — an integral of a load-dependent rate function over the *history* of operation, not a function of the current instant alone — is already tractable, closed-form, and validated machinery in robotics; it is just aimed at the manipulator's own actuators/joints, never at the object being manipulated.
> - The severity-switching CTMC is a direct structural analog for a *contact-force-severity* state in manipulation: the same "integrate a severity-dependent rate, track a remaining budget" recipe could in principle be re-targeted at an object's accumulated contact damage instead of a robot arm's position-accuracy drift.
> - The prediction-error trajectory (40%+ error early, tightening to ~3% near end-of-life) is a useful calibration reference for any manipulation-side damage-integral model asking "how much of the load history do I need to observe before my cumulative-damage forecast is trustworthy?"
