# RobotxR1: Enabling Embodied Robotic Intelligence on Large Language Models through Closed-Loop Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Enabling embodied robotic intelligence on edge devices, the RobotxR1 framework from ETH Zurich integrates Large Language Models into a closed-loop Reinforcement Learning pipeline. This method demonstrated a 3-billion-parameter LLM achieving 63.3% control adaptability, surpassing GPT-4o's 58.5%, with successful real-time deployment for behavioral adaptation on a physical autonomous racing car.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) for robotics often rely on static, abstract datasets, limiting their adaptability in dynamic, unpredictable real-world environments.
- Existing distillation techniques (e.g., SFT) for smaller LLMs lack the crucial closed-loop perception-action embodiment needed for robust robotic intelligence.
- Deploying computationally intensive, cloud-dependent LLMs on resource-constrained robotic edge platforms is infeasible due to latency, security, and hardware limitations.

## Method

- The **RobotxR1** framework extends the R1-Zero approach by integrating LLMs into a closed-loop Reinforcement Learning pipeline for dynamic robotic environments.
- A two-stage training process combines Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) for initial knowledge acquisition with Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) using GRPO for interactive behavioral adaptation.
- Small LLMs dynamically adjust the parameters of a low-level Model Predictive Controller (MPC) based on natural language prompts, learning through simulated environmental interaction, and are optimized for edge deployment via post-training quantization.

## Results

- The SFT+RLVR training approach improved decision-making accuracy for the Qwen2.5-1.5B model by 14.03 percentage points compared to SFT-only training.
- A Qwen2.5-3B model trained with SFT+RLVR achieved a **63.3%** control adaptability score, outperforming GPT-4o's **58.5%** on a complex racing track.
- The quantized Qwen2.5-3B model was successfully deployed on an embedded Jetson Orin AGX, demonstrating real-time, on-board behavioral adaptation on a 1:10 scaled autonomous racing car.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Embodied experience through closed-loop Reinforcement Learning is more effective for developing robust robotic intelligence than relying solely on static dataset training or model scale.
> - Smaller, edge-deployable LLMs can achieve control adaptability superior to much larger, cloud-based models when trained interactively with environmental feedback.
> - A two-stage training combining Supervised Fine-Tuning with Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards optimizes LLM performance by integrating both abstract knowledge and practical, adaptive reasoning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is rapidly advancing, with data-driven Machine Learning (ML) approaches becoming increasingly prevalent. The dominant paradigm has been to train large and complex Neural Networks (NNs) on massive datasets, with the assumption that larger datasets improve generalization and reduce encounters with unfamiliar scenarios. However, real-world environments present inherent unpredictability and an intractable number of edge cases that cannot be fully captured by any static dataset. Humans address this by leveraging prior knowledge and contextual reasoning to adapt behavior, a capability that Large Language Models (LLMs) are beginning to approximate in artificial systems.

The integration of LLMs into robotic systems for Embodied Artificial Intelligence (Embodied AI) is a highly active and promising area of research. Existing efforts largely fall into a few categories:
1. **Direct Control with LLMs:** Many initial attempts found LLMs ill-suited for direct low-level control, better serving as high-level planners or decision-makers.
2. **LLMs for Reward/Cost Shaping:** A common strategy involves using LLMs to influence robot behavior indirectly by generating reward or cost signals for dedicated low-level controllers, such as Model Predictive Controllers (MPCs).
3. **Reasoning with LLMs on Static Data:** Recent breakthroughs, such as the DeepSeek R1-Zero method, demonstrated how LLMs can be integrated into Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) loops for structured reasoning tasks on static datasets (e.g., mathematical problems). This approach achieved state-of-the-art reasoning performance but required training massive, computationally expensive LLMs.
4. **Distillation for Edge Deployment:** To address the high computational costs of large LLMs, distillation techniques (e.g., Supervised Fine-Tuning or SFT) are used to transfer reasoning capabilities from large, cloud-based models to smaller, more efficient ones suitable for edge deployment.

This paper critically analyzes the limitations of these existing approaches, particularly for high-stakes, real-world applications like Autonomous Driving Systems (ADS). It highlights two key gaps:
- **Static vs. Dynamic Environments:** The R1-Zero framework, while groundbreaking for reasoning, was designed for static datasets. Robotic operations, by contrast, demand continuous interaction with dynamic, unpredictable environments. Relying on models trained on abstract, static problems for embodied, context-dependent reasoning is questionable.
- **Abstraction vs. Embodied Experience:** Distilled models, while efficient, primarily learn by mimicking a larger model's abstract reasoning. This lacks the "closed-loop perception-action embodiment" that underpins robust robotic intelligence, analogous to learning to drive solely from a manual versus actual driving lessons.
- **Computational Constraints:** Deploying massive, cloud-dependent LLMs on edge robotic platforms is infeasible due to computational power, memory constraints, and critical security vulnerabilities and latency issues associated with continuous cloud connectivity.

RobotxR1 directly addresses these gaps by extending the R1-Zero framework to a closed-loop Reinforcement Learning setting in a dynamic robotic environment. This work positions itself at the intersection of LLMs, RL, and Embodied AI, arguing that small, edge-deployable LLMs can achieve superior performance through interactive "learning by doing" rather than merely through static supervision or distillation from larger models. It challenges the prevailing "bigger is better" paradigm in LLM development by demonstrating that interactive environmental feedback can enable smaller models to outperform significantly larger, cloud-bound counterparts in practical embodied tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this work is to enable on-board embodied intelligence for future robotic systems that can operate effectively in real-world environments without continuous cloud connection, while simultaneously adhering to practical constraints on computational power and memory.

The key specific objectives and motivations are:

1. **Extend R1-Zero to Robotics through Closed-Loop RL:** The primary motivation is to adapt the powerful reasoning capabilities demonstrated by the R1-Zero framework (which was originally applied to static mathematical reasoning datasets) to the dynamic and interactive nature of robotic domains. The authors aim to integrate LLMs into a *closed-loop Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework* where the model learns through direct interaction with its environment, moving beyond static dataset training.

2. **Enhance Embodied AI Reasoning without Sole Reliance on Distillation:** The paper seeks to overcome the limitations of Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)-based distillation, which, while useful for transferring abstract knowledge, lacks the crucial feedback loop necessary for robust embodied reasoning. The goal is to develop a method where LLMs acquire practical, adaptive reasoning capabilities grounded in physical interaction.

3. **Enable Effective Reasoning with Small-Scale, Edge-Deployable LLMs:** A core objective is to demonstrate that low parameter-count LLMs (e.g., 1.5B to 3B parameters) can achieve high reasoning performance in robotic tasks. This directly addresses the computational and memory constraints of edge deployment, particularly in critical applications like Autonomous Driving Systems (ADS), where cloud reliance is impractical and insecure. The authors are motivated to show that smaller models, when trained interactively, can even *outperform* much larger, cloud-based models.

4. **Validate on Autonomous Driving Systems:** The motivation to use ADS as a testbed stems from its demanding nature as a "concrete and high-stakes example of embodied robotic intelligence." This domain highlights the critical need for real-time adaptation, robust decision-making, and on-board processing, making it an ideal scenario to test the viability and effectiveness of their proposed approach.

5. **Achieve Low Computational Demands for Training and Deployment:** A practical motivation is to develop a system that is computationally accessible. This means making RL training feasible on consumer-grade GPUs and enabling deployment of the trained LLMs on embedded hardware, such as a Jetson Orin AGX, thus democratizing access to advanced embodied AI capabilities.

In essence, the motivation is to bridge the gap between abstract LLM reasoning and practical, adaptive robotic intelligence by leveraging interactive learning in real-world (or simulated real-world) settings, making advanced AI capabilities truly deployable at the edge.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, named **RobotxR1**, significantly extends the R1-Zero framework by integrating LLMs into a closed-loop Reinforcement Learning (RL) pipeline for embodied robotic intelligence, specifically in an autonomous driving context. It leverages and builds upon an existing open-source framework for fair comparison, while introducing novel RL-based training for LLMs in dynamic environments.

#### 3.1. Robotic Autonomy Stack and MPC

The system is built around a 1:10 scaled racecar platform and employs a kinematic Model Predictive Controller (MPC) for low-level control. The MPC tracks a minimum curvature racing line, with its behavior defined by state (longitudinal and lateral distance, heading error, steering angle, velocity) and control inputs (steering rate, acceleration). Critically, the MPC's cost function weights and constraints (e.g., velocity bounds, track safety margin) are exposed as online-tunable parameters, allowing the LLM to dynamically adapt the robot's driving policy in response to natural language instructions.

#### 3.2. LLM Architecture and Base Models

The work primarily uses the Qwen family of models (1.5B, 3B, 7B parameters) for local fine-tuning, chosen for their reported performance. GPT-4o is used as a cloud-based benchmark for comparison. Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) is employed to provide contextual information to the LLM, similar to the baseline, including five retrieved memories. Low Rank Adaptation (LoRA)-based Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) is used for initial training stages.

#### 3.3. RobotxR1 Framework - Two Main Modules

The RobotxR1 framework consists of two core, interacting modules:

1. **DecisionxR1 — Enhanced Reasoning:**
 - **Objective:** To teach an LLM to reason about the current driving behavior of the autonomous car and determine if it adheres to a human-prompted behavior, based on historical robot state information.
 - **Training Procedure:** A two-stage R1-zero process is adopted:
 - **Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Pre-trains Qwen models (1.5B, 3B) using embodiment-specific knowledge, following.
 - **Stage 2: Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) with GRPO:** Further optimizes the SFT-trained model.
 - **Decision-Making Dataset:** A static dataset is created by simulating the robot in eight distinct driving styles (e.g., centerline tracking, reversing), recording robot state information, and programmatically labeling behavior adherence (binary classification: 1 for adherence, 0 for non-adherence).
 - **Reward Modeling:** The total reward ($R_{DecisionxR1}$) is a sum of:
 - **Accuracy Reward ($R_{accuracy}$):** Awarded for correctly classifying behavior adherence.
 - **Formatting Reward ($R_{fmt}$):** Encourages structured output with reasoning, explanation, and answer sections (e.g., using `<reasoning>` tags).

2. **MPCxR1 — Enhanced Control Adaptability:**
 - **Objective:** To enable the LLM to adapt the MPC's control behavior based on human prompts, learning through interaction with the environment. This module moves beyond static datasets by placing the LLM in a closed-loop with the MPC and a driving simulator.
 - **Closed-Loop RL Environment:**
 - The LLM generates MPC parameters in response to a prompt (e.g., "Drive at 1.83 m/s as closely as possible").
 - These parameters are applied to the MPC, and the vehicle completes a lap in a simulation environment (F1TENTH simulator).
 - Behavioral adaptation rewards are computed based on the vehicle's performance.
 - **Generalization Test:** RL training is conducted on a simple circular track, while evaluation is performed on a more complex racing track ("The Grand Tour map") to assess generalization.
 - **Reward Modeling:** The total reward ($R_{MPCxR1}$) is a sum of three components:
 - **Driving Reward ($R_{drive}$):** Rewards the LLM for generating MPC parameters that lead to the desired driving behavior. It's computed as the relative improvement in behavior-specific RMSE (Root Mean Square Error) achieved by the LLM-adjusted MPC ($E_{LLM,B}$) over the default MPC ($E_{MPC,B}$), with a bounded range to manage extreme values.
 - **Formatting Reward ($R_{fmt}$):** Similar to DecisionxR1, encourages structured and reasoned outputs.
 - **Parameter Extraction Reward ($R_{param}$):** Penalizes the LLM for producing hallucinated or invalid MPC parameters that would cause extraction failures.

#### 3.4. Training Details

All RLVR training utilizes Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), adapted from the LoRA-based Unsloth implementation. This reduces VRAM requirements compared to the original DeepSeek R1, making training feasible on consumer-grade GPUs (RTX 4070 Ti, RTX 3090). Training runs for approximately 750 steps (24 hours for the 3B model).

#### 3.5. Computational Optimization for Deployment

To enable real-time on-board deployment, SFT and RLVR-trained LLMs undergo post-training quantization. LoRA adapters are merged into the base model, and the models are converted to Q5 k m quantized format (Q5 GGUF) and run using the `llama.cpp` inference engine. This approach significantly reduces inference latency and memory usage on embedded hardware like the Jetson Orin AGX.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the RobotxR1 framework, particularly the closed-loop RL approach, in enhancing the reasoning and control adaptability of small LLMs for embodied robotic intelligence.

#### 4.1. Decision Making Results (Table 1)

- **Impact of SFT:** SFT significantly improves decision-making accuracy across all Qwen models compared to their original counterparts. For instance, Qwen2.5-1.5B improved by 21.71%-points (from 47.09% to 68.80%).
- **RLVR Alone:** RLVR alone (without prior SFT) was less effective than SFT for improving decision-making accuracy, showing that theoretical knowledge acquisition via SFT is a strong baseline for this type of task.
- **Combined SFT + RLVR (Two-Stage Training):** This combined approach yielded the highest decision accuracy.
 - Qwen2.5-1.5B: Achieved 82.83% accuracy, a **14.03%-point improvement** over SFT-only training (68.80%).
 - Qwen2.5-3B: Achieved 86.82% accuracy, a 3.04%-point improvement over SFT-only training (83.78%).
 - This indicates that an initial SFT phase followed by RLVR refinement is optimal for decision-making tasks, integrating both theoretical and practical knowledge.

#### 4.2. Control Adaptability Results (Table 2)

- **Baseline (No SFT/RLVR):** Larger models like GPT-4o (58.5%) and Qwen2.5-7B (50.7%) showed better control adaptability, but the smallest Qwen2.5-1.5B had significant parameter extraction failures.
- **SFT-Only Training:** Qwen2.5-7B achieved a comparable 58.0% improvement, on par with GPT-4o. However, Qwen2.5-3B showed parameter extraction failures, and Qwen2.5-1.5B performed poorly (11.3%).
- **RLVR-Only Training:** The Qwen2.5-3B model showed benefit from RLVR alone, improving from 50.4% (SFT-only) to 57.2%. The Qwen2.5-1.5B model, however, performed worse than SFT and still exhibited extraction failures.
- **Combined SFT + RLVR Training (Key Finding):** This combined approach demonstrated substantial gains, especially for smaller models, and eliminated extraction failures.
 - Qwen2.5-1.5B: Achieved 41.5% adaptability, a **20.2%-point improvement** over SFT-only (11.3%).
 - Qwen2.5-3B: Achieved a remarkable **63.3% control adaptability score**. This **surpasses the performance of the much larger, cloud-based GPT-4o (58.5%)**.
- **Generalization:** The robust performance was achieved despite training on a simple circular track and evaluating on a complex "Grand Tour" racing circuit and a randomized, unseen set of behavioral prompts, highlighting the model's generalization capabilities.

#### 4.3. Computational Performance and Embedded Deployment (Tables 3, 4, 5)

- **Training Efficiency:** GRPO-based RLVR training for models up to 3B parameters was feasible on single consumer-grade GPUs, taking approximately 24 hours.
- **Quantization Impact:** Post-training quantization (Q5 k m) significantly improved inference speed and reduced memory usage, crucial for edge deployment.
 - On the Jetson Orin AGX, a Q5 Qwen2.5-3B model achieved a throughput of ~38.78 tokens/s with an average latency of ~3.74 seconds. This is a substantial speedup compared to its FP16 counterpart.
 - **Decision Accuracy with Quantization:** Quantization led to a moderate drop in decision accuracy (e.g., Qwen2.5-3B dropped from 86.82% to 79.88%), but the SFT+RLVR quantized models still outperformed base models and other configurations.
 - **Control Adaptability with Quantization:** The Qwen2.5-3B Q5 model maintained robust control adaptability (47.9% improvement) without any parameter extraction failures, making it a viable candidate for embedded deployment. The Qwen2.5-1.5B Q5, however, still suffered from extraction failures, indicating the 3B model offers a better balance for this task under quantization.

#### 4.4. Deployment on Physical Robot (Figure 4, Appendix D)

- The Q5 k m quantized Qwen2.5-3B model was successfully deployed on a physical 1:10 scaled autonomous racing car (Jetson Orin AGX).
- The robot demonstrated real-time adaptation to human prompts, successfully handling compound instructions like "Drive smoothly at 2m/s," "Reverse the car," and "Drive normal again," even from an initially perturbed state.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in "RobotxR1" carry significant implications for the future of embodied artificial intelligence and robotics, particularly for systems operating at the edge.

1. **Paradigm Shift for Embodied AI:** This work fundamentally challenges the prevalent "bigger is better" paradigm in LLM development for robotic applications. By demonstrating that a relatively small, edge-deployable 3B-parameter LLM, when trained through closed-loop environmental interaction (SFT+RLVR), can outperform a much larger, cloud-bound model like GPT-4o in control adaptability, RobotxR1 underscores the critical importance of *embodied experience* over sheer model scale or static data supervision. This paradigm shift could redirect research efforts towards interaction-based learning for efficiency and robustness.

2. **Feasibility of Edge Embodied Intelligence:** The successful deployment of a quantized Qwen2.5-3B model on a Jetson Orin AGX, an embedded platform, proves that sophisticated, adaptive embodied intelligence is not only feasible but also highly effective on constrained edge hardware. This breakthrough is vital for real-world robotic applications that require immediate decision-making, operate in environments with unreliable or no cloud connectivity, and demand high security and privacy (e.g., autonomous vehicles, industrial robots, critical infrastructure inspection). It moves AI closer to the data source, reducing latency and reliance on external infrastructure.

3. **Enhanced Robotic Adaptability and Robustness:** Robots traditionally rely on carefully engineered control systems or models trained on extensive, but ultimately finite, datasets. RobotxR1 introduces a method for robots to learn to adapt their behavior dynamically in response to novel or ambiguous human instructions and unforeseen environmental conditions, much like humans "learn by doing." This "practical knowledge" gained through interaction leads to more robust, flexible, and generalizable robotic systems capable of handling the inherent unpredictability of real-world scenarios.

4. **Accessibility and Cost-Effectiveness:** The demonstration that complex RL training for LLMs can be achieved on consumer-grade GPUs, and that the resulting models can run on affordable embedded hardware, democratizes access to advanced robotic AI. This lowers the barrier to entry for researchers, small businesses, and educational institutions, fostering innovation and wider adoption of LLM-powered robots.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** This work opens several avenues for future research:
 - **Scaling Interactive RL:** Addressing the current limitations regarding the scalability of LLM-in-the-loop RL training, especially parallel simulation.
 - **Generalization to Diverse Robotic Systems:** Adapting the RobotxR1 framework to other robotic platforms (manipulators, aerial vehicles, humanoid robots) and a wider range of tasks beyond autonomous driving.
 - **Robustness of Simulation-to-Real Transfer:** Further investigating and improving the robustness of transferring learned behaviors from simulation to the physical world.
 - **Understanding Embodied Reasoning:** Delving deeper into what specific types of reasoning capabilities are fostered in LLMs through embodied RL and how they differ from those acquired via static datasets.
 - **Human-Robot Interaction:** Improving the natural language interface and the robot's ability to interpret nuanced or complex human commands, leveraging the inherent linguistic capabilities of LLMs.

In conclusion, RobotxR1 makes a compelling case for interactive, feedback-driven learning as a cornerstone for future embodied AI, proving that intelligence in robotics is not solely about model size but about how models acquire and apply practical knowledge within their operational environment. This represents a significant step towards truly intelligent, autonomous, and deployable robotic systems.
