# Learning and Leveraging World Models in Visual Representation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Image World Models (IWM) introduces a self-supervised learning framework that learns visual representations by predicting photometric transformations in latent space. This approach enables control over representation properties along a spectrum from equivariant to invariant and allows for parameter-efficient fine-tuning on downstream tasks by directly leveraging the pre-trained world model.

## Problem

- Existing self-supervised learning methods often discard the learned 'world model' (predictor or decoder) after pre-training, losing potentially valuable information.
- Current self-supervised approaches typically yield representations with fixed properties (e.g., highly invariant), limiting their flexibility for diverse downstream applications.
- Adapting large pre-trained models to specific tasks often requires fine-tuning the entire encoder, which can be computationally and parameter-inefficient.

## Method

- ==Image World Models== (**IWM**) extends Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) by training a world model to predict how photometric transformations affect an image's latent representation.
- The architecture consists of an encoder, an exponential moving average (EMA) encoder, and a predictor/world model, trained to minimize the L2 distance between predicted and actual target representations.
- Instead of discarding the world model after pre-training, IWM reuses and fine-tunes it for downstream tasks like classification and segmentation.

## Results

- The framework successfully generates representations across a spectrum from equivariant to invariant, validated by correlation between Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) and task performance.
- Achieved comparable or superior performance on ImageNet classification and ADE20k semantic segmentation by fine-tuning only the predictor, using significantly fewer trainable parameters than traditional encoder fine-tuning.
- Visualizations demonstrate the world model's accurate prediction of photometric transformations in latent space, confirming its ability to learn how image properties change.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The capacity of the world model allows control over the learned representations, ranging from equivariant (preserving transformation information) to invariant (abstracted from transformations).
> - The pre-trained world model (predictor) can be directly fine-tuned for downstream tasks, offering a parameter-efficient alternative to fine-tuning the encoder.
> - The framework enables efficient multi-task learning by utilizing a single frozen encoder and task-specific predictors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

In the field of computer vision, self-supervised learning (SSL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for training models without requiring extensive labeled datasets. Various SSL methods have been developed, including contrastive learning approaches like SimCLR and BYOL, and masked image modeling techniques like MAE and BEiT. While these methods have shown impressive results, they typically discard a crucial component after pre-training: the "world model" (predictor or decoder).

The research by Garrido et al. introduces Image World Models (IWM), a novel approach that not only learns meaningful visual representations but also leverages the learned world model for downstream tasks. This approach draws inspiration from reinforcement learning, where world models learn to predict the consequences of actions, enabling planning and decision-making.

### 2. Understanding Image World Models

The IWM framework builds upon Joint-Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPAs) and extends them to include photometric transformations. The key insight is that by learning to predict transformations in latent space, the model develops a richer understanding of visual content.

In IWM, there are two views of an image:
1. A **source view** (x) generated by applying various augmentations to an image
2. A **target view** (y) of the same image with fewer augmentations

The world model is trained to predict how transformations affect the representation of the image, essentially learning the relationship between the source and target views in latent space.

### 3. The Architecture of IWM

The IWM architecture consists of several key components:

1. **Encoder Network (fθ)**: Processes the source view (x) to produce a latent representation.
2. **EMA Encoder (fEMAθ)**: An exponential moving average of the encoder that processes the target view (y).
3. **Predictor/World Model (pφ)**: Takes the encoded source representation, transformation parameters, and mask tokens as input and predicts the encoded target representation.

The training objective is to minimize the squared L2 distance between the predicted target representation and the actual target representation:

$$L(\theta, \phi) = ||p_\phi(f_\theta(x), a_{x\rightarrow y}, m_a) - f_\theta^{EMA}(y)||_2^2$$

Where:
- $f_\theta(x)$ is the encoded source view
- $a_{x\rightarrow y}$ are the transformation parameters
- $m_a$ are mask tokens
- $f_\theta^{EMA}(y)$ is the encoded target view

### 4. Equivariance vs Invariance in Representations

One of the most significant contributions of the IWM framework is the ability to control the properties of learned representations by adjusting the capacity of the world model:

- **Equivariant Representations**: When the world model has high capacity, it learns to predict transformations accurately, resulting in representations that change predictably when the input is transformed. These representations preserve more information about the input.

- **Invariant Representations**: When the world model has limited capacity, it struggles to predict transformations, forcing the encoder to learn representations that are invariant to these transformations. These representations are more abstract and similar to those learned by contrastive methods.

### 5. Leveraging World Models for Downstream Tasks

A core innovation of the IWM approach is the reuse of the world model for downstream tasks. Instead of discarding the predictor after pre-training (as is common in many SSL methods), the authors fine-tune it for tasks like image classification and semantic segmentation.

The fine-tuning process involves:
1. Freezing the encoder weights
2. Fine-tuning only the predictor network for the specific task

This approach offers several advantages:
- It is more parameter-efficient than fine-tuning the encoder
- It achieves comparable or better performance with fewer trainable parameters
- It works particularly well with equivariant representations

### 7. Experimental Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments to evaluate the IWM framework, focusing on:

1. **World Model Quality**: They assessed how well the model predicts transformations in latent space using Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR).

2. **Image Classification**: They evaluated performance on ImageNet classification under different fine-tuning strategies.

3. **Semantic Segmentation**: They tested the approach on the ADE20k dataset for semantic segmentation.

4. **Representation Analysis**: They visualized the similarity matrices between images to understand the properties of learned representations.

### 8. Implications and Future Directions

The IWM framework has several important implications for the field of computer vision:

1. **Representation Control**: It provides a way to control the properties of learned representations (invariance vs. equivariance), allowing researchers to tailor representations to specific tasks.

2. **Parameter Efficiency**: Predictor fine-tuning offers a more parameter-efficient way to adapt pre-trained models to specific tasks, which is important for resource-constrained scenarios.

3. **Bridge to Reinforcement Learning**: It bridges the gap between SSL and reinforcement learning by applying the concept of world models to visual representation learning.

4. **Flexible Representations**: The ability to modulate the level of abstraction in representations provides a valuable tool for deployment in various scenarios, from those requiring robustness to those needing generalization.

Future directions for this work could include:
- Extending the approach to video understanding
- Incorporating more complex transformations beyond photometric changes
- Exploring the use of IWM in domain adaptation scenarios
- Investigating how these principles might apply to other modalities beyond vision

### 9. Conclusion

The Image World Models (IWM) framework represents a significant advancement in self-supervised visual representation learning. By learning and leveraging world models, IWM not only produces high-quality visual representations but also offers a more efficient way to adapt these representations to downstream tasks.

The key contributions of this work include:
- A novel framework for learning visual representations that can be tailored between equivariance and invariance
- A demonstration that world models, often discarded in SSL, contain valuable information that can be reused
- A more parameter-efficient fine-tuning approach that achieves comparable or better performance than traditional methods
- The ability to perform multi-task learning efficiently by using a single encoder with task-specific predictors

This research opens up new possibilities for self-supervised learning in computer vision and provides a deeper understanding of the properties of learned representations. The ability to control representation properties and efficiently adapt models to new tasks has the potential to impact various applications, from image classification and segmentation to more complex visual understanding tasks.
