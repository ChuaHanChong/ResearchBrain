# Sharp Analysis for KL-Regularized Contextual Bandits and RLHF

> [!summary] Summary
> This work presents a sharp sample complexity analysis for KL-regularized contextual bandits and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), demonstrating that KL-regularization can improve sample complexity from an O(1/ϵ2)O(1/ϵ2) rate to an O(1/ϵ)O(1/ϵ) rate. Researchers at UCLA and University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign developed a two-stage mixed sampling algorithm that achieves this efficiency while showing an additive dependence on a novel data coverage coefficient.

## Problem

- The theoretical understanding of KL-regularization's impact on sample complexity in RL/RLHF was limited, often yielding O(1/ϵ2)O(1/ϵ2) bounds similar to unregularized methods, despite its practical benefits.
- The quantitative influence of the reference policy's coverage on the sample complexity of online RLHF algorithms had not been extensively characterized.
- Existing theoretical analyses for policy optimization with regularization often relied on strong assumptions (e.g., exact gradients, uniform value approximation errors) not fully reflecting general sample-based RL settings.

## Method

- Defined the KL-regularized contextual bandit problem and extended it to RLHF with preference feedback using the ==Bradley-Terry== model, incorporating a novel data coverage condition (quantified by D2D^2D2).
- Proposed a Two-stage Mixed-Policy Sampling (TMPS) algorithm that first collects data using a reference policy, then refines it using an intermediate learned policy to improve reward function estimation.
- Developed a novel suboptimality decomposition technique that leverages the strong convexity induced by KL-regularization, directly relating suboptimality to the squared error of the estimated reward function.

## Results

- Established a lower bound for sample complexity of Ω(min⁡(η/ϵ,1/ϵ2)log⁡NR(ϵ))Ω(η / ε, 1/ε^2) \log N_{R}}(ε))Ω(min(η/ϵ,1/ϵ2)logNR(ϵ)) for both KL-regularized contextual bandits and RLHF, suggesting potential O(1/ϵ)O(1/ϵ) efficiency.
- The TMPS algorithm achieved an upper bound of O(max⁡(η2D2,η/ϵ)log⁡NR(ϵ/δ))O(η^2 D^2, η/ε) \log N_{R}}(ε/δ))O(max(η2D2,η/ϵ)logNR(ϵ/δ)) for contextual bandits and O(max⁡(η2D2,η/ϵ)eBlog⁡NR(ϵ/δ))O(η^2 D^2, η/ε) e^B \log N_{R}}(ε/δ))O(max(η2D2,η/ϵ)eBlogNR(ϵ/δ)) for RLHF.
- These bounds confirm the O(1/ϵ)O(1/ϵ) sample complexity when ϵ is sufficiently small, demonstrating a quantifiable improvement over prior O(1/ϵ2)O(1/ϵ2) analyses and highlighting an additive dependence on the data coverage coefficient D2D^2D2.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - KL-regularization can fundamentally shift the theoretical sample complexity from O(1/ϵ2)O(1/ϵ2) to O(1/ϵ)O(1/ϵ) for achieving an ϵ-suboptimality gap in contextual bandits and RLHF.
> - The introduced data coverage coefficient D2D^2D2 allows for an *additive* dependence on coverage in the sample complexity bounds, indicating that strong reference policy coverage can provide efficiency gains without significant multiplicative overhead.
> - The two-stage mixed sampling approach efficiently collects data that is well-aligned with the evolving policy, enabling the achievement of these sharper theoretical bounds in practice.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper positions itself within the fields of reinforcement learning (RL) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), particularly in the context of post-training large language models (LLMs) and diffusion models. Reverse-Kullback-Leibler (KL) regularization is a technique frequently employed in these areas, noted for its role in stabilizing policy optimization and preventing learned policies from diverging significantly from a reference policy. This regularization is central to algorithms such as Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), and is a standard component in RLHF frameworks for aligning LLMs with human preferences.

Despite its empirical success, the theoretical understanding of KL-regularization's impact on sample complexity has been limited. Prior theoretical analyses for KL-regularized RLHF typically yield an $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ sample complexity, a rate comparable to methods without KL-regularization. This similarity in theoretical bounds contrasts with the observed practical benefits, suggesting a gap in the theoretical understanding of KL-regularization's fundamental advantages.

Furthermore, an emerging line of offline RLHF literature emphasizes the significance of the reference policy's coverage—its ability to generate diverse responses for various inputs. While this factor is recognized as crucial for reward function generalization in offline settings, its specific influence on the sample complexity of *online* RLHF algorithms has not been extensively characterized. Existing theoretical works on policy optimization with regularization often rely on assumptions such as access to exact or unbiased policy gradients or uniform value function approximation errors, which may not fully reflect general sample-based RL settings. The current work aims to address these limitations by providing a sharper theoretical analysis that accounts for the specific properties introduced by KL-regularization and data coverage in online decision-making contexts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to advance the theoretical understanding of KL-regularization in contextual bandits and RLHF by providing a sharp sample complexity analysis. This endeavor is motivated by a recognized disparity between the empirical effectiveness of KL-regularization and its current theoretical characterization.

Specifically, the paper seeks to address three core questions:

1. **Fundamental Distinction of KL-Regularization**: Current theoretical analyses of KL-regularized RLHF often yield an $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ sample complexity, which is identical to methods lacking KL-regularization. This raises the question of whether there exists a fundamental theoretical distinction between bandit learning objectives with and without KL-regularization that explains its observed practical benefits in enhancing policy optimization and preventing overfitting. The motivation here is to provide a theoretical justification for why KL-regularization is empirically effective in stabilizing learning and maintaining policy coherence, particularly in RLHF where models can otherwise learn undesirable behaviors to exploit reward models.
2. **Impact of Reference Policy Coverage in Online RLHF**: The coverage of the reference policy ($\pi_0$) is empirically understood to be a key factor in the generalization capabilities of learned reward functions, especially in offline RLHF. However, its quantitative impact on the sample complexity of *online* RLHF algorithms has been largely unexplored. This research aims to investigate how strong coverage of $\pi_0$ influences the efficiency of online RLHF.
3. **Efficiency of Online vs. Offline RLHF under Coverage**: Building on the previous point, a related motivation is to determine if online RLHF, when supported by strong $\pi_0$ coverage, can achieve theoretical efficiencies beyond those typically associated with offline RLHF. This involves understanding whether interactive data collection, even without explicit exploration mechanisms, can contribute to more efficient learning outcomes under certain coverage conditions.

By addressing these objectives, the authors aim to provide a comprehensive understanding of the roles of KL-regularization and data coverage, ultimately guiding the design of more efficient algorithms for online decision-making problems, including the alignment of large language models.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research establishes its theoretical framework by first defining the KL-regularized contextual bandit problem, which serves as a simplified model for RLHF with absolute-rating feedback. This is then extended to the more complex scenario of RLHF with preference feedback, using the Bradley-Terry (BT) model.

**Problem Formulation:**

- **Objective Function**: The core objective for both settings is to maximize the expected cumulative reward while keeping the learned policy $\pi$ close to a fixed reference policy $\pi_0$ via a reverse-KL regularization term: $Q(\pi) = E_{x \sim d_0} E_{a \sim \pi(\cdot|x)} \left[R(\theta^*, x, a) - \eta^{-1} \log \frac{\pi(a|x)}{\pi_0(a|x)}\right]$. Here, $\eta$ is the KL-regularization coefficient, $R(\theta^*, x, a)$ is the true reward function, and $d_0$ is the context distribution.
- **Reward Function Class ($\mathcal{R}$)**: The true reward function $R(\theta^*, x, a)$ is assumed to belong to a class $\mathcal{R}$ parameterized by $\theta \in \Theta$. The complexity of this class is characterized by its $\epsilon$-covering number, $\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{R}}(\epsilon)$.
- **Planning Oracle**: An oracle is assumed to exist that can compute the optimal policy $\pi_{\eta}^{\theta}(\cdot|x) \propto \pi_0(\cdot|x) \cdot \exp(\eta R(\theta, x, \cdot))$ for any given reward function $R(\theta, \cdot, \cdot)$. This simplifies the analysis by separating policy optimization from reward estimation.
- **Preference Oracle (for RLHF)**: For RLHF, preference feedback is modeled by a Bradley-Terry model, where the probability of preferring $a_1$ over $a_2$ is $\sigma(R(\theta^*, x, a_1) - R(\theta^*, x, a_2))$. An MLE estimation oracle is assumed to compute $\hat{\theta}$ from preference data.
- **Data Coverage Condition (Definitions 3.5 & 4.5)**: A novel coverage condition is introduced, quantified by a coefficient $D^2$. This condition measures how well in-sample errors, specifically the squared difference between two reward functions $R(\theta', x, a)$ and $R(\theta, x, a)$, sampled from $d_0 \times \pi_0$, can generalize to out-of-sample errors. It effectively quantifies the ability of $\pi_0$ to cover the relevant parts of the state-action space, crucial for learning generalizable reward functions. For linear models, this coefficient can be bounded by the feature dimension $d$.

**Proposed Algorithm: Two-stage Mixed-Policy Sampling (TMPS)**

The core algorithmic approach is a two-stage mixed sampling strategy, designed to leverage the reference policy and an intermediate learned policy for data collection.

1. **Stage 1 (Policy $\pi_0$ dependent)**:
 - $m$ samples of contexts ($x_i$) and actions ($a_i$) are drawn from the context distribution $d_0$ and the reference policy $\pi_0$, respectively.
 - For contextual bandits, rewards ($r_i$) are observed directly. For RLHF, pairs of actions are drawn, and preference labels are obtained from the preference oracle.
 - An initial reward function estimate, $\hat{\theta}_0$, is computed using least squares regression (bandits) or maximum likelihood estimation (RLHF) on this dataset.
 - This $\hat{\theta}_0$ is then used with the planning oracle to derive an intermediate policy $\pi_{\eta}^{\hat{\theta}_0}$.
2. **Stage 2 (Intermediate Policy $\pi_{\eta}^{\hat{\theta}_0}$ dependent)**:
 - $n$ additional samples are collected, where contexts ($x_i$) are drawn from $d_0$, but actions ($a_i$) are now drawn from the *intermediate policy* $\pi_{\eta}^{\hat{\theta}_0}$.
 - Rewards or preferences are observed as in Stage 1.
 - All collected data from both stages (total $m+n$ samples) are combined to compute a refined reward function estimate, $\hat{\theta}$, again via least squares or MLE.
 - The final policy, $\pi_{\eta}^{\hat{\theta}}$, is then derived using this refined $\hat{\theta}$.

**Theoretical Analysis Techniques:**

- **Novel Suboptimality Decomposition**: A key innovation lies in the decomposition of the suboptimality gap $Q(\pi^*) - Q(\hat{\pi})$. Instead of standard bandit techniques, the analysis exploits the strong convexity induced by the KL-regularization. This allows the suboptimality to be directly related to the squared error between the estimated reward function $\hat{\theta}$ and the true reward function $\theta^*$, under the sampling distribution of an *interpolated* policy $\pi_{\eta}^f$.
- **Leveraging Data Coverage**: The introduced data coverage coefficient $D^2$ plays a critical role in bridging the gap between in-sample and out-of-sample errors. It allows for bounding the $L_2$ error of the reward function under the chosen sampling policies (initially $\pi_0$, then $\pi_{\eta}^{\hat{\theta}_0}$).
- **Concentration Inequalities**: Standard tools such as Freedman's inequality are used to establish high-probability bounds for various estimation errors.
- **MLE Theory for Preference Learning**: For RLHF, a novel lemma connects the negative log-likelihood of the Bradley-Terry model to a squared difference between reward functions, facilitating the analysis of MLE estimators. This ensures that the estimated reward function is sufficiently close to the true one for theoretical guarantees.
- **Epsilon-Net Arguments**: These are used to control the generalization error over the reward function class $\mathcal{R}$ when applying concentration inequalities.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides novel theoretical bounds on the sample complexity for KL-regularized contextual bandits and RLHF, highlighting the benefits of regularization and data coverage.

#### 4.1. KL-Regularized Contextual Bandits (Section 3)

- **Lower Bound (Theorem 3.6)**: The paper establishes a lower bound for the sample complexity of the KL-regularized contextual bandit problem, indicating that any algorithm requires at least $\Omega\left(\min\left(\frac{\eta \log \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{R}}(\epsilon)}{\epsilon}, \frac{\log \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{R}}(\epsilon)}{\epsilon^2}\right)\right)$ rounds to achieve an $\epsilon$-suboptimality gap. This result is significant as it suggests an $O(1/\epsilon)$ dependence for sufficiently small $\epsilon$, which is a sharper rate than the $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ typically found in analyses without KL-regularization. The proof attributes this sharper dependence to the shift in the local landscape of the objective function due to the KL-regularization term.
- **Upper Bound (Theorem 3.8)**: The proposed Two-stage Mixed-Policy Sampling (TMPS) algorithm (Algorithm 1) achieves an $\epsilon$-optimal policy with a sample complexity of $O\left(\max(\eta^2 D^2, \eta/\epsilon) \log \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{R}}(\epsilon/\delta)\right)$ when the reward scale is constant. This is a primary contribution, demonstrating an $O(1/\epsilon)$ sample complexity when $\epsilon$ is sufficiently small. Crucially, this bound exhibits an *additive* dependence on the coverage coefficient $D^2$, defined in Definition 3.5. This implies that strong coverage by the reference policy enhances efficiency without a multiplicative penalty.
- **Results under Local-Coverage Condition (Corollary 3.13)**: When a local KL-ball coverage condition ($C_{\rho_{KL}}$) is assumed (as opposed to the new data coverage $D^2$), the sample complexity becomes $O\left(C_{\rho_{KL}} \eta/\epsilon \cdot B \log(\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{R}}(\epsilon/\delta))\right)$. This shows a *multiplicative* dependence on the coverage coefficient, which is generally looser than the additive dependence achieved under the novel data coverage condition.

#### 4.2. Reinforcement Learning from Preference Feedback (Section 4)

- **Lower Bound (Theorem 4.6)**: For KL-regularized RLHF with preference feedback, the lower bound on sample complexity is also $\Omega\left(\min\left(\frac{\eta \log \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{R}}(\epsilon)}{\epsilon}, \frac{\log \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{R}}(\epsilon)}{\epsilon^2}\right)\right)$, mirroring the contextual bandit case. This confirms the potential for $O(1/\epsilon)$ efficiency in this setting as well.
- **Upper Bound (Theorem 4.7)**: The extension of TMPS, called Two-stage Mixed-Policy Sampling from Preference Feedback (TMPS-PF) (Algorithm 2), achieves an $\epsilon$-optimal policy with a sample complexity of $O\left(\max(\eta^2 D^2, \eta/\epsilon) e^B \log \mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{R}}(\epsilon/\delta)\right)$. Similar to the bandit case, this bound features an $O(1/\epsilon)$ sample complexity and an *additive* dependence on the data coverage coefficient $D^2$. The presence of the $e^B$ term (where $B$ is the reward bound) is noted as common in RLHF literature due to the non-linearity of the preference model.
- **Results under Local-Coverage Condition (Corollary 4.10)**: Under a local KL-ball coverage condition, the sample complexity for RLHF becomes $O\left(C_{\rho_{KL}} \eta/\epsilon \cdot e^B \log(\mathcal{N}_{\mathcal{R}}(\epsilon/\delta))\right)$, again demonstrating a *multiplicative* dependence on the coverage coefficient.

In summary, the main findings establish that KL-regularization can fundamentally improve sample complexity from $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ to $O(1/\epsilon)$ in both contextual bandits and RLHF. Furthermore, the novel two-stage mixed sampling strategy, in conjunction with a specialized data coverage condition, enables this improvement while exhibiting only an additive dependence on the coverage coefficient, which is a stronger result compared to multiplicative dependencies found under other coverage definitions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides theoretical insights into the statistical properties of KL-regularized policy optimization, offering implications for both the theoretical understanding and practical design of algorithms in reinforcement learning and RLHF.

**Theoretical Advancements:**

- **Validation of KL-Regularization's Efficiency**: The work offers a theoretical explanation for the empirically observed benefits of KL-regularization. By demonstrating an $O(1/\epsilon)$ sample complexity, which is sharper than the $O(1/\epsilon^2)$ rates previously derived for both regularized and unregularized objectives, the paper establishes a fundamental distinction. This addresses a long-standing gap in the theoretical literature regarding the true impact of KL-regularization beyond its role in optimization stability.
- **Role of Data Coverage**: The introduction and utilization of a novel data coverage condition, characterized by coefficient $D^2$, clarify how the reference policy's breadth influences sample complexity. The discovery of an *additive* dependence on $D^2$ (compared to typical *multiplicative* dependencies) suggests that strong reference policy coverage can provide efficiency gains without incurring significant overhead, even in the absence of explicit exploration mechanisms.
- **Novel Suboptimality Analysis**: The fine-grained decomposition of the suboptimality gap, adapted to the strongly convex optimization landscape of KL-regularized objectives, represents a methodological contribution that may be applicable to future analyses in this domain.

**Practical Implications:**

- **Efficient Algorithm Design**: The results provide guidance for designing more efficient online RLHF algorithms. The proposed two-stage mixed sampling strategy, which effectively leverages an initial reference policy and then an intermediate learned policy for data collection, is shown to achieve near-optimal sample complexity. This suggests that practitioners can achieve significant efficiency by carefully managing data collection phases, potentially without requiring complex exploration strategies.
- **Decoupled Training**: The findings imply that the training process for reward models and language models can be more effectively decoupled. By using an intermediate policy to collect more aligned data, the overall learning system can achieve near-optimal performance, potentially simplifying the iterative alignment process.
- **Understanding LLM Alignment**: For the alignment of LLMs via RLHF, the paper reinforces the importance of a well-covered reference policy (e.g., a robust pre-trained model after supervised fine-tuning). A policy capable of generating diverse and relevant responses (high $D^2$) can significantly reduce the data needed to learn preferences and achieve alignment.

**Future Research Directions:**

The authors identify several promising avenues for future work:

- **Extension to MDPs**: Investigating whether similar sharp bounds and additive dependencies on coverage can be derived for KL-regularized Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), which represent a more general and complex setting than contextual bandits.
- **Alternative Coverage Conditions**: Exploring how to achieve sample complexity bounds with only additive dependence under other existing global and local coverage conditions (Definitions 3.10 and 3.11). This would generalize the current findings and provide a more unified understanding of coverage.

In conclusion, this paper significantly advances the theoretical understanding of KL-regularization and data coverage in online decision-making. Its findings validate long-held empirical beliefs, provide a foundation for designing more sample-efficient algorithms, and open new theoretical questions for future exploration.
