# Centroidal Trajectory Generation and Stabilization based on Preview Control for Humanoid Multi-contact Motion

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from CNRS-AIST JRL introduce a method for centroidal trajectory generation and stabilization for humanoid multi-contact motion, utilizing preview control to drastically reduce computational cost. The approach achieves control updates in 0.1-0.4 ms and demonstrates stable performance across diverse tasks like climbing handrail stairs and vertical ladders in simulation.

## Problem

- High computational cost of conventional Model Predictive Control (MPC) methods for multi-contact humanoid motion, hindering sub-millisecond control loop rates.
- Lack of general control frameworks for multi-contact humanoid robots, as existing solutions often target specific tasks or rely on biped-specific dynamics.
- Managing the high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) nature of humanoids while ensuring dynamic stability and adaptability during complex interactions with the environment.

## Method

- A 6-DoF centroidal preview control module generates CoM and base link orientation trajectories with associated resultant wrenches, relying on fixed gains and matrix multiplication for computational efficiency.
- A Quadratic Programming (QP) problem projects the planned resultant wrench onto the feasible contact wrench manifold, ensuring adherence to contact constraints.
- Centroidal feedback control compensates for state errors, and another QP distributes the desired resultant wrench among contact points, which are then realized through damping control at limb ends.

## Results

- The system achieved control update times between **0.1** ms and **0.4** ms, enabling a 2-second preview horizon in **0.4** ms, a notable improvement in computational speed over reported MPC methods.
- Stable multi-contact motions were demonstrated in simulation for the HRP-5P humanoid robot across various challenging tasks, including walking with hand support, climbing handrail stairs, and vertical ladders.
- The mean projection errors for the desired resultant wrench after feedback were comparable to MPC-based methods, indicating that the computationally efficient approach maintains effective stabilization performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Preview control can significantly reduce computation time for centroidal trajectory generation, achieving faster update rates (0.1-0.4 ms) compared to traditional MPC approaches, even for longer prediction horizons.
> - Despite not explicitly considering time-series constraints in the initial preview control phase, a subsequent wrench projection step successfully ensures dynamically feasible multi-contact motions.
> - The proposed framework offers a unified control system, capable of handling both bipedal walking and diverse multi-contact scenarios (e.g., climbing with hands) with largely consistent parameters.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics extensively explores multi-contact motion, which involves robots using not only their feet but also hands or other body parts to interact with the environment. This capability is crucial for humanoid robots to operate in diverse and complex real-world scenarios, extending beyond simple bipedal locomotion.

A significant challenge in multi-contact motion planning and control is managing the high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) nature of humanoid robots. To address this, many researchers have adopted approaches that focus on the robot's centroidal dynamics rather than its full dynamics. This reduction in dimensionality helps to simplify the control problem and reduce computational demands, as demonstrated by previous work in this area.

Within centroidal dynamics, Model Predictive Control (MPC) has been a prevalent technique for receding horizon control, enabling robots to adapt to environmental changes and tracking errors. MPC typically formulates the control problem as a Quadratic Programming (QP) problem, which incorporates equality and inequality constraints, including contact force constraints, across all sampling times within a specified prediction horizon. While effective, this approach often results in large-scale optimization problems that can be computationally intensive, making it difficult to achieve control loop rates in the sub-millisecond range, which are often desired for dynamic and robust robot control.

This paper positions itself by proposing an alternative approach that deviates from conventional MPC. It aims to reduce computational costs significantly by employing preview control, which does not explicitly consider time-series constraints in the same manner as MPC. Furthermore, while existing stabilization control methods often target specific tasks (e.g., ladder climbing) or rely on biped-specific dynamics like the Linear Inverted Pendulum Mode (LIPM) and Divergent Component of Motion (DCM), this work seeks to provide a general framework for various multi-contact motions. The authors aim to demonstrate that despite omitting explicit constraints in the initial trajectory generation phase, a combination of preview control, a suitable reference trajectory, and subsequent wrench projection can yield feasible and stable multi-contact motions for a diverse range of tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an online centroidal trajectory generation and stabilization control method for humanoid robots performing dynamic multi-contact motions. The method is intended to achieve two main goals:
1. **Drastically Reduce Computational Cost:** The authors are motivated by the computational intensity of conventional MPC approaches, which often consider constraints for all sample times within a horizon. They aim to achieve sub-millisecond control loop rates for multi-contact motion generation, which they state is a challenge for existing methods. By utilizing preview control, which relies on a single matrix multiplication at runtime rather than iterative optimization, they seek to overcome this limitation.
2. **Enable Stable and Versatile Multi-Contact Motion:** The goal is to create a control system capable of handling a wide variety of dynamic multi-contact tasks for humanoid robots, including those involving both feet and hands, without requiring task-specific parameter changes or relying on biped-specific dynamics. This includes complex scenarios such as walking on uneven terrain, climbing stairs with handrails, and ascending vertical ladders.

To achieve these objectives, the authors propose specific contributions:
- An online generation method for robot centroidal trajectories with low computational cost, based on the principles of preview control.
- A unified control system capable of handling both humanoid bipedal walking and general multi-contact motions, including hand contacts, using largely consistent parameters.
- Demonstrating the capability of a humanoid robot to perform various dynamic multi-contact tasks, such as handrail stair climbing and vertical ladder climbing, in simulation.

The motivation stems from the need for humanoid robots to operate robustly and dynamically in real-world environments, which often require multi-contact interactions. Achieving this requires control systems that are both computationally efficient for online execution and sufficiently general to adapt to diverse contact scenarios and external disturbances.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed control system integrates centroidal online trajectory generation with stabilization control, designed for humanoid multi-contact motion. An overview of the system is provided in Fig. 1. The robot is assumed to be joint position-controlled, and the sequence of contact positions for hands and feet is predetermined by a global planner or manual input.

**A. Centroidal Online Trajectory Generation**

This module generates a 6-DoF centroidal trajectory (CoM position and base link orientation) and a planned resultant wrench using preview control, followed by a projection step to ensure contact feasibility.

1. **6-DoF Centroidal Preview Control:**
 - **Approximated Centroidal Dynamics:** The method begins with the robot's Newton-Euler equations for centroidal dynamics (Equations 1a and 1b), relating CoM acceleration and centroidal angular momentum rate to contact forces and moments, adjusted for gravity. Resultant forces and moments (denoted with a bar, e.g., $\bar{f}$, $\bar{n}$) are introduced to combine contact and gravitational effects.
 - **State Equation for Preview Control:** The linear components of the centroidal dynamics (CoM) are modeled as a third-order system, with states including CoM position, velocity, and acceleration, and the input being CoM jerk. The outputs for the preview control are defined as the CoM position and the resultant force. This differs from traditional bipedal walking preview control (e.g., Kajita et al.) which uses the Zero Moment Point (ZMP) as an output. The authors' rationale is that their output definition supports general multi-contact motion by explicitly providing CoM and force information.
 - **Optimal Input Calculation:** Given reference trajectories for the CoM and resultant force (where the reference force is typically set to zero), an objective function (Equation 4) is minimized. This function penalizes deviations from reference CoM and resultant force, as well as the CoM jerk. The optimal input (CoM jerk) is then calculated using the preview control theory (Equation 5), which involves fixed feedback and feedforward gains (K_fb, K_ff) and a preview window of N_h time-steps. This calculation involves only matrix multiplication, leading to low computational cost.
 - **6-DoF Trajectory Generation:** To handle angular components, the centroidal angular momentum is approximated (Equation 6) by assuming negligible angular momentum from joint velocities, and a constant, diagonal approximation of the base link's moment of inertia ($I$). Similar state equations (Equation 7a and 7b) are formulated for the angular components (Euler angles), with the input being angular jerk and outputs being Euler angles and resultant moment. Optimal inputs for these angular components are derived similarly to the linear components. From these optimal jerks, the planned centroidal state ($r_p$) and planned resultant wrench ($\bar{w}_p$) are obtained. The validity of the angular momentum and Euler angle approximations is evaluated in the simulation experiments.

2. **Resultant Wrench Projection:**
 - **Wrench Distribution Formulation:** The planned resultant wrench ($\bar{w}_p$) from preview control may not satisfy contact constraints. To address this, contact wrenches at each limb end are defined using a generalized form (Equation 8), incorporating friction pyramids at contact polygon vertices, allowing for unilateral contact or grasping.
 - **Wrench Projection:** A Quadratic Programming (QP) problem (Equation 9) is solved to find contact wrench coefficients ($\lambda$) that minimize the difference between the planned wrench and the sum of feasible contact wrenches. The constraints are $\lambda \geq 0$. The resulting optimal contact wrenches are used to calculate a projected, feasible resultant wrench ($\bar{w}_p'$). This wrench is then integrated to obtain the desired centroidal state for the next control loop ($r_d$).

**B. Centroidal Stabilization Control**

This module ensures robust execution by compensating for deviations between desired and actual centroidal states and distributing the required wrenches.

1. **Centroidal Feedback Control:** A PD control scheme (Equation 11) is applied to the error between the desired centroidal state ($r_d$) and the actual centroidal state ($r_a$, measured from sensors). This generates a feedback modification ($\Delta\bar{w}_d$) to the resultant wrench. For orientation, the logarithm map of rotation matrices is used. This feedback is added to the projected planned resultant wrench ($\bar{w}_p'$) to yield the desired resultant wrench ($\bar{w}_d$). The relationship between this control and DCM-based bipedal control is discussed in Appendix A.
2. **Resultant Wrench Distribution:** The desired resultant wrench ($\bar{w}_d$) is distributed among the contact points by solving another QP problem (Equation 13). This QP minimizes the difference between the desired wrench and the aggregated contact wrenches, subject to contact force constraints ($\lambda \geq 0$). The actual CoM ($c_a$) is used in this QP to improve stability performance. The solution provides the desired contact wrench for each limb end ($w_{d_i}$).
3. **Damping Control for Limb Ends:** Damping control is applied to each limb end to achieve its desired contact wrench ($w_{d_i}$). This involves updating a compliance pose (position and orientation) for each limb end based on the difference between measured ($w_{a_i}$) and desired contact wrenches (Equation 15). The compliance pose is then commanded to a whole-body inverse kinematics solver, which calculates joint angles. These joint angles are then sent to low-level joint position PD controllers. Parameters for damping control are provided in Table II.

**C. Robot Controller Implementation**

The control system is implemented in C++ using the mc_rtc framework, with QLD as the QP solver. The control processes operate at 2 ms intervals. Kinematics commands (CoM, base orientation, limb end poses) are fed into an acceleration-based whole-body inverse kinematics (IK) solver. Sensor measurements from joint encoders, 6-axis force sensors, and an IMU are utilized for feedback. The humanoid robot HRP-5P is used in the Choreonoid dynamics simulator, with some validation also performed in MuJoCo.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents several findings from simulation experiments involving the HRP-5P humanoid robot.

**A. Controller Parameters**
The parameters for preview control (Table I), damping control (Table II), and centroidal feedback control (Table III) were established. A significant finding regarding parameters is that the same values could generally be used for bipedal walking and various multi-contact motions, with minor exceptions for hand damping control parameters in specific motions to prevent vibration.

**B. Bipedal Walking**
The robot successfully performed bipedal walking on flat ground, uneven floors, stairs, and ramps. For straight walking on flat ground, the planned CoM trajectory was observed to be consistent with conventional bipedal walking methods, even though ZMP tracking was not explicitly considered. The planned ZMP remained within the foot support region, and the robot's actual ZMP tracked the modified ZMP, demonstrating stable bipedal locomotion.

**C. Multi-contact Motion**
The proposed control system was applied to five distinct multi-contact scenarios (Fig. 4):
- Walking with hands on a wall (Fig. 4A).
- Climbing handrail stairs (Fig. 4B).
- Climbing a vertical ladder (Fig. 4C).
- Moving with both hands supporting the body (Fig. 4D).
- Contact with moving floors and walls (Fig. 4E).
The simulations demonstrated stable performance across these diverse motions, which included various contact transition orders, hand forces, and contact forms (unilateral vs. grasping). The system effectively tracked planned CoM trajectories and distributed desired contact wrenches to the limb ends through damping control. Reference CoM trajectories were generally piecewise-constant functions, synchronized with contact switching, and adjusted based on the specific motion (e.g., lateral CoM offset for hand support, forward offset for ladder climbing).

**D. Motion to Change the Base Link Orientation**
The centroidal trajectory generation component was tested for a cartwheel motion, which involves significant base link orientation changes (Fig. 8). The method successfully generated a dynamically feasible centroidal trajectory and contact wrenches that achieved one revolution of the base link while moving laterally. However, mapping this centroidal motion to joint space for execution on the robot was identified as a challenge for the inverse kinematics, and thus, full robot execution was not within the scope of this paper.

**E. Computation Time**
The computational time for one control update ranged from 0.1 ms to 0.4 ms, depending on the number of robot contacts (Table IV). This was notably faster than reported times for MPC-based methods. For example, the proposed method achieved a 2-second horizon with 400 sample points in 0.4 ms, whereas previous MPC methods required 1 ms for a 0.5-second horizon (10 sample points) or 20 ms for a 3-second horizon (20 sample points). This represents a significant reduction in computation time while simultaneously allowing for a much longer prediction horizon.

**F. Validation of Rotational Motion Approximation**
The approximations made for centroidal angular momentum (Equation 6) and angular velocity in (Equation 7) were evaluated. The mean error for approximating $I_{base}\omega$ with $I\omega$ was 0.044 kg m²/s, and for approximating $I_{all}\dot{q}$ with $I\omega$ was 3.1 kg m²/s. These errors did not lead to motion instability in simulations. The mean error between $I\omega$ and $I\dot{\alpha}$ was 0.0051 kg m²/s, attributed to small base link inclinations during motion. For cartwheel motion, the Euler angle approximation was still valid because the specific ZYX Euler angle parameterization was not significantly affected by rotation around the X-axis. Further, integrating an angular momentum task into the whole-body IK reduced the mean error for $I_{all}\dot{q}$ to 0.011 kg m²/s.

**G. Comparison with Constrained MPC**
A comparison with a linear MPC-based method indicated that while the proposed method's preview control does not explicitly consider constraints, the subsequent wrench projection successfully produces feasible motions. The mean projection errors of the planned resultant wrench onto the contact constraint manifold were 2.2 N and 4.4 Nm for climbing handrail stairs (zero for vertical ladder) for the proposed method, whereas they were zero for MPC. However, after feedback, the mean projection errors of the desired resultant wrench were comparable between the two methods (e.g., 3.7 N and 4.7 Nm for proposed vs. 0.42 N and 0.52 Nm for MPC for handrail stairs; 9.0 N and 8.7 Nm for proposed vs. 9.3 N and 9.1 Nm for MPC for vertical ladder). No significant differences were found in CoM acceleration or angular momentum. The MPC-based method required more than twice the motion duration in computation time, suggesting that the proposed method offers a computationally efficient alternative without significant loss in feedback-corrected performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of humanoid robotics by addressing the trade-off between computational efficiency and motion versatility in multi-contact control. The introduction of a centroidal trajectory generation method based on preview control, combined with wrench distribution, offers a distinct approach to online motion generation.

The primary significance lies in the **drastic reduction of computational cost**. By achieving control update rates of 0.1-0.4 ms for a 2-second preview horizon, the proposed method demonstrates an improvement in computational efficiency compared to conventional MPC techniques, which typically require more time for shorter horizons. This speed is critical for enabling reactive and robust control of dynamic tasks, allowing for faster responses to disturbances and environmental changes in real-world applications.

Secondly, the work demonstrates the **versatility and generality** of the proposed control system. The ability to handle diverse multi-contact motions—from bipedal walking to climbing handrail stairs and vertical ladders—with largely consistent parameters indicates a unified control framework. This contrasts with task-specific controllers or those reliant on biped-specific dynamic models, suggesting a broader applicability for humanoid robots operating in unstructured or semi-structured environments where different types of contacts are necessary. The finding that the control includes DCM-based bipedal control as a special case further supports its generalized nature.

The successful simulation of various challenging motions, including those involving significant hand forces and complex contact types, validates the approach's capability to generate dynamically feasible centroidal trajectories. While the full execution of highly dynamic motions like cartwheels on a robot remains a future challenge due to inverse kinematics limitations, the generation of such centroidal trajectories indicates the potential for more agile and complex robot behaviors.

The potential impact of this research is in advancing the practical deployment of humanoid robots. The high control frequency and general applicability could enable more robust and adaptive behaviors in scenarios such as construction, disaster response, or assistance in human environments, where multi-contact interaction is frequent and dynamic. The method's ability to maintain performance comparable to more computationally expensive methods after feedback also highlights its practical utility.

Future work outlined by the authors, such as integrating online trajectory generation in joint space and exploring hierarchical control schemes, suggests directions for further enhancing robustness, kinematic feasibility, and overall robot autonomy, building upon the foundations established by this efficient centroidal control approach.
