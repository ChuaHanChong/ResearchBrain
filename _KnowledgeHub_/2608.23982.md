# Memory Is Not Always Needed: Characterizing Conditional Memory in Scientific Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Conditional memory (Engram-style n-gram lookup injected inside Transformer blocks) helps scientific reasoning only sometimes. This work characterizes a behavioral knowledge boundary (answer failure, reasoning-step failure, prediction instability) plus layer-stage injection nodes, then builds a Knowledge Boundary-Aware Router: an external gate decides whether memory fires per input, an internal router picks which layer-stage nodes get memory and how strongly. It beats static and activation-rate-matched random routing across biology and chemistry tasks.

## Problem

- Domain-specialized models and retrieval-augmented generation blend knowledge lookup with neural computation, giving no clean separation to reason about when external knowledge should participate.
- Prior conditional-memory results on genomic and molecular models report aggregate gains in narrow settings, never showing memory should be activated uniformly across heterogeneous scientific reasoning.
- Final-answer correctness cannot separate genuine knowledge gaps from faulty derivation, so prior work lacks indicators for when a base model actually reaches its knowledge boundary.

## Method

- Behavioral boundary code: three binary indicators over answer failure, reasoning-step failure, and repeated-inference instability, computed offline against the memory-free backbone to calibrate routing.
- Knowledge-circuit view treats each layer-stage pair (prefill vs decode) as an intervention node where retrieved values are projected and gated into hidden states, enabling causal attenuation.
- Knowledge Boundary-Aware Router: an external data router quantizes pre-inference input proxies into an on/off gate, an internal parameter router masks layer/stage nodes and scales contribution strength.

## Results

- On BioProBench Qwen3-8B the router reaches the family-best error-correction accuracy 0.63 and question-answering accuracy 0.61, whereas Memory+LoRA collapses question answering to 0.39 accuracy, 0.50 Brier.
- On ChemCoTBench Qwen2.5 the router leads editing accuracy 0.51, mean property improvement 0.16, success rate 0.36, and MAE 0.50; Qwen3 internal routing lifts editing 0.495 to 0.513.
- Memory effects flip sign across boundary cohorts: biology instability cases gain 2.09 and 1.82 points while chemistry instability cases lose 4.45 and 3.81, chemistry reasoning-failure cases gain 4.71.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Memory augmentation is a selective computational resource, not a uniform improvement; gains come from choosing which inputs activate it, not activating it more often.
> - Injection location matters as much as activation: late layers favored error correction, middle layers favored question answering, and memory-free stayed best for step ordering.
> - Cheap pre-generation input proxies can approximate expensive boundary signals that need labels or repeated inference, giving routing without training an extra classifier.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the evolving capabilities of large language models (LLMs) in scientific reasoning, a domain that increasingly demands models to process and deduce information from complex biological, chemical, physical, and mathematical systems. Traditional approaches to enhancing scientific reasoning in LLMs typically involve domain-specialized models or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) techniques. While these methods store knowledge, the paper notes that they often lack a distinct separation between the knowledge lookup mechanism and the neural computation process.

Conditional memory, exemplified by architectures like Engram (Cheng et al. 2026), presents a complementary approach by offering an explicit lookup pathway. Previous studies have demonstrated the benefits of conditional memory in specific scientific contexts, such as genomic foundation models and molecular language models, where it aids in retrieving biological motifs and molecular patterns (Xu et al. 2026; Zhang et al. 2026).

However, this paper identifies a gap in the existing literature: the generalizability and consistent utility of conditional memory across a broader range of heterogeneous scientific reasoning tasks. The authors highlight that retrieved information, while potentially repairing missing scientific associations, can also introduce distracting shortcuts or interfere with reasoning paths that the base model could otherwise correctly execute. Therefore, the effectiveness of conditional memory is presented as inherently dependent on the base model's intrinsic knowledge boundaries and the specific computational location where the memory signal is integrated. This work positions itself as a systematic investigation into these dependencies, moving beyond aggregate performance gains to explore the nuanced, input- and context-specific utility of conditional memory. It aims to establish principles for its selective activation and integration, thereby contributing to the development of more reliable and robust scientific reasoning models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to systematically investigate *when, where, and to what extent* conditional memory should participate in the scientific reasoning processes of language models. This overarching objective is driven by several detailed motivations and specific goals:

**Motivation:**
1. **Complexity of Scientific Reasoning:** Scientific discovery and analysis necessitate LLMs that can reliably access specialized knowledge and integrate it faithfully throughout multi-step inference.
2. **Limitations of Existing Approaches:** Current methods like domain-specialized models or RAG often conflate knowledge lookup with neural computation, limiting the precise control over how and when external knowledge influences reasoning.
3. **Non-Uniform Utility of Conditional Memory:** While conditional memory offers an explicit lookup path, its benefit is not uniform. The authors observe that memory can repair knowledge gaps in some instances but may introduce noise, distractions, or interfere with a model's inherent reasoning capabilities in others. This heterogeneity means that blindly activating memory across all tasks and inputs can lead to suboptimal or regressive performance.
4. **Lack of Systematic Characterization:** Prior applications of conditional memory have been concentrated in specific scientific settings, without a comprehensive understanding of its utility relative to the base model's knowledge capabilities or the specific computational points of injection.

**Key Objectives:**
1. **Characterize Scientific Knowledge Boundaries:** To understand *when* conditional memory is genuinely needed, the research aims to characterize the scientific knowledge boundaries of the memory-free base model. This involves identifying specific instances where the base model fails in terms of final answers, reasoning steps, or exhibits predictive instability.
2. **Analyze Knowledge-Circuit Interventions:** To understand *where* memory should intervene, the study investigates the effects of controlled interventions at different layer-stage "knowledge-circuit nodes" within the Transformer architecture. This helps to determine optimal injection locations and strengths.
3. **Propose a Dynamic Routing Mechanism:** Based on the characterizations of knowledge boundaries and knowledge-circuit dynamics, a central objective is to propose and evaluate a novel routing mechanism. This **Knowledge Boundary-Aware Router** is designed to dynamically determine:
 - **Whether** memory is activated for a given input.
 - **Which** specific layer-stage nodes receive memory signals.
 - **How strongly** these signals contribute to the reasoning process.
 The router aims to make these decisions based on task-specific input proxies available *before* generation, without requiring reference answers or multiple model outputs at inference time.
4. **Establish Selective Memory Allocation:** Ultimately, the research aims to establish selective memory allocation as a fundamental design principle for robust and reliable scientific reasoning models, moving away from static or uniformly applied memory augmentations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research adopts a multi-faceted methodology to investigate and implement conditional memory management in scientific reasoning.

**Conceptual Framework:**
The problem is approached from two complementary perspectives:
1. **Behavioral Scientific Knowledge Boundary Characterization:** This view identifies *when* an input challenges the base model's capabilities. For each input, a 3-element binary "behavioral boundary code" `q_i` is defined:
 - `b_A_i`: Indicates final-answer failure.
 - `b_R_i`: Indicates reasoning-step failure.
 - `b_P_i`: Indicates prediction instability across repeated inferences.
 These indicators are non-exclusive, allowing for a nuanced understanding of different failure modes. This characterization is used *offline* to analyze samples and calibrate the routing mechanism, not directly during inference.
2. **Knowledge-Circuit View of Internal Knowledge Boundaries:** This perspective identifies *where* memory signals can influence computation within the Transformer architecture. Each layer-stage pair `(l, s)` (where `s` is "prefill" or "decoding") is treated as a "knowledge-circuit node." At each node, a memory operation involves querying the store, retrieving a value, projecting it into a residual, gating its contribution using the native memory gate, and adding it to the hidden state. This fine-grained model allows for controlled causal attenuation of memory contributions at specific points in the computational flow.

**Knowledge Boundary-Aware Router:**
The core of the proposed solution is a two-tiered routing mechanism:
1. **External Boundary-Aware Data Router:** This component makes a global memory-access decision *before* generation.
 - It extracts task-specific input proxy features (`phi_i^(t)`) for each input `x_i`. These proxies are designed to reflect knowledge demand, reasoning structure, and input ambiguity, and are available without requiring model generation.
 - Proxies are quantized (`e_phi_i^(t)`) into discrete bins.
 - An input score `rho_t(x_i)` is calculated using additive bucket contributions and explicit feature interactions.
 - A calibrated external gate `pi_t(x_i)` (binary: 0 or 1) decides whether memory is activated for the input. The parameters for scoring and gating are tuned via hyperparameter search using the offline behavioral boundary codes.
2. **Internal Boundary-Aware Parameter Router:** If the external router activates memory, this component configures the specific layer-stage knowledge-circuit nodes and their contribution strengths.
 - It maps a pre-inference context `c_i^(t)` (task ID and quantized input proxies) to a vector `eta_i^(t) = [u_L_i, u_S_i, a_i]`.
 - `u_L_i`: A binary mask for selecting specific layers for memory injection.
 - `u_S_i`: A binary mask for selecting prefill or decoding stages.
 - `a_i`: Scaling coefficients (in) for individual nodes, modulating the strength of memory contribution.
 - The final routed hidden-state update incorporates signals from both the external router (`pi_t(x_i)`) and the internal router (`u_L_i,l`, `u_S_i,s`, `a_i,l,s`), in conjunction with the native memory gate and retrieved residual. Parameters for this router are also derived through causal attenuation interventions on existing reasoning samples and hyperparameter search. At inference, this routing occurs without external answers or alternative model outputs.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Backbone Models:** Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen3-8B.
- **Baselines:**
 - Base model (memory-free).
 - LoRA-adapted variant.
 - Memory-only variant (using the Engram conditional memory adapter).
 - Memory+LoRA variant.
 - Activation-rate-matched random router and static "Always ON" / "Always OFF" configurations for ablation studies.
 - Strong proprietary API models as reference.
- **Datasets:**
 - **BioProBench:** A biological procedural reasoning benchmark, covering Protocol Question Answering (PQA), Step Ordering (ORD), and Error Correction (ERR).
 - **ChemCoTBench:** A chemical reasoning benchmark, covering Molecular Understanding (MolUnd), Molecule Editing (MolEdit), and Molecular Optimization (MolOpt).
 - Official test splits and task-specific evaluators were used.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** A range of task-specific metrics were employed, including accuracy, F1-score, Kendall's τ, Brier score (lower is better), editing accuracy, mean property improvement, success rate, extraction rate, MAE (lower is better), and Tanimoto molecular similarity (TMS). Additionally, answer failure, reasoning-step failure, and prediction instability were used for boundary characterization.
- **Reproducibility:** Experiments involved three repeated stochastic runs, with means and standard deviations reported. Detailed hyperparameters for SFT, memory adapter configuration, and generation settings are provided, along with compute environment specifications. A structural comparison of the Engram and MeKi memory adapters is also included.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across biological and chemical reasoning benchmarks, two backbone families (Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen3-8B), and six task types yielded several findings regarding the utility of conditional memory.

1. **Non-Uniformity of Conditional Memory Effects:**
 - Results consistently demonstrate that unconditional memory (Memory-only or Memory+LoRA) does not provide uniform benefits across all tasks and inputs.
 - On BioProBench, memory alone might improve specific metrics like macro precision or Brier score but not others, such as step ordering. Similarly, for Qwen3, Memory+LoRA could increase Error Correction accuracy while sharply degrading Protocol Question Answering performance.
 - On ChemCoTBench, no single fixed memory configuration dominated the entire suite of metrics. Memory-only models showed strengths in some areas (e.g., extraction rate, lowest MAE) but not others (e.g., molecular understanding accuracy).
 - This pattern indicates that memory can contribute positively in some contexts while potentially introducing regressions in others.

2. **Boundary-Dependent Memory Effects:**
 - An ablation study (Figure 3) explicitly shows that the impact of enabling memory changes sign across different knowledge boundary cohorts (final-answer failure, prediction instability, reasoning-step failure) and benchmarks.
 - For instance, BioProBench prediction-instability cohorts improved with memory, whereas analogous ChemCoTBench cohorts declined. Conversely, ChemCoTBench reasoning-failure-positive examples saw improvement.
 - These mixed effects provide empirical evidence that memory both repairs missing scientific knowledge and introduces interference, thereby motivating a conditional, rather than uniform, application strategy.

3. **Effectiveness of the Knowledge Boundary-Aware Router:**
 - The proposed **Knowledge Boundary-aware Router** generally demonstrated superior performance compared to static memory configurations and activation-rate-matched random routing.
 - **BioProBench:** The router yielded the highest Error Correction accuracy, recall, and F1 for Qwen2.5, matched LoRA ordering scores, and achieved the highest Protocol Question Answering accuracy. For Qwen3, it achieved the strongest Error Correction and Protocol Question Answering accuracies within its family.
 - **ChemCoTBench:** The router obtained the best editing accuracy, mean property improvement, success rate, and MAE for Qwen2.5. For Qwen3, it led in editing, all three optimization metrics, and understanding accuracy.
 - **External Routing Ablation (Figure 4):** The empirical feature-based Data Router consistently outperformed the random router in all tested settings across both benchmarks and backbone families. This indicates that the gains are attributed to *selectively* activating memory for appropriate inputs, rather than merely the frequency of activation.
 - **Internal Routing Ablation (Figure 5):** On a fixed cohort of samples where the external router activated memory, the internal router's empirical configuration further improved MolEdit accuracy, reduced MolUnd MAE, and increased MolUnd accuracy for Qwen2.5-7B. This suggests that fine-grained control over memory injection at specific knowledge-circuit nodes can preserve beneficial contributions while mitigating harmful ones.

4. **Task-Dependent Memory Placement:**
 - Experiments on memory-layer ablation (Figure 6) revealed that a globally fixed injection location is suboptimal. The optimal layer for memory injection varied significantly based on the specific reasoning task.
 - Late injection (e.g., layers 27-28) was most effective for Error Correction accuracy, while middle injection (e.g., layers 15-16) was best for Protocol Question Answering accuracy. The memory-free baseline sometimes outperformed memory for tasks like Step Ordering.
 - This finding supports the internal router's ability to control layer-stage contributions, emphasizing that both memory participation and its computational entry point should be tailored to the reasoning objective.

5. **Memory-Adapter Comparison:**
 - A diagnostic comparison between the Engram adapter (used in this work) and the MeKi memory-expert architecture on BioProBench Error Correction (Table 3) indicated that Engram achieved a higher parsing success rate.

Overall, the findings underscore that conditional memory is a valuable but heterogeneous resource. Its effective utilization necessitates a dynamic, context-aware approach that considers the specific input's knowledge demands and the appropriate computational location for signal injection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of large language models and scientific AI in several ways:

1. **Refined Understanding of LLM Augmentation:** The paper challenges the assumption that conditional memory is a uniformly beneficial addition to LLMs. By demonstrating that its effects vary substantially across inputs, tasks, metrics, and injection locations, it refines the understanding of how external knowledge should be integrated. This shifts the perspective from memory as a universal gain to a selective computational resource.

2. **Improved Model Reliability and Performance:** The proposed Knowledge Boundary-Aware Router offers a method for reliably incorporating conditional memory. By activating memory only when needed and at optimal computational points, it consistently preserves beneficial memory contributions while mitigating memory-induced regressions. This approach can lead to more robust and accurate LLMs for scientific reasoning, reducing instances where external knowledge might otherwise degrade performance.

3. **Guidance for Future LLM Design:** The concept of "selective memory allocation" is introduced as a significant design principle. This principle suggests that future LLMs for knowledge-intensive domains should incorporate mechanisms for dynamically assessing the need for external knowledge and precisely controlling its injection. This could inspire novel architectural designs for conditional computation, dynamic knowledge integration, and adaptable model behaviors.

4. **Broader Applicability of Routing Mechanisms:** While demonstrated in scientific reasoning, the methodology of characterizing a model's "knowledge boundaries" and employing input-driven, internal routing mechanisms could be generalized. This framework could be applied to other forms of external knowledge injection, diverse domains beyond science, or even other model augmentation techniques, leading to more adaptable and efficient AI systems.

5. **Enhanced Interpretability and Control:** By explicitly defining and routing memory based on task-specific input proxies and knowledge-circuit nodes, the approach offers a degree of transparency and control over the model's knowledge utilization. This could aid in debugging, understanding failure modes, and ensuring that external information is leveraged in a predictable and desirable manner.

In summary, this work provides both a systematic empirical characterization of conditional memory's complex effects and a practical, empirically-derived solution for its intelligent deployment. The findings advocate for a paradigm where external knowledge is not just added but strategically managed, contributing to the development of more capable, reliable, and nuanced scientific reasoning AI.
