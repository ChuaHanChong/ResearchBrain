# ReLAM: Learning Anticipation Model for Rewarding Visual Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The ReLAM framework automatically generates dense, structured rewards for visual robotic manipulation by learning an anticipation model to predict keypoint subgoals from action-free video demonstrations. This approach accelerates policy learning and achieves superior performance across diverse online and offline manipulation tasks, addressing challenges of manual reward design and sparse feedback.

## Problem

- Manually designing effective reward functions for visual robotic manipulation is a critical bottleneck, as precise state information is often unavailable in real-world scenarios plagued by sensory noise and occlusions.
- Existing reward learning methods suffer from limitations such as instability in adversarial training, sparsity in heuristic visual rewards, or inaccuracies in generating direct image-based subgoals.
- Complex, long-horizon tasks often face exploration challenges and compounding errors when relying on sparse rewards or struggling with consistent subgoal generation from high-dimensional visual inputs.

## Method

- Developed **ReLAM**, a two-stage framework that first learns a keypoint-based anticipation model from action-free video demonstrations and then uses it to provide dense rewards for policy learning.
- Utilizes state-of-the-art vision models (Grounded SAM, CoTracker) and Farthest Point Sampling to automatically extract task-relevant keypoints from video demonstrations, forming structured intermediate subgoals.
- Trains a Transformer-based anticipation model, leveraging DINOv2 features, to autoregressively predict sequences of these keypoint subgoals. This model then guides a low-level, goal-conditioned policy with a continuous, dense reward function derived from Euclidean distances to active subgoals.

## Results

- ReLAM achieved significantly higher success rates, often reaching **100%**, and demonstrated substantially faster learning on Meta-World online reinforcement learning tasks compared to existing baselines.
- In ManiSkill offline reinforcement learning tasks, ReLAM outperformed all baselines except the Oracle (ground-truth subgoals), showcasing robust performance and stability compared to stochastic Diffusion Reward and unstable DACfO methods.
- Ablation studies confirmed the accuracy of the anticipation model's keypoint predictions, demonstrated the superiority of keypoint-based subgoals over image-based subgoals, and identified an optimal number of keypoints and reward function shape for best performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Abstract keypoint representations are more robust and effective for defining subgoals in visual robotic manipulation than raw image-based targets, simplifying the generation and matching process.
> - An anticipation model that predicts a sequence of geometrically aligned keypoint subgoals provides a structured learning curriculum, significantly benefiting hierarchical reinforcement learning for long-horizon tasks.
> - A carefully designed piecewise linear reward function, based on the Euclidean distance to keypoint subgoals, offers continuous and dense guidance, leading to accelerated and more stable policy learning compared to sparse or adversarial reward signals.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) has shown immense promise in enabling robots to acquire complex manipulation skills. However, a persistent and critical bottleneck, especially in visual RL for robotic manipulation, remains the design of effective reward functions. In simulated environments, researchers often hand-engineer dense rewards based on precise geometric information (e.g., Euclidean distance to a target), which is typically unavailable in real-world visual settings due to sensory noise, occlusions, and perceptual ambiguities. This limitation renders hand-engineered rewards impractical and labor-intensive for real-world applications.

ReLAM directly addresses this "reward specification bottleneck" by proposing an automated framework for generating dense, structured rewards from readily available action-free video demonstrations. The current landscape of reward learning from demonstrations can be broadly categorized:
- **Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL) methods** (e.g., GAIL, DACfO): These approaches train a discriminator to distinguish between expert demonstrations and agent-generated trajectories, using the discriminator's output as a reward signal. However, they often suffer from instability and training difficulties, especially when dealing with high-dimensional visual inputs.
- **Heuristic Visual Rewards:** Recent works attempt to design visual rewards based on the distance between observations and target images/videos in a learned representation space (e.g., using foundation models like DINOv2). These methods often yield sparse rewards, rely on pre-given target images, and struggle with noise or blurriness in generated targets, limiting their applicability to open-ended and long-horizon tasks.
- **Behavior Cloning (BC) and Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models:** While powerful, these methods typically require large amounts of data and can accumulate compounding errors in long-horizon tasks, often lacking the continuous feedback provided by dense rewards.

ReLAM strategically positions itself by combining insights from several emerging areas:
- **Keypoint-based Representations:** It builds upon the recent understanding that object keypoints can serve as powerful, abstract intermediate representations for capturing task geometry and progression (e.g., ATM, Wen et al., 2024). This provides a more tractable representation space than raw pixels or full image embeddings for generating subgoals.
- **Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL):** HRL is a natural fit for long-horizon tasks, decomposing them into subproblems. ReLAM explicitly leverages HRL by employing a high-level anticipation model to generate subgoals and a low-level policy to execute them. This aligns with modern HRL trends where neural networks or foundation models generate subgoals (Nachum et al., 2018; Pang et al., 2023).
- **Anticipation Models:** The concept of an anticipation model, acting as a planner to propose intermediate waypoints (Yu, 2025), is central to ReLAM. By generating a sequence of geometrically aligned keypoint subgoals, ReLAM provides a structured learning curriculum.

In essence, ReLAM seeks to overcome the limitations of prior reward learning methods by offering a more stable, structured, and geometrically grounded approach that is directly applicable to real-world visual robotic manipulation, moving beyond the challenges of direct image generation or unstable adversarial training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel framework, ReLAM (Reward Learning with Anticipation Model), that can automatically generate dense and structured reward signals for visual reinforcement learning in robotic manipulation tasks. This framework aims to significantly accelerate policy learning and achieve superior performance compared to existing methods.

The motivation for ReLAM stems from several critical challenges and limitations in current visual robotic manipulation research:

- **Reward Specification Bottleneck:** The process of manually designing reward functions is recognized as a fundamental and severely limiting bottleneck in RL, especially for vision-based robotic tasks where precise state information (like object positions) is often unavailable.
- **Real-world vs. Simulation Discrepancy:** While dense rewards based on Euclidean distance are effective in simulations, their reliance on exact positional data makes them impractical for real-world scenarios plagued by sensory noise, occlusions, and perceptual ambiguities from visual observations.
- **Limitations of Existing Reward Learning Approaches:**
 - **Instability of Adversarial Methods:** Learning from Observation (LfO) approaches using adversarial frameworks often struggle with training difficulty and instability when faced with high-dimensional visual inputs.
 - **Sparsity and Lack of Structure in Heuristic Visual Rewards:** Methods that design visual rewards based on distances in representation spaces tend to produce sparse signals or lack an explicit, structured learning process. This makes them inefficient, particularly for complex, long-horizon tasks with extended periods of partial observability.
 - **Inaccuracy of Image-based Subgoal Generation:** Attempts to generate visual targets or subgoals directly using generative models often result in noisy or blurry images, making it difficult to define consistent similarity thresholds for reward computation.

Therefore, the core motivation is to develop a robust, practical, and scalable framework that can:
1. Implicitly infer spatial distances through abstract representations (keypoints) from images.
2. Automatically synthesize informative, dense, and continuous reward signals from action-free video demonstrations.
3. Guide the agent through a structured and geometrically grounded learning curriculum, particularly for long-horizon tasks.
4. Ultimately, address the critical reward specification bottleneck to facilitate the broader adoption and scalability of RL in practical robotic settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ReLAM is a novel framework that tackles reward design for visual robotic manipulation by learning from action-free video demonstrations. Its methodology is primarily divided into two stages: learning an anticipation model with keypoints and then using this model to provide rewards for policy learning within a hierarchical reinforcement learning (HRL) framework.

#### 3.1. Anticipation Model Learning with Keypoints

This stage focuses on extracting a structured representation of the task from video demonstrations and learning a high-level planner.

- **Subgoal Dataset Generation:**
 - **Keypoint Selection:** Instead of uniformly sampling pixels, ReLAM employs a more targeted strategy. For each video demonstration:
 1. The initial frame is processed using a grounded Segment Anything Model (SAM) (Zhang et al., 2024) to identify and segment task-relevant objects (e.g., "robot arm", "button").
 2. A tracking model (CoTracker, Karaev et al., 2024a) follows the motion trajectories of these segmented pixels throughout the video.
 3. Pixels with negligible motion (below a predefined threshold Θ) are filtered out, ensuring only truly task-relevant moving parts are considered.
 4. Finally, Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) (Eldar et al., 1997) is applied to select a sparse set of representative keypoints from the filtered pixels. These keypoints serve as a high-level abstraction of the task state, encoding object location and posture.
 - **Keyframe Selection:** To capture the temporal structure and task decomposition, ReLAM identifies "keyframes" which mark transitions between distinct linear motions of the robot arm. This is based on the assumption that complex manipulation tasks can be broken down into segments of linear motion. Keyframes are selected within a predefined step range [m, M] by identifying timesteps where the change in keypoint motion direction (angle between displacement vectors) is most pronounced. The keypoint configurations in these selected keyframes define the intermediate subgoals. This process generates a keypoint dataset (K) consisting of sequences of keypoint coordinates.

- **Anticipation Model Training:**
 - An autoregressive model, specifically a Transformer-based architecture, is trained to predict sequences of keypoint coordinates (subgoals) from the dataset K.
 - **Input:** The model takes the initial visual observation (RGB image I0) and optionally a task indication frame (Itask) to enable multi-task scenarios.
 - **Visual Feature Extraction:** A frozen DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2023) model is used as an image encoder to extract robust patch embeddings from the input images, leveraging its strong spatial understanding.
 - **Embedding Concatenation:** These image patch embeddings are concatenated with tokens derived from the normalized keypoint coordinates of historical keyframes (or a special token for the first prediction step).
 - **Transformer Processing:** The combined embeddings are fed through 12 layers of causal transformer blocks.
 - **Output Prediction:** The tokens corresponding to the keypoints are then processed by an MLP to predict future keypoint coordinates in a residual form.
 - **Loss Function:** The model is trained using Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss with a teacher-forcing scheme, comparing predicted coordinates to ground-truth keypoint coordinates from the demonstrations.

#### 3.2. Policy Learning with Point-based Reward

This stage leverages the trained anticipation model to guide a low-level, goal-conditioned policy within an HRL framework.

- **Hierarchical Structure:**
 - **High-level (Anticipation Model):** At the beginning of each episode, the anticipation model generates a sequence of intermediate keypoint subgoals (P0, P1..., Pk) based on the initial observation.
 - **Low-level (Goal-conditioned Policy):** This policy is trained to achieve these subgoals sequentially.
- **Reward Function Design:** A continuous and dense reward signal is designed based on the Euclidean distance between the current positions of the keypoints (pk) and the target keypoint positions (pk,j) of the *active* subgoal.
 - **Distance Metric:** The average squared Euclidean distance across all K keypoints is calculated: `l = (1/K) * sum(||pk - pk,j||^2)`.
 - **Reward Transformation:** A piecewise linear function (Eq. 5 in Appendix A.2) converts this distance `l` into a dense reward `r_dense`. This function is crafted to provide stronger incentives (increasing slope) as the policy gets closer to the subgoal, ensuring continuous guidance.
 - **Stage-success and Final Success Rewards:** An additional bonus reward (`I(l_s <= θ_s)`) is given when a subgoal is considered successfully achieved (distance `l` falls below a threshold `θ_s`). Upon achieving a subgoal, the policy transitions to aiming for the next subgoal in the sequence. A final success reward is granted upon completing the entire task.
 - **Overall Reward:** `r = r_dense + r_success + I(l_s <= θ_s)`.
- **Policy Input:** The low-level policy's input consists of the current RGB observation, the current keypoint coordinates, and the target keypoint coordinates for the active subgoal.
- **Policy Training:**
 - For online RL (Meta-World tasks), the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm is used. Modifications are made to PPO to be compatible with HRL, segmenting trajectories by subgoal achievement.
 - For offline RL (ManiSkill tasks), Implicit Q-Learning (IQL) (Kostrikov et al., 2022) is employed, with rewards labeled using the ReLAM distance-based function.
- **Theoretical Guarantee:** The authors provide a mathematical analysis (Appendix E) demonstrating a provable sub-optimality bound, suggesting that the trained policy will find a near-shortest path to complete the task, validating the soundness of the approach.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ReLAM's empirical evaluation demonstrates its significant effectiveness across a variety of complex visual robotic manipulation tasks in both online and offline reinforcement learning settings.

#### 4.1. Superior Performance and Accelerated Learning

- **Meta-World (Online RL):** On tasks like "Drawer Open," "Door Open," and "Button Press Wall," ReLAM consistently achieves significantly higher success rates (often reaching 100%) and learns much faster than all baseline methods. For instance, in "Drawer Open," ReLAM reaches 100% success rapidly, while baselines either plateau at lower success rates or require substantially more interaction steps.
- **ManiSkill (Offline RL):** In "Pick Cube" and "Push Cube" tasks, ReLAM outperforms all baselines except "Oracle" (which uses ground-truth subgoals, an unrealistic ideal). Its performance is comparable to Oracle, showcasing its ability to effectively guide learning even without privileged information.

#### 4.2. Robustness and Stability

- **Contrast with Diffusion Reward (DR):** The DR baseline, relying on auxiliary RND rewards for exploration, exhibited high stochasticity and sometimes completely failed to learn for certain random seeds. ReLAM, in contrast, provides inherent guidance through keypoint-based inputs and distance-based rewards, leading to more stable and reliable learning across different seeds.
- **Contrast with DACfO:** The adversarial imitation learning method DACfO showed substantial fluctuations in its learning curve, indicating instability in training, especially with high-dimensional visual inputs. ReLAM's approach avoids these stability issues.

#### 4.3. Efficacy of Keypoint-based Subgoals

- **Comparison to Image Subgoal (IS):** The IS baseline, which generates image subgoals via flow matching, performed poorly compared to ReLAM and Oracle. This critical finding underscores the difficulty of generating consistent, high-quality image subgoals and establishing reliable similarity thresholds in representation space. ReLAM's use of abstract keypoints significantly reduces the generation problem's complexity, leading to more accurate subgoals and effective policy guidance.
- **Accuracy of Anticipation Model:** An ablation study comparing policies trained with ReLAM-generated keypoint subgoals versus ground-truth keypoint subgoals showed a relatively small performance gap. This confirms that the anticipation model accurately predicts subgoals, contributing to ReLAM's strong performance.

#### 4.4. Insights from Reward Visualization and Ablation Studies

- **Reward Alignment:** A t-SNE projection of rewards revealed that ReLAM's distance-based rewards are substantially closer to the environment's true rewards compared to DR and DACfO. This indicates that ReLAM's reward signal is better aligned with the task's intrinsic objectives and can effectively reward useful trajectories even if they deviate slightly from expert demonstrations.
- **Effect of Keypoint Number:** For tasks like "Drawer Open," using 4 keypoints (three on the robot arm, one on the drawer) yielded the best performance. Increasing the number of keypoints to 8 or 12 made the anticipation model's prediction more difficult and imposed stricter constraints on the policy, hindering learning. This highlights the importance of choosing an optimal level of abstraction.
- **Reward Function Shape:** A piecewise linear function for transforming keypoint distance into dense rewards performed best. It struck a balance by providing increasing incentives as the agent approached the goal while maintaining stability, outperforming pure linear, exponential, and logistic functions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ReLAM framework presents a significant advancement in the field of visual reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation, with several important implications and potential impacts:

- **Overcoming the Reward Design Bottleneck:** ReLAM offers a practical and automated solution to one of the most persistent challenges in RL—the manual design of reward functions. By deriving dense, structured rewards directly from easily collectible action-free video demonstrations, it drastically reduces the engineering effort required for new tasks. This is crucial for enabling RL in real-world robotic applications where precise state information is typically unavailable.
- **Enhancing Scalability and Generalizability:** The ability to learn from demonstrations, combined with a robust keypoint-based representation, provides a pathway toward more scalable and generalizable robotic learning. ReLAM's demonstrated success across diverse Meta-World and ManiSkill tasks, involving different robotic platforms (Sawyer and Panda) and manipulation types, suggests its broad applicability.
- **Structured and Efficient Learning for Long-Horizon Tasks:** The hierarchical approach, with the anticipation model serving as a high-level planner generating geometrically grounded subgoals, provides a clear and structured learning curriculum. This is particularly beneficial for complex, long-horizon tasks, where traditional flat RL or sparse rewards often struggle due to exploration challenges and compounding errors. The provable sub-optimality bound further reinforces the theoretical soundness of this structured approach.
- **Leveraging Modern Computer Vision and Foundation Models:** ReLAM effectively integrates cutting-edge computer vision techniques (Grounded SAM, CoTracker, DINOv2) and transformer architectures. This demonstrates a powerful synergy between pre-trained visual models for perception and RL for control, allowing the system to robustly extract task-relevant information from high-dimensional visual inputs.
- **Setting a New Standard for Reward Learning from Observation:** By outperforming state-of-the-art baselines, including adversarial methods and image-based subgoal generation techniques, ReLAM establishes a more robust and stable paradigm for learning rewards from videos. Its emphasis on abstract keypoint representations, rather than direct image generation, is shown to be a more effective strategy for guiding policy learning.
- **Future Research Directions:** The paper thoughtfully outlines avenues for future work, which themselves highlight the potential impact. Scaling ReLAM with larger datasets (e.g., Open X-Embodiment) and more powerful Vision-Language Models (VLMs) as anticipation models (e.g., Qwen-VL-2.5) could lead to even more capable and generalized robotic agents. Addressing limitations such as viewpoint dependence and occlusions by incorporating multi-view observations and point clouds would further enhance its real-world applicability.

In conclusion, ReLAM provides a significant step forward in making visual reinforcement learning for robotic manipulation more practical, scalable, and effective, paving the way for smarter and more robust autonomous robots in complex environments.
