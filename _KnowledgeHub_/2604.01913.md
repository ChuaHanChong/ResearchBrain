# The Rank and Gradient Lost in Non-stationarity: Sample Weight Decay for Mitigating Plasticity Loss in Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> A theoretical analysis of plasticity loss in deep reinforcement learning identifies a "Θ(1/k)" decay in gradient magnitude as a primary cause, addressed by the new Sample Weight Decay (SWD) algorithm. SWD, which prioritizes recent experiences, consistently improves performance and sample efficiency across mainstream RL algorithms (TD3, Double DQN, SAC) and environments (MuJoCo, ALE, DMC), outperforming baselines and showing synergy with other plasticity-preserving methods.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning agents experience "plasticity loss," a phenomenon where neural networks gradually lose their capacity to adapt to new data as training progresses.
- This loss results in reduced learning capacity, slower convergence, or training collapse, hindering the development of robust and continuously learning agents.
- Prior empirical remedies for plasticity loss lack a unified theoretical framework to explain its underlying mechanisms, limiting principled algorithmic design.

## Method

- A unified theoretical framework is developed to characterize ==plasticity loss== in deep RL, attributing it to the non-stationarity of data distributions and bootstrapping targets.
- Sample Weight Decay (SWD) is proposed as a lightweight, plug-and-play algorithm that re-weights samples in the experience replay buffer.
- SWD assigns higher sampling probabilities to more recent experiences to counteract the theoretically identified "Θ(1/k)" decay in gradient magnitude.

## Results

- SWD consistently improved performance and sample efficiency for TD3, Double DQN, and SAC across MuJoCo, ALE, and DMC environments, outperforming baselines like Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) with less training time.
- Ablation studies confirmed that prioritizing older samples exacerbated gradient attenuation and plasticity loss, while SWD effectively mitigated gradient decay and reduced GraMa values (a measure of plasticity loss).
- SWD showed consistent effectiveness across varying Update-to-Data ratios, achieving a +**30.1%** IQM improvement at UTD=5, and demonstrated synergistic performance when combined with existing plasticity-preserving techniques like S&P.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Plasticity loss in deep RL is theoretically linked to two mechanisms: rank collapse of the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) Gram matrix and a "Θ(1/k)" decay of initial gradient magnitude.
> - Non-stationarity in both the replay buffer's data distribution and the evolving bootstrapping targets drives these plasticity loss mechanisms.
> - Counteracting the identified gradient attenuation, specifically by prioritizing newer samples through temporal weighting, can preserve gradient magnitude and mitigate plasticity loss.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep reinforcement learning (RL) has achieved notable successes across various domains, including robotics, game playing, and large language model post-training. These advancements are driven by the integration of RL principles with deep neural networks, which provide expressive capacity and adaptive learning abilities. However, a significant challenge in deep RL is "plasticity loss," where neural networks gradually diminish their capacity to adapt to new data or tasks as training progresses. This phenomenon can lead to reduced learning capacity, slower convergence, or even training collapse in later stages.

Prior research efforts have primarily addressed plasticity loss from an empirical perspective. Proposed remedies include Network Reset, Neuron Recycling (ReDo), Noise Injection, Plasticity Injection, and Reset guided by Gradient Magnitude (ReGraMa). These methods typically involve modifications at the model level, such as altering network architectures, re-initializing neurons, or injecting noise to boost adaptability. While empirically effective, these existing solutions often lack a clear theoretical foundation explaining the underlying mechanisms of plasticity loss and how these interventions precisely counteract them. This theoretical deficit represents a gap in the current understanding, hindering the development of more principled and robust algorithms.

This work endeavors to bridge this gap by providing a theoretical framework for understanding plasticity loss within deep RL. It investigates the problem from the perspective of network optimization dynamics, identifying fundamental mechanisms that contribute to the observed loss of plasticity. By offering theoretical explanations, the paper aims to inform the design of new algorithms that are grounded in a deeper understanding of the problem. Specifically, it proposes an approach that is orthogonal to existing model-level interventions, focusing instead on the data distribution aspect of experience replay.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research stems from the identified gap between empirical observations and theoretical understanding of plasticity loss in deep reinforcement learning. Despite the critical impact of plasticity loss on the continuous learning ability of RL agents, its fundamental causes and mechanisms remain underexplored from a theoretical viewpoint. Existing solutions, while empirically validated, rely on intuitions rather than formal characterizations of the problem.

The key objectives of this paper are:

- **To develop a unified theoretical framework for plasticity loss in deep RL:** This involves a rigorous analysis of the optimization process in RL, focusing on its dynamic nature. The framework posits that the loss function evolves with each optimization iteration, akin to initiating a new optimization "task" in each round, with initialization points derived from previous iterations' optima rather than random states.
- **To formally characterize the causal factors of plasticity loss:** The paper aims to identify and mathematically describe two principal factors contributing to plasticity loss in online RL processes:
 1. **Non-stationarity of data distributions:** The replay buffer's empirical distribution changes over time as new experiences are collected.
 2. **Non-stationarity of targets induced by bootstrapping:** The target value functions used for learning continually evolve during training.
- **To attribute plasticity loss to specific mechanisms:** Based on the theoretical framework, the research aims to identify two mechanisms responsible for plasticity loss:
 1. **Rank collapse of the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) Gram matrix:** This mechanism relates to the network's fitting capacity and echoes prior empirical findings, offering a theoretical explanation for the effectiveness of methods like network reset.
 2. **Decay of gradient magnitude:** This mechanism is quantified as a Θ(1/k) attenuation, where k represents the number of learning iterations. This decay directly impacts the network's fitting rate and its ability to escape suboptimal regions. The paper primarily focuses on addressing this second mechanism.
- **To propose a theoretically grounded algorithmic solution:** The objective is to design a lightweight method, Sample Weight Decay (SWD), which specifically addresses the gradient attenuation issue by restoring gradient magnitude. SWD is intended as a general plug-and-play remedy compatible with deep RL methods utilizing experience replay.
- **To empirically validate the efficacy and broad applicability of SWD:** This involves comprehensive experimental evaluation across various deep RL algorithms (TD3, Double DQN, SAC with SimBa architecture), different benchmark environments (MuJoCo, ALE, DeepMind Control Suite), and diverse training configurations, including varying Update-to-Data (UTD) ratios. The aim is to demonstrate consistent performance improvements and establish SWD's state-of-the-art capabilities in relevant tasks, as well as its orthogonality and complementarity to existing plasticity-preserving techniques.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a two-pronged approach: first, developing a theoretical framework to explain plasticity loss, and second, proposing and evaluating an algorithmic solution derived from this theory.

**Theoretical Framework:**
The paper analyzes the optimization dynamics of RL agents by viewing each iteration as initiating a new optimization task, where the starting point is the terminal state of the previous iteration's optimization. This deviates from random initialization typically assumed in supervised learning. The analysis focuses on Fitted Q-Iteration (FQI) for tractability, with extensions to entropy-regularized MDPs discussed.

1. **Non-stationarity Characterization:**
 - **Proposition 1** describes the recursive nature of the empirical distribution in the replay buffer (\mu_{k+1}^h = \frac{k}{k+1} \mu_k^h + \frac{1}{k+1} \hat{d}_{\pi_{k+1}}^h), highlighting how new experiences continuously shift the data distribution.
 - **Theorem 1** establishes the population loss limit, identifying two sources of non-stationarity in FQI: the evolving data distribution (\mu_k^h) and the non-stationary target values (\mathcal{T}_h \hat{f}_{h+1}^k).
 - **Theorem 2** links the agent's performance to the loss function through a suboptimality bound based on squared Bellman residuals. This means minimizing these residuals, which are part of the loss function, is crucial for improving policy performance.

2. **Mechanisms of Plasticity Loss:**
 - **Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) Degeneration:** The paper posits that the non-random, sequential initialization process in RL can lead to a degradation of the NTK matrix, causing it to become ill-conditioned or rank-deficient. This impairs the neural network's ability to approximate the optimal value function, echoing prior empirical findings that methods like network reset or noise injection address by effectively "re-randomizing" the kernel.
 - **Gradient Attenuation:** This is the primary focus of the paper. **Theorem 3** formalizes the "Gradient Dynamics at Initialization," showing that the initial gradient of the loss function (evaluated at the previous iteration's optimum) exhibits a Θ(1/k) decay due to the distributional shift. As training iterations (k) increase, this decay causes the gradient magnitude to approach zero, hindering the optimizer's ability to escape saddle points and thus slowing or stalling learning.

**Algorithmic Approach: Sample Weight Decay (SWD):**
Motivated by the theoretical finding of gradient attenuation, the authors propose Sample Weight Decay (SWD) as a method to restore gradient magnitude.

- **Core Idea:** SWD is a lightweight, plug-and-play method integrated into the experience replay mechanism. It counters the Θ(1/k) gradient decay by assigning higher sampling probabilities to more recent experiences.
- **Mechanism (Algorithm 1):**
 - For each sample *i* in the replay buffer, its "age" is calculated as *t - t_i* (current training step minus sample collection step).
 - A linear decay weighting scheme is applied: *w_i = max(w_min, 1 - age_i / T)*, where *T* is the total linear decay steps and *w_min* is a minimum weight threshold. This assigns a weight that linearly decreases with the sample's age.
 - Sampling probabilities (*p_i*) are then made proportional to these weights (*p_i = w_i / Σw_j*), ensuring that newer samples are preferentially sampled.
- **Orthogonality:** SWD operates at the data distribution level, distinct from existing plasticity-preserving methods that typically modify network structures or parameters. This suggests it can complement existing techniques.
- **Compute-Efficient Approximation:** To address potential computational overhead in large replay buffers, a bucket-based approximation method is introduced. This method partitions the buffer into a smaller number of buckets and estimates the weight of each bucket using its median sample. This reduces sampling complexity from O(N) to O(B) (where N is buffer size, B is number of buckets) while maintaining performance.

**Experimental Evaluation:**
The effectiveness of SWD is evaluated through a series of experiments focusing on five key research questions:

1. **Performance Improvement:** SWD is integrated with TD3, Double DQN, and SAC (using the SimBa network architecture) and tested across MuJoCo, Arcade Learning Environment (ALE), and DeepMind Control Suite (DMC) tasks. Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) is used as a baseline for comparison.
2. **Role of Temporal Weighting:** An ablation study is conducted using Sample Weight Augmentation (SWA), a variant that assigns higher weights to older samples, to reverse validate the hypothesis. GraMa (Gradient Magnitude-based neuron activity assessment) is used as a metric for plasticity.
3. **Adaptability to UTD Ratios:** SWD's performance is assessed under different Update-to-Data (UTD) ratios, particularly in the Humanoid Run environment, to evaluate its effectiveness in scenarios with varying data utilization efficiency.
4. **Comparison with Other Methods:** SWD is compared against other plasticity-addressing methods like ReGraMa, S&P, and Plasticity Injection. Its potential for synergistic combination with S&P is also explored.
5. **Hyperparameter Sensitivity and Decay Strategies:** Extensive experiments analyze the impact of key hyperparameters (linear decay steps T, minimum weight w_min) and compare the linear decay strategy with exponential and polynomial decay.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of Sample Weight Decay (SWD) yielded several findings regarding its effectiveness, mechanisms, and applicability across various deep reinforcement learning contexts.

**Consistent Performance Improvement (Q1):**
- SWD consistently improved the training performance of mainstream RL algorithms. In MuJoCo environments, TD3 + SWD demonstrated improved sample efficiency and higher episodic returns, particularly in tasks like Ant and Humanoid.
- In ALE environments, Double DQN + SWD also showed consistent improvements in episodic returns across Phoenix, DemonAttack, and Breakout.
- For DMC tasks, SAC with the SimBa architecture augmented by SWD exhibited superior performance in terms of aggregated reliable metrics (Median, IQM, Mean) and reduced optimality gap compared to standard SAC.
- A comparison with Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) demonstrated that SWD achieved superior performance with significantly less training time, indicating higher efficiency and more effective learning acceleration. SWD facilitated faster learning during early training phases and attained higher final policy quality upon convergence.

**Role of Temporal Weighting in Alleviating Plasticity Loss (Q2):**
- The ablation study using Sample Weight Augmentation (SWA), which prioritizes older samples, provided reverse validation for SWD. SWA consistently underperformed both SWD and the uniform sampling baseline in the Humanoid-Run environment.
- Analysis of gradient L1 norms showed that SWA led to a reduction in gradient magnitude during training, aligning with the theoretical analysis that prioritizing older data exacerbates gradient attenuation.
- GraMa (Gradient Magnitude-based neuron activity assessment) values, used to quantify neural plasticity, were higher for SWA compared to SWD and the baseline. This indicated sparser gradients and greater plasticity loss with SWA, empirically supporting the theoretical prediction that temporal weighting direction is crucial for maintaining plasticity.
- SWD itself effectively mitigated the increase in GraMa values, particularly in the middle and late stages of training (e.g., Humanoid Run, Walk, and Stand tasks), confirming its ability to alleviate plasticity loss by preserving gradient magnitude when attenuation is predicted to be most severe.

**Compatibility Against Higher Update-to-Data (UTD) Ratios (Q3):**
- SWD demonstrated consistent effectiveness across varying UTD ratios (1, 2, and 5) in the Humanoid Run environment.
- The method showed its largest performance improvement (+30.1% IQM) at UTD=5, indicating that SWD is particularly beneficial in scenarios where gradient updates are frequent, as these conditions are expected to exacerbate plasticity loss. This suggests robustness across different algorithmic configurations without requiring UTD-specific tuning.

**Comparison with Other Methods Designed to Address Plasticity Loss (Q4):**
- In the Humanoid Run environment, SWD outperformed other representative NTK-based methods designed to address plasticity issues, including ReGraMa, S&P, and Plasticity Injection, when applied to the SimBa network.
- The combination of SWD with S&P (SWD+S&P) yielded the best overall results, validating the theoretical premise of SWD's orthogonality and synergistic potential with existing NTK-based, model-level plasticity-preserving techniques.

**Hyperparameter Sensitivity and Decay Strategies (Q5):**
- A grid-search analysis of SWD's core hyperparameters—linear decay steps (T) and minimum weight threshold (w_min)—demonstrated low sensitivity. SWD maintained stable performance across a broad range of settings, indicating robustness to hyperparameter choices.
- Comparison of decay strategies revealed that the linear decay approach utilized by SWD significantly outperformed exponential and polynomial decay. Both exponential and polynomial decay resulted in worse performance than even the SAC baseline, suggesting that overly aggressive weight reduction can be detrimental to learning stability.

**Compute-Efficient Approximation:**
- A bucket-based approximation method for SWD was proposed and validated. This method successfully reduced the computational overhead for sample weighting, matching the wall-clock training time of uniform sampling while retaining the performance benefits of the exact SWD method.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of deep reinforcement learning by addressing the critical issue of plasticity loss through both theoretical and practical advancements.

**Theoretical Significance:**
The paper provides a unified theoretical framework that rigorously attributes plasticity loss in deep RL to two primary mechanisms: the rank collapse of the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) Gram matrix and, more prominently, the Θ(1/k) decay of gradient magnitude. This theoretical grounding helps bridge the existing gap between empirical observations of plasticity loss and a fundamental understanding of its origins within the network optimization process. By formally characterizing the non-stationarity of data distributions and targets in online RL, the work offers clearer insights into how these factors contribute to learning degradation. This theoretical contribution can inform future research into diagnosing and mitigating similar issues in dynamic learning systems.

**Algorithmic Innovation and Practical Impact:**
The proposed Sample Weight Decay (SWD) algorithm represents a lightweight, theoretically grounded, and plug-and-play solution for mitigating plasticity loss. Unlike prior methods that predominantly operate at the model level, SWD intervenes at the data distribution level through age-based experience replay weighting. This orthogonal approach allows SWD to complement existing plasticity-preserving techniques, potentially leading to synergistic performance gains. The consistent performance improvements demonstrated across diverse deep RL algorithms (TD3, Double DQN, SAC), environments (MuJoCo, ALE, DMC), and training configurations (including high UTD ratios) highlight its broad applicability and practical utility. Achieving state-of-the-art performance on challenging DMC Humanoid tasks further underscores its efficacy. The low computational overhead, especially with the bucket-based approximation, enhances its practicality for large-scale RL systems.

**Future Directions and Broader Implications:**
The research opens new avenues for understanding and combating plasticity loss, which is a critical aspect of continual learning and adaptation in AI systems. The preliminary nature of the theoretical framework and its practical application suggests substantial room for future exploration. Extensions of SWD to more complex scenarios, real-world environments, and its integration with other advanced RL techniques could lead to more robust and adaptable RL agents. Ultimately, this work contributes a crucial tool for preserving the learning capacity of deep RL agents over extended training horizons, impacting areas such as robotics, autonomous systems, and large-scale AI deployment where continuous adaptation is paramount.
