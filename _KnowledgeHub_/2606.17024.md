# ExpRL: Exploratory RL for LLM Mid-Training

> [!summary] Summary
> ExpRL, developed by researchers from Stanford University, Carnegie Mellon University, and OpenAI, introduces an on-policy reinforcement learning approach for mid-training large language models by using human-written reference solutions as dense reward scaffolds. This method improves the initial policy for subsequent sparse-reward RL, leading to enhanced reasoning capabilities and achieving 63.41% pass@1 on AIME-2026 after downstream training, outperforming supervised fine-tuning and sparse reward baselines.

## Problem

- Applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) to complex LLM reasoning tasks faces an exploration bottleneck due to sparse rewards when correct reasoning paths are rarely generated.
- Traditional mid-training methods like Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) on reference solutions can disrupt existing model abilities or lead to off-policy mismatches.
- Existing coverage-building methods often fail to effectively broaden the model's exploration to diverse productive reasoning paths.

## Method

- **ExpRL** employs an ==LLM-based judge== to assign dense, numerical scores (1-5, normalized to [0,1]) to on-policy generated reasoning traces based on their alignment with a human-written reference solution.
- It offers two reward variants: ExpRL-Outcome for full rollouts and ExpRL-Process for segment-level feedback, using advantages for localizing credit assignment.
- The policy is optimized using on-policy RL (GRPO/REINFORCE) with KL regularization against a base policy, leveraging the dense rewards to shift probability mass towards productive reasoning.

## Results

- ExpRL-primed policies, after downstream sparse-reward RL, achieved higher performance on held-out benchmarks, e.g., ExpRL-Process reached **63.41%** pass@1 on AIME-2026, surpassing baselines.
- Even before downstream RL, ExpRL-primed policies demonstrated improved `pass@1` and `pass@k` values and maintained higher token-level entropy during Stage-I training, indicating broader coverage.
- ExpRL significantly increased search-oriented reasoning behaviors (verification, self-correction, backtracking) compared to the base model and SFT, and its applicability extended to mixed domains (math, science, coding) using a smaller judge model.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Leveraging the 'verification-generation gap' where LLMs are better at verifying partial progress than generating full solutions can provide informative, graded feedback.
> - Using reference solutions as 'reward scaffolds' for dense feedback is more effective for RL priming than treating them as direct imitation targets.
> - Providing dense process-level rewards for partial progress helps in localizing credit assignment and improving exploratory capabilities without explicit skill definition.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has seen significant advancements in reasoning capabilities. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a key method for further enhancing these capabilities. However, a fundamental challenge in applying RL to complex reasoning tasks, particularly in domains like mathematics, is the *sparse reward problem*. When problems are difficult, LLMs may rarely generate completely correct reasoning paths, resulting in sparse or zero rewards from simple outcome-level verifiers. This lack of signal makes it difficult for RL algorithms to learn and improve.

Current approaches to address this exploration bottleneck often involve a "mid-training" or "RL priming" stage. This stage aims to prepare a base LLM before the main sparse-reward RL phase begins. Mid-training typically focuses on two aspects:
1. **Skill-inducing mid-training:** Imbuing the model with primitive reasoning skills such as decomposition, verification, backtracking, and self-correction, often through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on curated reasoning traces.
2. **Coverage-building mid-training:** Increasing the probability mass the model assigns to a wider range of productive reasoning paths for difficult problems, thereby improving its exploratory capabilities.

Traditional methods for coverage-building mid-training, such as SFT on reference solutions or self-distillation, face several limitations. Directly fine-tuning on human-written reference solutions, which often differ substantially from LLM-generated traces, can sometimes disrupt the model's existing reasoning abilities or lead to an off-policy mismatch if the target distribution is too far from the model's current capabilities. These imitation-based methods may also fail to broaden the model's exploration effectively, as they focus on replicating specific trajectories rather than encouraging the discovery of diverse productive paths.

This research introduces ExpRL as an alternative RL-based mid-training approach, positioning it within the coverage-building paradigm. Instead of treating reference solutions as direct targets for imitation, ExpRL leverages them as "reward scaffolds" to provide dense, problem-specific feedback during an on-policy RL phase. This approach differentiates itself from previous work that primarily uses sparse outcome rewards or relies on intrinsic bonuses and entropy regularization for exploration. By focusing on generating informative dense rewards for partial progress, ExpRL aims to create a more robust initialization for subsequent sparse-reward RL, thereby addressing the exploration bottleneck more effectively in the context of challenging reasoning tasks. The work also contributes to the understanding of how different priming strategies affect the model's reasoning behaviors and its readiness for downstream RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a more automated and effective approach for "RL-based mid-training" of LLMs, specifically targeting the improvement of their reasoning capabilities on challenging problems. This mid-training stage, termed Exploratory RL (ExpRL), aims to utilize large corpora of human-written question-answer data in a novel way.

The core motivation behind ExpRL stems from several observations and challenges in the application of RL to LLM reasoning:

1. **Exploration Bottleneck with Sparse Rewards:** For difficult reasoning problems, a base LLM often assigns very low probability to correct or even partially correct reasoning paths. Consequently, when only a sparse, binary final-answer reward is available (e.g., 0 for incorrect, 1 for correct), the RL algorithm receives little to no signal to learn from. In this regime, RL tends to reinforce behaviors the model already samples well, failing to broaden its exploration into new, productive solution strategies. This sparse reward signal becomes a central bottleneck for scaling RL-based reasoning.

2. **Limitations of Existing Mid-Training Methods:**
 - **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** While a natural baseline when reference solutions are available, directly fine-tuning an LLM to imitate human-written reasoning traces can be problematic. Human solutions may differ significantly in style from LLM-generated outputs, and directly cloning them can sometimes disrupt the model's existing reasoning abilities or lead to poor generalization.
 - **Self-Distillation:** This method attempts to mitigate the off-policy mismatch of SFT by training on the model's own rollouts but uses token-level targets induced by a "privileged teacher" (e.g., the base model conditioned on the reference solution). When the target distribution is substantially different from what the student can reliably produce, such supervision can also hinder generalization or lead to unstable optimization.
 - **Manual Specification:** Existing skill-inducing mid-training often requires manually specifying the primitive skills (like decomposition or self-correction) the model should learn. It remains unclear if this is sufficient for much harder problems requiring complex combinations of skills into broader solution strategies.

3. **Need for Broader Coverage:** The authors' goal for mid-training is to enhance the model's starting distribution by increasing the probability mass it assigns to *productive reasoning attempts* across a wide range of hard problems. This means improving the `pass@k` metric (the probability of sampling at least one correct rollout in `k` attempts), which serves as an operational proxy for coverage. A higher `pass@k` indicates that the model has expanded its set of accessible solution strategies, making downstream sparse-reward RL more effective. The aim is not merely to improve correctness on the mid-training distribution, but to upweight reasoning paths that are conducive to later success.

4. **Exploiting the Verification-Generation Gap:** The core insight motivating ExpRL's approach to dense rewards is that modern LLMs are often more proficient at *verifying partial progress* against a reference solution than at *generating a complete, correct solution* from scratch. This "verification-generation gap" can be leveraged to assign informative, graded scores to partial reasoning traces.

In summary, the key objectives are to develop an RL-based mid-training method that:
- Automatically builds broader coverage over productive reasoning paths.
- Uses reference solutions as "reward scaffolds" rather than imitation targets.
- Provides dense feedback on partial progress, useful intermediate steps, and productive reasoning behaviors.
- Yields a stronger initialization for subsequent sparse-reward RL, ultimately improving `pass@k` and `pass@1` on challenging held-out benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ExpRL is an online Reinforcement Learning approach designed as a mid-training (priming) stage before standard sparse outcome-reward RL. Its methodology centers on using reference solutions to generate dense rewards for on-policy reasoning traces.

**Problem Setup:**
The system is provided with a dataset $\mathcal{D}_{mid} = \{(x_i, y^\star_i)\}_{i=1}^N$, where $x_i$ is a problem and $y^\star_i$ is a step-by-step human-written reference solution. The goal is to train a base LLM policy $\pi_b$ to $\pi_\theta$ using $\mathcal{D}_{mid}$ to build broader coverage over productive reasoning paths. This primed policy then serves as an initialization for further RL training on a downstream dataset $\mathcal{D}'$ using a sparse binary outcome reward $r(x, y) \in \{0, 1\}$.

**Core Design Principle of ExpRL:**
The method's principle is to reward on-policy traces based on their alignment with reference solutions, even when the overall trace is incorrect. This provides a dense reward signal that differentiates between useful and unproductive partial progress, thereby shifting probability mass toward more productive reasoning paths.

**Step I: Assigning Numerical Dense Rewards via Reference-Guided Verification (LLM Judge):**
ExpRL uses an LLM-based judge, based on the same base LLM (Qwen3-4B-Instruct in main experiments), to assign numerical scores to generated solutions.
1. **Judge Functionality:** Given a problem $(x)$, a candidate solution $(y)$, and a reference solution $(y^\star)$, the judge $J$ outputs a score $\tilde{s}(x, y, y^\star) \in \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$. This score is then normalized to $s(x, y, y^\star) \in$. The judge is specifically instructed to *verify* the alignment of the generated trace with the reference solution under a fixed rubric, which measures alignment with techniques or high-level strategies in the reference. It avoids solving the problem, inferring missing steps, or correcting errors in the model's output.
2. **ExpRL-Outcome Rewards:** This variant assigns a dense outcome-level reward $s(x, y, y^\star)$ to the full generated rollout. Unlike binary sparse rewards, this graded feedback allows partially correct solutions that align with the reference to receive a non-zero score, providing a learning signal even when full correctness is rarely achieved.
3. **ExpRL-Process Rewards:** To localize credit assignment, this variant provides process-level rewards for partial rollouts or prefixes. For a generated solution $y$, a sequence of prefixes $\{y_{\le t}\}_{t=1}^T$ is formed by slicing the rollout using the delimiter "###" (a default delimiter emitted by the base model). The judge then scores each prefix $s_t = s(x, y_{\le t}, y^\star)$.
 - **Process-level Advantage Normalization:** These prefix scores $\{s_t\}_{t=1}^T$ are converted into centered segment-level advantages $A_t(x, y)$. The primary formulation used is:
 $A_t(x, y) = \begin{cases} s_t - s_{t-1}, & \text{if } t > 1 \\ s_1 - s_T, & \text{if } t = 1 \end{cases}$
 This design awards positive advantage only if a segment improves alignment relative to the previous prefix and penalizes regression. The first segment is centered relative to the final score to maintain comparable scale.

**Step II: Optimization Objective and Training Details:**
The policy $\pi_\theta$ is optimized using on-policy RL with KL regularization against a reference policy $\pi_0$:
$\max_{\theta} E_{(x,y^\star)\sim\mathcal{D}_{mid}} \left[ E_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} \left[ R(x, y, y^\star) \right] - \beta \text{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot | x) \parallel \pi_0(\cdot | x)) \right]$
- $R(x, y, y^\star)$ denotes the dense outcome or process-level rewards.
- **RL Algorithms:** GRPO is used for ExpRL-Outcome rewards with normalization across $n$ rollouts per problem. For ExpRL-Process rewards, the REINFORCE update with token/segment advantages is employed without group normalization.
- **Training Parameters:** Stage-I (ExpRL) is trained for 230 optimization steps. $G=10$ rollouts are sampled per prompt with temperature 0.8 and a maximum generation length of 16,384 tokens. A reward of 0 is assigned for generations overflowing this length.

**Running Downstream RL (Stage-II):**
After Stage-I mid-training with ExpRL, the resulting primed policy is used as the initialization for Stage-II.
- **Training:** Training occurs on the target dataset $\mathcal{D}'$ using standard sparse binary final-answer rewards.
- **RL Algorithm:** GRPO is used in this stage.
- **Evaluation:** The performance is measured on held-out answer-based reasoning benchmarks (HMMT, IMO-AnswerBench, AIME 2025, AIME 2026) using `pass@1` and `pass@k` (from 128 samples).

**Baselines and Comparisons:**
ExpRL is compared against:
1. **SFT (Supervised Fine-Tuning):** Fine-tuning on the reference solutions in the mid-training set.
2. **Verifiable Sparse Reward RL (GRPO):** Standard GRPO using only a binary final-answer reward from a rule-based verifier on mid-training prompts, without dense reference-guided feedback.
3. **Self-Distillation:** A distillation method where sampled rollouts are trained against a self-teacher (base model conditioned on the reference solution).

**Experimental Setup Details:**
- **Base Model:** Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507 (referred to as Qwen3-4B-Instruct).
- **LLM Judge:** A copy of the Qwen3-4B-Instruct model is used as the judge in main experiments.
- **Mid-Training Data:** A combination of hard math question-answer pairs from InT and POPE.
- **Mixed-Domain Experiment:** An additional experiment uses a larger Qwen3-8B policy with a smaller Qwen3-4B-Instruct judge on a mixture of math, science QA, and coding examples.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluate ExpRL's efficacy in priming LLMs for subsequent RL training, focusing on challenging math reasoning tasks and extending to mixed-domain scenarios.

#### 4.1. Finding 1: ExpRL Yields A Stronger Initialization for Downstream RL

Following a second stage of standard sparse-reward RL, ExpRL variants consistently demonstrated improved performance on held-out answer-based benchmarks compared to SFT, sparse GRPO, and self-distillation. For instance, on AIME-2026, ExpRL-Process achieved 63.41% pass@1, surpassing the next best baseline (sparse GRPO) at 58.75%. Across the remaining benchmarks (AIME-25, HMMT, IMO-Answer), ExpRL-Outcome and ExpRL-Process generally ranked among the top performers, indicating that using reference solutions to score sampled reasoning trajectories is more effective for RL priming than using them as imitation targets.

#### 4.2. Finding 2: ExpRL Improves the Primed Policy Before Downstream RL

ExpRL's benefits were evident even before the downstream sparse-reward RL stage. Directly after Stage-I mid-training, ExpRL-primed policies achieved higher pass@1 and pass@k values on held-out benchmarks compared to baseline priming methods. For example, on HMMT-Nov-2025, both ExpRL variants showed higher pass rates at low `k`, with ExpRL-Process maintaining strength at higher `k`. This indicates that ExpRL expands the set of solution strategies the model can sample, providing a stronger starting policy for subsequent RL.

Analysis of training dynamics during Stage-I revealed that ExpRL variants reduced the number of unsolvable prompts significantly faster, particularly ExpRL-Process. They also maintained substantially higher token-level entropy compared to sparse-reward GRPO, which experienced a rapid entropy collapse. This suggests ExpRL does not merely sharpen the policy but alters training dynamics in a way consistent with building broader coverage. Additionally, self-distillation's teacher policy was found to be much farther in KL divergence from the base model, potentially leading to distribution shift and optimization instability, which ExpRL avoids by staying within a more reachable KL space.

#### 4.3. Finding 3: ExpRL Changes Reasoning Behaviors Relative to the Base LLM

An LLM-based behavior analysis indicated that ExpRL alters the qualitative aspects of the model's reasoning. Relative to the base model, ExpRL increased the coverage of search-oriented behaviors, specifically verification, self-correction, and backtracking. In contrast, SFT exhibited a loss of verification behavior, and sparse-reward GRPO showed smaller behavioral changes. While self-distillation also increased some search-oriented behaviors, its overall performance in `pass@1`/`pass@k` and downstream RL was generally weaker than ExpRL. This suggests that effective RL priming requires both coverage over useful behaviors and coverage over productive problem-specific solution paths.

#### 4.4. Finding 4: ExpRL Extends to Mixed-Domain Mid-Training and Smaller Judges

ExpRL's applicability was tested in a mixed-domain setting (math, science QA, coding) using a larger Qwen3-8B policy and a smaller Qwen3-4B-Instruct judge. ExpRL-Outcome improved the 8B base policy on every `pass@1` evaluation across these domains, demonstrating its ability to improve coverage across a broader reasoning mixture. The method was particularly strong on Math-Aggregate and STEM-Aggregate scores.

For coding tasks (LiveCodeBench), while ExpRL-Outcome improved the base policy, sparse GRPO remained stronger. This was attributed to the robust, domain-specific sparse rewards available in coding (e.g., execution-based verification) and the potentially weaker suitability of reference-guided judging for assigning partial credit in highly diverse coding implementations.

A judge calibration study investigated whether the dense reward signal relied on the problem-matched reference. For math and science domains, correct-reference judging consistently yielded the lowest misplacement rate (FPR + FNR), while removing the reference or using a wrong reference significantly weakened the signal. This indicates that the useful reward signal is not generic judge confidence but verification against a correct, problem-matched reference. For coding, however, reference conditioning was less critical, suggesting the judge relied more on inferred functional correctness. The study also highlighted the need for a minimally capable judge, as a Qwen3-0.6B judge proved unstable.

#### 4.5. Ablation Study: Advantage Centering for Process Rewards (Appendix A.2)

An ablation on different process reward advantage normalizations (DeltaNorm, EndNorm, GroupNorm) showed broadly similar Stage-I `pass@k` curves across benchmarks, suggesting that ExpRL's performance is not highly sensitive to the precise centering scheme for process rewards.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of LLM reasoning by introducing ExpRL, a method designed to address the exploration bottleneck inherent in sparse-reward reinforcement learning for complex tasks.

The key significance of ExpRL lies in its ability to **provide a stronger, more automated RL-ready initialization for LLMs.** By leveraging large corpora of human-written solutions not as targets for imitation, but as scaffolds for dense reward generation, ExpRL shifts probability mass towards productive reasoning paths. This process demonstrably improves both the immediate exploratory capabilities (higher `pass@k`) of the primed model and its subsequent performance after downstream sparse-reward RL. This is crucial for enabling LLMs to tackle problems where correct solutions are rare and traditional sparse rewards offer insufficient learning signals.

The potential impact of ExpRL is multifaceted:
- **Enhanced LLM Reasoning:** By improving the initial state of LLMs for RL, ExpRL can contribute to models that are more capable of complex, multi-step reasoning, particularly in domains like mathematics. This could lead to advancements in areas requiring robust problem-solving, such as scientific discovery, engineering, and advanced analytics.
- **More Efficient RL Training:** A better initialized policy can reduce the sample complexity and training time required for downstream sparse-reward RL, making the overall training pipeline more efficient and scalable.
- **Automated Mid-Training:** ExpRL offers a more automated and scalable approach to mid-training compared to methods that require extensive manual curation of reasoning traces or careful specification of primitive skills. This automation can streamline the development cycle for high-performing reasoning LLMs.
- **Leveraging LLM Strengths:** The approach effectively exploits the "verification-generation gap" in LLMs, utilizing their strong verification capabilities to provide informative feedback. This paradigm could inspire other methods for LLM fine-tuning where verification is more tractable than direct generation.
- **Broader Applicability:** The demonstration of ExpRL's efficacy across mixed domains (math, science, coding) and with different model scales suggests its generalizability beyond specific tasks or model architectures.

Future directions highlighted by the authors, such as integrating natural-language feedback from judges and combining ExpRL with prefix-conditioned generation, suggest avenues for further enhancing the method's capabilities and exploring its theoretical limits. However, a notable limitation is the reliance on auxiliary information (reference solutions), which may not always be available or easy to obtain in all domains, particularly for open-ended or novel problems without established solutions. Despite this, ExpRL represents a step towards making LLM reasoning more robust and adaptable through principled, dense-reward-based mid-training.
