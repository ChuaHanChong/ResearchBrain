# Learning High-Speed Flight in the Wild

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Zurich and Intel developed an end-to-end learning framework that enables quadrotors to autonomously navigate complex, unknown environments at speeds up to 10 m/s using only onboard sensing and computation. The system, trained entirely in simulation, achieved zero-shot generalization to diverse real-world conditions like dense forests and disaster zones, reducing failure rates by up to 10 times compared to baseline approaches.

## Problem

- Autonomous quadrotors traditionally struggle to fully exploit their agility and fly at high speeds (comparable to human pilots) through complex, unknown environments due to system limitations.
- Traditional modular navigation pipelines (sensing, mapping, planning) introduce significant latency and suffer from compounding errors, limiting achievable speed and robustness in dynamic, cluttered settings.
- Previous end-to-end learning approaches for robotics face challenges with data requirements and the 'sim-to-real gap,' where policies trained in simulation fail to generalize effectively to physical systems.

## Method

- An end-to-end learning approach where a lightweight convolutional neural network directly maps noisy depth images and inertial measurements to short-term, collision-free trajectories.
- Training data is generated in simulation using a 'privileged expert' that has perfect environment knowledge and an unconstrained computational budget to sample optimal collision-free B-spline trajectories.
- Zero-shot sim-to-real transfer is enabled by training solely in simulation, utilizing abstract input representations (depth images), multi-modal trajectory prediction with a Relaxed Winner-Takes-All loss, and Dataset Aggregation (DAgger).

## Results

- The system achieved zero-shot generalization, autonomously navigating diverse real-world environments (e.g., dense forests, disaster zones) at average speeds up to 10 m/s using only onboard computation.
- In simulation, the approach reduced failure rates by up to 10 times compared to state-of-the-art baselines (FastPlanner, Reactive) at high speeds, demonstrating a **60%** success rate at 10 m/s where baselines consistently failed.
- The learned policy exhibited low processing latency of **41.6** ms on an onboard Jetson TX2, enabling a control update rate of approximately 24 Hz, crucial for real-time high-speed flight.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - End-to-end learning can effectively replace traditional modular sensing-mapping-planning pipelines for high-speed, agile autonomous navigation, significantly reducing latency and enhancing robustness.
> - Privileged learning, coupled with multi-modal output prediction via a Relaxed Winner-Takes-All (R-WTA) loss, is effective for training complex robotic policies in simulation, enabling zero-shot transfer to diverse real-world conditions.
> - Using abstract sensor representations like depth images, rather than raw RGB, is crucial for robust sim-to-real generalization as they are less sensitive to appearance variations and noise.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of autonomous quadrotor navigation has traditionally been dominated by modular, pipeline-based approaches. These methods typically decompose the complex problem of navigation into sequential subtasks: **sensing** (e.g., collecting camera or lidar data), **mapping** (building a representation of the environment), and **planning** (generating collision-free trajectories). While this "divide-and-conquer" strategy has enabled significant progress in autonomous flight, particularly at low speeds, it suffers from fundamental limitations when pushing the boundaries of speed and agility in cluttered, unknown environments.

The primary drawbacks of traditional modular pipelines include:
1. **Increased Latency:** Sequential execution of subtasks introduces significant processing delays between perception and action, making reactive high-speed maneuvers difficult or impossible.
2. **Compounding Errors:** Errors from one module (e.g., noisy sensor data leading to inaccurate maps) propagate and compound through the pipeline, reducing overall system robustness.
3. **Neglected Interactions:** The separation often neglects complex interdependencies between modules, limiting the system's ability to optimize for holistic performance.

In contrast, recent trends in robotics, particularly driven by advances in deep learning, have explored **end-to-end (E2E) learning** approaches. These methods aim to directly map raw sensor inputs to control commands, bypassing explicit intermediate representations like maps. Examples include imitation learning from human pilots or training directly in simulation. However, prior E2E approaches often struggled with generalization, high sample complexity (requiring vast amounts of real-world data or constrained motion models), or transferring from simulation to reality ("sim-to-real gap").

This paper sits at the cutting edge of this evolution, presenting a novel end-to-end learning framework that directly addresses the limitations of both traditional pipelines and earlier E2E methods. It aims to achieve **high-speed flight in complex, unstructured environments with purely onboard sensing and computation**, a capability previously limited to expert human pilots. By demonstrating **zero-shot transfer from simulation to diverse real-world conditions**, the authors make a crucial contribution to overcoming the sim-to-real gap, which has been a major bottleneck for data-driven robotics. The work challenges the long-standing assumption that explicit mapping is necessary for agile autonomous flight, proposing a more direct, robust, and low-latency alternative rooted in learned sensorimotor policies. This approach significantly advances the state-of-the-art in agile autonomous drone navigation, moving closer to the agility of biological counterparts.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable autonomous quadrotors to fly at **high speeds through complex, previously unknown natural and human-made environments, relying solely on onboard sensing and computation**. This goal is motivated by several critical limitations of existing autonomous flight systems:

1. **Exploiting Quadrotor Agility:** Quadrotors are inherently highly agile machines, capable of traversing intricate environments and reaching inaccessible locations. However, this agility has historically been fully exploited only by highly trained human pilots, who are a scarce and expensive resource. Current autonomous systems, whether traditional or learning-based, have been largely confined to low-speed operations, failing to unlock the full potential of these platforms.
2. **Addressing Limitations of Modular Pipelines:** Traditional autonomous navigation systems (sensing, mapping, planning) face fundamental challenges at high speeds. Their sequential nature introduces significant latency, making rapid reactive maneuvers difficult. Furthermore, errors from each module compound, reducing robustness, especially in challenging perception conditions (e.g., motion blur, sensor noise, varying illumination, low-texture environments). The authors argue that this "divide-and-conquer" principle imposes inherent limits on achievable speed and agility.
3. **Overcoming Sim-to-Real Gap for End-to-End Learning:** While end-to-end learning offers the promise of lower latency and greater robustness by directly mapping sensors to actions, its practical deployment has been hindered by two main issues: (a) the immense amount of data required for training generalizable policies, often necessitating simulated environments, and (b) the "sim-to-real gap," where policies trained in simulation fail to perform effectively in the real world due to discrepancies in physics and sensor models.
4. **Enabling New Applications:** Achieving robust, high-speed autonomous flight with onboard capabilities would revolutionize numerous applications, including search and rescue, logistics, security, infrastructure inspection, entertainment, and agriculture, by allowing drones to operate in challenging conditions without human intervention.

In summary, the authors' key objective is to **develop a robust, low-latency, and agile autonomous navigation system** that can perform complex maneuvers at speeds comparable to human pilots, thereby demonstrating the viability of end-to-end learning for challenging real-world robotic tasks and unlocking new application domains for quadrotors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this research lies in an **end-to-end learning approach** that directly maps noisy sensory observations to collision-free trajectories in a receding-horizon fashion. This direct mapping is crucial for drastically reducing processing latency and increasing robustness to perceptual uncertainties inherent in high-speed flight.

#### 3.1. Core Concept

The system learns a **sensorimotor policy** represented by a lightweight convolutional neural network. This network takes raw, noisy depth images and inertial measurements as input and directly outputs a set of short-term, dynamically feasible trajectories. The goal is to imitate an "expert" policy trained in simulation, leveraging "privileged learning" to overcome the sim-to-real gap. The approach also addresses the multi-modal nature of obstacle avoidance, where multiple equally valid avoidance paths might exist.

#### 3.2. Privileged Expert Design

To generate high-quality training data for the neural network, the authors design a **privileged expert**. This expert operates purely in simulation and has access to information unavailable to the real-world drone:
- **Perfect Knowledge:** Precise 3D structure of the environment (point cloud) and exact quadrotor state.
- **Unconstrained Computational Budget:** Allows for complex, computationally intensive planning.
The expert's task is to find collision-free trajectories that closely follow a high-level reference (which might be collision-prone).

- **Sampling-based Motion Planning:** The expert is a sampling-based planner that uses the **Metropolis-Hastings (M-H) algorithm** to sample from a probability distribution of collision-free trajectories. This distribution favors trajectories far from obstacles and close to the reference path. M-H is chosen because it can effectively explore complex, multi-modal distributions, representing the many ways to navigate around obstacles.
- **Trajectory Representation:** Trajectories are represented as **cubic B-splines** with three control points, offering a compact and computationally efficient representation that encourages dynamically feasible (smooth and differentiable) paths for the quadrotor.
- **Global Planning Bias:** To increase the expert's foresight and generate more robust trajectories, the sampling process is biased by an off-the-shelf global collision-free trajectory planner (Liu et al.). This helps the expert consider long-term consequences, even for short-horizon trajectory generation.
- **Data Generation:** The expert generates a multitude of collision-free trajectories, and the three lowest-cost trajectories are selected as labels for training the student policy.

#### 3.3. Sensorimotor Student Policy

The student policy is the neural network designed for real-time onboard execution:
- **Inputs:** Noisy depth images (estimated using Semi-Global Matching, SGM, from a stereo pair, mirroring real-world sensor characteristics), the quadrotor's current velocity and attitude, and a desired flight direction (encoded as a vector towards a future reference point). This input representation is chosen for its abstract nature, which minimizes the sim-to-real domain shift.
- **Network Architecture:** A lightweight architecture primarily using a pre-trained **MobileNet-V3** backbone for efficient feature extraction from depth images. Subsequent 1D convolutions and multi-layer perceptrons process visual, inertial, and reference information to produce multiple trajectory hypotheses.
- **Outputs:** The network predicts **M=3 short-term trajectories** (1-second horizon, represented as 10 discrete position points) and a corresponding collision cost for each.
- **Receding Horizon Control:** At test time, the predicted trajectories are projected onto the space of order-5 polynomials to ensure dynamic feasibility and continuity with the quadrotor's current state. The trajectory with the lowest predicted collision cost is selected for execution by a model predictive controller (MPC). If multiple trajectories have similar low costs, the one with the lowest actuation cost (smoothest/most energy-efficient) is chosen.

#### 3.4. Training and Sim-to-Real Transfer

- **Simulation-Only Training:** All data collection and training occur exclusively within the **Flightmare simulator** (using RotorS Gazebo plugin for physics and Unity for rendering). This enables generating unlimited samples without risking the physical platform.
- **Zero-Shot Transfer:** A key aspect is achieving **zero-shot generalization** from simulation to diverse real-world environments. This is enabled by:
 - **Abstract Input Representation (Depth):** Training on depth maps (generated via SGM in simulation) rather than raw RGB images. Depth information is less sensitive to lighting, texture, and appearance variations between simulated and real environments (Figure 5A). The simulation explicitly models common depth sensor artifacts like noise and missing values.
 - **Privileged Learning:** The expert provides robust, collision-free demonstrations even when the student's perception is imperfect.
 - **Multi-Modal Learning (R-WTA Loss):** The use of a **Relaxed Winner-Takes-All (R-WTA) loss** function allows the network to learn multiple plausible trajectories, preventing mode collapse (where the network averages different optimal paths, potentially leading to a collision).
- **Dataset Aggregation (DAgger):** The DAgger strategy is used during training, where the student policy is rolled out, and visited states are re-labeled by the expert. This iteratively improves the student's performance and prevents compounding errors from its own mistakes.
- **Training Environments:** Training environments consist of randomly generated obstacle courses (trees or convex shapes) with varying densities, ensuring exposure to diverse geometries and clutter.

#### 3.5. Experimental Platform

For real-world validation, a custom-built lightweight quadrotor is used, equipped with:
- **Compute:** NVIDIA Jetson TX2 (onboard GPU for NN inference, CPU for control stack).
- **Sensing:** Intel RealSense T265 for state estimation (VIO) and Intel RealSense D435i for hardware-accelerated depth estimation (stereo-based). These consumer-grade sensors are chosen to match the simulated sensor model and challenge the system with real-world noise.
- **Control:** BetaFlight flight controller for low-level control, receiving commands (thrust, angular rates) from the onboard MPC.

This integrated approach, combining a sophisticated privileged expert, a lightweight multi-modal neural network, and careful attention to sim-to-real transfer through abstract representations and DAgger, enables high-speed, robust, and autonomous flight.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling evidence that the proposed end-to-end learning approach significantly advances high-speed autonomous quadrotor flight in complex environments.

#### 4.1. Real-World Performance

- **Diverse Environments:** The policy, trained solely in simulation, achieved zero-shot generalization to a wide range of never-before-seen real-world environments, including dense forests, snowy mountain trails, airplane hangars, and complex disaster zones (e.g., collapsed buildings, derailed trains).
- **High Speed and Agility:** The quadrotor demonstrated successful navigation at impressive average speeds, ranging from 3 m/s up to 10 m/s.
 - In natural environments, success rates were 100% at 3 and 5 m/s, 80% at 7 m/s (2 failures out of 10), and 60% at 10 m/s (due to high angular velocity, external disturbances, and narrow openings).
 - In human-made environments, success rates were consistently 100% up to 7 m/s.
- **Outperforming Commercial Drones:** In a direct comparison, the proposed approach successfully navigated a narrow gap (0.8 m wide) at 3-5 m/s, whereas a commercial drone (Skydio R1), flying slower (2.7 m/s), consistently failed, often stopping or crashing. This highlights the superior latency and robustness of the learned policy.
- **Robustness to Perceptual Challenges:** The system exhibited remarkable robustness to real-world sensor artifacts like motion blur, missing data, and noise, even under challenging illumination and low-texture conditions (e.g., snow).

#### 4.2. Comparative Simulation Performance

Controlled simulation experiments demonstrated the superior performance of the proposed method against state-of-the-art baselines:
- **Baselines:** Compared against FastPlanner (a mapping and planning method) and a Reactive planner.
- **Failure Rate Reduction:** The proposed approach reduced the failure rate by up to **10 times** compared to baselines at high speeds.
- **Speed Scalability:** While all methods performed similarly at low speeds (3 m/s), the baselines' performance rapidly degraded with increasing speed. At 10 m/s in a dense forest, FastPlanner and Reactive baselines consistently failed to complete even a single run, whereas the proposed method achieved a 60% success rate. Similar trends were observed in narrow gap, disaster, and city street environments, where "Ours" maintained high success rates (e.g., 100% at 7 m/s in disaster zone, 80% at 7 m/s in narrow gap).
- **Reasons for Baseline Failure:** Baselines suffered from high processing latency, limited expressive power of motion primitives (Reactive), or reliance on mapping that became too slow and fragile with noisy high-speed observations (FastPlanner).

#### 4.3. Computational Efficiency

- **Lowest Latency:** The end-to-end nature of the learned policy resulted in significantly lower processing latency compared to traditional approaches.
 - On a desktop CPU/GPU, the proposed method achieved a total latency of **10.3 ms (2.6 ms with GPU inference)**, making it **25.3 times faster than FastPlanner (65.2 ms)** and **7.4 times faster than the Reactive baseline (19.1 ms)**.
 - Crucially, on the **onboard Jetson TX2 embedded computer**, the total latency was **41.6 ms**, enabling an update rate of approximately **24 Hz**, which is sufficient for high-speed flight. This low latency is a direct consequence of bypassing explicit mapping and planning stages.

#### 4.4. Robustness and Ablation Studies

- **Effect of Sensor Noise and Latency:** In controlled pole-avoidance experiments, the proposed method flew closer to the theoretical maximum speed limit and was only minimally affected by noisy depth estimates from stereo matching (10% performance drop at 10 m/s), unlike baselines that experienced significant performance degradation. This indicates that training on realistic sensor noise models (simulated SGM depth) builds robustness.
- **Ablation Studies:** Validated the importance of key design choices (Figure 7a):
 - **Depth as Input:** Training on depth images was critical; performance dropped significantly when using RGB images, confirming depth as a superior abstract representation for sim-to-real transfer.
 - **Multi-Modal Prediction:** Multi-modal trajectory prediction was crucial; a single-output network (UniModal) led to performance degradation because the averaging of diverse valid paths often resulted in collisions.
 - **Global Planning Initialization:** Biasing the expert's sampling with a global plan (LocalPlan) significantly improved high-speed success rates by encouraging more conservative and foresightful avoidance maneuvers.
 - **Full Trajectory Output:** Predicting a full 1-second trajectory (instead of a single waypoint) was essential for high-speed performance due to its richer representational power for agile maneuvers.

Overall, the findings conclusively demonstrate that the proposed end-to-end learning paradigm, with its focus on privileged learning, multi-modal outputs, and abstract input representations, enables unprecedented levels of speed, agility, and robustness for autonomous quadrotor flight in complex, unstructured environments, surpassing traditional methods and pushing the boundaries of sim-to-real transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the field of autonomous robotics, particularly for agile aerial platforms. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Breaking Traditional Paradigms:** The work fundamentally challenges the long-standing modular "sensing-mapping-planning" pipeline in robotics. By demonstrating that a single, end-to-end neural network can effectively replace these complex stages for high-speed navigation, it opens new avenues for robot control design, favoring learned, data-driven approaches over intricate hand-engineered systems. This paradigm shift can simplify development, reduce integration complexity, and enhance overall system performance.

2. **Unprecedented Speed and Agility:** The ability to autonomously fly quadrotors at speeds up to 10 m/s in dense, unstructured environments with purely onboard computation is a major breakthrough. This level of agility was previously only achievable by highly skilled human pilots. It effectively unlocks the full dynamic capabilities of quadrotors for autonomous operation.

3. **Robustness to Real-World Challenges:** The demonstrated robustness to noisy sensor data, motion blur, and varying environmental conditions (e.g., lighting, texture-less surfaces) is crucial for real-world deployment. The method's ability to operate in diverse, previously unseen natural and human-made environments without any adaptation (zero-shot transfer) validates its generalizability, a critical hurdle for learned robotic policies.

4. **Advancing Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The successful zero-shot transfer from simulation to reality, achieved primarily through the use of an abstract input representation (depth images) and privileged learning, is a key contribution. This methodology provides a robust framework for training complex robotic behaviors in safe, scalable simulated environments and directly deploying them in the physical world, significantly reducing the need for laborious and risky real-world data collection and fine-tuning.

5. **Enabling New Applications:** The capabilities demonstrated in this paper have the potential to revolutionize numerous applications that require agile autonomous drones:
 - **Search and Rescue:** Rapidly exploring collapsed buildings or dense forests to locate survivors.
 - **Inspection:** High-speed inspection of infrastructure (e.g., bridges, power lines, industrial facilities) in challenging conditions.
 - **Logistics and Delivery:** Navigating complex urban or natural terrains for efficient package delivery.
 - **Exploration:** Autonomous exploration of unknown or hazardous environments.
 - **Cinematography:** Capturing dynamic, high-speed shots that mimic human expert piloting.

6. **Future Research Directions:** The authors identify clear pathways for future work, including:
 - **Pushing Speed Limits Further:** Addressing challenges at even higher speeds (beyond 10 m/s), which currently suffer from extreme sparsity of feasible trajectories, dynamic mismatches between simulation and reality, and aerodynamic effects. This may require model-free methods or higher-fidelity simulation.
 - **Improving Sensor Technology:** Investigating the use of faster sensors like event cameras for even lower perception latency and improved performance in dynamic environments.
 - **Integrating Long-Term Planning:** Combining the short-horizon, reactive controller with a higher-level, long-term planner to enable more complex missions and global path optimization.

In essence, "Learning High-Speed Flight in the Wild" not only provides a powerful, practical solution for agile autonomous drone navigation but also serves as a strong testament to the transformative potential of deep learning in robotics, particularly when combined with thoughtful simulation design and robust transfer strategies. It opens the door for a new generation of autonomous systems capable of operating safely and efficiently in highly dynamic and unstructured real-world environments.
