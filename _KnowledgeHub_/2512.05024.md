# Model-Free Assessment of Simulator Fidelity via Quantile Curves

> [!summary] Summary
> Columbia University researchers developed a model-free framework to profile simulator fidelity by constructing calibrated quantile curves of the sim-to-real discrepancy, offering a comprehensive risk assessment. The methodology, demonstrated on large language models, revealed GPT-4o consistently aligned best with human populations on the WorldValueBench dataset.

## Problem

- The 'sim-to-real' gap is a scenario-dependent phenomenon, meaning a single average error metric is insufficient to capture the variability of simulator performance.
- Assessing simulator fidelity requires characterizing the full distribution of the scenario-dependent discrepancy, moving beyond scalar summaries or fixed-level bounds.
- Quantifying uncertainty in latent population parameters from finite batches of samples, often of heterogeneous sizes, is a significant challenge for existing uncertainty quantification methods.

## Method

- A two-step ==model-free framework== constructs a ==calibrated quantile curve== that profiles the discrepancy between real and simulated output distributions across diverse scenarios.
- For each scenario, a ==pseudo-discrepancy== is calculated as the supremum of a chosen discrepancy function over a statistically rigorous ==confidence set== for the unobservable true parameter.
- Theoretical guarantees ensure the calibrated quantile curve provides a valid ==upper bound== for the true discrepancy's quantile with high probability, adjusting for finite scenarios and average coverage.

## Results

- Application to LLMs on the WorldValueBench dataset showed that all LLM-simulators generally outperformed a random benchmark but struggled in tail scenarios, with GPT-4o exhibiting the lowest discrepancy.
- The method's tightness improved significantly with larger human sample sizes, and adaptive coverage was crucial for reducing conservativeness compared to fixed coverage rates.
- Confidence bands derived from the calibrated curves allowed for principled, data-driven comparisons between simulators, confirming GPT-4o's systematic better alignment with human data compared to Llama 3.3.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The 'sim-to-real' gap is best understood as a random variable, requiring a distribution-level risk profile (quantile function) for comprehensive assessment.
> - Robust assessment of latent distributional parameters from finite, heterogeneous samples can be achieved by constructing per-scenario confidence sets and a conservative pseudo-discrepancy.
> - Adaptive confidence set coverage, which increases with sample size, significantly tightens the fidelity profiles in data-rich scenarios while maintaining statistical validity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The proliferation of generative Artificial Intelligence (AI) models for simulating real-world systems across diverse domains, including operations, manufacturing, social science, user surveys, and robotics, necessitates robust methods for quantifying the discrepancy between simulated and real-world outcomes. This "sim-to-real" gap is a critical challenge, as simulator performance is not static but varies significantly with the input scenario (e.g., operating conditions, user prompts). Consequently, assessing simulator fidelity requires characterizing the distribution of this scenario-dependent discrepancy, rather than relying on single average error metrics.

Existing uncertainty quantification (UQ) literature traditionally distinguishes between input uncertainty, which focuses on how errors in input distribution characterization propagate to output errors, and output uncertainty, which directly assesses the sim-to-real discrepancy of the output, treating the simulator as a black box. Much of the classical UQ work is asymptotic and primarily concerned with Monte Carlo noise. Recent advancements in AI, particularly with large language models (LLMs), have driven the development of complex models that are often treated as black boxes, shifting the focus towards output UQ.

While some contemporary approaches, especially in the LLM literature, have begun to address output uncertainty using model-agnostic discrepancy measures (e.g., aggregating errors into scalar summaries or bounding discrepancies at fixed quantile levels), they often provide only partial views of the risk profile. These methods typically focus on individual outcome validity or scalar summaries, rather than comprehensive, distribution-level characterizations of simulator alignment across a heterogeneous population of scenarios. Furthermore, many existing methods assume homogeneous data, overlooking the common practical challenge of finite batches of samples, often of heterogeneous sizes, from both real and simulated systems. Standard predictive inference methods, such as conformal prediction, are often designed for observable outcomes rather than latent population parameters, limiting their direct applicability to quantifying underlying distributional discrepancies. This work aims to address these limitations by providing a model-agnostic framework that rigorously accounts for sampling error and heterogeneous batching to characterize the full distribution of the sim-to-real gap.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to characterize the quantile function of the discrepancy between real and simulated output distributions across a population of diverse scenarios. This characterization provides a comprehensive "risk profile" of a simulator, illustrating not just average performance but also the frequency and severity of misalignments, including rare but significant failures.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges and limitations in current simulator assessment practices:

- **Scenario-Dependent Discrepancy:** The sim-to-real gap is not a fixed value but a random variable, driven by the stochastic nature of real-world scenarios. A single average error metric is insufficient to capture this variability and does not provide insight into how well a simulator performs across its operational spectrum.
- **Latent Distributional Parameters:** For any given scenario, the ground-truth and simulated output distributions are not directly observable. Instead, they are inferred from finite batches of samples, which often vary in size across different scenarios (heterogeneous batching). This makes standard predictive inference methods, which typically quantify uncertainty in observable outputs, inapplicable for assessing latent population parameters.
- **Need for Robust Proxy:** To address the unobservability of true distributional parameters, a robust proxy for the sim-to-real discrepancy is required. This proxy must account for the sampling error inherent in finite observations.
- **Model Agnosticism and Generality:** Many modern simulators, especially those built using advanced AI models like LLMs or deep neural networks, operate as "black boxes." A practical assessment framework should not require structural assumptions about the simulator, making it applicable across a wide range of model types and general output spaces (e.g., categorical survey responses, multi-dimensional continuous sensor data).
- **Statistical Inference and Comparison:** A robust assessment framework should support downstream statistical inference tasks, such as forming confidence sets for real output distributions in new scenarios, calculating risk measures like Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR), and enabling principled, data-driven comparisons between different simulators.
- **Addressing Over-reliance on Scalar Summaries:** Existing literature often relies on scalar summaries or fixed-level bounds of discrepancy. This work aims to move beyond these limited views to provide a calibrated, distribution-level risk profile, offering a more complete understanding of simulator fidelity.

By developing a model-free method with finite-sample, distributional guarantees under heterogeneous batching, the authors aim to provide a practical tool for evaluating and comparing simulators, thereby supporting informed decision-making in the deployment of AI-enabled digital twins and generative agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology proposed in this paper constructs a calibrated quantile curve to profile simulator fidelity. This approach addresses the challenges of latent distributional parameters and heterogeneous sample sizes through a two-step procedure:

**1. Per-Scenario Pseudo-Discrepancy Calculation:**
For each of `m` observed scenarios (`ψ_j`), the true (latent) population parameter `p_j` (e.g., mean response) is estimated by `ˆp_j` from `n_j` real-world samples. Similarly, the simulator's parameter `q_j` is estimated by `ˆq_j` from `k` simulated samples. The process involves:
- **Confidence Set Construction:** For each scenario `j`, a `γ_j`-confidence set `C_j(ˆp_j, γ_j)` is constructed for the true parameter `p_j`. This set quantifies the uncertainty of the estimate `ˆp_j`, ensuring that `p_j` lies within `C_j` with at least probability `γ_j`. The coverage level `γ_j` can be chosen adaptively as an increasing function of the human sample size `n_j`, which allows for tighter curves in data-rich scenarios while maintaining reliability when data is scarce. Examples of confidence sets provided include:
 - **Multinomial Outcomes:** For categorical outputs with parameter `p_j` in a `d`-simplex, a confidence set is derived using the KL divergence, based on a variant of the Chernoff-Hoeffding Bound.
 - **Bounded Outcomes:** For outcomes `Y_j,i` within a bounded interval `[a, b]`, a confidence set for the mean `p_j` is constructed around the sample mean `ˆp_j` using a Hoeffding-type inequality.
 - **Nonparametric Wasserstein-1:** For true outcome distributions `P_j`, a confidence set `C_{W1,j}` is formed by distributions `Q` whose Wasserstein-1 distance from the empirical distribution `bP_j` is within a calculated radius `r_j`, justified by a concentration inequality.
- **Pseudo-Discrepancy Definition:** A pseudo-discrepancy, `ˆ∆_j`, is then defined as the supremum of a user-chosen discrepancy function `L(u, ˆq_j)` over all parameters `u` within the confidence set `C_j(ˆp_j, γ_j)`. The function `L` quantifies the difference between `u` (a potential true parameter) and the simulated estimate `ˆq_j`. This construction ensures that `ˆ∆_j` is a conservative estimate of the actual discrepancy `∆_j = L(p_j, ˆq_j)`. The paper notes that `ˆ∆_j` can be efficiently computed for common discrepancy functions like KL-divergence and Wasserstein-1 distance.

**2. Quantile Curve Estimation:**
After computing `ˆ∆_j` for all `m` scenarios, the empirical `α`-quantile of the collection `{ˆ∆_j}^m_{j=1}` is computed and denoted as `ˆV_m(α)`. This `ˆV_m(α)` serves as the calibrated quantile curve.

**Theoretical Guarantees (Theorem 3.1):**
The method is supported by theoretical guarantees derived under two assumptions:
- **Independent data (Assumption 3.1):** Scenarios are i.i.d., real and simulated data are conditionally independent given the scenario, and human sample sizes are fixed design quantities.
- **Regular Discrepancy (Assumption 3.2):** The discrepancy function `L` is jointly continuous and `L(u, u) = 0`.
Theorem 3.1 demonstrates that `ˆV_m(1 - α_eff(α))` provides a valid upper bound for the `(1 - α)`-quantile of the true discrepancy `∆_ψ` with high probability. The "effective index" `α_eff(α)` incorporates adjustments for finite `m` scenarios and the average coverage `¯γ_m = (1/m) Σ γ_j`. This index inflation makes the procedure inherently conservative, ensuring coverage despite the approximations. The dependence on `¯γ_m` motivates the adaptive choice of `γ_j`, where `γ_j` increases with `n_j` to reduce conservativeness in data-rich scenarios.

**Benefits of Distribution-Level Characterization:**
The calibrated quantile curve `ˆV_m(τ)` offers several benefits:
- **Confidence Sets for Real Parameters:** For an unseen scenario, the curve can be used to derive a confidence set for the real parameter `p_ψ` given a simulated `ˆq_ψ`.
- **Summary Statistics:** The curve can be summarized into scalar metrics, such as:
 - **Calibrated AUC:** `∫^1_0 ˆV_m(τ) dτ` for overall average simulator bias.
 - **Calibrated CVaR_α:** `(1/α) ∫^1_{1-α} ˆV_m(u) du` for quantifying tail risk, reflecting average discrepancy in the worst `α` fraction of scenarios.
- **Principled Comparisons:** The method allows for comparison across simulators by plotting and analyzing their respective calibrated quantile curves.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research demonstrates the utility of its methodology through an application evaluating the fidelity of four large language models (LLMs) against human populations on the WorldValueBench dataset, along with additional applications on the EEDI and OpinionQA datasets provided in the appendix.

**Application on WorldValueBench:**
- **Dataset and Preprocessing:** The WorldValueBench dataset, derived from the World Values Survey, contains 235 distinct questions and responses from 96,220 individuals across 64 countries. To standardize responses, categorical answers are mapped to numerical values in `[-1, 1]`, with direction determined by GPT-5's assessment of "idealness." Individual-level covariates (e.g., country, age, gender) are used to construct synthetic profiles for LLM prompting.
- **Methodology Application:** Four LLMs (GPT-4o, GPT-5 mini, Llama 3.3 70B, Qwen 3 235B) and a uniform random baseline are evaluated. For each question, 200 LLM samples (`k=200`) are generated, and 500 human respondents are subsampled (`n_j ≈ 450-500`) to construct empirical estimates `ˆp_j` and `ˆq_j`. Confidence sets `C_j` are built using Example 3.2 (bounded outcomes) with an adaptive coverage rate `γ_j = 1 - n_j^(-1/3)`. The discrepancy function used is squared error, `L(p, q) = (p - q)^2`.
- **Fidelity Profiling Results:**
 - The calibrated quantile functions (Figure 3) show that all LLM-simulators generally outperform the uniform benchmark on over 80% of questions but struggle with tail scenarios.
 - GPT-4o consistently exhibits the lowest discrepancy across all quantiles, indicating the most reliable alignment with human populations. GPT-5 mini is close but shows some failures in outlier questions. Llama 3.3 and Qwen-3 trail behind.
 - A robustness check (Figure 4) varying human sample sizes (`n_j` from 50 to 10000) confirms that the dominance relationships between LLMs remain consistent.

**Tightness Analysis of Calibrated Quantiles:**
- **Oracle Comparison:** Leveraging the large sample size of WorldValueBench, an "oracle" discrepancy `∆*j = L(p_j, ˆq_j)` (where `p_j` is estimated from all available human data) and its corresponding quantile curve are computed. This allows for empirical assessment of the tightness of the calibrated curves.
- **Impact of `n_j` and Adaptive `γ_j`:**
 - When `n_j` is small (e.g., 50), the calibrated envelope is substantially conservative compared to the oracle curve (Figure 5). As `n_j` increases (≥ 500), the calibrated curves become significantly tighter, aligning with the methodology's design that larger `n_j` lead to more concentrated `ˆp_j` estimates and smaller `C_j` sets.
 - The importance of adaptive `γ_j` is highlighted by comparing results with a fixed `γ_j = 1/2` (Figure 6). Fixed `γ_j` leads to a persistent vertical separation and non-vanishing conservativeness at lower quantiles, even with large `n_j`, due to the fixed index inflation `(0.5τ + 0.5)`. This underscores the benefit of allowing scenario-dependent coverage levels.
- **Confidence Bands for Comparison:** Theorem B.1 (confidence band) is introduced, which provides both lower and upper envelopes for the true (unknown) quantile function `V(τ)`. This allows for data-driven comparisons of simulators without requiring access to oracle ground-truth discrepancies. Figure 7 illustrates confidence bands for GPT-4o and Llama 3.3, showing that the GPT-4o band lies systematically below Llama 3.3, confirming GPT-4o's better alignment and fewer extreme failures.

**Additional Applications (Appendix E):**
- **EEDI Dataset:** Evaluation on the EEDI dataset (student responses to math questions, binarized correct/incorrect) using absolute error `L(p,q)=|p-q|` reveals that DeepSeek-V3 performs best. Notably, several LLMs do not outperform a random baseline, suggesting they may be ill-suited for simulation in this context with the chosen discrepancy.
- **OpinionQA Dataset:** Evaluation on OpinionQA (US population survey responses, multinomial) using total variation distance `L(p,q)=(1/2)||p-q||_1` shows GPT-4o achieving the most reliable alignment. Simulator curves are steeper than the random benchmark, indicating question-dependent alignment, but generally lower than the random baseline.

Overall, the findings validate the proposed methodology as a practical and robust framework for comprehensive simulator fidelity assessment, particularly for black-box AI models like LLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a robust, model-agnostic framework for assessing simulator fidelity, offering several significant contributions and potential impacts:

- **Comprehensive Risk Profiling:** By characterizing the entire quantile function of the sim-to-real discrepancy, the method moves beyond simple average error metrics. This provides a detailed "risk profile" that captures not only typical performance but also the likelihood and magnitude of rare but critical failures, enabling a more nuanced understanding of simulator reliability. This is particularly impactful in high-stakes domains where tail risks are crucial.
- **Rigorous Uncertainty Quantification for Latent Parameters:** The method directly addresses the fundamental challenge of quantifying uncertainty in latent, scenario-specific distributional parameters when only finite and heterogeneously sized sample batches are available. This is a common and difficult problem in real-world applications of simulation, and the provision of finite-sample, distributional guarantees offers a substantial advancement over asymptotic or less robust methods.
- **Model Agnosticism and Broad Applicability:** The framework operates independently of the internal structure or specific algorithms of the simulator or the real system. This black-box approach makes it widely applicable to diverse AI models, including complex LLMs and deep neural networks, and to various output spaces (categorical, continuous, multi-dimensional). This broadens the scope of systems that can be rigorously evaluated.
- **Adaptive and Interpretable Assessment:** The adaptive choice of coverage levels (`γ_j`) based on sample size (`n_j`) allows the method to generate tighter fidelity profiles in data-rich scenarios while preserving reliability when data is scarce. This adaptability ensures efficiency without compromising statistical validity, leading to more interpretable and actionable insights.
- **Support for Downstream Statistical Inference:** The calibrated quantile curve supports various downstream analyses, including the construction of confidence sets for real-world parameters in new scenarios and the calculation of risk measures like Conditional Value-at-Risk (CVaR). This moves beyond mere evaluation to enabling informed decision-making and risk management based on simulator performance.
- **Principled Simulator Comparison:** The framework facilitates objective and data-driven comparisons across different simulators, even when oracle ground truth is limited. The introduction of confidence bands further enhances this capability, allowing researchers and practitioners to robustly determine which simulator aligns better with real-world phenomena across different risk levels.
- **Practical Utility in AI Alignment:** The demonstration on LLMs using the WorldValueBench, EEDI, and OpinionQA datasets highlights the immediate practical utility in evaluating the alignment of generative AI models with human populations. As LLMs are increasingly used as "generative agents" or "digital twins" of human behavior, this methodology provides a critical tool for assessing their fidelity and identifying biases or systematic misalignments.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies several natural extensions, such as tightening concentration bounds, expanding to dynamic simulation processes, and addressing distribution shifts (e.g., covariate shift), which suggest fertile ground for future research to further enhance the method's robustness and applicability.

In essence, this research provides a foundational methodological tool for ensuring the trustworthiness and utility of AI-enabled simulators, particularly crucial as these technologies become more integrated into critical decision-making processes across various industries and scientific domains.
