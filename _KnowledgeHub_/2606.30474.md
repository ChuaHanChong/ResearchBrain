# Grasp-Oriented Non-Prehensile Manipulation via Learning a Graspability Field

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the National University of Singapore developed GOMP, a robotic policy that uses a learned graspability field to perform non-prehensile manipulation, preparing objects for stable grasping without requiring predefined target poses. This system achieved a 75.5% manipulation success rate in simulation and a 62% total success rate on a real robot, with 97% grasp success once manipulation was complete.

## Problem

- Current non-prehensile manipulation methods for grasping require predefined target object poses, which are often unknown or difficult to determine in unstructured environments.
- Optimizing for geometric similarity to a target pose does not always correlate with improved grasp success or object stability.
- Existing pipelines separate manipulation and grasping, requiring external planners or manual stopping conditions, leading to inefficient and disjointed control.

## Method

- The **Grasp-Oriented Non-Prehensile Manipulation Policy (GOMP)** reformulates the problem from ==pose-reaching to graspability optimization==, guiding the robot to any configuration suitable for stable grasping.
- A novel ==scalar graspability field== is introduced, measuring an object's proximity to a ==graspable set== (multiple good grasp configurations), providing a dense learning signal without explicit target poses.
- GOMP utilizes a ==closed-loop, single policy== to both learn manipulation actions and autonomously determine when to cease manipulation and execute a terminal grasp, based on the predicted ==graspability distance==.

## Results

- In simulation, the GOMP student policy achieved **75.5%** manipulation success on seen objects and **68.1%** on unseen objects, significantly outperforming a retrained pose-conditioned baseline (**36.1%**).
- On a real robot, GOMP demonstrated a **62%** total success rate across diverse objects in challenging initial configurations, with **97%** of grasps succeeding after manipulation successfully prepared the object.
- Ablation studies showed that using soft aggregation for graspability distance (**84.0%** vs. **81.1%**) and temporal anchoring (**84.0%** vs. **78.1%**) improved manipulation success rates in simulation, confirming their design benefits.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Guiding non-prehensile manipulation toward an abstract 'graspable set' via a continuously defined field is more effective and robust than targeting a single, explicit object pose.
> - A smooth, differentiable graspability field, enhanced with temporal anchoring, provides a stable and consistent learning signal, preventing oscillations and improving manipulation trajectories.
> - The learned graspability distance can serve as an effective, scale-invariant criterion for autonomously transitioning from manipulation to grasping, unifying the pipeline.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic manipulation in unstructured environments frequently involves initial object configurations that are not immediately suitable for stable grasping. Non-prehensile actions, such as pushing, sliding, or reorienting, serve as a preparatory stage to transform objects into graspable states. This work positions itself within the field of learning-based non-prehensile manipulation, specifically for the purpose of enhancing subsequent robotic grasping.

Existing learning-based approaches for non-prehensile manipulation, such as CORN and DyWA, have demonstrated proficiency in repositioning objects. However, these methods typically formulate manipulation as a goal-conditioned control problem, requiring a predefined target object pose. This approach assumes that grasp preparation can be reduced to reaching a single, predetermined configuration. In practical scenarios, objects often admit multiple configurations from which a stable grasp is possible, and the optimal target pose may not be known in advance or easily derivable. A pose-reaching objective primarily optimizes geometric similarity to a target, which may not directly correlate with improved grasp success or object stability, potentially leading to suboptimal manipulation trajectories or oscillations.

Simultaneously, advancements in learning-based robotic grasping have focused on predicting grasp actions directly from sensory observations, often assuming the object is already in a graspable configuration. While some research explores interacting with the environment to make specific grasp poses reachable (e.g., extrinsic dexterity), these methods still aim to execute a *given* grasp by modifying its reachability. Similarly, affordance-guided manipulation approaches, which connect manipulation and grasping through grasp-related predictions, often require a grasp to be selected or evaluated during planning, aiming to realize a particular grasp candidate or maximize predicted grasp success.

This paper addresses a perceived mismatch between these established paradigms: the goal of preparing an object for grasping is viewed not as a pose regulation task but as a state improvement process. The authors argue that manipulation should be guided by the object's inherent graspability rather than its proximity to an explicit target pose or the enablement of a specific pre-selected grasp. This work diverges by proposing a framework that avoids the need for predefined target poses, external grasp planners during manipulation, or the explicit selection and evaluation of grasp candidates. Instead, it introduces a unified objective to continuously improve the object's configuration for grasping, thereby integrating manipulation and grasping into a single, cohesive policy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to reformulate non-prehensile manipulation for robotic grasping from a pose-reaching task to a graspability optimization task. The core motivation stems from several limitations observed in existing methodologies:

1. **Reliance on Predefined Target Poses**: Current non-prehensile manipulation methods commonly require a specific target object pose. This poses a significant challenge in real-world scenarios where the ideal graspable pose is often unknown or difficult to determine beforehand. Objects can frequently be grasped from multiple stable configurations, making a single target pose an overly restrictive and potentially suboptimal objective.

2. **Mismatch between Geometric Similarity and Grasp Feasibility**: Formulating manipulation as reaching a target pose primarily optimizes geometric similarity. However, a configuration geometrically close to a target pose might not be dynamically stable or genuinely graspable, while other configurations with different geometric properties could be highly graspable. This divergence can lead to policies that successfully match a target pose but fail to improve grasp success meaningfully or even move the object away from more easily graspable states if the target lies in a distant "basin" of graspability. The authors contend that the objective should be to guide the object toward a *region* of high graspability, not a specific point.

3. **Lack of Unified Decision-Making**: Existing pipelines often separate manipulation and grasping, requiring external planners or manually specified stopping conditions to transition from preparation to execution. This separation can lead to inefficiencies, inconsistencies, and a lack of a cohesive decision-making process driven by a single policy.

To address these limitations, the authors propose "GOMP" (Grasp-Oriented non-prehensile Manipulation Policy) with the following specific objectives:

- **Develop a graspability-centric formulation**: The objective is to enable the robot to transform an object into *any* configuration that permits reliable grasps, rather than enforcing movement towards a specific predefined pose. This involves defining a "graspable set" of desirable object states.
- **Introduce a novel graspability field representation**: This scalar field quantifies how suitable an object's current configuration is for successful grasp execution. Its purpose is to provide a dense, continuous learning signal for reinforcement learning, guiding the manipulation policy without requiring explicit target poses.
- **Enable closed-loop manipulation-to-grasp transitions**: The graspability field is designed to serve a dual purpose: not only to guide manipulation learning but also to provide an autonomous termination condition, allowing the robot to decide when the object is sufficiently prepared for grasping and to transition to grasp execution seamlessly, all within a single policy.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to enhance the robustness, flexibility, and autonomy of robotic systems performing manipulation for grasping in diverse and unstructured environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Grasp-Oriented non-prehensile Manipulation Policy (GOMP), reformulates manipulation for grasping as an optimization problem where the policy maximizes an object-centric graspability objective. The framework is built around a teacher-student architecture operating in a closed-loop manner.

**Problem Formulation:**
The goal is to find a policy $\pi(a_t | o_t)$ that produces non-prehensile actions $a_t$ to transform an object from an initial state $s_0^o$ into a configuration $s_T^o$ that maximizes a scalar graspability measure $G(s_T^o)$. This measure quantifies how close the object's state is to a predefined "graspable set" $S_g$, which contains states where stable grasps are possible. The policy operates on partial sensory observations $o_t$ (e.g., point clouds) without direct knowledge of the 6D object pose $s_t^o$.

**Architecture:**
GOMP utilizes a perception-control architecture inspired by CORN.
- **Encoder**: An object-centric encoder processes raw observations (point clouds and robot states) into object and robot tokens. For this work, the final attention layer of the CORN encoder is fine-tuned, while earlier layers are frozen to preserve geometric feature extraction capabilities.
- **Cross-Attention**: These tokens are then processed via cross-attention, conditioned on the robot's state, to form a compact representation of the current object configuration.
- **Recurrent Module (GRU)**: To address partial observability from single frames, the student policy incorporates a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU). This module aggregates temporal information, allowing the policy to infer object motion and improve robustness to occlusion and contact dynamics.
- **Prediction Heads**: The resulting feature representation is fed into prediction heads that output the manipulation action, a predicted graspability distance, and a terminal grasp pose.
- **Teacher-Student Framework**: A teacher policy, with a similar perception backbone but without recurrent memory, is used during training to generate stable manipulation behaviors. This teacher policy uses full object observations, while the student policy (deployed in real-world scenarios) operates on partial observations.

#### 3.1. Graspable Reference Pose Generation

The graspable set $S_g$ is not directly known for arbitrary objects. To approximate it, an off-the-shelf grasp synthesis model (e.g., SE(3)-DiffusionFields) is used to predict multiple feasible grasps from the object's full geometry (available only during training). Each gripper-object relative grasp pose is then converted into a canonical object pose by fixing a vertical top-down gripper orientation and inverting the transformation, followed by a vertical translation to rest the object on a surface. This process generates a set of reference object states, $S_g = \{\hat{s}_i^o\}_{i=1}^G$, each representing a configuration conducive to a stable grasp. These reference configurations are used for defining the graspability field during training but are never provided as observations or targets to the manipulation policy during inference.

#### 3.2. Graspability Field Definition

To quantify progress toward graspability, a scalar graspability measure $\tilde{d}(s_o)$ is defined. Instead of measuring distance to a single target pose, it measures proximity to the entire graspable set $S_g$.
- **Geometric Discrepancy $D(\cdot, \cdot)$**: The discrepancy between two object configurations $s_o$ and $\hat{s}_i^o$ is computed as the mean Euclidean distance of a fixed set of keypoints sampled from the object's bounding box after being transformed by their respective rigid transforms. This focuses on object geometry rather than specific pose parameters.
- **Anchor Distance $d_{anchor}(s_o)$**: Initially, graspability could be defined as the minimum distance to the graspable set: $d_{anchor}(s_o) = \min_{i \in \{1...,G\}} D(s_o, \hat{s}_i^o)$.
- **Soft Aggregation $d_{soft}(s_o)$**: To overcome discontinuities caused by abrupt switching of the closest reference, a smooth, differentiable soft aggregation is used: $d_{soft}(s_o) = -\tau \log \sum_{i=1}^G \exp\left(-\frac{D(s_o, \hat{s}_i^o)}{\tau}\right)$. This approximates the minimum distance smoothly.
- **Temporal Anchoring $\tilde{d}(s_t^o)$**: To promote temporal consistency and prevent oscillations between graspable modes, an anchor term based on the previously selected closest configuration $\hat{s}_{i^*}^o$ is added: $\tilde{d}(s_t^o) = d_{soft}(s_t^o) + \lambda D(s_t^o, \hat{s}_{i^*}^o)$. This measure, $\tilde{d}(s_o)$, constitutes the graspability field; lower values indicate higher graspability.

#### 3.3. Learning with Graspability-Driven Rewards

The graspability field $\tilde{d}(s_o)$ is converted into a training signal for reinforcement learning.
- **Potential-Based Reward Shaping**: Following prior work, a potential function $\Phi(s_o) = \phi(\tilde{d}(s_o))$, where $\phi(d) = k_1 \beta^{k_2 d}$, is used. This function is monotonically decreasing with graspability distance.
- **Shaped Reward**: The instantaneous reward is defined as $r_t = \gamma \Phi(s_{t+1}^o) - \Phi(s_t^o)$. This reward structure incentivizes the policy to progressively reduce the graspability distance, thereby moving the object toward graspable configurations. This provides a dense supervision signal without needing to execute actual grasp attempts during training.

#### 3.4. Grasp-Oriented Manipulation Policy (GOMP) Execution

At inference time, the GOMP policy receives partial observations $o_t$ and outputs a non-prehensile manipulation action $a_t$, a predicted graspability distance $\hat{d}_t$, and a grasp pose $g_t$ (used only for the final grasp execution). The policy itself does not receive reference configurations or target poses as input. The predicted distance $\hat{d}_t$ is supervised during training to regress the ground-truth graspability distance $\tilde{d}(s_t^o)$.

#### 3.5. Graspability-Guided Manipulation-to-Grasp Transition

Since there is no predefined target pose, GOMP must determine when to terminate manipulation. This decision is made using the policy-predicted graspability distance $\hat{d}_t$.
- **Scale-Invariant Threshold**: To account for objects of varying sizes, the predicted distance is normalized by the object's bounding box diagonal length $L$: $\bar{d}_t = \hat{d}_t / L$.
- **Transition Criterion**: Manipulation ceases and a grasp is executed when the normalized predicted graspability distance remains below a threshold $\epsilon$ for a short temporal window $K$: $\bar{d}_{t:t+K} < \epsilon$.
- At the moment of transition, the policy outputs a final grasp pose $g_t$ for execution. This unified approach means the same graspability measure drives both learning and the decision to stop manipulating, creating a fully closed-loop system.

The training process involves a teacher-student framework. The teacher is trained using PPO with full observations and domain randomization. An action-scale curriculum is then applied. Finally, the student policy, incorporating a GRU and operating on partial observations, is distilled from the teacher using a combination of behavior cloning and grasp-related prediction losses (for graspability distance, position, and orientation of the terminal grasp).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated GOMP's performance through extensive experiments in both simulation and on a real robot, assessing its ability to perform non-prehensile manipulation for grasp preparation and to transition autonomously to grasping.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup & Implementation Details

- **Simulation**: Training and evaluation were conducted in IsaacSim and IsaacLab using a Franka Emika Panda robot. Objects were selected from DexGraspNet, GraspFactory, and the YCB dataset. The teacher policy received full object point clouds, while the student policy used partial observations.
- **Real World**: Experiments used a Franka Research 3 robot with a parallel-jaw gripper. Perception relied on multi-view depth images from Intel RealSense D435IF cameras, fused into a single partial point cloud. The evaluation set included diverse objects (e.g., PaperBox, SmallBookend, DustBin, Bottle) without external tracking systems.
- **Training**: A teacher-student framework was employed. The teacher policy was trained with reinforcement learning for 15,000 iterations, followed by an additional 10,000 iterations with an action-scale curriculum. Domain randomization (object mass, scale, friction, initial poses) was applied throughout training.

#### 4.2. Non-Prehensile Manipulation Evaluation (Simulation)

This evaluation focused on whether the policy could reconfigure objects into graspable configurations, not on actual grasp success.
- **Baselines**: Comparisons included a pretrained CORN/DyWA teacher checkpoint, a retrained version of the same policy in the authors' setup, a "Multi-reference oracle" (retrained baseline selecting the best outcome from multiple targets), and a "Nearest-reference oracle" (retrained baseline using the closest reference as its target at each step).
- **Metric**: Success required both geometric alignment (position error < 0.05m, orientation error < 15°) and dynamic stability (linear velocity < 0.01m/s, angular velocity < 0.1rad/s). GOMP succeeded if the final configuration reached *any* graspable reference within these tolerances.
- **Results (Table 1)**:
 - Pretrained baselines exhibited low success rates (1.9-6.1% with stability check), indicating a fundamental mismatch between their pose-reaching objectives and the dynamically unstable graspable reference poses used in this study.
 - The retrained baseline improved performance (36.1% on seen objects, 28.8% on unseen) but still experienced oscillations.
 - Oracle baselines provided upper bounds (Nearest-reference: 52.0% seen, 50.2% unseen; Multi-reference: 42.2% seen, 39.4% unseen).
 - GOMP (Teacher: 84.0% seen, 77.9% unseen; Student: 75.5% seen, 68.1% unseen) substantially outperformed all baselines. It achieved higher success rates and generally shorter average manipulation times, demonstrating its ability to robustly guide objects to graspable states without an explicit target pose.

#### 4.3. Manipulation-to-Grasp Evaluation (Real World)

This experiment validated the full closed-loop pipeline on a physical robot. Challenging initial configurations were used, where direct grasping typically failed (8% success).
- **Process**: GOMP performed non-prehensile manipulation until its predicted graspability distance met the transition threshold, then executed its internally predicted grasp pose.
- **Results (Table 2)**:
 - **Manipulation Success**: 64% (32 out of 50 trials across 10 objects) of trials resulted in successful object reconfiguration into a graspable state.
 - **Grasp Success (conditioned on manipulation success)**: A high 97% (31 out of 32 successful manipulations) of subsequent grasps were successful. This indicates that once GOMP successfully prepares an object, the final grasp is highly reliable.
 - **Total Success**: 62% (31 out of 50 trials).
 - Failures primarily occurred during the manipulation stage, often due to objects like the "Snack" (highly deformable/slippery) or issues such as sliding, insufficient reconfiguration within time limits, or reaching safety force limits.
- **Validation of Graspability-Based Transition (Figure 6)**: Analysis of real-world data showed a clear separation in predicted graspability distances between graspable and non-graspable object configurations. This supports the use of a single, normalized threshold ($\epsilon$) for autonomously triggering the transition from manipulation to grasping, confirming that the learned representation captures grasp feasibility.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies (Simulation)

- **Number of Reference Poses (Table 3)**: Increasing the number of reference poses from 1 to 10 generally improved performance on unseen objects, suggesting better coverage of the graspable configuration space. 10 references yielded the best trade-off between success rate and execution time (84.0% seen, 77.9% unseen). Too many references (e.g., 20) slightly reduced success, potentially due to conflicting descent directions. Even with a single reference pose, GOMP achieved reasonable performance (82.2% seen, 72.8% unseen), indicating that the policy learns to navigate towards a graspable "basin" rather than meticulously matching a specific pose.
- **Graspability Distance Design (Table 3)**:
 - Using the `hardmin` (Eq. 3) instead of `soft aggregation` (Eq. 5) resulted in slightly lower performance (81.1% vs. 84.0% on seen objects), confirming the benefit of the smooth, differentiable soft aggregation for stable learning.
 - Removing the temporal anchoring term (Eq. 6) also reduced performance (78.1% vs. 84.0% on seen objects), highlighting its role in stabilizing trajectories and preventing oscillations between competing graspable modes.

In summary, the experiments demonstrate that GOMP effectively redefines non-prehensile manipulation by focusing on graspability optimization. It consistently outperforms target-pose-conditioned baselines in simulation and achieves robust closed-loop manipulation-to-grasp performance on a real robot, guided by a learned graspability field without requiring explicit target poses or external planning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a paradigm shift in how non-prehensile manipulation is conceptualized for robotic grasping, moving from a pose-matching objective to a graspability optimization objective. This reframing has several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Autonomy in Unstructured Environments**: By eliminating the need for predefined target object poses, the proposed GOMP system allows robots to operate more autonomously in diverse and unstructured environments. This is particularly valuable where the optimal graspable configuration for an object is unknown, variable, or difficult to specify in advance. The robot can adapt to the object's initial state and dynamically determine the most suitable configuration for grasping.

2. **Increased Robustness and Flexibility**: The graspability field approach inherently handles the existence of multiple graspable configurations for a single object. Instead of forcing the robot towards a specific, potentially suboptimal, target, the policy is guided toward *any* region in the object's configuration space that permits stable grasps. This flexibility can lead to more robust manipulation strategies, as the robot can pursue alternative graspable states if the primary path is blocked or unstable. The real-world results, demonstrating a high success rate for subsequent grasps once manipulation is complete, support this claim.

3. **Unified and Closed-Loop Control**: The integration of manipulation learning and termination criteria into a single graspability field provides a cohesive, closed-loop pipeline. This eliminates the need for separate external planners, heuristic stopping conditions, or pre-selected grasp candidates during manipulation. The robot autonomously decides both *how* to manipulate and *when* to stop and grasp, streamlining the robotic manipulation workflow.

4. **Dense and Meaningful Learning Signal**: The continuously defined graspability field offers a dense learning signal for reinforcement learning, facilitating more efficient policy acquisition compared to sparse reward signals tied to specific goal achievement. The correlation between the predicted graspability distance and real-world grasp success suggests that the learned representation captures a fundamental property of grasp feasibility, moving beyond task-specific reward heuristics.

5. **Generalizability**: The ablation studies demonstrate that the approach, especially with soft aggregation and temporal anchoring, provides better coverage of graspable configurations and more stable learning. The reasonable performance even with a single reference pose further highlights the policy's ability to learn general principles of improving graspability rather than overfitting to specific configurations. This suggests potential for generalization to novel objects and scenarios.

**Potential Impact**:
The development of a truly grasp-oriented manipulation policy could significantly impact applications requiring pick-and-place operations in logistics, manufacturing, and service robotics, where objects often present in arbitrary or unfavorable orientations. By enabling robots to prepare objects for grasping intelligently, this work contributes to the development of more capable and adaptable robotic systems that can perceive, reason about, and interact with their physical environment more effectively.

**Limitations and Future Directions (as stated by authors)**:
The authors acknowledge limitations, including degraded performance for highly deformable or extremely low-friction objects, and the reliance of the graspable set on the coverage of the initial grasp synthesis model. Future work could focus on learning graspability directly from interaction data, reducing reliance on pre-computed grasp sets, and extending the framework to handle cluttered and dynamic environments. These directions aim to further enhance the robustness and applicability of the graspability field concept.
