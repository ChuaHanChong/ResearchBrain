# One LR Doesn't Fit All: Heavy-Tail Guided Layerwise Learning Rates for LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> A Layerwise Learning Rate (LLR) scheme, guided by Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization theory, dynamically allocates learning rates to different layers of Large Language Models based on their spectral properties. This approach improves pre-training perplexity (e.g., LLaMa-1B validation perplexity from 9.77 to 9.59), boosts zero-shot reasoning accuracy by approximately 2% (e.g., 47.09% to 49.02% for LLaMa-1B), and achieves up to a 1.5x training speedup.

## Problem

- Traditional uniform learning rates are suboptimal for modern Large Language Models (LLMs) due to their heterogeneous architectural components.
- Existing layer-wise learning rate methods for LLMs often show limited performance gains or increased sensitivity to hyperparameter tuning, sometimes underperforming well-tuned uniform rates.
- Different LLM layers and modules exhibit distinct optimization landscapes and varying levels of "heavy-tailedness" in their weight spectra, indicating diverse training needs that a single LR cannot address.

## Method

- Introduces an adaptive Layerwise Learning Rate (LLR) scheme based on Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization (HT-SR) theory.
- Quantifies each layer's "heavy-tailedness" using the PL_Alpha_Hill metric, dynamically assigning higher LRs to under-optimized layers (weaker heavy-tailedness) and lower LRs to well-optimized layers via a bounded scaling function.
- Incorporates LLM-specific designs, including a fixed upper-bound LR for embedding layers, a soft-switch mechanism for smooth LR transitions, and an efficient active phase for spectral updates.

## Results

- LLR consistently lowered pre-training perplexity (e.g., LLaMa-1B validation perplexity improved from 9.77 to 9.59) and enhanced zero-shot reasoning accuracy by ~**2%** for LLaMa-1B, outperforming uniform and other layer-wise methods.
- The method demonstrated up to a **1.5x** training speedup, achieving comparable or superior training loss with significantly fewer tokens.
- LLR showed strong generality across various LLM architectures (LLaMa, GPT-nano), optimizers (AdamW, Muon), and also improved performance in finetuning scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Different LLM components, such as attention and FFN layers, exhibit distinct and persistent heavy-tailedness dynamics throughout training, with embedding layers showing uniquely high PL_Alpha_Hill values.
> - Layers with weaker heavy-tailedness (higher PL_Alpha_Hill) indicate a need for faster optimization, providing a robust, data-driven signal for differential learning rate allocation.
> - The proposed LLR method significantly reduces tuning overhead for learning rates, demonstrating robustness across various base LR settings while inheriting optimal values from uniform baselines.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The configuration of learning rates (LRs) is a fundamental aspect of training deep neural networks. Traditionally, a uniform learning rate is applied across all layers of a model. This practice originated with architectures such as multi-layer perceptrons (MLPs) and convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which exhibit relatively homogeneous internal structures. However, with the advent of Transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs), which possess inherently heterogeneous architectures, the suitability of a uniform LR has been questioned. Recent research indicates that Transformer components, such as attention and feed-forward network (FFN) layers, display distinct characteristics, including differing Hessian spectra and varying patterns in the Empirical Spectral Density (ESD) of their weight matrices. This structural heterogeneity suggests that a single learning rate may be suboptimal for simultaneously optimizing all layers.

Prior work has explored layer-wise learning rate adjustments. For instance, methods like LARS (You et al., 2017) and LAMB (You et al., 2019) scale learning rates based on the ratio of weight to gradient norms, primarily for stabilizing large-batch training in CNNs and earlier Transformer models like BERT. However, these methods have shown limited gains and increased sensitivity to LR tuning when applied to modern LLMs, often underperforming optimally tuned uniform LRs. Other approaches, such as Wang et al. (2025), have identified sharpness disparities across Transformer modules and proposed fixed block-wise LR schedules, but these typically rely on extensive grid search for optimal configuration. Hayou & Liu (2025) noted that optimal embedding-layer LRs depend on vocabulary size, indicating component-specific optimization needs.

This research specifically addresses the limitations of existing layer-wise LR methods, particularly their tuning sensitivity and lack of optimal performance against well-tuned uniform LRs in the context of LLM pre-training. It draws upon Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization (HT-SR) theory, which describes how the weights of well-trained neural networks exhibit strong correlations, leading to heavy-tailed patterns in the ESDs of layer-wise weight matrices. Different LLM components (embedding, attention, FFN) have been observed to exhibit distinct heavy-tailed structures across training stages (Couillet & Liao, 2022; Kothapalli et al., 2025; Ba et al., 2022). HT-SR theory has been applied to various deep learning challenges, including model selection, module-wise adaptive training, LLM pruning (Lu et al., 2024), and module-wise weight-decay allocation (He et al., 2025). This paper extends the application of HT-SR theory to dynamically adjust layer-wise learning rates during the pre-training of LLMs, a domain where its utility for LR design has not been comprehensively established. The work distinguishes itself from related HT-SR-based methods like TempBalance (Zhou et al., 2023), which focused on CNNs and fine-tuning scenarios with limited data, by explicitly tailoring the approach for the unique characteristics and large-scale pre-training of Transformer-based LLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core motivation for this research stems from the observation that the prevailing practice of applying a uniform learning rate across all layers of a neural network overlooks the inherent structural heterogeneity of modern Transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs). While effective for earlier, more homogeneous architectures like MLPs and CNNs, this uniform approach may constrain the convergence dynamics and generalization capabilities of LLMs.

Specific motivations include:
- **Structural Heterogeneity of Transformers:** Recent studies indicate that different components within Transformer architectures (e.g., attention modules, feed-forward networks, embedding layers) exhibit distinct optimization landscapes, as evidenced by differences in their Hessian spectra and the spectral properties (heavy-tailedness) of their weight matrices. A single global learning rate is unlikely to cater optimally to these varying needs simultaneously.
- **Suboptimality of Existing Layer-wise Methods:** Prior attempts at layer-wise learning rate allocation, such as LARS and LAMB, or sharpness-based methods (Wang et al., 2025), often prove highly sensitive to hyperparameter tuning for LLMs and can even underperform a carefully tuned uniform learning rate baseline. This indicates a need for a more principled and robust layer-wise strategy.
- **Insights from Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization (HT-SR) Theory:** HT-SR theory suggests that the spectral properties of weight matrices, particularly their degree of heavy-tailedness, are correlated with the training quality of different layers. Layers exhibiting stronger heavy-tailedness are considered better optimized, while those with weaker heavy-tailedness require further training. This theoretical framework provides a basis for differential learning rate allocation.
- **Coexistence of Training States:** Within an LLM, some layers or components may be well-optimized while others are under-optimized throughout the training process. A uniform LR cannot effectively address this simultaneous presence of varying training states.

The key objectives of this work are:
- **Develop a principled Layer-wise Learning Rate (LLR) scheme:** Introduce an adaptive learning rate strategy that assigns distinct learning rates to individual Transformer layers based on their dynamic spectral characteristics.
- **Ground LLR in HT-SR theory:** Utilize the empirical spectral density (ESD) of layer-wise weight correlation matrices to quantify heavy-tailedness, using the Power Law (PL) exponent (specifically, the PL_Alpha_Hill metric) as a direct measure.
- **Promote balanced training:** Implement a mechanism where layers with weaker heavy-tailedness (higher PL_Alpha_Hill, suggesting under-training) are assigned larger learning rates to accelerate their optimization, while layers with stronger heavy-tailedness (lower PL_Alpha_Hill, suggesting better training) receive smaller learning rates. This aims to foster more balanced progress across all layers.
- **Achieve improved convergence and generalization:** Demonstrate that this adaptive LLR scheme leads to faster training convergence and enhanced generalization performance, surpassing both uniform learning rates and existing layer-wise allocation methods, even when baselines are optimally tuned.
- **Ensure low tuning overhead:** Design the LLR method such that it maintains robustness to variations in initial learning rate settings and minimizes additional hyperparameter tuning requirements, facilitating practical adoption.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed Layerwise Learning Rate (LLR) method introduces an adaptive scheme for learning rate allocation in LLMs, grounded in Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization (HT-SR) theory. The methodology involves periodically analyzing the spectral properties of each layer's weight matrix to dynamically adjust its learning rate.

#### 3.1. HT-SR Theory and Metric Selection

The foundation of LLR is the HT-SR theory, which characterizes the Empirical Spectral Density (ESD) of weight matrices in neural networks. Well-trained models often exhibit ESDs with pronounced heavy-tails, indicative of strong correlations within the weights. The degree of heavy-tailedness is quantified using the Power Law (PL) exponent, $\alpha$. In the context of LLMs, a smaller $\alpha$ value for a layer implies stronger heavy-tailedness, which is associated with better trainability and optimization of that layer's weight matrix. Conversely, a larger $\alpha$ indicates weaker heavy-tailedness, suggesting the layer may be less optimized.

To quantify this, LLR employs the **PL_Alpha_Hill** metric. For each layer with a weight matrix $W_l$, its correlation matrix $X_l = W_l^T W_l$ is computed. The ESD is derived from the eigenvalues of $X_l$. A power law distribution $p(\lambda) \propto \lambda^{-\alpha}$ is then fitted to the ESD. The Hill estimator (Equation 2 in the paper) is used to estimate the PL exponent $\alpha$, denoted as PL_Alpha_Hill. The parameter $k$ for the Hill estimator is set to $n/2$, using the largest half of the eigenvalues for the estimation.

#### 3.2. Layerwise Learning Rate (LLR) Allocation

LLR periodically computes the PL_Alpha_Hill values for all layers. Based on these values, it assigns learning rates such that layers with higher PL_Alpha_Hill (weaker heavy-tailedness, less optimized) receive larger LRs, while layers with lower PL_Alpha_Hill (stronger heavy-tailedness, more optimized) receive smaller LRs. This is designed to balance the optimization progress across the heterogeneous layers.

The mapping from PL_Alpha_Hill values to per-layer LRs is performed using a bounded scaling function (Equation 3):
$f_T(i) = \eta \cdot \left( \frac{\alpha_i^T - \alpha_{\min}^T}{\alpha_{\max}^T - \alpha_{\min}^T}(s-1) + 1 \right)$
where:
- $\eta$ is the global base learning rate from the primary scheduler (e.g., cosine decay).
- $(1, s)$ defines the range of scaling ratios applied to $\eta$, such that the per-layer LR remains within $[\eta, s\eta]$.
- $\alpha_i^T$ is the PL_Alpha_Hill value of layer $i$ at update step $T$.
- $\alpha_{\min}^T$ and $\alpha_{\max}^T$ are the minimum and maximum PL_Alpha_Hill values observed across all layers at step $T$.
This function ensures adaptive adjustment while preventing excessively large or small learning rates.

#### 3.3. Tailored Designs for LLM Architectures

To adapt HT-SR theory effectively for LLMs, three specific design choices were integrated:
1. **Tailored Embedding Treatment:** The Embedding (and Output Head) layer consistently exhibits high PL_Alpha_Hill values throughout training, indicating its critical role and distinct optimization needs. To ensure sufficient updates, its learning rate is fixed at the scaling function's upper bound, $s\eta$, rather than being adaptively scaled by the function itself.
2. **Soft Layer-wise LR Switch:** Instead of abruptly changing learning rates at each update interval (a "Hard Switch" that can cause "LR spikes"), LLR employs a "Soft Switch" mechanism. The current layer-wise LR $\eta_t$ is linearly transitioned towards the newly calculated target LR $f_{t+t_{\text{switch}}}^T(i)$ over a window of $t_{\text{switch}}$ steps. This smoothing strategy enhances training stability. By default, $t_{\text{switch}}$ is set to half of the interval for PL_Alpha_Hill calculation, $\tilde{t}$ (e.g., 50 steps).
3. **Efficient Active Phase:** Empirical observations showed that the layer-wise PL_Alpha_Hill statistics tend to stabilize after approximately the first 20% of training tokens. To reduce computational overhead, the computationally intensive layer-wise LR updates are restricted to this initial 20% phase. For the remaining training duration, the layer-wise ratios are maintained, or the LR reverts to a uniform schedule, depending on the implementation details.

The overall LLR algorithm (Algorithm 1 in the paper) integrates these components. It periodically re-evaluates PL_Alpha_Hill, computes target LRs using the bounded scaling function and tailored embedding treatment, applies the soft switch for smooth transitions, and then continues training with the updated layer-wise LRs under a global scheduler (e.g., cosine decay).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents an extensive empirical evaluation of LLR across various LLM architectures, parameter scales, optimizers, and training scenarios, demonstrating consistent improvements over baseline methods.

#### 4.1. LLM Pre-training Performance

- **Consistent Superiority:** LLR consistently outperformed the Uniform baseline and other layer-wise adaptive methods (LARS, LAMB, Sharpness) in pre-training LLaMa models across parameter scales (60M, 135M, 350M, 1B) on the FineWeb dataset (Table 2). For LLaMa-1B, LLR achieved a validation perplexity of 9.59, compared to 9.77 for Uniform.
- **Scalability:** The performance gains of LLR were observed across all evaluated model sizes, from 60M to 1B, indicating its scalability for LLM pre-training.
- **Comparison with other Layer-wise Methods:** Existing methods like LARS and LAMB, originally designed for different architectures, provided marginal or negative gains for LLMs, suggesting their limitations in handling Transformer-specific characteristics. LLR also surpassed Adammini, Mup-AdamW, and CompleteP-AdamW variants (Table 5).
- **Beyond Chinchilla Scaling Law:** For LLaMa-350M trained with 20B tokens (3x Chinchilla tokens), LLR demonstrated lower training loss and higher average zero-shot accuracy compared to Uniform, under both AdamW and Muon optimizers (Figure 6 and Figure 7). Similarly, for LLaMa-1B trained with 100B tokens (5x Chinchilla tokens), LLR maintained performance gains, outperforming Uniform in validation perplexity and average zero-shot accuracy (Table 4).

#### 4.2. Downstream Task Performance (Zero-shot and Finetuning)

- **Zero-shot Reasoning:** LLR-pretrained LLaMa-1B models achieved higher average zero-shot accuracy (49.02%) across seven commonsense reasoning benchmarks, compared to Uniform (47.09%) (Table 3). This indicates that pre-training benefits effectively transfer to downstream tasks.
- **Large-scale Zero-shot:** For LLaMa-3B trained on 30B tokens, LLR improved average zero-shot accuracy from 48.58% to 50.61%, confirming effectiveness at larger scales (Table 4).
- **Finetuning:** LLR also demonstrated effectiveness in finetuning scenarios, achieving the best performance on 6 out of 8 Commonsense Reasoning tasks using Llama-3.2-1B, boosting average accuracy from 52.94% (Uniform) to 53.58% (LLR) (Table 8).

#### 4.3. Generality and Efficiency

- **Architectural Generality:** LLR consistently outperformed baselines on the GPT-nano architecture, achieving the lowest validation perplexity (Table 9), which supports its applicability across different Transformer architectures.
- **Optimizer Compatibility:** LLR showed consistent improvements when paired with the Muon optimizer (Figure 7 and Table 10), indicating that its benefits are not restricted to AdamW and stem from fundamental spectral characteristics rather than specific optimizer mechanics.
- **Training Speedup:** LLR achieved up to a 1.5x training speedup, reaching comparable or superior training loss with fewer tokens (e.g., 3B/7B tokens) than Uniform models trained with more tokens (4.5B/10.5B tokens) (Figure 8).
- **Low Tuning Overhead:** A key advantage is LLR's low sensitivity to base learning rate tuning. Figure 1 illustrates that LLR's performance is robust across a range of learning rates, and it can inherit nearly optimal LR settings directly from a standard uniform LR, minimizing practical tuning efforts.

#### 4.4. Dynamics of PL_Alpha_Hill and Ablation Studies

- **Layer-wise Heterogeneity:** The PL_Alpha_Hill dynamics showed that attention parameters (Att.q, Att.k, Att.v, Att.o) consistently exhibited smaller PL_Alpha_Hill values (stronger heavy-tailedness), while FFN parameters (FFN.gate, FFN.up, FFN.down) maintained larger values (weaker heavy-tailedness) throughout training (Figure 5 and Figure 14). Embedding layers showed persistently high PL_Alpha_Hill.
- **Impact on Dynamics:** LLR reduced the PL_Alpha_Hill across all parameter groups compared to Uniform, correlating with improved perplexity (Figure 5). This suggests LLR effectively balances heavy-tailed properties. LLR also resulted in lower perplexity and smaller inter-layer alpha standard deviations, indicating improved stability (Table 19).
- **Tailored Design Validation:**
 - The "Tailored Embedding Treatment" was critical; removing it ("LLR-unembed") led to significant performance degradation (Figure 4 Left).
 - The "Soft Layer-wise LR Switch" improved perplexity (17.03 vs. 17.18) compared to a "Hard Switch" (Figure 4 Middle), enhancing stability.
 - The "Efficient Active Phase" (restricting LLR updates to the initial 20% of training) maintained performance while reducing computational overhead (Figure 4 Right).
- **Methodological Robustness:** Ablation studies confirmed that the chosen PL_Alpha_Hill metric was superior to other spectral metrics (Figure 9) and that the "Median" PL fitting method was effective (Figure 10). LLR maintained stable performance even with larger PL fitting gaps (Figure 11) and was robust to variations in the scaling ratio hyperparameter $s$ (Figure 12). Inverting the LLR projection led to substantial performance degradation, validating the directional application of the HT-SR principle (Table 14). LLR was also effective in a more homogeneous attention-only architecture (Table 15).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces Layerwise Learning Rate (LLR), a principled and empirically validated method for optimizing Large Language Models (LLMs) that challenges the conventional uniform learning rate paradigm. Its significance and potential impact span theoretical understanding, practical efficiency, and future research directions in deep learning.

**Theoretical Advancements:**
- **Bridging Theory and Practice:** LLR effectively translates insights from Heavy-Tailed Self-Regularization (HT-SR) theory into a practical, adaptive learning rate scheme for LLMs. This demonstrates the utility of spectral analysis (specifically, PL_Alpha_Hill) in understanding and guiding the optimization process in complex, heterogeneous architectures like Transformers.
- **Understanding Optimization Dynamics:** The detailed analysis of PL_Alpha_Hill dynamics across different Transformer layers provides empirical evidence for the distinct optimization needs of various modules (e.g., attention vs. FFNs). This contributes to a deeper understanding of why uniform learning rates are suboptimal for LLMs.

**Practical Implications:**
- **Improved Performance and Generalization:** LLR consistently achieves lower perplexity and higher zero-shot accuracy across a range of LLM sizes (60M to 3B, and up to 100B tokens) and architectures (LLaMa, GPT-nano). This means that models trained with LLR can achieve better performance on various downstream tasks without requiring more parameters or data.
- **Enhanced Training Efficiency and Speedup:** The observed training speedup of up to 1.5x directly translates to reduced computational costs and time. For training large-scale LLMs, which are highly resource-intensive, this efficiency gain can be substantial, making advanced models more accessible and sustainable.
- **Reduced Tuning Overhead:** A notable practical advantage is LLR's low sensitivity to learning rate tuning. Its ability to inherit nearly optimal learning rate settings from standard uniform LR baselines simplifies the training process for practitioners, lowering the barrier to adoption and reducing the extensive hyperparameter search typically required.
- **Robustness and Generality:** The demonstrated effectiveness across different optimizers (AdamW, Muon), various model scales, and extended training regimes indicates LLR's robustness and broad applicability in diverse training environments. Its effectiveness in finetuning further extends its utility beyond just pre-training.

**Future Research Directions:**
- **Further Integration with Optimization Techniques:** LLR could inspire research into integrating HT-SR theory with other adaptive optimization techniques or regularization schemes to further enhance model performance and efficiency.
- **Architectural Specificity:** The success of LLR's tailored designs for LLMs (e.g., embedding treatment, soft switching) suggests that future optimization strategies could benefit from even more architecture-specific adaptations of theoretical principles.
- **Beyond Learning Rates:** The use of spectral properties to guide optimization could be extended to other hyperparameter adjustments, such as weight decay, dropout rates, or batch normalization parameters, in a layer-wise or module-wise fashion.
- **Theoretical Generalization:** Further theoretical work could explore the universality of heavy-tailed self-regularization across different deep learning tasks and model types beyond LLMs.

In conclusion, LLR offers a robust, efficient, and theoretically grounded approach to LLM optimization, addressing a critical limitation in current training practices. Its potential to accelerate training, improve model performance, and reduce tuning complexity could significantly impact the development and deployment of future large language models.
