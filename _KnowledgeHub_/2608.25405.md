# LAC: Linear and Angular Compliance for Humanoid Whole-body Control

> [!summary] Summary
> LAC is a whole-body humanoid controller realizing independently commanded linear and angular compliance to wrenches anywhere on the upper body. It builds a 378,051-episode augmented dataset by imposing sampled force and couple events on retargeted human-interaction contact frames, driving a virtual admittance whose angular channel is anchored to the passively yielding kinematic chain, then trains one teacher-student RL policy. On a real 23-DoF G1, hand attitude change spans 84 degrees to 15 degrees under stiffness command.

## Problem

- Most humanoid controllers reject external forces as disturbances, holding the reference rigidly and producing uncontrolled contact forces during collaboration or fragile-object handling.
- Existing compliant humanoid policies confine compliance to a few links, with lower bodies tracking reference motion instead of joining the whole-body compliant response.
- No prior policy commands angular compliance: setting link rotation proportional to torque about a fixed point is neither physically feasible nor observable from proprioception.

## Method

- ==Compliant motion augmentation==: force and couple events imposed on contact frames retargeted from human-object and human-human interaction data, converted to feasible whole-body motion via ==differential inverse kinematics==.
- ==Virtual admittance== whose angular channel is driven by a virtual torque proportional to the passive joint deflection of the kinematic chain, anchoring the angular stiffness command to a physical reference.
- ==RMA-style teacher-student RL==: privileged encoder over wrenches and residuals plus ==PPO== actor, then a proprioception-only estimator, with wrench curriculum bins, symmetry augmentation, and rare-contact resampling. Together this forms **LAC**.

## Results

- Attitude change falls monotonically from **60 degrees** at angular stiffness **10** to **17 degrees** at **100** under a **4 N·m** couple, with linear position response preserved; real-robot curve matches simulation.
- Only method whose contact-link displacement decreases monotonically with stiffness in all three scenarios; torso displacement spans **49 cm** to **8 cm**, versus flat FALCON and narrow SoftMimic and GentleHumanoid ranges.
- Real G1: twisting the hand yields **84 degrees** rotation at stiffness **10** versus **15 degrees** at **100**; high angular stiffness carries a **4 kg** object that low stiffness drops.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Compliance targets for articulated links must be derived from the kinematic chain's passive deflection, not from free-rigid-body assumptions, to stay feasible and proprioceptively observable.
> - Decoupling linear and angular stiffness commands matters because tasks invert the requirement: grasp twisting needs soft rotation with firm position, tray carrying needs the reverse.
> - Compliance is a whole-body property; letting the base step and lower its height widens the achievable stiffness range far beyond arm-only or upper-body-only compliant controllers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid robotics is progressing from merely imitating human motions to performing complex real-world tasks that necessitate physical interaction with objects and humans. Current motion-tracking controllers, while capable of faithfully reproducing human movements and enabling teleoperation, typically address external forces by treating them as disturbances to be rejected. This approach, exemplified by methods like FALCON, is effective for tasks requiring resistance to external forces but results in rigid and uncontrolled responses when compliance is required, such as during physical collaboration or handling fragile objects.

In contrast, another area of research focuses on enabling robots to comply with external wrenches. This includes classical impedance control, which prescribes deviations based on commanded stiffness, and more recent learning-based controllers applied to both quadrupedal and humanoid platforms. However, existing compliance solutions often exhibit limitations. Many confine compliance to a few specific body links, such as end-effectors or arms, without extending it to the whole body. Furthermore, most approaches primarily address linear compliance, neglecting the angular channel. Real-world interactions frequently demand control over the attitude of contact links; for instance, twisting a robot's hand might require low angular stiffness while maintaining a high linear stiffness to hold its position. No prior policy, to the authors' knowledge, effectively realizes independently commanded linear and angular compliance for wrenches applied across the entire upper body of a humanoid robot.

The challenge of achieving this capability involves synthesizing large-scale training data where the compliant responses are both physically feasible and observable through proprioception. While linear displacement can be set proportionally to force, directly applying this principle to angular rotation is problematic. The rotation of a link is an accumulation of joint deflections, and setting target rotations based on torque about a fixed point may not guarantee physical feasibility or observability from joint-level signals. LAC addresses this gap by developing a general whole-body controller that simultaneously realizes commanded linear and angular compliance through a novel data synthesis and reinforcement learning framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a general learning-based whole-body controller, named LAC (Linear and Angular Compliance), capable of simultaneously achieving commanded linear and angular compliance for external wrenches applied to the upper body of a humanoid robot. This objective is motivated by several critical needs in advancing humanoid robotics:

- **Enabling Real-World Physical Interaction:** Real-world tasks, particularly in loco-manipulation, often involve continuous and dynamic physical interaction with diverse objects, tools, and people. Current controllers that primarily reject external forces as disturbances are insufficient for such interactive scenarios, leading to rigid and potentially unsafe behavior.
- **Addressing the Need for Tunable Compliance:** Robots require the ability to adjust their stiffness, both linearly and angularly, to suit varying task demands. For instance, physical collaboration with a human or handling fragile items necessitates compliant behavior, allowing the robot to yield gracefully to external forces. Conversely, carrying heavy objects or resisting unwanted perturbations requires stiff responses.
- **Independent Control of Linear and Angular Stiffness:** Many complex interactions demand differentiated control over translational and rotational compliance. For example, during a grasp-type interaction where a person twists the robot's hand, the robot might need to maintain its hand position (high linear stiffness) while allowing its attitude to change (low angular stiffness). Conversely, carrying an object on the arms requires high angular stiffness to keep the arms stable and level. Existing methods often lack this independent control, focusing predominantly on linear compliance or restricting it to specific body parts.
- **Whole-Body Compliant Response:** Previous approaches typically confine compliant behavior to limited body links (e.g., end-effectors), neglecting the potential for the entire robot's body (including the base and legs) to contribute to a more stable and natural compliant response. A whole-body approach can distribute external forces more effectively and maintain balance during interaction.
- **Overcoming Data Synthesis Challenges for Angular Compliance:** Synthesizing training data for angular compliance presents unique challenges. The rotation of a link under a wrench is dictated by the deflections of its underlying kinematic chain, which must be physically feasible and observable from proprioceptive signals. Directly setting target rotations proportional to applied torque about a fixed point may not align with the robot's physical capabilities or internal sensing. LAC aims to overcome this by anchoring the angular stiffness command to a physical reference derived from the passively yielding kinematic chain.
- **Developing a Unified, Learning-Based Policy:** The goal is to train a single, robust policy through reinforcement learning that can generalize across different wrench application sites on the upper body and handle varying linear and angular stiffness commands, thereby providing a versatile and adaptable control solution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

LAC employs a two-stage approach: first, a compliant motion augmentation process synthesizes a large-scale dataset of physically feasible compliant responses; second, a teacher-student reinforcement learning scheme trains a single policy to reproduce these responses under external wrenches.

#### 3.1. Compliant Motion Augmentation

The data synthesis process generates augmented episodes that capture how the robot should deform under external wrenches and commanded stiffness.

- **Data Preprocessing:** The process begins by collecting "contact frames" from existing human interaction datasets, specifically OMOMO (human-object interactions) and Inter-X (human-human interactions). These human motions are retargeted to the humanoid robot using OmniRetarget. For two-robot interactions (Inter-X), each actor is retargeted separately, one is frozen as an environment object, and the other's motion is re-optimized to minimize interpenetration while preserving contact. Contact frames are extracted where at least one of the eleven upper-body links makes contact. Each frame stores upper-body joint angles (`q_ref`), a contact activation mask (`m_j`), and for each contacted link `j`, its contact point (`p_j`) and inward surface normal (`n_hat_j`) in the link frame.

- **Compliant Response Synthesis (Algorithm 1):** For each sampled contact frame, an augmented episode is synthesized over `T` frames.
 - **Reference Trajectory:** A baseline motion is established with the robot's base at a commanded height (`h_cmd`), identity orientation, default leg stance, and upper body frozen at `q_ref`. Locomotion commands are zero during synthesis.
 - **Wrench Event Imposition:** Wrench events (either force or couple) are imposed at active contact points, following a trapezoidal time profile (`Π(t)`).
 - **Force Events:** Forces (`f_ext_j`) are applied along the world-frame contact normal (`d_hat_j`). The peak force magnitude is determined by displacing a virtual environment spring of sampled stiffness (`K_env_j`) by `Δx_j`, ensuring the peak force (`F_peak_j`) and commanded deviation are capped.
 - **Couple Events:** Couples (`τ_ext_j`) are applied independently of any application point, with sampled peak magnitude (`τ_peak_j`) and direction (`c_hat_j`) (either uniform or cone-biased around joint axes).
 - **Angular Response Mechanism (Crucial Step):** The angular response is not directly driven by the external couple on the link. Instead, the external wrench (force or couple) is mapped to upper-body joint torques via the translational (`J_p,j`) and rotational (`J_r,j`) Jacobians. These torques cause joint deflections (`Δq_u`) based on the robot's joint-level PD stiffness (`Λ_q`). Forward kinematics then determines the contact link's orientation under this deflection (`R_Δ_j`), providing a passive rotation (`Δθ_j`) from the reference. This `Δθ_j` is converted into a *virtual torque* (`τ_vir_j = κ Δθ_j`) which drives the angular admittance. The torso only uses the linear channel.
 - **Virtual Admittance System:** Each active contact link's center of mass is modeled as a virtual second-order system. This system integrates the external wrench (`f_ext_j` for linear, `τ_vir_j` for angular) against an impedance wrench (`f_imp_j`, `τ_imp_j`) to determine a target pose (`x_vir_j`, `R_vir_j`). The impedance wrench pulls the link back to its reference pose with the commanded linear (`K_rob_j`) and angular (`K_rob,θ_j`) stiffness, along with critical damping terms. At steady state, the impedance torque balances the virtual torque, effectively scaling the passive rotation `Δθ_j` by `κ/K_rob,θ_j`.
 - **Whole-Body Inverse Kinematics (IK):** A differential inverse kinematics step (solved as a quadratic program at the velocity level) realizes the admittance targets on the robot. The optimizer minimizes the tracking error to the contact link's admittance target (`x_vir_j`, `R_vir_j`), while also anchoring the base to its reference pose (`p_ref_b`, `R_ref_b`) and regularizing upper-body joint angles towards `q_ref`. A hierarchical weighting prioritizes contact target tracking. Constraints include joint limits, base height range, fixed leg stance, and capped base velocities. The solution yields the augmented episode: `q_aug(t)`, `v_aug(t)`, and `h_aug(t)`.
 - **Feasibility Check:** Generated episodes undergo a feasibility check to ensure consistency (impedance wrench closely balances external wrench), absence of self-collision, and total applied wrench within budget. Failed attempts trigger a scaling down of the external wrench and re-synthesis, allowing the system to find the strongest feasible response.

- **Dataset Composition:** The augmentation process generates 378,051 accepted episodes (approximately 1,050 hours of data), divided into force and couple events. Contact points are predominantly at the hands and torso. The dataset is partitioned into curriculum bins based on the sum of peak wrench magnitudes, for progressive training.

#### 3.2. Reinforcement Learning with Augmented Samples

A teacher-student reinforcement learning framework (RMA-style) is used to train the policy.

- **Observations:** The robot's state and commands are structured into four groups:
 - `o_t` (Deployable): Standard proprioception (base angular velocity, gravity, joint angles/velocities, previous action) and current commands (base velocity, height, reference joint angles, stiffness commands). The actor uses a 10-frame history.
 - `o_priv_t` (Privileged): Ground-truth base orientation/linear velocity, external wrenches, and residuals (base velocity, height, upper-body joint angles) relative to the reference. An encoder processes a 5-frame history.
 - `o_crit_t` (Critic-only): Locomotion mode (stance/walk), derived from the target velocity.
 - `o_t-63:t` (Estimator-only): A 64-frame history of `o_t` for the estimator.
- **Teacher-Student Training:**
 - **Stage 1:** A privileged encoder (`E_priv`) compresses `o_priv_t` into a latent `Z_t`. An actor (`π`), conditioned on `o_t` and `Z_t`, is trained using PPO. An asymmetric critic (`V`) also uses `o_t`, `o_priv_t`, and `o_crit_t`. Simultaneously, an estimator (`E_est`) is regressed to predict `Z_t` from the 64-frame history of `o_t`.
 - **Stage 2:** The encoder and actor are frozen. Rollouts are collected with the actor conditioned on the estimated latent `Z_hat_t`. The regression of `E_est` continues. The deployed controller consists of the `E_est`-`π` pair, driven purely by proprioception and commands.
 - The actor outputs 23 joint angle targets at 50 Hz, which are tracked by joint-level PD controllers.
- **Rewards:** The reward function comprises three components:
 - **Tracking Terms:** Gaussian kernels reward tracking of the augmented motion targets (base velocity `v_tar`, base height `h_aug`, upper-body joint angles `q_aug`).
 - **Gait Terms:** Standard bipedal shaping rewards (stride period, swing height, contact schedule, foot placement), adapted for stance or walk modes.
 - **Auxiliary Terms:** Regularization for base orientation, energy consumption, action smoothness, penalties for joint limit approaches, and a survival bonus.
- **Training Details:** Training is conducted in Isaac Lab across multiple GPUs. Wrench strength is progressively introduced using the curriculum bins from the dataset. Symmetry augmentation is applied to address dataset imbalances, and episode sampling is reweighted to up-sample rare contact links, improving performance at those locations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents extensive simulation and real-world experiments to evaluate LAC's performance in achieving linear and angular compliance.

#### 4.1. Effectiveness of Linear and Angular Compliance

- **Linear Stiffness Modulation (Fig. 3):** Experiments varied the left-arm linear stiffness command from 10 to 500 N/m while applying a 20 N force to the hand in different directions (forward, lateral, vertical). The results indicate a monotonic decrease in the center of mass (CoM) displacement of the hand link as linear stiffness increases. The attitude change of the hand, however, remained nearly constant across the stiffness range. Vertical displacement showed a narrower modulation range compared to horizontal directions, attributed to the robot's need to maintain an upright posture, which limits the CoM drop at softer settings.
- **Angular Stiffness Modulation (Fig. 4):** The arm angular stiffness was varied from 10 to 100 N m/rad, testing a 20 N vertical force, a 4 N m couple on the hand, and a 20 N lateral pull on the palm. The attitude change of the contact link decreased monotonically with increasing angular stiffness, demonstrating effective control over rotational compliance. For instance, the couple test showed attitude change dropping from 60° at 10 N m/rad to 17° at 100 N m/rad. The CoM displacement remained largely preserved, although it increased by about 15 cm from stiff to soft ends during the lateral-pull test, indicating a minor trade-off in position stability when realizing large attitude changes with the arm's limited degrees of freedom.
- **Real-World Validation:** A lateral-pull test on a physical robot corroborated the simulation results. The real robot's response to a 20 N lateral pull on its right palm, measured via a force gauge, closely matched the simulated trend and range for angular stiffness modulation, confirming the policy's transferability to hardware.

#### 4.2. Comparison with Baseline Methods

LAC was compared against SoftMimic, GentleHumanoid, and FALCON in MuJoCo simulations under various force application scenarios (hand pull, elbow pull, torso push).

- **Qualitative Comparison (Fig. 5):** When a forward-downward force was applied to the palm:
 - **LAC** exhibited a natural, balanced, and whole-body compliant response by stepping forward, extending the arm, and lowering its height as the force increased, then recovering to the initial state.
 - **SoftMimic** and **GentleHumanoid** showed compliance, but their lower bodies struggled to maintain contact, leading to the robot's feet lifting off the ground or sliding.
 - **FALCON** displayed no compliance, merely sliding forward under the force.
- **Quantitative Comparison (Fig. 6):** Measuring the CoM displacement of the contact link at peak force across low, mid, and high stiffness commands for each method:
 - **LAC** was the only method that demonstrated a monotonic decrease in CoM displacement with increasing stiffness commands across all three tested scenarios (hand pull, elbow pull, torso push).
 - LAC also covered the widest range of displacements, with torso displacement varying from 49 cm at the softest setting to 8 cm at the stiffest.
 - SoftMimic and GentleHumanoid showed narrower modulation ranges, and their compliance commands were ineffective for torso pushes.
 - FALCON provided a fixed, non-compliant response.
 - Overall, LAC achieved the largest displacements at the soft end and matched or exceeded FALCON's stiffness at the stiff end, demonstrating superior adaptability and range.

#### 4.3. Performance in Real-World Tasks

Real-world evaluations using a Unitree G1 humanoid robot further demonstrated LAC's capabilities.

- **Whole-Body Compliant Behavior (Fig. 7a):** With low linear and angular stiffness commands, the robot showed integrated whole-body responses to external wrenches. Pulling the hand forward made the robot extend its arm and step forward. A sideways pull on the elbow resulted in the robot raising its elbow and shifting its base sideways. Reducing a backward push on the shoulder caused the robot to move forward to recover. Pressing down on the torso led to the robot bending its knees and lowering its height.
- **Angular Stiffness for Task Success (Fig. 7b):**
 - In a twisting interaction with the robot's left hand, a low angular stiffness (10 N m/rad) allowed the palm to be twisted significantly (84° rotation).
 - A high angular stiffness (100 N m/rad) effectively resisted the twist, limiting rotation to 15°.
 - This modulation was critical for tasks like carrying an object: with low angular stiffness, a 4 kg object placed on the arms fell off due to arm tilting; with high angular stiffness, the arms remained level, and the object was carried steadily.
- **Loco-Manipulation Tasks via VR Teleoperation (Fig. 7c):** The system was tested in teleoperated loco-manipulation scenarios:
 - **Low Stiffness:** Using low linear and angular stiffness commands on both arms facilitated transporting soft objects like a yoga ball. The robot could pick up and hand over the ball with ease, requiring less fine adjustment from the operator due to its inherent compliance.
 - **High Stiffness:** With high stiffness commands, the robot successfully handled force-demanding tasks such as carrying a heavy box and placing it on a table, or pushing open a spring-loaded door.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents LAC, a humanoid whole-body controller that addresses a significant gap in robotics: the simultaneous and independently commanded control of linear and angular compliance across the upper body. Its significance and potential impact are multifold:

- **Enhanced Human-Robot Interaction:** By enabling tunable and whole-body compliant responses, LAC facilitates more natural, safer, and effective physical interactions between humanoids and their environment, including humans. This is crucial for collaborative robotics, assistive tasks, and operation in unstructured environments where unexpected forces are common.
- **Increased Versatility in Manipulation Tasks:** The ability to command both linear and angular stiffness allows humanoids to adapt to a broader range of manipulation tasks. From gently handling fragile objects (low stiffness) to firmly pushing open heavy doors or carrying substantial loads (high stiffness), the robot can adjust its physical behavior to match task requirements precisely. This includes nuanced grasp-type interactions where rotational compliance is paramount.
- **Robust Whole-Body Control:** LAC's whole-body approach ensures that compliance is not limited to isolated links but is distributed across the entire robot, including the base and legs. This integrated response improves stability, balance, and force distribution, making the robot more resilient to perturbations and capable of performing compliant loco-manipulation tasks.
- **Foundation for Autonomous Stiffness Adjustment:** While current stiffness commands are operator-set, the framework lays the groundwork for future research into autonomous and dynamic adjustment of compliance based on task context and perceived interactions. This could lead to more intelligent and self-adaptive robotic systems.
- **Advancement in Learning-Based Control:** The methodology, involving large-scale compliant motion augmentation and teacher-student reinforcement learning, demonstrates an effective strategy for training complex, physically grounded behaviors in humanoid robots, particularly for scenarios where direct real-world data collection for varied compliance is impractical.
- **Transferability and Generalization:** The successful transfer of the learned policy from simulation to a real humanoid robot, along with its ability to generalize across different upper-body links and various wrench types, highlights the robustness and practical applicability of the approach.
- **Potential for Other Mobile Manipulation Platforms:** The authors note that the method's command structure for the lower body (base velocity and height) suggests its potential extension to other mobile manipulation platforms, such as quadrupedal or wheeled robots, broadening its impact beyond humanoids.

In summary, LAC represents a step forward in equipping humanoid robots with the sophisticated physical interaction capabilities necessary for their deployment in complex, real-world scenarios, fostering a future where robots can more effectively and safely work alongside humans and interact with their environments.
