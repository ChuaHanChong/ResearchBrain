# One-Policy-Fits-All: Geometry-Aware Action Latents for Cross-Embodiment Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The One-Policy-Fits-All (OPFA) framework introduces a method to learn a single visuomotor policy capable of cross-embodiment manipulation across diverse robotic end-effectors, including simple grippers and complex dexterous hands. It achieves this by developing a geometry-aware latent representation and a unified decoder, demonstrating robust generalization and few-shot learning capabilities in both simulated and real-world tasks.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation data is inherently coupled with specific end-effector morphology and kinematics, leading to significant variations in action spaces and hindering policy generalization across different robots.
- The substantial cost and time required to collect new, embodiment-specific data for each new robot limits scalability and creates data scarcity for learning robust manipulation skills.
- Existing cross-embodiment learning approaches for multifingered hands often rely on embodiment-specific decoders or human motion priors, which restrict data efficiency and can introduce action conflicts due to structural discrepancies.

## Method

- A Geometry-Aware Latent Representation (GaLR) is learned in an annotation-free manner by encoding the end-effector's 3D point cloud using multi-scale local structure encoding with KPConv and global gesture perception via a geometric transformer with semantic positional embeddings.
- A unified decoder, distinct from embodiment-specific decoders, predicts joint angles for a hypothetical 'universal hand model,' from which only the physically existing joints are selected for a specific embodiment, optimized end-to-end with the GaLR training framework.
- The learned GaLR is integrated into a visuomotor policy (e.g., Diffusion Policy 3) to enable cross-embodiment co-training, where the policy learns to map visual observations directly to the GaLR representation of target actions.

## Results

- OPFA demonstrated strong generalization across 9 end-effectors on a spray-picking task, showing an average improvement of 19.8% in success rate over 'Naive Co-training' in spatial generalization scenarios.
- Exhibited robust few-shot learning ability, achieving high success rates (e.g., XHand surpassed 80%) with only 8 new trajectories for a banana-picking task, outperforming baselines that struggled with limited data or faced training conflicts.
- Validated in real-world experiments with 4 different end-effectors across 7 diverse tasks, consistently achieving high success rates and outperforming baseline methods, particularly on challenging long-horizon and fine-motor control tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Directly encoding the end-effector's geometric structure into a latent action representation effectively decouples the visuomotor policy from specific physical morphologies, enabling joint training across a wide range of robot hands.
> - A unified decoder that predicts actions for a conceptual 'universal hand model' and then retargets them to specific embodiments allows for shared knowledge transfer without the need for separate, less data-efficient decoders.
> - The use of semantic positional embeddings within the geometric transformer enhances global gesture perception by providing a consistent structural understanding across diverse end-effector designs, aiding in robust latent space learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Imitation learning has emerged as a strategy for developing solutions for robotic manipulation tasks. However, its application in robotics faces challenges distinct from other domains like text or vision, primarily due to the physical characteristics of robotic end-effectors. Robotic manipulation data is inherently coupled with the specific morphology and kinematics of the robot arm and gripper or hand used for data collection. This coupling leads to two primary issues for policy generalization: significant variations in end-effector morphology and degrees of freedom (DoF) across different robots (e.g., simple grippers versus anthropomorphic dexterous hands), and the substantial cost associated with collecting new, embodiment-specific data for each new robot.

Existing approaches to cross-embodiment learning have largely focused on robot arms equipped with parallel-jaw grippers. Methods such as RT-1, RT-2, Octo, and RDT-1B aim to unify action spaces or use modular adapters to handle data heterogeneity at the arm-gripper level. These methods typically standardize end-effector motions and gripper states or learn shared latent spaces to bridge morphological gaps between different arms. While effective for simpler grippers, extending these strategies to dexterous hands with higher DoF and complex geometries presents additional challenges.

For multifingered robotic hands, cross-embodiment learning is more complex. Common strategies include training embodiment-specific decoders, which limits data efficiency as each decoder only learns from its specific embodiment's data. Other works leverage human motion priors, often by retargeting human hand models (e.g., MANO-based features) to robotic hands. However, relying on pre-defined human hand models may introduce action conflicts due to structural discrepancies between human and robotic hands.

This work, One-Policy-Fits-All (OPFA), seeks to address these limitations by developing a framework that enables learning a single, versatile policy across a wide range of end-effectors, including both simple grippers and complex dexterous hands. It differentiates itself by creating a geometry-aware latent representation that is learned directly from the end-effector's physical characteristics, rather than relying on external human hand models or embodiment-specific decoders. This approach aims to facilitate joint training across diverse robot data, improve data efficiency, and enhance generalization capabilities, particularly in few-shot learning scenarios for new embodiments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, One-Policy-Fits-All (OPFA), that enables learning a single, versatile visuomotor policy capable of cross-embodiment manipulation. This policy is intended to operate across a diverse range of robotic end-effectors, including both simple grippers and complex dexterous hands with varying degrees of freedom.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges inherent in current robotic manipulation learning paradigms:

1. **Heterogeneity of Action Spaces and Structural Disparities:** Robotic end-effectors exhibit substantial differences in their morphology and kinematics. From two-finger parallel grippers to multi-fingered anthropomorphic hands, the number of joints, their movement ranges, and their overall physical structures vary significantly. These differences lead to fundamentally distinct action spaces for each embodiment, which hinders the direct joint training of data collected from multiple, different robots. Current methods often address this by training separate action heads or decoders for each embodiment, which restricts the sharing of learned knowledge across systems.

2. **High Cost and Data Scarcity:** Learning robust manipulation skills typically requires large datasets of demonstrations. When a new end-effector is introduced into a robotic system, a new large dataset often needs to be collected specifically for that embodiment. This data collection process is costly, time-consuming, and limits the scalability of robot learning to new hardware. The research is motivated by the need to alleviate this data scarcity problem by enabling effective skill transfer and few-shot learning for new end-effectors.

3. **Inefficient Utilization of Cross-Embodiment Data:** Despite the existence of data from various robotic systems, current methods often struggle to effectively leverage this heterogeneous data for joint training. The "embodiment gap"—the pronounced structural and action space differences—prevents seamless co-training, leading to policies that are often specific to the robot they were trained on. A unified policy that could co-train data without conflicts would significantly improve data efficiency and lead to more generalizable and versatile policies.

4. **Limitations of Prior Generalization Approaches:** Previous attempts at cross-embodiment learning for dexterous hands often rely on human motion priors or embodiment-specific decoders. Relying on pre-defined human hand models can induce action conflicts due to inherent structural discrepancies between human and robot hands. Training separate decoders for each embodiment, while providing some flexibility, prevents the decoders from fully exploiting shared information across embodiments, potentially leading to overfitting in data-scarce scenarios and limiting generalization.

In addressing these motivations, OPFA aims to enable end-to-end co-training of data from diverse embodiments, significantly improving data efficiency, reducing the cost of skill transfer, and facilitating rapid adaptation to new robotic hardware. The core idea is to decouple policy learning from a specific end-effector by establishing a shared, geometry-aware latent action representation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The One-Policy-Fits-All (OPFA) framework adopts a two-stage paradigm for enabling cross-embodiment manipulation across diverse end-effectors.

#### 3.1. A. Learning Geometry-Aware Latent Representation (GaLR)

The first stage focuses on constructing a Geometry-Aware Latent Representation (GaLR), which is designed to unify the action spaces of different end-effectors by encoding their geometric structures. This stage is entirely annotation-free, as all training data are generated automatically.

1. **Data Generation:** For each embodiment `m`, a set of reachable states (joint angles, `a^m`) is sampled. These joint angles are then used with the forward kinematics function `f^m_FK` of the specific embodiment to obtain a corresponding 3D point cloud `P` representing the end-effector's configuration. The training data consists of pairs `{(a^m, P)}`.

2. **Multi-scale Local Structure Encoding:**
 - The raw point cloud `P` can be dense, leading to redundancy and computational inefficiency. To address this, a multi-stage downsampling process is applied, resulting in multi-scale spatial features. The process generates downsampled points `~P` and then further downsampled superpoints `^P`, where `|^P| < |~P| < |P|`.
 - 3D convolutional networks, specifically KPConv, are used for multi-scale feature extraction on these downsampled point clouds. KPConv operates by defining kernel points `{~x_k}` within a ball of radius `r` around a query point `x`. The convolution output at `x` is a weighted sum of features from neighboring points `x_i`, where weights `h(y_i, ~x_k)` depend on the proximity between the relative coordinate `y_i = x_i - x` and the kernel points `~x_k`. This process effectively captures local geometric features.

3. **Global Gesture Perception:**
 - Following the 3D convolution, a geometric transformer is applied at the superpoint level `^P` to capture global gesture-aware representations.
 - To address positional ambiguity that might arise from directly applying cross-attention over superpoints, two types of positional embeddings are utilized:
 - **Naive Coordinate Positional Embedding (`r_p`):** Standard coordinate-based positional information.
 - **Semantic Positional Embedding (`r_s`):** This embedding provides a unified structural encoding across different embodiments by incorporating spatial semantics. Each superpoint `p` is assigned a 2D semantic index `π(p) = (u_p, v_p)`, where `u_p` denotes the finger-level index (e.g., palm, thumb, index finger) and `v_p` denotes the segment-level index along that finger. This 2D index vector `s_p = [u_p, v_p]^T` is projected into the feature space `r_s = s_p W_S`.
 - These two positional embeddings (`r_p` and `r_s`) are combined and fed into the geometric transformer along with the superpoint features `f_p`.
 - Finally, the refined superpoint features `{~f_p}` are aggregated via global average pooling to obtain the GaLR, denoted as `z ∈ R^(d_latent)`. This `z` serves as the unified latent action representation.

4. **Unified Decoder with Latent Retargeting:**
 - A key component of OPFA is a unified decoder `g_ψ` that predicts embodiment-specific joint angles from the latent vector `z`. Unlike prior methods that train separate decoders for each embodiment, this unified decoder handles all diverse end-effectors.
 - The decoder `g_ψ` predicts all joint angles `^Θ` for a hypothetical "universal hand model" `H`. This model `H` is conceptual and contains every physically meaningful joint that could exist across all considered end-effectors (e.g., thumb yaw, index finger flexion).
 - For a specific embodiment `m`, only the joints that physically exist on that hand are selected from the predicted `^Θ` to form `^Θ^m`.
 - The entire GaLR training framework is optimized end-to-end using an RMSE loss between the recovered joint angles `^Θ^m` and the ground-truth joint angles `a^m`.

#### 3.2. B. Cross-Embodiment Policy Co-Training

The second stage integrates the learned GaLR into a visuomotor policy for cross-embodiment co-training.

1. **GaLR Integration:** The GaLR `z` acts as a general latent action representation, unifying the training and inference processes for different end-effectors. For each embodiment `m`, the function `G` maps hand-specific joint angles `a^m` to their corresponding GaLR `z`, i.e., `G = f_θ ◦ f^m_FK`.
 The visuomotor policy `π` is modified to learn a latent policy `π^m : o^m_t → G(a^m_t) ∈ R^(d_latent)`, mapping visual observations `o^m_t` to the GaLR representation of the target action.

2. **Base Policy Agnostic:** OPFA is designed to be compatible with various policy architectures, such as ACT or Diffusion Policy (DP/DP3). In this work, DP3 is adopted as the underlying policy backbone. The required modifications are to transform the action prediction process into GaLR prediction and replace any state terms in the observation with their corresponding GaLR representation `G(s^m_t)`.

3. **Training and Inference:** During training, the denoising process typical of diffusion policies is applied in the latent space of GaLR. During inference, the trained DP3 model directly predicts the GaLR, which is then fed into the pre-trained unified decoder to recover the embodiment-specific joint angles `^Θ^m`. This allows the policy to learn embodiment-agnostic visuomotor skills from a combined dataset of heterogeneous embodiments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted validate OPFA's performance in cross-embodiment manipulation across diverse settings, focusing on generalization and few-shot learning capabilities.

#### 4.1. A. Experimental Setup

- **Embodiments:** 11 different end-effectors were evaluated, encompassing two-finger grippers (UMI, Robotiq-2F), three-finger (Robotiq-3F), four-finger (Leap, Allegro), and five-finger hands (Inspire Hand, XHand, Shadow Hand, Ability Hand, Schunk SVH Hand).
- **Tasks:** 14 manipulation tasks were used, including kettle-pulling, button-pressing, bucket-lifting, pick&place in simulation, and real-world tasks such as basket pick-and-place, tissue pulling, broom sweeping, pot pouring, mango pick-and-place, drawer closing, and syringe pressing.
- **Baselines:**
 - **w/o Co-training:** A policy trained only on data from the specific tested embodiment.
 - **Naive Co-training:** A cross-embodiment co-training method that assigns a separate decoder to each embodiment, similar to some existing approaches.

#### 4.2. B. Generalization Across Different Embodiments

The research evaluated OPFA's ability for cross-embodiment generalization under two conditions: spatial generalization and object generalization.

1. **Spatial Generalization:**
 - **Setup:** For tasks like spray-picking, training data (72 trajectories per embodiment) for different embodiments were collected in distinct spatial regions. Policies were then evaluated on regions covered by data from *other* co-training embodiments.
 - **Results (Inspire Hand & XHand, Table I):** The "w/o Co-training" baseline largely failed, exhibiting poor positional generalization due to unseen test regions. The "Naive Co-training" baseline showed partial improvement by leveraging broader wrist-level knowledge from multiple embodiments. However, it struggled with fine-grained end-effector spatial information, which separate decoders discard. OPFA, by co-training in a shared, geometry-aware latent action space, enabled joint exploitation of both wrist-level and end-effector-level information. This resulted in strong generalization, achieving success rates above 90% on tasks such as bucket-lifting and banana-picking, even on unseen test data.
 - **Results (9 End-effectors, Figure 3):** On a spray-picking task with nine end-effectors and nine distinct subregions for training data, OPFA significantly improved success rates for all embodiments, demonstrating an average improvement of 19.8% over the "Naive Co-training" method, while the "w/o Co-training" baseline failed completely.

2. **Object Generalization:**
 - **Setup:** Inspire Hand data grasping a can and XHand data grasping a spray were co-trained, and then tested for cross-object generalization.
 - **Results (Table I):** OPFA outperformed the baselines, achieving a 26% improvement over "Naive Co-training" on Inspire Hand and an 18% improvement on XHand, indicating its capability for transferring skills across different objects.

#### 4.3. C. Few-Shot Learning Ability

The ability of OPFA to learn new skills with limited data for new end-effectors was also evaluated.

1. **Setup:** Experiments were conducted on the banana-picking task with Inspire Hand and XHand. Subsets of 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 18, and 36 trajectories from one hand were co-trained with all 72 trajectories from the other hand.
2. **Results (Figure 4):** OPFA demonstrated rapid performance improvement with increasing numbers of sampled trajectories. With only 8 new trajectories, Inspire Hand achieved a high success rate, and XHand surpassed 80%, approaching the performance of models trained with 72 demonstrations. In contrast, "Naive Co-training" sometimes performed worse than "w/o Co-training" as sample size increased, indicating potential issues with training inhibition or overfitting.
3. **Results (9 End-effectors, Figure 5):** When extending to nine end-effectors, each provided with only eight trajectories for a spray-picking task, OPFA showed substantial accuracy improvements. It achieved a 93% success rate on Leap Hand and an average success rate of 62.1%, representing a gain of over 20% compared to "Naive Co-training." The "Naive Co-training" method reportedly suffered from conflicts due to significant geometric gaps between diverse grippers and dexterous hands, sometimes underperforming "w/o Co-training."

#### 4.4. D. Real-World Experiments

Real-world experiments further substantiated OPFA's performance.

1. **Setup:** A UR5e arm with four different end-effectors (XHand, Inspire Hand, Leap Hand, Robotiq-2F gripper) and a Microsoft Azure Kinect depth camera was used. Seven manipulation tasks were designed, including pick-and-place, contact-intensive, long-horizon sequential, and fine-motor control tasks. 24 demonstrations were collected per embodiment per task, with 10 evaluation trials per task.
2. **Results (Table II):** OPFA demonstrated consistently high success rates across most evaluated tasks and embodiments. On challenging long-horizon tasks (e.g., Drawer) and fine-grained control tasks (e.g., Pot), OPFA consistently outperformed baseline methods. For instance, on the Pot task, "Naive Co-training" performed worse than "w/o Co-training" for some hands, suggesting that structural differences induced action conflicts. OPFA's ability to share geometric knowledge across diverse end-effectors enabled a level of performance that embodiment-specific heads could not achieve, particularly in data-efficient settings. The framework also exhibited task-agnostic skill transfer, generalizing across deformable (Tissue) and rigid (Broom) objects, as well as pick&place (Mango) and dexterous (Syringe) tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The One-Policy-Fits-All (OPFA) framework represents an advancement in robotic manipulation learning, particularly in addressing the challenges of cross-embodiment skill transfer and data efficiency. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enhanced Data Efficiency and Scalability:** A core contribution of OPFA is its ability to effectively co-train data from diverse robotic embodiments, ranging from simple grippers to complex dexterous hands. This capability significantly reduces the reliance on large, embodiment-specific datasets, thereby lowering the cost and time associated with data collection for new robots. This data efficiency is crucial for scaling robot learning solutions to a wider variety of hardware, making advanced manipulation skills more accessible and deployable.

2. **Improved Policy Generalization:** By learning a Geometry-Aware Latent Representation (GaLR), OPFA enables policies to generalize across substantial variations in end-effector morphology and action spaces. The demonstrated ability to generalize spatially (to unseen regions) and across different objects highlights a step towards more robust and adaptable robot policies. This generalization capability means that a policy trained on a heterogeneous dataset can perform tasks in novel environments or with different objects without extensive re-training.

3. **Strong Few-Shot Learning Capabilities:** The framework's ability to achieve high performance with a limited number of demonstrations for a new embodiment is particularly impactful. This "few-shot learning" directly addresses the problem of introducing new hardware into existing robotic systems, allowing rapid adaptation and deployment without prohibitive data collection efforts. This accelerates the iterative development and deployment cycle for robotic applications.

4. **Unified and End-to-End Learning Paradigm:** OPFA introduces an end-to-end co-training method that does not require embodiment-specific decoder tuning, which simplifies the learning pipeline. The unified latent retargeting decoder is a methodological advancement that facilitates shared learning across embodiments, contrasting with prior approaches that often relied on separate decoders, thereby limiting knowledge transfer.

5. **Robustness to Structural Discrepancies:** The explicit encoding of geometric structure in GaLR and the unified decoder's ability to map to a hypothetical universal hand model appear to mitigate action conflicts that can arise from significant structural differences between end-effectors. This is particularly relevant for dexterous hands, where naive alignment or separate decoders can lead to suboptimal performance due to the complexity of their kinematics.

6. **Real-World Applicability:** The extensive validation in real-world environments across multiple robotic platforms and diverse, challenging manipulation tasks (including long-horizon and fine-motor control) suggests that OPFA is not merely a theoretical concept but has practical utility. Its consistent performance improvements over baselines in realistic settings indicate its potential for direct application in industrial, service, and research robotics.

In summary, OPFA contributes to the field by offering a more unified, data-efficient, and generalizable approach to robot manipulation learning. It represents a move towards developing "one-policy-fits-all" solutions, which could pave the way for more versatile and rapidly deployable robotic systems in complex and varied environments.
