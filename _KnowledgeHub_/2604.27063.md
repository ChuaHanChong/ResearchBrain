# Learning to Forget: Continual Learning with Adaptive Weight Decay

> [!summary] Summary
> Forgetting through Adaptive DEcay (FADE) automates the management of knowledge retention in neural networks by dynamically adjusting individual parameter decay rates, demonstrating enhanced learning performance and stability in online continual learning scenarios.

## Problem

- Continual learning models struggle with 'catastrophic forgetting,' where acquiring new knowledge leads to the loss of previously learned information due to finite capacity.
- The 'stability-plasticity trade-off' is difficult to manage, requiring a balance between retaining old knowledge and adapting to new tasks.
- Conventional weight decay uses a single, fixed rate uniformly across all parameters, which is inefficient for selectively forgetting irrelevant or outdated information in non-stationary environments.

## Method

- **FADE** implements ==per-parameter adaptive weight decay==.
- Each weight has its own decay rate, dynamically updated online via ==meta-gradient descent==, derived for linear models and applied to the final layer of neural networks.
- Combinable with adaptive optimizers like ==IDBD== or ==Adam==, enabling simultaneous adaptation of learning and forgetting rates.

## Results

- **FADE** achieved up to **50% lower** prediction error in non-linear tracking and **over 30% higher** online accuracy on streaming image classification.
- Enhanced robustness to hyperparameter initialization — recovers strong performance even from sub-optimal initial decay rates.
- Learned distinct per-parameter decay rates, rapidly forgetting irrelevant features while retaining relevant ones.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Controlled and adaptive forgetting, rather than just retention, is essential in continual learning to free up model capacity and effectively manage the stability-plasticity trade-off.
> - Per-parameter adaptive weight decay allows a model to automatically differentiate between relevant and irrelevant information, selectively retaining stable knowledge while discarding outdated parameters.
> - Adaptive decay rates complement adaptive learning rates, suggesting that optimally managing both how fast new information is integrated and how much old information is retained can lead to better continual learning performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of continual learning, a sub-area of machine learning focused on developing agents that can acquire new knowledge over time without forgetting previously learned information. A central challenge in continual learning is managing the "stability-plasticity trade-off," which involves balancing the retention of old knowledge (stability) with the ability to acquire new knowledge (plasticity).

Traditional approaches to mitigating forgetting, known as catastrophic forgetting, often involve regularizing parameters towards past solutions or employing replay buffers. However, some literature also recognizes the necessity of controlled forgetting, particularly in agents with finite capacity, to free up resources for new learning. Weight decay, a common regularization technique, is often viewed in stationary settings as a means to prevent overfitting and encourage simpler models. In online, non-stationary settings characteristic of continual learning, weight decay can also function as a forgetting mechanism by gradually driving weights towards zero, thereby discarding old information.

The existing limitation is that standard weight decay typically uses a single, fixed scalar rate applied uniformly across all parameters and over time. This uniform application is considered mismatched for continual learning scenarios where different parameters may encode knowledge with varying degrees of stability or relevance to changing tasks.

Previous work has explored adaptive weight decay for batch training in stationary settings, primarily for regularization and improved generalization. These methods often adapt decay rates across layers or based on gradient magnitudes. This paper differentiates itself by focusing on online continual learning where non-stationarity is the primary concern, and it proposes an adaptive, per-parameter weight decay mechanism specifically designed to facilitate controlled forgetting. The approach draws inspiration from meta-learning techniques, particularly those using meta-gradients for online adaptation of learning rates, such as IDBD (Incremental Delta-Bar-Delta), but applies these principles to weight decay rates instead. It extends the concept of "forget gates," commonly used in recurrent neural networks for short-term memory, to the long-term memories stored in network weights.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and evaluate an adaptive, per-parameter weight decay mechanism, termed Forgetting through Adaptive DEcay (FADE), designed for online continual learning settings.

The motivation for this work stems from several observations:

- **Necessity of Controlled Forgetting in Continual Learning:** In continual learning, agents with finite capacity cannot retain all past information indefinitely. Judicious forgetting of irrelevant or outdated knowledge is proposed as a necessary mechanism to free up capacity, thereby enabling the acquisition of new information and navigating the stability-plasticity trade-off more effectively. This contrasts with traditional views of forgetting as purely "catastrophic."
- **Limitations of Fixed Weight Decay:** While weight decay can act as a forgetting mechanism in online, non-stationary environments, its conventional form suffers from significant limitations:
 - **Uniformity across parameters:** A single scalar decay rate applies equally to all weights, regardless of whether they encode stable, long-term knowledge or rapidly changing task-specific information. This prevents selective retention or discarding of information.
 - **Constancy over time:** The fixed rate does not adapt to changes in the learning environment or the varying demands for forgetting/retention as tasks evolve.
- **Absence of Weight-Level Forgetting Mechanisms:** While recurrent neural networks like LSTMs employ learned "forget gates" for short-term memories stored in activations, a corresponding mechanism for long-term knowledge stored in slowly changing weights has largely been absent in the context of controlled forgetting.
- **Improved Robustness and Performance:** The authors hypothesize that an adaptive, per-parameter weight decay system could lead to improved learning performance and greater robustness to hyperparameter selection, especially the initial decay rate, in non-stationary settings. The goal is for the system to automatically discover appropriate forgetting rates for different parts of the model or different types of knowledge.

By addressing these limitations, the research aims to provide a more sophisticated and effective mechanism for controlled forgetting in continual learning agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of this work is the introduction of Forgetting through Adaptive DEcay (FADE), an algorithm that adapts per-parameter weight decay rates online using meta-gradient descent.

**Core Mechanism: Adaptive Per-Parameter Decay**
FADE replaces a single, static global weight decay hyperparameter with dynamic, per-parameter decay rates. Each weight $w_i$ is associated with its own decay rate $\lambda_i$. These rates are parameterized using meta-parameters $\gamma_i$, such that $\lambda_i = \exp(\gamma_i)$. This exponential mapping ensures that decay rates are positive. The meta-parameters $\gamma_i$ are then updated online by gradient descent on the prediction error.

**Meta-Gradient Derivation for Online Linear Regression**
The derivation of FADE is presented for the online linear regression setting, where the prediction is a linear function of weights.
1. **Weight Update Rule:** The standard weight update rule with per-parameter weight decay and the delta rule (equivalent to SGD for linear regression with squared error) is:
 $w_{i,t+1} = (1 - \lambda_{i,t+1}) w_{i,t} + \alpha \delta_t x_{i,t}$
 where $\alpha$ is the step size, $\delta_t$ is the prediction error, and $x_{i,t}$ is the $i$-th component of the input.
2. **Meta-Parameter Update ($\gamma_i$):** To adapt $\gamma_i$, the loss $J_t = \delta_t^2/2$ is differentiated with respect to $\gamma_i$. This involves a chain rule through the weight update. A key approximation, borrowed from IDBD (Sutton, 1992), is used: changing $\gamma_i$ primarily affects $w_i$ and has a negligible direct effect on other weights ($w_j$ for $j \neq i$). This approximation enables online updates using forward-mode differentiation.
 The update rule for $\gamma_i$ is:
 $\gamma_{i,t+1} \leftarrow \gamma_{i,t} + \theta_\lambda \delta_t x_{i,t} g_{i,t}$
 where $\theta_\lambda$ is the meta step size for decay rates.
3. **Sensitivity Trace Update ($g_i$):** The term $g_{i,t}$ is a "sensitivity trace" that tracks $\partial w_{i,t} / \partial \gamma_i$. Its update rule is derived by differentiating the weight update equation with respect to $\gamma_i$. This trace captures how sensitive $w_i$ is to changes in its associated decay meta-parameter.
 The update rule for $g_i$ is:
 $g_{i,t+1} \leftarrow g_{i,t} [1 - \lambda_{i,t+1} - \alpha (x_{i,t})^2]_+ - \lambda_{i,t+1} w_{i,t}$
 The `[.]_+` denotes `max(., 0)`, a positive-bounding operation for stability.

**Combination with Adaptive Step Sizes (FADE + IDBD)**
FADE can be combined with other adaptive optimization strategies. The paper specifically outlines its integration with IDBD, an algorithm that adaptively adjusts per-parameter learning rates (step sizes) using meta-gradients. In this combined approach, both the decay rate ($\lambda_i$) and the step size ($\alpha_i$) are adapted per-parameter. The derivation of the IDBD trace ($h_i$) is modified to account for the presence of adaptive weight decay.

**Computational Cost**
FADE adds two scalar states per parameter ($\gamma_i$ and $g_i$) and one scalar hyperparameter ($\theta_\lambda$). This maintains an $O(d)$ computational cost per step of online gradient descent, where $d$ is the number of parameters.

**FADE with Neural Networks**
While derived for the linear setting, FADE is applied to neural networks by targeting the final layer. The final layer of a neural network typically acts as a linear function of the hidden representation. This approach has been successfully employed in other meta-gradient methods.
- **Cross-Entropy Extension:** For classification tasks using cross-entropy loss, the derivative of the loss with respect to weights and logits is incorporated into the meta-gradient and trace updates. The `(x_{i,t})^2` term in the trace update is replaced by $p_{k,t}(1-p_{k,t})(x_{j,t})^2$, where $p_{k,t}$ is the softmax probability for class $k$.
- **Combination with Adam:** FADE can also be combined with Adam, a widely used optimizer, where Adam provides an effective per-parameter step size. The FADE trace update then incorporates this effective step size. This combination is an approximation, as it does not fully account for Adam's first moment (momentum).

**Experimental Setup**
The methodology is evaluated across three online non-stationary tasks:
1. **Linear Tracking:** A synthetic task where a linear model tracks a target with dynamically changing "relevant" weights. This tests FADE's ability to differentiate and adapt decay rates for relevant vs. irrelevant features and its complementarity with IDBD.
2. **Non-linear Teacher-Student Tracking:** A more complex setting where a student neural network learns to mimic a teacher network whose output layer exhibits different rates of non-stationarity (stable, slow-changing, fast-changing outputs). FADE is applied to the final layer of the student network.
3. **Streaming Image Classification with Label Permutation (EMNIST):** A standard continual learning benchmark where label mappings are periodically permuted. FADE is applied to the final classification layer of an MLP, evaluated on online accuracy. A variant with partial label permutations is also tested.

Hyperparameter tuning is performed via grid search for each method, with results averaged across multiple random seeds.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate that FADE consistently improves performance over fixed weight decay and other baselines across different online non-stationary learning problems, while also improving robustness to initial hyperparameter choices.

#### 4.1. Linear Tracking

- **FADE Discovers Distinct Decay Rates:** In the noiseless setting, FADE automatically differentiates between relevant and irrelevant features. Decay rates for irrelevant weights converged to a high value ($\lambda \approx 0.9$), effectively forgetting them, while rates for relevant weights settled at a low value ($\lambda \approx 0.02$), promoting retention (Figure 1).
- **Complementarity with Adaptive Step Sizes:** FADE combined with IDBD (FADE + IDBD) achieved the lowest Mean Squared Error (MSE) overall (1.246 for $\sigma_n=0$ and 2.646 for $\sigma_n=1.0$), outperforming either FADE or IDBD alone. This suggests that adaptive step sizes (controlling how quickly new information is incorporated) and adaptive decay rates (controlling how much past information is retained) are complementary mechanisms.
- **Performance Improvement:** FADE alone (1.653 MSE) showed a substantial improvement over SGD with fixed weight decay (2.726 MSE) in the noiseless setting. A 'coupled' variant where step size and decay were integrated into a single term performed slightly worse than the decoupled FADE+IDBD.

#### 4.2. Non-linear Tracking

- **Significant Performance Gains:** FADE, when applied to the final layer of a neural network, substantially reduced prediction error. FADE + SGD achieved an average MSE of 0.0073, roughly half the error of the best performing baseline, AdamW (0.0138). FADE + Adam also performed well, achieving 0.0087 MSE.
- **Adaptation Across Non-Stationarity Rates:** FADE successfully adapted decay rates across output units with different rates of non-stationarity (fast, slow, stable). Decay rates increased for fast-changing outputs and decreased for stable outputs, demonstrating effective selective forgetting and retention (Figure 3). The performance improvements were observed across all output groups (Figure 2).
- **Robustness to Initialization:** FADE significantly reduced the sensitivity to the initial decay rate ($\lambda_0$). With fixed decay ($\theta_\lambda = 0$), performance varied widely with $\gamma_0$. FADE, even starting from sub-optimal $\gamma_0$ values, recovered to achieve consistently strong performance (Tables 3 and 4). For example, from $\gamma_0 = -2.3$ (high initial decay), fixed decay yielded 0.0360 MSE, while FADE recovered to 0.0125 MSE.

#### 4.3. Streaming Image Classification with Label Permutation

- **Outperformance on Benchmark:** FADE + SGD achieved the highest average online accuracy (0.807), surpassing other methods, including SGD + Weight Clipping (0.612), which was reported as the best prior method on this problem. FADE + Adam also performed strongly at 0.750 accuracy (Table 5).
- **Importance of Head-Only Decay:** The experiments revealed that applying a suitable fixed non-zero weight decay rate *only* to the final layer (the head) could itself be a strong baseline (e.g., 0.801 accuracy with $\gamma_0 = -6.9$ for SGD), outperforming uniform weight decay. This emphasizes the benefit of differentiated decay rates for different network parts.
- **Enhanced Robustness:** FADE further improved upon this "head-only" baseline by making performance less dependent on the precise initial decay rate. With fixed decay on the head, performance could drop significantly with poor initialization (e.g., 0.531 accuracy for $\gamma_0 = -11.5$). FADE mitigated this, adapting from poor initializations to achieve higher accuracy (e.g., 0.750 from $\gamma_0 = -11.5$ for FADE + SGD, Figure 5, Tables 6 and 7).
- **Limitations of Full-Network FADE:** A naive extension of FADE to all layers of the network performed worse than head-only FADE, suggesting that the linear meta-gradient approximation derived for the output layer is insufficient for hidden layers.

#### 4.4. Streaming Classification with Partial Label Permutations

- FADE demonstrated strong performance in this mixed non-stationarity setting, with FADE+SGD achieving 0.841 average online accuracy, surpassing other baselines (Table 8). The benefits of adaptivity over fixed decay were even more pronounced than in the fully permuted setting.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces FADE, an online, per-parameter adaptive weight decay mechanism, demonstrating its utility in continual learning settings. The work contributes to the understanding and practical implementation of controlled forgetting in neural networks.

**Key Contributions and Implications:**
- **Novel Forgetting Mechanism:** FADE reframes weight decay as an active, adaptive forgetting mechanism rather than solely a regularization technique. By allowing each parameter to have its own decay rate, it provides a fine-grained control over how much past information is retained or discarded.
- **Addressing the Stability-Plasticity Trade-off:** The ability of FADE to automatically discover distinct decay rates for different types of knowledge (e.g., stable vs. rapidly changing features) directly addresses the stability-plasticity trade-off inherent in continual learning. It enables selective retention of stable knowledge while facilitating rapid adaptation to new information by quickly forgetting outdated parameters.
- **Improved Performance in Online Non-Stationary Settings:** FADE consistently improves performance across diverse online continual learning tasks, including linear tracking, non-linear teacher-student learning, and streaming image classification. Its significant gains over established baselines, such as AdamW and weight clipping, indicate its practical effectiveness.
- **Enhanced Robustness to Hyperparameter Choices:** A notable impact is FADE's ability to reduce sensitivity to the initial decay rate. This robustness simplifies hyperparameter tuning and makes the approach more practical for deployment in dynamic environments where optimal fixed decay rates are unknown or difficult to determine.
- **Complementarity with Other Adaptive Methods:** The finding that FADE complements per-parameter step-size adaptation (IDBD) suggests that a comprehensive continual learning agent might benefit from simultaneously optimizing both how fast it learns new information and how much old information it retains.
- **Highlighting Non-Uniform Decay:** The research implicitly highlights the importance of non-uniform weight decay across different parts of a neural network. The observation that a well-tuned fixed decay on the final layer alone can be a strong baseline, further automated and improved by FADE, suggests that future optimization efforts for weight decay might benefit from layer- or parameter-specific treatments.
- **Minimal Computational Overhead:** FADE integrates with standard optimizers with minimal additional computational cost, making it a feasible addition to existing continual learning frameworks.

**Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges limitations, particularly the challenge of extending FADE's linear meta-gradient approximation to hidden layers of deep neural networks. Future work could focus on developing more generalizable meta-gradient approximations that are invariant to architectural specifics, enabling end-to-end adaptive decay throughout deep models. Such advancements could expand FADE's applicability to complex architectures and real-world scenarios like reinforcement learning where non-stationarity is common.
