# The Evolution of Tool Use in LLM Agents: From Single-Tool Call to Multi-Tool Orchestration

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey systematically reviews the evolution of Large Language Model agents from single-tool calls to complex multi-tool orchestration, unifying task formulations and analyzing advancements across six critical dimensions. It identifies key challenges and future research directions for developing reliable, scalable, and verifiable multi-tool agents for real-world applications.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) are inherently limited by static parametric knowledge, hallucination, and inability to interact with external environments.
- The existing literature suffers from conceptual ambiguity, using various terms like "tool use" and "orchestration" interchangeably despite distinct capabilities.
- Research on LLM agents is fragmented, with critical aspects like planning, training, and safety often studied in isolation, hindering holistic system development.

## Method

- The authors establish a formal problem formulation for ==multi-tool orchestration== to provide a unified conceptual basis for research.
- They systematically organize the extensive literature into ==six core dimensions==, covering ==inference==, ==training==, ==safety==, ==efficiency==, ==completeness==, and ==benchmarking==.
- The survey identifies and summarizes representative applications, major challenges, and future research directions to guide the development of robust ==multi-tool agents==.

## Results

- Inference-time paradigms have advanced to include graph-structured execution, hierarchical planning, dynamic memory management, and agent self-improvement capabilities.
- Efficiency improvements are achieved through parallel execution, asynchronous decoupling, speculative reasoning, dynamic tool search, and adaptive model routing.
- Benchmarks now emphasize system-level evaluation, assessing topological complexity, temporal scale in long-horizon tasks, adaptation to dynamic environments, and mechanisms for state persistence and self-correction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Advanced LLM agents require a shift from isolated tool invocation to complex multi-tool orchestration involving topology-aware planning and hierarchical delegation.
> - Training paradigms have moved towards sophisticated data synthesis, large-scale supervised fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning to optimize multi-tool decision-making.
> - Safety in multi-tool systems necessitates robust defense mechanisms against parallel execution risks (e.g., race conditions) and long-horizon threats (e.g., state contamination, memory poisoning).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated advanced reasoning and generation capabilities; however, they are inherently limited by their static parametric knowledge, susceptibility to hallucination, and inability to interact directly with external environments. Tool learning emerged to address these limitations by enabling LLMs to invoke external APIs, search engines, and code interpreters, thereby establishing a perception-action loop. Early research in this domain focused on fundamental aspects such as a model's ability to select and execute a correct *single* tool call, laying the groundwork with methods like TALM, MRKL, Toolformer, and ReAct.

As LLM agent systems have evolved and tasks have grown in complexity, the focus of research has shifted. The current landscape necessitates agents capable of coordinating *multiple* tools over extended trajectories, managing intermediate states, handling execution feedback, adapting to changing environments, and adhering to practical constraints such such as safety, cost, and verifiability. This transition from isolated tool invocation to complex multi-tool orchestration constitutes a significant paradigm shift. This survey paper positions itself within this evolving landscape by providing a comprehensive review and analysis of recent progress in multi-tool LLM agents. It distinguishes itself from existing surveys by focusing specifically on multi-tool orchestration as the primary unit of analysis, organizing the field across six interconnected dimensions, and clarifying conceptual boundaries often conflated in prior work.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to comprehensively review and analyze the state of the art in multi-tool LLM agents, specifically focusing on the evolution from single-tool calls to multi-tool orchestration. The authors aim to unify task formulations, distinguish between single-call tool use and long-horizon orchestration, and organize the extensive literature around core dimensions that characterize advanced agent capabilities. Furthermore, the paper seeks to summarize representative applications and identify major challenges and future directions for building reliable, scalable, and verifiable multi-tool agents.

This survey is motivated by two identified gaps in the existing literature. The first is conceptual ambiguity: various terms such as "tool use," "tool calling," "tool retrieval," "workflow execution," and "orchestration" are often used interchangeably, despite referring to distinct levels of capability. The authors contend that a clearer demarcation of these concepts is necessary to advance research. The second motivation stems from a structural fragmentation in the research: critical aspects of agent development, including planning, training, safety, efficiency, benchmarking, and open-environment adaptation, are frequently studied in isolation. However, the successful deployment of agent systems depends on the synergistic interaction of these components. By providing a unified framework, the paper intends to bridge these disciplinary divides and offer a holistic perspective on the challenges and advancements in multi-tool orchestration. The overarching motivation is to provide theoretical references and technical pathways for the development of robust, efficient, and scalable multi-tool agent systems that can effectively tackle complex real-world problems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

As a comprehensive review paper, the methodology primarily involves a structured and systematic analysis of existing literature on multi-tool LLM agents. The authors delineate a formal problem formulation for multi-tool orchestration to establish a unified conceptual basis.

**Problem Formulation:**
A multi-tool learning instance is defined by:
- A query or task ($q \in Q$).
- An interactive environment with a latent state ($s_t \in S$) and observation ($o_t \in O$) at each step ($t$).
- A tool inventory ($D = \{d_i\}_{i=1}^N$), where each tool ($d$) is described by a schema/signature ($\Sigma_d$).
- A tool execution interface `Exec(d, u; s) → (f, s')`, which takes a tool ($d$), valid arguments ($u$), and environment state ($s$), returning tool feedback ($f$) and the post-call environment state ($s'$).
- A model or policy ($\pi_\theta$) with parameters ($\theta$).
- A tool-use trajectory ($\tau$) and a cost functional `Cost(τ)` (e.g., number of calls, latency, API fees, risk).

The interaction history up to time $t$ is denoted as $h_t := (q, o_0, a_0, f_0, o_1, a_1, f_1, \dots, o_t)$, where $a_k$ is the action and $f_k$ is the feedback. The model's internal memory/state ($m_t$) is updated based on previous memory and current history. At each step, the model selects an action $a_t \in A$, which can be a tool call $(d_t, u_t)$ or `Finish(y)` (terminating with final answer $y$). The policy acts conditioned on history and memory: $a_t \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot | h_t, m_t)$. If a tool is called, the environment provides feedback and transitions to a new state. A trajectory $\tau$ is a sequence of actions and feedback, culminating in a final answer. The objective is to maximize an expected task reward while penalizing cost: `max_θ E_h [R_task(q, τ, y) - λ · Cost(τ)]`. This formulation highlights long-horizon scheduling, failure recovery, and replanning under dynamic feedback.

**Literature Organization:**
The authors organize the literature around six core dimensions, reflecting a transition from single-call correctness to system-level reliability:

1. **Inference-Time Planning and Execution:** This dimension explores how LLM agents reason and execute actions, covering shifts from linear to topological planning, explicit search algorithms, reflection, self-improvement, and system-level orchestration with persistent memory.
2. **Training and Trajectory Construction:** This section examines methodologies for adapting LLMs to multi-tool scenarios, including training-free methods, synthetic data generation, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning (RL) frameworks.
3. **Safety and Control:** This dimension investigates security challenges and defense mechanisms in multi-tool orchestration, distinguishing between risks in parallel execution (e.g., race conditions, state inconsistencies) and long-horizon tool chains (e.g., context drift, memory poisoning).
4. **Efficiency under Resource Constraints:** This section addresses bottlenecks related to latency in multi-tool chains (e.g., parallel execution, asynchronous decoupling, speculative reasoning) and the management of tool call costs and inference budgets (e.g., dynamic tool retrieval, adaptive model routing, intelligent caching).
5. **Capability Completeness in Open Environments:** This dimension explores how agents perceive their own capability boundaries, autonomously expand their toolsets (e.g., tool creation, skill synthesis), and adapt to dynamic, open-ended environments through experience and memory.
6. **Benchmark Design and Evaluation:** This section reviews the evolution of evaluation standards, moving from single-point functional verification to system-level topological orchestration, temporal scale, dynamic environments, and mechanisms for state persistence and self-correction.

Finally, the paper summarizes representative applications across domains like software engineering, enterprise workflows, graphical user interfaces, and mobile systems, abstracting common orchestration patterns and engineering constraints.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper's main findings are presented as an comprehensive overview of the current state and evolutionary trends within each of the six core dimensions of multi-tool LLM agents:

#### 4.1. Inference-Time Paradigms

The field has moved beyond sequential tool usage (e.g., ReAct) towards **topology-aware planning**, utilizing graph-structured execution (e.g., ToolNet, StructuredAgent) to manage non-linear dependencies, parallel operations, and reduce search space. For long-horizon tasks, monolithic planning is being replaced by **hierarchical delegation** (e.g., HIPLAN, ADaPT) and **state-space search** methods (e.g., Smurfs, AB-MCTS) to mitigate context contamination and improve decision quality. **Long-horizon orchestration** emphasizes system-level architectures with cognitive division of labor (e.g., MARS, CodeTool), standardized semantic routing (e.g., HuggingGPT), and **dynamic memory** (e.g., COMPASS, MIRIX) to handle large tool ecosystems and persistent state. Furthermore, **agent self-improvement** mechanisms (e.g., Reflexion, SPIRAL) are increasingly vital, enabling agents to diagnose and revise their own trajectories, and even evolve capabilities through tool creation and knowledge distillation (e.g., MetaAgent, Test-Time Tool Evolution).

#### 4.2. Tuning Paradigms

Adaptation strategies for LLMs range from **training-free methods** (e.g., ToolLLM, AnyTool) that use external augmentation (retrieval, prompt engineering, non-parametric memory) to activate tool-calling capabilities without parameter updates, to more computationally intensive approaches. **Multi-tool trajectory data synthesis** (e.g., Seal-Tools, BUTTON) has become dominant for creating high-quality training data, evolving towards a "Synthesis-Validation-Expansion" framework that covers complex interactions and error recovery. **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)** (e.g., Gorilla, ToolLLM) has transformed from merely ensuring syntax compliance to enabling tool selection from massive repositories and inter-tool dependency planning. Finally, **Reinforcement Learning (RL)** (e.g., PORTool, ToolRL) is employed to optimize high-level decision-making, address sparse rewards, credit assignment, and training instability, developing specialized paradigms for process reward design and turn-level policy optimization.

#### 4.3. Safety

Security concerns have shifted from text generation to harmful actions in environments. **Parallel execution** introduces risks like race conditions and state inconsistencies, addressed by pre-execution static constraints (e.g., AARM), in-execution transaction management (e.g., SagaLLM, Atomix), and post-execution dynamic verification (e.g., CRITIC, DVR). In **long-horizon tool chains**, threats include state contamination, context drift, and memory/plan poisoning (e.g., MINJA attacks, Butterfly Effects). Defenses involve trajectory-level dynamic intervention (e.g., TRICE, LATS), closed-loop auditing, and specialized RL-based safety alignment in sandbox environments.

#### 4.4. Efficiency

Efficiency bottlenecks in multi-tool agents are tackled by mitigating **latency** and managing **costs**. Latency reduction involves **parallel execution** (e.g., SoT, LLM-Compiler) to run independent subtasks concurrently, **asynchronous decoupling** (e.g., MACI, Temac) to separate planning from I/O-intensive execution, and **speculative reasoning** (e.g., ReWoo, LATS) to predict and pre-execute actions. **Tool call cost and inference budget** management focuses on **dynamic tool search** (e.g., ToolLLM, AnyTool) to retrieve relevant tools, **adaptive model routing** (e.g., FrugalGPT, RouteLLM) to match model size to task difficulty, and **intelligent caching and memory** (e.g., Reflexion, MemGPT) to reuse results and avoid redundant inferences.

#### 4.5. Completeness

Completeness addresses situations where predefined toolkits are insufficient. It involves **capability boundary perception** (e.g., FAIL-TALMS, AskToAct) to identify missing tools or ambiguous intents and safely terminate. **Autonomous tool expansion** (e.g., LATM, CREATOR, ToolMaker) allows agents to dynamically construct temporary tools or composite skills. **Open environment adaptation** (e.g., Voyager, ExpeL) enables agents to accumulate successful tools and interaction traces into long-term experience, supporting dynamic updates and lifelong learning in evolving environments.

#### 4.6. Benchmarks

Evaluation has evolved from isolated API correctness to **system-level intelligence**. Benchmarks now focus on:
- **Topological complexity:** Assessing reasoning over non-linear, nested, and graph-shaped tool dependencies (e.g., NESTFUL, ToolHop, TaskBench).
- **Temporal scale:** Evaluating performance in long-horizon tasks (e.g., Tool Decathlon, UltraHorizon) to expose memory decay, context saturation, and error accumulation.
- **Dynamic environments:** Testing adaptation to changing conditions via stateful interactive simulators (e.g., ToolSandbox, AppWorld), human-in-the-loop systems (e.g., $\tau$-bench), and real-world OS/web environments (e.g., OSWorld, Windows Agent Arena).
- **State persistence and self-correction:** Measuring the ability to maintain intermediate artifacts, remember prior commitments, detect drift, and recover from partial failures (e.g., OdysseyBench, CRITICTOOL).

#### 4.7. Applications

Real-world applications across software engineering, enterprise workflows, graphical user interfaces, and mobile systems demand closed-loop orchestration, iterative execution, and strict verification. The paper emphasizes a unified abstraction for application-side orchestration, highlighting the importance of execution environments, topologies, closed-loop control, and operational constraints like functional correctness, cost, safety, and verifiability.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey paper significantly contributes to the field by systematically synthesizing the rapidly evolving domain of multi-tool LLM agents. Its primary significance lies in providing a structured conceptual framework that clarifies the paradigm shift from single-tool calls to multi-tool orchestration. By defining and organizing the literature around six core dimensions, the paper offers a holistic view of the interconnected challenges and advancements, which can guide future research and development efforts.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. By identifying specific gaps and proposing a future research agenda, the paper provides a roadmap for developing more reliable, scalable, and verifiable multi-tool agents. Specifically:
- **For Researchers:** The unified task formulations, clear conceptual distinctions, and organized literature review serve as a valuable reference, enabling researchers to identify underexplored areas, understand interdependencies between different aspects of agent design, and build upon existing foundations more effectively. The emphasis on system-level reliability, rather than isolated capabilities, encourages a more integrated approach to agent development.
- **For Practitioners and Engineers:** The analysis of operational efficiency, safety, and completeness, alongside a review of applications, directly informs the design and deployment of industrial-grade multi-tool agents. The discussion on engineering essentials and governance for industrial orchestration (e.g., tool governance, continuous observability, cost-latency budget management) provides practical insights for building robust systems that can operate under real-world constraints.
- **Advancing LLM Agent Capabilities:** The paper highlights the necessity of moving beyond stronger base models to focus on advancements in memory, orchestration, verification, and benchmark design. Progress in these areas, as outlined in the paper's future directions, is critical for LLM agents to transition from promising prototypes to dependable, auditable, and scalable systems capable of addressing complex tasks in diverse domains. The challenges identified, such as mitigating behavioral drift, establishing process-level verifiability, optimizing cost/latency, and developing robust failure recovery, are crucial for realizing the full potential of LLM-powered autonomous agents in professional environments.
