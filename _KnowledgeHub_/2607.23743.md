# Learning Traversability-Aware Global Planners for Long Horizon Off-Road Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> A framework for long-horizon off-road navigation learns continuous traversability maps and goal-conditioned route preferences by integrating satellite imagery, aerial LiDAR, and OpenStreetMap data via a dual-stream neural network. The system achieved an 85% reduction in operator interventions and a 21-43% improvement in path fidelity to human trajectories compared to baseline methods in field trials and benchmarks.

## Problem

- Off-road autonomous navigation struggles with long-range planning due to unstructured environments, absence of road networks, and limited perception range of onboard sensors.
- Effectively leveraging large-scale geospatial data for traversability estimation is difficult due to the lack of dense, pixel-level negative labels.
- Existing traversability methods either lack integration of human navigation intent or fail to provide explicit physical grounding in terrain constraints.

## Method

- A ==dual-stream, multi-modal neural network== processes satellite imagery, aerial LiDAR data (height, slope, intensity), and OpenStreetMap features.
- It predicts a continuous ==traversability map== and goal-conditioned path likelihoods, supervised by human GPS trajectories using a ==Positive-Unlabeled (PU) loss==.
- ==Self-supervised geometric priors== from LiDAR (intensity, slope, height-gradient) are integrated into the loss function to ensure physical feasibility.

## Results

- Achieved a Discrete Fréchet distance of **61.3 m** to human paths, showing a **21-43%** improvement over baseline planning methods in a costmap benchmark.
- Reduced operator interventions by approximately **85%** (from one every **140m** to one every **480m**) in real-world field trials compared to local-planner-only autonomy.
- The learned costmaps support generating multiple distinct and feasible long-horizon routes, and enable successful autonomous replanning around unforeseen obstacles.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning from heterogeneous geospatial data with a PU formulation effectively addresses the challenge of sparse traversability labels, enabling large-scale, informed traversability map generation.
> - Fusing semantic context from satellite imagery with geometric details from LiDAR and structural information from OSM creates a robust representation for off-road environments.
> - Physically grounded priors derived directly from LiDAR significantly improve the calibration and terrain discrimination capabilities of learned traversability maps.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous navigation in urban environments often employs a hierarchical planning paradigm, utilizing global route planners based on map services for high-level decisions and reactive local planners for immediate context. However, extending this paradigm to off-road environments presents challenges due to the absence of defined road networks, significant spatial variability, and the presence of noisy or incomplete prior information. Traditional off-road navigation methods often rely on onboard sensors, which provide perception only of the immediate surroundings, limiting long-range planning capabilities.

The existing research landscape for off-road traversability estimation broadly consists of two main approaches:
1. **Geometry-driven methods**: These learn traversability from locally traversed terrain, often derived from onboard sensor data like LiDAR. While effective for local navigation, these approaches typically struggle to capture human navigation intent and are limited in their spatial extent for long-range planning.
2. **Demonstration-based methods**: These approaches learn from expert driving behavior, sometimes using inverse reinforcement learning or differentiable planners. However, they may lack explicit physical grounding in terrain constraints or require specific planning algorithms that restrict gradient flow.

A significant gap exists in effectively leveraging large-scale geospatial data (such as satellite imagery, aerial LiDAR, and vector maps) for off-road traversability estimation. While these data sources offer extensive spatial coverage, they lack dense, direct traversability labels, making it challenging to train learning models. Existing geospatial datasets often support large-scale semantic understanding but are not designed for navigation tasks. Prior work on global traversability often uses simple models, limiting generalization, or relies on semantic reasoning without strong geometric grounding.

This work addresses these limitations by introducing a framework that learns a continuous traversability map from diverse overhead geospatial data. It aims to bridge the gap between local, geometry-driven methods and demonstration-based approaches by integrating physical feasibility (via geometric priors from LiDAR) with human navigation intent (via supervision from human GPS trajectories). By utilizing a positive-unlabeled learning formulation, the framework attempts to overcome the scarcity of dense, explicit negative traversability labels, which is a common challenge when learning from large-scale, unstructured environments. The proposed approach is designed to provide a global, traversability-aware representation that is both physically grounded and predictive of human driving behavior, thereby enabling more robust long-horizon off-road navigation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a global planning framework capable of jointly learning and exploiting traversability-aware representations for long-horizon off-road autonomous navigation. This framework aims to integrate heterogeneous geospatial data sources to predict both a continuous traversability representation and a goal-conditioned estimate of route preference.

The motivation behind this work stems from several challenges inherent in off-road autonomy:
- **Limited Sensor Horizon**: Onboard sensors in autonomous vehicles typically perceive only the immediate surroundings. Safe and efficient off-road navigation, however, requires an understanding of terrain features and traversability that extend well beyond this limited sensor range. This necessitates a mechanism to incorporate broader spatial context.
- **Lack of Structured Information**: Unlike urban environments with well-defined road networks and detailed map services, off-road terrains are unstructured. Feasible routes may not correspond to visible trails, and existing paths can be suboptimal or impassable. This unpredictability demands planning directly over the terrain with an emphasis on accurate, large-scale traversability estimation.
- **Difficulty in Learning Traversability from Geospatial Data**: While geospatial data sources (e.g., satellite imagery, aerial LiDAR, vector maps) offer large-scale coverage, learning traversability from them is complex. Dense, pixel-level traversability labels are generally unavailable at scale for these environments, and existing learning methods often rely on short-range sensing or indirect supervision, which may not capture the nuances of physical feasibility or human driving preferences over long distances.
- **Need for Physically Grounded and Human-Aligned Planning**: Current approaches either derive traversability from local geometry, which might miss human intent for route selection, or use demonstration-based methods that may lack explicit grounding in physical terrain constraints. There is a need for a representation that is both physically feasible and predictive of how a human would navigate, allowing for robust and natural-feeling long-range plans.
- **Improving Autonomy Reliability**: Existing local-planner-only autonomy systems in off-road settings often face safety risks or require frequent operator interventions due to their limited predictive horizon. A robust global planner that considers long-range traversability could reduce these risks and improve overall system reliability and efficiency.

In response to these motivations, the specific objectives include:
- Developing an efficient formulation to learn a continuous traversability map from overhead data.
- Supervising this learning process directly using human-driven GPS trajectories through a Positive-Unlabeled (PU) formulation.
- Shaping the learned traversability map with self-supervised geometric priors derived from LiDAR data.
- Creating a public multi-modal geospatial dataset that provides extensive geographic coverage and paired human driving data to facilitate this research and future studies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces a global planning framework centered around a dual-stream, multi-modal neural network architecture. This network jointly processes satellite imagery, aerial LiDAR data, and OpenStreetMap (OSM) vector maps to predict a continuous traversability map and goal-conditioned path logits.

**A. Data Collection and Processing**
The research involved creating a dataset of 299 unique scenes, spanning approximately 1,244 km², with diverse terrain types including deserts, grasslands, forests, mines, and mountainous regions. This dataset is paired with 1,130 km of human-driven GPS trajectories.
- **Data Sources**: USGS LiDAR Explorer provided aerial LiDAR point clouds (vertical accuracy of 10 cm, 5-27 points/m²). ArcGIS supplied co-registered 30 cm/pixel satellite imagery. OpenStreetMap contributed vector features (highways, trails, waterways) and public GPS traces.
- **Preprocessing Pipeline**:
 - **LiDAR Point Clouds**: Rasterized into grid maps with three channels: height, slope, and intensity. The grid resolution (G_res) is determined by local point density to ensure sufficient points (at least 15 per cell) for stable surface normal and slope estimation. The point cloud is voxelized (0.25 * G_res) for regularization. Slope is computed from the vertical component of the surface normal. LiDAR intensity is normalized and stored as its complement (1-I) to align with a "higher value means less traversable" convention.
 - **OpenStreetMap Features**: Rasterized onto the same grid as the LiDAR data to serve as semantic priors.
 - **Satellite Imagery**: Cropped and reprojected to match LiDAR extents under a common datum, ensuring spatial consistency.
 - **GPS Traces**: Augmented into trajectories ranging from 200m to 1.5km, then rasterized onto a grid map with occupied cells set to 1. Start and goal points are extracted for a separate start-goal grid map.

**B. Model Architecture**
The architecture is a dual-stream, multi-modal network designed to process diverse inputs and produce two outputs: a path logits map and a continuous traversability map. Fusion occurs at a reduced resolution (256x256), with final predictions upsampled to 1024x1024.
- **Satellite Image Encoder**: Utilizes DINOv3-SAT, a ViT-L/16 model pretrained on SAT-493M. This encoder is kept frozen to leverage its pre-trained semantic features from the satellite domain. Its final-layer patch tokens are reshaped into a 64x64 grid.
- **Feature Pyramid Network (FPN)**: Decodes the DINO features to the 256x256 fusion resolution. It consists of a 1x1 projection, a bottom-up pyramid with three stride-2 stages, and top-down lateral merges with smoothing, ultimately producing 64-channel features.
- **LiDAR Encoder**: A three-stage Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) (with 5x5, 3x3, 3x3 convolutions, GroupNorm–ReLU) encodes the rasterized LiDAR grid maps (height, slope, intensity) and the OSM raster channel. It outputs 64-channel features, which are then average-pooled to the fusion resolution. Learnable per-channel input scales are applied.
- **Multi-Modal Fusion via FiLM**: LiDAR and satellite features are fused using spatially-varying Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM). Modulation parameters (γ, β) are predicted from the DINO features and applied to the LiDAR features, allowing the network to reweight LiDAR features based on local semantic context.
- **Goal Conditioning**: Start and goal positions, represented as spatial heatmaps, are encoded by a two-layer CNN into 32-channel goal features. These are concatenated with the fused multi-modal features, forming a 96-channel representation that enables goal-directed path hypotheses.
- **Post-Fusion Refinement and Output Heads**: The 96-channel representation is refined by a shallow CNN. It then feeds into two independent heads:
 - **Path Logits Head**: Predicts path likelihoods, comprising Conv(3x3, 32->16)–GN–ReLU–Dropout2d followed by a 1x1 convolution to a single output channel.
 - **Traversability Head**: Predicts a continuous traversability map, similarly structured, with its output sigmoid-normalized to.
 Both outputs are bilinearly upsampled to the original 1024x1024 resolution for loss computation.

**C. Loss Function**
The model is trained with a composite loss function that primarily supervises the path-logits head and indirectly shapes the traversability head.
- **Path Corridor Loss (L_path)**: A Positive-Unlabeled (PU) formulation is used for path supervision. Pixels within a 5-pixel radius of human GPS trajectories are considered positives (P), while all other pixels are unlabeled (U). The non-negative PU (nnPU) loss is applied to binary cross-entropy terms, with a mass penalty (L_mass) and a total-variation (L_TV) term for output smoothness.
- **Auxiliary Traversability Supervision**:
 - **OSM Track Loss (L_track)**: Pulls the predicted traversability (T_hat) towards 1 on known road and trail geometries from OSM, leveraging prior map knowledge.
 - **Combined Trajectory Loss (L_traj)**: Softly anchors T_hat to a value of 0.75 on aggregated historical paths, reinforcing preference for these corridors without overfitting.
- **LiDAR-derived Priors (Self-supervised)**: Three priors are used to penalize high traversability on geometrically hazardous terrain. These employ a Gaussian form to represent smooth changes in traversability.
 - **Intensity Prior (L_int)**: Supervises T_hat towards the original LiDAR intensity (I), where higher intensity corresponds to more traversable surfaces.
 - **Slope Prior (L_slope)**: Penalizes steep terrain by driving T_hat towards a Gaussian decay function of slope, with a crossover point at approximately 30 degrees (σ_s = 0.445).
 - **Height-gradient Prior (L_height)**: Addresses abrupt elevation changes (e.g., cliffs, ditches) by driving T_hat towards a Gaussian decay function of the normalized height-gradient magnitude.
- **Total Objective**: The total loss is a weighted sum of all eight loss terms. Auxiliary and prior terms are linearly ramped up over the first three epochs to ensure training stability, with the path corridor loss (λ_p=1.0) serving as the dominant signal.

**D. Global Planner (for Inference)**
For downstream planning, the learned traversability map is transformed into a cost representation (C = 1 - T_hat). Given a goal, the Long Range Planner (LRP) uses Yen's algorithm to compute K-shortest paths (K=4) via A* search. The A* objective balances traversal cost and path length. The selected path is discretized into waypoints and streamed to a Short Range Planner (SRP) for reactive execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the proposed framework through a costmap benchmark, an ablation study, and real-world field trials.

**A. Costmap Benchmark**
The model was trained on 7,533 samples and validated on 1,716 samples, achieving a total loss of 0.065 on the training set and 0.076 on the validation set. Costmaps were benchmarked against human-driven reference trajectories (n=356, avg. 412m) from 20 held-out test scenes. An A* planner, selecting the best of K=4 candidate paths, was used.
- **Metrics**: Discrete Fréchet distance (d_F) to human path (lower is better), planner/human mean cost ratio (r_c) (closer to 1 is better), and planner/human path-length ratio (r_d) (closer to 1 is better).
- **Results**:
 - The proposed method achieved a d_F of 61.3 m, representing a 21–43% improvement over baselines (Height map: 108.4 m, Trailblazer: 91.6 m, OVerSeeC: 77.5 m).
 - The r_c for the proposed method was 0.93, closest to unit parity, indicating the planner traversed cells of similar cost magnitude to human drivers. In contrast, OVerSeeC had an r_c of 0.23, suggesting its planner exploited costmap regions that humans strongly avoided.
 - The r_d for the proposed method was 0.89. All planners showed r_d values below unity (0.81–0.92), as A* minimizes cost while human drivers may not solely prioritize shortest paths. The proposed method's r_d of 0.89, combined with the best Fréchet distance and cost alignment, indicates that shorter paths were achieved by adhering to the human corridor rather than detouring through cheaper, less preferred terrain.

**B. Ablation Study**
An ablation study with four variants (A: Path corridor only; B: A + auxiliary terms; C: A + LiDAR priors; D: Full loss) was conducted to assess the contribution of each loss group. Evaluation involved comparing mean traversability along "dense human corridors" (regions with multiple overlapping human trajectories) versus "sparse alternative routes" (geometrically accessible but less preferred paths). A larger gap (∆) between these two indicates stronger terrain discrimination.
- **Results**:
 - Variant A (path corridor only) produced nearly identical scores for both route types (∆ = 0.019), indicating insufficient terrain discrimination.
 - Adding auxiliary terms (Variant B) and LiDAR priors (Variant C) progressively improved discrimination (∆ = 0.035 and 0.124, respectively).
 - The full loss (Variant D) achieved the largest discrimination gap (∆ = 0.200), with mean traversability scores of 0.980 for dense corridors and 0.780 for sparse alternatives. This confirms the cumulative benefit of the auxiliary terms and LiDAR-derived priors in calibrating the costmap for terrain-aware navigation.

**C. Real-World Field Experiments**
Experiments were conducted on a Clearpath Warthog across a 250m Levee site and a 9km² off-road site at Texas A&M’s RELLIS Campus, at speeds up to 2 m/s. The proposed LRP provided global waypoints to ARL's Phoenix autonomy stack, which used MPPI control as its SRP.
- **Q1: Does the learned costmap sufficiently reflect terrain characteristics?**
 - At the Levee site, the costmap correctly assigned high cost to embankment slopes and water, guiding the robot through a safe, level corridor. LRP successfully routed around the levee, preventing a failure mode observed with SRP-only autonomy that involved climbing the embankment and approaching water.
 - At the off-road site, the costmap identified established trails as low-cost corridors and correctly excluded quarries (negative obstacles). It also resolved traversable corridors through wooded areas.
 - **Observation**: The costmap exhibited conservatism in transitional zones (e.g., grass-to-brush boundaries), which contributed to slightly longer path lengths compared to human driving.
- **Q2: Does the global planner outperform local-planner-only autonomy?**
 - **Comparison**: Human teleoperation (baseline), full-stack autonomy (LRP), and SRP-only navigation across seven routes. SRP-only runs were limited due to safety concerns.
 - **Results**: LRP achieved trajectories with an average distance overhead of 3.66% compared to human teleoperation, whereas SRP-only showed a 9.7% overhead. Operator interventions were reduced by approximately 85% for LRP (one intervention per 480 m) compared to SRP (one per 140 m).
 - **LRP Shortcomings**: LRP paths were, on average, 3.66% longer than human paths. This was attributed to two factors:
 - Tracking-induced deviations: Accumulated errors from the SRP tracking LRP waypoints, Warthog kinematic constraints, and reactive local replanning.
 - Model conservatism: The learned costmap sometimes routed around lightly-wooded corridors that a human operator would traverse directly.
- **Q3: Is the learned costmap expressive enough to yield multiple distinct yet feasible trajectories?**
 - **Evaluation**: The planner was queried for top-K alternative routes.
 - **Results**: At the Levee site, the LRP generated multiple paths of comparable length that circumnavigated the embankment via distinct corridors. At the off-road site, the LRP produced substantially different long-horizon routes to the same goal. One alternative route, while longer in absolute distance, had a cost-per-distance ratio only 5.9% higher than the primary route. This indicates that the costmap assigns consistent and meaningful cost values across different terrains, allowing for diverse yet competitively feasible route options and preserving operator choice.
- **Q4: How does the planner handle outdated costmaps when encountering unseen obstacles or terrain changes?**
 - **Mechanism**: If the SRP detects an obstacle not present in the global costmap, it halts the robot and triggers a replanning request to the LRP. A high-cost region is injected at the obstacle's estimated location, and an alternative route is computed.
 - **Result**: During testing on Route 6, the platform successfully replanned around an unmarked sand heap and reached the goal without further intervention.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses a key challenge in autonomous off-road navigation: generating traversability-aware global plans over long horizons using heterogeneous geospatial data. The core contribution lies in the proposed dual-stream multi-modal network that learns continuous traversability maps from satellite imagery, aerial LiDAR, and OpenStreetMap priors, effectively integrating semantic context, geometric detail, and map knowledge.

The significance of this work is multifaceted:
- **Improved Long-Range Navigation**: By learning global traversability maps that are both physically grounded and informed by human driving behavior, the system enables autonomous vehicles to generate more efficient and safer long-range plans in unstructured environments. The field trial results demonstrate a substantial reduction in operator interventions (approximately 85%) and closer path length to human teleoperation compared to local-planner-only autonomy, highlighting its practical utility.
- **Data Utilization Innovation**: The approach effectively leverages large-scale, readily available geospatial data, which typically lacks dense traversability labels. The use of a Positive-Unlabeled (PU) learning formulation and self-supervised LiDAR-derived geometric priors provides a method to overcome this supervision sparsity, making vast amounts of overhead data actionable for robotic navigation.
- **Public Dataset Release**: The creation and release of a public multi-modal geospatial dataset, spanning 1,244 km² with 1,130 km of human-driven GPS trajectories, is a notable contribution to the research community. This dataset, offering extensive geographic coverage and diverse terrain types, can serve as a benchmark and resource for future research in off-road autonomy, perception, and planning.
- **Enhanced Decision-Making**: The learned costmaps are shown to be expressive enough to generate multiple distinct yet feasible trajectories, offering operators diverse route options based on mission priorities. The ability to replan around unforeseen obstacles demonstrates the system's robustness in dynamic environments.
- **Bridging Research Gaps**: The work successfully integrates aspects of geometry-driven local traversability with demonstration-based planning by providing explicit physical grounding while aligning with human navigation intent, addressing limitations present in previous approaches.

Potential impacts include advancements in autonomous systems for various applications such as:
- **Defense and Security**: Enhancing the capabilities of unmanned ground vehicles for reconnaissance, logistics, and patrol in complex, off-road terrains.
- **Agriculture and Forestry**: Improving efficiency and safety of autonomous equipment for tasks like crop monitoring, harvesting, and timber management.
- **Exploration and Environmental Monitoring**: Facilitating robotic exploration in remote or hazardous natural environments.

Future work identified by the authors includes online costmap refinement by fusing the global prior with onboard perception data, which could address the limitations of static costmaps regarding transient obstacles or seasonal changes. Additionally, tighter co-design between the Long Range Planner (LRP) and Short Range Planner (SRP) is proposed to improve system integration and further enhance reliable high-speed off-road autonomy.
