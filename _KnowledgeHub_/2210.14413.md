# InterSim: Interactive Traffic Simulation via Explicit Relation Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> InterSim is an interactive traffic simulator that reacts to a tested ego plan by explicitly modeling pass/yield relations. It detects bounding-box conflicts between the plan and environment-agent trajectories, classifies each conflicting pair into influencer and reactor, then regenerates the reactor's trajectory with a goal-driven predictor, recursing until conflicts clear. On Waymo Open Motion it beats SimNet on realism and collision rates while modifying only ~23% of agents.

## Problem

- Log replay and heuristic car-following models cannot react to diverging ego plans or produce diverse interactive scenarios beyond predefined trajectories.
- Learned simulators roll out agents independently without reasoning over future interactions, yielding inconsistent, colliding trajectories in dense traffic.
- Collision losses, rule-based fallback filters, and latent-variable joint models are hand-crafted, uninterpretable, or need many samples to hit rare risky events.

## Method

- ==Conflict detection== flags environment agents whose future bounding boxes overlap the new ego plan, labeling only those as relevant agents to resimulate.
- A ==relation predictor== with dual ==VectorNet== and ==VGG16== raster encoders classifies each conflicting pair as influencer versus reactor, trained with cross-entropy on who reaches the crossing point first.
- A ==goal-driven trajectory predictor==, sharing the encoder plus a goal channel, regresses the reactor's trajectory to the crossing point, applied recursively to resolve environment-environment conflicts.

## Results

- On **40k** Waymo interactive scenarios with a learned ego planner, InterSim reaches **7.11** ADE and **6.17** FDE versus SimNet's **8.74** and **16.99**.
- Under a forced -1.5 m/s^2 slowdown, rear collision rate falls from **33.90%** (SimNet) and **35.00%** (no-resolution variant) to **7.48%**, with side collisions at **1.95%**.
- Only **23%** (Task 1) and **10.49%** (Task 2) of agents needed resimulation; ablation InterSim-M1 cut collisions further but lost progress, **26.67** versus **28.47**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Making interaction structure explicit as pass/yield labels buys interpretability and lets users hand-specify relations to author rare scenarios sampling would rarely reach.
> - Most agents are irrelevant to any given ego plan, so conflict-triggered partial resimulation is far cheaper than joint rollout over the whole scene.
> - Collision avoidance trades against progress: yielding both agents minimizes collisions but stalls traffic, so asymmetric reactor-only edits balance realism and reactivity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Traffic simulation serves as a critical component in the development and validation of autonomous driving systems. It offers a scalable and safe alternative to real-world road testing, enabling the reconstruction of challenging or rare traffic scenarios that are difficult or unsafe to collect in physical environments.

Historically, traffic simulators have adopted several approaches. Traditional methods often rely on static log replay, which replicates agent behaviors observed in collected data. While straightforward, this approach lacks reactivity; environment agents do not adapt their behaviors when an ego vehicle's plan deviates from the original log. Heuristic-based models, such as the Intelligent Driver Model (IDM), address reactivity by generating behaviors based on predefined rules. However, these models are limited in their ability to produce diverse scenarios and often struggle with complex interactions.

The advent of machine learning has introduced learning-based models that train agent behaviors from large-scale driving data, offering the potential for more realistic and diverse simulations. Early learning-based approaches often focused on simulating individual agent trajectories conditioned on observations and environmental context. A significant challenge in this paradigm, particularly in crowded scenes, has been producing *consistent* and *diverse* multi-agent interactive behaviors, especially in scenarios involving potential collisions. Uncoordinated individual agent predictions can lead to inconsistent or colliding trajectories.

To mitigate this, some prior works integrated collision-avoidance mechanisms, such as adding a task loss to penalize collisions or implementing post-hoc feasibility checks to filter out conflicting trajectories. Other approaches attempted to model joint trajectories of all agents through implicit latent variables, aiming for simulation consistency. However, these generative models, while capable of producing diverse scenarios, can be inefficient when targeting specific, rare, or dangerous scenarios crucial for testing autonomous driving planners, as they may require extensive sampling.

InterSim positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of existing learning-based simulators. It focuses on explicitly modeling interaction relations between agents, offering a method to generate consistent and reactive multi-agent behaviors without relying on hand-crafted losses, ad-hoc filters, or implicit latent variable models that may struggle with targeted scenario generation. This explicit relation modeling aims to enhance interpretability, efficiency, and the ability to manipulate specific interaction scenarios, thereby advancing the capability of simulators to rigorously test autonomous driving planners.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an interactive traffic simulator, InterSim, capable of generating realistic, consistent, and reactive multi-agent behaviors for evaluating autonomous driving planners. The work specifically aims to overcome the challenges associated with producing coherent interactive behaviors in crowded and dynamic traffic environments.

The motivation for InterSim stems from several limitations observed in the broader research landscape of traffic simulation:

1. **Lack of Reactivity in Traditional Simulators:** Traditional log replay systems fail to adapt environment agent behaviors when an ego vehicle's plan deviates from recorded data. Heuristic models offer some reactivity but lack diversity, limiting their utility for comprehensive planner testing. The goal is to develop a simulator where environment agents react realistically to novel ego plans.
2. **Inconsistency in Learning-Based Individual Agent Simulators:** Many modern learning-based models, while generating realistic individual agent trajectories, often assume independence in future trajectory rollouts for multiple agents. This can lead to physically impossible or inconsistent outcomes, such as collisions, in interactive scenarios. A key objective is to ensure simulation consistency by preventing inter-agent collisions without resorting to arbitrary post-processing or hand-crafted rules.
3. **Limitations of Implicit Interaction Modeling:** Approaches that model joint behaviors through implicit latent variables, while offering consistency, can be difficult to interpret. More critically, they often rely on probabilistic sampling, which can be inefficient for generating specific, rare, or dangerous scenarios that are highly valuable for testing autonomous driving planners (e.g., specific conflict resolutions or near-collision events). A core motivation is to provide a more direct and efficient mechanism for generating and manipulating such scenarios.
4. **Desire for Interpretability:** Understanding *why* simulated agents behave in a certain way is crucial for debugging planners and gaining insights into traffic dynamics. Explicitly modeling interaction relations (e.g., yielding, passing) can provide a more interpretable framework compared to opaque latent variable models.
5. **Efficiency in Multi-Agent Simulation:** Simulating all agents in a scene, especially when only a few are critically affected by an ego plan, can be computationally expensive. The motivation includes developing a method that efficiently identifies and modifies only the "relevant" agents, thereby improving simulation runtime without sacrificing realism for critical interactions.
6. **Enabling Scenario Manipulation:** Autonomous driving development benefits from the ability to systematically test specific interaction modalities or "what-if" scenarios. The aim is to create a simulator where interaction relations can be directly specified or altered, allowing for controlled manipulation of interactive scenes beyond what data-driven generative models can easily produce.

In summary, InterSim aims to build a simulator that integrates explicit reasoning about agent interactions to deliver consistent, reactive, interpretable, and efficient multi-agent traffic simulations, particularly to support the rigorous testing and validation of autonomous driving planning systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

InterSim operates as an iterative, reactive traffic simulator designed to generate consistent and realistic trajectories for environment agents in response to a given ego agent plan. The approach involves a pipeline of conflict detection, relation-aware conflict resolution, and goal-driven trajectory prediction.

**A. Overview of the Simulation Loop**

At each simulation step, InterSim receives an ego agent's planned trajectory. It then follows these sequential steps:
1. **Conflict Detection:** Identify potential collisions between the ego plan and the current or projected trajectories of environment agents.
2. **Relation-Aware Conflict Resolution (Ego-Environment):** For agents in conflict with the ego, predict their interaction relation (influencer/reactor) and adjust the reactor's trajectory (or ego plan if ego is reactor) based on this relation to resolve the conflict.
3. **Conflict Resolution (Environment-Environment):** Iteratively resolve any new conflicts that arise among environment agents due to trajectory modifications in the previous step, using the same relation prediction and trajectory adjustment mechanisms.
4. **Simulation Step Advancement:** Commit the newly generated consistent trajectories for all agents for one simulation step and then repeat the process for the next step, considering any new ego plans.

**B. Conflict Detection**

Conflicts are defined as collisions between agents, determined by the overlap of their bounding boxes at any point in their predicted future trajectories. If an overlap is detected between the ego plan and an environment agent's trajectory, that environment agent is identified as "relevant" and requires trajectory adjustment.

**C. Relation-Aware Conflict Resolution**

To resolve a detected conflict, InterSim first determines the roles of the interacting agents: the "influencer" and the "reactor." The reactor is defined as the agent that will yield to the influencer.

- **Relation Predictor:** A deep neural network is trained to classify the interaction relation (e.g., pass or yield).
 - **Input Encoding:** The network uses a dual-head encoder for context:
 - **Vectorized Representation:** Leverages a VectorNet-like architecture, processing observed agent trajectories and map states as polylines. It uses MLPs for vector features, a graph neural network, and max-pooling for polyline features, followed by cross-attention between agent and map polyline features.
 - **Rasterized Representation:** Converts input states (agent positions over time, map information) into a multi-channel image (224x224 pixels, 1m/pixel). A pre-trained VGG16 model processes this image to extract rasterized features.
 - **Output and Training:** The concatenated vectorized and rasterized features are fed into a two-layer MLP that outputs probabilities over binary interaction relations. The model is trained using a cross-entropy loss against ground truth interaction labels, which are derived from observing which agent reaches a common crossing point first in the training data.
- **Conflict Resolution Rule:** Based on the predicted relation, the reactor's goal is modified to avoid collision with the influencer. Specifically, the goal point of the reactor is updated to the predicted collision point (or crossing point) between the two trajectories. This approach aims to prioritize simulation consistency while maintaining some level of progress for the reactor.

**D. Goal Driven Trajectory Prediction**

A dedicated goal-driven trajectory predictor is employed to generate realistic agent trajectories conditioned on a specified goal point. This predictor is used when the reactor's goal has been modified, or when simulating trajectories from novel states.

- **Architecture:** It shares the same dual-head context encoders (vectorized and rasterized) as the relation predictor. An additional channel is added to the rasterized representation to encode the target goal point. The decoder is an MLP that regresses the 2D positions for the full future trajectory.
- **Training:** The model is trained using a teacher forcing technique with L2 loss between the regressed trajectory and the ground truth future trajectory from the dataset, conditioned on the ground truth goal point.

**E. Conflict Resolution between Environment Agents**

After resolving conflicts between the ego agent and relevant environment agents, new collisions might inadvertently arise among the environment agents themselves due to their modified trajectories. InterSim iteratively applies the same conflict detection and relation-aware resolution procedure for these newly detected environment-environment conflicts. This iterative process, described as a "tree-like structure," continues until all conflicts within the scene are resolved, ensuring consistency across all agents.

**F. Simulation Advancement**

Once consistent trajectories for all relevant agents are determined, InterSim advances the simulation by one time step. If the ego plan remains unchanged, the simulation continues using the remaining predicted trajectories. If a new ego plan is provided, the entire conflict detection and resolution process is re-initiated for the current step.

**G. Dataset and Implementation Details**

The models are trained and evaluated on the Waymo Open Motion Dataset (WOMD), specifically its interactive subset, which contains scenarios with close interactions. The simulator takes 1 second of observed trajectory and environmental context to predict agent behaviors over the next 8 seconds at a frequency of 2Hz. The implementation of the relation and goal-driven predictors follows established models like VectorNet for vectorized encoding and VGG16 for rasterized encoding.

**H. Baselines**

For comparison, the authors introduce three baselines:
1. **SimNet:** A learning-based simulator using a ResNet-50 backbone for trajectory prediction without explicit interaction reasoning.
2. **InterSim-M0:** A variant of InterSim that predicts agent trajectories but does not perform any collision conflict resolution.
3. **InterSim-M1:** A variant that resolves collision conflicts by setting the goals of *both* interacting agents to the cross point, without considering specific interaction relations, representing a conservative approach.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of InterSim involved two distinct simulation tasks and a set of quantitative metrics measuring realism, reactivity, and progress. Qualitative examples further illustrate the model's capabilities.

**A. Simulation Tasks**

1. **Task 1: Simulation with Respect to Proper Plans:** The ego agent's behavior was controlled by a state-of-the-art marginal trajectory predictor. This task assessed InterSim's ability to generate realistic and consistent environment agent trajectories when the ego plan closely, but not identically, follows real-world data distributions.
2. **Task 2: Simulation with Respect to Novel Plans:** The ego agent was commanded to execute a slowing-down action with a constant deceleration of -1.5 m/s². This task evaluated the simulator's stability and robustness in generating reactive behaviors for environment agents, particularly those behind the ego, in response to out-of-distribution ego inputs.

**B. Quantitative Results**

The models were evaluated using the following metrics:
- **Relevant Ratio:** Percentage of environment agents whose trajectories are modified by the simulator.
- **Average Displacement Error (ADE) & Final Displacement Error (FDE):** L2 distance between simulated and ground truth trajectories, measuring simulation realism and scenario reconstruction.
- **Collision Rate (Front, Side, Rear):** Number of colliding agent pairs divided by the number of simulated agents, measuring simulation reactivity and interaction reasoning.
- **Progress:** Total distance traveled by simulated agents divided by the number of simulated agents, indicating goal achievement.

The results are summarized in Table I (Task 1) and Table II (Task 2).

1. **Simulation Efficiency:**
 - In Task 1, approximately 23-26% of environment agents were identified as relevant.
 - In Task 2, this figure was lower, around 10-11%.
 - This indicates that only a minority of agents typically require trajectory modification in response to an ego plan, supporting InterSim's design choice to focus resolution efforts on "relevant" agents for potential runtime efficiency improvements compared to simulating joint behaviors for all agents.

2. **Simulation Realism (Task 1):**
 - InterSim achieved the lowest ADE (7.11) and FDE (6.17) among all models, including SimNet and its own variants.
 - This suggests that explicitly modeling interaction relations contributes to better simulation realism and scenario reconstruction, producing trajectories closer to ground truth while handling interactive dynamics.

3. **Simulation Reactivity (Collision Rates):**
 - **Task 1:** InterSim demonstrated substantially lower collision rates (Front: 0.19%, Side: 0.71%, Rear: 1.91%) compared to SimNet (Front: 0.89%, Side: 2.80%, Rear: 4.97%) and InterSim-M0 (Front: 0.57%, Side: 1.99%, Rear: 9.28%). InterSim-M1 achieved slightly lower collision rates across all types (Front: 0.10%, Side: 0.41%, Rear: 1.00%), attributed to its conservative conflict resolution strategy.
 - **Task 2 (Slowing Down Ego):** This task highlighted the importance of reactive collision resolution. SimNet and InterSim-M0 exhibited high rear collision rates (9.33% and 11.06%, respectively) as they struggled to adapt to the slowing ego. InterSim significantly reduced these rates (Front: 0.30%, Side: 1.95%, Rear: 7.48%), outperforming SimNet and InterSim-M0, and aligning closely with InterSim-M1's lower collision rates (Front: 0.24%, Side: 1.98%, Rear: 6.94%). This indicates the effectiveness of InterSim's explicit collision resolution process, particularly for scenarios requiring significant reactive adjustments.

4. **Simulation Progress:**
 - In Task 1, InterSim (42.85) achieved better progress than InterSim-M1 (39.83), demonstrating a balance between safety (low collision rate) and goal achievement, whereas InterSim-M1's conservative approach leads to less progress.
 - Similar trends were observed in Task 2, where InterSim (28.47) maintained more progress than InterSim-M1 (26.67) while effectively reducing collision rates. The overall lower progress scores in Task 2 reflect the impact of the ego agent's slowing behavior on traffic flow.

**C. Qualitative Examples**

1. **Realistic Behavior Simulation (Fig. 3):** InterSim demonstrated the ability to identify relevant agents and simulate consistent interactive behaviors in complex scenarios such as platooning, turning, and merging, suggesting its capability to handle dense interactive traffic.
2. **Interaction Manipulation (Fig. 4):** The model showed the capacity to manipulate interaction relations. By reversing a predicted relation (e.g., forcing a turning agent to yield to a forward agent instead of the other way around), InterSim successfully simulated distinct interaction modalities. This highlights its potential for generating diverse and specific test scenarios, including rare events, which can be challenging for probabilistic generative models.

**D. Collision Analysis**

The authors acknowledge that collision rates, while significantly reduced, were not entirely eliminated. Potential reasons cited include:
- **Instability of Goal-Driven Trajectory Prediction:** The trajectory prediction model can occasionally overshoot or produce unrealistic trajectories given novel or challenging inputs, leading to collisions.
- **Noise in Perception Data:** Inaccuracies in bounding box labels and agent positions from perception data can result in scenarios starting with pre-existing collisions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of InterSim contributes to the field of autonomous driving research and its application in several significant ways:

1. **Advancement in Interactive Traffic Simulation:** InterSim introduces an approach to interactive traffic simulation that explicitly models agent interaction relations. This represents an evolution from methods relying on implicit interaction modeling or post-hoc collision avoidance mechanisms.
2. **Enhanced Testing for Autonomous Driving Planners:** By generating realistic, consistent, and reactive multi-agent behaviors, InterSim provides a more robust and reliable platform for testing autonomous driving planners. This allows for comprehensive evaluation in a wider range of scenarios, including challenging interactive situations, which is crucial for safety assurance.
3. **Improved Simulation Safety and Scalability:** Simulation offers a safer and more scalable alternative to real-world testing. InterSim's ability to reconstruct and manipulate risky, rare, or complex interactive scenarios efficiently allows for extensive testing without the logistical and safety constraints of physical road tests.
4. **Increased Interpretability:** Explicitly reasoning about "influencer" and "reactor" roles and the underlying interaction relations provides a more interpretable simulation framework. This can aid in understanding why certain behaviors emerge and facilitate the debugging and refinement of autonomous driving systems.
5. **Targeted Scenario Generation and Manipulation:** The demonstrated capability to manipulate interaction relations (e.g., specifying who yields to whom) offers a powerful tool for generating specific "what-if" scenarios or corner cases. This is particularly valuable for stress-testing planners against diverse and challenging situations that might be difficult to observe or generate with purely data-driven generative models.
6. **Efficiency Gains in Multi-Agent Simulation:** By identifying and modifying trajectories only for agents relevant to an ego plan, InterSim proposes a method for improving the computational efficiency of interactive simulation. This is beneficial for large-scale simulation campaigns where resources are a concern.
7. **Contribution to Robustness in Novel Scenarios:** The performance in Task 2, where InterSim effectively generated reactive behaviors in response to an out-of-distribution ego plan (slowing down), indicates improved robustness. This is critical for evaluating autonomous systems that must operate reliably even when encountering unexpected or novel behaviors from other road users.

Overall, InterSim's explicit relation modeling approach has the potential to enhance the realism, safety, efficiency, and diagnostic capabilities of traffic simulators, directly supporting the development and validation of more capable and reliable autonomous driving technologies.
