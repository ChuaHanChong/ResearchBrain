# Self-Verifying Reflection Helps Transformers with CoT Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers investigated self-verifying reflection in Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning using tiny transformer models, establishing theoretical conditions for reflection's accuracy benefits and analyzing its evolution under reinforcement learning (RL) fine-tuning. The study found that while reflection improves in-distribution performance and can be incentivized by RL, RL often optimizes verification through a trade-off in error types rather than fostering generalizable problem-solving skills, particularly for out-of-distribution tasks.

## Problem

- Despite the empirical benefits and prevalence of reflection in RL-tuned large language models (LLMs), their actual error detection capabilities are often limited.
- There is a lack of clear understanding regarding how reflection genuinely contributes to performance improvements and why Reinforcement Learning (RL) incentivizes such behaviors with imperfect verifiers.
- The high computational cost and linguistic complexity of LLMs hinder comprehensive experimental and theoretical analysis of reflection mechanisms across diverse settings.

## Method

- Developed a minimalistic reasoning framework that formalizes Chain-of-Thought as a ==Markov Thought Process==, supporting essential reasoning behaviors without natural language.
- Introduced two self-verifying reflection mechanisms: Reflective MTP (RMTP) for instant self-correction and Reflective Trace-Back Search (RTBS) for depth-first error propagation.
- Utilized tiny transformer models (1M-16M parameters) and a four-stage training pipeline, including Reinforcement Learning via GRPO, to enable extensive and analytic experimentation.

## Results

- Reflective Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) significantly improved the non-reflective execution performance of tiny transformers, with detailed verification showing particular benefits for out-of-distribution tasks in **16M** models.
- Reflective MTP (RMTP) execution enhanced reasoning accuracy for **4M** and **16M** models, provided the verifier's false-negative rate was low, and Reflective Trace-Back Search (RTBS) outperformed RMTP in Sudoku due to the task's higher discriminability of incorrect steps.
- Reinforcement Learning fine-tuning using GRPO improved in-distribution accuracy but did not generalize to out-of-distribution problems, and shifted verifier errors by decreasing false negatives while increasing false positives, suggesting an opportunistic learning strategy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-verifying reflection, even without active use during inference, acts as a regularizer during supervised fine-tuning, improving the underlying planning policy's performance.
> - The effectiveness of reflective execution is critically dependent on the verifier's false-negative rate; high false negatives are more detrimental to accuracy than high false positives.
> - Reinforcement Learning fine-tuning on outcome rewards can induce an optimistic bias in the verifier, decreasing false negatives at the cost of increasing false positives, and primarily improves in-distribution performance without strong generalization to out-of-distribution tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper addresses a critical, yet often opaque, aspect of advanced large language models (LLMs): the mechanism and effectiveness of "reflection" in Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning. The broader landscape of LLM research has seen significant advancements in CoT prompting and subsequent improvements through various techniques like reflective thinking and external verifiers. More recently, reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a powerful tool for fine-tuning LLMs to exhibit sophisticated reasoning behaviors, including frequent self-verification.

However, a puzzling observation in recent literature is that despite the empirical benefits of reflection and its prevalence in RL-tuned LLMs, the actual error detection capabilities of these models can be limited. This raises fundamental questions about *how* reflection genuinely contributes to performance improvements and *why* RL incentivizes such behaviors, even with imperfect verifiers.

This paper positions itself directly to address these ambiguities. Instead of scaling up LLMs or focusing on natural language, the authors deliberately *simplify* the problem by investigating self-verifying reflection using *tiny transformer models* and a *minimalistic reasoning framework that operates without natural language*. This approach is inspired by other works that use small transformers to gain insights into LLM behaviors. By stripping away the complexities of natural language and the prohibitive computational costs of LLMs, the authors aim to achieve "analytic clarity and comprehensive experiments across settings."

Thus, the work contributes to the landscape by:
- **Providing a foundational understanding:** It moves beyond empirical observations in LLMs to theoretically and experimentally analyze the core mechanics of self-verifying reflection.
- **Bridging scales:** It explores how insights from small, controllable models can inform our understanding of large, complex systems.
- **Clarifying RL's role:** It scrutinizes how RL fine-tuning influences reflective behaviors and the quality of verification, challenging the assumption that RL necessarily leads to "faithful" improvements in generalized problem-solving skills.

By focusing on integer multiplication and Sudoku – tasks that are simple in definition but challenging for models – the paper maintains relevance to the broader LLM reasoning paradigm while enabling efficient and reproducible experiments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The paper sets out to answer two primary research questions:
1. **What role does self-verifying reflection play in the training and execution of reasoning models?** This objective aims to unravel the mechanisms through which models benefit from the ability to check their own reasoning steps, both during the learning phase and when generating solutions.
2. **How does reflective reasoning evolve in Reinforcement Learning (RL) with verifiable outcome rewards?** This seeks to understand the interplay between RL fine-tuning, which often uses sparse outcome rewards, and the emergence and quality of self-verification behaviors.

The core motivation behind these objectives stems from the observed disconnect in current LLM research: advanced LLMs frequently use reflection, and RL promotes this behavior, yet their actual error detection capabilities are often imperfect. This apparent contradiction necessitates a deeper, more controlled investigation.

The authors explicitly state that the "complexity of natural language and the prohibitive training cost of LLMs make it difficult to draw clear conclusions from theoretical abstraction and comprehensive experiments across settings." This pragmatic constraint motivates their innovative approach:
- **Simplification:** They develop a "minimalistic reasoning framework" that supports essential reasoning behaviors (planning, verification, resampling, trace-back) without relying on natural language. This ensures analytical clarity.
- **Efficiency:** They employ "tiny transformer models" (1M, 4M, 16M parameters) to conduct "massive experiments" that would be infeasible with LLMs. This allows for thorough exploration of different settings and hyperparameters.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to provide a rigorous, foundational understanding of self-verifying reflection in multi-step reasoning, contributing generalizable insights that can inform the development of more effective and robust reasoning models, including future LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology is characterized by a "minimalistic" and "analytic" approach, combining novel framework design, a multi-stage training pipeline, theoretical proofs, and extensive empirical validation using small-scale models.

#### 3.1. Reasoning Formulation: CoT as a Markov Thought Process (MTP)

The paper formalizes CoT reasoning as a Markov Thought Process (MTP), where a query $Q$ evolves through a sequence of intermediate reasoning steps $R_1, \ldots, R_{T-1}$ to a final answer $A$. Each step involves a planning policy $\pi$ generating an action $R_{t+1}$ based on the current state $S_t$, and a deterministic transition function $T$ updating the state to $S_{t+1}$. Crucially, for tiny transformers, the state representations ($S_t$) are "reduced" to carry only necessary information, avoiding long-context inference challenges.
- **Tasks:** Two canonical reasoning tasks are used:
 - **Integer Multiplication (Mult):** States are expressions like $[x_t \times y_t + z_t]$, where $x_t, y_t$ are remaining multiplicands and $z_t$ is the cumulative product. Steps involve eliminating digits in multiplicands.
 - **Sudoku:** States are $9 \times 9$ game boards. Steps involve filling blank cells.

#### 3.2. Framework of Self-Verifying Reflection

The core innovation is the introduction of discriminative self-verification, where the model self-verifies its own thought steps. Two variants are proposed:
- **Reflective MTP (RMTP):** A "self-verifying policy" $\tilde{\pi} = \{\pi, V\}$ (combining planning policy $\pi$ and verifier $V$) generates a step $R_{t+1}$ and a verification label $V_{t+1}$ ("✓" for correct, "×" for incorrect). If "×" is detected, a "reflective transition function" $\tilde{T}$ keeps the state unchanged, allowing $\pi$ to resample. This simulates instant self-correction.
- **Reflective Trace-Back Search (RTBS):** This extends RMTP by simulating depth-first search. If `m` attempts on a state $S_t$ are rejected (either instantly by $V$ or recursively because subsequent reasoning from that step failed), the negative label "×" propagates back, causing the reasoner to trace back to an earlier ancestral state with remaining attempts.

#### 3.3. Training Pipeline

Tiny transformers are trained from scratch using a four-stage pipeline, mirroring LLM training practices:
1. **Pretraining (I):** CoT examples are treated as a textual corpus, and the transformer is trained with next-token prediction to minimize cross-entropy loss.
2. **Non-reflective Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT) (II):** The model learns the planning policy $\pi$ by imitating expert CoT examples, mapping states $S_t$ to steps $R_{t+1}$.
3. **Reflective SFT (III):** To integrate self-verification, $\pi$ first samples non-reflective CoTs. An *expert verifier* (rule-based) labels each step as "✓" or "×". The model then undergoes SFT to learn the self-verifying policy $\tilde{\pi}$, predicting both $R_{t+1}$ and $V_{t+1}$ from $S_t$. CoT examples from non-reflective SFT are mixed to prevent catastrophic forgetting. Two types of verification are explored: *binary* (overall correctness) and *detailed* (element-wise correctness).
4. **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Fine-tuning (IV):** Using GRPO (Group-Reward Policy Optimization), the policy is further strengthened. It involves iteratively executing $\pi$ (or $\tilde{\pi}$ for RMTP) to collect experience CoTs, assigning binary *outcome rewards* (1 for correct final answer, 0 otherwise) via a rule-based answer checker (ORM), and updating the policy to favor higher-reward solutions. In RMTP, $\tilde{\pi}$ optimizes planning and verification jointly. GRPO is preferred over PPO due to its stability for tiny transformers.

#### 3.4. Theoretical Results

The authors present a simplified model of reasoning where states have a "complexity scale" $n$, and each correct step reduces complexity by 1. They define probabilities for planning ability ($\mu$), false positive rate ($e^+$), false negative rate ($e^-$), and rejection on negative states ($f$).
- **Theorem 1:** Provides conditions under which self-verifying reflection improves accuracy: RMTP improves if $e^- + e^+ \le 1$ (verifier is better than random guessing). RTBS outperforms RMTP for large $n$ if $f > \alpha$ (negative states are more discriminable) and $m$ (search width) is sufficiently large.
- **Proposition 1:** Shows that a high false negative rate ($e^-$) leads to a higher expected number of steps, implying it's more detrimental than a high false positive rate given limited computational budgets.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Models:** Causal-attention transformers (1M, 4M, 16M parameters) implemented using LitGPT.
- **Tasks:** Integer Multiplication (Mult) and Sudoku.
- **Difficulties:** ID Easy, ID Hard (trained on), and OOD Hard (tested on). Defined by number of digits (Mult) or blanks (Sudoku).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings, supported by both theoretical analysis and comprehensive empirical experiments:

#### 4.1. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) Results

- **Self-Verification Facilitates Forward Reasoning:** Learning to self-verify through reflective SFT significantly improves the performance of models in non-reflective execution (i.e., when verification is not actively used at test time). This benefit is particularly notable for 1M and 4M models in in-distribution tasks, and for 16M models in out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks with detailed verification. The authors suggest this acts as a regularizer, improving the quality of hidden embeddings and thus planning.
- **Reflection Improves Accuracy if Verification Errors are Bounded:** RMTP execution generally enhances reasoning accuracy over non-reflective execution for 4M and 16M models. Crucially, this improvement is contingent on a sufficiently low false-negative rate ($e^-$) – the probability of incorrectly rejecting a correct step. High $e^-$ can severely compromise performance, aligning with theoretical predictions.
- **Trace-Back Search is Task-Dependent:** RTBS outperforms RMTP in Sudoku, particularly for the 4M model with detailed verification, but shows no advantage in multiplication. This is because Sudoku states have explicit, verifiable rules, making incorrect steps more discriminable (high `f`), a condition predicted by Theorem 1. Multiplication errors, conversely, require recalculating historical steps, making verification harder.
- **Reduced State Representations are Crucial:** The paper demonstrates the necessity of using reduced state representations (only essential information) for tiny transformers to perform complex reasoning, showing that "direct" or "complete" thought implementations fail significantly.

#### 4.2. Theoretical Results

- **Conditional Improvement Guarantee:** Theorem 1 mathematically proves that self-verifying reflection (RMTP) guarantees accuracy improvements if verification errors ($e^-$ and $e^+$) are properly bounded ($e^- + e^+ \le 1$), meaning the verifier is better than random guessing. It also specifies conditions for RTBS to be superior to RMTP (high discriminability of negative states, sufficient search width).
- **False Negatives are Costlier:** Proposition 1 shows that a higher false-negative rate ($e^-$) leads to a greater expected number of steps to find a correct solution. This suggests that in practical settings with limited computational budgets, minimizing $e^-$ is more critical than minimizing $e^+$.

#### 4.3. Reinforcement Learning (RL) Results (GRPO)

- **ID Performance Boost, OOD Generalization Lacking:** GRPO effectively boosts accuracy for in-distribution (ID) hard problems, but these improvements largely fail to generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) cases. This suggests that RL mainly optimizes shallow statistical patterns rather than developing fundamental, generalizable problem-solving skills.
- **RL Induces an Error-Type Trade-off in Verification:** Instead of genuinely reducing both false positive ($e^+$) and false negative ($e^-$) rates, RL fine-tuning tends to decrease $e^-$ while *increasing* $e^+$. This indicates that models learn an "optimistic bias" – avoiding rejection of potentially correct steps, even at the cost of accepting more incorrect ones – a "superficial" way to obtain rewards, rather than truly enhancing verification capabilities.
- **RL Incentivizes Reflection for Exploratory Policies:** Similar to findings in large LLMs like DeepSeek-R1, RL can promote frequent reflection, especially on harder queries and when the policy operates with a higher exploratory temperature (e.g., 1.25). Conversely, with a more exploitative temperature (1.0), reflection frequency remains low. This implies that RL aligns reflection frequency with the planning policy's ability to explore potential solutions and discover higher rewards.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper makes a significant contribution to the fundamental understanding of reasoning in neural networks, particularly concerning the role of self-verification and its interaction with reinforcement learning.

**Foundational Understanding of Reflection:** By isolating and studying self-verifying reflection in a minimalistic, non-linguistic setting, the authors provide clear theoretical conditions and empirical evidence for its benefits. The proof that reflection guarantees improvements under bounded verification errors offers a crucial theoretical underpinning for a technique widely used but poorly understood in LLMs. This clarity is invaluable for designing future reasoning systems.

**Insights for LLM Development:** While conducted on tiny transformers, the findings have direct implications for advanced LLMs. The demonstration that self-verification improves both training and execution, and that RL can incentivize reflection, suggests that these mechanisms are not solely emergent properties of scale or natural language, but fundamental aspects of multi-step reasoning. Future LLM research can leverage these insights to design more principled reflection mechanisms and RL training strategies, potentially leading to more robust and accurate reasoners. The observation that *detailed verification* sometimes outperforms binary verification (e.g., for OOD tasks) also offers a practical guide for designing verifier feedback.

**Computational Efficiency in Reasoning Models:** The work highlights that effective multi-step reasoning with reflection is achievable with very limited computational resources (few-million-parameter transformers). This could inspire the development of computationally efficient, specialized reasoning models for specific tasks, reducing the reliance on massive, energy-intensive LLMs for certain applications.

**Nuanced Understanding of RL for Reasoning:** The discovery that RL primarily optimizes for in-distribution performance and can induce superficial strategies (e.g., trading off error types, promoting reflection based on exploration ability) rather than fostering generalizable problem-solving skills is a critical insight. This challenges simplistic views of RL's benefits and urges researchers to develop more sophisticated RL objectives or architectures that truly improve underlying reasoning capabilities and generalization.

**Future Research Directions:** The paper explicitly points to several promising avenues for future work, including extending reflection frameworks for better generalization, exploring novel training approaches that strengthen the generative-discriminative connection, and investigating how these findings transfer to natural-language LLMs. This lays groundwork for building more adaptive and genuinely intelligent reflective agents.

In conclusion, "Self-Verifying Reflection Helps Transformers with CoT Reasoning" provides a meticulously designed investigation that demystifies key aspects of reflective reasoning. By focusing on fundamental principles in a controlled environment, it offers actionable theoretical and empirical insights that are highly relevant to the ongoing quest for more capable and reliable AI reasoning systems, from tiny specialized models to the largest language models.
