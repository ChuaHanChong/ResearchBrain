# From What to How: Attributing CLIP's Latent Components Reveals Unexpected Semantic Reliance

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces a framework combining Sparse Autoencoders with attribution patching to explain how CLIP's latent components influence predictions. This method identifies unexpected semantic reliance and spurious correlations in CLIP, demonstrating its utility in debugging and improving model robustness, particularly in medical image analysis.

## Problem

- Existing methods for Vision-Language model interpretability primarily explain "what" concepts are encoded but lack instance-wise quantification of "how" these concepts influence specific model predictions.
- There is a need for scalable frameworks that can uncover and attribute the influence of individual latent components in complex transformer-based models like CLIP.
- Identifying unexpected semantic reliance, such as spurious correlations or reliance on unintended visual features, within CLIP's internal mechanisms remains a challenge.

## Method

- The paper introduces a framework that first extracts sparse, interpretable latent components from CLIP's final class token embedding using ==Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)==.
- It then quantifies the instance-wise influence of individual SAE components on CLIP's predictions using an adapted ==attribution patching technique==, specifically an Activation Gradient approach.
- The framework identifies ==unexpected concept reliance== by flagging components that are highly relevant to a prediction but have low semantic alignment, or by detecting components with unusually high relevance compared to a reference distribution.

## Results

- The proposed Act Grad attributions consistently demonstrated superior faithfulness in deletion and insertion experiments compared to other interpretability methods, accurately reflecting component influence.
- Sparse Autoencoders extracted a large number of diverse and interpretable components (e.g., **17,455** for ViT-L/14), with hundreds found to encode unexpected or unintended semantic features not present in standard taxonomies.
- The framework identified over **220** failure cases across four recurrent modes including polysemous prompts and visual typography, and in a melanoma detection case study, it revealed and helped mitigate a significant "red hue" spurious correlation, improving classifier robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The framework successfully bridges the "what" (concept encoding) and "how" (predictive influence) gaps in CLIP interpretability by combining component extraction with instance-wise attribution.
> - CLIP's latent space contains a rich set of interpretable components, including many unexpected or unintended features like visual typography, dataset artifacts, and polysemous concepts.
> - Spurious correlations and semantic ambiguities can significantly impact CLIP's robustness, but the proposed framework can systematically identify and help mitigate these issues, especially in sensitive applications like medical diagnosis.
> - The research from Fraunhofer HHI and TU Berlin highlights that understanding specific component influences is crucial for developing robust and trustworthy AI systems, moving beyond global explanations to actionable insights for debugging foundation models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

The research paper "From What to How: Attributing CLIP’s Latent Components Reveals Unexpected Semantic Reliance" by Dreyer et al. presents a novel framework for mechanistically interpreting Vision-Language (VL) models, specifically CLIP. The work addresses the critical need to understand the internal decision-making processes of these increasingly complex models, moving beyond merely identifying *what* concepts are encoded to *how* these concepts influence predictions. By combining Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) with an adapted attribution patching technique, the authors provide a scalable, instance-wise method to uncover surprising and often spurious semantic dependencies in CLIP's latent space, demonstrating its utility in debugging and improving model robustness, particularly in sensitive applications like medical diagnosis.

### 2. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision-language models like CLIP have revolutionized multimodal AI by learning powerful, transferable representations from vast amounts of paired image and text data. Their widespread adoption in tasks ranging from retrieval to captioning underscores the urgent need for transparency into their internal mechanisms. The broader research landscape for interpreting such models can be broadly categorized:

- **Concept-based Interpretability:** Early work focused on explaining model representations using predefined, human-understandable concepts. Methods like those from project CLIP embeddings onto known concept sets. While useful for high-level understanding, these approaches often miss unexpected or spurious features, which may arise from biases in training data (e.g., spurious correlations or linguistic ambiguities). They explain "what" a model might perceive but not necessarily the full spectrum of its learned features, nor "how" these features drive specific predictions.
- **Neuron/Component-level Analysis:** A more granular approach involves inspecting individual neurons, attention heads, or MLP layers within transformer architectures. This line of research aims to uncover the "meaning" of internal components. Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) have recently emerged as a powerful tool in this space, particularly for language models, by decomposing high-dimensional latent spaces into sparse, interpretable "features" or "components." Recent works have begun applying SAEs to CLIP's visual embeddings, revealing interpretable concepts. However, a significant gap remains: while these methods explain *what* components encode, they generally do not quantify *how* these components contribute to specific model predictions. Some work, like Rao et al., trains downstream classifiers on SAE activations, but this no longer explains the original model.
- **Holistic Interpretability Frameworks:** To address the "what" and "how" questions simultaneously, frameworks like CRP and SemanticLens have emerged for convolutional neural networks (CNNs). These frameworks map neurons to highly activating examples and compute their relevance to outputs. However, their applicability to transformer-based architectures like CLIP, with their distinct architectural properties and lack of directly interpretable intermediate representations, has been limited.
- **Latent Attributions:** For language models, Logit Lens gained popularity by projecting intermediate activations to output logits, providing a global measure of alignment. However, it lacks instance-wise attribution and struggles with interaction effects. Activation patching offers a causal intervention approach but is computationally expensive. Attribution patching, an efficient gradient-based alternative, has recently been applied to SAE components in language models.

This paper directly addresses the aforementioned gap by proposing a holistic framework that integrates SAEs with an adaptation of attribution patching for CLIP. It is among the first to provide scalable, component-level analysis for CLIP that explains both input-component and component-output relations, bridging the interpretability gap between understanding *what* a component represents and *how* it causally influences a model's prediction for a specific input.

### 3. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objectives and motivations of this research can be summarized as follows:

- **Understanding CLIP's Internal Mechanisms:** The primary goal is to shed light on the opaque internal workings of widely-used CLIP models. As these models are deployed in increasingly critical applications, it becomes paramount to understand *what kinds of patterns* they rely on and *how* these patterns contribute to their predictions.
- **Bridging the "What" and "How" Gap:** Previous interpretability methods for CLIP or SAEs either focused on identifying the concepts encoded by components ("what") or on global alignments, without providing instance-specific insights into their predictive influence ("how"). The authors aim to close this gap by developing a framework that can quantify the contribution of individual latent components to specific predictions.
- **Quantifying Component Importance:** Beyond merely describing latent components, the work seeks to assign faithful, instance-wise attribution scores that reflect the actual influence of each component on the model's output. This contrasts with global alignment measures like Logit Lens, which may overlook local specificities.
- **Revealing Unexpected and Spurious Reliance:** A key motivation is to automatically identify instances where CLIP models rely on semantically unexpected concepts or spurious correlations. This includes patterns not explicitly part of human-defined taxonomies, such as visual typography, dataset artifacts, or unintended polysemous interpretations of text. Such insights are crucial for diagnosing model failures and improving robustness.
- **Improving Robustness and Safety in Downstream Applications:** By identifying hidden shortcuts and spurious dependencies, the framework aims to enable developers to debug models and build more robust and reliable AI systems, especially in sensitive domains like medical imaging, where such biases can have significant consequences.

### 4. Methodology and Approach

The paper introduces a comprehensive framework for CLIP interpretability that operates at the level of sparse, interpretable latent components.

#### 4.1. Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)

The first step involves extracting interpretable components from CLIP's high-dimensional latent representations. The authors employ **Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs)**, specifically top-k SAEs, which have proven effective in decomposing neural network activations into a sparse combination of monosemantic feature vectors. For a latent representation $\mathbf{x}$ from a hidden layer (specifically, the class token embedding after the last transformer block), the SAE reconstructs it as $\mathbf{\hat{x}}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{i=1}^{d_{\text{SAE}}} a_i(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{v}_i + \mathbf{b} + \mathbf{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})$, where $a_i(\mathbf{x})$ are sparse activations, $\mathbf{v}_i$ are feature vectors (components), $\mathbf{b}$ is a bias, and $\mathbf{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x})$ is a reconstruction error. The SAE is post-hoc trained to minimize reconstruction error and enforce sparsity (e.g., via $L_1$-norm or top-k activation functions).

#### 4.2. Understanding What Semantics Components Encode

To understand *what* each SAE component represents, the authors follow a semantic labeling approach similar to SemanticLens. For each component $j$, they identify its top-k most activating samples from a dataset. They then compute the average CLIP visual embedding $\mathbf{\bar{x}}_j$ of these samples. This average embedding is then aligned with a predefined set of textual labels $\mathcal{T}$ (e.g., ImageNet-21k class names) using cosine similarity. The label that best aligns with $\mathbf{\bar{x}}_j$ (after subtracting alignment with an empty prompt to reduce generic matches) is assigned as the component's semantic description.

#### 4.3. Instance-wise Attribution of Components

This is a core methodological contribution. To understand *how* latent components influence specific predictions, the authors adapt **attribution patching** for CLIP. CLIP predictions are based on cosine similarity between a visual embedding $\mathbf{x}$ and a textual embedding $\mathbf{t}$. The image embedding $\mathbf{x}$ is derived from the class token of the last transformer block, followed by LayerNorm and a projection.

The attribution score $R_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t})$ for a component $j$ is approximated by $a_j \frac{\partial y(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t})}{\partial a_j}$, which resembles an Activation×Gradient approach. This quantifies the change in model output $y(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t})$ when the component's activation $a_j$ is set to zero. The authors provide a detailed derivation showing that this approximation reveals Logit Lens as a component within a broader formula, along with an additional scaling factor that captures the component's activation strength and a local correction term. This distinction highlights that Logit Lens only provides a global alignment and ignores activation magnitude and local interactions, making it less faithful for instance-wise attribution.

#### 4.4. Revealing Unexpected Concept Reliance

The framework employs two complementary strategies to identify unexpected model behavior:

- **Hidden Concepts (High Relevance, Low Alignment):** This strategy identifies components that are highly relevant to a prediction (quantified by $R_j(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t})$ or activation $a_j$) but lack strong semantic alignment with expected textual labels $s_j(\mathbf{t})$. By thresholding relevance and inversely thresholding alignment, the method surfaces novel, often surprising, features that CLIP implicitly learns but which fall outside standard concept taxonomies.
- **Failure Cases (Higher Relevance Than Expected):** This approach identifies outlier behaviors by comparing a component's relevance on a given input to its expected relevance distribution (mean $\mu_j$ and standard deviation $\hat{\sigma}_j$) derived from a reference dataset (e.g., training data). A z-score is computed: $z = \frac{R_j(\mathbf{x}_{\text{test}}, \mathbf{t}) - \mu_j}{\hat{\sigma}_j}$. Components with significantly high z-scores (e.g., >3.0) indicate unusual reliance, flagging potential failure points like spurious correlations.

### 5. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted across various CLIP ViT variants (B/32, B/16, L/14, H/14) and datasets (ImageNet-1k, ImageNet-21k, ISIC).

#### 5.1. Evaluating Attribution Faithfulness (Q1)

The authors rigorously evaluated the faithfulness of their instance-wise attributions (Act×Grad) through deletion and insertion experiments on the ImageNet test set. Act×Grad attributions consistently resulted in the largest performance drop during deletion (setting relevant activations to zero) and the fastest recovery during insertion (sequentially reintroducing relevant activations). This indicates superior faithfulness compared to Logit Lens (which performs poorly due to ignoring magnitude and interactions), Activation×Logit Lens, Energy, Integrated Gradients (which is computationally expensive), and random baselines. This confirms that the proposed attribution method accurately reflects the actual influence of individual latent components on CLIP predictions.

#### 5.2. Interpretability and Diversity of SAE Components (Q2)

SAEs were found to extract a large number of interpretable components from CLIP's latent space (e.g., 17,455 for ViT-L/14). A strong correlation (0.75) was observed between a component's interpretability (clarity of its top-activating samples) and its activation magnitude, suggesting that more strongly activated components tend to be more coherent and meaningful. SAEs yielded a significantly larger and more specific set of unique concepts (5,363) compared to PCA or inherent neural bases, and larger CLIP models (e.g., ViT-H/14) encoded a broader range of semantic concepts.

Critically, probing these components with ImageNet-21k class names revealed a substantial fraction of components (hundreds) encoding semantically *unexpected* or *unintended* features not present in the standard class taxonomy. Examples include high-level visual abstractions like "clipart," "game rendering," "US states," or patterns related to polysemous words, compound nouns, and visual typography (e.g., watermarks, specific letters). This highlights a rich latent knowledge base within foundation models that goes beyond explicit labels.

#### 5.3. Robustness of CLIP to Semantic Ambiguity and Spurious Correlations (Q3)

The framework identified over 220 failure cases across four recurrent modes:
- **Polysemous prompts:** e.g., "crane" referring to a bird vs. a machine.
- **Compound nouns:** e.g., "Egyptian cat" (model might focus on "Egyptian" or "cat" separately).
- **Visual typography:** text/logos embedded in images.
- **Spurious visual correlations:** e.g., an object always appearing with a specific background.

Evaluations using "spurious AUC" (distinguishing true images from distractors with spurious features) and "valid AUC" (distinguishing true images from other ImageNet classes) showed that CLIP is generally more robust than linear classifiers trained on visual embeddings. However, CLIP performed significantly worse in the presence of semantic ambiguity and typographic artifacts. Larger CLIP variants consistently improved performance. Prompt enrichment (e.g., templating or detailed descriptions) generally improved robustness, especially against typographic distractors, though its effectiveness varied across failure types.

#### 5.4. Case Study: Spurious Correlation Analysis in Melanoma Detection (Q4)

In a critical medical application—melanoma detection using WhyLesion-CLIP on ISIC datasets—the framework revealed a significant spurious correlation. A linear classifier trained on CLIP image embeddings for melanoma vs. non-melanoma classification showed that two highly influential SAE components encoded "red hue" or "pink hue" background artifacts for non-melanoma predictions. This was confirmed by artificially manipulating the red color channel in images: increasing red hue significantly boosted non-melanoma accuracy while decreasing melanoma accuracy. To mitigate this, the authors trained a "robust" classifier by augmenting embeddings in the latent space along the identified "red hue" direction. This intervention significantly improved robustness, making the classifier less sensitive to spurious red coloration and demonstrating the practical utility of the framework for debugging and improving clinical AI systems.

### 6. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

This research represents a significant step forward in the mechanistic interpretability of vision-language models.
- **Holistic Interpretability:** By integrating SAEs with instance-wise attribution, the framework provides a powerful, unified approach to answer both "what" concepts a model learns and "how" those concepts influence specific predictions. This goes beyond global summaries, offering granular insights.
- **Scalability and Practicality:** The adaptation of attribution patching for CLIP is computationally efficient, making the framework scalable for large foundation models. Its gradient-based nature simplifies implementation and adoption.
- **Automated Failure Detection:** The ability to automatically flag components that exhibit unexpected reliance (either by low semantic alignment or unusually high relevance) provides a systematic way to discover hidden biases, spurious correlations, and limitations in multimodal datasets and models.
- **Enhanced Model Robustness and Safety:** The case study in melanoma detection vividly demonstrates the practical value of identifying and mitigating spurious correlations. Such insights are crucial for developing trustworthy and robust AI systems, especially in high-stakes domains like healthcare, where hidden biases can lead to unreliable or harmful outcomes.
- **Scientific Discovery:** Beyond debugging, the framework can facilitate scientific discovery by revealing novel, implicit knowledge or perceptual strategies learned by large AI models that might not align with human annotations or expectations.

**Limitations:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Focus on Final Class Token Embedding:** The analysis primarily focuses on the class token embedding from the final transformer block, which is most predictive. However, this might overlook important dynamics or concept formation in earlier layers of the model.
- **Reliance on SAEs:** While SAEs are powerful, other sparse dictionary learning methods exist, and challenges like polysemanticity (where a component might encode multiple concepts) or hidden latent concepts that are hard to describe textually might still persist.
- **Semantic Alignment Model:** The semantic alignment of components relies on CLIP-Mobile-S2, which itself is a CLIP-based model and could potentially suffer from similar spurious correlations or ambiguous language as the models being analyzed, potentially affecting the accuracy of component labeling.

In conclusion, "From What to How" offers a vital toolset for researchers and practitioners alike, moving the field towards a more profound and actionable understanding of complex multimodal foundation models, thereby paving the way for their more reliable and safe deployment.
