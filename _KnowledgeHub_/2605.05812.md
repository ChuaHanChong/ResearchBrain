# Long-Horizon Q-Learning: Accurate Value Learning via n-Step Inequalities

> [!summary] Summary
> Long-horizon Q-learning (LQL) introduces a method to improve off-policy value learning by augmenting standard TD error with hinge penalties derived from n-step optimality inequalities. This approach leads to more accurate and stable Q-function estimates, particularly for long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks, with minimal computational overhead.

## Problem

- Deep value-based reinforcement learning suffers from instability due to compounding estimation errors, especially when bootstrapping with off-policy data and function approximation.
- Traditional multi-step backups (e.g., n-step returns) introduce off-policy bias, as they incorporate rewards from potentially suboptimal logged actions, leading to inaccurate value updates.
- Prior methods for long-horizon consistency (like optimality tightening) often require additional Q-evaluations or auxiliary networks, leading to computational inefficiency.

## Method

- **LQL** augments the standard temporal-difference (TD) error with two-sided hinge penalty terms derived from optimality inequalities across trajectories.
- It uses Lower-Bound penalties to prevent Q-values from being too low and Upper-Bound penalties to prevent them from being too high relative to optimal values.
- The method reuses existing network computations for the penalties, incurring a minimal per-update slowdown without needing auxiliary networks or additional forward passes.

## Results

- LQL achieved higher average success rates across various robot locomotion and manipulation tasks, outperforming 1-step TD and traditional n-step TD (e.g., **71.2%** vs. **48.6%** on OGBench for Best-of-N policies).
- It demonstrated a substantial advantage on extremely long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks like `humanoidmaze-giant`, achieving **75.7%** success where 1-step TD failed entirely (**0%**).
- The algorithm introduced only a small average per-update slowdown of **4.7%** and maintained Q-value estimates within analytically valid ranges, preventing runaway overestimation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimality inequalities over trajectories provide a principled and computationally efficient way to impose long-horizon consistency on learned Q-values.
> - Asymmetric hinge penalties effectively correct value function inconsistencies without relying on high-variance importance sampling or additional complex architectures.
> - Trajectory length can serve as a robust scaling axis for value-based reinforcement learning when combined with these principled long-horizon constraints.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of off-policy, value-based reinforcement learning (RL), particularly focusing on Q-learning. Off-policy methods are appealing due to their ability to learn from diverse experience, including data collected by older policies or other agents, without requiring fresh on-policy rollouts. This is beneficial in domains where interaction is expensive, such as robotics, and for tasks requiring long-horizon behavior.

A significant challenge in deep value-based RL, however, is the instability introduced by bootstrapping, especially when combined with off-policy learning and function approximation—a combination often referred to as the "deadly triad." This instability manifests as estimation errors at later states propagating backward and compounding over time, leading to brittle long-horizon learning.

A common approach to mitigate this is the use of multi-step backups, such as n-step returns or λ-returns. These methods replace a single-step Bellman backup with a longer segment of observed rewards before bootstrapping. While this can accelerate reward propagation and reduce sensitivity to 1-step temporal difference (TD) error, it introduces its own issues in off-policy settings. Specifically, an n-step target necessarily incorporates rewards generated by the logged actions in the replayed trajectory. If these subsequent actions are suboptimal, the resulting backup can be pessimistic or biased, as it partially evaluates the initial state-action pair under the incorrect assumption that the agent will continue following the behavior policy rather than switching to the improving learned policy. Existing off-policy corrections for multi-step learning, such as importance sampling, often suffer from high variance at long horizons or require behavior and target action likelihoods, which can be computationally inconvenient for expressive generative policies used in modern robotics.

The concept of "optimality tightening" is a related prior approach that also leverages optimality inequalities to derive hinge losses. However, these methods typically require additional Q-evaluations per update or auxiliary networks, which can significantly reduce computational efficiency.

Long-horizon Q-learning (LQL) positions itself as an advancement that aims to retain the benefits of 1-step off-policy TD learning (simplicity, low variance) while introducing a principled long-horizon backstop against compounding error. It seeks to resolve the tension between leveraging multi-step information and avoiding the off-policy bias of traditional n-step targets, and to do so without incurring the computational overhead of previous optimality tightening methods or requiring explicit action likelihoods.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a practical and robust algorithm for value learning in off-policy reinforcement learning that can accurately estimate optimal action-value functions, particularly over long horizons, while mitigating the known issues of error propagation and instability.

The key motivations driving this work are:
1. **Addressing Compounding Error in Bootstrapping:** Off-policy value-based methods, while powerful for learning from diverse data, suffer from the practical fragility of bootstrapping over long horizons. Errors in value estimates at future states can amplify as they propagate backward through TD updates, leading to unstable and inaccurate Q-function learning. This is particularly problematic in tasks requiring extended sequences of actions to achieve sparse rewards.
2. **Overcoming Off-Policy Bias in Multi-step Targets:** While multi-step returns can reduce reliance on single-step bootstrapped estimates and accelerate learning, their reliance on logged actions for intermediate steps introduces a bias when the behavior policy differs from the target policy (i.e., in off-policy settings). The learned target policy is improving, but the n-step return incorporates rewards from potentially suboptimal actions taken by the data-collecting policy. This can lead to pessimistic or inaccurate updates that hinder effective long-horizon credit assignment.
3. **Maintaining Computational Efficiency:** Existing methods that attempt to incorporate long-horizon consistency, such as prior optimality tightening approaches, often introduce significant computational overhead, such as requiring auxiliary networks or multiple additional forward passes of the Q-network per update. The authors are motivated to develop a mechanism that can stabilize long-horizon learning without substantially increasing runtime, ideally by reusing computations already necessary for standard TD updates.
4. **Leveraging Optimality Principles:** The work is motivated by a fundamental observation from optimality theory: for any given state, the optimal policy cannot achieve a worse expected return than following an arbitrary sequence of observed actions for some duration and then switching to optimal behavior. This "optimality inequality over trajectories" provides a principled foundation for deriving constraints on value estimates across time.
5. **Enabling Scalable Learning for Complex Tasks:** Many real-world applications of RL, such as robotics, involve long horizons, sparse rewards, and the need to learn from large, heterogeneous datasets. The current limitations of value-based methods in these scenarios necessitate a more robust learning mechanism that can extract maximum learning signal from each transition and scale effectively with problem complexity.

The overarching goal is to augment standard TD learning with a principled, computationally efficient long-horizon backstop that can detect and correct inconsistencies in value estimates, thereby fostering accurate value learning and improved policy performance over extended temporal sequences.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The core methodology of Long-horizon Q-learning (LQL) involves augmenting the standard temporal-difference (TD) error with additional hinge penalty terms derived from optimality inequalities. This approach aims to impose long-horizon consistency on the learned Q-function without sacrificing the benefits of 1-step TD learning.

The approach can be broken down into three main components:
1. **Derivation of Optimality Inequalities over Trajectories:**
 The foundation of LQL lies in the principle that, from any state, acting optimally immediately should not yield a worse expected return than committing to an observed sequence of actions for a period and then acting optimally. Two variants of this inequality are used:
 - $Q^*(s_i, a^*(s_i)) \ge E[G_{i:j} + \gamma^{j-i} Q^*(s_j, a_j)]$ (Equation 4): This states that acting optimally at state $s_i$ should be at least as good as following the observed rewards $G_{i:j}$ and then committing to the *observed* action $a_j$ at state $s_j$ (followed by optimal actions thereafter).
 - $Q^*(s_i, a_i) \ge E[G_{i:j} + \gamma^{j-i} Q^*(s_j, a^*(s_j))]$ (Equation 5): This states that the value of the *observed* action $a_i$ at state $s_i$ should be at least as good as following the observed rewards $G_{i:j}$ and then committing to an *optimal* action $a^*(s_j)$ at state $s_j$. When $j = i+1$, this reduces to the Bellman optimality equation.
 These inequalities provide soft constraints on the relationships between value estimates across different time steps within a trajectory.

2. **Formulating Two-Sided Hinge Penalties:**
 Instead of enforcing hard constraints, LQL translates these inequalities into asymmetric (hinge) penalty terms, added to the standard TD loss. These penalties are designed to push Q-values in the correct direction when inconsistencies are detected.
 - **Lower-Bound (LB) Penalties:** Derived from a rearrangement of Equation 5, these penalties apply when $Q_\theta(s_k, a_k)$ is *too low* compared to the return realized by following observed actions ($G_{k:l}$) and then bootstrapping with an (approximate) optimal action ($a^*_l$) at a later state $s_l$.
 The penalty term is $\delta_{LB}(k, \ell; \theta) = [G_{k:\ell} + \gamma^{\ell-k} Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s_\ell, a^*_\ell) - Q_\theta(s_k, a_k)]^2_+$. The $a^*_\ell$ (bootstrap action) is obtained from the learned policy (e.g., argmax in discrete spaces, or $\pi_\phi(s_\ell)$ in continuous control), and $Q_{\bar{\theta}}$ is the target network for stability. These penalties are aggregated over a set of future indices $F(k)$, typically excluding $k+1$ as that largely overlaps with the 1-step TD target.
 - **Upper-Bound (UB) Penalties:** Derived from a rearrangement of Equation 4, these penalties apply when $Q_\theta(s_k, a_k)$ is *too high* relative to what could be achieved by acting optimally earlier, as indicated by $Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s_i, a^*_i)$ and the preceding observed rewards $G_{i:k}$.
 The penalty term is $\delta_{UB}(i, k; \theta) = [G_{i:k} + \gamma^{k-i} Q_\theta(s_k, a_k) - Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s_i, a^*_i)]^2_+$. Here, $Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s_i, a^*_i)$ serves as a stable reference. A crucial aspect is that for $i=k$, this term provides a same-state upper bound, pushing down $Q_\theta(s_k, a_k)$ if it exceeds $Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s_k, a^*_k)$. These penalties are aggregated over a set of past indices $P(k)$, including $i=k$.

3. **Final Training Objective and Computational Efficiency:**
 The overall LQL training objective for a transition index $k$ within a sampled trajectory is defined as a sum of the standard 1-step TD loss and the weighted LB and UB penalties:
 $L_{LQL}(k; \theta) = L_{TD}(k; \theta) + \lambda_{UB} L_{UB}(k; \theta) + \lambda_{LB} L_{LB}(k; \theta)$.
 The weights $\lambda_{UB}$ and $\lambda_{LB}$ are typically set to 1. The total loss for a trajectory is the average over all $k$.

 A key design principle for LQL is **computational efficiency**. The calculation of both LB and UB penalties reuses the network outputs already required for the standard TD loss computation. Specifically:
 - $Q_\theta(s_k, a_k)$ is computed for all observed state-action pairs in the trajectory (needed for $L_{TD}$).
 - $Q_{\bar{\theta}}(s_k, a^*_k)$ (target network evaluations for bootstrap actions) is computed for corresponding states (needed for TD targets).
 This means LQL introduces **no auxiliary networks and no additional forward passes** of the Q-network compared to standard Q-learning. Discounted partial returns $G_{i:j}$ are calculated efficiently using prefix sums.
 The method is primarily formulated as an augmentation to 1-step TD learning but is also shown to be compatible with action chunking, where penalties are applied across action chunks. While the optimality inequalities are stated in expectation, LQL uses a single sampled replay trajectory to approximate these expectations, which is unbiased under deterministic dynamics but introduces bias under stochastic dynamics, a trade-off explored empirically.

### 4. Main findings and results

The empirical evaluation of Long-horizon Q-learning (LQL) consistently demonstrated its effectiveness in improving value learning and policy performance across various challenging reinforcement learning benchmarks.

1. **Superior Performance Across Diverse Benchmarks:** LQL consistently achieved higher average success rates compared to both 1-step TD and length-matched n-step TD across a range of robot locomotion and manipulation tasks from OGBench and RoboMimic datasets. This performance advantage was observed across four distinct policy extraction mechanisms: Gaussian actors (RLPD-style), Best-of-N (BFN) sampling from flow-matching behavior policies, Flow Q-learning (FQL), and Action Chunking policies (QC-FQL style).
 - For example, in OGBench, LQL achieved a total average success rate of 71.2% for Best-of-N policies, compared to 48.6% for 1-step TD and 54.5% for length-matched n-step TD. Similar improvements were noted for other policy types.
 - N-step TD often showed only modest improvements over 1-step TD, and in some cases, it performed worse (e.g., cube-triple with FQL), consistent with the known off-policy bias of n-step targets. LQL's method of using multi-step information via optimality inequalities rather than direct targets effectively addresses this bias.

2. **Exceptional Performance on Long-Horizon, Sparse-Reward Tasks:** LQL exhibited a notable advantage on tasks requiring very long horizons and sparse rewards. On the `humanoidmaze-giant` task, which demands thousands of environment steps for completion:
 - 1-step TD failed to solve any of the five tasks (0% success).
 - N-step TD showed some benefit, peaking at n=4 with 38.4% average success, but performance degraded as $n$ increased, reaching only 6.1% at $n=64$.
 - LQL with a trajectory length of L=64 achieved 75.7% success, saturating performance on two tasks (97.3% and 98.7%). This demonstrates LQL's capacity to effectively absorb and utilize information from very long trajectories, where traditional multi-step TD methods falter due to off-policy bias.

3. **Trajectory Length as a Robust Scaling Axis:** The research identified trajectory length (L) as an effective scaling axis for LQL. When the number of trajectories per batch was fixed (meaning increased total compute for larger L), LQL's final success rate consistently improved with increasing L. This contrasts with traditional TD learning, where increasing batch size (an equivalent increase in transitions/compute) does not reliably improve performance and can even degrade it beyond a certain point, a phenomenon often attributed to overfitting to inaccurate target estimates.

4. **Minimal Computational Overhead:** The observed gains were achieved with a small average per-update slowdown of 4.7% relative to standard TD methods. This overhead results from computing pairwise discounted returns within sampled trajectories and is considered negligible, especially as network sizes grow or for longer trajectories (e.g., L=64).

5. **Attribution of Gains to Hinge Penalties:** Ablation studies confirmed that the hinge penalties are the primary source of LQL's performance improvements. Simply sampling short trajectories (without the hinge penalties) led to substantially reduced performance compared to the full LQL, indicating that the long-horizon constraints provide a crucial backstop.

6. **Robustness to Environmental Stochasticity:** Despite a theoretical bias introduced by approximating in-expectation inequalities with sampled trajectories in stochastic environments, LQL maintained performance on par with or better than 1-step TD across various levels of induced Gaussian action noise.

7. **Stable Q-Value Estimates:** Direct analysis of learned Q-values showed that LQL maintained estimates within the analytically valid range for tasks with non-positive rewards, preventing the runaway overestimation observed in 1-step TD (where Q-values could diverge to large positive magnitudes). This directly supports the effectiveness of the hinge upper-bound penalty in stabilizing value function learning.

8. **Outperformance of Prior Optimality Tightening:** A comparison to the closest prior method, Optimality Tightening (He et al., 2016), demonstrated that LQL achieved superior performance. This validates LQL's design choices regarding the formulation of optimality inequalities and the reuse of network outputs, which avoid the additional computational demands of OT.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The introduction of Long-horizon Q-learning (LQL) carries several significant implications for the field of reinforcement learning:

1. **Enhanced Reliability of Off-Policy Value Learning:** LQL provides a practical and principled solution to a long-standing challenge in deep off-policy RL: the brittleness and instability of bootstrapping over long horizons due to compounding TD errors. By introducing a "backstop" against these errors, LQL enables more accurate and stable value function learning, which is fundamental for effective policy improvement.

2. **Efficient Integration of Long-Horizon Information:** The method's ability to leverage long-horizon optimality inequalities without requiring auxiliary networks or additional forward passes of the Q-network is a notable advancement. This computational efficiency allows LQL to be readily integrated into existing Q-learning frameworks with minimal overhead, making it practical for a wide range of applications, including those with large neural networks.

3. **Enabling Complex, Long-Horizon Applications:** The stark performance improvement of LQL on tasks like `humanoidmaze-giant`, which are characterized by sparse rewards and extremely long horizons where traditional methods fail, is particularly impactful. This suggests that LQL can unlock the potential for value-based RL in complex, real-world domains such as robotics and autonomous systems, where long-term planning and robust credit assignment are crucial and data collection is expensive.

4. **A Novel and Robust Scaling Axis:** The finding that trajectory length serves as an effective scaling axis for LQL, leading to improved performance with increasing trajectory length (and corresponding increased compute), offers an alternative to the often problematic scaling of batch sizes in TD learning. This suggests a more stable and effective way to infuse more data and computational resources into value-based RL, potentially leading to better performance without the degradation sometimes observed with larger batch sizes.

5. **Improved Q-Value Stability:** The empirical evidence demonstrating LQL's ability to maintain Q-values within analytically valid ranges, preventing runaway overestimation, is a critical contribution. Stable and accurate value estimates are essential for reliable policy extraction and overall system robustness, especially in safety-critical applications.

6. **Broader Applicability and Compatibility:** LQL's demonstrated compatibility with various policy extraction mechanisms (e.g., Gaussian, Flow Q-learning, Best-of-N, Action Chunking) underscores its generality and potential for widespread adoption across different contemporary deep RL architectures and design choices.

Future impacts could include:
- **Advancements in Robotics:** Given the prevalence of long-horizon and sparse-reward problems in robotics, LQL could significantly enhance the capabilities of robots to learn complex manipulation and navigation tasks from diverse datasets.
- **Foundation for Future Algorithmic Development:** The LQL framework provides a foundation for further research, such as combining its stability benefits with the faster reward propagation properties of n-step TD, or conducting deeper mechanistic studies into the individual contributions of the upper- and lower-bound terms.
- **More Stable Offline RL:** The principles employed by LQL could contribute to more stable and effective offline reinforcement learning, where the challenges of off-policy data are paramount.

In summary, LQL represents a meaningful step toward making off-policy, value-based reinforcement learning more robust and scalable, particularly for challenging long-horizon tasks, by integrating principled optimality constraints in a computationally efficient manner.
