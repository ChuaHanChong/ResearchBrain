# Training Vision Transformers with Only 2040 Images

> [!summary] Summary
> A self-supervised learning approach, Instance Discrimination with Multi-crop and CutMix (IDMM), enables effective pretraining of Vision Transformers (ViTs) using datasets with as few as 2040 images. This method achieves state-of-the-art accuracy on various small datasets and substantially narrows the performance difference compared to ViTs pretrained on large-scale datasets like ImageNet.

## Problem

- Vision Transformers (ViTs) are inherently data-hungry, requiring pretraining on massive datasets (e.g., ImageNet, JFT-300M) due to their lack of convolutional inductive biases.
- The necessity of large-scale pretraining for ViTs incurs high computational costs, limiting accessibility and flexibility for researchers and practitioners.
- Existing self-supervised learning methods for ViTs primarily focus on large datasets, leaving a gap in strategies for effective training from scratch on truly limited data.

## Method

- The **Instance Discrimination with Multi-crop and CutMix (IDMM)** framework uses ==parametric instance discrimination==, treating each image as a unique class with learnable output layer weights.
- It integrates data augmentation strategies like ==multi-crop== and ==CutMix== with ==label smoothing== to provide frequent, diverse gradient updates to the learnable weights, even with limited instances.
- Training is conducted in two stages: self-supervised pretraining at a small resolution (112x112) followed by supervised fine-tuning at higher resolutions (224x224, 448x448) on the target small dataset.

## Results

- IDMM consistently outperformed other self-supervised methods and random initialization on 7 small datasets, achieving **79.9%** accuracy on Flowers (2040 images) where the next best was **72.3%**.
- The method significantly reduced the performance gap between ViTs trained from scratch on small datasets and those pretrained on ImageNet, making small-data ViT training viable.
- Representations learned via IDMM on one small dataset demonstrated effective transferability to other small datasets, and pretraining on a 10,000-image ImageNet subset improved full ImageNet training by approximately **1 percentage point**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Parametric instance discrimination, with its learnable weights, is approximately equivalent to non-parametric contrastive learning but offers advantages by separating representation learning and capturing implicit instance similarities.
> - The learnable weights in parametric instance discrimination can obviate the need for an auxiliary projection MLP head, which is often crucial for non-parametric contrastive methods.
> - Combining label smoothing and CutMix significantly addresses the 'infrequent updating' problem in instance discrimination, ensuring all instance weights receive sufficient gradients and regularizing the model for small datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Vision Transformers (ViTs) have emerged as an alternative to Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for various visual recognition tasks, demonstrating competitive performance. However, a known limitation of ViTs is their significant data hunger compared to CNNs. This characteristic is often attributed to their lack of inherent convolutional inductive biases, such as locality, translation invariance, and hierarchical structure, which are naturally present in CNN architectures. Consequently, ViTs are typically pretrained on massive datasets like JFT-300M or at least ImageNet (1.28 million images) to achieve high performance.

The broader research landscape for ViTs has seen efforts to address this data scarcity issue in several ways. One prominent approach involves integrating convolutional layers or inductive biases into ViT architectures (e.g., Swin Transformer, PVT, T2T-ViT, CvT). While these hybrid architectures have shown improved performance on large datasets like ImageNet, their behavior and effectiveness when trained on genuinely small datasets remain less explored.

Simultaneously, self-supervised learning (SSL) has become a dominant paradigm for learning visual representations without explicit labels, particularly useful for pretraining deep neural networks. Many contemporary SSL methods, such as SimCLR, MoCo, SupCon, and DINO, primarily focus on scenarios involving large-scale datasets. These methods often employ contrastive learning principles to bring positive pairs (augmented views of the same image) closer in embedding space while pushing negative pairs (views from different images) apart. While some SSL methods have been adapted for ViTs (e.g., MoCov3, DINO), these adaptations largely operate within the large-dataset context.

This work specifically addresses a gap in the literature by investigating the training of ViTs *from scratch* with *limited data*. Existing research, such as that by Liu et al., has examined ViTs with small datasets, but often focuses on the fine-tuning stage. In contrast, this paper centers on improving the self-supervised pretraining stage on small datasets, which is a less investigated area, particularly for pure ViT architectures. The motivation extends beyond mere performance to encompass practical considerations of data availability, computational cost, and deployment flexibility, positioning this work as a foundational step towards making ViTs more accessible and applicable in resource-constrained environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate methodologies for effectively training Vision Transformers (ViTs) from scratch using only limited data, exemplified by datasets containing as few as 2040 images.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several practical and theoretical considerations:

- **Addressing ViT's Data Hunger:** ViTs inherently lack the inductive biases (e.g., locality, translation invariance) found in CNNs, which makes them data-hungry. They typically require pretraining on extremely large datasets, such as JFT-300M or ImageNet, to achieve competitive performance. This dependency limits their applicability in domains where large datasets are not readily available.
- **Mitigating High Computational Costs:** The necessity of pretraining ViTs on massive datasets with complex architectures leads to extremely high computational demands. This restricts the use and research of ViTs to institutions with substantial computing resources, creating a barrier for broader scientific community access. The goal is to explore methods that reduce this computational burden by enabling effective training on smaller, task-specific datasets.
- **Enhancing Training Flexibility:** The standard paradigm of pretraining on a large dataset followed by fine-tuning on a downstream task can be cumbersome. For scenarios requiring deployment of multiple models of varying sizes or adaptation to diverse hardware platforms, pretraining numerous models on large datasets is impractical. Training directly from scratch on target datasets offers greater flexibility and potentially better parameter-accuracy trade-offs for specific deployment contexts.
- **Understanding ViT Behavior in Data-Scarce Regimes:** While extensive research focuses on ViTs with abundant data, their performance characteristics and optimal training strategies under severe data constraints are not fully understood. This paper aims to contribute to this understanding, especially for architectures that might not incorporate convolutional inductive biases.
- **Improving Self-Supervised Pretraining for Small Data:** The work specifically focuses on enhancing the self-supervised pretraining stage, recognizing its critical role in learning robust representations even when labels are scarce or for subsequent supervised fine-tuning. By optimizing this stage for limited data, the researchers aim to unlock ViT's potential in such challenging environments.

In summary, the research is motivated by the desire to democratize ViT usage by demonstrating that high-performance ViT models can be developed and deployed without reliance on vast datasets and immense computational power, thereby expanding their applicability to a wider range of real-world problems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology proposed in this paper, named Instance Discrimination with Multi-crop and CutMix (IDMM), focuses on a two-stage learning process: initial self-supervised pretraining followed by supervised fine-tuning. Both stages are performed exclusively on the target small dataset. The core of the contribution lies in the self-supervised pretraining stage.

#### 3.1. Core Pretraining Method: Parametric Instance Discrimination

The foundation of IDMM is parametric instance discrimination, an approach where each input image is treated as a unique class.
- **Mechanism:** An input image `x_i` is passed through a network `f(·)` to obtain a representation `z_i`. This `z_i` is then fed into a fully connected (FC) layer with weights `W = [w_1 | ... | w_N]`, where `N` is the total number of training instances (classes). The output `O^(i) = W^T z_i` is then normalized via a softmax layer to yield a probability distribution `P^(i)`. The loss function, `L_InsDis`, is a standard cross-entropy loss, where the model is trained to classify `x_i` correctly into its own instance ID `i`.
- **Theoretical Analyses:**
 - **Equivalence to Contrastive Loss:** The paper demonstrates that instance discrimination is approximately equivalent to the contrastive learning (CL) loss when the weights `w_j` in the FC layer are set to the representations `z_j` of other instances. Both loss formulations include an "alignment" term (encouraging positive features to be close) and a "uniformity" term (encouraging features to be distributed on a hypersphere). This establishes a theoretical link between parametric instance discrimination and non-parametric contrastive methods.
 - **Advantages of Learnable Weights `W`:** The key distinction of parametric instance discrimination is that `W` is learnable, unlike in typical contrastive methods where `w_j` is often `z_j` itself. This learnable `W` provides two primary advantages:
 1. **Separation of Representation Learning:** It allows for separation of representation learning from learning specific properties tied to the loss function. The paper empirically shows that this makes an auxiliary projection MLP head (often critical in contrastive methods like SimCLR) unnecessary for instance discrimination.
 2. **Capturing Instance Similarities:** When `W` is appropriately learned, the instance discrimination loss can become equivalent to a deep clustering loss, where `w_j` for instances within the same cluster `C_k` converge to a shared `w_k`. This mechanism allows the method to implicitly discover and leverage potential similarities between instances (images), going beyond mere instance-level discrimination.
- **Stability for ViTs:** The cross-entropy formulation of instance discrimination is noted for its simplicity and stability, which is advantageous given the known instability issues in self-supervised ViT training.

#### 3.2. Gradient Analysis and Strategies for Small Data

The paper performs a gradient analysis of `L_InsDis` with respect to instance representations `z_i` and FC layer weights `w_k`. This analysis highlights a critical issue for large `N`: infrequent instances `k ≠ i` receive very small or zero gradients for `w_k`, leading to infrequent weight updates and potential under-optimization. To address this, especially for small datasets, the authors integrate three strategies:

1. **Small Resolution:** Inspired by prior work, smaller image resolutions (e.g., 112x112) are used during pretraining. This reduces computational load and can be beneficial for small datasets.
2. **Multi-crop:** Multiple augmented views (crops) of an input image are generated. By extending the loss formulation to incorporate these views, instance discrimination can simultaneously capture both feature alignment (between positive views) and uniformity across the feature space, similar to how multi-crop is used in contrastive learning.
3. **CutMix and Label Smoothing:**
 - **Label Smoothing:** By slightly distributing the target probability (`1-ε`) to the correct class and a small `ε/(N-1)` to all other classes, label smoothing ensures that all `w_k` receive a non-zero, albeit small, gradient, alleviating the "infrequent updating" problem.
 - **CutMix:** This data augmentation technique involves cutting a patch from one image and pasting it onto another, blending their labels according to the mixed region ratio (`λ`). When combined with label smoothing, CutMix further ensures that the weights `w_k` for *both* original instances (from which the mixed input `˜z_ii'` was derived) and even other instances receive updated gradients. This mechanism helps regularize the model, preventing overfitting on limited data and contributing to more frequent and diverse weight updates.

These combined strategies form the IDMM framework.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

- **Datasets:** Experiments were conducted on 7 small datasets: Flowers (2040 images), Pets (3680 images), DTD (3760 images), Indoor67 (5360 images), CUB200 (5994 images), Aircrafts (6667 images), and Cars (8144 images).
- **Backbones:** Various ViT architectures were used, including DeiT-Tiny, DeiT-Base, PVTv2-B0, PVTv2-B3, and T2T-ViT-7.
- **Training Protocol:**
 - Pretraining: 800 epochs with 112x112 resolution for all methods.
 - Fine-tuning: 200 epochs with 224x224 resolution. For best results, some models were further fine-tuned for 100 epochs at 448x448 resolution.
 - Optimization: AdamW optimizer, cosine learning rate decay, batch size 256.
- **Comparisons:** IDMM was compared against random initialization and several prominent self-supervised methods: SimCLR, SupCon, MoCov2, MoCov3, and DINO. Ablation studies were conducted to evaluate the individual contributions of each component within IDMM and the necessity of the projection MLP head. Transfer learning capabilities were also evaluated by pretraining on one small dataset and fine-tuning on another, as well as by pretraining on a 10,000-image subset of ImageNet (SIN-10k) and transferring to small datasets or the full ImageNet.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding training Vision Transformers (ViTs) with limited data.

#### 4.1. Training from Scratch Results

- **Superior Performance on Small Datasets:** IDMM consistently outperformed other self-supervised learning (SSL) methods (SimCLR, SupCon, MoCov2, MoCov3, DINO) and random initialization when training from scratch on the 7 small datasets. For instance, on the Flowers dataset (2040 images), IDMM achieved 79.9% accuracy compared to the next best, SupCon, at 72.3% (Table 2).
- **State-of-the-Art Accuracy:** With extended fine-tuning (800 epochs at 224x224, then 100 epochs at 448x448), IDMM achieved results on par with or exceeding previously reported state-of-the-art for training ViTs from scratch on these datasets. For example, using a PVTv2-B3 backbone, IDMM achieved 96.7% accuracy on Flowers, 91.9% on Pets, and 94.3% on Cars (Table 3).
- **Reduced Gap to ImageNet Pretraining:** The performance gap between models trained from scratch using IDMM and those pretrained on ImageNet was significantly reduced across various ViT backbones and datasets (Table 3). This indicates the viability of training ViTs without relying on large-scale pretraining.
- **Improved Parameter-Accuracy Trade-off:** The approach enabled training smaller ViT models (e.g., 0.8M parameters with IDMM) that achieved competitive accuracy, offering better parameter-accuracy trade-offs for deployment on resource-constrained devices compared to larger, ImageNet-pretrained models (Figure 3).
- **Training Stability:** Standard deviation analysis over multiple runs confirmed the stability of the IDMM pretraining and fine-tuning stages, even on the smallest datasets (Table 4).

#### 4.2. Transfer Ability of Small Datasets

- **Effective Transfer Learning:** ViT representations learned using IDMM on one small dataset demonstrated effective transferability to other small datasets, even those in different domains (Table 5). This suggests that features learned on limited, domain-specific data can be generalized.
- **Superior Transfer Accuracy:** IDMM consistently yielded higher transferring accuracy compared to SimCLR and SupCon across most cross-dataset transfer scenarios. This supports the theoretical argument that IDMM's learnable FC layer facilitates better feature alignment and instance similarity capture.
- **Impact of Pretraining Data Size:** While larger pretraining datasets (e.g., Cars) generally led to better transfer performance, the quality or domain relevance of the pretraining data also played a role. For instance, pretraining on Indoor67 sometimes outperformed Cars when transferring to Aircraft, despite Indoor67 having fewer images.
- **Enhancing ImageNet Training:** Pretraining ViTs with IDMM on a small subset of ImageNet (SIN-10k, 10,000 images) significantly benefited subsequent full ImageNet training. This initialization accelerated the training process and resulted in approximately a 1 percentage point increase in final Top-1 accuracy compared to random initialization (Table 7). The method also outperformed other SSL baselines when pretraining on SIN-10k for transfer to small datasets (Table 6).
- **Robustness to Sampling Strategy:** For SIN-10k, whether the images were sampled in a balanced manner (e.g., 10 images per class) or completely randomly had a negligible effect on the ImageNet transfer results, indicating robustness to the specific label-aware sampling strategy.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Contribution of IDMM Components:** All four components of IDMM—label smoothing, small resolution, multi-crop, and CutMix—were found to contribute positively to performance when training from scratch on Flowers. The combined application of these strategies yielded the highest accuracy (Table 8).
- **Necessity of Projection MLP Head:** For IDMM (parametric instance discrimination), the presence or absence of an additional projection MLP head (common in contrastive learning) had a minimal effect on performance, and in some cases, not using it performed slightly better (Table 9). In contrast, for non-parametric contrastive methods like SimCLR, the projection MLP head was observed to be essential for achieving optimal results, validating the theoretical claim that the learnable `W` in instance discrimination implicitly fulfills the role of such a head.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of computer vision by demonstrating that Vision Transformers (ViTs) can be effectively trained and deployed in data-scarce environments, challenging the prevailing notion that they are inherently data-hungry and necessitate massive pretraining datasets.

The significance and potential impact of this work are multi-faceted:

- **Expanded Applicability of ViTs:** By providing a method to train ViTs efficiently with limited data, the research broadens the practical applicability of these powerful architectures. This enables their use in specialized domains where data collection is inherently difficult or costly, such as medical imaging, fine-grained classification of rare species, industrial inspection, or specific scientific imaging tasks, without requiring extensive initial data collection or pretraining on general-purpose datasets.
- **Reduced Computational Barriers:** The proposed IDMM approach alleviates the reliance on extensive computational resources typically needed for large-scale ViT pretraining. This makes ViT research and application more accessible to a wider range of academic institutions and small businesses that may lack supercomputing infrastructure, fostering greater innovation and participation in the field.
- **Enhanced Deployment Flexibility:** The ability to train ViTs effectively from scratch on smaller, task-specific datasets allows for more flexible model development and deployment. Researchers and practitioners can tailor ViT models more precisely to target applications, potentially achieving better parameter-accuracy trade-offs for varied hardware platforms and latency requirements (e.g., edge devices), without the overhead of adapting general-purpose large models.
- **Advancements in Self-Supervised Learning Theory and Practice:** The theoretical analysis distinguishing parametric instance discrimination from non-parametric contrastive methods offers deeper insights into how self-supervised learning operates. The finding that a learnable final classification layer can obviate the need for an auxiliary projection head is a significant practical and theoretical simplification, potentially guiding future SSL architecture designs.
- **Novel Pretraining Strategies:** The demonstration of good transferability from models pretrained on small datasets, and even their ability to improve large-scale ImageNet training, suggests new paradigms for hierarchical or progressive pretraining. This could lead to more efficient pretraining strategies where initial learning occurs on smaller, potentially domain-specific datasets before scaling to larger, more generalized ones.
- **Improved Resource Utilization:** By reducing the need for massive data collection and extensive pretraining, the method contributes to more efficient utilization of computational and data resources in AI development, aligning with growing concerns about the environmental impact of large-scale deep learning.

In conclusion, this paper provides a robust framework and empirical evidence for the viability of training ViTs on limited data. It opens avenues for democratizing ViT technology, advancing the theoretical understanding of self-supervised learning, and enabling more resource-efficient and flexible AI solutions in computer vision.
