# World Model Failure Classification and Anomaly Detection for Autonomous Inspection

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed a hybrid framework for real-time failure classification and anomaly detection in autonomous inspection robots, leveraging a world model backbone and conformal prediction. The system achieved over 90% accuracy in distinguishing success, known failures, and out-of-distribution conditions for gauge inspection, often identifying issues earlier than human observation.

## Problem

- Autonomous inspection robots face challenges from environmental factors like occlusions, glare, and limited viewpoints, leading to inaccurate readings or perception failures.
- There is a need to differentiate between known, repeatable failure modes and novel, unexpected out-of-distribution (OOD) anomalies for appropriate robot responses.
- Ensuring real-time, online failure detection is critical for timely corrective actions and enhancing safety in industrial robotic systems.

## Method

- A hybrid framework integrates ==supervised failure classification== with ==anomaly detection==, built upon a ==world model backbone== for architectural simplicity and real-time applicability.
- Two predictive models are trained: one for successful task data and another for known failure data, to predict future observations from current inputs.
- ==Conformal Prediction== (CP) calibrates fixed thresholds on anomaly scores using separate success and failure datasets, enabling the classification of inputs as success, known failure, or OOD.

## Results

- The framework achieved over **90%** classification accuracy for success, known failure, and out-of-distribution cases using the latent prediction error metric at a **90%** conformal prediction quantile.
- Classifications were made, on average, **1 to 3 seconds** earlier than human observers, particularly with the latent prediction error, indicating sensitivity to precursor patterns.
- Successful deployment on a Boston Dynamics Spot robot for gauge inspection demonstrated operational feasibility, with the system capable of prompting corrective actions like camera adjustments for OOD conditions.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The latent prediction error metric consistently delivered robust performance, achieving over 90% accuracy across all three classification categories (success, known failure, OOD) while also providing anticipatory detection.
> - Conformal Prediction effectively calms thresholds for anomaly scores, allowing for reliable separation between nominal, known failure, and OOD conditions without requiring extensive labeled anomaly data.
> - The framework's policy-agnostic design, coupled with a world model backbone, demonstrates adaptability for various autonomous inspection tasks and enables deployment on resource-constrained robotic platforms.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous inspection robots offer potential benefits in industrial environments by reducing costs, improving consistency, and mitigating risks associated with human-led monitoring. These robots are increasingly deployed for tasks such as thermal and acoustic monitoring, 3D facility mapping, and gauge inspection. However, their reliance on vision-based perception systems introduces challenges. Environmental factors such as occlusions, limited viewpoints, glare, and shadows can compromise the accuracy of readings. Furthermore, robots may encounter conditions not present in their training data, leading to perception failures. Addressing these challenges is critical for the reliable and safe operation of autonomous inspection systems.

The broader research landscape for ensuring robust autonomous operation encompasses several areas:
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection:** This field focuses on identifying when a system's input deviates significantly from the data it was trained on. OOD detection is crucial for flagging potential anomalies or novel conditions that could lead to failures. Existing methods range from statistical and reconstruction-based approaches to embedding-based, distribution modeling, ensemble, and Bayesian techniques. Recent work has explored OOD detection without explicit failure data, often leveraging world models and conformal prediction for uncertainty quantification.
- **Failure Classification:** In contrast to OOD detection, failure classification aims to categorize known types of deviations from nominal behavior. For industrial inspection, this can include specific perception failures (e.g., occluded gauges, poor viewing angles) or mobility issues. Supervised learning methods are commonly used for classification when failure modes are identifiable and labeled data is available.
- **Hybrid Approaches:** Some research attempts to combine the strengths of both OOD detection and failure classification. These approaches aim to both identify novel anomalies and categorize previously encountered failure types, providing more informative responses than either method alone. Examples include adding supervised heads to autoencoders or combining separate detection frameworks.
- **Conformal Prediction (CP):** CP is a statistical framework used for quantifying uncertainty and calibrating prediction intervals or thresholds. It is model-agnostic and can be applied to black-box models, making it suitable for safety-critical robotic applications. In anomaly detection, CP is used to set thresholds on anomaly scores, separating nominal from OOD inputs. Previous work has used CP for failure detection by calibrating scalar signals or prediction errors from world models.

This work addresses a gap in current approaches by proposing a unified hybrid framework that not only detects novel, unseen conditions (OOD) but also classifies known failure modes, leveraging a single world model backbone for architectural simplicity and real-time applicability. This combination aims to provide more actionable intelligence for autonomous systems operating in complex, dynamic environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a real-time, online failure detection system for autonomous inspection robots, specifically focusing on gauge inspection. The system aims to robustly classify the outcome of an inspection task into three categories: a success, a known failure, or an out-of-distribution (OOD) anomaly.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges inherent in autonomous industrial inspection:
- **Reliability and Accuracy in Dynamic Environments:** Autonomous robots in industrial settings need to provide consistent and accurate instrument readings. However, their vision systems are susceptible to various challenges such as partial occlusions, limited viewpoints, glare, shadows, and blurriness. These conditions can lead to inaccurate readings or a complete inability to obtain a reading.
- **Distinguishing Failure Types:** Not all deviations from ideal conditions are the same. Some are known, repeatable failure modes (e.g., consistent misreads due to fixed occlusions or poor camera angles) for which specific corrective actions can be defined. Others are novel, unexpected anomalies (e.g., unusual lighting conditions, environmental debris) that the robot has not encountered during training. The ability to distinguish between these cases is crucial for appropriate, informed decision-making by the robot. OOD detection alone signals a deviation but does not prescribe an action, while supervised failure classification cannot anticipate every possible unforeseen circumstance.
- **Reducing Costs and Risks:** Human-led inspections are costly, prone to inconsistencies due to fatigue, and can expose personnel to hazardous environments. Mobile robots offer a solution, but an undetected failure in a safety-critical system (e.g., a high-pressure gauge) could escalate into a major safety risk. Therefore, early and accurate failure detection is vital to leverage the benefits of autonomy while mitigating its risks.
- **Real-time Operability:** For autonomous inspection robots to be effective, failure detection must occur online and in real-time, enabling prompt corrective actions. The framework needs to be efficient enough for deployment on robot hardware.
- **Training Data Quality Assessment:** Beyond real-time operation, the ability to identify OOD cases and known failures can provide valuable feedback for improving robot training. By highlighting instances where the robot struggles or encounters novel conditions, the system can guide targeted data collection efforts, thereby improving the robustness of future models.

To address these motivations, the authors propose a hybrid framework designed to provide anticipatory failure detection, allowing the robot to take informative actions based on the classification and potentially serving as a feedback signal for model training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a hybrid framework that integrates supervised failure classification with anomaly detection, underpinned by a world model backbone and conformal prediction (CP) for threshold calibration.

**A. Problem Formulation**
The problem is framed as determining if and when a robot will fail its task during a trajectory $\tau_T = \{s_0, a_0, \dots, s_T, a_T\}$. The objective is to identify a failure time $k \leq T$ and classify the nature of the failure. The framework operates on observable trajectories $\tau_t \triangleq \{o_0, \dots, o_t\}$, assuming noise-free and fully observable camera observations. The robot's policy $\pi(s_t) = a_t$ is tele-operated and unknown to the framework, making it policy-agnostic. The detector is defined by two decision functions, $D_{\text{success}}(\tau_t; \theta_s)$ and $D_{\text{fail}}(\tau_t; \theta_f)$. An OOD classification occurs if both decision functions return 1. The detected time $\hat{k}$ is the earliest time either decision function signals an anomaly, and the classification $\hat{c}(\tau_{\hat{k}})$ is then determined by the combined output of $D_{\text{success}}$ and $D_{\text{fail}}$.

**B. Failure Classification and Anomaly Detection Framework**
The framework's core steps are:
1. **Model Training:** Two predictive models are trained: $M_{\text{success}}(\cdot; \phi_s)$ using successful task data and $M_{\text{fail}}(\cdot; \phi_f)$ using known failure data. These models predict future observations from current ones.
2. **Score Computation:** For any trajectory $\tau_t$, scores $\ell_{\text{success}}(\tau_t)$ and $\ell_{\text{fail}}(\tau_t)$ are computed using the respective models. These scores quantify the deviation of the input from what each model considers "in-distribution."
3. **Conformal Prediction Thresholding:** Fixed thresholds $\eta_s$ and $\eta_f$ are derived using CP on separate success and failure calibration datasets. These thresholds define the boundaries for what is considered nominal for each model. The decision functions $D_{\text{success}}(\tau_t; \theta_s)$ and $D_{\text{fail}}(\tau_t; \theta_f)$ return 1 if their respective scores exceed these thresholds.
4. **Consensus OOD Decision:** The two detectors are combined. A trajectory is classified as a "success" if it falls below the success threshold but above the failure threshold. It is a "known failure" if it falls below the failure threshold but above the success threshold. It is an "anomalous" (OOD) case if it exceeds both thresholds.

**C. Classifying Failures for Gauge Detection**
The framework is applied to autonomous gauge inspection.
- **Success:** Robot correctly centers and orients to obtain an accurate gauge reading.
- **Failure:** Robot produces an inaccurate reading (e.g., partial/full occlusion, poor angle, limited PTZ configuration space).
- **OOD:** Robot cannot produce a reading due to suboptimal but non-safety-critical conditions (e.g., shadows, glare, blurriness).

The objective is early detection ($k \leq T$) for timely corrective actions.

**D. Dataset Creation**
The dataset consisted of 290 videos, each averaging 10 seconds in length, collected from both office and industrial sites between July and August 2025. This included:
- **Training:** 14 success and 14 failure videos.
- **Validation:** 6 success and 6 failure videos.
- **CP Calibration:** 45 success and 37 failure videos.
- **Test:** 45 success, 37 failure, and 53 OOD videos.
Known failure types included missing the gauge, partial obstruction, and viewing at a wide angle. OOD cases involved conditions like shadows, glare, and blurriness preventing a reading.

**E. Model Architecture and Training**
The model architecture builds on a world model approach that predicts future frames from video inputs.
- **Tokenizer:** The NVIDIA Cosmos Tokenizer (Continuous Videos) is used with frozen pre-trained weights to tokenize video frames into latent representations. Images are compressed from 1200 × 700 to 512 × 288 before encoding to reduce memory and speed up training.
- **Latent World Model:** A latent world model predicts $z_{t+1}$ from $z_t$, where $z_t$ are the tokenized latents.
- **Training:** Training is implemented using PyTorch Lightning. The world model is optimized with a composite loss function: $L_{\text{total}} = L_{\text{rec}} + |L_{\text{rec}} - L_{\text{cross}}| + 0.5 L_{\text{hyb}}$.
 - $L_{\text{rec}}$: Weighted pixel loss (MSE+SSIM) for frame reconstruction.
 - $L_{\text{cross}}$: Enforces temporal consistency.
 - $L_{\text{hyb}}$: Combines latent prediction error, perceptual similarity (LPIPS), and a weak center-region prior.
- A 70/30 training/validation split is used, and a skip parameter is applied to reduce redundancy in consecutive frames. Each model training session lasted approximately 34 hours with early stopping.

**F. Conformal Prediction Threshold Calibration**
CP is used to calibrate thresholds on anomaly scores.
- **Calibration Process:** For each trained model ($M_{\text{success}}$ and $M_{\text{fail}}$), separate CP bands are created using success and failure calibration datasets. Instead of timestep-level scoring, the maximum score per trajectory is used to avoid temporal correlation issues. Thresholds are set at a chosen quantile level $(1-\alpha)$, typically 90% or 95%.
- **Scoring Metrics:** Various CP residual and distance-based scoring metrics (Table I) are evaluated, including:
 - Reconstruction error
 - Latent Prediction error
 - Latent standard deviation
 - Mahalanobis distance
 - Latent distance (L2)
 - Latent cosine distance
 - Training loss (for comparison)
- **Evaluation:** During testing, new videos are scored, and the decision function (Equation 1) classifies them. Constant-value bands are adopted for real-time deployment efficiency.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of the framework focused on classification accuracy and detection time, using various conformal prediction metrics and thresholding quantiles.

**A. Detection Accuracy**
Table II summarizes the detection accuracy for success, known failure, and OOD cases across three different quantile thresholds (90%, 95%, 100%).
- **Top Performers:**
 - **Mahalanobis distance** achieved 100% OOD detection accuracy across all thresholds. However, the paper notes it was prone to overfitting to the calibration set in high-dimensional settings, potentially misclassifying visually distinct but valid successes as OOD. Regularization attempts reduced instability but also collapsed distinctions between nominal and OOD data.
 - **Latent prediction error** demonstrated robust performance, achieving over 90% accuracy across all three classes (success, known failure, OOD) at the 90% quantile. It measures discrepancies between predicted and actual latent states without relying on covariance inversion, unlike Mahalanobis distance. This metric was found to be more reliable.
- **Underperformers:**
 - **L2 distance** and **latent standard deviation** showed lower accuracy. L2 distance ignores covariance, while latent standard deviation captures variability but not dynamic progression.
 - **Reconstruction error** and **latent cosine difference** performed the worst. The Cosmos tokenizer, being trained for reconstruction, left minimal anomaly signal for reconstruction error. Latent cosine difference struggled in the high-dimensional latent space where a consistent dominant direction was not present.
 - **Training loss**, while good at identifying nominal data, showed weaker separation for anomalies as it is not designed to amplify them.
- **Threshold Impact:** Lower quantiles generally increased OOD accuracy but reduced nominal accuracy, and vice versa for higher quantiles. The 90% quantile was identified as striking the best balance, with latent prediction error achieving approximately 91% correctness across all classes.
- **Model Observation:** The success model consistently detected all OOD cases that the failure model detected. This was attributed to the success model being trained on a more consistent, singular class, leading to better separation compared to the failure model which was exposed to a diverse range of failure types.

**B. Detection Time**
Table III presents the difference between the framework's classification time ($\hat{k}$) and the human-observed failure time, in seconds.
- **Anticipatory Detection:** Most metrics successfully predicted the class earlier than a human observer, indicating sensitivity to precursor visual or latent patterns.
- **Latent Prediction Error:** This metric, also a top performer in accuracy, made classifications on average between 1 and 3 seconds earlier than a human observer, consistently exhibiting low standard error.
- **Mahalanobis Distance:** This metric provided the earliest detections, but its susceptibility to overfitting raises concerns about increased false positives in real-world scenarios.
- **Training Loss:** This was the only metric that detected failures later than human observers, consistent with its design to amplify nominal patterns rather than contrast anomalies.

**C. Hardware Results**
The framework was successfully deployed and validated on a Boston Dynamics Spot robot equipped with a Pan-Tilt-Zoom (PTZ) camera for gauge inspection.
- **Operational Flow:** The PTZ camera captures frame pairs, which are processed by the framework. In a success scenario, the robot remains idle. For a known failure, the system records the gauge for later revisits. For OOD inputs (e.g., blurry reading), the robot initiates a zoom operation and reprocesses the frames.
- **Deployment and Latency:** Due to shared-use restrictions, inference was hosted externally. The full pipeline requires approximately 1.5 GB storage (1.3 GB for Cosmos weights, ~60 MB for class-specific models, <5 MB for CP artifacts), suggesting potential for onboard deployment. A single classification run took approximately three minutes, primarily due to network latency from data transfer to external models. The actual scoring with precomputed thresholds was instantaneous. The latency is deemed acceptable for the current application but would require local hosting for higher-risk tasks needing immediate intervention.

**D. Low Resolution Results (Appendix A)**
Additional experiments with higher image compression (from 1200x700 to 256x144) were conducted. Table IV shows the results. The performance of latent prediction error remained high, with 94.34% OOD accuracy at the 90% threshold, suggesting robustness to increased compression. Mahalanobis distance also maintained 100% OOD accuracy but continued to carry the risk of overfitting. Reconstruction error performance slightly improved with higher compression.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The presented hybrid framework for anomaly detection and failure classification offers several significant contributions and potential impacts in the field of autonomous systems:

**A. Robust Real-time Monitoring:**
The framework’s ability to classify inspection tasks as success, known failure, or out-of-distribution (OOD) with over 90% accuracy, and often earlier than human observation, represents a step toward robust runtime monitoring for autonomous inspection robots. This anticipatory detection allows for timely and informed actions, which can be critical in industrial settings where delays can lead to increased risks or operational inefficiencies.

**B. Enhanced Safety and Reliability:**
By distinguishing between known failure modes (which may have pre-defined corrective actions) and novel OOD conditions (which signal uncertainty or unexpected environmental changes), the framework enhances the safety and reliability of autonomous systems. It enables a robot to react appropriately—whether it's adjusting its camera for a blurry reading, flagging a persistently occluded gauge for human intervention, or pausing its mission due to an unprecedented environmental anomaly. This layered understanding goes beyond simple "pass/fail" detection.

**C. Broader Applicability and Scalability:**
While demonstrated on gauge inspection, the policy-agnostic and distribution-free nature of the framework, relying on a world model backbone and conformal prediction, suggests its potential for broader impact across various autonomous inspection tasks and safety-critical industrial domains. The use of lightweight models and observation-based metrics makes it amenable to deployment on resource-constrained platforms, as partially demonstrated by its real-time operation on a Boston Dynamics Spot robot.

**D. Improved Data-Driven Development:**
The framework can provide valuable feedback for the training and development of autonomous systems. By clearly identifying OOD cases, it highlights gaps in the training data, enabling targeted data collection efforts. This systematic approach can lead to more comprehensive and robust training datasets, ultimately improving the generalization capabilities and resilience of future autonomous models.

**E. Foundations for Future Autonomy:**
The work lays a foundation for future advancements by outlining several opportunities for improvement. These include:
- **Adaptive Conformal Prediction:** Incorporating adaptive, time-varying CP bands using techniques like martingales to better handle distribution shifts and temporal drift in real-world environments.
- **Efficient Dimensionality Reduction:** Exploring advanced compression modules like singular value decomposition to further enhance efficiency and accuracy in processing high-dimensional visual data.
- **Semantic Understanding:** Integrating semantic failure detection and more historical context to enable the recognition of higher-level failures, moving towards a more human-like understanding of situations.
- **Architectural Consolidation:** Developing a single model with a multi-class classification head, rather than maintaining separate success and failure models, to further reduce computational overhead for online deployment.

In summary, this research contributes to the development of more intelligent, safer, and adaptable autonomous robots by providing a robust mechanism for understanding and reacting to perceived failures and anomalies in complex operational environments.
