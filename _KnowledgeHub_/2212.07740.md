# Sim-to-Real Transfer for Quadrupedal Locomotion via Terrain Transformer

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Digital Brain Lab introduced the Terrain Transformer (TERT), a Transformer-based architecture that uses a two-stage training framework for sim-to-real transfer in quadrupedal locomotion. This method yielded a robust and agile control policy, demonstrating a 100% success rate on real-world sand pits and 60% on stairs, outperforming TCN-based baselines that failed on these complex terrains.

## Problem

- Existing sim-to-real methods for quadrupedal locomotion often struggle with diverse and unpredictable real-world terrains.
- Traditional student policies (TCNs, RNNs) in privileged learning frameworks have limited capacity, are sensitive to imprecise latent vector estimation, and perform poorly on complex terrain.
- Policies trained offline face input distribution shift when deployed online, degrading real-world performance.

## Method

- Introduce Terrain ==Transformer== (**TERT**), a GPT-like Transformer, to directly predict teacher actions from historical proprioceptive observations, bypassing intermediate latent vector estimation.
- Implement a two-stage training framework: offline pretraining from teacher demonstrations followed by online correction to mitigate distribution shifts.
- Leverage Isaac Gym for parallel simulation and extensive domain randomization to train a robust teacher policy and collect diverse training data for **TERT**.

## Results

- TERT achieved higher returns, smoother control, and lower energy consumption in simulation across various terrains compared to baselines.
- On a real Unitree A1 robot, TERT successfully traversed 9 challenging terrains, demonstrating **100%** success on sand pits and **60%** on stairs, where a TCN-based baseline (RMA) failed (**0%** success).
- Ablation studies confirmed the necessity of TERT's two-stage training and the architectural benefits of the Transformer, with t-SNE showing more distinct terrain representations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Transformer architecture excels in modeling long-term temporal dependencies in robot control, enabling more robust sequence prediction for diverse terrains.
> - Direct behavioral cloning of a privileged teacher's actions using a Transformer, rather than estimating an intermediate latent vector, improves robustness and reduces sensitivity to estimation errors.
> - Combining offline pretraining for initial policy learning with online correction effectively addresses the sim-to-real distribution shift, crucial for deploying Transformer-based policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged locomotion for robots faces challenges due to varied and unpredictable terrains. Deep reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a strategy to train controllers in simulation for transfer to real-world robots, a process known as sim-to-real transfer. This approach aims to reduce the need for extensive human expertise in controller design.

A common technique in this domain is the "privileged learning framework." In this framework, a "teacher" policy is trained in simulation with access to "privileged information" (e.g., heightmaps, precise physical parameters) that is unavailable or difficult to obtain in real-world scenarios. This privileged information is typically encoded into a low-dimensional latent vector. A "student" policy is then trained to recover this latent vector using only proprioceptive observations (e.g., joint angles, velocities, IMU data) available on the real robot, often utilizing conventional sequence models such as Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) or Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs).

While privileged learning has yielded success, it presents limitations. The teacher policy's reliance on a versatile latent vector makes online estimation difficult. Furthermore, the student policy can be sensitive to inaccuracies in this estimated latent vector, potentially leading to performance degradation if the estimation falls outside its training distribution. This issue prompts the investigation of methods that might circumvent direct latent vector estimation or provide more robust sequence modeling.

Concurrently, the Transformer architecture has demonstrated capabilities in large-scale sequence modeling across various fields, including natural language processing (NLP), computer vision (CV), and decision-making problems within RL (e.g., Decision Transformer, Trajectory Transformer). Transformers offer high modeling capacity and robustness for decoding sequences, along with a self-attention mechanism that allows for effective utilization of historical information.

This work integrates these two areas by proposing the use of a Transformer-based architecture to address the limitations of traditional sequence models in sim-to-real quadrupedal locomotion. It specifically aims to improve robustness and generalization on challenging terrains by directly imitating the teacher policy's actions via sequence prediction, rather than estimating an intermediate latent vector. This is positioned as the first application of Transformer-based sequence modeling for sim-to-real transfer in multi-terrain quadrupedal locomotion, distinguishing it from prior Transformer applications in robotics that might focus on information fusion within a single timestep or online RL training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a high-capacity and robust control policy for quadrupedal locomotion that can effectively transfer from simulation to diverse and challenging real-world terrains. The authors aim to overcome limitations identified in existing sim-to-real deep reinforcement learning approaches, particularly those utilizing the privileged learning framework.

The specific motivations and objectives include:

- **Addressing limitations of traditional student policies:** Existing methods often rely on traditional neural networks (e.g., TCNs, RNNs) to train a student policy that estimates a latent vector encoding privileged information. The authors highlight that these models have limited capacity and scalability, which can restrict their performance in complex environments. Additionally, such student policies are observed to be sensitive to the precision of the estimated latent vector, leading to potential catastrophic performance degradation if the estimate deviates from the training distribution. A key motivation is to reduce or eliminate this dependence on precise latent vector estimation.
- **Leveraging Transformer architecture for sequence prediction:** The authors propose that the problem of predicting optimal actions based on historical proprioceptive sequences is well-suited for the Transformer architecture. This is motivated by the Transformer's demonstrated superiority in a wide range of sequence modeling tasks due to its high capacity for modeling complex relationships and its robustness in decoding. The self-attention mechanism is expected to allow the policy to better utilize long-term historical information for credit assignment and to capture environmental characteristics and changes.
- **Improving generalization to unseen and challenging terrains:** The ability of Transformers to model diverse behaviors is identified as crucial for successful transfer to varied and previously unseen real-world scenarios. The goal is to develop a policy that exhibits agile and stable locomotion across a broader spectrum of difficult terrains beyond what traditional models can achieve.
- **Developing an integrated training framework:** To effectively apply Transformers in a sim-to-real context, the authors aim to introduce a novel two-stage training framework. This framework is designed to integrate the benefits of Transformer-based sequence modeling with the principles of privileged learning, thereby enabling the Transformer to learn an effective policy from teacher demonstrations while mitigating issues like input distribution shift during deployment.

In essence, the research seeks to introduce a "Terrain Transformer" (TERT) that learns to directly imitate a privileged teacher's actions from proprioceptive history, leveraging the Transformer's strengths to achieve more robust, smoother, and energy-efficient quadrupedal locomotion in real-world, challenging environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, Terrain Transformer (TERT), combines a Transformer architecture with a two-stage training framework designed for sim-to-real transfer in quadrupedal locomotion. The approach builds upon the privileged learning paradigm but modifies the student policy training.

**A. Reinforcement Learning for Quadrupedal Locomotion**
The problem is formulated as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP). The robot, at each timestep, receives a partial observation ($o_t$) and executes an action ($a_t$). The objective of reinforcement learning is to find an optimal policy ($\pi^*$) that maximizes the expected sum of discounted rewards.

**B. The Transformer Model**
TERT utilizes a GPT-like Transformer architecture. It comprises 'n' stacked self-attention layers with causal masking. For a given trajectory of observation-action pairs ($\tau = (o_1, a_1, \dots, o_T, a_T)$), the Transformer first embeds the inputs and their positions. Self-attention layers process these embeddings, producing outputs where each output ($z_i$) is calculated based on an attention mechanism over preceding tokens ($j \le i$). The output of the final self-attention layer is then fed into a linear layer to predict actions ($\hat{a}_t$) conditioned on the historical observation-action sequence ($o_1, a_1, \dots, o_t$). The model is trained by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) between predicted actions and target actions. For online deployment, the Transformer takes the most recent $T$ observation-action pairs as input and executes the predicted action for the current timestep. Unlike the original Decision Transformer, returns-to-go are omitted as preliminary experiments suggested a performance degradation with their inclusion.

**C. Teacher Policy Training**
Similar to standard privileged learning, an oracle teacher policy is first trained in simulation. This teacher policy has access to:
1. **Elevation map:** Terrain height information around the robot.
2. **Contact force:** Information about ground contact.
3. **Ground-truth physical parameters:** Domain randomization parameters like friction and mass.
This "privileged information" ($e_t$) is encoded into a latent vector ($l_t$) by a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) encoder ($\mu$). The teacher policy ($\bar{\pi}$) then determines actions ($\bar{a}_t$) based on this latent vector and the robot's proprioception ($o_t$), i.e., $\bar{a}_t = \bar{\pi}(o_t, l_t)$. The teacher policy and encoder are jointly trained using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm.

**D. Terrain Transformer Training (Two-Stage Framework)**
TERT's training consists of two distinct stages:

1. **Offline Pretraining:**
 - The pre-trained teacher policy is executed in simulation across various terrains to collect a dataset of observation-action trajectories. These trajectories contain pairs of proprioceptive observations ($o_t$) and the corresponding teacher-generated actions ($\bar{a}_t$).
 - A GPT Transformer (TERT) is then trained offline on this collected dataset. Its objective is to predict the teacher's actions based on the historical observation-action sequences. The loss function is the mean squared error between TERT's predicted actions and the teacher's actions. This stage resembles supervised learning or behavioral cloning.

2. **Online Correction:**
 - The TERT model, after offline pretraining, is then used to interact directly with the simulator.
 - During this interaction, TERT generates its own on-policy observation-action sequences ($o_t, \hat{a}_t$).
 - Simultaneously, the teacher policy is queried to provide target actions ($\bar{a}_t$) for the observations ($o_t$) encountered by TERT.
 - TERT is subsequently trained to predict the teacher's actions conditioned on *its own* generated observation-action sequences. This stage aims to mitigate the input distribution shift problem that typically arises when a policy trained offline is deployed online (similar to the DAgger algorithm).

The authors emphasize that both stages are critical. Offline pretraining provides a robust initial policy, preventing the collection of low-quality on-policy data. Online correction addresses the distribution shift, ensuring the Transformer performs well when interacting with the environment.

**E. Experimental Setup**
- **Robot:** Unitree A1 quadruped robot.
- **Simulator:** Isaac Gym, enabling parallel simulation of thousands of robots across diverse terrains (smooth slopes, rough slopes, stair up/down, discrete obstacles).
- **Observations ($o_t$):** A 48-dimensional vector including base linear/angular velocities, gravity projection, command velocities, joint linear/angular velocities, and the previous action ($a_{t-1}$).
- **Actions ($a_t$):** A 12-dimensional vector specifying target positions for each joint, converted to torques using a PD controller.
- **Reward Function:** Standard rewards encouraging commanded velocities and penalizing undesirable states (e.g., non-commanded velocities, large torques, accelerations, collisions). Two additional terms were included to penalize large action magnitude and encourage torque smoothness, benefiting sim-to-real transfer.
- **Model Architecture:** Teacher policy's privileged encoder is a 3-layer MLP (256 units); the teacher policy itself is a 3-layer MLP (512, 256, 128 units). TERT uses 3 self-attention layers with an embedding size of 256 and a trajectory length ($T$) of 20 timesteps.
- **Training Details:** Teacher policy trained for 20,000 iterations (approx. 9.8 × 10^7 timesteps). Each TERT training stage involved collecting 20 million timesteps and 200,000 updates with a batch size of 64. Domain randomization was applied to physical parameters (friction, added mass, PD gains) during training to enhance robustness.
- **Baselines for Comparison:** Teacher (oracle), RMA (TCN-based student policy for latent estimation), PPO (direct RL), StackedPPO (PPO with stacked historical observations/actions), and GRU (PPO with GRU).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments aimed to evaluate TERT's performance against state-of-the-art methods, understand its underlying mechanisms, and validate its sim-to-real transfer capability.

**A. Simulation Comparison**
TERT was benchmarked against several baselines in simulation across various terrains, evaluating return, control smoothness, and energy consumption.
- **Return:** TERT achieved higher average returns than all baselines on most terrains, with a notable advantage in challenging scenarios such as stair up and stair down. The performance gap between TERT and the Teacher (oracle) was smaller compared to the gap between RMA (a strong TCN-based baseline) and the Teacher, suggesting TERT's effectiveness in sequence modeling. Baselines like StackedPPO and GRU, while incorporating historical information, did not achieve comparable performance to TERT or RMA, indicating the value of privileged information guidance or the Transformer architecture's capacity.
- **Control Smoothness:** TERT demonstrated superior control smoothness, indicated by a lower average difference between consecutive actions, across all tested terrains.
- **Energy Consumption:** TERT exhibited lower energy consumption compared to baselines (excluding the Teacher), even though energy consumption was not explicitly penalized in the reward function. This suggests a more efficient control strategy.

**B. Empirical Study**
A series of empirical studies were conducted to dissect TERT's components and characteristics.

- **Ablation Study:**
 - **TERT-w/o-OC (without Online Correction):** Performance decreased, validating the necessity of the online correction stage to address input distribution shift.
 - **TERT-w/o-OP (without Offline Pretraining):** Performance also decreased, confirming the importance of the offline pretraining stage for providing a functional initial policy and high-quality data.
 - **TERT-TCN (Transformer replaced with TCN):** This variant showed severe performance degradation, highlighting the architectural superiority of the Transformer over TCN for this task.
 - **TERT-Latent (Transformer estimates latent vector):** This variant, where the Transformer estimated the latent vector similar to RMA, retained most of TERT's advantages, further supporting the benefits of the Transformer architecture itself.
- **Hidden Layer Visualization (t-SNE):** T-SNE visualization of the last hidden layer activations revealed that TERT's representations for different terrains formed more distinct clusters with clearer boundaries compared to RMA, whose representations largely overlapped. This indicates TERT's enhanced ability to capture and differentiate environmental characteristics, contributing to its generalization capabilities.
- **Attention Weight Analysis:** Analysis of attention weights during a stair-down trajectory demonstrated that TERT effectively utilized long historical information, with attention weights assigned to earlier timesteps (e.g., t-19) being comparable to those for more recent ones. The attention weights also showed a cyclical pattern, correlating with the robot's periodic gait.
- **Sequence Length:** Experiments with varying sequence lengths ($T$) showed that increasing $T$ generally improved performance up to a certain point (T=20 was chosen as a balance between performance and computational efficiency), confirming the benefit of longer historical context for Transformer-based control.

**C. Real-world Application**
TERT and RMA policies were deployed on a Unitree A1 robot without any fine-tuning.
- **Terrain Coverage:** TERT successfully traversed nine different challenging terrains, including grass, soil, pebble, smooth floor, damp wood, slope up, slope down, sand pit, and stair down. RMA also performed well on the first seven terrains but failed consistently on sand pit and stair down.
- **Success Rates:** TERT achieved a 100% success rate on sand pit and other simpler terrains, and a 60% success rate on stair down. In contrast, RMA recorded 0% success on both sand pit and stair down.
- **Control Behavior:** TERT exhibited more stable control behavior in real-world scenarios compared to RMA. The failures of RMA on sand pit and stair down were attributed to its sensitivity to estimated latent vectors in more complex and dynamic environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces Terrain Transformer (TERT), a novel application of the Transformer architecture to the problem of sim-to-real transfer for quadrupedal locomotion across multiple challenging terrains. This work is presented as the first to specifically address this challenge using Transformer-based sequence modeling, which could influence future research paradigms in robotic control.

The significance of TERT lies in several key aspects:

- **Enhanced Robustness and Generalization:** TERT demonstrates a capacity for more robust and stable locomotion compared to existing state-of-the-art baselines, particularly on complex and dynamic terrains such as sand pits and stairs. This is attributed to the Transformer's high modeling capacity and its self-attention mechanism, which allows for better utilization of historical information and a more effective capture of environmental characteristics. The empirical studies confirm that TERT learns distinct representations for different terrains, contributing to its improved generalization.
- **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The proposed two-stage training framework (offline pretraining followed by online correction) provides a principled method to integrate Transformer models with privileged learning, addressing critical challenges like input distribution shift. This framework facilitates direct transfer of policies trained in simulation to real-world robots without additional fine-tuning, a major objective in robotics research.
- **Potential New Paradigm for Robot Control:** By demonstrating the efficacy of Transformers in learning agile and adaptable quadrupedal locomotion from observation-action sequences, the work suggests that sequence modeling, a technique primarily advanced in NLP, could become a foundational approach for robot control tasks involving complex temporal dependencies and environmental adaptability.
- **Efficiency and Smoothness:** Beyond high returns, TERT also yields controllers that exhibit smoother movements and lower energy consumption in simulation, which are desirable properties for real-world robotic systems, contributing to mechanical longevity and operational efficiency.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The successful integration of Transformers opens avenues for future work, such as incorporating more sophisticated sensory inputs like LiDAR or camera data into the Transformer framework, or extending the approach to multi-agent control tasks. This could lead to more perceptive and cooperative robotic behaviors in highly dynamic and complex environments.

In summary, TERT represents an advancement in sim-to-real transfer for legged robots by demonstrating the effectiveness of the Transformer architecture and its tailored training framework. Its success on challenging real-world terrains suggests a promising direction for developing adaptable and high-performance robot controllers.
