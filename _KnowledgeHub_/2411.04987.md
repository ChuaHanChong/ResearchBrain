# Few-Shot Task Learning through Inverse Generative Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at MIT, Harvard, and Johns Hopkins developed Few-Shot Task Learning through Inverse Generative Modeling (FTL-IGM), a framework that leverages a pretrained conditional diffusion model to infer latent task concepts from a few demonstrations of novel tasks. This approach enables agents to generalize learned behaviors to new initial states and synthesize new behaviors by composing learned concepts.

## Problem

- Intelligent agents struggle to learn novel task concepts from limited demonstrations and generalize this knowledge to new situations, unlike human learners.
- Traditional imitation learning methods like Behavior Cloning suffer from covariate shift, hindering generalization to new initial states or environments.
- Reinforcement learning and Inverse Reinforcement Learning are typically data-intensive and computationally expensive for acquiring new skills.

## Method

- The **Few-Shot** ==Task Learning== through Inverse Generative Modeling (FTL-IGM) framework involves two phases: pretraining and inverse modeling.
- A conditional diffusion model is pretrained on a large dataset of paired trajectories and explicit task concepts.
- To learn a new task from few demonstrations, the pretrained diffusion model is frozen, and a latent concept representation (or multiple components) is optimized via backpropagation to best explain the observed demonstrations.

## Results

- FTL-IGM consistently outperformed baselines such as Behavior Cloning and VAEs in few-shot concept learning across five diverse domains, including object rearrangement and autonomous driving.
- The method demonstrated strong generalization capabilities, successfully applying learned concepts to novel initial states and composing them with other known concepts.
- In real-world table-top manipulation, FTL-IGM learned a novel "elevated pushing" concept with **55%** accuracy, and achieved competitive closed-loop performance in goal-oriented navigation and autonomous driving.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Formulating few-shot task learning as an inverse generative modeling problem allows leveraging strong priors learned from large datasets to infer task concepts efficiently from sparse demonstration data.
> - By learning an explicit latent representation of task concepts, the system can generalize behaviors to novel initial states and environments, mitigating issues like covariate shift.
> - The inherent compositional properties of diffusion models enable the system to learn novel tasks and then synthesize behaviors by combining them with existing concepts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research sits at the intersection of few-shot learning, imitation learning, and generative modeling, specifically addressing the challenge of enabling intelligent agents to acquire novel skills and task concepts from minimal data.

Traditionally, few-shot learning in robotics and machine learning has explored various avenues:
- **Behavior Cloning (BC):** Directly learns a policy from state-action pairs. However, BC often suffers from "covariate shift," where deviations from the demonstration distribution lead to compounding errors, and struggles to generalize to new scenarios or compose behaviors without explicit retraining.
- **Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL):** Infers a reward function from demonstrations, which can then be used to learn a policy. While more data-efficient than BC in some contexts, IRL is computationally intensive due to the inner reinforcement learning loop and typically requires interaction with the environment for policy learning.
- **Inverse Planning:** Aims to infer latent intentions (goals, beliefs) by observing an agent's actions, assuming access to a planner, environmental dynamics, and a predefined task/goal space. This approach is powerful but often relies on strong assumptions about the planning capabilities and knowledge available to the system.
- **In-Context Learning:** More recent approaches, particularly with large language models and transformers, represent tasks directly with demonstrations, allowing for few-shot generalization without further training. However, these methods can still struggle with explicit concept representations and complex compositional generalization.

**FTL-IGM carves out a unique niche** by drawing inspiration from the success of generative models, particularly diffusion models, in few-shot *visual* concept learning. Instead of directly learning policies or reward functions, FTL-IGM focuses on learning *task concepts* – a latent representation of the goal or motion style – from demonstrations. The core distinguishing factor is its "inverse generative modeling" approach:
1. It *pretrains* a conditional generative model (specifically, a diffusion model) on a large dataset of *paired* trajectories and their corresponding explicit "concepts" (e.g., natural language descriptions of tasks). This allows the model to learn strong priors about how concepts map to behaviors.
2. Crucially, when presented with a *few demonstrations of a novel task*, it *freezes* the pretrained generative model and instead *optimizes the latent concept representation* (the input to the generative model) via backpropagation, rather than finetuning the model weights. This inversion allows the system to infer the underlying concept that best explains the observed demonstrations.

This approach addresses several limitations of prior work:
- **Generalization to Novel Scenarios:** By learning a rich concept space via pretraining and then inferring concepts, FTL-IGM can generalize to unseen environments and new initial states, mitigating covariate shift.
- **Compositionality:** Generative models, especially diffusion models, have demonstrated strong compositional properties. FTL-IGM leverages this to learn concepts that are compositions of training concepts or even entirely novel concepts, and then generate behaviors that combine these learned concepts with other known concepts. This goes beyond simply interpolating within known task families.
- **Data Efficiency:** Once the generative model is pretrained, learning a new concept requires only a few demonstrations and no further interaction with the environment for policy learning, making it highly data-efficient in the few-shot phase.
- **Explicit Concept Learning:** Unlike many imitation learning methods that learn implicit policies, FTL-IGM explicitly learns a latent "concept" representation, offering potential for interpretability and easier combination with other concepts.

Thus, FTL-IGM represents a significant advancement by bridging the gap between powerful generative modeling techniques and the demanding requirements of few-shot task learning in sequential domains, offering a robust and generalizable solution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable intelligent agents to **learn novel task concepts from only a few demonstrations and generalize this learned knowledge to new situations.** The authors refer to this as "task concept learning."

The primary motivations for pursuing this objective are rooted in both biological intelligence and the limitations of current machine learning approaches:

- **Human-like Learning:** Humans, even from a young age, possess a remarkable ability to grasp new tasks from minimal exposure and apply that knowledge broadly. The authors are inspired by this human capacity and aim to replicate a similar level of few-shot generalization in artificial agents.
- **Addressing Data Scarcity and Generalization Gaps in Robotics/AI:**
 - Traditional imitation learning methods (like Behavior Cloning) often struggle with **covariate shift**, where small deviations from training data lead to compounding errors, hindering generalization to new initial states or environments.
 - Model-free reinforcement learning and Inverse Reinforcement Learning are typically **data-intensive** and **computationally costly**, requiring extensive interaction with the environment for each new task, which is impractical for real-world robotic deployment.
 - Many existing few-shot learning techniques for tasks often assume that new tasks are similar to or direct interpolations of previously seen tasks, limiting their ability to handle truly **novel or compositional behaviors**.
- **Leveraging Generative Model Success:** The success of generative models, particularly diffusion models, in visual concept learning (e.g., generating images from text descriptions or personalized images from a few examples) provides a strong precedent. The authors hypothesize that similar benefits can be achieved for sequential task data if a strong conditional generative model can be learned and then "inverted."
- **Acquiring Strong Priors:** By pretraining a generative model on a large dataset of paired behaviors and explicitly defined task representations, the system can acquire strong prior knowledge about the structure and nature of behaviors within a domain. This prior knowledge is crucial for effectively learning new concepts from sparse data.

In essence, the authors aim to develop a framework that allows agents to learn a *representation* of a novel task from limited demonstrations and then leverage a powerful, pretrained generative model to produce diverse and generalizable behaviors corresponding to that task, even in unseen scenarios or when combined with other learned concepts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **Few-Shot Task Learning through Inverse Generative Modeling (FTL-IGM)**, operates in two main phases: pretraining a conditional generative model and then leveraging it for few-shot concept learning via inversion.

#### 3.1. Phase 1: Pretraining a Conditional Generative Model

- **Dataset:** The method assumes access to a large pretraining dataset, $D_{pretrain} = \{(\tau_i, c_i)\}_{i=1}^N$. Each entry consists of a state-based sequence (trajectory) $\tau_i = \{s_0, s_1, \dots\}$ and a corresponding "concept" $c_i \in \mathbb{R}^n$ which is a latent representation describing the task or behavior (e.g., natural language embeddings). The initial state $s_0$ of the trajectory is also provided.
- **Generative Model Choice:** The authors choose a **diffusion model** as their conditional generative model, denoted as $G_\theta$. Diffusion models are known for their high-quality sample generation and robust interpolation capabilities.
- **Model Function:** $G_\theta: C \times S \to T$, meaning it takes a concept $c$ and an initial state $s_0$ as input and generates a trajectory $\tau$.
- **Training Objective:** The parameters $\theta$ of the diffusion model are trained to maximize the likelihood of generating the trajectories given their concepts and initial states: $\arg \max_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{\tau,c \sim D_{pretrain}}[\log G_\theta(\tau | c, s_0)]$.
- **Implementation Details (Diffusion Model):**
 - The core of the diffusion model is a denoising network $\epsilon_\theta$.
 - It's trained to predict the noise added to a noisy data sample $x_t$ (which is a noisy version of a trajectory $\tau$) given the diffusion timestep $t$, the conditioning concept $c$, and the initial state $s_0$.
 - **Classifier-Free Guidance:** To enhance generation quality and controllability, the model uses classifier-free guidance. This involves training a single model that can operate both conditionally (on $c$) and unconditionally (by replacing $c$ with a dummy condition $c_\emptyset$ via dropout during training). During inference, the unconditional prediction is steered towards the conditional one by a guidance scale $\omega$. This allows for stronger influence of the concept during generation.

#### 3.2. Phase 2: Few-Shot Concept Learning (Inverse Generative Modeling)

- **Input:** Given a small set of *unlabeled* demonstrations $D_{new} = \{\tilde{\tau}_j\}$ of a novel concept.
- **Inversion Formulation:** Instead of finetuning the model weights $\theta$, the authors formulate learning the new concept as an *inverse generative modeling problem*. The goal is to find a latent concept representation $\tilde{c}$ that maximizes the likelihood of the model $G_\theta$ (with *frozen* weights $\theta$) generating these new demonstrations.
- **Learning Multiple Concepts:** The approach is extended to learn *multiple* latent concept components $\{\tilde{c}_1, \dots, \tilde{c}_K\}$ and their corresponding weights $\{\omega_1, \dots, \omega_K\}$ for $K \ge 1$. This allows for representing new concepts as compositions of more basic, potentially interpretable, latent factors.
- **Optimization:** Starting from randomly initialized concept embeddings $\tilde{c}_k$, these embeddings and their weights $\omega_k$ are optimized using backpropagation, while the diffusion model $\epsilon_\theta$ itself remains frozen. The objective for this optimization is:
 $\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,I)} \left[ \left\| \epsilon - \left( \epsilon_\theta(x_t(\tilde{\tau}), c_\emptyset, s_0, t) + \sum_{k=1}^K \omega_k (\epsilon_\theta(x_t(\tilde{\tau}), \tilde{c}_k, s_0, t) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t(\tilde{\tau}), c_\emptyset, s_0, t)) \right) \right\|^2 \right]$
 This objective essentially asks: "What concept(s) and weights, when input into the pretrained generative model, best explain the observed noisy versions of the new demonstrations?"
- **Key Advantage:** This design leverages the rich priors about behavior learned during pretraining to infer concepts efficiently from limited data without the need for extensive retraining or environment interaction in the few-shot phase.

#### 3.3. Generating the Learned Concept

Once the concept $\tilde{c}$ (or its components $\{\tilde{c}_k\}$ and weights $\{\omega_k\}$) has been learned, FTL-IGM can generate diverse trajectories in various settings:

- **Learned Concept with Demonstrated Initial States:** Generate trajectories exhibiting the learned concept from the same initial states observed in the demonstrations.
- **Learned Concept with Novel Initial States:** A crucial test of generalization, this involves generating trajectories from the learned concept but starting from initial states *not* seen in the demonstrations.
- **Learned Concept Composed with Other Concepts:** By adding additional terms (training concepts $c_j$ with their own weights $\omega_j$) to the sum in the guidance equation (similar to how multiple learned concepts are combined), the model can synthesize novel behaviors that are compositions of the newly learned concept and existing training concepts.
- **Closed-Loop Execution:** For domains where actions are required (e.g., robotics, driving), the generated state sequences can be fed into an inverse dynamics model or a separate planner. The planner translates the predicted state trajectory into a sequence of actions, allowing for real-world execution and dynamic replanning based on new observations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted an extensive evaluation of FTL-IGM across five diverse domains, showcasing its efficacy in learning novel task concepts and generalizing them.

#### 4.1. Evaluated Domains and Task Concepts

- **Object Rearrangement:** Concepts are spatial relations between objects (e.g., "triangle right of square"). New concepts include compositions of training relations ("triangle right of square AND circle above square") and entirely novel relations ("square diagonal to triangle", "objects on a circle").
- **Goal-Oriented Navigation (AGENT):** Concepts are attributes of target objects (e.g., "go to red object", "go to bowl"). New concepts involve novel compositions of attributes ("go to red bowl") with challenging distractor settings.
- **Motion Capture (MoCap):** Concepts are human actions. Training on various common actions, new concepts include "jumping jacks," "chop wood," and "breaststroke."
- **Autonomous Driving (HighwayEnv):** Concepts are driving scenarios (e.g., "highway," "exit," "merge," "intersection"). A novel concept is "roundabout."
- **Real-World Table-Top Manipulation:** Concepts are manipulation tasks (e.g., "pick green circle and place on book," "push green circle to orange triangle"). A novel concept is "push green circle to orange triangle on a book" (elevated pushing).

#### 4.2. Baselines

FTL-IGM was compared against several strong baselines:
- **Goal-conditioned Behavior Cloning (BC):** A deterministic policy learned from state-concept pairs.
- **Variational Autoencoder (VAE):** Learns a latent space for trajectories without explicit concept labels during pretraining, then optimizes this latent code to fit new demonstrations.
- **In-Context Learning:** A transformer-based approach that generates actions by conditioning on a sequence of demonstrations.
- **Conditioning on Language Descriptions of New Concepts:** Directly uses T5 embeddings of natural language descriptions for new concepts, rather than learning them from demonstrations.

#### 4.3. Key Findings and Results

- **Superior Performance in Few-Shot Concept Learning:**
 - **Object Rearrangement & AGENT:** FTL-IGM consistently outperforms BC, VAE, and In-Context Learning in learning both novel compositions of training concepts and entirely new concepts, as measured by task accuracy (e.g., 0.75-0.82 accuracy for FTL-IGM vs. 0.28-0.67 for baselines in object rearrangement new concept settings).
 - **Generalization to New Initial States:** A significant strength of FTL-IGM is its ability to generalize learned concepts to novel initial states, a common failure point for BC and In-Context methods due to covariate shift. This is clearly demonstrated in AGENT (0.73 accuracy for FTL-IGM vs. 0.40-0.63 for baselines) and Autonomous Driving.
- **Compositional Generalization:** FTL-IGM successfully generates behaviors that are compositions of learned new concepts and existing training concepts (e.g., "square diagonal to triangle" + "circle right of square"). This highlights the inherent compositional properties of the underlying generative model.
- **Real-World Application:** In the table-top manipulation task, FTL-IGM successfully learns the novel "elevated pushing" concept (55% accuracy) while a baseline using a training concept representation fails (15%), demonstrating its practical utility.
- **Closed-Loop Performance:** In Goal-Oriented Navigation and Autonomous Driving, where closed-loop execution is critical, FTL-IGM achieves competitive and often superior success and crash rates compared to baselines, indicating its robustness for dynamic environments.
- **Ablation on Number of Concepts:** The study found that learning *two* concept components (and their weights) generally yielded higher accuracy than learning one, suggesting that novel concepts can often be better represented as a composition of simpler latent factors learned by the model.
- **Relationship to Training Concepts:** Analysis shows that learned concept components can either align with individual parts of a composite training concept (e.g., one component for "red," another for "bowl") or a single learned component might capture the entire composite task. t-SNE visualizations suggest learned novel concepts remain relatively close to the distribution of training concepts in the embedding space.
- **Limitations in Results:** The authors acknowledge some limitations:
 - The "circle" concept in Object Rearrangement had lower accuracy, likely due to being far outside the training distribution.
 - Some specific composite tasks showed lower accuracy, possibly due to unregularized concept-weight optimization.
 - MoCap generation quality, while capturing the widest range of motion, could be noisy compared to smoother but less diverse baselines.
 - Diffusion models incur high inference time.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

FTL-IGM represents a significant step forward in the domain of few-shot task learning, offering a flexible and powerful framework with broad implications for intelligent agents and robotics.

#### 5.1. Core Significance

- **Novel Formulation:** The key innovation lies in formulating few-shot *task* learning as an *inverse generative modeling problem*. By pretraining a strong conditional generative model and then inverting it to infer latent concepts from new demonstrations, the method leverages the model's learned priors exceptionally well. This avoids the pitfalls of finetuning on sparse data or requiring extensive environment interaction during the few-shot phase.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** FTL-IGM demonstrates remarkable generalization capabilities:
 - **To New Environments/Initial States:** Unlike many imitation learning techniques that struggle with covariate shift, FTL-IGM can generate relevant behaviors in situations not directly seen in the limited demonstrations.
 - **To Novel and Compositional Concepts:** It can learn tasks that are either entirely new or complex compositions of simpler, known concepts. Crucially, it can then combine these newly learned concepts with existing ones to synthesize even more complex behaviors, showcasing a strong capacity for compositional reasoning and generation.
- **Data and Computation Efficiency:** Once the expensive pretraining phase is complete (which can be done offline), learning a new task concept is highly data-efficient (requiring only a few demonstrations) and computationally efficient (optimizing only concept embeddings, not model weights). This makes it more practical for real-world robotic deployment where collecting vast amounts of data for every new task is infeasible.
- **Explicit Concept Learning:** The method learns explicit latent representations of tasks. This not only facilitates the generalization and compositionality discussed above but also opens avenues for interpretability (understanding what the agent has learned) and potentially for human-in-the-loop concept refinement.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **Robotics:**
 - **Faster Skill Acquisition:** Robots could rapidly learn new manipulation, navigation, or interaction skills from human demonstrations, even if limited. This drastically reduces the programming effort required for new tasks.
 - **Human-Robot Collaboration:** Facilitates more natural human-robot interaction, as robots can infer human intent from a few examples and adapt their behavior accordingly.
 - **Autonomous Systems:** Enables autonomous vehicles or drones to quickly adapt to novel driving scenarios or environmental conditions demonstrated by human operators.
- **AI for Dynamic Systems:** The framework is generalizable beyond robotics to any domain involving sequential decision-making where behaviors can be represented as trajectories (e.g., character animation, game AI, human behavior modeling).
- **Foundation for Future AI:** By demonstrating how robust task concepts can be learned and generalized, FTL-IGM contributes to the broader goal of building more adaptable and intelligent AI systems. As generative AI models continue to advance in scale and capability, integrating them with frameworks like FTL-IGM could unlock even more powerful few-shot learning abilities, pushing towards more human-like general intelligence.

While the current implementation faces challenges such as inference time and some qualitative issues in complex motion generation, the underlying formulation and demonstrated capabilities mark a significant step towards practical and generalizable few-shot task learning. The paper's emphasis on strong priors, inverse modeling, and inherent compositionality provides a compelling direction for future research in AI.
