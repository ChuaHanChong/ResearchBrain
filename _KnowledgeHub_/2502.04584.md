# Joint State and Noise Covariance Estimation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The University of Queensland and The Australian National University developed a framework for jointly estimating system states and unknown noise covariance matrices from noisy measurements in robotics and computer vision. Their Block-Coordinate Descent algorithm achieves state and covariance estimation accuracy comparable to, or exceeding, methods that use true known covariances, while significantly outperforming ad hoc settings with minimal computational overhead.

## Problem

- Accurate noise covariance matrices are essential for robust state estimation and reliable uncertainty quantification in robotics and computer vision, but are rarely known a priori.
- Current methods for obtaining noise covariances face challenges like difficult offline calibration, non-linear effects from data preprocessing, and noise characteristics that change dynamically.
- Many practical applications use ad hoc or manually tuned noise covariances, leading to suboptimal state estimation accuracy and unreliable confidence intervals.

## Method

- The problem is formulated as a joint ==Maximum A Posteriori (MAP)== or ==Maximum Likelihood (ML)== estimation of states and the noise precision matrix.
- ==Analytical, closed-form solutions== are derived for the optimal noise precision matrix given fixed states, accommodating structural constraints (e.g., diagonal or eigenvalue-bounded).
- A **Hybrid Block-Coordinate Descent (BCD)** algorithm is developed, which iteratively updates states using standard sparse solvers and then updates the noise precision matrix analytically.

## Results

- Proposed Block-Coordinate Descent algorithms achieved state estimation accuracy comparable to or better than using perfectly known covariances, significantly outperforming baselines with fixed identity covariances.
- The algorithms accurately estimated noise covariances, demonstrating low 2-Wasserstein errors relative to true distributions, with MAP variants showing improved performance due to Wishart prior regularization.
- The BCD method exhibited fast convergence and minimal computational overhead (e.g., **0.9-1.5 ms** per covariance update), making it practical for integration into existing large-scale systems.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The subproblem of estimating the optimal noise precision matrix for a fixed state is convex, allowing for efficient analytical, closed-form solutions even with structural constraints.
> - Unconstrained Maximum Likelihood estimation of noise covariance is ill-posed if the residual sample covariance is singular, necessitating regularization via priors or constraints.
> - A Block-Coordinate Descent approach effectively integrates computationally inexpensive analytical covariance updates with optimized state estimation solvers, preserving problem sparsity.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The estimation of states (parameters such as poses and points) from noisy measurements is a fundamental problem in robotics and computer vision, with applications in simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) and bundle adjustment (BA). These problems are frequently framed as maximum likelihood (ML) or maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation, which, under the assumption of zero-mean Gaussian noise (and Gaussian priors for MAP), reduce to weighted least squares problems. A critical component of these least squares formulations is the noise information matrix (inverse of the covariance matrix), which dictates the weight assigned to each measurement. An accurate noise covariance is essential for high estimation accuracy, correctly accounting for measurement correlations, and quantifying the uncertainty of state estimates, which in turn impacts downstream control and decision-making processes.

Current state-of-the-art estimation frameworks for SLAM and computer vision, including widely used sparse nonlinear least squares solvers, generally assume that the noise covariance matrix is known a priori. However, the paper identifies several practical challenges associated with obtaining accurate noise covariance estimates:
1. **Calibration difficulty**: Offline calibration is often labor-intensive, requires ground truth data for primary parameters (which can be hard to acquire), and may not always be feasible.
2. **Preprocessing effects**: Raw sensor measurements are frequently preprocessed by intermediate algorithms (e.g., in a SLAM front-end), which can nonlinearly transform the noise characteristics, making accurate modeling difficult.
3. **Dynamic noise characteristics**: Noise models can evolve over time due to environmental factors (e.g., temperature changes), rendering pre-calibrated models obsolete during deployment.

Due to these challenges, many applications resort to ad hoc noise covariances, such as arbitrary isotropic or diagonal matrices, often set through manual tuning or trial-and-error. The paper notes that the problem of noise covariance estimation, while recognized as a significant challenge in SLAM, has remained largely unsolved and understudied within the robotics and computer vision literature.

This work aims to address this gap by developing principled algorithms for the online (during deployment) joint ML/MAP estimation of states and noise covariance matrices. It distinguishes itself from existing approaches in several ways:
- **Generalization of IGLS**: The paper extends methods like Iterated Generalized Least Squares (IGLS), which has seen limited application in robotics (e.g., Zhan et al. for PnP). This work generalizes the framework to heteroscedastic measurements, nonlinear noise models, manifold-valued parameters, and large-scale graph-structured problems like SLAM, while also addressing issues of singularity in sample covariance matrices.
- **Alternative to variational inference**: Unlike EM-type or variational inference methods (e.g., Barfoot et al., Wong et al.) that seek analytical approximations of entire posteriors, this paper focuses on MAP/ML point estimation, leading to computationally faster solutions that can be more readily integrated into existing nonlinear least squares solvers.
- **Extension of variable elimination**: It builds upon ideas of analytical elimination of covariance from the objective (e.g., Barfoot), extending it to scenarios where subsets of measurements share a covariance matrix and proposing more computationally efficient block-coordinate descent algorithms.
- **Beyond diagonal assumptions**: Unlike some prior work (e.g., Lu et al.), this approach does not restrict the covariance matrix to be diagonal.
- **Online estimation without ground truth**: In contrast to bilevel optimization frameworks that learn covariances from calibration datasets with known ground truth (e.g., Qadri et al.), this method jointly estimates states and covariances directly from observed data, suitable for online deployment.
- **Distinction from adaptive Kalman filtering**: While sharing some underlying principles with adaptive Kalman filtering techniques, this work targets batch and smoothing formulations, nonlinear measurement models, sparse large-scale problems, and manifold-valued states, which are distinct from the linear/linearized recursive estimation settings common in Kalman filtering literature.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient framework for jointly estimating the unknown noise covariance matrix alongside the primary parameters (states) within common estimation problems in robotics and computer vision. This joint estimation is performed directly from noisy measurements, potentially augmented with prior information about the noise characteristics.

The motivation for this work stems from the recognized limitations and practical challenges associated with current practices in noise modeling:
- **Improved Estimation Accuracy**: Accurate noise covariance matrices determine the weights assigned to individual measurements in least squares problems. Using correct weights allows the estimator to account for sensor precision and correlations between measurement components, thereby improving the accuracy of the final state estimate.
- **Reliable Uncertainty Quantification**: The estimated noise covariance matrix directly influences the estimated covariance of the primary parameters. An inaccurate noise model can lead to overconfidence or underconfidence in the state estimates, which can result in suboptimal or potentially catastrophic decisions in robotic systems where state uncertainty is used for control, path planning, or decision-making.
- **Overcoming Calibration Hurdles**: Traditional methods rely on extensive offline calibration to estimate noise parameters, which is often labor-intensive, time-consuming, and difficult to perform accurately in real-world scenarios, particularly when ground truth for the primary parameters is unavailable or hard to obtain.
- **Adaptability to Dynamic Environments**: Sensor noise characteristics are not always static; they can change over time due to environmental factors (e.g., temperature, lighting) or sensor degradation. Pre-calibrated models become obsolete under such conditions. An online joint estimation approach allows the system to adapt to evolving noise characteristics.
- **Addressing Preprocessing Complexities**: Many raw sensor measurements undergo nonlinear preprocessing (e.g., conversion from polar to Cartesian coordinates) before being used in an estimator. This can significantly alter noise distributions in non-trivial ways, making it challenging to derive accurate noise models for the preprocessed measurements.
- **Moving Beyond Ad Hoc Solutions**: The prevalent use of arbitrary or manually tuned noise covariances (e.g., isotropic identity matrices) in many applications reflects the difficulty of proper noise characterization. This research seeks to provide a principled, data-driven alternative to these ad hoc approaches.
- **Filling a Research Gap**: Despite its recognized importance, the problem of noise covariance estimation, particularly in the context of online, joint estimation for general nonlinear and sparse problems common in robotics and computer vision, has remained comparatively understudied.

By addressing these motivations, the research aims to enable more autonomous, robust, and reliable robotic systems that can operate effectively without human intervention for noise model tuning or extensive pre-calibration.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology involves formulating the joint state and noise covariance estimation problem within a maximum a posteriori (MAP) or maximum likelihood (ML) framework and then leveraging its inherent mathematical structure to derive analytical solutions for the covariance component and efficient algorithms for the combined problem.

**Problem Formulation:**
The paper considers the problem of estimating an unknown primary parameter vector `x_true` from `k` noisy `m`-dimensional measurements `z_i = h_i(x_true) ⊞ ϵ_i`, where `ϵ_i` are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) zero-mean Gaussian noise with unknown covariance `Σ_true`. The problem is framed in terms of estimating the noise information (precision) matrix `P_true = Σ_true^-1`. A Wishart prior `W(P; V, ν)` is assumed for `P_true`, where `V` is a scale matrix and `ν` is the degrees of freedom. Algorithm 1 provides a procedure to set `V` and `ν` based on a prior estimate `Σ_0` and a weight `w_prior`.

The **Joint MAP Problem 1** is formulated as:
`minimize_{x∈M, P⪰0} F(x, P) ≜ -log det P + ⟨M(x), P⟩`
where `M(x) ≜ (kS(x) + V^-1) / (k + ν - m - 1)` and `S(x) = (1/k) Σ_{i=1}^k r_i(x)r_i(x)^T` is the sample covariance matrix, with `r_i(x)` being the residual of measurement `z_i` at `x`.

The paper also introduces three variants of Problem 1 by imposing additional structural constraints on the noise covariance matrix:
- **Problem 2 (Diagonal Joint MAP)**: `P` is constrained to be diagonal.
- **Problem 3 (Eigenvalue-constrained Joint MAP)**: The eigenvalues of `Σ = P^-1` are constrained within `[λ_min, λ_max]`, expressed as `λ_max^-1 I ⪯ P ⪯ λ_min^-1 I`. This is designed to prevent `Σ` from becoming singular or poorly conditioned.
- **Problem 4 (Diagonal Eigenvalue-constrained Joint MAP)**: Both diagonal and eigenvalue constraints are applied simultaneously.

For **Joint ML Estimation (Remark 1)**, the objective function takes a similar form: `F_ML(x, P) ≜ -log det P + ⟨S(x), P⟩`.

**Optimal Information Matrix Estimation (Inner Subproblem):**
The joint problem `F(x, P)` is generally non-convex in `x`. However, for a fixed `x`, the "inner subproblem" of minimizing `F(x, P)` with respect to `P` is a convex optimization problem (Proposition 1). Theorem 1 provides analytical, closed-form solutions for the unique optimal `P^*(x)` for each of the four problem variants:
1. **Problem 1**: `P^*(x) = M(x)^-1`.
2. **Problem 2 (Diagonal)**: `P^*(x) = Diag(M(x))^-1`.
3. **Problem 3 (Eigenvalue-constrained)**: `P^*(x)` is obtained by adjusting the eigenvalues of `M(x)^-1` to fit within the `[λ_min, λ_max]` bounds, while preserving its eigenvectors.
4. **Problem 4 (Diagonal Eigenvalue-constrained)**: `P^*(x)` is a diagonal matrix whose elements are adjusted to fit within the `[λ_min, λ_max]` bounds.

**Ill-posedness of Unconstrained ML (Theorem 2):**
The paper demonstrates that if `S(x)` (the sample covariance) is singular, the unconstrained joint ML problem (without a Wishart prior or eigenvalue constraints) is unbounded below and thus ill-posed. This highlights the importance of the prior or constraints in real-world scenarios where `S(x)` can be singular (e.g., `k < m` or at specific `x` values).

**Extensions:**
The framework extends to:
- **Heteroscedastic Measurements**: When different measurement types have distinct noise distributions, the inner problem decomposes into independent subproblems, each solvable with Theorem 1.
- **Preprocessed/Non-Additive Noise**: For measurements transformed nonlinearly, the sample covariance `S(x)` is replaced by an effective covariance `eS(x)` involving Jacobians of the transformation.

**Algorithms for Joint Estimation:**
Two types of algorithms are proposed:
1. **Algorithm 2 (Variable Elimination)**:
 - This approach analytically substitutes `P^*(x)` (from Theorem 1) back into the objective, resulting in a reduced problem `minimize_x [-log det P^*(x) + ⟨M(x), P^*(x)⟩]`.
 - For Problems 1 and 2, this simplifies to `minimize_x log det M(x)` or `log det Diag(M(x))`, respectively.
 - The reduced problem is then solved using a local optimization method.
 - **Drawbacks**: This method typically loses the sparse structure of the problem, making it less suitable for large-scale applications and incompatible with existing sparse nonlinear least squares solvers.

2. **Algorithm 3 (Hybrid Block-Coordinate Descent - BCD)**:
 - This iterative algorithm alternates between two steps until convergence:
 - **Step 1 (Optimize `x`)**: Fix `P` to its current estimate and minimize the joint objective with respect to `x`. This becomes a standard weighted nonlinear least squares problem, solvable using existing sparse solvers (e.g., g2o, Ceres, GTSAM). The paper suggests using a Riemannian gradient descent step for `x`.
 - **Step 2 (Optimize `P`)**: Fix `x` to its current estimate and minimize the joint objective with respect to `P`. This is the inner subproblem, for which Theorem 1 provides analytical, efficient solutions.
 - **Advantages**: This approach retains sparsity and leverages highly optimized existing solvers for the `x` update. The `P` update is computationally inexpensive, typically `O(k)` (linear in number of measurements) since `m` (residual dimension) is small.
 - **Convergence**: Theorem 3 provides a sublinear convergence rate for Algorithm 3 under assumptions of compact manifolds and Lipschitz smoothness.
 - **Algorithm 4 (Block-Exact BCD)**: A variant where `x` is exactly minimized in Step 1. Theorem 4 guarantees convergence to a local minimum under specific assumptions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents experimental results across linear measurement models and Pose-Graph Optimization (PGO) problems, validating the proposed algorithms.

**A. Linear Measurement Model (Section VI.A)**
- **Setup**: Experiments were conducted with a linear measurement model (`M=R^20`, `k=50` measurements, `m=5`). Noise levels (`σ^2`) were varied, and joint ML estimation was performed (no Wishart prior).
- **`x_true` Estimation Accuracy (RMSE)**: Both Elimination (Algorithm 2) and Block-Coordinate Descent (BCD, Algorithm 4) achieved nearly identical RMSE values across all noise levels. At low noise, their accuracy matched that obtained when the true covariance matrix (`Σ_true`) was perfectly known, indicating successful joint estimation without prior knowledge of `Σ_true`. Performance degraded gracefully with increasing noise, but consistently outperformed a baseline using a fixed identity covariance (`Σ=I`), particularly at low to moderate noise levels.
- **`Σ_true` Estimation Accuracy (2-Wasserstein Distance)**: The estimated noise covariances from both BCD and Elimination were consistently close to the true noise distribution. The error in covariance estimation increased with higher noise levels, as expected.
- **Convergence**: BCD exhibited faster convergence of the objective function compared to the Elimination algorithm.

**B. Pose-Graph Optimization Ablations (Section VI.B)**
- **Dataset**: Manhattan dataset (synthetic, `3,500` poses, `5,598` measurements) and an augmented version with more loop closures (`12,593` measurements).
- **Noise Models**: Both homoscedastic (all measurements share one covariance) and heteroscedastic (separate covariances for odometry and loop closures) scenarios were tested, varying an "information level" scaling factor (`α`).
- **Algorithms Evaluated**: BCD variants incorporating ML, diagonal ML, MAP (Wishart prior), and diagonal MAP (Wishart prior). Compared against g2o with `Σ_true` and `Σ=I`. A relatively weak and inaccurate Wishart prior (`w_prior=0.1`, `Σ_0=0.002I`) was used for MAP variants.
- **Computational Overhead**: The per-iteration computational overhead for updating the covariance matrix in BCD was negligible (0.9 to 1.5 milliseconds on a laptop CPU).
- **Trajectory RMSE**: All BCD variants successfully produced trajectories with RMSE values comparable to or lower than the reference case where `Σ_true` was known (g2o with `Σ_true`). Notably, BCD variants incorporating the Wishart prior (MAP estimates) generally achieved lower average RMSE, highlighting the benefit of even a loosely informed prior, especially in PGO where sample covariance eigenvalues can be very small. The diagonal constraints did not significantly impact RMSE when the true covariance was diagonal. BCD variants consistently outperformed the `Σ=I` baseline in most cases.
- **`Σ` 2-Wasserstein Error**: All BCD variants achieved small 2-Wasserstein errors in covariance estimation, significantly outperforming the `Σ=I` baseline (which had errors over 20 times larger). Diagonal BCD variants often yielded lower errors when the true covariance was diagonal. MAP variants generally outperformed ML variants in covariance estimation, suggesting the prior helps regularize the problem when information is sparse.

**C. RIM Dataset (Real-world 3D PGO) (Section VI.C)**
- **Dataset**: A large real-world 3D PGO dataset (`10,195` poses, `29,743` measurements).
- **Results**: While g2o struggled to produce a coherent trajectory with its default covariance settings, BCD (both ML and MAP variants) produced significantly more accurate trajectory estimates. The MAP estimate with a Wishart prior visually appeared more precise than the ML estimate, suggesting the prior's role in preventing covariance collapse. The estimated covariance, even with an isotropic prior guess, reflected the actual geometry of the trajectory (e.g., larger information along the z-coordinate for a flat trajectory), indicating that measurements contribute meaningful information to the estimation.

**Summary of Findings:**
The experiments consistently demonstrate that the proposed BCD algorithms can jointly estimate both primary parameters and noise covariance matrices with accuracy comparable to, and often exceeding, traditional methods that assume a fixed known covariance or rely on ad hoc settings. The integration of a Wishart prior is shown to be beneficial, especially in large-scale, sparse problems like PGO, by regularizing the covariance estimation and preventing ill-posedness or collapse of the estimated covariance. The algorithms exhibit fast convergence and minimal computational overhead, making them suitable for practical integration into existing systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers a significant contribution to the fields of robotics and computer vision by addressing a long-standing and critical challenge: the accurate and online estimation of noise covariance matrices. The findings have several important implications and potential impacts:

- **Enhanced Estimation Accuracy and Robustness**: By providing a principled method for jointly estimating noise covariances, the work enables more accurate state estimation (e.g., robot poses, landmark positions). This reduces reliance on potentially inaccurate pre-calibrated or ad hoc noise models, leading to more robust performance in real-world applications where noise characteristics can be complex, unknown, or dynamic.
- **Improved Uncertainty Quantification**: Accurate noise covariance matrices are crucial for correctly quantifying the uncertainty of state estimates. This capability is vital for downstream tasks in robotics, such as safe motion planning, collision avoidance, and decision-making under uncertainty. Over- or under-confident uncertainty estimates, a consequence of poor noise modeling, can lead to suboptimal or hazardous system behavior.
- **Reduced Calibration Overhead**: The ability to perform online joint estimation eliminates the need for labor-intensive and often challenging offline calibration processes. This simplifies sensor deployment and system integration, making it more practical to adapt robotic systems to new environments or sensor configurations without extensive manual tuning.
- **Adaptability to Dynamic Environments**: The proposed algorithms allow estimation systems to adapt to evolving noise characteristics that might change due to environmental factors (e.g., temperature, lighting, dust) or sensor degradation over time. This enhances the long-term operational reliability and autonomy of robotic platforms.
- **Seamless Integration with Existing Solvers**: A key practical impact is the design of the Block-Coordinate Descent (BCD) algorithm, which can be readily integrated into widely used sparse nonlinear least squares solvers (like g2o, Ceres Solver, GTSAM) with negligible computational overhead. This low integration barrier facilitates rapid adoption within the robotics and computer vision communities.
- **Theoretical Advancement**: The work provides a rigorous theoretical framework, including the full characterization of the convex structure of the noise covariance estimation problem for a fixed state, and the derivation of analytical, closed-form solutions for optimal covariance estimates under various structural constraints (diagonal, eigenvalue bounds). It also highlights the ill-posedness of unconstrained ML estimation when sample covariance matrices are singular, a common issue in real-world sparse data.
- **Broad Applicability**: The generality of the framework means it is applicable across a broad spectrum of estimation problems beyond SLAM, including bundle adjustment, perspective-n-point (PnP), point cloud registration, and potentially other fields requiring state and covariance estimation from noisy data.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: The work opens avenues for further research, such as integrating robust M-estimation techniques to handle outliers in measurements, extending convergence guarantees to non-compact manifolds (e.g., for unbounded translational components), and drawing further insights from related literature in econometrics and statistics (e.g., IGLS and FGLS).

In summary, this research provides a practical, theoretically grounded solution to a fundamental problem, promising to enhance the accuracy, reliability, and autonomy of estimation systems across various engineering disciplines, particularly in robotics and computer vision.
