# TurboMPC: Fast, Scalable, and Differentiable Model Predictive Control on the GPU

> [!summary] Summary
> TurboMPC is a GPU-native, differentiable Model Predictive Control (MPC) solver that supports expressive formulations crucial for robotics applications, bridging advanced optimization with modern GPU-accelerated learning. It achieves substantial speedups and robust performance in both simulations and on autonomous racing hardware, enabling long-horizon planning and automated parameter tuning.

## Problem

- Traditional CPU-based MPC solvers lack scalability for the large batch sizes and parallel computations demanded by modern GPU-accelerated learning pipelines in robotics.
- Existing GPU-based differentiable MPC solvers often simplify problem classes, omitting essential features like implicit integrators, cross-time-coupled costs, or state inequality constraints required for realistic robotics applications.
- An architectural mismatch exists between high-level automatic differentiation frameworks (like JAX) and low-level GPU kernel execution, hindering the efficient co-design of fast and differentiable solvers.

## Method

- Developed **TurboMPC**, a GPU-native differentiable MPC solver, utilizing a Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) outer loop for nonlinear Optimal Control Problems (OCPs).
- Employed a custom ==Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM)== inner solver, adapted for efficient slack variable handling, with adaptive step-size and problem rescaling for enhanced robustness.
- Designed a co-located architecture combining a JAX frontend for high-level control and automatic differentiation with a performance-optimized fused CUDA backend for the ADMM loop, leveraging `cuDSS` for sparse linear systems.

## Results

- TurboMPC achieved up to **14.6x** speedup for forward and **9.4x** for backward passes over `mpc.pytorch` (and up to **58x**/**15x** over `mpc.pytorch`/`acados` with looser tolerance) on linear-system Reinforcement Learning benchmarks.
- On autonomous racing hardware, GPU-accelerated Bayesian optimization with TurboMPC achieved a ~**50x** speedup for parameter tuning, leading to **2.44** m/s faster average racing speeds compared to hand-tuned baselines.
- The solver scaled to planning horizons exceeding 8000 discretization nodes in racing, enabling multi-corner planning, and successfully demonstrated differentiability for humanoid imitation learning and neural network training while satisfying constraints.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A custom ADMM inner solver with adaptive step-size tuning and a specialized, smoothed projection for slack variables enhances convergence and robustness, particularly during parameter exploration in learning environments.
> - Implicit differentiation for the backward pass, combined with `cuDSS` as a uniform sparse direct factorization primitive, allows for efficient gradient computation despite differing sparsity structures between forward and backward passes.
> - A careful co-design involving JAX for the outer loop and a fused CUDA kernel for the ADMM inner loop minimizes GPU overheads, enabling both low-latency online control and high-throughput batched offline evaluation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is experiencing a paradigm shift, increasingly relying on Graphics Processing Units (GPUs) for computationally intensive tasks such as parallel simulation, large-scale machine learning, and neural-network inference. Model Predictive Control (MPC) is recognized as a robust and reliable tool for robotic systems, enabling model-based planning, constraint reasoning, and adaptive behavior. However, the majority of high-performance MPC solvers have historically been developed for Central Processing Units (CPUs). These CPU-centric designs often exploit the temporal structure of optimal control problems (OCPs) using sequential algorithms like Riccati recursions, but they exhibit limited scalability when integrated into modern, GPU-accelerated learning pipelines that require batching and parallel computations.

Existing GPU-based differentiable MPC solvers offer a pathway to scale MPC and embed its structured decision-making within learning-based robotics. Nevertheless, these solvers frequently impose restrictions on the problem classes they can handle in order to expose parallelism. Common limitations include a lack of support for implicit integrators (crucial for stiff dynamics), cross-time-coupled costs (e.g., control-rate penalties for input smoothness), or state inequality constraints (essential for safety and obstacle avoidance). In contrast, CPU-based solvers like `acados` support a broader range of complex MPC formulations, but their scalability for large models and high batch sizes remains constrained.

A significant challenge in integrating GPU-native MPC is the architectural mismatch between general learning infrastructures (e.g., JAX, with its automatic differentiation and batching capabilities) and the low-overhead kernel execution required for efficient iterative solver design in CUDA. This divergence often necessitates a careful co-design of solvers and learning infrastructure. Furthermore, the sparsity structures of the linear systems encountered in the forward (solving the OCP) and backward (computing gradients for differentiation) passes of an MPC solver can differ, posing a challenge for using uniform solution primitives.

In the context of autonomous racing, a particularly demanding application, MPC requires long-horizon planning with track bounds, stiff dynamics, control-rate costs to protect actuators, and slack variables to maintain recursive feasibility despite model uncertainties. Current approaches in this domain often employ CPU-only solvers or provide only partial GPU acceleration, which limits the potential for large-scale and automated tuning of MPC parameters. This research seeks to address these gaps by developing a differentiable MPC solver that runs entirely on the GPU, is scalable, and supports a comprehensive set of features relevant for real-world robotics applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop TurboMPC, a differentiable Model Predictive Control (MPC) solver that operates entirely on the GPU. This solver aims to achieve high computational efficiency, scalability, and compatibility with expressive MPC formulations relevant for challenging robotics applications, thereby bridging the gap between advanced optimization-based control and modern GPU-accelerated learning paradigms.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations and existing limitations in the robotics and control landscape:

1. **Scaling with Modern Robotics Trends**: Robotics is increasingly characterized by large datasets, parallel simulation environments, and neural networks, all of which heavily leverage GPUs. For MPC to remain a central tool, its computational infrastructure must evolve to match this GPU-centric paradigm. Traditional CPU-based MPC solvers, despite their reliability, often present a bottleneck when integrated into such large-scale, data-driven workflows due to their sequential nature and limited parallelization capabilities.

2. **Addressing Expressiveness and Differentiability Trade-offs**: Current GPU-based differentiable MPC solvers often simplify the problem class to achieve parallel execution. This simplification frequently omits crucial features required for realistic robotics applications, such as:
 - **Implicit integrators**: Essential for accurately modeling stiff dynamics (e.g., in contact-rich systems or high-fidelity vehicle models) without sacrificing numerical stability.
 - **Control-rate costs**: Necessary for smoothing control inputs, preventing actuator wear, and improving system performance.
 - **State inequality constraints**: Fundamental for ensuring safety, obstacle avoidance, and operating within physical limits (e.g., joint limits, friction cones).
 - **Slack variables**: Critical for maintaining recursive feasibility of the MPC problem in the face of model mismatch, disturbances, or exploration in learning environments, preventing solver failures.
 Existing CPU-based solvers support these features but lack the necessary scalability for large-batch, real-time GPU deployments. The objective is to combine these expressive capabilities with GPU acceleration and differentiability.

3. **Enabling Advanced Learning Pipelines**: Differentiability of the MPC solver is crucial for its integration into gradient-based learning algorithms such as Reinforcement Learning (RL), Imitation Learning (IL), and Bayesian Optimization (BO). These pipelines require efficient computation of gradients of the MPC solution with respect to its parameters (e.g., cost weights, model parameters). Current methods either lack full differentiability or incur significant computational overhead during the backward pass. The goal is to provide a fast, differentiable MPC primitive that can be embedded directly into neural networks or used for automated parameter tuning.

4. **Addressing Computational Bottlenecks in GPU Development**: The interaction between high-level automatic differentiation frameworks (like JAX) and low-level GPU programming (like CUDA) often introduces performance overheads. A key motivation is to co-design the solver algorithm and its GPU implementation to mitigate these overheads, ensuring both ease of use (via JAX) and high performance (via fused CUDA kernels and efficient linear algebra primitives).

By addressing these motivations, TurboMPC aims to enable planning over long horizons, handle high-dimensional systems, use expressive cost and constraint functions (including neural networks), and facilitate data-driven control development at scale.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TurboMPC is designed as a differentiable MPC solver that operates entirely on the GPU, integrating optimization algorithms, GPU-specific implementation strategies, and features tailored for learning pipelines. The overall architecture consists of a JAX frontend for high-level control and automatic differentiation, bridged to a performance-optimized CUDA backend.

The solver addresses Optimal Control Problems (OCPs) by employing a Sequential Quadratic Programming (SQP) outer loop. At each SQP iteration, the nonlinear OCP is approximated by a Quadratic Program (QP). This QP approximation involves quadratizing the cost function and linearizing the dynamics and constraints around the current primal-dual iterate. The resulting QP benefits from the time-induced sparsity inherent in OCPs, manifesting as block-tridiagonal (cost Hessian), block-bidiagonal (equality constraint Jacobian), and block-diagonal (inequality constraint Jacobian) matrices. This sparsity is crucial for exploiting GPU parallelism.

The core of TurboMPC lies in its custom Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers (ADMM) inner solver, which efficiently solves these QPs. This ADMM scheme is inspired by OSQP but incorporates a specific method for handling slack variables that avoids increasing the problem size, a technique derived from prior work by Lew et al. (2026). The ADMM procedure involves three main updates:
1. **Primal Update**: This step minimizes the augmented Lagrangian over a set of variables, leading to an equality-constrained QP. The KKT conditions of this QP yield a sparse linear system that is solved via a Schur complement approach. This Schur complement system maintains a block-tridiagonal structure, allowing for efficient GPU-based solution.
2. **Slack Update**: This step involves projecting variables onto the feasible set, incorporating slack variables for inequality constraints. When slack variables are disabled, a standard projection is used. With slack variables enabled, a smoothed projection is applied, which is critical for solver robustness and learning stability, as it prevents crashes due to infeasible states during parameter exploration.
3. **Dual Update**: Multipliers associated with the augmented Lagrangian constraints are updated.
The ADMM scheme includes an adaptive strategy for adjusting step-size parameters (ρ) to balance primal and dual residuals, thereby enhancing convergence. An internal problem rescaling mechanism is also employed to improve numerical robustness, particularly for problems with widely varying state and control magnitudes.

For differentiability (the backward pass), TurboMPC utilizes implicit differentiation. After the SQP loop converges, the solution satisfies the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions. Gradients of a downstream loss with respect to OCP parameters are computed by differentiating these KKT conditions while keeping the active set fixed. This approach avoids unrolling all solver iterations, which can be computationally intensive. The backward pass requires solving a linear system derived from the KKT conditions. A notable aspect of the design is the distinct sparsity structure of this backward-pass linear system compared to the forward-pass linear system (specifically, a zero block in the KKT matrix that differs from the invertible block in the forward pass).

To efficiently handle both the forward and backward linear systems, TurboMPC makes a deliberate co-design choice: it employs `cuDSS` (NVIDIA's CUDA Dense and Sparse Solver library) as a uniform sparse direct factorization primitive. `cuDSS` can handle general symmetric sparse systems and offers batched factorization, making it suitable for both passes despite their structural differences. The symbolic factorization in `cuDSS` is reused across ADMM iterations and only re-factorized when `ρ` (the ADMM penalty parameter) adapts.

The implementation is a result of a three-way co-design involving the optimization algorithm, GPU implementation, and learning pipeline. The outer SQP loop, problem linearization via automatic differentiation, and `vmap`-based batching are managed by JAX. The entire ADMM inner loop is implemented as a fused CUDA kernel, accessed via a C++ Foreign Function Interface (FFI) bridge. This fused CUDA implementation significantly reduces per-kernel launch overheads compared to a JAX-only ADMM loop, leading to substantial speedups. Custom parallel-over-time kernels are developed for matrix-vector products with the sparse OCP matrices, further exploiting GPU capabilities. This allows the same compiled artifact to support both low-latency online control (batch size one) and high-throughput batched offline evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides extensive validation of TurboMPC through both simulation experiments and hardware deployment on an autonomous racing vehicle.

**Simulation Experiments:**

1. **Constrained Drone Obstacle Avoidance**: TurboMPC was demonstrated on a 6-DoF drone task involving circular obstacles, initial/final state constraints, and control box constraints. The solver successfully handled these state inequalities, control constraints, and the optional use of slack variables. Introducing slack variables improved the solver's convergence rate, albeit with a slight increase in constraint violation, illustrating a trade-off between strict feasibility and robustness.

2. **Linear-System Reinforcement Learning Scaling**: A benchmark comparison was conducted against `mpc.pytorch` (a PyTorch-based iLQR solver) and `acados` (a C-based CPU solver) on an RL task involving linear-quadratic optimal control problems with control input box constraints. This problem class allowed for a direct comparison of raw solver scaling.
 - TurboMPC demonstrated significant speedups, particularly as planning horizon, problem size, and batch size increased. For a nominal configuration (B=64, N=160, $n_x=8, n_u=4$) with $u_{max}=1.0$ and solver tolerance $10^{-5}$, TurboMPC achieved up to 14.6x speedup for the forward pass and 9.4x for the backward pass over `mpc.pytorch`.
 - When a looser tolerance of $10^{-3}$ was permitted, TurboMPC's speedups further increased to up to 15x over `acados` and 58x over `mpc.pytorch`.
 - TurboMPC exhibited superior scalability, successfully solving configurations where baselines failed (e.g., `mpc.pytorch` encountered GPU memory limits, `acados` could not compile for larger dimensions). TurboMPC began outperforming `acados` in backward pass speeds for batch sizes between 8 and 32, with its advantage growing significantly for larger batch sizes and horizons.
 - Gradient accuracy, measured by cosine similarity with finite differences, was found to increase with tighter solver tolerances, with a tolerance of $10^{-5}$ yielding accurate gradients.

3. **Differentiability on Constrained Nonlinear Systems**:
 - **Humanoid Imitation Learning (IL)**: TurboMPC was used to recover expert cost weights for a Unitree G1 humanoid performing standing balance. The solver differentiated through the constrained MPC policy. Across 20 random initializations, the median weight-recovery error was $0.59$, and the median imitation loss reached $1.4 \times 10^{-4}$. The recovered weights produced a functional controller capable of closed-loop balancing in MuJoCo from perturbed states.
 - **Neural Network Training**: A TurboMPC policy with a neural-network cost function was trained for a 3D point-mass system tracking a periodic zig-zag reference under input bounds. Using analytic policy gradients, the policy reduced the two-cycle closed-loop tracking RMSE from $1.2 \times 10^{-1}$ m at initialization to $8.3 \times 10^{-2}$ m after 500 gradient steps, while consistently satisfying control constraints.

**Hardware Deployment: Autonomous Racing:**

1. **Auto-tuning of MPC Parameters**: TurboMPC was deployed on a Lexus LC500 vehicle for minimum-time racing. Bayesian optimization (BO) leveraging GPU-batched closed-loop simulations was used to automatically tune MPC parameters (tire friction coefficients, terminal-time penalty, cost weights).
 - **BO Speedup**: GPU acceleration enabled a trial throughput of ~150 trials/hour (24 secs/trial) for a batch size of 8, a ~7.12x speedup over a CPU-based BO setup (~21 trials/hour, 171 secs/trial). Increasing the batch size to 1024 further boosted throughput to ~1,029 trials/hour (~3.5 secs/trial), representing a ~50x speedup over the CPU baseline.
 - **Racing Performance**: The auto-tuned MPC parameters resulted in significantly faster racing compared to a hand-tuned baseline. The vehicle achieved an average speed 2.44 m/s faster, with higher yaw rates and more aggressive total torque application, demonstrating improved driving closer to the vehicle's handling limits.

2. **Scalability to Longer Planning Horizons**: TurboMPC's performance was compared against an SQP solver utilizing OSQP as its inner QP solver on the racing platform.
 - TurboMPC consistently outperformed the OSQP-based baseline, with the performance gap widening significantly at longer planning horizons.
 - TurboMPC successfully scaled to planning horizons exceeding 8000 discretization nodes, allowing the controller to plan multiple corners ahead within a single MPC solve while maintaining vehicle control. In contrast, the OSQP baseline lost control of the vehicle at a horizon of N=1024.

These results collectively demonstrate TurboMPC's efficiency, scalability, and differentiability across diverse robotics tasks, both in simulation and on a full-scale vehicle, enabling new capabilities in learning-based control and long-horizon planning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

TurboMPC represents a significant contribution to the field of robotics and optimal control by addressing a critical need for fast, scalable, and differentiable Model Predictive Control (MPC) on GPUs. This work integrates several advanced computational and algorithmic techniques to overcome the traditional trade-offs between solver expressiveness, computational speed, and compatibility with modern learning frameworks.

The primary significance of TurboMPC lies in its ability to simultaneously provide:
1. **GPU-native Acceleration**: By running entirely on the GPU with a fused CUDA ADMM loop and `cuDSS`, TurboMPC achieves substantial speedups over both CPU-based and hybrid GPU-CPU solvers. This allows for real-time operation of complex MPC problems, even with long planning horizons and high-dimensional systems.
2. **Comprehensive Feature Set**: Unlike many existing GPU-based differentiable solvers, TurboMPC supports a full range of features essential for realistic robotics applications: state and control inequality constraints, implicit integrators for stiff dynamics, cross-time-coupled costs (e.g., control-rate penalties), and slack variables for robust feasibility. This expressiveness enables its application to challenging problems like autonomous racing, humanoid locomotion, and drone control, where such features are critical for performance and safety.
3. **End-to-end Differentiability**: Through implicit differentiation, TurboMPC provides accurate gradients of its solution with respect to problem parameters. This characteristic is fundamental for integrating MPC into data-driven learning pipelines, including Reinforcement Learning (RL), Imitation Learning (IL), and Bayesian Optimization (BO).

The potential impact of TurboMPC is multifaceted:

- **Advancing Learning-based Control**: TurboMPC can serve as a powerful, constraint-aware, and model-based policy class within machine learning frameworks. Its differentiability and scalability allow researchers to train MPC policies more efficiently with large datasets and parallel simulations, leading to more robust and adaptable robotic behaviors. The demonstrated auto-tuning via batched Bayesian optimization on a real racing car highlights its utility in automating complex control parameter calibration, reducing manual effort and improving performance.
- **Enabling Complex Robotic Applications**: The solver's ability to handle expressive OCP formulations and plan over very long horizons (e.g., 8000+ knot points for racing) unlocks new possibilities for complex, dynamic, and safety-critical robotic tasks. This can lead to advancements in autonomous driving (especially at the limits of handling), legged locomotion, drone navigation, and other domains requiring sophisticated real-time decision-making.
- **Bridging Algorithmic and Hardware Innovations**: The co-design approach, which carefully integrates the optimization algorithm with GPU implementation details (JAX frontend, fused CUDA backend, `cuDSS` as a uniform primitive), demonstrates a successful strategy for maximizing performance while maintaining usability. This methodology can inform future development of high-performance solvers for other complex optimization problems in robotics and beyond.
- **Open-Source Contribution**: The decision to open-source TurboMPC facilitates its adoption and further development by the broader research community. This can accelerate innovation in differentiable optimization and its application to robotics.

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as the constant-factor overhead of direct sparse factorization, reliance on double precision, and potential gradient discontinuities, TurboMPC's current capabilities establish it as a significant tool. It provides a robust and efficient foundation for integrating structured, optimal control principles with scalable, data-driven learning, thereby paving the way for more intelligent and capable autonomous systems.
