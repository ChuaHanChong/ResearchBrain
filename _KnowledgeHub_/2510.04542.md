# Code World Models for General Game Playing

> [!summary] Summary
> Code World Models (CWM) use an LLM to translate game rules and a few random-play trajectories into executable Python world models covering transitions, legal moves, observations, and termination. Planners such as MCTS and information-set MCTS run on this model, aided by LLM-synthesized value and hidden-state inference functions. The agent matches or beats Gemini 2.5 Pro in 9 of 10 games, including 4 novel ones.

## Problem

- Prompting an LLM directly for moves relies on fragile implicit pattern matching, producing frequent illegal moves and strategically shallow play that lacks deep multi-step lookahead, even for reasoning models.
- LLM-as-policy agents generalize poorly to novel games absent from training data, so contamination-free out-of-distribution games expose weak strategic performance.
- Prior LLM code-world-model work assumes fully observed, deterministic environments or hindsight access to hidden states, leaving partially observed, stochastic, multi-agent games with never-revealed hidden information unaddressed.

## Method

- **Code World Model** synthesis: the LLM writes an OpenSpiel-style Python model from rules plus 5 random trajectories, refined against auto-generated ==unit tests== via conversation or ==Thompson-sampling tree search==, feeding back stack traces.
- ==Inference-as-code==: the LLM writes samplers over hidden histories or states, verified by unit tests that replay observations, enabling ==information-set MCTS== in imperfect-information games. LLM-written ==value functions== are chosen by tournament to speed search.
- ==Closed-deck autoencoder==: with no hidden information ever revealed, the inference function acts as encoder and the **CWM** as decoder, refined only on ==observation-reconstruction== and execution-error tests, with game rules as regularizer.

## Results

- In perfect-information games, CWM-MCTS beats Gemini 2.5 Pro in **all five** games and is roughly even with MCTS on ground-truth code, showing the synthesized models are near-correct.
- Open-deck transition accuracy is near **1.0** on four imperfect-information games, but Gin rummy reaches only **78%** train and **75%** test, with inference accuracy around **54-59%**, needing the full **500**-call budget.
- Closed-deck synthesis degrades inference: Bargaining test accuracy falls from **1.00** to **0.67** and Gin rummy to **0.10**, yet CWM-ISMCTS still beats or matches Gemini 2.5 Pro, with high variance on Leduc poker.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Shifting the LLM's job from choosing moves to writing a verifiable world model lets classical search convert compute into strength and rules out illegal moves by construction.
> - Unit tests derived from trajectories give a free, checkable refinement signal, and inference functions can be trained without ground-truth hidden states by requiring reconstruction of observations.
> - Code models struggle with rules containing intricate multi-stage procedures, like Gin rummy scoring; hidden-history inference proved harder than learning dynamics, so procedural complexity is the frontier.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of artificial intelligence has seen a rapid expansion in the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to various reasoning tasks, including classical board and card games. Historically, general game playing has been a significant challenge in AI, requiring systems to adapt to new game rules without human intervention. This paper intervenes in this evolving landscape by proposing a fundamentally different paradigm for LLM-based game agents.

The dominant approach in recent years has been to treat LLMs as direct policy agents, prompting them to generate moves based on natural language descriptions of game states and trajectories. While this approach has shown impressive qualitative results, it suffers from several significant drawbacks:
- **Fragile Pattern Matching:** LLMs often rely on implicit, brittle pattern matching, leading to frequent illegal moves.
- **Shallow Strategic Depth:** Direct LLM policies typically lack the deep, multi-step lookahead capabilities necessary for strategic mastery, akin to "System 1" (intuitive) thinking rather than "System 2" (deliberative) reasoning.
- **Poor Generalization:** These models struggle to adapt to novel games not explicitly encountered during their vast training.

This work directly challenges the "LLM as policy" paradigm. Instead, Lehrach et al. position their research within the model-based AI landscape, specifically leveraging LLMs as **model induction engines**. This means the LLM's primary role is not to play the game directly, but to *understand* the game rules and translate them into a formal, executable world model (Python code) that can then be used by traditional, high-performance planning algorithms like Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS).

This approach builds upon and extends prior model-based LLM work, such as "WorldCoder" (Tang et al., 2024a) and GIF-MCTS (Dainese et al., 2024), which also explored using LLMs for symbolic world model learning and planning. However, this paper introduces several key advancements:
- **Handling Imperfect Information and Stochasticity:** Unlike most prior work that assumed fully observed and deterministic environments, this research tackles partially observed and stochastic multi-agent games (e.g., Poker), a significantly harder problem.
- **Synthesis of Auxiliary Functions:** Beyond the core world model, the LLM is tasked with generating heuristic value functions (to enhance planning efficiency) and inference functions (to estimate hidden states in imperfect information games).
- **Novel "Closed Deck" Scenario:** The paper introduces and addresses the challenging "closed deck" setting, where the agent learns a world model from strictly partially observable trajectories, never having access to hidden state information, even in hindsight—a crucial step towards truly autonomous model discovery in unknown environments.

While other research efforts, like those by Gemp et al. (2024) or Deng et al. (2025), focus on using LLMs to construct game-theoretic models or explicit game trees for smaller instances, this work's emphasis on executable code world models offers a potential path to scale to much larger and more complex game instances due to the efficiency of code-based representations. By shifting the LLM's role from intuitive player to inductive model builder, this research offers a compelling alternative in the quest for truly general and strategically deep AI game players.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a general game-playing agent that transcends the limitations of existing LLM-as-policy approaches by transforming the role of Large Language Models. Instead of directly generating moves, the proposed methodology aims to use LLMs as powerful tools for **inducing formal, executable world models** from natural language game descriptions and observational data.

The primary motivations driving this alternative approach stem directly from the identified drawbacks of existing LLM policies in game-playing:

1. **Addressing Fragility and Illegal Moves:** Directly prompting LLMs for moves often leads to frequent violations of game rules. This fragility arises because LLMs implicitly learn patterns from vast data, which may not constitute a robust or verifiable understanding of formal rules. The authors are motivated to create a system where legal actions are algorithmically guaranteed.
2. **Achieving Strategic Depth:** While LLMs excel at pattern recognition, they typically lack the capacity for deep, multi-step lookahead and systematic deliberation—the "System 2" thinking crucial for strategic mastery in complex games. The research aims to combine the LLM's semantic understanding with the profound search capabilities of classical planning algorithms, thereby enabling agents to develop much deeper tactical foresight.
3. **Enhancing Generalization to Novel Games:** A significant limitation of direct LLM policies is their poor performance on games not present in their training data (Out-of-Distribution, OOD games). The motivation here is to enable LLMs to generalize not by memorizing game-specific strategies, but by mastering the "meta-task" of translating rules and examples into executable code. This shifts the learning burden from specific game policies to general model induction, which should inherently facilitate adaptation to new, unseen games.

To achieve these motivations, the methodology focuses on providing three distinct advantages:

- **Verifiability:** The generated Code World Model (CWM) acts as a formal, unambiguous specification of the game's rules. This allows planning algorithms to precisely enumerate valid actions and prevent illegal moves, contingent on the correctness of the synthesized model.
- **Strategic Depth:** By decoupling the LLM's rule understanding from the actual decision-making process, the system can harness the power of classical planners (like MCTS and ISMCTS) to perform extensive lookahead and develop sophisticated strategies.
- **Generalization:** By concentrating the LLM's intelligence on the task of "data-to-code" translation, the model can more effectively adapt to new game environments, focusing on the underlying mechanics rather than specific game patterns.

Furthermore, the research explicitly aims to extend existing CWM frameworks by tackling more complex scenarios, specifically by enabling model learning in partially observed and stochastic multi-agent environments, and by introducing the synthesis of heuristic value functions and inference functions to further enhance planning performance and belief state estimation. The introduction of the "closed deck" scenario represents a particularly challenging and novel objective in learning from strictly partial observability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology, named CWM-(IS)MCTS, is a pipeline that leverages Large Language Models (LLMs) to synthesize executable Python code representing a game's world model, subsequently using classical planning algorithms to play. The approach involves several distinct phases:

#### 3.1. General Agent Workflow

1. **Offline Trajectory Collection:** For any new game, the agent first plays a small number of games (typically 5) to completion using a random policy. This generates a set of "offline trajectories," comprising observations, rewards, legal actions, and ground truth states (for open deck scenarios) at each timestep. This data serves as concrete examples for the LLM.
2. **Code World Model (CWM) Synthesis:** An LLM (specifically Gemini 2.5 Pro) is given a natural language description of the game rules along with the collected offline trajectories. Its task is to synthesize Python code for the game's world model, adhering to the OpenSpiel API format. This CWM includes functions for:
 - `apply_action`: State transition.
 - `get_legal_actions`: Legal move enumeration.
 - `get_observations`: Observation function (crucial for imperfect information games).
 - `get_rewards`: Reward function.
 - `get_current_player`: Current player identification.
 - Termination checks.
3. **Iterative CWM Refinement:** Initial CWMs are often imperfect. To improve quality, an iterative refinement process is employed. Unit tests are automatically generated from the offline trajectories, comparing the CWM's predictions against the recorded ground truth. Failed unit tests (including stack traces) are fed back to the LLM to guide code corrections. Refinement continues until all tests pass or a budget of LLM calls is exhausted. Two refinement strategies were explored:
 - **Conversation (Sequential Refinement):** A "chat mode" where stack traces from new failures are appended to the ongoing prompt.
 - **Tree Search (Main Approach):** Maintains multiple CWM hypotheses in a tree structure, using Thompson sampling to select and refine promising candidates, providing more resilience and backtracking capabilities.
4. **Auxiliary Function Synthesis:**
 - **Inference Functions (for Imperfect Information Games - IIGs):** For IIGs, the LLM also synthesizes code to estimate the hidden state. Two methods are investigated:
 - **Hidden History Inference:** The LLM generates a function `resample_history` that samples a plausible full action history (including chance actions and opponent actions) consistent with the player's observed actions and observations. The CWM then reconstructs observations from this history, and unit tests verify consistency. This ensures sampled states are valid within the CWM.
 - **Hidden State Inference:** The LLM directly generates a function `resample_state` to sample a hidden state `s_t` based on observations `o_i_1:t` and actions `a_i_1:t`. Unit tests verify that `get_observations(s_t)` matches `o_t`. This is simpler but doesn't guarantee a valid history or posterior support.
 - **Heuristic Value Functions:** The LLM generates a `value_function(state, player_id)` to estimate the value of non-terminal leaf nodes in the search tree. These are not refined but are selected via a tournament among multiple generated functions. This speeds up (IS)MCTS by providing better initial evaluations than random rollouts.
5. **Game Play Execution:** With a refined CWM (and optionally synthesized inference/value functions), the agent plays against opponents. For perfect information games, standard Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) is used. For imperfect information games, Information Set MCTS (ISMCTS) is employed, leveraging the synthesized inference function to sample belief states. Both MCTS and ISMCTS are augmented by the synthesized value function for efficiency.

#### 3.2. Closed Deck Synthesis (Novel Contribution)

A significant innovation is the **closed deck synthesis** scenario. This addresses cases where hidden state information is *never* available, not even during the offline trajectory collection used for training. In this setting:

- The LLM generates a CWM and a hidden history inference function.
- Unit tests are restricted to what is observable: verifying consistency between inferred hidden histories, CWM state transitions, and the player's own observations (`o_i_t` = `o_i_t` and `a_i_t` = `a_i_t`).
- Execution error checks are crucial to ensure the CWM produces playable game states.
- This setup essentially creates a "regularized CWM autoencoder," where the inference function acts as an encoder and the CWM as a decoder, with game rules and the OpenSpiel API serving as strong structural regularizers.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

The methodology is evaluated across 10 diverse two-player games, categorized into:
- **Perfect Information (5):** Tic-tac-toe, Connect four, Backgammon, Generalized tic-tac-toe (OOD), Generalized chess (OOD).
- **Imperfect Information (5):** Leduc poker, Bargaining, Gin rummy, Quadranto (OOD), Hand of war (OOD).
Four of these are novel "Out-of-Distribution" (OOD) games specifically designed for the paper to prevent contamination from LLM training data.

Performance is measured against three baselines:
- **Random:** An agent playing random legal moves.
- **GT-(IS)MCTS:** An (IS)MCTS agent with access to the *ground truth* game code (an "upper bound" for planning performance).
- **Gemini 2.5 Pro:** The LLM acting directly as a policy, using "dynamic thinking."

All MCTS/ISMCTS agents run 1,000 simulations per action, using either the synthesized value function or 10 random rollouts for leaf node evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of the Code World Model (CWM) approach, particularly in comparison to LLMs directly acting as policies.

#### 4.1. Synthesis Accuracy

- **Perfect Information Games:** The CWM approach achieved near-perfect accuracy (1.0 training, test, and online transition accuracy) across all five perfect information games (Tic-tac-toe, Connect four, Backgammon, Generalized tic-tac-toe, Generalized chess). Both conversation and tree search refinement strategies proved highly effective, with tree search showing a slight edge in overall performance and efficiency. This indicates that LLMs can reliably synthesize executable world models for fully observable, deterministic games.
- **Imperfect Information Games (Open Deck):**
 - **Transition Accuracy:** Most imperfect information games (Bargaining, Leduc Poker, Quadranto, Hand of War) also achieved high transition accuracy (0.98-1.0), demonstrating the LLM's ability to model complex game dynamics even with hidden information available during offline trajectory analysis. However, **Gin Rummy** proved to be an outlier, with significantly lower transition accuracy (78% train, 74% test), which the authors attribute to its intricate, multi-stage procedural rules. This highlights a limitation in handling highly complex procedural logic.
 - **Inference Accuracy:** When synthesizing inference functions for hidden history, accuracy varied. While Bargaining, Leduc Poker, and Quadranto showed high inference accuracy (0.98-1.0), Hand of War was slightly lower (0.93-1.0), and **Gin Rummy** again performed poorly (52-96%). This suggests that inferring a full hidden history can be more challenging than learning the basic transition dynamics, especially for games with complex hidden states. Hidden history inference slightly outperformed hidden state inference.
- **Imperfect Information Games (Closed Deck):** As expected, restricting observability during training (closed deck) led to a degradation in synthesis quality, particularly inference accuracy. Gin Rummy, in this setting, exhibited extremely poor inference accuracy (5-53%). Despite this, game play performance did not degrade proportionally, suggesting some resilience or an ability to find simpler yet effective models under strict partial observability.

#### 4.2. Arena: Game Play Performance

The game play results robustly support the CWM-(IS)MCTS approach over direct LLM policies.

- **Perfect Information Games:** CWM-MCTS agents performed exceptionally well, achieving win/loss/draw rates comparable to or matching the GT-MCTS agent (which uses the ground truth game code). Critically, CWM-MCTS **outperformed Gemini 2.5 Pro (LLM as policy) in all five perfect information games**, often winning by a significant margin or forcing forfeits from Gemini 2.5 Pro. This confirms the strategic depth and verifiability benefits of combining LLM-induced models with classical planning. The use of synthetic value functions proved beneficial for Generalized Tic-Tac-Toe.
- **Imperfect Information Games (Open Deck):** CWM-ISMCTS agents generally **beat or matched Gemini 2.5 Pro in 4 out of 5 imperfect information games**, with high variance observed in Leduc Poker. Gin Rummy performance against Gemini 2.5 Pro was notable, though influenced by Gemini's very high forfeit rate in this game. This demonstrates the method's ability to handle the added complexity of partial observability and stochasticity. Synthetic value functions improved performance in Bargaining.
- **Imperfect Information Games (Closed Deck):** Even with the reduced information for model synthesis, CWM-ISMCTS-Closed agents largely **continued to beat or match Gemini 2.5 Pro**. While synthesis quality degraded, game play performance remained strong, highlighting the robustness of the approach. Surprisingly, CWM-ISMCTS-Closed showed an *improvement* in Hand of War compared to the open deck setting, which the authors hypothesize might be due to the LLM synthesizing simpler, yet effective, state spaces under stricter constraints.
- **PPO Planning (Appendix D):** An ablation study in Appendix D showed that training a PPO agent on top of the learned CWMs (PPO-CWM) also outperformed random and Gemini 2.5 Pro agents. However, PPO-CWM was generally outperformed by the CWM-MCTS agent (which performs online planning), suggesting that for the tested games and CWM quality, the deep search of MCTS provided a stronger policy than a learned reactive PPO policy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant advancement in the field of general game playing and the application of Large Language Models to complex reasoning tasks. By proposing a paradigm shift from LLM-as-policy to LLM-as-model-inducer, Lehrach et al. address fundamental limitations of current LLM-based agents and open new avenues for AI development.

The key significance and potential impacts are multi-faceted:

- **Elevating LLM Capabilities:** The work reframes LLMs from mere pattern-matchers to powerful inductive reasoning engines. This highlights their capability to translate high-level natural language instructions and sparse demonstrations into formal, executable, and verifiable knowledge (code), rather than just generating free-form text or direct actions. This could broaden the scope of LLM applications in areas requiring formal verification and logical consistency.
- **Robust General Game Playing:** The CWM framework delivers on the promise of more robust general game playing. By combining the semantic understanding of LLMs with the computational rigor of classical planning algorithms like MCTS and ISMCTS, the resulting agents achieve verifiable legal moves, deep strategic foresight, and strong generalization capabilities to novel, out-of-distribution games. This significantly outperforms LLMs used as direct policy generators, moving closer to truly intelligent and adaptable game-playing AI.
- **Advancements in Model Learning for Complex Environments:** The novel contributions of synthesizing heuristic value functions and, more critically, inference functions for imperfect information games, push the boundaries of automated model learning. The successful tackling of the "closed deck" scenario—where learning occurs under strict partial observability without any access to hidden states—is a pioneering step towards developing agents that can autonomously discover world models in highly uncertain real-world settings. This has profound implications for robotics, scientific discovery, and decision-making in opaque systems.
- **Implications for AI Safety and Interpretability:** The generation of an explicit, executable code world model offers inherent advantages for AI safety and interpretability. Unlike opaque neural network policies, a CWM provides a clear, inspectable representation of the agent's understanding of the environment's rules. This verifiability allows for algorithmic checks, debugging, and potentially formal verification of the agent's environmental understanding, contributing to more reliable and trustworthy AI systems.
- **New Research Frontiers:** The paper explicitly points towards critical future research directions, such as active and online learning of world models (where agents continuously refine their models during interaction) and extending the methodology to open-world games with free-form text or visual interfaces. These directions suggest that the CWM framework could be a foundational component for building more sophisticated, adaptable, and general-purpose AI agents capable of understanding and navigating increasingly complex and unknown environments.

In essence, "Code World Models for General Game Playing" represents a pivotal shift in how LLMs can contribute to sophisticated AI agents. By empowering LLMs to build foundational knowledge in the form of executable code, this research paves the way for a new generation of AI systems that are not only capable of complex reasoning but also operate with a higher degree of verifiability, strategic depth, and adaptability.
