# CARE: Enhancing Safety of Visual Navigation through Collision Avoidance via Repulsive Estimation

> [!summary] Summary
> The CARE framework enhances the safety of existing visual navigation models by integrating monocular depth estimation and repulsive force calculations to adjust trajectories, thereby preventing collisions in out-of-distribution environments. It reduces collision rates by up to 100% and increases collision-free travel distance by up to 10.7 times across diverse robot platforms, operating without additional sensors or fine-tuning.

## Problem

- Learning-based visual navigation models, particularly foundation models, frequently generate unsafe trajectories and collide in out-of-distribution (OOD) environments due to their reliance on appearance-based reasoning.
- These models often struggle with unseen objects or variations in camera configurations (e.g., field of view, pose), lacking explicit geometric understanding for robust collision avoidance.
- Addressing safety issues in new environments typically demands extensive, resource-intensive data collection and retraining efforts, hindering practical zero-shot deployment.

## Method

- **CARE** constructs a top-down local obstacle map from a single RGB observation using a pretrained monocular metric depth model.
- It computes repulsive forces on the robot's planned trajectory (waypoints) from detected obstacles using an ==Artificial Potential Fields== formulation.
- The framework adjusts the entire trajectory by rotating it away from the direction of maximum repulsion and implements a ==Safety-Enhancing Mechanism (Safe-FOV)== to prevent collisions during sharp turns by conditionally suppressing forward motion.

## Results

- **CARE** improved collision-free travel distance in undirected exploration by **2.9 to 10.7** across three robot platforms, with LoCoBot achieving an average of **21.67 meters**.
- It reduced collision rates in image goal-conditioned navigation by **50% to 100%**, achieving complete collision elimination for LoCoBot on both NoMaD and ViNT baselines.
- The framework significantly enhanced goal arrival rates (e.g., NoMaD on LoCoBot from **50% to 90%**) and demonstrated robust avoidance of dynamic human obstacles with **0 collisions** in scenarios where baseline models experienced **7-10 collisions**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Monocular depth estimation can be effectively leveraged to generate local 3D obstacle maps, enabling reactive collision avoidance without dedicated range sensors.
> - An Artificial Potential Field approach, when applied to predicted trajectories, offers a plug-and-play method to steer robots away from obstacles while largely preserving the original navigation policy's intent.
> - A conditional motion control strategy that prioritizes in-place rotation before proceeding (Safe-FOV) helps mitigate risks associated with camera blind spots during turns, ensuring the robot faces a clear path.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The field of robotic navigation has seen significant advancements with the development of vision-language models (VLMs) and vision-based foundation models. These models leverage deep learning, particularly image representation learning and large language models (LLMs), to enable robots to interpret contextual information, follow high-level instructions, and perform complex navigation tasks using primarily RGB images. Models such as LM-Nav, LFG, and NavGPT focus on semantic reasoning and high-level planning, while vision-based foundation models like GNM, ViNT, and NoMaD aim to provide low-level control that generalizes across varied camera intrinsics, robot dynamics, and hardware configurations, often with minimal or zero-shot fine-tuning.

Despite their impressive generalization capabilities and ability to operate solely on monocular RGB input, these learning-based visual navigation models exhibit limitations, particularly concerning safety in out-of-distribution (OOD) environments. Their reliance on appearance-based reasoning, without explicit geometric understanding, can lead to the generation of unsafe trajectories and collisions when encountering unseen objects or operating under different camera setups (e.g., variations in field of view, camera pose, or focal length). Adapting these models to new platforms or environments often necessitates extensive data collection and retraining efforts, which can be resource-intensive and impractical for real-world deployment.

Prior work has attempted to address safety in learning-based navigation through various methods. These include waypoint filtering and fallback strategies (Safe-VLN), risk estimation from visual input (Failure Prediction), perception model calibration (PwC), and adaptive control methods like online constraint updates, control barrier functions, and velocity-obstacle-based shielding. Some approaches, such as MonoNav, reconstruct local 3D metric maps from monocular depth for safer planning, but may compromise task success or assume static environments. NavRL integrates safety shields but still requires retraining for different robot platforms. These existing safety mechanisms often entail tradeoffs between safety and navigation performance, or impose requirements for additional sensors, significant retraining, or assumptions about the environment.

The CARE framework is introduced within this context as a method to enhance the safety of existing vision-based navigation models without requiring additional range sensors or fine-tuning of pretrained policies. It aims to bridge the gap between the generalization capabilities of foundation models and the necessity for robust collision avoidance in diverse, unseen real-world scenarios. By integrating monocular depth estimation with reactive local planning, CARE seeks to provide a plug-and-play solution that dynamically adjusts unsafe trajectories, thereby improving the real-world applicability of learning-based visual navigation systems.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary motivation for developing CARE stems from a critical limitation observed in current learning-based visual navigation models, particularly foundation models: their performance can degrade significantly in out-of-distribution (OOD) environments. While these models excel at generating viable trajectories using only RGB images in familiar settings, they often struggle with unseen objects or variations in camera configurations (e.g., changes in field of view, camera pose, or focal length). This lack of robustness can result in trajectories that lead to collisions, thereby compromising safety and reliability. Addressing these generalization failures typically requires substantial efforts in data collection and additional fine-tuning, which limits the practical deployability of these models in diverse real-world scenarios.

To mitigate this limitation, the key objectives of the CARE framework are:

- **Enhance Safety:** Develop a mechanism to significantly improve the collision avoidance capabilities of learning-based visual navigation models, thereby increasing their robustness in OOD environments.
- **Plug-and-Play Integration:** Design an attachable module that can be seamlessly integrated into any existing RGB-based navigation model that generates local robot trajectories (e.g., waypoints or full trajectories). This modularity is intended to ensure broad applicability across different vision-based navigation policies.
- **Sensor Parity:** Achieve enhanced safety without necessitating additional range sensors (such as LiDAR or dedicated depth cameras). The system is designed to leverage only the RGB input already available to the base navigation model.
- **Zero-Shot Adaptation:** Operate effectively without requiring fine-tuning or retraining of the pretrained base navigation models. This objective aims to preserve the generalization benefits of foundation models while overcoming their safety deficiencies in novel environments.
- **Dynamic Trajectory Adjustment:** Implement a reactive mechanism that can dynamically adjust the trajectories produced by the pretrained navigation model. This adjustment should be based on real-time environmental perception to steer the robot away from detected obstacles.

In essence, the motivation is to provide a practical, hardware-agnostic, and training-free solution to a fundamental safety challenge in learning-based visual navigation, making these advanced navigation systems more reliable and suitable for deployment in dynamic and previously unseen real-world settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CARE (Collision Avoidance via Repulsive Estimation) is proposed as a plug-and-play module designed to augment the output of vision-based navigation policies. It operates by utilizing the same RGB observation input as the navigation model to estimate depth and subsequently adjust the predicted path. The CARE framework processes information in three stages: Top-Down Range Estimation, Repulsive Force Estimation and Trajectory Adjustment, and a Safety-Enhancing Mechanism.

**Stage 1: Top-Down Range Estimation**
The initial step involves constructing a top-down local obstacle map from the RGB observation.
- **Depth Prediction:** A pretrained monocular metric depth model, specifically UniDepthV2 (ViT-S backbone), is employed to predict a dense depth map from the input RGB image (I). This model can either output a depth map for subsequent projection or directly a 3D point cloud.
- **Point Cloud Filtering:** The predicted 3D points P = {(X_i, Y_i, Z_i)} are filtered to retain only relevant obstacles. Points are selected if they satisfy (Z > 0) ∧ (Z ≤ τ_z) ∧ (Y ≥ −ϵ), where τ_z defines the maximum sensing range and ϵ is a vertical margin to exclude points that are too high (e.g., ceilings) or too low (e.g., the ground plane, if ϵ is positive). Coordinates are represented in the local robot frame, with the positive x-axis forward and positive y-axis to the left.
- **Obstacle Set Formation:** The x-axis of the filtered 3D points is discretized into M uniform bins. For each bin, the closest point along the Z-axis (depth) is selected, forming a 2D obstacle set O = {(x*_j, z*_j)}. This set represents a top-down view of obstacles in the robot's immediate vicinity.

**Stage 2: Repulsive Force Estimation and Trajectory Adjustment**
This stage calculates repulsive forces from the detected obstacles to modify the robot's planned trajectory.
- **Repulsive Force Calculation:** For a given trajectory T = {p_1..., p_K} (or a single waypoint p_k) generated by the vision-based policy, a repulsive force F_rep(p_k, O) is computed for each waypoint p_k. This computation utilizes an Artificial Potential Fields (APF) formulation, where each obstacle o_m in O exerts a repulsive force that is inversely proportional to the square of its distance from the waypoint and directed away from the obstacle. The formulation used is F_rep(p_k, O) = Σ_{m=1}^M −1/∥p_k − o_m∥^3_2 ⋅ (p_k − o_m)/∥p_k − o_m∥_2.
- **Maximum Repulsion Waypoint Selection:** The waypoint k* within the trajectory T that experiences the maximum repulsive force magnitude, ∥F_rep(p_k*, O)∥_2, is identified. For policies that output a single waypoint, this point is used directly.
- **Heading Adjustment Angle:** The repulsive vector at p_k* is converted into a heading adjustment angle θ_rep = arctan2(F_rep,y(p_k*), F_rep,x(p_k*)).
- **Angle Clipping:** To prevent excessive deviation from the goal-directed path, the adjustment angle θ_rep is clipped to θ_rot = clip(θ_rep, −θ_clip, θ_clip), where θ_clip is a predefined maximum allowable rotation angle.
- **Trajectory Rotation:** A 2D rotation matrix R(θ_rot) is applied to all waypoints in the original trajectory T, yielding an adjusted trajectory T' = {p'_1..., p'_K}. This rotation steers the entire trajectory away from the detected obstacles while attempting to preserve its general direction toward the goal.

**Stage 3: Safety-Enhancing Mechanism (Safe-FOV)**
This mechanism addresses the limitations posed by the camera's field of view (FOV) and aims to prevent collisions that might occur during sharp turns.
- **Desired Heading Computation:** The adjusted waypoint p'_k* (the one corresponding to k* from Stage 2) is used to compute the desired heading θ_des = arctan2(p'_k*,y, p'_k*,x).
- **Conditional Motion Control:** A rotation threshold θ_thres is defined.
 - If the absolute desired heading change |θ_des| exceeds θ_thres, forward motion is suppressed. The robot performs an in-place rotation (`RotateInPlace`) until the heading falls within the threshold. This prioritizes turning to face the safe direction before proceeding.
 - If |θ_des| is less than or equal to θ_thres, the robot performs a combined forward and turning motion (`MoveForwardAndTurn`).
- **Reactive Activation:** CARE only activates its adjustment mechanism when obstacles are detected within the defined sensing range τ_z (i.e., O ≠ ∅). If no obstacles are detected, the original trajectory generated by the vision-based model is preserved, ensuring minimal deviation when not necessary.

**Experimental Setup:**
CARE was evaluated on three distinct mobile robot platforms: LoCoBot (with a 170° FOV fisheye camera), Clearpath TurtleBot4 (89.5° FOV OAK-D Pro camera), and DJI RoboMaster S1 (120° FOV camera). All robots operated under consistent velocity constraints (0.2 m/s linear, 0.8 rad/s angular). Parameters specific to CARE (e.g., τ_z, ϵ, θ_clip, θ_thres) were empirically tuned for each platform to optimize performance. Baseline navigation models used were NoMaD and ViNT, with trajectory selection typically focusing on the second waypoint of the first generated trajectory. Experiments included undirected exploration in OOD environments with static obstacles and image goal-conditioned navigation with both static OOD and dynamic human obstacles.

### 4. Main findings and results

The evaluation of CARE involved real-world experiments across three diverse robot platforms in both undirected exploration and image goal-conditioned navigation tasks, incorporating out-of-distribution (OOD) and dynamic obstacles.

**Undirected Exploration Task in Unseen Environments:**
In this task, robots explored a confined area (3.5 m × 2.8 m) containing 10 OOD plastic boxes, with the goal of maximizing collision-free travel distance. NoMaD was used as the baseline navigation model, both with and without CARE integration.
- **Collision-Free Distance:** CARE consistently improved the distance traveled before the first collision across all robot platforms. The improvement ranged from 2.9× to 10.7× compared to NoMaD without CARE.
 - **LoCoBot:** Achieved the most significant improvement (10.7×), traveling an average of 21.67 m with CARE versus 2.02 m without. This was attributed to its wide-angle fisheye camera providing extensive environmental perception and NoMaD's prior training on similar camera data.
 - **TurtleBot4:** Showed a moderate improvement (3.3×), increasing from 1.39 m to 4.55 m. Its camera mounting position aided in maintaining obstacle visibility during turns, despite a narrower FOV.
 - **RoboMaster S1:** Exhibited the smallest improvement (2.9×), from 2.91 m to 8.52 m. While its wider FOV contributed to a relatively higher baseline distance, the front-mounted camera created blind spots during turning, limiting the gains from CARE.

**Image Goal-Conditioned Navigation with Unseen Obstacles:**
This task involved navigating a 24-meter corridor with random OOD obstacles (boxes) placed along a pre-defined topological graph. NoMaD and ViNT were used as baseline models, evaluated with and without CARE. Metrics included path length, completion time, collision counts, and arrival rate over 10 distinct obstacle setups.
- **Path Length and Completion Time:** Integration of CARE resulted in slightly longer paths (up to 4.27% increase) and increased completion times (up to 23.89% increase). This was attributed to the adjusted trajectories that prioritized safer navigation around detected obstacles, leading to less direct paths.
- **Collisions:** CARE consistently reduced collision rates across all platforms:
 - **LoCoBot:** Achieved complete collision elimination (100% reduction) for both NoMaD (0.7 to 0.0 collisions) and ViNT (0.4 to 0.0 collisions).
 - **RoboMaster S1:** Demonstrated substantial reductions (80–88%), with collisions for NoMaD dropping from 0.8 to 0.1 and for ViNT from 1.0 to 0.2.
 - **TurtleBot4:** Showed meaningful reductions (50–57%), with NoMaD collisions decreasing from 0.7 to 0.3 and ViNT from 0.2 to 0.1.
 - The inability to eliminate all collisions in some cases (TurtleBot4 and RoboMaster) was attributed to inaccuracies in monocular depth estimation (e.g., on reflective surfaces, low-texture areas, or challenging lighting) and limitations arising from the camera's field of view or mounting position, which could cause side collisions with obstacles outside the camera's view.
- **Arrival Rate:** CARE significantly enhanced the goal arrival rates across all platforms:
 - LoCoBot: NoMaD's arrival rate improved from 50% to 90%, and ViNT's from 50% to 80%.
 - RoboMaster S1: NoMaD improved from 30% to 80%, and ViNT from 50% to 80%.
 - TurtleBot4: ViNT, which had a baseline of 70%, reached 100% arrival rate with CARE. NoMaD, starting at 20%, improved to 50% with CARE, indicating that while CARE enhances safety, the quality of the base navigation model's trajectories still influences overall success.

**Navigation with Dynamic Obstacles (Appendix A.1):**
Additional tests with up to three people abruptly crossing the robot's path demonstrated CARE's ability to handle unpredictable dynamic obstacles.
- Baseline models (NoMaD, ViNT) often failed to react effectively, resulting in 7 to 10 collisions out of 10 trials in various dynamic scenarios (e.g., person appearing from side, behind, or front).
- CARE-integrated robots, in contrast, consistently exhibited reactive stopping and safe avoidance, achieving 0 collisions across all dynamic obstacle scenarios. This improvement was linked to the repulsive force estimation based on predicted depth, enabling reliable detection of human legs, and the Safe-FOV mechanism that suppressed forward motion until a safe heading was achieved.

**Performance in Seen Environments (Appendix A.2):**
A sanity check in seen environments without OOD obstacles confirmed that both NoMaD and ViNT achieved a 100% success rate without CARE, validating the correct implementation and functionality of the base models and the experimental setup. This supports the conclusion that performance degradation in other experiments was due to OOD conditions rather than inherent flaws.

Overall, the findings indicate that CARE provides a consistent and substantial enhancement in navigation safety across various robot platforms and environmental conditions without requiring additional training or sensor hardware.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The CARE framework presents a notable contribution to the field of robotic navigation, particularly concerning the safety and practical deployment of learning-based visual navigation systems. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Enhanced Safety in Out-of-Distribution Environments:** CARE directly addresses a critical vulnerability of current vision-based foundation models: their tendency to generate unsafe trajectories and collide in novel or out-of-distribution (OOD) environments. By providing a mechanism for reactive collision avoidance, CARE significantly improves the safety and robustness of these systems, making them more reliable for real-world applications where unexpected obstacles are common. The demonstrated reduction in collision rates (up to 100%) and increase in collision-free travel distance underscores this impact.
- **Enabling Zero-Shot Deployment:** A key strength of CARE is its "plug-and-play" nature and its ability to function without fine-tuning the underlying navigation model or requiring additional range sensors. This capability is crucial for achieving truly zero-shot deployment of visual navigation models in unseen environments. It eliminates the need for extensive data collection and retraining efforts, which are typically resource-intensive and often prohibitive, thereby lowering the barriers to entry for deploying advanced visual navigation systems.
- **Increased Real-World Applicability:** By improving safety and facilitating zero-shot deployment, CARE broadens the practical applicability of sophisticated visual navigation models. Robots can operate more reliably in dynamic and varied environments, from industrial settings to public spaces, where unexpected changes or previously unseen objects are a constant factor. The successful handling of dynamic human obstacles further highlights its potential for human-robot interaction scenarios.
- **Modularity and Generalizability:** The design of CARE as an attachable module allows it to be integrated seamlessly with a wide range of existing and future RGB-based navigation policies (e.g., those generating waypoints or trajectories). Its demonstrated effectiveness across diverse robot platforms with varying camera configurations (fisheye, narrow, wide FOV, different mounting positions) attests to its generalizability, suggesting it could become a standard enhancement for various mobile robot systems.
- **Cost-Effectiveness and Accessibility:** By relying solely on monocular RGB input for depth estimation and collision avoidance, CARE avoids the need for expensive additional sensors like LiDAR or dedicated stereo/depth cameras. This approach makes advanced safety features more accessible and cost-effective, potentially accelerating the adoption of autonomous navigation solutions in a broader range of applications and industries.
- **Addressing Fundamental Limitations of Vision-Only Systems:** CARE offers a method to inject geometric reasoning and reactive planning into appearance-based visual navigation models that inherently lack explicit geometric understanding. This addresses a fundamental limitation, allowing these models to maintain their strengths in generalization while mitigating their weaknesses in safety-critical situations.

In summary, CARE represents a practical and impactful solution that enhances the safety and deployability of learning-based visual navigation systems, helping to bridge the gap between impressive research advancements and the demands of reliable real-world robotic operations.
