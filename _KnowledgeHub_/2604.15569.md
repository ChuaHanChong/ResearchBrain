# ShapeGen: Robotic Data Generation for Category-Level Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ShapeGen is a simulator-free 3D data-generation pipeline that augments robot demonstrations with in-category shape variation. It trains neural spatial warpings supervised by signed distance fields to build plug-and-play Shape Libraries of functionally corresponding points, then substitutes novel 3D objects into a source demo with function-aware alignment and gripper-action correction, raising real-world category-level manipulation success from 1/20 to 10/20 on hard mug hanging.

## Problem

- Category-level manipulation needs shape-diverse demonstrations, but manual collection demands intense human labor and large physical collections of geometrically divergent everyday objects.
- Replay-based augmentation like RoboSplat and ReBot ignores object functionality and affordance, restricting them to simple pick-and-place rather than fine-grained functional skills.
- Keypoint-constraint generation is limited to axis-aligned scaling and runs only inside simulators, missing non-linear real-world deformation and incurring a reality gap.

## Method

- ==Spatial warping== networks map points between same-category shapes, supervised by ==signed-distance-function== equality plus point-wise and point-pair rigidity regularizers, yielding dense function-aware correspondence from geometry alone.
- **Shape Library** curation trains warpings from one template to all shapes, so adding a new object costs one warping; composite warping plus ==SDF-gradient line-search== refinement gives any-pair correspondence.
- **Function-Aware Generation** solves per-keypoint ==SE(3)== alignment from one-minute annotation, interpolates object pose trajectories, applies translation-only ==gripper action correction==, and composites observations in 3D via ==segmentation== and ==tracking==.

## Results

- On unseen mug instances, hanging success rose from **1/20** to **9/20** and hard hanging from **1/20** to **10/20**; serve kettle rose **7/20** to **15/20**, pour water **11/20** to **12/20**.
- Realism check: replaying source trajectories on a novel scanned mug failed, while replaying ShapeGen-generated trajectories succeeded and policies trained purely on generated data learned the task.
- Generation runs at **15 fps**, about **20 seconds** per episode versus **1 minute** of manual teleoperation; warping training takes **3 minutes** per pair and neural SDF fitting **5 seconds**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Purely geometric SDF supervision suffices to recover functional part correspondence across shapes, so semantic labels or vision-foundation features are not prerequisites for affordance transfer.
> - Globally regularized dense warping beats naive feature matching on textureless regions like mug rims, where per-point descriptor matching lacks the constraint to stay precise.
> - Template-anchored libraries make augmentation scale linearly in new objects, but the approach assumes rigid bodies and breaks for deformable or articulated categories.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of visuomotor robotic policies has progressed, enabling deployment in real-world manipulation scenarios. A significant challenge in these environments is the geometric diversity of everyday objects within a single category. For robust performance, policies must operate in a "category-level" manner, adapting to various shapes of an object type rather than being confined to specific instances seen during training. This in-category generalizability typically necessitates training data exhibiting substantial shape diversity. However, manually acquiring such a dataset is often impractical due to the intensive human labor and extensive collections of diverse physical objects required.

In response to this data collection bottleneck, robotic data generation has emerged as a practical solution. Existing research in this area has explored augmenting various aspects of robotic data, including visual textural appearance, spatial configuration, viewpoint, and embodiment, through techniques such as 3D reconstruction, video inpainting, and world models. However, the augmentation of *in-category shape diversity* has remained comparatively underexplored for complex manipulation tasks. Prior methods like RoboSplat and ReBot address shape augmentation by replaying trajectories on novel objects, but these are generally limited to simpler pick-and-place tasks because they do not process object functionality or affordance information. CP-Gen represents a step towards finer-grained manipulation by modeling skills as keypoint-trajectory constraints in simulation. However, CP-Gen's shape variation capabilities are restricted to axis-aligned scaling, which limits the geometric diversity compared to the complex, non-linear deformations encountered in real-world settings, and it operates exclusively within a simulated environment.

Furthermore, the broader literature on inter-object correspondence has demonstrated its utility in robotic manipulation, particularly for transferring object affordances or generating optimal trajectories. Many existing approaches focus on discrete correspondences, which can suffer from inaccuracies and often require subsequent refinement. Dense correspondence methods, such as DenseMatcher, aim to map vertices between meshes, but their formulation can have limitations. Works built on Neural Descriptor Fields (NDFs) or category-level geometric representations have shown that semantic-aware dense correspondence can be established on purely geometric foundations.

ShapeGen positions itself within this landscape by specifically addressing the need for a simulator-free, 3D data generation method that supports complex, non-linear shape variations for fine-grained manipulation skills in real-world scenarios. It aims to provide dense, function-aware inter-object correspondence purely from geometric clues, building on and extending the capabilities of prior work in data generation and object correspondence for robust category-level robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a data generation method that enhances the in-category shape generalizability of robotic manipulation policies. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations and specific aims:

1. **Address the Data Collection Bottleneck:** The central motivation stems from the difficulty and impracticality of manually collecting shape-diversified training data for category-level manipulation. Real-world scenarios expose robots to significant geometric variations of everyday objects (e.g., different shapes of mugs, kettles). Training robust policies capable of interacting with *any* object in a category requires exposure to this diversity, but manually gathering data for a large number of distinct object shapes is resource-intensive, demanding extensive human labor and a vast collection of physical objects. ShapeGen aims to reduce this human effort and the need for physical assets.

2. **Enable Category-Level Skills for Real-World Deployment:** The authors aim for policies that can generalize to previously unseen object shapes within the same category. This implies moving beyond policies trained for specific object instances to those that implicitly understand the functional properties and affordances common to an entire category. For instance, a policy for "pouring water" should function correctly with any kettle and any mug, irrespective of their specific dimensions or contours.

3. **Support Complex Shape Variations:** Current methods often have limited capabilities regarding the complexity of shape variations they can introduce (e.g., axis-aligned scaling in simulation). A key objective for ShapeGen is to generate data with complex, non-linear shape deformations that accurately reflect the diversity found in real-world objects, thereby fostering stronger generalizability in policies.

4. **Operate in a Simulator-Free and 3D Manner:** Many existing data generation techniques rely heavily on simulation environments, which can suffer from reality gaps. ShapeGen is motivated by the need for a method that is simulator-free, generating data directly applicable to real-world observations and actions, and operates directly on 3D object representations to capture geometric specifics accurately.

5. **Facilitate Fine-Grained Manipulation Tasks:** Unlike methods restricted to simple pick-and-place operations, ShapeGen aims to support the generation of data for finer-grained manipulation skills (e.g., hanging a mug, pouring water). Such tasks require precise interaction with specific functional parts of an object (e.g., a mug handle, a kettle spout), which in turn necessitates understanding of object functionality and affordances.

6. **Develop Function-Aware Correspondence:** The underlying principle guiding ShapeGen is the observation that fine-grained skills rely on correctly exploiting an object's functional parts, and physically plausible data must conform to an object's affordances (how it is placed, gripped, etc.). Both requirements suggest the need to place novel shapes in the "right" functional position, which can be achieved by finding optimal alignment between novel shapes and the real objects in a demonstration. This alignment problem is further reduced to establishing dense, *function-aware* point correspondence between shapes, a core challenge that ShapeGen aims to solve through geometric cues.

In summary, ShapeGen is motivated by the practical limitations of data collection for category-level robotics and aims to provide a scalable, realistic, and functionally-aware data generation framework to overcome these challenges, enabling more generalizable manipulation policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ShapeGen proposes a two-stage framework for robotic data generation: "Shape Library Curation" and "Function-Aware Generation." The system captures raw observations using a calibrated RGB-D camera and trains policies on uncolored pointcloud observations.

#### 3.1. Shape Library Curation

This stage focuses on creating a mechanism to automatically find functionally corresponding points across different 3D shapes of the same category.

##### 3.1.1. Spatial Warping Training

The core of this stage is to establish dense, function-aware correspondence between two 3D shapes, $S_i$ and $S_j$, within the same category. This is achieved through a spatial warping function $W_{ij}: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3$, which maps points from one shape's space to another. The authors hypothesize that functional correspondence across shapes is derived from geometric correspondence.
- **Neural Network-based Warping:** Neural networks are used to fit these complex warping functions.
- **Geometric Supervision (SDF):** Signed Distance Function (SDF) is adopted as the supervising signal. The objective is that for any point $x_i$ near the surface of $S_i$, the warped point $W_{ij}(x_i)$ on $S_j$ should maintain the same SDF value, i.e., $SDF(x_i, S_i) = SDF(W_{ij}(x_i), S_j)$. This ensures that surface points map to surface points and preserves relative spatial relationships. A differentiable neural SDF model, $f_{sdf_j}$, is first fitted for $S_j$, then $W_{ij}$ is trained to minimize the primary loss: $L_{sdf} = \|f_{sdf_i}(x_i, S_i) - f_{sdf_j}(W_{ij}(x_i), S_j)\|_1$.
- **Regularization:** To prevent overly deformed warpings, two regularization losses are added:
 - **Point-wise regularization ($L_{pw}$):** Penalizes large displacements of points.
 - **Point-pair regularization ($L_{pp}$):** Encourages local rigidity, preventing arbitrary stretching or shrinking between nearby points.
- **Total Loss:** The final training loss is a weighted sum of $L_{sdf}$, $L_{pw}$, and $L_{pp}$.
- **Implementation:** The warping networks use an LSTM architecture with an additional residual connection. Training inputs are sampled on a 256x256x256 grid, focusing on points near the surface.

##### 3.1.2. Shape Library Construction and Usage

- **Template-based Aggregation:** For a given object category, a template shape $S_0$ is chosen. Spatial warpings $W_{0j}$ are trained from this template to all other shapes $S_j$ in the collection.
- **Plug-and-Play Scalability:** This template-based approach allows for efficient extension of the library. To integrate a new shape, $S_a$, into the library, only one additional warping $W_{a0}$ (from $S_a$ to $S_0$) needs to be trained. This process is stated to be O(1) in terms of adding new shapes to the pre-existing library.
- **Composite Warping with Refinement:** In the data generation phase, if correspondence between $S_a$ (the actual object in the source demo) and any library shape $S_j$ is needed, it is obtained by composing warpings: $W_{aj} = W_{0j} \circ W_{a0}$. An extra refinement step, `refine(x, S)`, is applied to the output of each warping to find the nearest point on the target surface, mitigating errors from imperfect warpings. This refinement uses a line search method along the SDF gradient.

#### 3.2. Function-Aware Generation

Building upon the curated Shape Libraries, this stage outlines the pipeline for generating novel manipulation data by substituting objects in a source demonstration.

##### 3.2.1. Minimal Human Annotation

For each source demonstration, a human annotator provides minimal input:
- **Task-relevant Keypoints:** An ordered set of 3D points ($P_{t_i}$) fixed to the task-relevant objects at specific timestamps ($t_i$). These keypoints guide the alignment of new shapes.
- **Alignment Cost Function:** A function $G_{t_i}$ (e.g., simple square error) that defines how the keypoint positions should be preserved or aligned.
- **Gripper Keypoint:** A point $x_g$ representing the gripped part of the object.
This annotation process is required only once per source demo and typically takes about one minute.

##### 3.2.2. Function-Aware Alignment

- **Optimal Alignment Calculation:** For each timestamp $t_i$ with annotations, an optimal alignment transformation $\tilde{T}_{t_i} \in SE(3)$ is calculated. This transformation minimizes the cost function $G_{t_i}$ between the original keypoints $P_{t_i}$ and their warped counterparts $W_{aj}[P_{t_i}]$ applied by $\tilde{T}_{t_i}$. In cases where the solution is not unique (e.g., single keypoint and square error), the rotation component of $\tilde{T}_{t_i}$ is constrained to be an identity.
- **Trajectory Interpolation:** The calculated $\tilde{T}_{t_i}$ transformations are interpolated across the entire demonstration timeline to generate a continuous alignment sequence $(\tilde{T}_0..., \tilde{T}_{L-1})$.
- **Novel Object Pose Trajectory:** The desired pose trajectory $T_j$ for the substituted object $S_j$ is then computed by composing the original object pose trajectory $T_a$ with the alignment transformations: $T_j = (T_0 \tilde{T}_0..., T_{L-1} \tilde{T}_{L-1})$. For tasks requiring alignment to a secondary variable object (e.g., a mug for pouring), an additional alignment sequence $\hat{T}_t$ in camera space is computed and applied.

##### 3.2.3. Gripper Action Correction

- **Necessity:** Variations in object size and gripped part position between $S_a$ and $S_j$ necessitate modifying the robot's gripper action to maintain physical plausibility.
- **Method:** A sequence of translation-only transformations $T_g = (T_{g_0}..., T_{g_{L-1}})$ is calculated. This sequence best aligns the original gripper keypoint $x_g$ with its warped counterpart $W_{aj}(x_g)$ in camera space.
- **Modified Action:** The original gripper action $a_t$ (an SE(3) transformation from end-effector to robot base frame) is then modified to $a'_t = T_{c2b} T_{g_t} T^{-1}_{c2b} a_t$, where $T_{c2b}$ is the camera-to-base frame transformation. The translation-only nature of $T_g$ simplifies this calculation, relies only on the rotation component of $T_{c2b}$, and helps preserve visual fidelity by avoiding rendering the arm from new angles.

##### 3.2.4. Novel Observation Generation

- **3D Scene Composition:** After determining $T_j$ and $T_g$, the new observation is composited in 3D space. Video segmentation (using SAM2) is used to obtain masks for task-relevant objects. A background RGB-D image of the empty workspace is captured.
- **Element Transformation:** The segmented arm pointcloud is transformed using $T_g$. The 3D model of $S_j$ is placed using $T_j$.
- **Output:** The final composited scene pointcloud is sampled (using a camera-aware 3D post-processing method), forming the observation for pointcloud-based policies. The system can also generate rendered images for 2D policies.

#### 3.3. Implementation Details

- **Hardware:** UR5 robotic arm, parallel jaw gripper, ORBBEC Femto Bot RGB-D camera.
- **Software/Libraries:** Instant-NGP for neural SDF, SAM3D for 3D model generation from images, RealityComposer for scanning 3D models, SAM2 for video segmentation, FoundationPose for object tracking, ManiFlow for policy architecture.
- **Computational Resources:** A single RTX A6000 GPU for data generation and network training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted aimed to validate ShapeGen's effectiveness in boosting policy generalizability, realism of generated data, efficiency, and the quality of its correspondence method.

#### 4.1. Shape Generalizability of Policy

- **Experimental Setup:** Four manipulation tasks (hang_mug, hang_mug_hard, serve_kettle, pour_water) were tested, involving mug and kettle categories. Five source demonstrations were collected per task, using the same object instance but different spatial configurations. ShapeGen generated 15 novel demonstrations from each source demo, using 5 distinct 3D models at 3 different scales. ManiFlow policies were trained on (1) source demos only ("source") and (2) source + ShapeGen-augmented data ("+ShapeGen").
- **Evaluation:** Policies were tested on novel object instances not seen in either source or generated data, placed at approximately the same initial spatial configurations as in source demos to isolate shape generalizability.
- **Results:**
 - ShapeGen significantly enhanced the generalizability of policies, particularly for the mug-hanging tasks, which require precise functional alignment (e.g., 1/20 success rate for "source" vs. 9/20 for "+ShapeGen" in "hang mug"; 1/20 vs. 10/20 in "hang mug hard").
 - Performance gains were also observed for kettle-related tasks (7/20 vs. 15/20 for "serve kettle"; 11/20 vs. 12/20 for "pour water").
 - **Failure Analysis:** Policies trained solely on source data frequently failed due to imprecise exploitation of object functions (e.g., collision during mug insertion, misaligned kettle spout) and out-of-distribution effects (e.g., dropping the kettle). ShapeGen-augmented data helped policies better exploit the functions of unseen objects and reduced out-of-distribution failures by enriching the training corpus.

#### 4.2. Realism of Generated Data

- **Experimental Setup:** ShapeGen was used to generate demonstrations with a specific test object (mug_white) model from the Shape Library. Policies were trained on these generated data (policy-ShapeGen) and compared to policies trained on source demos only (policy-source). Additionally, direct replays of collected (replay-source) and generated (replay-ShapeGen) action trajectories were performed on the scanned test object.
- **Results:**
 - While both the source trajectory replay and the source-trained policy failed, ShapeGen-generated trajectories successfully completed the task and enabled policies to learn successfully from them.
 - This indicates that ShapeGen produces functionally correct trajectories and that the generated observations are sufficiently realistic, placing real-world observations during execution within the distribution learned by the policies.

#### 4.3. Efficiency Analysis

- **Runtime Breakdown:** The typical time requirements for ShapeGen components were analyzed:
 - Neural SDF training: 5 seconds
 - Warping network training (per pair): 3 minutes
 - Object scanning ($S_a$): 5 minutes
 - Training $W_{a0}$ (for a new object $S_a$): 3 minutes
 - Collecting a source demo: 1 minute
 - Annotating a source demo: 1 minute
 - Generating data (per episode): 20 seconds (at 15 fps for episodes < 300 frames)
- **Comparison to Manual Collection:** ShapeGen generates data at approximately 15 frames per second, meaning an episode takes about 20 seconds. In contrast, manual data collection takes approximately 1 minute per episode. This makes ShapeGen considerably more time-efficient for generating large volumes of data, especially as the number of shape-varied demonstrations increases. The dominant factor for ShapeGen's scalability is the $T_{gen}$ (20s/episode) coefficient when generating many novel demonstrations.

#### 4.4. Comparison with Feature Matching

- **Methodology:** A qualitative comparison was made between ShapeGen's spatial warping network and a naive feature-matching method based on DINOv3 for extracting single-point correspondences.
- **Results:** The feature-matching method, lacking global regularization, was prone to imprecise single-point matches. In contrast, ShapeGen's spatial warping, by considering both local and global geometry through SDF supervision, produced higher-quality, function-aware single-point correspondences, particularly in "plain" regions like mug rims and lower kettle parts.

#### 4.5. Visualizations

- **Shape Libraries:** Visualizations confirmed that the trained spatial warping networks, supervised solely by geometric information, could satisfactorily establish function-aware point correspondence across diverse shapes (e.g., mugs, kettles, hammers, scissors). Points mapped from the same template point maintain their functional location on different object instances.
- **Generated Data:** Rendered pointcloud images of generated observations demonstrated that novel object shapes were correctly placed at the start, manipulated appropriately by the gripper to fulfill their functionalities during execution, and the gripper's position was translated accordingly for correct grasping and manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ShapeGen presents a notable contribution to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing a fundamental challenge: the acquisition of diverse training data for category-level skills. The significance and potential impact of this work can be summarized as follows:

1. **Overcoming the Data Bottleneck for Category-Level Robotics:** The primary impact is providing a practical, scalable solution to the resource-intensive problem of collecting shape-diverse data for robust robot learning. By automating the generation of varied manipulation demonstrations from minimal human input, ShapeGen can significantly reduce the human labor and extensive physical object collections typically required. This lowers the barrier for developing and deploying generalizable manipulation policies in real-world settings.

2. **Enhanced Policy Generalizability:** The experimental results demonstrate that policies trained with ShapeGen-augmented data exhibit improved in-category shape generalizability. This means robots can interact effectively with novel objects within the same category, a crucial capability for operating in unstructured, dynamic environments where exact object instances are often unknown or varied. This is particularly impactful for tasks requiring precise functional interactions, where ShapeGen's method of ensuring functional correctness proves beneficial.

3. **Novel Approach to Dense Function-Aware Correspondence:** The development of "Shape Libraries" based on neural-based dense, function-aware geometric correspondences is a key technical contribution. By using SDFs and geometric regularization to train spatial warpings, ShapeGen establishes a robust method for understanding and mapping functional parts across geometrically diverse objects without explicit semantic labels. This approach is demonstrably more reliable than feature-matching methods for establishing precise point correspondences.

4. **Simulator-Free and 3D Data Generation:** ShapeGen's design as a simulator-free, 3D data generation framework offers benefits in terms of realism and direct applicability to real-world systems. It circumvents the "reality gap" often associated with simulation-based data, providing physically plausible and functionally correct demonstrations that effectively generalize to real robot deployments. The 3D nature of the generation process also allows for direct integration with 3D-aware policies, such as those using pointcloud inputs.

5. **Efficiency and Scalability:** The analysis of system runtime highlights ShapeGen's efficiency. With a generation rate of approximately 15 frames per second, it can generate novel demonstrations much faster than manual data collection, enabling the creation of large-scale, diversified datasets from a limited number of source demonstrations. The O(1) time complexity for adding new shapes to the Shape Library further underscores its scalability.

**Potential Limitations and Future Directions (as identified by the authors):**

- **Scope of Experimentation:** While effective, the full potential of ShapeGen for highly intricate tasks that demand a deeper understanding of object functional parts has not been exhaustively demonstrated within the current experimental scope. Future work aims to explore such complex scenarios.
- **Deformable Objects:** The current framework does not support deformable objects. Handling complex dynamics of deformable objects would require integrating dynamics models or physics simulators, which is outside the scope of the current research.
- **Articulated Objects:** Extending ShapeGen to support articulated objects (e.g., scissors, pliers) would necessitate additional engineering efforts, such as adopting mesh reconstruction methods for articulation, training part-level Shape Libraries, and aggregating parts under articulation constraints. The authors plan to pursue this as a future extension.

Despite these limitations, ShapeGen establishes a foundation for automated, high-quality data generation that promises to accelerate the development of generalizable robotic manipulation skills, particularly for interacting with the geometric diversity of everyday objects in unstructured environments.
