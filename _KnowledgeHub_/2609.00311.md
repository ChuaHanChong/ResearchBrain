# Inverse kinematic solution for generic 3R positional robots using Conformal Geometric Algebra

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper solves the inverse kinematics of generic 3R positional robots with Conformal Geometric Algebra, recasting the problem as the intersection of a fixed circle with a circle rotated about the second joint axis. The intersection-reality condition yields a quartic univariate polynomial directly in theta-2, with no elimination step; theta-1 and theta-3 follow from directed arc angles. A worked 3R example recovers four solutions.

## Problem

- Pieper's univariate-polynomial formulation of 3R inverse kinematics breaks down in degenerate cases where the first link parameters a1 and alpha1 both vanish.
- Prior algebraic solutions require eliminating two of the three joint variables, an opaque manipulation offering no intuition about the robot's underlying kinematic model.
- Existing conic-intersection interpretations live purely in joint space, and earlier geometric-algebra 3R results contain errors that prevent generalization to generic robots.

## Method

- Formulates both loop-closure circles as ==Conformal Geometric Algebra== elements: each is the ==meet== of a dually represented sphere and a plane, with centers, radii and normals built from ==Denavit-Hartenberg motors==.
- Rotates the second circle by a ==rotor== about the ==bivector== encoding the second joint axis, so the meet of rotated and fixed circles becomes an explicit function of theta-2.
- Imposes the ==null condition== on the intersection point, giving a ==quartic polynomial== in the tangent half-angle of theta-2; theta-1 and theta-3 come from projected-line angles with bivector sign disambiguation.

## Results

- The worked generic 3R example with the given link parameters and target position produces a trigonometric intersection condition whose solution yields **four** distinct theta-2 values.
- Back-substitution recovers **four** complete joint triples, matching the known maximum of **four** inverse kinematic solutions for a generic positional 3R chain.
- Intersection points share the third-coordinate value of the target point, a consistency check confirming that all intersections lie in the fixed circle's plane.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Choosing an algebra where circles and spheres are first-class objects can eliminate variable-elimination entirely, turning an algebraic manipulation into a direct geometric intersection.
> - The workspace-level picture of a rotating circle sweeping a fixed one explains solution count and distribution in a way joint-space conic plots cannot.
> - Sign recovery still requires care: arc-cosine angles are ambiguous, so orientation bivectors must be computed separately to fix each joint angle's sign.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics extensively utilizes kinematics, with the inverse kinematics problem (IKP) being a fundamental challenge. The IKP involves determining the joint angles of a robot manipulator required to achieve a desired end-effector position and/or orientation. For 3R (three revolute joint) positional robots, this problem has a long history of investigation, primarily through algebraic methods.

Donald Pieper's work in 1968 is frequently cited as an early contribution, where he derived a univariate polynomial for one joint angle and subsequently obtained others through back-propagation. However, Pieper's method is known to have degeneracy conditions, failing for specific configurations such as `a_1 = 0` and `α_1 = 0`. Later, Jonathan Selig presented a more compact solution utilizing Lie algebra, which indicated that the IKP could be reduced to finding the intersection of a conic and a circle in a `cos(θ_1)-sin(θ_1)` plane. Similar geometric interpretations have been applied to Pieper's approach in the `cos(θ_3)-sin(θ_3)` plane to analyze the number of inverse kinematic solutions and cuspidal properties of robots. While these approaches offer geometric insights, they typically involve geometric interpretation of polynomials derived after algebraic elimination of variables, which may not provide intuitive understanding of the robot's kinematic model in the workspace.

Conformal Geometric Algebra (CGA) has emerged as an alternative mathematical framework for geometric computing in robotics and computer graphics. CGA provides a unified representation for geometric entities such as points, lines, planes, circles, and spheres, and operations like rotations and translations can be expressed as single transformations (motors or versors). This unified framework can simplify the representation and manipulation of geometric elements. In the context of inverse kinematics, CGA has been applied to specific types of serial robots, often non-generic structures like anthropomorphic manipulators. However, the authors note that some claims regarding 3R serial chains in previous CGA literature could not be generalized to generic 3R robots due to specific formulation issues.

This paper positions itself within this landscape by seeking to provide a CGA-based solution for the IKM of *generic* 3R robots. It aims to leverage CGA's advantages to offer a direct geometric interpretation in the workspace, addressing the limitations of prior algebraic methods and the lack of direct kinematic intuition in earlier geometric approaches. The goal is to provide an elimination-free method that yields a univariate polynomial for one joint angle and overcomes degeneracy conditions, thereby unifying the inverse kinematic model for generic 3R robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and present a novel approach for solving the Inverse Kinematic Model (IKM) of a generic 3R positional robot using Conformal Geometric Algebra (CGA).

The motivation behind pursuing this objective is multifaceted:

- **Addressing Limitations of Traditional Methods:** Existing algebraic approaches for 3R robot inverse kinematics, such as Pieper's method, are known to have specific degeneracy conditions where the solution breaks down (e.g., when `a_1 = 0` and `α_1 = 0`). A key motivation is to develop a method that does not suffer from such degeneracies, thus providing a universally applicable and unified IKM for generic 3R robots.
- **Enhancing Geometric Intuition:** Previous geometric interpretations of inverse kinematics solutions, often based on the intersection of conics in joint-space planes, typically arise *after* algebraic elimination of variables. These interpretations, while valid, may not provide intuitive insights into the kinematic behavior of the robot in its physical workspace. The authors are motivated to use CGA to offer a more direct and intuitive geometric understanding of the IKM by representing kinematic elements (like joint axes and end-effector positions) and their transformations as fundamental geometric objects within the algebra.
- **Leveraging Conformal Geometric Algebra's Advantages:** CGA is highlighted for two specific advantages pertinent to kinematic analysis:
 1. **Unification of Transformations:** It allows for rotations and translations to be treated as unified orthogonal transformations (versors or motors), simplifying the mathematical representation of robot motion.
 2. **Fundamental Geometric Elements:** Circles and spheres are fundamental elements within CGA, meaning they can be directly manipulated and transformed in the same way as points, lines, and planes. This capability is central to the proposed method.
- **Elimination-Free Solution Approach:** Many inverse kinematic solutions require algebraic elimination of variables (e.g., `θ_1` and `θ_3`) to arrive at a univariate polynomial for one joint angle (`θ_2`). This process can be algebraically intensive and obscure the underlying geometry. The authors are motivated to develop an approach that directly yields a univariate polynomial in `θ_2` without the need for such explicit eliminations, thereby simplifying the derivation and enhancing clarity.
- **Providing Deeper Kinematic Insights:** By reframing the problem in CGA, the authors aim to gain a deeper understanding of how inverse kinematic solutions are represented in both the workspace (where the robot operates) and joint-space (the configuration of its joints), including insights into the number of possible solutions and their distribution.
- **Correction of Prior Work:** The authors explicitly mention that previous claims made in some CGA literature regarding 3R serial chains (specifically reference) cannot be generalized to generic 3R robots due to identified inaccuracies in their formulation. This implies a motivation to present a correct and robust CGA framework for generic 3R robots.

In summary, the research is motivated by the desire to provide a more general, geometrically intuitive, and computationally straightforward method for solving the inverse kinematics of generic 3R robots, building upon and improving previous work in both traditional and geometric algebra domains.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research leverages Conformal Geometric Algebra (CGA) to reformulate and solve the inverse kinematics problem for a generic 3R positional robot. The approach centers on interpreting the kinematic equations as the intersection of geometric primitives represented within the CGA framework.

#### 3.1. Conformal Geometric Algebra Fundamentals

The paper begins by outlining the core concepts of CGA, specifically the 5-dimensional conformal geometric algebra `G_4,1`.
- **Basis Vectors:** It uses orthogonal unit vectors (`e_1, ..., e_4` with `e_i^2 = +1` and `e_5` with `e_5^2 = -1`).
- **Geometric Product:** Defines `ab = a · b + a ∧ b` for vectors `a` and `b`, yielding scalar (inner product) and bivector (outer product) components.
- **Null Vectors:** Introduces `e_0` (origin) and `e_∞` (infinity) null vectors, derived from `e_4` and `e_5`, such that `e_0^2 = e_∞^2 = 0` and `e_∞ · e_0 = -1`.
- **Pseudoscalar and Duals:** Defines the conformal pseudoscalar `I = e_0123∞` and the dual of a multivector `A` as `A* = A I^-1`.
- **Point Embedding:** An Euclidean point `x ∈ R^3` is embedded into CGA as a null vector `x = up(x) = e_0 + x + (1/2)x^2 e_∞`.
- **Geometric Primitives:** Table 1 in the paper lists the direct (OPNS) and dual (IPNS) representations for various 3D CGA primitives, including points, point pairs, spheres, planes, lines, and circles. Notably, a circle `C` can be represented as the intersection of two spheres (`S_1 ∨ S_2`) or a sphere and a plane (`S_1 ∨ E_1`).

#### 3.2. Kinematic Transformations in CGA

- **Translation and Rotation:** CGA defines versors for translation `R_∞(r, n)` and rotation `R(φ, B)`.
- **Motors:** Denavit-Hartenberg (D-H) parameters (`a, d, α`) are used to define transformations between robot frames using motors `M(θ)` (for joint rotation) and `G(a, d, α)` (for link transformation), expressed as combinations of translation and rotation versors.
- **Angle Calculation:** The angle between two lines `L_1` and `L_2` in CGA can be computed using `cos(θ) = (L_1 · L_2) / (√(L_1 · L_1)√(L_2 · L_2))`.

#### 3.3. Inverse Kinematic Model (IKM) Formulation

The research adopts Selig's inverse kinematics approach for 3R robots, which formulates the problem in terms of two rotating vectors, `a_1` and `b_1` (equation 11):
`e^(θ_2 S_2) (a_1) = (b_1)`
where `(a_1) = e^(θ_3 S_3) (p_H)` and `(b_1) = e^(-θ_1 S_1) (p)`. Here, `p_H` is the end-effector position in its 'Home' configuration, and `p` is the target end-effector position.

#### 3.4. Geometric Reframing in CGA: Intersection of Circles

The central innovative step is to geometrically interpret `a_1` and `b_1` as points on circles within the CGA framework. The problem then reduces to finding the intersection of a fixed circle (`C_B`) and a rotating circle (`C_A`).

- **Fixed Circle `C_B`:**
 - This circle is associated with `b_1 = e^(-θ_1 S_1) (p)`. Its center lies on the z-axis (first joint axis).
 - `C_B` is constructed as the intersection of a sphere `S_B` and a plane `E_B`.
 - `S_B` has its center at the origin `p_0 = up(p_0)` and a radius `r_b = ||p||`.
 - `E_B` is a plane whose normal `n_B` is `e_3` (representing the z-axis) and its distance from the origin `d_B` is the z-component of the target point `p`.

- **Rotating Circle `C_A`:**
 - This circle is associated with `a_1 = e^(θ_3 S_3) (p_H)`. Its center is `p_2` (the point where the second and third links connect in the home position).
 - `C_A` is constructed as the intersection of a sphere `S_A` and a plane `E_A`.
 - `S_A` has its center at `p_2 = up(p_2)` and a radius `r_A = √(a_3^2 + d_3^2)` (based on D-H parameters of the third link).
 - `E_A` is a plane whose normal `n_A` represents the third joint axis in the home position. `n_A` is obtained by transforming `e_3` using motors derived from the D-H parameters of the first two links (`T_3 = M(0)G(α_1, a_1, d_1)M(0)G(α_2, a_2, d_2)`). The distance `d_A` is `n_A · p_0`.

#### 3.5. Finding `θ_2`

The core equation `e^(θ_2 S_2) (a_1) = (b_1)` implies that `C_A` is rotated about the second joint axis by `θ_2` to meet `C_B`.
- The rotation is represented by a rotor `R_2 = exp(-B_j_2 θ_2 / 2)`, where `B_j_2` is the bivector representing the plane of rotation perpendicular to the second joint axis, derived by transforming `e_12` using the motor `T_2 = M(0)G(α_1, a_1, d_1)`.
- The rotated circle is `C_A_θ_2 = R_2 C_A R_2^-1`.
- The intersection point `x` of `C_A_θ_2` and `C_B` is given by `x = C_A_θ_2 ∨ C_B` (regressive product).
- For `x` to be a real point, the condition `x · x = 0` must be satisfied. This condition, when expanded, directly yields a univariate quartic polynomial in `t_2 = tan(θ_2/2)`, without the need for algebraic elimination of `θ_1` or `θ_3`.

#### 3.6. Calculating `θ_1` and `θ_3`

Once `θ_2` values are obtained from the polynomial, the corresponding `θ_1` and `θ_3` values are determined:
- **`θ_1` Calculation:** For a specific `θ_2` and the resulting intersection point `x` on `C_B`, `θ_1` is the angle between two lines: `L_11 = p_0 ∧ p ∧ e_∞` (from origin to target point) and `L_12 = p_0 ∧ x ∧ e_∞` (from origin to intersection point). These lines are projected onto `E_B`, and their angle is found using CGA's angle formula (Eq. 9). The sign of `θ_1` is determined by the sign of the bivector `B_1 = -v_L11 ∧ v_L12` (where `v` denotes the vectorized lines).
- **`θ_3` Calculation:** Similarly, the intersection point `x` is rotated back to the original `C_A` by `R_2^-1` to obtain `x_-θ_2`. `θ_3` is the angle between `L_31 = p_2 ∧ p_H ∧ e_∞` (from `p_2` to home position end-effector) and `L_32 = p_2 ∧ x_-θ_2 ∧ e_∞` (from `p_2` to the back-rotated intersection point). These lines are projected onto `E_A`, and their angle is found. The sign of `θ_3` is determined by the bivector `B_3 = v_L31 ∧ v_L32`.

#### 3.7. Example

The paper provides a numerical example using specific D-H parameters and a target end-effector position. It demonstrates the calculation of `C_B`, `C_A`, `B_j_2`, and the resulting `x · x = 0` equation, which leads to a quartic polynomial in `θ_2`. Four solutions for `θ_2` are presented, and for one of them, the subsequent calculations for `θ_1` and `θ_3` are detailed, illustrating the complete solution process.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents a novel and unified approach for solving the inverse kinematics problem (IKP) of generic 3R positional robots using Conformal Geometric Algebra (CGA). The main findings and results are summarized as follows:

- **Geometric Reframing of IKP:** The study successfully reformulates the inverse kinematics problem for a generic 3R robot as the geometric intersection of two circles in the CGA framework. Specifically, the problem is recast as finding the intersection points between a *fixed circle* (`C_B`) and a *rotating circle* (`C_A_θ_2`). This reinterpretation provides a direct geometric understanding of the kinematic model in the workspace.
- **Direct Derivation of Univariate Polynomial:** By expressing the intersection condition of the two circles in CGA (`x · x = 0`), the authors directly derive a univariate quartic polynomial in `t_2 = tan(θ_2/2)`. This is a significant result as it eliminates the traditional requirement for algebraic elimination of `θ_1` and `θ_3` to isolate a single joint variable, streamlining the solution process.
- **Elimination of Degeneracy Conditions:** The proposed CGA-based method is demonstrated to be free from the degeneracy conditions that affect earlier algebraic solutions, such as Pieper's method. This universality makes the approach applicable to *generic* 3R robots without specific exclusions based on D-H parameters (`a_1 = 0` or `α_1 = 0`).
- **Comprehensive Solution for All Joint Angles:** After solving the quartic polynomial for `θ_2` (which typically yields up to four real solutions for a 3R positional robot), the corresponding `θ_1` and `θ_3` values are systematically calculated. This is achieved by determining the angles between specific lines, constructed from relevant points and projected onto appropriate planes, using CGA's inner product for angle computation and bivector signs for direction.
- **Geometric Intuition and Solution Visualization:** The framework provides an intuitive geometric understanding of the inverse kinematic solutions. The example provided illustrates how four inverse kinematic solutions correspond to the four possible intersection points of the fixed and rotated circles, offering a clear visualization of solution multiplicity directly in the workspace.
- **Validation through Example:** A detailed numerical example of a generic 3R robot with specified D-H parameters and a target end-effector position is presented. This example demonstrates the step-by-step application of the methodology, from defining the CGA elements for `C_A` and `C_B`, formulating the `x · x = 0` condition, solving for `θ_2`, and subsequently deriving `θ_1` and `θ_3` for each solution. The example confirms the method's ability to yield the complete set of inverse kinematic solutions.

In essence, the research successfully demonstrates that CGA can be effectively utilized to solve the IKM for generic 3R robots, providing an elegant, geometrically intuitive, and robust method that bypasses the limitations and complexities of some conventional algebraic approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research "Inverse kinematic solution for generic 3R positional robots using Conformal Geometric Algebra" contributes to the field of robotics and geometric computation with several significant implications and potential impacts:

- **Advancement in Robot Kinematics Theory:** The paper offers a new theoretical framework for understanding and solving the inverse kinematics problem for 3R robots. By reframing the problem as the intersection of geometric primitives (circles) within CGA, it provides an alternative perspective that complements and extends traditional algebraic and Lie algebra-based methods. This approach can enrich the theoretical foundation of robot kinematics.
- **Enhanced Geometric Intuition:** A primary motivation for using CGA is to provide deeper geometric intuition. By directly representing and manipulating geometric entities like points, lines, and circles in a unified algebra, the method allows engineers and researchers to visualize and understand the kinematic behavior of robots in a more intuitive manner, both in the robot's physical workspace and in joint-space. This can be particularly beneficial for conceptual understanding, design, and analysis of robot mechanisms.
- **Robustness and Generality:** The developed method overcomes the degeneracy conditions often encountered in classical inverse kinematic solutions for 3R robots (e.g., Pieper's solution). This means the CGA approach is applicable to *any* generic 3R robot configuration without special case handling, enhancing the robustness and universality of the solution. This is crucial for practical applications where robots may operate in diverse configurations.
- **Simplified Solution Derivation:** The direct derivation of a univariate quartic polynomial for `θ_2` without the need for algebraic elimination of other joint variables (`θ_1` and `θ_3`) represents a simplification in the solution process. While the underlying CGA computations can be complex, the high-level formulation is more compact and conceptually direct, potentially leading to more elegant and less error-prone implementations.
- **Foundation for Complex Robotic Systems:** The success of this CGA approach for 3R robots provides a strong foundation for future research into more complex robotic manipulators. The authors explicitly state that this work will be extended to study the IKM for generic 6R robots and their kinematic properties. A unified, geometric framework like CGA could be instrumental in tackling the increased complexity of higher-degree-of-freedom manipulators, particularly in handling both position and orientation.
- **Potential for New Computational Tools:** The methodology could inspire the development of new computational libraries and software tools for robotics that leverage CGA. Such tools could offer a more streamlined and geometrically intuitive way to program and control robots, potentially simplifying the development of advanced robotic applications.
- **Educational Impact:** CGA, with its powerful and intuitive representation of geometry, could serve as an effective pedagogical tool for teaching kinematics and other areas of robotics. Presenting concepts like inverse kinematics through the intersection of circles can make complex mathematical ideas more accessible to students and practitioners.

In conclusion, this research is significant for providing a robust, geometrically intuitive, and unified solution for the inverse kinematics of generic 3R robots using CGA. Its impact extends from advancing theoretical understanding and improving solution methodologies to laying the groundwork for more complex robotic systems and potentially influencing the development of next-generation robotic control and simulation tools.
