# End-to-End Humanoid Robot Safe and Comfortable Locomotion Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> An end-to-end framework enables humanoid robots to achieve safe and comfortable locomotion by integrating raw 3D LiDAR perception, Control Barrier Function (CBF)-based safety, and Human-Robot Interaction (HRI)-informed comfort rewards into a P3O reinforcement learning policy. This system demonstrated a 53% reduction in unsafe time and a 30% reduction in uncomfortable time compared to reward-shaping baselines, alongside successful deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid robot.

## Problem

- Existing reinforcement learning (RL) based humanoid locomotion policies are often "blind" or rely on limited 2D exteroceptive sensing, failing to perceive non-ground-level 3D obstacles and posing collision risks.
- Traditional safety methods in RL, like reward shaping, are unreliable and lack strong theoretical guarantees, leading to inconsistent or overly conservative robot behaviors.
- Locomotion policies typically overlook human comfort and social awareness, resulting in robot motions that can be perceived as unpredictable or threatening in shared environments.

## Method

- The framework processes raw 3D LiDAR point clouds directly for robust environmental perception, providing comprehensive 3D awareness for humanoid robots.
- A principled safety mechanism translates ==Control Barrier Function (CBF)== principles into instantaneous costs for a model-free Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) framework, trained with Penalized Proximal Policy Optimization (P3O).
- A composite reward function incorporates Human-Robot Interaction (HRI)-informed comfort rewards, including proxemic comfort, safe approach dynamics, and tangential avoidance, to encourage socially aware motions.

## Results

- The P3O-CBF policy reduced time spent in unsafe zones by **53%** and uncomfortable zones by **30%** compared to a PPO-RewardShaping baseline in static obstacle course simulations.
- It achieved significantly higher success rates in challenging scenarios, such as narrow passages (**60%** vs **33%** for P3O) and dynamic agent environments (**86%** vs **70%** for P3O).
- Successful sim-to-real transfer was demonstrated on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot, which safely navigated cluttered real-world environments and reacted to dynamic human interaction in real-time.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating raw 3D LiDAR perception directly into an end-to-end RL policy significantly enhances a humanoid robot's ability to navigate complex, cluttered environments beyond the capabilities of 2D vision.
> - Translating model-based safety guarantees from CBFs into cost functions within a model-free CMDP/P3O framework effectively enforces proactive safety constraints during learning, overcoming limitations of reactive reward shaping.
> - Explicitly incorporating HRI-informed comfort-oriented rewards leads to smoother, more predictable robot motions that maintain appropriate social distances, improving human-robot interaction and acceptance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses significant challenges in the field of humanoid robotics, particularly concerning their deployment in complex, unstructured, and human-centric environments. Current state-of-the-art research in legged locomotion frequently employs reinforcement learning (RL) to develop controllers. However, many of these controllers are "blind," relying solely on proprioceptive feedback, which limits their ability to navigate environments with obstacles.

To overcome this, some research has incorporated exteroceptive sensing, primarily using depth cameras to generate 2D height maps. While an improvement, these systems have limitations, including sensitivity to lighting conditions, limited field of view, and the inability to perceive non-ground-level obstacles (e.g., overhanging clutter, upper bodies of agents). This deficiency poses a substantial collision risk for full-body humanoid robots. The authors argue for the necessity of a lighting-invariant perception modality that provides direct 3D information, such as LiDAR, whose integration into end-to-end locomotion policies remains limited.

Furthermore, existing approaches to safety often rely on reward shaping (penalties for collisions), which can be inconsistent, difficult to tune, and may lead to either overly conservative or still unsafe behaviors. More structured methods, like Constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs) and Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), offer stronger theoretical safety guarantees but often require accurate system models, making their direct application in model-free RL challenging.

Beyond mere collision avoidance, the paper also integrates the concept of human comfort into robot motion planning. Research in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) indicates that a robot's motion must be predictable, fluid, and non-threatening to foster trust and acceptance. This higher-level, human-centric aspect is often overlooked in traditional locomotion policies.

This work contributes to the broader landscape by bridging these identified gaps. It proposes an integrated end-to-end framework that:
1. Utilizes raw 3D LiDAR point clouds for robust perception in complex environments.
2. Develops a principled safety framework within a CMDP, translating model-based CBF principles into costs for a model-free RL algorithm (P3O).
3. Introduces comfort-oriented rewards derived from HRI research to promote socially aware and non-intrusive motions.
4. Demonstrates successful sim-to-real transfer and deployment on a physical humanoid robot, showcasing its practical applicability.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable humanoid robots to navigate complex, human-centric environments safely, efficiently, and comfortably. This overarching goal is motivated by several critical limitations in existing approaches:

1. **Addressing Perception Limitations**: Current advanced RL-based legged locomotion policies are often "blind" to external environments, relying only on internal state feedback. When external perception is incorporated, it typically uses depth cameras or 2D height maps, which are susceptible to lighting conditions, have limited fields of view, and fail to detect non-ground-level 3D obstacles. This creates significant collision risks for humanoid robots. The motivation is to overcome these limitations by developing a policy that directly processes raw, spatio-temporal LiDAR point clouds, providing robust 3D environmental awareness suitable for cluttered and dynamic scenes.

2. **Ensuring Principled Safety**: Traditional methods of ensuring safety in RL, such as penalizing collisions via reward functions, are often unreliable, difficult to tune, and do not provide strong safety guarantees. This can lead to policies that are either overly conservative or still prone to unsafe behaviors. The motivation is to establish a more formal and provable safety framework. This is achieved by formulating the control problem as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) to formally separate safety constraints from task objectives. A key objective is to translate the principles of Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), which offer strong theoretical safety guarantees but typically require accurate system models, into costs that can be effectively utilized by a model-free RL algorithm, specifically Penalized Proximal Policy Optimization (P3O), during training.

3. **Promoting Human-Centric Comfort and Social Awareness**: For robots operating in proximity to humans, mere collision avoidance is insufficient. The robot’s movements must also be perceived as comfortable, meaning they should be smooth, predictable, and non-threatening, as indicated by Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) research. Existing locomotion policies rarely consider these higher-level, human-centric aspects. The motivation is to explicitly integrate comfort-oriented behaviors into the policy learning process. This involves introducing a set of comfort-oriented rewards, grounded in HRI principles, to encourage motions that maintain appropriate proxemic distances, approach obstacles tangentially, and exhibit overall smoothness, thereby fostering trust and acceptance.

4. **Demonstrating Real-World Viability**: A fundamental objective is to validate the developed framework through real-world deployment. The motivation here is to demonstrate the practical efficacy and robustness of the end-to-end policy, including its perception, safety mechanisms, and comfort-aware behaviors, through a successful sim-to-real transfer onto a physical humanoid robot operating in cluttered and dynamic real-world environments with both static and moving obstacles.

In summary, the authors aim to develop an integrated, end-to-end locomotion policy for humanoid robots that is perceptive in 3D, formally safe, and socially comfortable, thereby advancing their readiness for deployment in complex human environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on learning a Comfortable Policy (Π∗C) within a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) framework, integrating raw LiDAR perception, Control Barrier Function (CBF)-based safety, and Human-Robot Interaction (HRI)-inspired comfort rewards, all trained using the Penalized Proximal Policy Optimization (P3O) algorithm.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The learning task is formulated as a CMDP, defined by a tuple (S, A, P, R, {Cj}, {ϵj}, γRL). The objective is to maximize the expected discounted sum of rewards (JR(π)) while satisfying constraints on the expected discounted sum of costs (JCj(π) ≤ ϵj).
- **State Space Partition**: The state space S is partitioned into safe states (S_safe) and unsafe states (S_unsafe), where S_safe represents configurations without risk.
- **Interactive Comfortable Space (ICS)**: A subset of S_safe, denoted S_IC, is defined. This subspace represents states where the robot's actions are intended to avoid causing discomfort to proximate passive agents (e.g., humans). Its definition is based on proximity, velocity, and agent state.
- **Policy Definitions**: Policies are categorized as safe (P(s_t ∈ S_safe, ∀t ≥ 0 | s0, π) = 1), comfortable (safe and P(s_t ∈ S_IC, ∀t ≥ 0 | s0, π) = 1), uncomfortable but safe, or unsafe. The objective is to find an optimal comfortable policy π∗C that maximizes task performance subject to safety and comfort constraints.
- **System Dynamics**: The robot's dynamics are modeled in discrete time as x_k+1 = f(x_k, u_k), assuming a locally linear approximation.

#### 3.2. Network Inputs and Architecture

The system employs a neural network architecture with distinct inputs for the Actor (policy) and Critics (reward and safety).
- **Actor Inputs**:
 - **Proprioceptive and Command History**: History of 10 timesteps including joint positions, velocities, accelerations, previous action, base linear/angular velocities, gravity vector, base height, and user command (linear x/y, angular yaw velocity).
 - **Exteroceptive (LiDAR) Features**: A 64-dimensional feature vector extracted from raw LiDAR point clouds, capturing 3D environmental geometry.
- **Critic Inputs (Privileged Information)**: During training, critics receive all actor inputs plus privileged information available only in simulation, such as true distance and velocity of the nearest obstacle in 8 directions, whole-body link contact forces, and hard safety conditions (joint limits, minimum safe distance).
- **Network Architecture**: LiDAR feature history is processed by a Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU). Its output is concatenated with proprioceptive and command history, then fed into a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) for both actor and critic networks.

#### 3.3. Enforcing the Safe State Space via LDCBF Cost

To prevent the policy from entering the unsafe state space (S_unsafe), a minimum distance D_min from obstacles is enforced using a Linear Discrete-Time Control Barrier Function (LDCBF).
- **LDCBF Barrier Function**: h_D(s_k) = ηᵀ_k(p(s_k) - o_k) - D_min, where o_k is the closest obstacle point, η_k is the outward normal, and p(s_k) is the robot's position. Safety requires h_D(s_k) ≥ 0.
- **Constraint Transformation**: For a linear system, the one-step-ahead safety condition h_D(s_k+1) ≥ (1 - γ_CBF)h_D(s_k) can be expressed as a linear constraint on the control input u_k: G_D(s_k, u_k) ≥ 0. Since the framework is model-free, this is not used for direct action filtering. Instead, its violation is translated into an instantaneous cost function for the CMDP: C_D(s_k, u_k) = max{0, -G_D(s_k, u_k)}. This cost becomes positive only if the chosen action is predicted to violate the safety barrier.

#### 3.4. Learning Comfortable Policies via Rewards and Costs

The policy is trained using a composite reward function and explicit cost functions within the CMDP framework.
- **Reward Function (R_k)**: A weighted sum of task-oriented, auxiliary, and comfort-oriented components.
 - **Task-Oriented**: Velocity and yaw rate tracking.
 - **Auxiliary**: Penalties for Z velocity, link collisions, high joint torques, velocities, and accelerations, and for large changes in consecutive actions (action smoothing).
 - **Comfort-Oriented (HRI-informed)**:
 - **Proxemic Comfort**: Rewards maintaining an ideal social distance (d_social = 1.2m) from humans, based on proxemics studies.
 - **Safe Approach Dynamics**: Penalizes components of velocity and acceleration that are normal to the nearest obstacle surface (Safe Approach Velocity/Acceleration), discouraging direct and rapid approaches.
 - **Tangential Avoidance**: Rewards robot velocity vectors perpendicular to the direction of the nearest obstacle, promoting smoother, arcing avoidance maneuvers.
- **Cost Functions (C_j)**: These define hard safety boundaries (S_unsafe) and operational limits, handled as CMDP constraints.
 - **Safety Distance Violation**: A binary cost (1 if D_obs,k < d_safe = 0.8m) defining the primary S_unsafe.
 - **Joint Limit Violation**: Penalty for exceeding joint position limits.
 - **Self-Collision**: Penalty for inter-link collisions.

#### 3.5. Training with P3O

The Normalized Penalized Proximal Policy Optimization (P3O) algorithm is used to find an optimal policy maximizing rewards subject to cost constraints.
- **P3O Objective**: L_P3O(θ′) = L_CLIP,N_R(θ′) - Σ_j κ_j · max{0, L_VIOL,N_C_j(θ′)}.
 - L_CLIP,N_R is the standard PPO clipped objective with normalized reward advantages.
 - L_VIOL,N_C_j is the violation term for each cost, connecting instantaneous costs C_j to policy updates. It includes the clipped advantage estimate of the expected cumulative cost J_C_j(π_θ) and accounts for the cost threshold d_j.
- **κ_j**: A tunable hyperparameter weighting the penalty for each constraint.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the proposed end-to-end locomotion policy was demonstrated through experiments in both high-fidelity simulation and on a physical humanoid robot. The evaluation focused on robust navigation in complex 3D environments, principled safety enforcement, and the generation of comfortable, socially aware motions.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

- **Robot Platform**: Unitree G1 humanoid robot with a Livox Mid-360 LiDAR.
- **Simulation**: NVIDIA Isaac Sim and Genesis, utilizing domain randomization and a structured curriculum to facilitate sim-to-real transfer.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

A comparison was conducted between three policies:
1. **PPO-RewardShaping**: Safety achieved solely through negative reward penalties for obstacle proximity.
2. **P3O**: Utilizes P3O with explicit safety constraints (similar to P3O-CBF but without comfort-oriented rewards).
3. **P3O-CBF (Ours)**: The full proposed policy, with CBF-based costs and comfort-oriented rewards.

- **Qualitative Comparison (Fig. 3)**:
 - PPO-RewardShaping produced aggressive trajectories, often resulting in collisions or overly conservative behavior, indicating the difficulty of ensuring safety solely via reward shaping.
 - P3O showed improved performance due to explicit safety constraints but exhibited reactive paths close to obstacles, leading to less efficient and jerky maneuvers, as its cost function primarily reacted to imminent safety violations.
 - P3O-CBF generated smoother and safer paths, proactively initiating avoidance maneuvers with wider turns and larger safety margins due to its dynamics-aware, CBF-based cost function.

- **Quantitative Analysis (Table II)**: In a 6-meter static obstacle course over 10 test runs, the time spent in "Unsafe Space" (D_obs < 0.6m) and "Uncomfortable Space But Safe" (0.6m ≤ D_obs < 1.2m) was measured.
 - PPO-RewardShaping: 1.7 s in unsafe space, 3.4 s in uncomfortable space.
 - P3O: 1.2 s in unsafe space (30% reduction from PPO), 3.1 s in uncomfortable space.
 - P3O-CBF (Ours): 0.8 s in unsafe space (53% reduction from PPO), 2.2 s in uncomfortable space.
 The P3O-CBF method demonstrated superior performance in minimizing time in both unsafe and uncomfortable zones, indicating that comfort-oriented rewards successfully encouraged maintaining larger, more socially acceptable distances from obstacles.

#### 4.3. Evaluation Scenarios

Policies were tested across 30 trials per scenario, with success rates documented in Table III.
- **Suspended Obstacle**: Robot navigates under a low-hanging platform, requiring 3D awareness. PPO-RewardShaping failed significantly (20% success), while P3O (90%) and P3O-CBF (83%) performed well, highlighting the benefit of explicit constraints for 3D navigation.
- **Narrow Passage**: Robot navigates a confined corridor requiring precise control. PPO-RewardShaping failed entirely (0%). P3O achieved 33% success, while P3O-CBF achieved 60% success, nearly doubling the rate of P3O. This suggests comfort-oriented rewards assisted in preventing oscillations and over-corrections in tight spaces.
- **Cluttered Static Course**: Robot navigates a dense field of static pillars. PPO-RewardShaping achieved 93% success, while both P3O and P3O-CBF achieved 100% success.
- **Dynamic Agents**: Robot maneuvers among multiple moving humanoids. PPO-RewardShaping achieved 56% success. P3O achieved 70% success, and P3O-CBF significantly outperformed with an 86% success rate.

The results consistently demonstrated the superiority of the P3O-CBF framework, particularly in challenging scenarios involving narrow passages and dynamic agents, where its principled safety and comfort considerations translated into higher success rates.

#### 4.4. Physical Tests

The trained policy was successfully deployed on the Unitree G1 humanoid robot in real-world scenarios.
- **Cluttered Environment (Fig. 5)**: The robot successfully navigated a laboratory space with various static obstacles, utilizing its 3D LiDAR perception to avoid collisions.
- **Dynamic Human Interaction (Fig. 6)**: The robot demonstrated reactive avoidance by safely maneuvering away from a human agent who suddenly approached from behind. This validated the robustness of the learned safety constraints and the end-to-end framework's effectiveness for real-world dynamic environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that makes notable contributions to the field of humanoid robot locomotion, addressing several critical limitations in current approaches.

Firstly, the development of a LiDAR-driven end-to-end policy directly processing raw 3D point clouds is significant. This approach overcomes the inherent limitations of "blind" controllers and those relying on 2D vision (e.g., depth cameras and height maps) by providing comprehensive 3D environmental awareness. This capability is crucial for humanoid robots operating in complex, cluttered, and dynamic environments, enabling them to perceive and react to non-ground-level obstacles and varying terrain.

Secondly, the introduction of a principled safety framework is a key contribution. By formulating the control problem as a Constrained Markov Decision Process (CMDP) and, more notably, by translating Control Barrier Function (CBF) principles into cost functions for a model-free Reinforcement Learning (RL) algorithm (P3O), the work provides a robust mechanism for enforcing safety constraints. This approach offers stronger theoretical safety guarantees compared to traditional reward-shaping methods, allowing the robot to proactively avoid unsafe states by considering its dynamics and future trajectories, rather than merely reacting to imminent collisions. This method bridges the gap between model-based control theory and model-free RL, enhancing the reliability and safety of learned policies.

Thirdly, the integration of comfort-oriented rewards, grounded in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) research, represents a step towards more socially aware robot behavior. By penalizing abrupt movements, encouraging appropriate proxemic distances, and promoting tangential obstacle avoidance, the policy generates motions that are smoother, more predictable, and less intrusive. This consideration of psychological comfort is essential for fostering human trust and acceptance, which are prerequisites for the widespread deployment of humanoid robots in shared human environments.

Finally, the successful sim-to-real transfer and real-world deployment on a physical humanoid robot (Unitree G1) validate the practical applicability and robustness of the entire framework. The robot's ability to navigate static obstacles and react safely to dynamic human approaches in real-time demonstrates the framework's effectiveness beyond simulation.

The potential impact of this work is substantial. It moves humanoid robots closer to seamless coexistence and collaboration with humans by endowing them with advanced perception, provable safety, and socially intelligent locomotion. This could accelerate the deployment of humanoid robots in a variety of real-world applications, including assistance in homes, workplaces, and public spaces, where safe, efficient, and comfortable interaction with humans is paramount. The methodology for integrating CBF principles into model-free RL also offers a valuable blueprint for future research in safe learning for complex robotic systems.
