# MENLO: From Preferences to Proficiency -- Evaluating and Modeling Native-like Quality Across 47 Languages

> [!summary] Summary
> Meta Superintelligence Labs developed MENLO, a framework and dataset for evaluating and improving the native-like quality of Large Language Model (LLM) responses across 47 language varieties. The work demonstrates that reinforcement learning (RL) trained LLM judges can effectively guide policy models to enhance native-like proficiency, with human evaluation confirming improvements in output quality.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) need to produce high-quality, native-like responses across many languages, incorporating cultural and linguistic nuances, to be globally useful.
- Existing multilingual LLM evaluation benchmarks often rely on translated content, introducing "translationese" biases, or focus on general naturalness/task-specific metrics, failing to capture comprehensive native-like proficiency.
- Scaling human evaluation for many languages is expensive and time-consuming, while LLMs as zero-shot judges often lack the reliability required for consistent multilingual assessment.

## Method

- Introduced **MENLO** (Multilingual Evaluation of ==Native-Like Output==), a framework that defines and measures native-like quality across four dimensions: Fluency, Tone, Localized Tone, and Localized Factuality, using audience-design inspired prompts.
- Created a large human-annotated dataset comprising 6,423 prompt-response preference pairs and 81,014 annotations across 47 typologically diverse language varieties, achieving high inter-annotator agreement.
- Developed and fine-tuned LLM judges using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) with a composite reward signal, demonstrating that RL-trained judges can effectively serve as generative reward models to directly improve the native-like proficiency of policy LLMs.

## Results

- The MENLO dataset achieved an average Krippendorff’s α of **0.84**, demonstrating high inter-annotator agreement in assessing native-like quality across 47 language varieties.
- RL-trained LLM judges consistently outperformed SFT models; for example, Qwen3-**4B**-RL-Judge improved Macro-F1 by **4.0%** and Preference accuracy by **2.9%** over its SFT counterpart, surpassing frontier API models like gpt-4.1.
- Post-training a base Qwen3-**4B** policy model using an RL-trained judge resulted in consistent quality gains, with automated evaluation showing average score improvements of +**0.80** to +1.16 and human validation confirming a **55.7%** win rate with +**10.9%** average score improvement in a 10-language subset.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pairwise evaluation consistently and significantly outperforms pointwise scoring for LLM judges in assessing native-like quality, with detailed rubrics further enhancing performance.
> - Reinforcement learning (RL) with reward shaping proved more effective than SFT for training LLM judges, achieving higher agreement with human annotators and surpassing frontier API models in certain metrics.
> - Localized Factuality remains a persistent challenge for LLM judges, showing limited benefits from RL training and suggesting a need for external tools or retrieval mechanisms.
> - While RL-trained LLM judges can effectively improve policy model quality, they tend to overestimate the magnitude of these improvements compared to human evaluators, indicating a potential for stylistic "caricature" without careful calibration.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) capable of generating high-quality responses across numerous languages is a significant area of research. Prior work has evaluated LLM proficiency across various dimensions such as relevance, factual accuracy, and naturalness. However, a specific challenge remains in comprehensively evaluating and improving "native-like quality" in multilingual LLM outputs, particularly in the context of real-world conversations and localized cultural nuances.

Existing multilingual evaluation benchmarks often rely on translated prompts and responses, which may introduce biases towards "translationese" rather than genuinely native expression. These benchmarks typically focus on aggregate performance across task-oriented evaluations with verifiable answers or specific linguistic aspects like consistency and naturalness in narrow settings. For instance, datasets like RECON, PARIKSHA, BIGGEN BENCH, M-REWARDBENCH, and MM-EVAL address aspects such as instruction following, hallucinations, task quality, reasoning, cultural knowledge, and linguistic acceptability across multiple languages. PARIKSHA, for example, utilizes human-written prompts and annotations for Indic languages but focuses on high-level dimensions with moderate inter-annotator agreement.

This paper addresses a gap by proposing a framework that operationalizes native-like quality beyond general naturalness and task-specific metrics, incorporating cultural and linguistic nuances across a broad spectrum of language varieties. It also advances the methodology for training LLM-as-a-judge models in a massively multilingual context, particularly through the application of reinforcement learning (RL) and reward shaping, which has been less explored in cross-lingual settings compared to English-centric reward model development. The work unifies scalable evaluation with direct optimization of LLM multilingual proficiency, moving beyond mere assessment to model improvement.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a comprehensive framework for evaluating and improving the native-like quality of Large Language Model (LLM) responses across a wide range of languages. This overarching goal is motivated by several key challenges and specific requirements:

- **Ensuring LLM Utility in Global Contexts:** For LLMs to be maximally useful worldwide, they must produce high-quality responses in many languages. This quality extends beyond basic correctness to include aspects that make responses indistinguishable from those of a native speaker, which is crucial for seamless interaction.
- **Limitations of Existing Evaluation Methods:** Traditional language proficiency tests for humans are not easily scalable or directly applicable to evaluating LLMs in dynamic, real-world conversational settings. Moreover, existing multilingual LLM benchmarks often fall short in capturing the nuances of native-like quality. Many rely on translated content, which can introduce "translationese" artifacts rather than genuinely localized expression. They also tend to focus on task-oriented evaluations with verifiable answers or general linguistic attributes like naturalness in limited contexts, rather than comprehensive native-like proficiency.
- **Operationalizing Native-Like Quality:** The concept of "native-like" is inherently complex and subjective, often dependent on speakers' and listeners' interpretations of the target audience. The research aims to operationalize this concept by breaking it down into measurable dimensions to facilitate consistent evaluation.
- **Scalability of Evaluation:** Human evaluation, while gold-standard, is expensive and time-consuming, particularly across a large number of languages. A core motivation is to investigate the efficacy of LLMs as automatic judges and to improve their reliability to scale multilingual evaluation.
- **Improving Multilingual LLM Proficiency:** Beyond mere evaluation, the research seeks to use the insights gained from human and LLM judges to directly enhance the multilingual proficiency of LLMs, specifically through fine-tuning methods.
- **Addressing Specific Quality Dimensions:** The authors identified the need to go beyond naturalness by incorporating dimensions like cultural alignment and localized factual grounding, which are critical for truly native-like responses but often overlooked in broader multilingual benchmarks.

To address these motivations, the study sets out with the following specific objectives:
1. **Develop MENLO Framework:** Introduce Multilingual Evaluation of Native-Like Output (MENLO), a framework that defines and measures native-like response quality across four key dimensions: language quality and coherence (Fluency), overall writing style and helpfulness (Tone), alignment with cultural and linguistic nuances (Localized Tone), and factual correctness grounded in local context (Localized Factuality). This framework is inspired by audience design principles to evoke local contexts effectively.
2. **Create a High-Quality Multilingual Dataset:** Construct a human-annotated dataset based on the MENLO framework, comprising prompt-response preference pairs across a diverse set of languages and varieties, ensuring high inter-annotator agreement and localization.
3. **Evaluate LLM-as-a-Judge Performance:** Assess the capability of various LLMs (open-source and API-based) to act as judges of native-like quality in zero-shot and few-shot settings, examining the impact of evaluation setups (pointwise vs. pairwise) and explicit grading rubrics.
4. **Train Advanced LLM-Judges:** Fine-tune LLM judges using supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL), exploring different reward designs and multi-task learning, with the aim of achieving agreement levels comparable to human annotators.
5. **Utilize Judges as Generative Reward Models:** Demonstrate that these trained LLM judges can function as generative reward models to directly improve the native-like proficiency of policy LLMs through post-training, thus integrating evaluation and optimization within a unified framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the MENLO framework and proceeds through several stages: dataset creation, evaluation of LLM-as-a-judge capabilities, training of improved LLM judges, and finally, utilizing these judges as reward models for policy LLMs.

#### 3.1. The MENLO Dataset Construction

The MENLO framework defines native-like quality across four dimensions: **Fluency**, **Tone**, **Localized Tone**, and **Localized Factuality**. These dimensions are operationalized by drawing inspiration from audience design principles (Bell, 1984).
- **Prompt Design:** Human-written parametric English prompt templates are created for each dimension. These templates include placeholders (e.g., `[locale_nationality]`, `[locale_country]`, `[locale_holiday]`) to evoke local contexts and guide LLMs towards contextually appropriate native styles.
- **Language Coverage and Localization:** 47 typologically diverse language varieties are selected, including major variants (e.g., South American and European Spanish/Portuguese). Native speakers are recruited to translate and localize these prompt templates, instantiating placeholders with locally relevant entities. Annotators are also native speakers from the specific regions. Each language variety receives approximately the same number of examples.
- **Response Generation:** Two responses for each localized prompt are generated using several state-of-the-art LLMs (e.g., GPT-4o, Llama4-Maverick, Gemini 1.5).
- **Human Annotation:** Annotators receive detailed, human-written guidelines that break down the four dimensions into rubrics and self-explanatory signals. A customized annotation tool is used, incorporating annotator screening tests. For each prompt, annotators provide 1-5 Likert scale ratings for each of the two responses, allowing for ties. Each pair is annotated by at least three annotators, with final scores aggregated by majority vote. The dataset achieves an average Krippendorff’s α of 0.84, indicating high inter-annotator agreement.
- **Dataset Size:** The MENLO dataset comprises 6,423 annotated prompt-response preference pairs and a total of 81,014 annotations across 47 language varieties.

#### 3.2. Evaluating LLM-Judges on MENLO

The study evaluates LLMs as automatic judges of native-like quality on a held-out test set of 1,766 pairs.
- **Benchmark Models:** A range of open-source (Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-32B, Llama-3.1-8B, Llama-3.3-70B, Llama4-Scout) and API-based models (o3, gpt-4o, gpt-4.1) are benchmarked.
- **Evaluation Setups:**
 - **Zero-shot pointwise:** Model receives a prompt, a single response, and a 5-point grading rubric, then assigns a grade.
 - **Few-shot pointwise:** Same as zero-shot pointwise, but with three graded in-context examples.
 - **Zero-shot pairwise:** Model receives a prompt and two responses, assigning a grade to each, with the order randomized.
- **Metrics:** Macro-F1 for 5-way classification, Preference accuracy (Win/Loss/Tie inferred from grades), and Krippendorff’s α for agreement with human annotators.
- **Impact of Rubrics:** Performance is also compared with and without explicit grading rubrics.

#### 3.3. Training LLM-Judges on MENLO

This stage aims to improve LLM judges by fine-tuning them on the MENLO training split (4,675 response pairs).
- **Models:** Qwen3-4B (dense, reasoning-oriented) and Llama4-Scout (Mixture-of-Experts, non-reasoning).
- **Learning Strategies:**
 - **Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):** Models directly predict 5-point grades using cross-entropy loss without intermediate reasoning generation.
 - **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** GRPO (Generalized Reinforcement Learning with Policy Optimization) is used, incentivizing step-by-step reasoning before score assignment. Training data is augmented by including both (A,B) and (B,A) response orders to mitigate positional bias.
- **Reward Designs for RL:** A composite reward signal is designed, combining:
 - Pointwise binary reward (+1 for exact match, +0.5 for a difference of one grade).
 - Preference bonus (+1 if the sign of score difference matches human preference).
 - Penalties (-1 for invalid/missing scores, -0.2 for formatting violations).
- **Training Scope:** Comparison of single-task (dimension-specific) vs. multi-task (all dimensions) training, and evaluation of cross-language performance.

#### 3.4. From LLM-Judges to Reward Models

The final stage investigates using the RL-trained pairwise judges as generative reward models (RMs) to improve LLM native-like response quality.
- **Policy Model and RM:** Qwen3-4B serves as the policy model, and an RL-trained Qwen3-4B judge (Qwen3-4B-RL-Judge) acts as the reward model.
- **Post-training:** The Qwen3-4B policy model is post-trained using GRPO. Responses are sampled from the policy, paired, and fed to the RM, with the final reward for each rollout averaged across paired comparisons. Localized Factuality is excluded from this training and evaluation due to its persistent challenge for judges.
- **Two-Stage Evaluation Strategy:**
 - **Automated Evaluation:** Three diverse, high-performing LLM judges (Qwen3-32B, gpt-4.1, Llama4-Scout-RL-Judge) evaluate the baseline vs. post-trained policy model across all 47 language varieties. Win/loss/tie rates and average score improvements are computed.
 - **Human Validation:** A subset of 10 high-resource languages is selected for human evaluation using the same pairwise annotation guidelines as MENLO construction. This serves to anchor automated evaluation results and identify potential discrepancies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several key findings across the evaluation and training of LLM judges and their application as reward models:

#### 4.1. MENLO Dataset Characteristics

- The MENLO dataset consists of 6,423 human-annotated prompt-response preference pairs and 81,014 annotations across 47 language varieties.
- It demonstrates high inter-annotator agreement, with an average Krippendorff’s α = 0.84, indicating consistency in human judgments. This agreement is notably higher than some comparable multilingual preference datasets.
- The framework successfully operationalizes native-like quality into four dimensions: Fluency, Tone, Localized Tone, and Localized Factuality, with localized prompts and responses reflecting cultural and linguistic nuances.

#### 4.2. LLM-Judge Evaluation (Zero-shot)

- **Pairwise Superiority:** Zero-shot pairwise evaluation consistently and significantly outperforms both zero-shot and few-shot pointwise scoring across all benchmarked models. Gains in Macro-F1 reached up to +12.4% and in Preference accuracy up to +18.0% over zero-shot pointwise. This suggests that evaluating responses side-by-side provides a stronger relative signal and improved reliability.
- **Benefits of Rubrics:** Detailed human-written grading rubrics substantially improve judge performance, particularly for pointwise evaluation, yielding average gains of +4.3% in Macro-F1 and +2.5% in Preference accuracy. The impact on pairwise evaluation is more modest, suggesting that pairwise comparison inherently offers a strong grounding signal.
- **Underperformance of Zero-shot Judges:** Despite these improvements, zero-shot LLM judges, even with pairwise evaluation and rubrics, still underperform human annotators on the MENLO dataset, indicating a gap for further improvement.

#### 4.3. Training LLM-Judges

- **RL Outperforms SFT:** Reinforcement Learning (RL) trained models consistently outperform their Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) counterparts. For Qwen3-4B, SFT without Chain-of-Thought reasoning negatively impacted performance, while RL, which incentivizes reasoning, improved Macro-F1 by +4.0% and Preference by +2.9%, surpassing frontier API models like gpt-4.1. For Llama4-Scout, SFT provided gains, but RL further improved performance, especially in Preference (+2.5%).
- **Reward Shaping Effectiveness:** Ablation studies on RL reward design demonstrate clear benefits from combining reward smoothing (partial credit for near-miss predictions) and a preference bonus, achieving the best overall Macro-F1 and Preference accuracy (+2.3% and +1.7% respectively over binary-only reward for Qwen3-4B).
- **Multi-task vs. Single-task Training:** Joint multi-dimension training performs comparably to single-dimension optimization while offering greater efficiency, except for Localized Factuality.
- **Localized Factuality Challenge:** Localized Factuality consistently lags behind other dimensions in performance, showing limited benefits from RL training. This suggests a persistent challenge for LLM judges in this area, potentially requiring external tools or retrieval mechanisms.
- **Cross-Language Variability:** Performance of RL-trained judges varies significantly across the 47 language varieties (e.g., tr-TR at 82.1% vs. bn-BD at 37.9% in Preference accuracy). English-only training was found insufficient to generalize across all 47 varieties, highlighting the necessity of multilingual training data.

#### 4.4. LLM-Judges as Generative Reward Models

- **Policy Model Improvement:** Using an RL-trained Qwen3-4B judge to post-train a base Qwen3-4B policy model (excluding Localized Factuality) resulted in consistent quality gains. Automated evaluation by independent LLM judges showed average score improvements ranging from +0.80 to +1.16, with win rates between 63.4% and 77.9%.
- **Consistency Across Dimensions:** Gains were observed across all evaluated dimensions (Fluency, Tone, Localized Tone), with Tone showing the largest improvement (+1.04 average score boost).
- **LLM vs. Human Judgment Discrepancy:** While human validation on a subset of 10 languages confirmed quality improvements (55.7% win rate, +10.9% average score improvement), LLM judges tended to overestimate the magnitude of these improvements compared to human raters. The automated judges reported an average improvement +0.5 higher than humans, suggesting a potential stylistic "caricature" by LLMs. However, RL-trained judges exhibited less of this discrepancy among LLM evaluators.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of natural language processing, particularly in the domain of multilingual Large Language Model (LLM) development and evaluation, with several areas of significance and potential impact:

- **Standardized Framework for Multilingual Native-like Quality:** The introduction of MENLO provides a structured and detailed framework for defining and evaluating native-like response quality across a broad range of languages. By breaking down this complex concept into four distinct dimensions and anchoring them with audience-design inspired prompts and granular rubrics, MENLO offers a more comprehensive and nuanced approach compared to previous generalized evaluations. This framework can serve as a foundation for future research and development in multilingual LLM assessment.
- **High-Quality Multilingual Evaluation Dataset:** The release of the MENLO dataset, with its extensive coverage (47 language varieties) and high inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff’s α = 0.84), is a significant contribution. This dataset addresses the scarcity of human-annotated, culturally and linguistically localized evaluation resources, which are crucial for developing and benchmarking truly proficient multilingual LLMs. Its public availability (https://huggingface.co/datasets/facebook/menlo) will facilitate further research and comparison within the community.
- **Advancements in LLM-as-a-Judge Methodology:** The findings underscore the efficacy of pairwise evaluation over pointwise and the critical role of detailed rubrics for improving LLM judge reliability. The demonstration that reinforcement learning (RL) with reward shaping significantly enhances LLM judges' performance, even surpassing frontier API models in certain metrics, marks an advancement in the training of automated evaluators. This can lead to more scalable, cost-effective, and consistent evaluation pipelines for multilingual LLMs, reducing reliance on expensive human annotation at scale.
- **Unified Evaluation and Optimization Loop:** The research successfully demonstrates that RL-trained LLM judges can effectively serve as generative reward models to directly improve the native-like proficiency of policy LLMs. This closes the loop between evaluation and model optimization, offering a practical and scalable approach to align multilingual LLM outputs towards desired quality attributes. This integrated framework could accelerate the development cycle for multilingual LLMs, making iterative improvements more feasible.
- **Highlighting Challenges and Future Directions:** The consistent underperformance of LLM judges in Localized Factuality, and the observed discrepancy between LLM and human judgments regarding the *magnitude* of improvements, highlight important ongoing challenges. These findings point towards critical areas for future research, such as incorporating retrieval mechanisms or external tool use for factuality, and developing more sophisticated reward modeling techniques to better capture the nuances of human perception of native-like quality. The discrepancy in judgment magnitude suggests a need for calibration or more human-aligned reward functions to prevent LLMs from converging on "caricatures" of native-like style.
- **Practical Implications for Global LLM Deployment:** The ability to accurately evaluate and incrementally improve LLM outputs across a wide array of languages and cultural contexts has direct practical implications for deploying LLMs globally. It contributes to making LLMs more helpful, trustworthy, and culturally appropriate for users worldwide, fostering more inclusive and effective human-AI interaction.
