# Dynamics-Predictive Sampling for Active RL Finetuning of Large Reasoning Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Dynamics-Predictive Sampling (DPS) is introduced as an online prompt selection method for RL finetuning of large reasoning models, which predicts prompt informativeness using a Bayesian inference-based Hidden Markov Model. This approach reduces LLM rollouts by over 70% and cuts total runtime by approximately 50% compared to state-of-the-art methods like Dynamic Sampling, while achieving comparable or superior performance across various reasoning tasks.

## Problem

- High-quality, informative training data is crucial for effective Reinforcement Learning (RL) finetuning of large language models (LLMs) to enhance their reasoning capabilities.
- Current online prompt selection methods, such as Dynamic Sampling (DS), rely on extensive LLM rollouts to identify informative prompts, incurring substantial computational overhead.
- The significant computational cost of these rollouts limits the scalability and efficiency of adaptive RL finetuning, particularly for complex reasoning tasks requiring long Chain-of-Thought generation.

## Method

- **DPS** models the evolution of each prompt's '==solving state==' (fully unsolved, partially solved, fully solved) during RL finetuning as a Hidden Markov Model (HMM).
- It uses online Bayesian inference to track the probability of a prompt being in each state and learns non-stationary transition dynamics between these states over time.
- Prompts are actively selected for training based on their *predicted* probability of being in the 'partially solved' state (most informative) *before* performing costly LLM rollouts for filtering.

## Results

- DPS consistently achieved faster policy improvement and higher final performance across diverse tasks (mathematics, planning, visual geometry) compared to uniform and history-based sampling baselines.
- The method reduced the number of LLM rollouts by over **70%** and lowered total runtime by approximately **50%** relative to the computationally intensive Dynamic Sampling, while maintaining or surpassing its performance.
- Ablation studies confirmed the optimality of a three-state partition for prompt-solving dynamics and validated the importance of a non-stationary decay mechanism for prediction accuracy and overall performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Prompt-solving progress during RL finetuning can be effectively represented and predicted as a non-stationary dynamical system, enabling adaptive data selection.
> - Prompts where the model yields a mix of correct and incorrect responses ('partially solved') provide the strongest gradient signals for policy improvement.
> - A non-stationary decay mechanism within the Bayesian inference framework implicitly encourages exploration, ensuring under-sampled prompts are revisited and adapting to the model's evolving competence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement learning (RL) finetuning has become a prominent method for enhancing the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs), leading to the development of large reasoning models (LRMs) that excel in multi-step inference across various knowledge-intensive applications such as symbolic mathematics, logical deduction, and program synthesis. The effectiveness of this RL finetuning process is significantly influenced by the quality of the training data.

Current approaches to data curation can be broadly categorized into offline data filtering and online prompt selection. Offline methods rank or select prompts based on static heuristics (e.g., estimated difficulty, domain balance) prior to training. While offering some benefits, these methods lack adaptivity to the model's evolving competence during the training process. Online prompt selection strategies address this limitation by dynamically adjusting the training data to the model's current behavior, typically by prioritizing "informative" prompts that provide strong training signals.

A representative state-of-the-art online method, Dynamic Sampling (DS), identifies informative prompts by performing extensive LLM rollouts over large candidate batches. Prompts with consistent rewards (either all correct or all incorrect responses) are considered uninformative and are discarded. This strategy demonstrably accelerates RL finetuning in terms of training steps by improving sample quality. However, for reasoning-intensive tasks requiring long Chain-of-Thought (CoT) generation, the extensive LLM rollouts required by methods like DS incur substantial computational overhead. This overhead, in many practical scenarios, can be comparable to or even exceed the cost of the finetuning process itself, thereby limiting the scalability and efficiency of such adaptive strategies.

This work, Dynamics-Predictive Sampling (DPS), is positioned within this landscape as a solution to the computational bottleneck of online prompt selection. It aims to preserve the adaptivity and training efficiency benefits of online methods like DS, but crucially, without incurring the high cost of extensive LLM rollouts for prompt filtering. By predicting prompt informativeness prior to rollout, DPS seeks to make adaptive RL finetuning more computationally efficient and scalable for enhancing large reasoning models.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient online prompt selection method for RL finetuning of large reasoning models that can adapt to the model's evolving competence without the substantial computational overhead typically associated with existing online strategies.

The motivation for this objective stems from several observations within the field:
- **Critical Dependence on Data Quality:** Prior research consistently highlights that the performance and effectiveness of RL finetuning for LLMs are highly sensitive to the quality and informativeness of the training data. This underscores the necessity of intelligent data curation strategies.
- **Limitations of Offline Data Filtering:** While simple, offline data filtering methods use static heuristics and cannot adapt to the dynamic learning process of an LLM. As an LLM's capabilities evolve, the informativeness of different prompts changes, rendering static selections suboptimal.
- **Computational Burden of Current Online Methods:** State-of-the-art online prompt selection techniques, such as Dynamic Sampling (DS), have proven effective in identifying informative samples. These methods typically involve generating multiple responses (rollouts) for a large candidate batch of prompts and then filtering out those that provide limited training signal (e.g., consistently correct or consistently incorrect responses). This process, however, demands extensive LLM inference, which, especially for complex reasoning tasks, translates into significant computational costs that can outweigh the actual model finetuning expense. This high computational cost hinders the practical scalability of these otherwise effective methods.
- **Identifying Informative Prompts:** Research indicates that prompts for which the model yields a mix of correct and incorrect responses (termed "partially solved" prompts) provide the strongest gradient signals for policy improvement. Therefore, a core motivation is to efficiently identify and prioritize these "partially solved" examples.

By addressing these motivations, this work aims to enable more efficient and scalable RL finetuning, allowing large reasoning models to be enhanced adaptively without prohibitive computational resource requirements. This is achieved by introducing a predictive mechanism that infers prompt informativeness before engaging in costly generative rollouts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Dynamics-Predictive Sampling (DPS), addresses the computational challenge of online prompt selection by predicting the informativeness of prompts prior to expensive LLM rollouts. This is achieved by modeling the prompt-solving progress as a dynamical system, from which learning dynamics are inferred through online Bayesian inference.

#### 3.1. Generative Modeling of Prompt-Solving Dynamics

DPS formalizes the evolution of each prompt's solving state during RL finetuning as a dynamical system, specifically a Hidden Markov Model (HMM).
- **Prompt-Solving States:** For each prompt $\tau$, an implicit state $z_{\tau,t} \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ is defined at training step $t$, based on the outcomes of $k$ responses generated by the current policy:
 - **State 1 (fully unsolved):** All $k$ responses are incorrect ($\sum r(\tau, y_i) = 0$).
 - **State 2 (partially solved):** Some responses are correct, and some are incorrect ($0 < \sum r(\tau, y_i) < k$). These are considered the most informative for training.
 - **State 3 (fully solved):** All $k$ responses are correct ($\sum r(\tau, y_i) = k$).
- **Hidden Markov Model (HMM):** The evolution of these states is modeled as an HMM.
 - **Initial State:** The initial solving state $z_1$ for each prompt is drawn from a uniform categorical prior $\mu_{\text{prior},1} = [1/3, 1/3, 1/3]$ in the absence of prior knowledge.
 - **Transitions:** Subsequent states evolve according to a Markov process governed by a column-stochastic transition matrix $\Phi \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$. $\Phi(i, j)$ represents the probability of transitioning from state $j$ to state $i$.
 - **Observations:** If a prompt is selected for training at step $t$, its true state $y_t$ is observed (derived from the $k$ rollouts and reward evaluations). If not selected, the state remains unobserved ($y_t = \emptyset$). This results in a degenerate emission model where $P(y_t | z_t) = \delta(y_t, z_t)$ if observed, and $1$ if unobserved.
- **Joint Distribution:** The joint distribution over states and observations is formulated to enable Bayesian inference over the hidden solving states.

#### 3.2. Online Inference and Transition Learning

DPS employs an online Bayesian inference strategy to track each prompt's solving state. This procedure occurs in three stages at each training step $t$:
- **Observation Update:** If prompt $\tau$ is selected for training at step $t$ and its state $y_{\tau,t}$ is observed, Bayes' rule is applied to update the prior belief $\mu_{\tau,\text{prior},t}$ to a posterior belief $\mu_{\tau,\text{post},t}$. If $y_{\tau,t}$ is unobserved, $\mu_{\tau,\text{post},t}$ is set equal to $\mu_{\tau,\text{prior},t}$.
- **Transition Update:** Dirichlet priors are placed on the columns of the transition matrix $\Phi$. When $y_{\tau,t}$ is observed, the parameters $\alpha_{\tau,t}(i, j)$ of the Dirichlet posterior are updated using the soft transition pseudo-counts $\xi_{\tau,t}(i, j)$, which represent the posterior probability of a transition from state $j$ to state $i$ given all observations up to $t$.
 - **Non-stationary Extension:** To account for evolving dynamics in LLM learning, an exponentially decayed Dirichlet posterior update is introduced: $\alpha_{\tau,t} = \lambda \cdot \alpha_{\tau,t-1} + (1-\lambda) \cdot \alpha_{\tau,0} + \xi_{\tau,t}$, where $\lambda \in (0, 1)$ is a decay ratio. This allows the model to adapt to recent trends while gradually forgetting outdated patterns. $\alpha_{\tau,0}$ is an initial uninformative prior. If $y_{\tau,t}$ is unobserved, $\xi_{\tau,t}=0$, and the parameters simply decay.
- **Next-State Prediction:** The updated posterior belief $\mu_{\tau,\text{post},t}$ and the inferred transition matrix $\Phi_t$ are used to predict the prior belief for the next step: $\mu_{\tau,\text{prior},t+1} = \Phi_t \mu_{\tau,\text{post},t}$. This predicted prior then serves as the belief for the subsequent inference iteration.
- **Computational Efficiency:** The inference framework's computational cost is minimized by involving only low-dimensional matrix operations, making it negligible compared to LLM response generation or model finetuning.

#### 3.3. Prompt Sampling with Predicted Dynamics

The core mechanism for active sampling in DPS is to prioritize prompts with the highest predicted probability of being in State 2 (partially solved) before any new rollouts are performed.
- **Selection Criterion:** At each training step $t$, the $B$ prompts with the highest values of $\mu_{\tau,\text{prior},t}(2)$ are selected to form the training batch $B_t$. This "Top-B" selection is purely exploitative in terms of maximizing the predicted informativeness.
- **Implicit Exploration:** The non-stationary decay mechanism implicitly encourages exploration. For infrequently sampled prompts, the decay causes their transition posteriors to drift back towards the uniform prior, increasing their predicted uncertainty (and thus probability of being "partially solved" if initially "fully unsolved" or "fully solved"). This leads to them being revisited and re-evaluated, mitigating the risk of negative feedback loops where under-sampled prompts remain un-sampled.
- **Curriculum Learning:** This sampling strategy naturally induces a curriculum, starting with easier "partially solved" prompts and gradually progressing to harder ones as the model improves.

The complete DPS algorithm iteratively selects informative prompts, fine-tunes the LLM, and updates the HMM states and transitions for all prompts in the dataset.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical evaluation of Dynamics-Predictive Sampling (DPS) was conducted across diverse reasoning tasks including competition-level mathematics (MATH), numerical planning (Countdown), and visual geometric reasoning (Geometry3k), utilizing various large language and multi-modal models. The findings detail DPS's prediction accuracy, training efficiency, generalization performance, and resource utilization.

#### 4.1. Prediction Accuracy of Prompt-Solving States

DPS demonstrated robust capabilities in online prediction of prompt-solving states:
- **High Accuracy:** The predictor maintained high overall accuracy throughout training, with strong precision, recall, and F1 scores specifically for Class 2 (partially solved) prompts, which are critical for training efficiency.
- **Improved Discriminability:** Analysis of confusion matrices showed a progressive strengthening of diagonal entries and a reduction in off-diagonal errors over training steps, indicating improved discriminability and confidence in state predictions as more data became available. The increasing prominence of the center cell (True 2, Predicted 2) indicated that the predictor effectively focused on partially solved prompts.
- **Effective Sample Ratio:** Compared to Uniform Sampling (US) and History Resampling (HR), DPS consistently yielded a significantly higher concentration of partially solved prompts within its sampled batches, often reaching approximately 90% in various tasks. This indicates its success in identifying and prioritizing informative examples.

#### 4.2. RL Finetuning Efficiency and Performance

DPS exhibited substantial advantages in both training efficiency and ultimate performance:
- **Faster Policy Improvement:** DPS consistently achieved substantially faster policy improvement and higher final performance compared to US and HR across all tested tasks and model sizes (1.5B, 3B, 7B). US and HR often experienced performance degradation, potentially due to insufficient informative samples.
- **Comparable to Oracle Baseline:** DPS attained performance comparable to, and in some cases slightly superior to, Dynamic Sampling (DS), which serves as a computationally intensive oracle baseline. This indicates that DPS can achieve similar benefits in sample quality and learning acceleration without DS's overhead.
- **Generalization Performance:** On mathematics benchmarks (AIME24, AMC23, MATH500, MinervaMath, OlympiadBench), numerical planning (CD-34, CD-4), and visual geometry (Geometry3k), DPS consistently outperformed US and HR. It also matched or exceeded DS in generalization performance, including on general reasoning benchmarks like ARC-c and MMLU-Pro, and under extended response length evaluations (32k tokens).

#### 4.3. Rollout and Runtime Efficiency

A key contribution of DPS is its superior resource efficiency:
- **Reduced Rollouts:** DPS achieved strong performance with significantly fewer LLM rollouts than DS, typically requiring less than 30% of DS's rollout budget to match or surpass its results. This directly addresses the main computational bottleneck.
- **Lower Runtime:** DPS incurred substantially lower total runtime compared to DS (approximately half on the MATH dataset). While slightly longer than US and HR, this difference was attributed to the generation of longer, more complex responses indicative of higher performance, rather than the prediction mechanism itself.
- **Negligible Computational Overhead:** The computational cost of DPS's prediction and selection operations was found to be negligible compared to LLM training and generation. Its runtime and memory usage scaled approximately linearly with dataset size, requiring minimal resources even for large datasets.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Non-stationary Decay (λ):** A moderate decay ratio ($\lambda=0.7$ for MATH, $\lambda=0.5$ for Countdown/Geometry3k) yielded the best performance and prediction accuracy. Removing decay ($\lambda=1$) or relying solely on recent feedback ($\lambda=0$) led to degraded performance, confirming the non-stationary nature of prompt-solving dynamics and the importance of adapting to recent observations.
- **Solving-State Partitions:** The three-state partition (fully unsolved, partially solved, fully solved) proved optimal. Both coarser (two states) and finer (more than three states) partitions resulted in lower performance and effective sample ratios. Coarse partitions obscured distinct dynamics, while finer ones led to data sparsity and reduced prediction reliability.
- **Transition Priors:** While structured transition priors (e.g., stability or progress-promoting) could offer minor early-stage improvements, the uninformative prior generally performed robustly, suggesting that learned dynamics often overcome initial biases.
- **Response Group Size (k):** DPS's performance advantage over US was most pronounced at smaller response group sizes (e.g., $k=4$). In such scenarios, US struggled to find informative prompts due to the reduced probability of mixed-outcome responses, whereas DPS maintained high prediction accuracy and effective sample ratios.

#### 4.5. Additional Analysis

- **Implicit Exploration:** The non-stationary decay mechanism in DPS implicitly encourages exploration. Smaller decay parameters led to more uniform sample counts across the dataset, indicating that under-sampled prompts were naturally revisited, even with a purely exploitative Top-B selection strategy.
- **Prediction Accuracy on Representative Prompts:** DPS maintained high prediction accuracy even for "difficult" (predicted fully unsolved) and "under-sampled" prompts. This was attributed to these prompts often having more confidently predicted states (e.g., confidently fully unsolved or fully solved), thus mitigating the impact of sparse observations.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents Dynamics-Predictive Sampling (DPS) as a methodology with significant implications for the efficient and scalable training of large reasoning models (LRMs) through reinforcement learning (RL) finetuning.

**Significance:**
- **Addressing a Critical Bottleneck:** DPS directly confronts the primary computational bottleneck in modern RL finetuning for LLMs – the extensive and costly LLM rollouts required by existing online prompt selection strategies. By predicting prompt informativeness *prior* to these rollouts, it offers a fundamentally more efficient approach.
- **Maintaining Adaptivity with Efficiency:** The method successfully preserves the crucial adaptivity of online prompt selection, allowing the training data to dynamically adjust to the model's evolving capabilities, without the prohibitive computational cost.
- **Performance and Resource Efficiency:** DPS demonstrates that comparable or even superior reasoning performance can be achieved with a substantially lower computational budget (e.g., less than 30% of the rollouts and about half the runtime compared to state-of-the-art oracle baselines like Dynamic Sampling). This represents a significant improvement in the efficiency frontier of LRM finetuning.
- **Generality Across Tasks and Models:** The method's effectiveness across diverse reasoning tasks (mathematics, planning, visual geometry) and various large language and multi-modal models (DeepSeek-R1-Distill, Qwen2.5, Llama-3.2) indicates its broad applicability and robustness.
- **Principled Approach:** By formalizing prompt-solving progress as a dynamical system and employing online Bayesian inference, DPS provides a principled and tractable framework for inferring learning dynamics, which contrasts with heuristic-based approaches.
- **Implicit Curriculum Learning:** The adaptive nature of DPS naturally leads to an implicit curriculum, where the model progresses from easier to harder tasks, optimizing the learning trajectory without manual intervention.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Accelerated LRM Development:** The substantial reduction in computational resources (rollouts, runtime) can accelerate the research and development cycle for advanced LRMs, making complex RL finetuning more accessible and cost-effective.
- **Enhanced Scalability:** For increasingly large models and datasets, the efficiency gains offered by DPS will become even more critical, enabling the finetuning of models that might otherwise be prohibitively expensive to train with existing adaptive methods.
- **Improved LLM Reasoning:** By focusing training on the most informative examples, DPS can contribute to more robust and higher-performing reasoning capabilities in LLMs, which are vital for applications in science, engineering, and various knowledge-intensive domains.
- **Foundation for Future Active Learning:** The HMM-based predictive framework could serve as a foundation for more sophisticated active learning strategies in LLM training, allowing for richer definitions of informativeness beyond simple binary outcomes and potentially incorporating other forms of uncertainty or diversity.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The work acknowledges certain limitations. The current method primarily relies on correctness-based binary rewards to define solving states. Future work could explore extensions to more complex reward structures, such as dense or process-based rewards, by adapting the state partitioning strategy. Additionally, while the Top-B selection strategy is effective, it is a purely exploitative mechanism. Exploring more sophisticated criteria, such as entropy-based prioritization of uncertain samples or explicit exploration bonuses, could further enhance the method's robustness and long-term learning capabilities.
