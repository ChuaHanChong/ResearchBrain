# HG-DAgger: Interactive Imitation Learning with Human Experts

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University and the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign developed HG-DAgger, an imitation learning framework that allows human experts to provide uninterrupted control during interventions, addressing limitations of traditional methods. The approach also learns a data-driven safety threshold for its risk metric, which contributed to zero collisions and zero road departures on an autonomous test vehicle, outperforming baseline algorithms.

## Problem

- Behavioral Cloning (BC) suffers from data mismatch and compounding errors, causing learned policies to degrade when encountering states outside the expert's training distribution.
- Standard DAGGER's stochastic shared control, where an expert intermittently corrects an imperfect novice, leads to reduced human control authority, perceived actuator lag, and degraded quality of expert labels, particularly in safety-critical human-in-the-loop applications.
- Prior confidence-based autonomy approaches often rely on arbitrarily set or manually tuned thresholds for risk metrics, which may not be robust or meaningful across diverse scenarios.

## Method

- The authors developed **HG-DAgger** (Human-Gated DAgger), an imitation learning framework that provides the human expert with full, uninterrupted control whenever an intervention is deemed necessary, rather than using ==stochastic control switching==.
- Expert action labels are collected only when the human expert is in full control during ==recovery trajectories==, ensuring high-quality and unbiased data for policy training.
- A data-driven method learns a ==safety threshold== for a ==doubt== metric (derived from an ==ensemble of neural networks==) by recording the novice's doubt at the moment of human intervention, allowing for a dynamically determined and meaningful risk boundary.

## Results

- HG-DAgger demonstrated faster and more stable learning in simulation, converging to lower road departure and collision rates, while standard DAGGER showed instability in later training epochs.
- On a physical test vehicle, HG-DAgger achieved **zero collisions** and **zero road departures** over test configurations, outperforming standard DAGGER (**1** collision, **1** road departure) and Behavioral Cloning (**1** collision, **6** road departures).
- The learned safety threshold for the 'doubt' metric proved effective: initializing the policy within the learned 'permissible set' (below the threshold) resulted in **12x** lower collision rates and **20x** lower road departure rates in simulation, and the policy exhibited **21.1%** more human-like steering behavior.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Providing human experts with uninterrupted control during interventions is crucial for obtaining high-quality training data and stable learning in interactive imitation learning, addressing issues like perceived actuator lag and degraded feedback in shared control settings.
> - A data-driven approach to learning a risk threshold from human interventions can effectively identify safe operating regions for a learned policy, enhancing trustworthiness and allowing for dynamic safety mechanisms.
> - Policy confidence, quantified via ensemble uncertainty (doubt), can serve as a reliable, model-free approximator for risk, indicating regions of the state space where the learned policy is less confident or prone to errors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper is situated within the field of **imitation learning (IL)**, a paradigm where an autonomous agent learns a policy by observing expert demonstrations. It specifically addresses critical limitations of existing IL techniques when applied to **human-in-the-loop** scenarios.

Historically, imitation learning began with simple **behavioral cloning (BC)**, which treats policy learning as a supervised learning problem. An expert demonstrates desired behaviors, and a novice policy is trained to mimic these actions given similar observations. While straightforward, BC suffers from two major drawbacks:
1. **Data Mismatch:** The novice policy, being imperfect, may deviate from the expert's demonstrated trajectories. When it encounters states not seen in the expert's training data, its performance can degrade unpredictably.
2. **Compounding Errors:** Small errors made by the novice can accumulate over time, leading to larger and larger deviations from the desired trajectory, eventually causing catastrophic failure.

To address these issues, the **Dataset Aggregation (DAGGER)** algorithm was introduced. DAGGER is an online iterative algorithm that mitigates data mismatch and compounding errors by allowing the novice policy to influence the state distribution from which expert corrections are sampled. In DAGGER, the novice is periodically allowed to execute actions, and if it deviates, a human expert provides corrective labels for the states the novice visited. This "Robot-Centric" (RC) sampling scheme helps the novice learn how to recover from its own mistakes, leading to more robust policies with theoretical performance guarantees.

However, the authors identify a critical gap: while DAGGER is effective, its direct application with *human* experts in real-world systems presents significant challenges. The core issue lies in DAGGER's shared control scheme, where a "gating function" (often a probabilistic coin toss) determines whether the expert's or novice's action is executed. For human experts, this stochastic switching causes several problems:
- **Reduced Control Authority & Feedback:** Humans rely on continuous feedback from the system to perform optimally. If their commanded actions are not always executed, or if control is frequently swapped, their perception of control authority is diminished, leading to a degraded feedback signal.
- **Perceived Actuator Lag:** Frequent switching can create a sensation of delay or lag in the system's response to human inputs, which humans are highly sensitive to. This can disrupt their natural control rhythm.
- **Degradation of Label Quality:** As a result of these issues, human experts may adapt their behavior, leading to "unbiased" expert demonstrations. They might overcorrect, become more cautious, or their control inputs may become erratic, ultimately degrading the quality of the collected training labels and potentially destabilizing the combined system.
- **Safety Concerns:** Allowing an incompletely trained novice to take partial or complete control during training rollouts, especially in safety-critical domains like autonomous driving, poses inherent safety risks.
- **Tuning Difficulty:** The `β` parameter in DAGGER, which controls the mixture of expert and novice actions, presents a difficult trade-off between learning performance (requiring more novice exposure) and training-time safety/label quality (requiring more expert control). Optimal tuning is challenging, especially on physical systems.

Prior work has attempted to address some aspects of human-in-the-loop IL, primarily by reducing the expert's workload through query efficiency (e.g., querying the expert only when novice and expert behaviors diverge significantly). While valuable, these methods often don't fully resolve the fundamental issues of shared control and its impact on human performance and safety.

The paper also builds upon concepts from **ENSEMBLEDAGGER**, a probabilistic extension that uses an ensemble of neural networks to approximate risk based on policy confidence (or "doubt"). This allows for a more informed decision on when the novice can act safely. However, a key limitation of ENSEMBLEDAGGER, acknowledged by the authors, is the open problem of identifying appropriate and meaningful thresholds for this risk metric.

HG-DAgger distinguishes itself from these predecessors by directly addressing the human-specific challenges of shared control. It departs from stochastic switching and instead empowers the human expert with full, uninterrupted control when intervention is deemed necessary. Furthermore, it introduces a novel, data-driven method for *learning* a safety threshold for its risk metric, rather than relying on arbitrary or ad-hoc settings. This positions HG-DAgger as a significant advancement for practical and safe interactive imitation learning with human experts, particularly in complex, safety-critical domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop an imitation learning framework that is more suitable and effective for **interactive learning from human experts in real-world systems**, particularly addressing the shortcomings of traditional DAGGER-like algorithms in such contexts.

The primary motivations for developing HG-DAgger stem from the limitations of existing imitation learning approaches when humans are in the control loop:

1. **Overcoming Behavioral Cloning's Deficiencies:** Like DAGGER, HG-DAgger aims to mitigate the well-known data mismatch and compounding error issues that plague simple behavioral cloning, ensuring the learned policy is robust to deviations from expert trajectories.
2. **Addressing DAGGER's Human-Centric Challenges:** The most critical motivation is to resolve the problems associated with DAGGER's "Robot-Centric" (RC) sampling paradigm when the "robot" is interacting with a human expert. Specifically:
 - **Improving Safety During Training:** By minimizing direct control by an untrained novice, especially in dangerous situations, HG-DAgger seeks to enhance training-time safety.
 - **Enhancing Expert Label Quality:** Preventing perceived actuator lag and maintaining the human expert's full control authority during interventions is crucial for obtaining high-fidelity, unbiased action labels, thereby improving the efficiency and stability of the learning process.
 - **Ensuring System Stability:** Mitigating control switching that could lead to pilot-induced oscillations or system instability in combined human-AI systems.
 - **Simplifying Control Parameter Tuning:** Eliminating the need to carefully tune a stochastic control parameter like DAGGER's `β`, which is challenging in real-world, safety-critical applications.
3. **Developing a Meaningful Risk Metric and Threshold:** A secondary but equally important objective is to learn a reliable and interpretable risk metric that can predict the novice policy's performance in different regions of the state space. Crucially, the authors aim to **learn** an appropriate safety threshold for this metric directly from human intervention data, moving beyond ad-hoc or manually chosen thresholds found in prior confidence-based autonomy approaches. This learned threshold allows for a better understanding of the policy's confidence and can inform future automated safety mechanisms.

In summary, the authors' key contributions and objectives are:
- To propose **Human-Gated DAGGER (HG-DAgger)**, a novel DAGGER variant specifically designed for effective human-expert interaction, emphasizing uninterrupted human control during interventions.
- To develop a **data-driven method for learning a safety threshold** for a model-uncertainty-based risk metric, ensuring its practical utility and meaningfulness.
- To **demonstrate the efficacy** of HG-DAgger on an autonomous driving task (both simulated and real-world), showcasing improved sample efficiency, greater training stability, and more human-like behavior compared to existing methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HG-DAgger introduces a novel gating function that is controlled directly by the human expert, fundamentally changing the interaction paradigm compared to traditional DAGGER.

**Core Control Loop (Human-Gated Gating Function):**
Unlike DAGGER, which stochastically switches control between the expert and novice, HG-DAgger allows the novice policy (`π_N`) to control the system during rollouts until the human expert (`π_H`) observes that the novice has entered an unsafe region of the state space. At this point, the human expert *manually takes full, uninterrupted control*. The expert then guides the system back to a safe and stable region. Once safety is re-established, the human manually hands control back to the novice. This uninterrupted control for the human is a key differentiator. The human's decision to intervene defines a "permitted set" of states (`P`) where the novice is allowed to operate. The gating function `g(x_t)` is therefore `1` (expert in control) if `x_t` is outside `P`, and `0` (novice in control) if `x_t` is inside `P`.

**Data Collection Strategy:**
Expert action labels are *only collected* and added to the training dataset (`D`) during these human "recovery trajectories" – i.e., when the human expert has exclusive and uninterrupted control of the system (`g(x_t) = 1`). This ensures that all collected expert data represents high-quality, uncompromised demonstrations, both for nominal behavior (from initial behavioral cloning) and for recovery maneuvers. The dataset `D` is initialized with samples gathered via behavioral cloning (`D_BC`) to provide a starting point for the novice.

**Risk Metric (Doubt) using Neural Network Ensembles:**
To assess the novice's confidence and potential risk, HG-DAgger employs a "doubt" metric, inspired by ENSEMBLEDAGGER. The novice policy (`π_N`) is represented as an **ensemble of neural networks**. This ensemble approach efficiently approximates a Gaussian process, allowing the system to estimate not only the mean output (the action) but also the variance around that output.
- The **covariance matrix** (`C_t`) of the ensemble's outputs given an observation (`o_t`) provides a measure of policy confidence.
- The "doubt" (`d_N(o_t)`) is defined as the `L2-norm` of the main diagonal of `C_t`. High doubt indicates regions of the state space that are poorly represented in the training data, where the ensemble members disagree significantly, or which are intrinsically riskier (as the expert might have avoided them).

**Learning the Safety Threshold (`τ`):**
A critical innovation of HG-DAgger is its data-driven approach to learning the safety threshold (`τ`) for the doubt metric. Instead of being set arbitrarily, `τ` is learned from observed human interventions.
- During training rollouts, whenever the human expert decides to intervene and take control, the novice's doubt (`d_N(o_t)`) at that exact moment is recorded in a "doubt intervention logfile" (`I`).
- The threshold `τ` is then computed as the mean of the final 25% of entries in `I`. This strategy ensures that `τ` is learned from interventions made when the novice policy is more mature and better resembles the fully trained policy, providing a more relevant and robust threshold.
- This learned `τ` can then be used at test-time to understand and assess the performance of the final trained policy, potentially indicating regions where the policy might fail or where a fallback controller should be engaged.

**Experimental Setup:**
The methodology was evaluated on an **autonomous driving task** involving an ego vehicle navigating a two-lane road with stationary obstacles, requiring lane keeping and obstacle avoidance.
- **Environments:** Experiments were conducted both in a **simulation environment** (using a bicycle model for vehicle dynamics) and on a **physical test vehicle** (an MG-GS vehicle provided by SAIC, equipped with LiDAR and high-fidelity localization) with simulated obstacles.
- **Comparison Baselines:** HG-DAgger's performance was compared against **Behavioral Cloning (BC)** and the standard **DAGGER algorithm**.
- **Training Protocol:** All methods were initialized with a BC policy trained on 10,000 expert labels. They were then refined over five additional training epochs, each involving the collection of 2,000 more expert labels. DAGGER's `β` parameter was initialized at 0.85 and decayed by a factor of 0.85 per epoch.
- **Observations and Actions:** The novice policy received observations including distances from the median, orientation, speed, and distances to lane edges and leading obstacles. It outputted steering angle and speed commands.
- **Performance Metrics:** Key metrics included **road departure rate** and **collision rate** (both per meter), and the **Bhattacharyya distance** to quantify the similarity of the learned policy's steering angle distribution to human driving data.
- **Safety and Risk Evaluation:** Additional experiments in simulation and on the test vehicle specifically analyzed the utility of the learned doubt metric and `τ`. This involved evaluating performance when initialized inside vs. outside the estimated "permissible set" (`ˆP`, defined by `d_N(o_t) ≤ τ`) and assessing the pixel-wise correspondence between `ˆP` and "free space" in the environment.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental results consistently demonstrate the superior performance of HG-DAgger compared to both Behavioral Cloning (BC) and standard DAGGER, particularly in the context of human-in-the-loop imitation learning.

**A. Simulation: Learning Performance**
- **Faster and More Stable Learning:** HG-DAgger exhibited significantly faster and more stable learning curves for both road departure and collision rates (per meter). It quickly converged to lower error rates and maintained them, indicating robust learning.
- **DAGGER's Instability:** In contrast, standard DAGGER showed instability and an increase in error rates in later training epochs. The authors hypothesize that as DAGGER's `β` parameter decayed (giving more control to the novice), the perceived actuator lag and degraded feedback negatively impacted the human expert's ability to provide high-quality, unbiased action labels, leading to a deterioration in the collected data and subsequent policy performance. Behavioral Cloning consistently performed the worst, as expected, due to its inherent limitations.

**B. Simulation: Safety and Risk Evaluation**
- **Utility of Doubt Metric and Learned Threshold:** The evaluation of the learned doubt metric and threshold (`τ`) was highly compelling. When the novice policy was initialized in states within the estimated permissible set (`ˆP` – where doubt was below `τ`), its performance was vastly superior compared to initializations outside `ˆP` (`ˆP'`).
 - The mean collision rate was **12 times lower** inside `ˆP`.
 - The mean road departure rate was **20 times lower** inside `ˆP`.
 - The average duration of road departures was less than **half** when initialized inside `ˆP`.
- These results strongly validate the effectiveness of novice doubt as a model-free risk approximator and demonstrate that HG-DAgger successfully learns a `τ` that can effectively distinguish between similar states that vary significantly in their inherent riskiness for the learned policy.

**C. Test Vehicle: Driving Performance**
- **Superior Real-World Performance:** On the physical test vehicle, HG-DAgger achieved **zero collisions and zero road departures** over a fixed set of test configurations. This outperformed both DAGGER (1 collision, 1 road departure) and Behavioral Cloning (1 collision, 6 road departures).
- **More Human-like Behavior:** The steering angle distribution induced by the HG-DAgger policy was **21.1% closer** to the human driving data, as measured by Bhattacharyya distance, compared to DAGGER. This suggests that HG-DAgger's approach leads to policies that not only perform well but also exhibit behaviors more akin to the human expert.
- **Qualitative Observations:** Visualizations of test trajectories corroborated the quantitative results, showing HG-DAgger maintaining safer distances from road edges and avoiding deviations, qualitatively appearing superior. While the limited real-world test data implies a need for further statistical validation, these results provide strong heuristic evidence of HG-DAgger's real-world potential.

**D. Test Vehicle: Safety and Risk Evaluation**
- **Meaningful Learned Threshold:** Further analysis validated that the learned `τ` provides a meaningful characterization of safe versus unsafe regions in the workspace. Visualized risk maps demonstrated that the map generated with the learned `τ` accurately represented risky areas, whereas slightly higher or lower thresholds led to inaccurate or overly conservative risk characterizations.
- **Near-Optimal Threshold:** Quantitative analysis across 40 randomly generated obstacle configurations showed that the `τ` learned by HG-DAgger was **near-optimal** for pixel-wise classification of free space vs. occupied space across various performance metrics (e.g., F1 score, balanced accuracy). This confirms that the data-driven method for learning `τ` effectively identifies a threshold that balances safety and practicality.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The HG-DAgger algorithm represents a significant step forward in the field of imitation learning, particularly for applications involving human experts in safety-critical domains. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Effective Human-in-the-Loop Learning:** The most profound impact of HG-DAgger is its direct and innovative solution to the challenges of interactive imitation learning with human experts. By providing the human expert with *uninterrupted control* during interventions, HG-DAgger addresses the core issues of perceived actuator lag, degraded feedback, and altered human behavior that plague stochastic shared control methods like standard DAGGER. This dramatically improves the quality of collected expert demonstrations, leading to more robust and reliable learned policies. This paradigm shift makes interactive learning significantly more practical and less frustrating for human operators.

2. **Enhanced Safety and Training Stability:** HG-DAgger demonstrably improves training-time safety by minimizing the exposure of the system to an incompletely trained novice policy. When the novice might make a mistake, the human takes full control, ensuring recovery. The enhanced training stability, evidenced by the simulation results, means more reliable and predictable learning processes, which is crucial for real-world system development where training failures can be costly or dangerous.

3. **Data-Driven Risk Assessment and Management:** The novel approach of learning a safety threshold (`τ`) for a model-uncertainty-based risk metric (`doubt`) directly from human interventions is a major contribution. This moves beyond arbitrary or expert-tuned thresholds, providing a more objective, adaptive, and meaningful way to quantify and understand the learned policy's confidence and potential failure modes. This learned `τ` can be invaluable not only for evaluating the policy but also for potentially integrating into future automated gating mechanisms or hierarchical control systems at test time, where the autonomous system can proactively hand over control or engage a safer backup controller when its own confidence is low. This capability is vital for building trustworthy autonomous systems.

4. **Real-World Applicability, Especially in Autonomous Driving:** The successful validation of HG-DAgger on a complex, real-world autonomous driving task, both in simulation and on a physical vehicle, underscores its immediate applicability. Autonomous driving is a prime example of a safety-critical domain where human expertise is indispensable for training and validation, and where human-AI interaction is paramount. HG-DAgger's demonstrated ability to yield more human-like behavior, along with superior safety metrics, positions it as a promising candidate for advancing the development and deployment of safer self-driving cars.

5. **Broader Impact on Robotics and Autonomous Systems:** While demonstrated in autonomous driving, the principles underlying HG-DAgger are generalizable to other domains requiring human expertise for learning and operation. This includes various forms of robotics (e.g., industrial robots, surgical robots), aerospace control systems, or any scenario where an AI agent learns from and collaborates with human operators in potentially dangerous or complex environments. The framework provides a blueprint for developing more human-compatible and safer interactive learning paradigms across diverse applications.

In conclusion, HG-DAgger addresses a critical practical bottleneck in imitation learning, moving the field closer to developing truly safe, robust, and effectively interactive autonomous systems that can learn efficiently from human experts in high-stakes environments. The combination of a human-centric control strategy and a data-driven risk assessment mechanism makes this work particularly impactful for the future of AI deployment.
