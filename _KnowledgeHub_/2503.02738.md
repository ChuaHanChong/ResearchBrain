# Variable-Friction In-Hand Manipulation for Arbitrary Objects via Diffusion-Based Imitation Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Imperial College London and Princeton University developed an end-to-end learning method using diffusion policies and sim-real co-training to enable variable-friction grippers to precisely manipulate arbitrary objects to continuous target poses on real hardware. This approach achieved an average 71.3% success rate across diverse objects with reduced data and training requirements, outperforming prior learning and model-based methods.

## Problem

- Previous variable-friction (VF) in-hand manipulation (IHM) methods relied on hard-coded strategies, limiting manipulation to simple polygonal objects and specific, predefined target poses.
- These earlier approaches required object-specific policies, leading to high engineering effort for diverse objects.
- Training robust IHM policies with reinforcement learning (RL) in simulation demanded substantial computational power and extensive real-world data collection, while behavior cloning (IL) struggled with multimodal action distributions.

## Method

- Developed an end-to-end learning-based method using a diffusion policy imitation learning framework for a variable-friction gripper.
- Implemented a hybrid action space combining discrete high-level operating modes with continuous joint angle changes to control finger movements and friction states.
- Utilized sim-real co-training, mixing a large volume of simulated demonstrations (generated by a smoothness-optimized RL policy with hindsight relabeling) with a small set of real-world demonstrations.

## Results

- The method demonstrated general manipulation capability, achieving an average success rate of **71.3%** across five diverse objects (including irregular polygons and non-polygons) to arbitrary goal poses on real hardware.
- It exhibited data and computational efficiency, reaching a **76.7%** success rate for the Cube object with a total training duration of **5.5** hours, representing a **63.3%** reduction compared to a baseline reward-engineered reinforcement learning policy (15 hours).
- Sim-real co-training substantially improved performance, increasing the average success rate from **32.0%** (simulation-only) and **54.0%** (real-only) to **71.3%** across test objects, with 100 real trajectories more than doubling the success rate compared to using no real data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Diffusion policies are capable of learning high-precision manipulation behaviors even when trained on demonstrations generated by an initial reinforcement learning policy optimized primarily for smoothness rather than exact precision.
> - A sim-real co-training framework effectively bridges the sim-to-real gap, enabling robust real-world performance with significantly reduced real-world data collection requirements.
> - Variable-friction grippers can achieve versatile in-hand manipulation of arbitrary object shapes and target poses on real hardware when integrated with an end-to-end learning approach.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic in-hand manipulation (IHM) aims to enable robots to reorient and reposition objects within their grasp, analogous to human dexterity. This capability is crucial for many everyday tasks but presents significant challenges for robotic systems due to the complexity of contact dynamics and control.

Traditional approaches to IHM often involve dexterous hands with multiple degrees of freedom (DOFs). While these platforms have demonstrated IHM capabilities, they typically entail high mechanical complexity, substantial hardware and maintenance costs, and intricate control challenges.

An alternative approach to dexterity is the use of variable-friction (VF) hands. These grippers, characterized by simpler morphology, are designed to mimic human fingertips by actively switching their finger surfaces between high and low friction states. This allows objects to be either slid or rolled along the finger surfaces, offering a means to achieve manipulation with fewer actuators. Previous work with VF grippers, such as that by Spiers et al., demonstrated robust 2D IHM. However, these methods often relied on hard-coded manipulation strategies, which limited their applicability to simple polygonal objects and restricted target poses where flat surfaces were in contact with the fingers. These approaches also required policies to be specifically tailored for each object.

In parallel, learning-based methods have gained prominence in IHM. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has shown potential but often requires extensive data collection, typically in simulation, and significant computational resources for training. The complexity of reward function engineering in RL, especially for intricate IHM tasks, can also be a limiting factor. Imitation Learning (IL) offers an alternative by leveraging expert demonstrations to train policies, thereby reducing the need for extensive real-world interaction samples and complex reward shaping. Recent advancements in IL, particularly with diffusion policies, have demonstrated efficacy in learning complex manipulation behaviors for various tasks, including IHM with sophisticated multi-finger grippers.

This work integrates the concept of variable-friction manipulation with modern learning-based techniques, specifically diffusion policy imitation learning. It addresses the limitations of prior VF hand manipulation by moving beyond hard-coded primitives and object-specific tailoring. By applying diffusion-based imitation learning with sim-to-real co-training, the research aims to extend the versatility and precision of VF grippers to arbitrary objects and goal poses, while also addressing the data efficiency and computational challenges commonly associated with learning-based robotics. The methodology's focus on uncommon gripper morphologies also positions it as potentially relevant for a wider range of novel robotic hand designs with non-holonomic constraints.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an end-to-end learning-based method that enables a variable-friction (VF) gripper to perform in-hand manipulation (IHM) of arbitrary objects to any target pose on real hardware. This objective is motivated by several limitations observed in previous work with VF hands and the broader context of robotic IHM:

- **Overcoming Limitations of Hard-Coded VF Manipulation:** Previous state-of-the-art methods for VF grippers utilized hybrid planning and visual servoing techniques, often involving offline A\* pathfinding with hard-coded manipulation primitives. These methods faced significant constraints:
 - **Restricted Object Geometry:** Manipulation was limited to simple polygonal objects (e.g., cubes, hexagonal prisms) due to reliance on model-based controllers and predefined contact surfaces. The motivation is to enable manipulation of arbitrary object shapes, including irregular polygons and non-polygons.
 - **Limited Target Poses:** Rotational primitives often only allowed rolling an object from one flat surface to another, restricting target orientations and locations to those where a flat object surface was in contact with a finger. The objective is to achieve precise manipulation to arbitrary initial and target poses within a continuous manipulation range.
 - **Object-Specific Policies:** Policies had to be specifically tailored for each object, requiring significant engineering effort per new object. The motivation is to reduce this overhead, requiring minimal engineering efforts and data collection for diverse objects.

- **Enhancing Data and Computational Efficiency:** Training robust IHM policies via reinforcement learning (RL) in simulation often demands substantial computational power and extended training times. Collecting sufficient real-world data for direct RL is resource-intensive. The research aims to leverage the data efficiency of imitation learning (IL) and combine it with simulation and limited real-world data to achieve robust performance with reduced training time and data collection effort.

- **Leveraging Diffusion Policies for Multimodal Behavior:** Behavior cloning in IL often struggles with multi-modal action distributions inherent in complex manipulation tasks. Diffusion policies have demonstrated an ability to effectively capture such distributions. A key objective is to validate if diffusion policies can learn high-precision manipulation behaviors even from demonstrations generated by an initial, less precise RL policy optimized for smoothness rather than exact precision.

- **Bridging the Sim-to-Real Gap:** Directly transferring policies trained in simulation to the real world remains a challenge. The work aims to develop a co-training framework that mixes real and simulated data to mitigate the sim-to-real gap, thereby improving task success rates and resource efficiency on physical hardware.

- **Demonstrating Applicability to Uncommon Gripper Morphologies:** Beyond the specific VF gripper, the research aims to show that the developed methodology is suitable for other uncommon gripper designs that may have non-holonomic object motion constraints, potentially broadening the impact of the approach.

In summary, the motivation stems from the desire to unlock the full dexterity potential of variable-friction grippers for general-purpose manipulation, moving beyond previous restrictions, while doing so in a data-efficient and scalable manner using modern learning paradigms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology features an end-to-end learning-based manipulation method built upon a diffusion policy-based imitation learning framework. It incorporates co-training from both simulation and real-world data to address challenges related to data scarcity and the sim-to-real gap.

**A. System Overview**

1. **Hardware Setup:**
 - **Variable Friction Hand:** The gripper is a 3D-printed PLA design building on previous work. It consists of two rotary fingers. Two Dynamixel XM-430 servo motors actuate the rotation of these fingers, while two lower-cost Dynamixel XL-320 servos control the friction modes (high or low) via a pulley-cam mechanism.
 - **Control Scheme:** A 'push-pull' strategy is employed. The pulling finger operates in position control mode, moving away from the object. The pushing finger operates in current control mode, applying a constant torque to press the object against the pulling finger. In low-friction mode, objects slide along the finger surface, while in high-friction mode, they typically pivot around the contact point.
 - **Rig and Tracking:** The VF hand is mounted on an aluminum strut frame. An optional transparent acrylic board supports objects during some experiments. Object poses (X, Y location, Z orientation) are tracked using a 1080p webcam and ArUco markers placed on the objects and a reference marker on the hand's base. The tracking system exhibits a mean absolute error of approximately 1 mm with a standard deviation of 0.75 mm.

2. **Simulation Environment:**
 - The simulation environment is constructed using the MuJoCo physics engine and includes the 2-DOF VF hand and various manipulation objects.
 - **Friction and Contact Modeling:** To accurately simulate the friction pad properties, MuJoCo’s soft contact model is hardened by adjusting solver parameters (solref, solimp), utilizing an elliptical friction cone, a Newton solver, and a high impratio. An optional Noslip solver and the MultiCCD flag are enabled for stable contact with meshes, aligning simulation behavior with the real gripper.
 - **Sim-to-Real Gap Mitigation:** Physical, geometric, and dynamic parameters are calibrated through system identification by comparing simulated and real trajectories. Domain randomization is applied to these parameters during policy training and data collection to enhance transferability.

**B. Manipulation Policy Formulation**

The policy formulation is shared between the RL (used for demonstration generation) and IL (final policy) stages.

1. **Observation Space:** The observation `o_t` is a 25-dimensional vector comprising joint states, object states (pose), and goal information. For IL, velocity information is zero-padded to account for discrepancies between simulation and real-world velocity measurements.

2. **Hybrid Action Space:** A hybrid action space `A = A_continuous × A_discrete` is designed for robust manipulation.
 - **Discrete Action Space (`A_discrete`):** Consists of 6 high-level operating modes (Table I). These modes specify the rotation direction of the fingers and the friction state (high-friction (HF) or low-friction (LF)) for each finger, along with their control type (position-controlled (PC) or torque-controlled (TC)). Friction mode switching and finger movements are executed alternately within each action step to maintain object stability.
 - **Continuous Action Space (`A_continuous`):** Specifies a relative change `a_t` in the active finger's joint angle, such that `q_target_t = q_active_t + a_t`. The action range is constrained to [0, 18.9] degrees to limit finger movement velocity and prevent excessive acceleration.
 - The control frequency for the system is 2.5 Hz.

**C. Smooth Manipulation via Reinforcement Learning (for Demonstration Generation)**

A reinforcement learning approach is employed to generate smooth, albeit not necessarily precise, manipulation trajectories. This avoids the extensive reward engineering required for a high-precision RL policy for each object.

1. **Algorithm and Training:** The TD3 algorithm with Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) is used. Training is performed with parallel environments (44 CPU cores) for efficient data sampling. The exploration RL policy is initially trained on a single object (Cube) for approximately 3.5 hours, providing a general manipulation capability. Episodes terminate after 10 action steps if the goal is not reached.

2. **Reward Function:** The reward function is designed to prioritize smoothness and overall task progression rather than exact precision.
 - **Sparse Success Reward:** `c_1 * 1(|∆θ_t| < ¯θ ∧ |∆d_t| < ¯d)` awarded when Euclidean distance `∆d` and angular difference `∆θ` to the target pose are within predefined thresholds `¯d` and `¯θ`.
 - **Dense Task Reward:** `-c_2 * |∆r_t|` where `∆r_t` is the average difference in angular coordinates (distances from finger bases to object position) between the current and goal state, promoting progression along the gripper's characteristic side-to-side movement pattern.
 - **Sparse Penalty:** `-c_3 * 1(Object out of legal range)` applied if the object moves outside the operational area.

**D. Collecting Demonstrations with Hindsight Relabeling**

The smoothness-optimized RL policy is used to collect demonstration trajectories in both simulation and the real world. Hindsight goal relabeling is applied, where the final state of any sampled trajectory is treated as the goal state and the trajectory itself becomes an expert demonstration. This method is effective because it does not require the underlying RL policy to be optimal for arbitrary targets; it only needs to generate smooth, valid manipulations.

**E. Sim-Real Co-Trained Diffusion Imitation Learning**

The core of the proposed method is the diffusion policy, which uses a Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (DDPM) to learn a policy `π(a|o)` from the collected demonstrations.

1. **Diffusion Policy Formulation:** The DDPM approximates the conditional distribution `p(a|o)` by learning to denoise a noisy action sample back to a clean action `a_0`. The diffusion loss `L` (Eq. 2) optimizes a model `ε_θ` to predict the noise added to the action.

2. **Co-Training Framework:** For each object, a dataset is compiled comprising 100 real-world demonstrations (approximately 1 hour of collection per object) and 10,000 simulation demonstrations (approximately 30 minutes of generation per object). The diffusion policy is trained by sampling with equal probability from both the simulated (`D_sim`) and real (`D_real`) demonstration datasets in batches of 1024. Observations are normalized based on real-data statistics.
 - The training objective (Eq. 3, 4) minimizes the diffusion loss on this mixed dataset.
 - This co-training strategy aims to bridge the sim-to-real gap, leverage the scalability of simulation data, and benefit from the realism of limited real-world data.
 - The total training time for the IL policy (after demonstration generation) is approximately 2 hours on an A100 GPU.

**F. Objects Used:** Five distinct objects are used for experiments (Fig. 5): Cube, Hexagon (regular polygons), Star (irregular polygon), Cube Cylinder, and Three-Cylinder (non-polygons). These objects are 3D-printed with geometries identical to their simulated counterparts.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents quantitative results and comparisons to evaluate the proposed variable-friction in-hand manipulation method.

**A. IL Performance Results**

1. **General Manipulation Capability:** The method demonstrates the ability to manipulate five diverse objects (Cube, Hexagon, Star, Cube Cylinder, Three-Cylinder), encompassing regular and irregular polygons, as well as non-polygons, to arbitrary goal poses on the real hardware.

2. **Overall Performance (Table II):** Across all five test objects, the co-trained IL method achieved an average success rate of 71.3% when evaluated on the real VF hand. A successful trial was defined as reaching within 5 mm positional error and 5.73 degrees (0.1 radians) rotational error of the target within a maximum of 10 action steps. For successful episodes, the average positional error was 2.676 ± 1.158 mm, and the average rotational error was 1.902 ± 1.359 degrees. The error levels remained largely consistent across objects of varying complexity, suggesting the errors are likely attributed to inherent system limitations. Manipulations typically required at most 4 friction changes, contributing to fast task completion and reduced hardware wear.

3. **Comparison with Baseline RL Policy (Table III):** The proposed IL method was compared against a reward-engineered RL-only policy for the Cube object.
 - The baseline RL policy required 15 hours of training to achieve a high success rate.
 - The IL method achieved a success rate of 76.7% with a total training duration of 5.5 hours (3.5 hours for exploration RL policy generation + 2 hours for IL policy training), which is approximately 36.7% of the baseline RL training time.
 - The IL method demonstrated a higher success rate (76.7% vs. 66.7%) and comparable precision (3.336 mm / 2.163° for IL vs. 2.094 mm / 3.032° for RL) for successful episodes on the Cube.
 - For manipulating unseen objects, only the 2-hour IL training portion is needed, as the exploration RL policy is shared.

4. **Comparison with Model-based Policy (Table IV):** The IL method was compared against the A\* planner method, a previous model-based approach for variable friction IHM.
 - For 4 pre-specified goal poses (similar to those used in), the IL method achieved lower positional errors (Cube: 3.055 mm; Hexagon: 2.719 mm) compared to the A\* planner (3.800 mm for both).
 - Crucially, the IL method is capable of manipulating objects to arbitrary goal poses, a functionality not supported by the A\* planner due to its reliance on hard-coded primitives and restricted target conditions.

**B. The Effects of Co-Training**

1. **Real-Data Efficiency Ablation Study (Fig. 6):** An ablation study on the Cube Cylinder object investigated the impact of the amount of real-world data used during co-training (0, 100, 200, 300 trajectories, with 100 trajectories collected per hour).
 - The success rate increased with more real data, plateauing after 200 trajectories.
 - Using 100 real trajectories (1 hour of collection) resulted in a 70% success rate, a more than twofold improvement over using no real data (33.3%).
 - Simulation data consistently provided a significant performance boost across all amounts of real data.
 - The highest success rate observed was 93.3% for co-training with 200 trajectories, compared to 73.3% for using only real data with 200 trajectories.

2. **Co-Training vs. Real/Sim Demos Only (Fig. 7):** The efficacy of co-training was demonstrated by comparing it against training with only simulation data or only real-world data.
 - Co-training significantly improved the average success rate across the five objects from 32.0% (simulation only) and 54.0% (real data only) to 71.3%.
 - Co-training also generally reduced the standard deviation of success rates, indicating improved stability and consistency.
 - The Three-Cylinder object was an exception, where the success rate improvement was less pronounced, attributed to mechanical design challenges of the gripper for its specific shape.

3. **Co-Training vs. Fine-tuning (Table V):** A comparison between co-training and fine-tuning (pre-training on simulation data, then fine-tuning on real data) using the Cube Cylinder object (with 200 real data episodes) showed that co-training (93.3% success) outperformed fine-tuning (76.7% success). Fine-tuning resulted in a lower success rate and higher standard deviation, suggesting potential overfitting to the limited real data and reduced generalization.

**C. Failure Cases**

Observed failure modes included instances where compounded errors caused the gripper to miss the target initially, although the policy often recovered within the allowed 10 action steps by performing additional manipulations. A specific failure observed with the Three-Cylinder object involved unintended rolling instead of sliding, attributed to the VF hand's fixed base width, which limits independent control over finger angles and can lead to non-ideal forces. It is suggested that an actuated prismatic palm could mitigate such failures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents several contributions with implications for the field of robotic manipulation, particularly concerning grippers with non-standard actuation principles.

The primary significance of this work lies in **unlocking and extending the in-hand manipulation capabilities of variable-friction (VF) grippers**. Prior VF manipulation methods were constrained by hard-coded primitives, limiting them to simple polygonal objects and specific, achievable target poses. This research demonstrates a learning-based approach that enables the VF gripper to manipulate arbitrary objects, including irregular polygons and complex non-polygonal shapes, to any continuous target pose (both position and orientation) on real hardware. This greatly expands the operational versatility of this gripper morphology.

Another key impact is the **demonstration of data and computational efficiency** in acquiring complex manipulation skills. The proposed diffusion policy-based imitation learning framework, coupled with sim-real co-training and hindsight goal relabeling, achieves high success rates with notably reduced resource requirements. Policies are learned with approximately 2 hours of GPU training and only 1 hour of real-world data collection per object. This efficiency is a substantial improvement over traditional reinforcement learning approaches that often demand tens or hundreds of hours of training and extensive real-world interaction, making the development of new manipulation skills more accessible and scalable.

The methodology's ability to **effectively bridge the sim-to-real gap** through co-training is also significant. By strategically combining a large volume of simulated demonstrations with a small, critical set of real-world data, the approach mitigates discrepancies between simulation and reality, leading to robust and consistent performance on physical hardware. The finding that diffusion policies can learn precise behaviors even from demonstrations generated by an "imprecise" but smooth RL policy highlights the robustness and representational power of diffusion models for complex, multi-modal control.

Furthermore, this work **reduces the engineering effort** typically associated with developing manipulation policies. By employing a learning-based approach, it eliminates the need for extensive, object-specific reward engineering required by previous RL methods or the manual design of manipulation primitives for each object geometry. The use of hindsight goal relabeling simplifies the demonstration collection process, allowing smooth trajectories to be automatically repurposed as expert data.

Finally, the authors suggest that the proposed methodology is **well-suited for other uncommon gripper morphologies with non-holonomic object motion constraints**. This indicates a broader potential impact, offering a robust framework for developing control policies for novel robotic hands that may not fit conventional multi-fingered or parallel-jaw gripper paradigms, thus contributing to the diversification of robotic manipulation hardware and capabilities. Future work focused on creating a general policy that can manipulate arbitrary objects without object-specific training would further amplify these benefits.
