# Lessons from Learning to Spin "Pens"

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from UC San Diego, Carnegie Mellon University, and UC Berkeley demonstrated continuous pen spinning by a robotic hand using a learning-based system. Their approach trains an oracle policy in simulation and then fine-tunes a proprioceptive policy using limited real-world data obtained from replaying oracle trajectories, achieving robust generalization to various unseen pen-like objects with improved success rates compared to pure open-loop replay.

## Problem

- Enabling a robotic hand to perform continuous, dynamic dexterous in-hand manipulation, specifically the challenging task of pen spinning.
- Overcoming the limitations of existing learning paradigms, such as insufficient high-quality real-world demonstrations via teleoperation and a significant sim-to-real gap for direct policy transfer in dynamic contact tasks.
- Achieving generalization of learned manipulation skills to diverse pen-like objects with varying physical properties (mass, length, diameter, friction) using only proprioceptive feedback.

## Method

- Training an 'oracle policy' in randomized simulation using ==reinforcement learning== (PPO) with privileged information to synthesize high-fidelity, dynamic manipulation trajectories.
- Pre-training a proprioceptive sensorimotor policy in simulation using a large dataset of joint positions and actions collected from rollouts of the trained oracle policy.
- Fine-tuning the pre-trained sensorimotor policy with a small, high-quality real-world dataset, which is collected by replaying successful oracle trajectories on the physical robot ('oracle replay') and manually selecting effective instances.

## Results

- The proposed hybrid method enabled continuous pen spinning, outperforming a pure open-loop oracle replay baseline by 10-**30%** in success rate and radians rotated on unseen objects.
- Policies trained with only simulation pre-training or only real-world behavior cloning performed poorly, demonstrating that both simulation pre-training and real-world fine-tuning are critical for success.
- The method successfully generalizes to various unseen pen-like objects with different physical properties, rotating them for multiple revolutions with smooth finger gaiting.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A carefully designed initial state distribution and a 'z-reward' encouraging horizontal object orientation are crucial for training stable oracle policies for dynamic manipulation in simulation.
> - For highly dynamic and contact-rich tasks like continuous pen spinning, direct sim-to-real policy transfer or distillation is largely ineffective due to the substantial sim-to-real gap.
> - Combining diverse motion priors from large-scale simulation pre-training with efficient real-world fine-tuning using limited, high-quality data is essential for robust generalization and cannot be simply replaced by more real-world data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous in-hand manipulation, the ability of a robotic hand to reorient or move an object within its grasp, has been a long-standing challenge in robotics. This paper addresses a particularly difficult instance of this task: the continuous spinning of pen-like objects. This is highly relevant because many everyday tools (e.g., hammers, screwdrivers) are similarly shaped, making this skill foundational for general-purpose robotic manipulation.

Historically, in-hand manipulation has been approached from two main perspectives:
- **Classic Robotics Methods:** These approaches typically rely on precise analytical models of the robot hand and object, often combined with sophisticated control strategies (e.g., open-loop force control, trajectory optimization). While some classic methods have demonstrated impressive feats like object rotation or finger gaiting, they often require accurate object models, struggle with dynamic and contact-rich tasks, and generally lack generalization to unseen objects or varying physical properties. Previous work on pen spinning using high-speed cameras and specialized hardware also falls into this category, suffering from similar limitations.
- **Learning-Based Methods:** More recently, learning-based techniques, predominantly imitation learning from human demonstrations or reinforcement learning (RL) in simulation with sim-to-real transfer, have gained prominence. These methods promise greater generalizability by learning from data rather than explicit models.
 - **Imitation Learning (Teleoperation):** Advances in low-cost teleoperation systems have enabled data collection for various manipulation tasks. However, as the authors point out, current teleoperation systems struggle to collect high-quality, agile, and dynamic demonstrations for tasks like pen spinning due to latency and retargeting errors.
 - **Sim-to-Real Transfer (Reinforcement Learning):** Training policies in randomized simulations and then transferring them to the real world has shown success for tasks like manipulating spherical or cuboid objects. However, the "sim-to-real gap" becomes a major impediment for highly dynamic and contact-rich tasks like pen spinning, making direct transfer or distillation challenging. Previous work in this area has generally not extended to complex, elongated objects.

This paper positions itself at the intersection of these learning-based approaches, explicitly acknowledging and addressing their limitations. It pushes the boundaries of learning-based in-hand manipulation by demonstrating, for the first time, continuous pen spinning in the real world using a learning-based system. The key innovation lies in its hybrid approach that leverages the strengths of simulation (for generating complex behaviors) and real-world data (for fine-tuning and bridging the sim-to-real gap), while circumventing the weaknesses of direct teleoperation or pure sim-to-real transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objective of this research is to enable a robotic hand to continuously spin pen-like objects, a challenging and practical skill. The authors are motivated by several factors:

1. **Advance Dexterous In-Hand Manipulation:** Pen spinning serves as an ideal "testbed" due to its requirement for dynamic balancing, sophisticated finger coordination, and continuous contact interactions. Achieving this task pushes the state-of-the-art in learning-based robotic dexterity.
2. **Overcome Limitations of Existing Learning Paradigms:**
 - **Lack of High-Quality Demonstrations:** Traditional teleoperation systems are insufficient for collecting the complex, dynamic, and continuous trajectories required for pen spinning.
 - **Significant Sim-to-Real Gap:** For highly dynamic and contact-rich tasks, the discrepancies between simulation physics and real-world dynamics are too large for direct policy transfer or distillation to succeed. The authors specifically note that this task "exposes the gap even further."
3. **Generalization to Diverse Objects:** The goal is not just to spin one pen, but to generalize the learned skill to a variety of pen-like objects with different physical properties (mass, length, diameter, friction) using only proprioception as feedback.
4. **Develop a Robust and Practical System:** The aim is to create a deployable system that can robustly execute this skill, paving the way for more general tool manipulation capabilities in robotics.
5. **Derive "Lessons Learned":** Beyond just achieving the task, the authors aim to provide a comprehensive analysis of their design choices and share insights, contributing valuable knowledge to the broader robotics research community.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology is a multi-stage approach that ingeniously combines reinforcement learning in simulation with limited real-world data collection and fine-tuning. An overview is presented in Figure 2 of the paper.

**A. Oracle Policy Training with Reinforcement Learning (in Simulation)**
The first critical step is to train an "oracle policy" in simulation using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). This policy is granted "privileged information" (e.g., ground-truth object pose, velocity, physical properties, fine-grained tactile signals) not available in the real world. The primary goal is not direct real-world deployment, but to synthesize high-fidelity, complex, and dynamic manipulation trajectories that are realistic enough for later use.

- **Observations:** The oracle policy's observation space includes robot joint positions and targets, detailed binary tactile signals from augmented fingertip sensors, fingertip positions, the pen’s full pose and angular velocity, and a PointNet-encoded point cloud of the pen. Crucially, it also includes physical properties of the object (mass, center of mass, friction coefficient, object size).
- **Actions:** The policy outputs relative joint position targets, which are then converted to torques via a low-level PD controller.
- **Reward Function:** The reward encourages continuous rotation around the z-axis, penalizes linear object velocity, deviations from initial joint positions, mechanical work, and applied torque. A novel and critical component is the `r_z` reward, which penalizes the height difference between the highest and lowest points on the pen, effectively encouraging the robot to keep the pen horizontal during rotation. This `z-reward` is found to be essential for stable gaits.
- **Initial State Design:** Unlike previous work that often relies on gravity or simple placement, the authors found that a carefully designed initial state distribution is crucial for exploration and learning this complex task. They manually designed six "canonical grasp" patterns inspired by human pen spinning, adding noise to generate stable initial states. This prevents the policy from getting stuck in local minima where finger gaiting does not emerge.
- **Policy Optimization:** PPO is used with an MLP for both policy and value networks. Extensive domain randomization is applied to perception inputs, physical parameters, and object properties to improve generalization.

**B. Sensorimotor Policy Pre-training (in Simulation)**
The oracle policy cannot be directly deployed due to its reliance on privileged information. Traditional distillation (e.g., DAgger) of the oracle policy into a sensorimotor policy (using only proprioception or visuotactile feedback) was found to be ineffective for this task due to the large sim-to-real gap and the challenging dynamics.

Instead, the authors propose an alternative:
- The trained oracle policy is rolled out in simulation to collect a large dataset of proprioception (joint positions, previous targets) and corresponding actions.
- This dataset is then used to pre-train a proprioceptive policy in simulation. This policy, consisting of a temporal transformer and an MLP, takes 30 historical steps of joint positions and targets as input. The goal is to expose the sensorimotor policy to diverse and dynamic motion priors, making it robust and capable of learning complex sequences, even if the physics mismatch prevents zero-shot transfer.

**C. Fine-tuning Sensorimotor Policy with Oracle Replay (Real World)**
To bridge the substantial sim-to-real gap, the pre-trained sensorimotor policy is fine-tuned using real-world trajectories. The challenge, again, is collecting these high-quality real-world demonstrations.

- **Real-World Data Collection (Oracle Replay):** The critical insight is that while the oracle policy cannot be directly transferred, its *generated trajectories* can be used as an "open-loop controller" on the real robot. The authors select 15 long-duration successful trajectories from the oracle's simulated rollouts. These recorded action sequences are then replayed on the real Allegro Hand with three different training objects.
- **Human-in-the-Loop Selection:** Only trials where the open-loop replay successfully rotates the object by more than 2π (indicating at least one complete finger gaiting cycle) are stored as high-quality real-world demonstrations. This process is repeated until 15 successful trajectories per object (45 total) are collected.
- **Fine-tuning:** This small, high-quality real-world dataset (proprioception and actions) is then used to fine-tune the pre-trained proprioceptive policy. The pre-training provides a strong motion prior, allowing the policy to adapt to real-world physics efficiently with very limited real-world data.

**D. Experiment Setup:**
- **Hardware:** Allegro Hand (4 fingers, 16 DoF) with a 20 Hz control frequency for neural network outputs, and a 333 Hz low-level PD controller.
- **Simulation:** Isaac Gym at 20 Hz control/200 Hz simulation frequency, with 20 simulated binary tactile sensors per fingertip.
- **Objects:** Cylindrical objects with randomized physical properties in simulation. Real-world experiments used 3 training objects (for data collection and training) and 7 unseen test objects, varying in mass, length, diameter, and material.
- **Metrics:** Cumulative Rotation Reward and Duration (simulation); Radians of Rotation (RR.) and Success Rate (Suc., defined as >180 degrees rotation) in the real world.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments provide clear insights into the effectiveness of the proposed multi-stage approach and the necessity of various design choices.

**A. Oracle Policy Training (Simulation Insights):**
- **Importance of Initial State Distribution (Q1):** Training with a single canonical initial pose leads to unstable training and prevents finger gaiting from emerging, getting stuck in local minima (Figure 4 left, Figure 5c). The proposed method with multiple, well-designed canonical grasps is critical for successful learning and exploration.
- **Necessity of Privileged Information (Q2):** The oracle policy *requires* comprehensive privileged information, including detailed visuotactile feedback (tactile signals, point cloud) and object properties (mass, CoM). Without these, the policy does not converge well, indicating the difficulty of the task even in simulation (Figure 4 right). Knowledge of the pen's shape and when to lift fingers is crucial.
- **Impact of Z-Reward (Q3):** The `r_z` reward, which encourages keeping the pen horizontal, is vital for training a stable oracle policy. Policies trained without it result in tilted objects and unstable rotations (Figure 5b), which are impractical for real-world open-loop replay.

**B. Sensorimotor Policy Training (Real-World Deployment):**
- **Oracle Replay as a Baseline (Q4):** Using the oracle's trajectories as a pure open-loop controller ("Oracle Replay") achieves reasonable real-world performance, especially on training objects (37.62-54.29% success rate). However, it is consistently outperformed by the authors' full method, particularly on unseen objects, where the authors' method achieves 10-30% higher success rates and radians rotated. When initialized from random stable grasps (a harder setting), the authors' method shows even more significant improvements (22-40% higher success rate), indicating superior generalization due to simulation pre-training.
- **Failure of Direct Distillation (Q5):** Directly distilling the oracle policy into either a proprioceptive or visuotactile sensorimotor policy (using DAgger, as in prior work) fails for pen spinning.
 - Proprioceptive-only policies struggle to converge even in simulation.
 - Visuotactile policies show reasonable performance in simulation but suffer from a "significant sim-to-real gap" in the real world due to object oscillation and mismatch between simulated and real image distributions. This highlights the extreme difficulty of transferring policies for this dynamic, contact-rich task.
- **Crucial Role of Both Pre-training and Fine-tuning (Q6):** This is a key finding (Table 2).
 - Policies with *only* simulation pre-training ("Only Pretrain") perform poorly in the real world due to the large sim-to-real gap, rarely completing finger gaiting on some objects.
 - Policies with *only* real-world behavior cloning ("No Pretrain") also perform poorly, especially on unseen objects. They tend to overfit to the limited real-world data, demonstrating poor generalization.
 - The proposed method, which combines diverse simulation pre-training with real-world fine-tuning, achieves the best performance across all objects, demonstrating that both components are critical and complementary.
- **Sim-to-Real cannot be Substituted by More Data (Q7):** Increasing the number of real-world demonstrations for the "No Pretrain" baseline (from 15 to 75 trajectories) improves performance, but it saturates and still performs significantly worse than the authors' method, especially on unseen objects (Table 3). This underscores that the diverse motion prior learned from large-scale simulation pre-training is indispensable and cannot simply be replaced by more limited real-world data, as the latter can lead to overfitting.

**C. Qualitative Experiments:**
- The policy successfully rotates various pen-like objects for multiple revolutions with smooth finger gaiting, even significantly out-of-distribution objects (Figure 1), confirming its robustness and generalization.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work marks a significant advancement in dexterous in-hand manipulation, particularly for complex, dynamic, and contact-rich tasks.

- **First Learning-Based Continuous Pen Spinning:** It demonstrates an unprecedented capability, moving beyond the simpler manipulation of spheres or cubes to continuous reorientation of elongated objects. This is a crucial step towards general-purpose manipulation of tools.
- **Novel Sim-to-Real Bridging Strategy:** The paper introduces an effective hybrid approach that bypasses the limitations of direct teleoperation and traditional sim-to-real distillation for highly dynamic tasks. The use of an oracle policy to generate high-fidelity trajectories for *both* simulation pre-training *and* real-world open-loop data collection is a powerful methodology.
- **Efficient Real-World Adaptation:** The finding that a policy pre-trained in simulation can be effectively fine-tuned with as few as 50 real-world trajectories is highly significant. This drastically reduces the real-world data collection burden, a major bottleneck in robotics research.
- **Valuable "Lessons Learned":** The explicit articulation of the "lessons learned" provides a roadmap for future research in challenging manipulation domains. These insights—regarding the critical design of simulation environments (initial states, privileged info, reward shaping), the inherent difficulty of direct sim-to-real transfer for dynamic contact tasks, the continued utility of simulation for skill exploration, and the data efficiency of combined pre-training and fine-tuning—are invaluable contributions to the robotics community.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Tool Manipulation:** This research directly paves the way for robots to manipulate and reorient common tools like hammers, screwdrivers, or brushes, expanding their utility in industrial, service, and domestic settings.
- **Foundation for Complex Dexterity:** The established methodology can be extended to other challenging in-hand manipulation tasks that involve continuous dynamics and intricate contacts.
- **Hybrid Learning Paradigms:** The success of this hybrid sim-to-real approach might inspire new paradigms for robot learning, emphasizing synergistic benefits of simulated exploration and targeted real-world refinement.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations:
- **Limited Axis of Rotation:** Currently, the system only rotates along the z-axis. Extending this to general multi-axis rotation is a promising future direction.
- **Dependence on Initial Stable Grasp:** The work assumes the object is initially placed at a stable grasp position. Integrating advanced grasping techniques or chaining different skills (e.g., grasping then spinning) would enhance generality.
- **Exclusion of Vision/Touch in Deployed Policy:** While the paper identifies bottlenecks for vision and touch in sim-to-real for this task, the authors do not discard their potential utility. Future work could explore how to effectively incorporate these modalities to further improve performance, perhaps by addressing calibration or robust perception challenges.

Overall, "Lessons from Learning to Spin “Pens”" represents a substantial step forward in robotic dexterity, offering both an impressive demonstration of a complex skill and critical methodological insights for future research in learning-based manipulation.
