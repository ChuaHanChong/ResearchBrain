# LARC: Lazy Adaptive Reachability Certification of Robot Manipulator Trajectories

> [!summary] Summary
> LARC certifies planned robot manipulator trajectories in continuous time by bisecting only intervals whose clearance test is inconclusive. Each interval's link occupancy is bounded by a midpoint capsule inflated using exact componentwise Hermite speed maxima. On 160 AgileX PIPER trajectories, it matched every fixed-fine decision using 24.8% of interval evaluations, a 10.28x median paired speedup.

## Problem

- Discrete waypoint collision checks sample the trajectory, so collisions occurring between sampled states can go entirely undetected during execution.
- Reachability certifiers bound motion between states, but uniform time partitions spend equal computation where clearance is already large and refinement is unnecessary.
- Long intervals cut evaluation counts yet yield enclosures too loose to certify, forcing a fixed tightness-versus-cost tradeoff chosen before checking the scene.

## Method

- ==Piecewise-cubic Hermite reconstruction== of recorded joint positions and velocities, with exact per-joint speed maxima found at clipped segment endpoints or interior acceleration roots.
- ==Midpoint-capsule occupancy envelopes==: eleven fitted capsules inflated by a motion radius from calibrated ancestor-joint distance constants times interval half-width and speed bounds.
- **Lazy adaptive certificate tree**: depth-first bisection of intervals failing the margin clearance test, up to depth nine, certifying only if all leaves pass.

## Results

- LARC and fixed-fine both certified **112 of 160** trajectories with zero disagreements, while fixed-coarse certified only **70** under the identical geometric model.
- Interval evaluations dropped to **20328** from **81920** (ratio **0.248**); median runtime **81.8 ms** versus **801.4 ms**, median paired speedup **10.28x**.
- A separate MoveIt/FCL audit over **158051** states flagged **21** colliding direct controls, none certified; **27 of 139** sampled-clear trajectories were conservatively rejected.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Let the safety test itself allocate temporal resolution: refine only where clearance is inconclusive, leaving obstacle-free stretches certified at the root interval.
> - Speedups depend strongly on scene clutter, ranging from 260x in open space to about 3x in cluttered windows, so open-scene numbers overstate benefit.
> - A negative certificate is not a collision proof, and sampled audits cannot validate continuous-time clearance; conservatism traces to capsule enclosure error, not the refinement scheme.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a fundamental challenge in robotic manipulation: ensuring collision-free motion of robot links in continuous time. While robot end-effectors often follow planned paths, the complex geometry and dynamics of the entire manipulator arm mean that collisions can occur between discrete waypoints, even if those waypoints themselves are collision-free. This "gaps between waypoint checks" problem is critical for robot safety and reliable operation in cluttered environments.

Existing approaches to collision avoidance in robotics include:
- **Sensing and Mechanical Adaptation**: Tactile sensors and compliant/underactuated hands offer contact information and adaptability, but do not inherently provide clearance guarantees for upstream links during motion.
- **Reachability Analysis**: This class of methods aims to enclose a link's swept volume over a time interval. If this enclosure can be proven to be separated from obstacles, then the link is guaranteed to be clear throughout that interval. Previous work such as ARMTD, SPARROWS, and CROWS integrates reachable sets directly into online trajectory design or uses specific occupancy models (e.g., spherical, learned predictions).

The current research focuses on **post-planning trajectory certification** using deterministic analytic enclosures. It distinguishes itself from methods that modify trajectories online or design them with reachability in mind. Instead, LARC (Lazy Adaptive Reachability Certification) takes a pre-planned trajectory and efficiently verifies its continuous-time clearance. A key aspect of existing reachability methods is the trade-off between interval length, enclosure tightness, and computational cost: longer intervals reduce evaluations but can lead to loose enclosures, while uniformly short intervals can be computationally wasteful. LARC aims to optimize this trade-off by adaptively allocating computational resources based on local clearance requirements. This positions LARC as a method to improve the efficiency of existing continuous-time certification processes, rather than to develop new occupancy models or planning strategies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient and adaptive method for certifying the continuous-time external-obstacle clearance of robot manipulator trajectories. This certification aims to guarantee that all links of a robot manipulator maintain a prescribed clearance margin from static external obstacles throughout a planned trajectory.

The motivation behind LARC stems from several identified limitations in current robot trajectory validation practices:
- **Insufficiency of Discrete Trajectory Checks**: Standard discrete checks, where only sampled robot states are evaluated for collisions, can fail to detect collisions that occur between these sampled points. This introduces a safety risk, as a seemingly collision-free trajectory based on discrete checks might still collide in continuous time.
- **Inefficiency of Uniform Temporal Partitioning**: While reachability-based certification methods can bound motion between states, applying a uniform, fine-grained temporal partitioning across an entire trajectory is computationally inefficient. Computation is expended even in regions where the robot has ample clearance from obstacles, leading to wasted resources. Conversely, using uniformly coarse partitions might result in loose enclosures that fail to certify clearance, even if the robot is actually clear.
- **Need for Adaptive Resource Allocation**: There is a motivation to develop a certification approach that intelligently allocates computational effort. Specifically, computation should be concentrated on trajectory segments where clearance is tight or uncertain, and reduced where clearance is substantial. This adaptive strategy aims to achieve the same certification rigor as a fine-grained uniform approach but with significantly less computational overhead.

To address these motivations, the authors aim to make three specific contributions:
1. **Develop an adaptive interval certifier**: This certifier employs capsule enclosures for link occupancy, leveraging exact componentwise speed maxima derived from piecewise-cubic Hermite joint trajectories.
2. **Provide a continuous-time external-obstacle clearance guarantee**: This guarantee is conditional upon specified geometric containment properties and static scene assumptions.
3. **Conduct a comparative evaluation**: The method is to be compared against fixed-resolution certifiers on a dataset of robot trajectories, including analysis of generation failures and an audit against a sampled collision checker.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem considers an $n$-joint serial manipulator executing a candidate trajectory $q(t; k)$ over a time horizon $t \in [0, T_h]$. $V_\ell(q)$ represents the geometry of link $\ell$ at configuration $q$. The **forward occupancy** of link $\ell$ over a time interval $I$ is defined as the union of all geometries occupied by the link within that interval: $FO_\ell(I; k) = \bigcup_{t \in I} V_\ell(q(t; k))$.

The method seeks to compute an **outer envelope** $\hat{R}_\ell(I; k)$ for each link, such that it satisfies the containment condition: $FO_\ell(I; k) \subseteq \hat{R}_\ell(I; k)$.
External obstacles $O$ are modeled as static, axis-aligned bounding boxes (AABBs). A positive **clearance margin** $\rho$ is prescribed (e.g., 10 mm in experiments).
The **clearance test** for an interval $I$ is defined as $g(I; k) = \min_\ell d(\hat{R}_\ell(I; k), O) - \rho$, where $d$ is the minimum Euclidean distance between sets. An interval passes the clearance test if $g(I; k) > 0$. If $g(I; k) \le 0$, the interval is considered inconclusive, as the outer envelope might intersect an obstacle even if the actual link does not. A trajectory is certified if all passing leaf intervals collectively cover its entire duration.

#### 3.2. LARC (Lazy Adaptive Reachability Certification)

LARC employs an adaptive strategy to determine the temporal resolution required for certification.

1. **Hermite Trajectories and Speed Bounds**: Each MoveIt trajectory is reconstructed as a piecewise-cubic Hermite curve based on recorded joint positions and velocities. For each joint $j$ within a segment, $q_j(t)$ is cubic, and its derivative $\dot{q}_j(t)$ is quadratic. The maximum absolute speed $|\dot{q}_j(t)|$ over an interval $I$, denoted $\bar{\omega}_j(I)$, is exactly computed by evaluating candidate points (clipped-segment endpoints or interior roots of $\ddot{q}_j(t)$). This bound is exact for the reconstructed polynomial.

2. **Midpoint Capsules and Motion Inflation**: The robot's geometry is modeled using eleven capsules, fitted to enclose the official PIPER collision meshes. For an interval $I = [t_m - h, t_m + h]$ centered at $t_m$, $C_\ell(q(t_m))$ represents the capsule for link $\ell$ at the midpoint configuration. A calibrated constant $a_{\ell j}$ bounds the distance from joint $j$'s axis to any point on capsule $\ell$. The motion radius $b_\ell(I)$ for capsule $\ell$ over interval $I$ is then calculated as $b_\ell(I) = h \sum_{j=1}^n a_{\ell j} \bar{\omega}_j(I)$. The occupancy envelope $\hat{R}_\ell(I; k)$ is generated by taking the Minkowski sum of the midpoint capsule $C_\ell(q(t_m))$ and a ball of radius $b_\ell(I)$: $\hat{R}_\ell(I; k) = C_\ell(q(t_m)) \oplus B(0, b_\ell(I))$. The implementation evaluates segment-AABB distances, adding the capsule radius, $b_\ell(I)$, and $\rho$ to obtain the pass/fail test equivalent to equation (4).

3. **Adaptive Certificate Tree**: The core of LARC is an adaptive binary refinement process.
 - The certification process starts with the root interval $[0, T_h]$ and proceeds depth-first (Algorithm 1).
 - For any interval $I$, if its clearance test $g(I; k)$ is positive, the interval becomes a certified leaf, and the process for that branch terminates.
 - If $g(I; k) \le 0$, the interval is considered inconclusive. It is then bisected into two child intervals $I_L$ and $I_R$.
 - This bisection continues recursively up to a maximum depth $d_{max}$ (set to 9 in the experiments).
 - If an interval remains inconclusive after reaching $d_{max}$, the trajectory is deemed UNCERTIFIED.
 - Both child branches are evaluated before their results are combined, ensuring a complete leaf cover for every trajectory. The certifier does not replan or provide braking logic.

#### 3.3. Fixed-Resolution Baselines

For comparison, two fixed-resolution certifiers are used:
- **Fixed-coarse**: Evaluates 16 uniform intervals across the trajectory.
- **Fixed-fine**: Evaluates 512 uniform intervals across the trajectory (equivalent to LARC's maximum depth $d_{max}=9$).
These baselines use identical trajectories, capsules, speed bounds, obstacles, distance evaluation, and clearance margin as LARC. This setup aims to isolate the effect of temporal refinement strategy on computational cost under a shared certificate model.

#### 3.4. Conditional Safety Analysis

The continuous-time external-obstacle clearance guarantee provided by LARC is conditional on four assumptions:
- **A1: Geometric Containment**: The static capsules accurately contain the link geometry, and the motion bounds correctly establish the containment condition for the swept volume.
- **A2: Static Obstacles**: External obstacles remain fixed throughout the certified horizon.
- **A3: Fixed Clearance Margin**: The clearance margin $\rho$ is fixed in the model space and applied consistently.
- **A4: Scope**: The certificate specifically addresses link-obstacle separation, excluding continuous-time self-collision.

**Theorem 1** states that if the leaf intervals generated by LARC cover the entire trajectory duration and satisfy the containment condition for every link, and if $g(I_r; k) > 0$ for every leaf interval $I_r$, then each link remains more than $\rho$ away from the static obstacles $O$ throughout the entire horizon. The proof relies on the fact that if an enclosing set is clear, any subset contained within it must also be clear. The authors acknowledge that a finite-depth guarantee is not provided, as the gap between the actual mesh and its enclosing capsule can prevent certification even as interval length approaches zero.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Model and Trajectory Pipeline

The study used a six-degree-of-freedom AgileX PIPER model. Trajectories were generated using MoveIt 2 and the OMPL `geometric::RRTConnect` planner, time-parameterized, and reconstructed as piecewise-cubic Hermite curves. These were checked on a 50 Hz grid before acceptance.

#### 4.2. Dataset, Pairing, and Generation Failures

The dataset comprised 160 trajectories derived from 80 independent start-goal pairs across four scenes: open, easy window, medium window, and easy shelf. Each pair yielded an `ompl_planned` trajectory and a `direct_smoothstep` control trajectory. Out of 115 generation attempts, 35 failures occurred (invalid starts/goals, planning failures, Hermite recheck failures), which were recorded but excluded from the 160 trajectories used for certification comparisons. The fixed simulation parameter for the clearance margin $\rho$ was 10 mm.

#### 4.3. Sampled MoveIt/FCL Audit and Computational Timing

A separate audit was performed using MoveIt and the Flexible Collision Library (FCL) on the official collision geometry, checking a 250 Hz time grid and all original waypoints, totaling 158,051 states across the 160 trajectories.
- **Sampled-clear**: No collision detected by the audit at discrete states.
- **FCL-detected false-safe**: A trajectory certified by LARC, but where the audit detected a collision.
- **Conservative rejection**: A sampled-clear trajectory that LARC did not certify.
Computational timing involved running each method three times per trajectory in randomized order, using the median runtime for each method. Paired speedup was calculated as `fixed-fine runtime / LARC runtime` for the same trajectory.

#### 4.4. Decision Agreement and Sampled Audit

- **Certification Counts**: LARC certified 112 trajectories, `fixed-fine` certified 112, and `fixed-coarse` certified 70.
- **Agreement**: LARC and `fixed-fine` exhibited complete agreement on all 160 certification decisions, indicating that LARC achieves the same certification outcome as the `fixed-fine` baseline but with fewer computations.
- **FCL-detected Collisions**: The audit detected collisions in 21 trajectories, all of which were `direct_smoothstep` controls, and none of the `ompl_planned` trajectories. LARC correctly identified all 21 of these trajectories as uncertified, resulting in **zero FCL-detected false-safe trajectories**.
- **Conservative Rejections**: Among the 139 sampled-clear trajectories (i.e., no collision detected by the FCL audit), LARC did not certify 27 of them, resulting in a **conservative-rejection rate of 19.4%**. For OMPL trajectories, this rate was 27.5% (22/80), and for sampled-clear direct trajectories, it was 8.5% (5/59).

#### 4.5. Interval Work and Runtime

- **Interval Evaluations**: LARC performed 20,328 interval evaluations across all trajectories, which represents **24.8% of the 81,920 evaluations** required by `fixed-fine` (a 75.2% reduction).
- **Runtimes**: The marginal median runtime for LARC was 81.8 ms, compared to 801.4 ms for `fixed-fine`.
- **Paired Speedup**: LARC achieved a median paired speedup of **10.28×** compared to `fixed-fine`.

#### 4.6. Scene Dependence

The computational savings of LARC varied depending on the scene's complexity:
- **Open Scene**: All 40 trajectories in the open scene (no obstacles) were certified at the root interval, leading to an evaluation ratio of 0.002 (near-zero evaluations) and a median paired speedup of 260.31×.
- **Cluttered Scenes (Easy Window, Medium Window, Easy Shelf)**: These scenes required more refinement. Evaluation ratios ranged from 0.223 to 0.392, and median paired speedups ranged from 3.14× (medium window) to 11.58× (easy window). The median-window and easy-shelf speedups of 3.14× and 4.18× are considered more representative of cluttered settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The LARC method demonstrates an effective strategy for reducing the computational burden of continuous-time trajectory certification for robot manipulators. Its primary significance lies in its ability to achieve the same certification outcome as a uniformly fine-grained method (`fixed-fine`) but with substantially reduced computational cost. This reduction is attributable to its adaptive temporal allocation mechanism, which focuses computational resources only on trajectory segments where clearance tests are inconclusive.

**Key Impacts:**
- **Enhanced Computational Efficiency**: By using 24.8% of the interval evaluations and achieving a median paired speedup of 10.28× compared to a fixed-fine baseline, LARC offers a more efficient means to verify the continuous-time safety of pre-planned robot trajectories. This efficiency can contribute to faster validation cycles in robot development and deployment.
- **Continuous-Time Safety Guarantee**: LARC provides a conditional guarantee of continuous-time external-obstacle clearance, addressing the limitation of discrete waypoint checks that can miss inter-waypoint collisions. This contributes to improved safety assurances for robot operation in environments with static obstacles.
- **Zero False Positives in Audit**: The absence of FCL-detected false-safe trajectories in the tested dataset indicates that LARC did not erroneously certify any trajectories that the independent audit detected as colliding. This suggests a conservative and reliable certification behavior in the tested scenarios.
- **Applicability to Existing Planners**: As a post-planning certification method, LARC can be integrated with various existing motion planners without requiring modifications to the planning algorithms themselves, thereby extending their safety validation capabilities.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper also acknowledges several limitations, which point to areas for future research and refinement:
- **Conditional Guarantee**: The continuous-time guarantee is conditional on assumptions regarding capsule containment of link geometry, valid motion bounds, and a static obstacle model. Further work could aim to quantify or reduce the uncertainty associated with these assumptions.
- **Conservative Rejections**: The 19.4% conservative rejection rate (sampled-clear trajectories that LARC did not certify) indicates that the geometric model (capsule fitting, ancestor-joint bounds) and the fixed clearance margin can prevent certification even when a trajectory appears clear based on discrete sampling. This suggests that tighter link enclosures or more sophisticated occupancy models might improve certification rates without compromising safety.
- **Scope of Certification**: The current method focuses exclusively on external-obstacle clearance and does not address self-collision, tracking errors, or moving obstacles. Extending the certificate to cover these aspects would significantly broaden its applicability and impact on robot safety.

In conclusion, LARC presents a step towards more efficient and rigorous safety certification for robot manipulators, particularly by optimizing the temporal aspect of reachability analysis. Its impact lies in enabling more practical and reliable deployment of robots in environments requiring continuous collision-free operation, while also highlighting specific avenues for future research to enhance its capabilities.
