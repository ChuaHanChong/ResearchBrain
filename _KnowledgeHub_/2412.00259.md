# One-Shot Real-to-Sim via End-to-End Differentiable Simulation and Rendering

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Yale University developed a one-shot real-to-sim framework that identifies the geometry, appearance, and physical properties of novel rigid objects from a single robot interaction sequence, leveraging an end-to-end differentiable simulator and renderer. This approach demonstrated a 64% reduction in Chamfer distance and 64% reduction in positional error compared to baselines in predicting novel object trajectories in simulation, and successfully inferred occluded object geometry to be physically plausible in real-world scenarios.

## Problem

- Robots need comprehensive world models that can predict both realistic visual observations and physically consistent interactions for effective planning in novel environments.
- Current differentiable simulators typically require known object geometries and appearances, preventing the joint optimization of all object properties from observations.
- Learnable rendering models for geometry and appearance often lack the explicit surface definitions needed for rigid body physics and demand extensive data, making them unsuitable for sparse, online robot learning.

## Method

- A hybrid differentiable object representation combining ==Shape-as-Points (SaP)== for geometry and a 3D grid for appearance, which converts into an ==explicit mesh== for rigid body simulation.
- An ==end-to-end differentiable rigid body simulator== capable of propagating gradients through its physics engine to jointly optimize geometry, appearance, and physical parameters from robot interactions.
- A ==two-stage optimization== strategy that first refines geometry and appearance from a single observation (with a ==foundation model prior==) and then fine-tunes all parameters using a full robot trajectory.

## Results

- In simulation, achieved significantly lower prediction errors for novel trajectories compared to a baseline (e.g., **8.69 mm** unilateral Chamfer distance vs. **24.2 mm**, **15.5 mm** positional error vs. **42.8 mm**).
- Accurately identified physical parameters in simulation, with an average mass identification error of **0.0728 kg** and friction coefficient error of **0.106**.
- In real-world experiments, successfully adapted occluded object geometry to satisfy physics constraints, such as correcting an overly thin object to properly contact the terrain, and achieved an average mass identification error of **0.186 kg**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A hybrid object representation, bridging implicit fields for complex geometry/appearance and explicit meshes for physics, is crucial for end-to-end differentiable simulation.
> - Physics-constrained optimization, guided by sparse robot interaction data, effectively resolves ambiguities in occluded geometry and accurately identifies physical properties like mass and friction.
> - Leveraging a pre-trained 3D foundation model for an initial geometry guess significantly improves the robustness and efficiency of identifying complete and physically plausible object models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper addresses a challenge in robotics concerning the identification of comprehensive world models for task planning and execution in novel environments. An accurate world model requires the ability to render realistic observations and predict consistent physical interactions. The broader research landscape includes several sub-areas that this work seeks to integrate and advance:

Firstly, **differentiable rigid body simulators** have emerged as powerful tools for system identification, robot planning, and policy optimization. These simulators allow for gradient-based optimization of physical parameters such as mass, inertia, and friction coefficients by matching simulated motion to sparse robot observations. However, existing differentiable simulators typically assume known geometries and appearances of objects, limiting their ability to adapt to novel, unstructured environments where these properties are unknown.

Secondly, **learnable geometry and appearance models**, such as Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) and Gaussian Splatting (GS), have advanced the capability to reconstruct 3D geometry and appearance from 2D images. While these methods excel at novel view synthesis and learning detailed object representations, they generally lack clear definitions of rigid object surfaces necessary for physically accurate collision detection in rigid body simulators. Their representations (continuous neural fields or individual 3D Gaussians) are often incompatible with the volumetric representations (e.g., meshes with convex hulls) required by conventional rigid body physics engines. Additionally, these methods often require numerous diverse views of an object, which may not be practical in typical robotic manipulation scenarios.

Thirdly, the field of **world models** in robotics aims to equip robots with an internal understanding of how their actions affect the environment. While some approaches use generative modeling techniques (e.g., variational autoencoders, diffusion models) to learn black-box models from large offline datasets of action-labeled videos, these models can be susceptible to distribution shifts, lack physical consistency, and often cannot provide critical physical information like contact forces. Conversely, approaches that leverage differentiable simulators offer physical consistency and generalizability but have been limited in their capacity to simultaneously identify geometry and appearance.

This paper positions itself at the intersection of these domains by proposing a novel rigid object representation and an end-to-end differentiable pipeline. It aims to overcome the limitations of existing differentiable simulators (by allowing geometry and appearance optimization) and learnable rendering models (by ensuring compatibility with rigid body physics and working from sparse observations). The core contribution is enabling the joint identification of geometry, appearance, and physical properties of rigid objects from sparse online observations, which has been a gap in the existing literature.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop a method for identifying predictive world models for rigid objects from sparse online observations, specifically a single robot action sequence, in novel environments. This entails the joint optimization of an object's geometry, appearance, and physical properties, which is a capability not fully achieved by existing methods.

The motivation for this objective stems from several key challenges and requirements in robotics:
- **Need for comprehensive world models:** Robots require accurate internal models to plan and control their actions effectively. These models must not only predict consistent and accurate physical interactions but also render realistic visual observations (e.g., RGB images from arbitrary viewpoints).
- **Partially observable parameters:** In novel real-world environments, the geometry, appearance (e.g., color, reflectance), and physical parameters (e.g., mass, friction) of objects are typically unknown and only partially observable through sensor data.
- **Limited resources for online learning:** Robots operating in real-world scenarios often have limited time and computational resources, necessitating efficient methods for model identification, ideally from minimal interaction data.
- **Limitations of existing approaches:**
 - **Differentiable simulators:** While effective for identifying physical parameters, they usually presuppose known object geometries and appearances, preventing simultaneous adaptation of all properties.
 - **Learnable geometry and appearance models (e.g., NeRF, GS):** These models struggle with the explicit surface definitions required for robust collision detection in rigid body simulators and typically demand extensive, diverse viewing angles for reconstruction, which is impractical for single-shot robotic interaction.
 - **Generative world models:** Despite advances, these data-driven models often lack physical consistency, generalize poorly to novel scenarios, and cannot provide physical information like contact forces, which are critical for downstream robot tasks.
- **Bridging the simulation-to-reality gap (Real-to-Sim):** The ability to quickly and accurately build a physically consistent world model from real-world observations allows for "real-to-sim" transfer, where a robot can effectively learn a simulator-ready model online for subsequent planning and control.

To address these motivations, the authors aim to introduce a novel rigid object representation compatible with general-purpose rigid body simulators that facilitates the end-to-end differentiable optimization of geometry, appearance, and physics (GAP) parameters from sparse visual and tactile robot observations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology integrates a novel rigid object representation with an end-to-end differentiable simulation and rendering pipeline, enabling the joint optimization of geometry, appearance, and physical properties from sparse robot observations.

**A. Differentiable Object Representation:**
The core of the method is a hybrid object representation designed to be flexible for learning complex properties while compatible with rigid body simulators.
- **Shape Representation:** The method employs Shape-as-Points (SaP), which represents an object's geometry and topology using an oriented point cloud `P = {(p ∈ R^3, n ∈ R^3)}`. These points and their normal directions induce a discrete vector field.
- **Implicit Indicator Field:** A differentiable Poisson reconstruction algorithm is then used to recover an implicit indicator field `χ(x)` from `P`. This field describes the occupancy of the solid geometry (whether a point `x` is inside or outside the object). `χ` is discretized on a uniform 128x128x128 grid, and its solution is efficiently computed via GPU-accelerated Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), with well-defined derivatives.
- **Mesh Generation:** The indicator field `χ` is converted into a triangle mesh `M` using a differentiable marching cubes algorithm. This mesh provides the explicit surface definition required for collision detection in rigid body simulators.
- **Appearance Representation:** To model appearance, the method augments the representation with a 3D grid of appearance properties, `ψ`, at the same resolution as the indicator field. Currently, this grid stores color information. The appearance properties are propagated to the mesh vertices via tri-linear interpolation, enabling differentiable rendering.
- **Parameterization:** The object's physical and appearance parameters are jointly parameterized as `θ ≜ ⟨M (q_i), μ, P, ψ⟩`, where `M(q_i)` denotes mass-inertial properties, `μ` is the frictional coefficient, `P` is the oriented point cloud, and `ψ` is the color field.
- **Terrain Representation:** The terrain is represented as an oriented and colored point cloud `P_t` and rendered with an alpha compositor. Its geometry is assumed flat and completed by fitting a plane of points, with colors interpolated from visible points. Terrain point cloud positions are not optimized, only colors.

**B. Differentiable Simulator:**
The method utilizes a differentiable rigid body simulator that handles unconstrained rigid body dynamics with dry frictional contacts.
- **Equation of Motion:** The standard equation of motion for rigid bodies is used: `M(q_i)¨q_i = C(q_i, ˙q_i) + J_i u_i + J_⊥τ_⊥ + J_∥τ_∥`. This includes generalized mass matrix, centrifugal/Coriolis/gravitational forces, and Jacobian matrices for external, normal, and tangent contact forces.
- **Contact Dynamics:** At each timestep, a mixed Linear Complementarity Problem (LCP) is solved to determine contact forces (`τ_⊥`, `τ_∥`), which then yield accelerations `¨q_i`. These are integrated forward in time to update object configurations (`q_i`, `˙q_i`).
- **Differentiation:** The simulator adopted is based on Strecke et al., which enables differentiation through the LCP solution via a primal-dual method and sensitivity analysis. This allows gradients to propagate back to the Jacobian matrices and ultimately to the object geometric parameters `P`.

**C. World Model Identification:**
The identification problem is formulated as a physics-constrained optimization (Eqn. 1) that minimizes the difference between simulated and observed robot observations over a motion sequence.
- **Two-Stage Optimization:** To mitigate issues arising from poor initial guesses and local minima, a two-stage optimization approach is used:
 - **Stage 1 (Initial Fit):** Focuses on matching the very first observation `⟨t_1, e_{t_1}, f_{t_1}, o_{t_1}, c_{t_1}, m_{t_1}⟩`. The loss function `L(ˆo_{t_1}, o_{t_1})` includes:
 - `L_rgb`: Weighted sum of `l_1` distance and D-SSIM on RGB images (robot masked).
 - `L_depth`: `l_1` distance on depth images (robot masked).
 - `L_pcd`: Unilateral Chamfer distance between the observed object point cloud and the mesh vertices (guides geometry expansion).
 - `L_pen`: Penalizes object mesh penetration into the terrain.
 - `L_balance`: Encourages the object to settle into static equilibrium by penalizing positional changes when simulated without robot actions for `k` steps (guides occluded geometry to contact terrain).
 - `L_reg`: `L_2` norm regularization of SaP points against initial points.
 - `L_smooth`: Laplacian smoothing on the object mesh.
 The combination of `L_depth` and `L_pcd` is crucial to correctly expand/contract geometry.
 - **Stage 2 (Full Trajectory):** Incorporates all timesteps, applying robot controls. It continues to use parts of the Stage 1 loss on the first frame (excluding penetration and balance losses) to maintain geometry and appearance. For subsequent frames, the loss includes `L_pcd` and `L_robot` (squared distance between ground-truth and predicted robot end-effector positions). For computational efficiency, intermediate and the last frames are rendered rather than every frame. Gradients are back-propagated recursively through the rendering equation and simulator.

- **Geometry Prior:** To obtain a reasonable initial guess for the object's geometry and appearance, especially for occluded parts, the method leverages a 3D foundation model, TripoSR. Given a segmented RGB image of the object, TripoSR predicts a 3D mesh. This mesh is then registered to the partial object point cloud (from the first RGB-D image) using RANSAC and scale-aware ICP with Open3D to resolve scale and transformation ambiguities. This provides a strong starting point for the optimization, particularly in guiding the physics-aware completion of occluded geometries.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The method was evaluated through a series of world model identification tasks in both simulated and real-world environments.

**A. Simulation Experiments:**
- **Setup:** Experiments were conducted using the PyBullet simulator with 9 diverse objects: a simple green box and 8 objects from the YCB object dataset (Gelatin, RubiksCube, Spam, TunaCan, Mustard, Bleach, Drill, Sugar). Objects were pushed by a floating spherical robot on a checker-patterned flat surface, observed by a static overhead camera.
- **Trajectories:** 24 pushing trajectories were generated for each object, with one used for training (optimizing the world model) and the remaining 23 for evaluating the optimized model's generalization. Pushes were designed to be diverse, varying in starting locations (8 around the object) and directions (3 at each location). Trajectories moved objects up to 12 cm and 80 degrees over 30 timesteps (`δt = 0.01` s).
- **Comparison:** The proposed method was compared against PhysGS, which represents the world with Gaussian splats and physical particles. PhysGS was adapted to optimize total mass and friction coefficient via grid search for more accurate predictions, rather than just virtual forces.
- **Quantitative Results (Table I):**
 - **Dynamics Parameter Identification:** The proposed method achieved an average mass identification error of 0.0728 kg and a friction coefficient error of 0.106. PhysGS showed significantly higher errors (0.225 kg mass error, 0.400 friction coefficient error).
 - **Novel Trajectory Prediction (averaged across all objects):**
 - Unilateral Chamfer distance: 8.69 mm (Ours) vs. 24.2 mm (PhysGS).
 - Positional error: 15.5 mm (Ours) vs. 42.8 mm (PhysGS).
 - Rotational error: 16.7 degrees (Ours) vs. 31.8 degrees (PhysGS). The rotational error for the proposed method was heavily influenced by the cylindrical TunaCan object (28.0 degrees), where surface point cloud tracking struggled to differentiate rotation.
 - Translational velocity error: 0.0351 m/s² (Ours) vs. 0.436 m/s² (PhysGS).
- **Qualitative Results:**
 - **Trajectory Prediction:** Figure 5 illustrates that the predicted poses from the proposed method, after applying control forces and integrating forward, align closely with the ground-truth object poses across various objects and testing trajectories, indicated by overlaid yellow silhouettes. PhysGS showed poor generalization.
 - **Shape Correction:** Figure 8 (left) demonstrates the method's ability to correct occluded geometry based on physics. For the Box object, the initial guess had a bulge on the underside that would intersect the terrain; the optimization corrected this to a physically plausible shape.
 - **Novel View Synthesis:** The method achieved a mean squared error (MSE) of 0.00225, a structural similarity index measure (SSIM) of 0.965, and a peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) of 26.5 for synthesized RGB images from 10 novel viewpoints. Figure 6 provides qualitative examples for the Drill object, showing good correspondence between ground-truth and predicted RGB images, although with some loss of fine detail not observable from the top view and less sharp terrain textures due to point rendering.

**B. Physical Experiments:**
- **Setup:** Experiments involved a UR5e robot arm with an ATI Gamma F/T sensor and a semispherical pusher, along with a static overhead RealSense D435 RGB-D camera. Three YCB objects were used: power drill (Drill), sugar box (Sugar), and mustard bottle (Mustard).
- **Trajectories:** Similar pushing trajectories were used, with 6 trajectories per object (two different starting locations, 3 push directions each). One trajectory was used for training, and the rest for evaluation. Trajectories involved 48 timesteps (`δt = 0.03` s).
- **Results:**
 - **Mass Identification Error:** An average mass identification error of 0.186 kg was reported. Frictional coefficients were not quantitatively reported due to the specialized setup required for ground truth measurement.
 - **Unilateral Chamfer Distance:** An average unilateral Chamfer distance of 6.10 mm was observed between the observed object point cloud and the predicted object geometry at the last frame of the trajectory.
- **Qualitative Results:** Figure 7 visualizes train and test prediction results for sample trajectories, showing good alignment between the predicted object and robot poses (highlighted with yellow silhouette) and the real-world observations. Figure 8 (right) shows that for the Sugar object, the initial mesh was too thin and did not contact the terrain; the algorithm successfully optimized the occluded geometry to satisfy physics constraints.

**C. Limitations Identified by Authors:**
- Relies on ground-truth object masks.
- Does not consider advanced appearance models, lighting sources, or shadows, which can lead to rendering artifacts.
- Current runtime (under 15 minutes on high-end PC) is not ideal for online robotic applications.
- Struggles with objects whose rotation cannot be uniquely identified from surface point clouds (e.g., cylinders), necessitating future work on texture-based pose tracking.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces a novel methodology that addresses a critical gap in the field of robot learning and control: the ability to simultaneously identify the geometry, appearance, and physical properties (GAP) of rigid objects from sparse online observations. This is a significant advancement for several reasons:

- **Comprehensive World Models:** By enabling the joint optimization of GAP, the method allows for the creation of more comprehensive and accurate internal world models for robots. These models are not only capable of rendering photorealistic novel views but also predicting physically consistent interactions, which is essential for robust robot planning and control in dynamic, novel environments.
- **Online Learning from Sparse Data:** The "one-shot" aspect, meaning identification from a single robot action sequence, significantly enhances the practical applicability of the method. This addresses the constraint of limited time and computational resources often faced by robots in real-world scenarios, making it viable for rapid deployment and adaptation.
- **Bridging Simulation and Reality (Real-to-Sim):** The proposed end-to-end differentiable pipeline facilitates the direct learning of simulation-ready world models from real-world observations. This reduces the simulation-to-reality gap, allowing robots to quickly acquire predictive models that can be used for downstream tasks in a physics-grounded simulator, such as motion planning, collision avoidance, and manipulation strategy development.
- **Integration of Diverse Research Areas:** The work successfully combines advancements from differentiable rigid body simulation, learnable 3D geometry and appearance modeling, and physics-based system identification. By developing a rigid object representation compatible with both differentiable physics and rendering, it overcomes limitations of previous approaches that often focused on these aspects in isolation.
- **Improved Physical Consistency:** Unlike purely data-driven generative world models, this method is grounded in physics, ensuring physical consistency in predictions and the provision of critical physical information like contact forces. This leads to models that generalize better to novel scenarios and are more suitable for practical robotic manipulation tasks.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies several limitations, such as the reliance on ground-truth masks, simple appearance models, current runtime, and challenges with specific object symmetries (e.g., cylinders). Addressing these points opens up avenues for future research, including the identification of multi-body dynamic systems, advanced appearance and lighting models, physics-based perception to refine masks, and more efficient implementations.

In essence, this research provides a foundational framework for robots to learn a predictive, physics-aware understanding of novel rigid objects in their environment with minimal interaction, thereby enhancing their autonomy and capability in unstructured settings.
