# Learning In-Hand Object Reaching to General 6D Poses

> [!summary] Summary
> POISE is a sim-to-real reinforcement learning framework that moves an already-grasped object to arbitrary palm-relative 6D poses using finger motions alone. It combines diverse physics-validated stable-grasp resets, a basis-point-set geometry-conditioned PPO policy, an adaptive rotation and translation goal curriculum, and a wrench-coverage grasp-maintenance reward, raising real three-target sequence success from 20% to 80%.

## Problem

- Learning-based in-hand manipulation mostly targets reorientation, continuous rotation, or constrained translation, leaving joint control of object position and orientation largely unaddressed.
- Model-based in-hand pose control depends on predefined contact modes, fixed contact sequences, or simplified known geometry, so it does not scale to random SE(3) goals.
- Earlier full-pose in-hand reaching stayed object-specific and simulation-only, and single-grasp resets leave alternative contact arrangements underrepresented, hurting generalization and post-drop recovery.

## Method

- ==Diverse stable-grasp initialization==: optimization-based grasp synthesis samples in-hand object poses, optimizes contacts and position commands, then caches only candidates surviving a 2-second gravity simulation.
- ==Geometry-conditioned PPO actor== consumes a 64-dimensional ==basis point set== descriptor plus goal and noisy pose history, trained with an ==adaptive curriculum== expanding limits from 5 degrees and 10 mm to 180 degrees and 30 mm.
- Reward combines staged pose terms, a verified goal-completion bonus, and a ==grasp-maintenance term== from a generalized mean over six bidirectional ==friction-cone wrench margins==, referenced to the episode's initial quality.

## Results

- Diverse initialization lifts held-out-grasp first-target success from **40.1%** to **51.5%** and post-drop recovery from **33.8%** to **72.9%**, cutting mean recovery time from **11.63 s** to **9.45 s**.
- The adaptive curriculum raises full-range strict success from **6.2%** to **59.5%** and completed goals per episode from **0.08** to **1.55** on the hexagonal prism.
- The grasp reward raises wrench quality about **41%** and real three-target sequence success from **2/10** to **8/10**, with planned targets completed rising from **8/30** to **27/30**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reset-state diversity is a training-distribution choice, not something exploration discovers: broad grasp coverage buys both unseen-grasp generalization and recovery after contact is lost.
> - Rewarding balanced wrench coverage rather than absolute grasp force prevents squeeze-harder degeneracy and makes sequential targets feasible where pose reward alone leaves fragile grasps.
> - Curricula should schedule goal magnitude only, keep axes randomized, and track per-object progress; uniform full-range sampling never bootstraps coupled large translations and rotations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

In-hand manipulation concerns changing the pose or configuration of an object while it remains held by a dexterous robotic hand. This capability differs from conventional pick-and-place manipulation, in which the robot typically grasps an object, transports it with the arm, and releases it. In-hand manipulation can reduce the need for repeated grasp acquisition and can allow a robot to expose specific object surfaces, reposition a tool, or align an object for subsequent interaction.

Earlier work established analytical foundations for in-hand manipulation through rolling, finger gaiting, motion-cone analysis, contact-mode planning, trajectory optimization, and model-predictive control. These approaches can explicitly represent contact and physical constraints, but they often require accurate models, predefined contact sequences, or simplified object geometries. Such assumptions become restrictive when the robot must reach arbitrary combinations of object position and orientation.

Learning-based approaches, particularly reinforcement learning, have subsequently enabled contact-rich behaviors such as object reorientation, continuous rotation, and constrained translation. Several systems have also investigated generalization across object shapes through visual or geometric conditioning. However, most of these methods control only part of the object pose. A translation-only controller may move an object along a limited direction, while a reorientation controller may change orientation without independently controlling three-dimensional position.

This paper addresses the combined problem of **6D pose reaching**, in which the object must simultaneously achieve a target three-dimensional position and three-dimensional orientation relative to the palm. The work is also distinct from systems that use coordinated arm-and-hand motion toward world-frame goals. POISE keeps the wrist fixed and uses finger motions alone, making the task specifically one of palm-relative in-hand reconfiguration. Earlier full-pose reaching studies were primarily demonstrated in simulation or for object-specific environments. The paper’s stated contribution is to develop a general sim-to-real framework that handles varied grasps, multiple object geometries, large pose changes, and successive target commands without resetting the grasp.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to enable a multi-fingered dexterous hand to move an already-grasped object to arbitrary commanded poses in the palm frame. The target belongs to the special Euclidean group, \(SE(3)\), and therefore specifies both translation and rotation. The hand is not allowed to release and regrasp the object as a separate action, although the fingers may change contacts or perform finger-gaiting behaviors during manipulation.

The motivation is that many practical manipulation tasks require more than simple rotation or translation. A robot may need to shift its grasp to a different region of a tool, orient a functional axis, expose a surface for insertion or assembly, or place an object into a configuration suitable for interaction with the environment. In these cases, position and orientation are coupled: a motion that improves orientation may worsen the object’s position, and vice versa.

The paper identifies three additional challenges. First, the task begins from an existing grasp, but the initial contact arrangement may vary substantially. A policy trained from one reset grasp may perform well only near that configuration and fail on unseen grasps. Second, object geometry determines how finger movements generate object motion. The same desired pose change may require different contact patterns for a cube, prism, bifrustum, or elongated hammer. Third, reaching a target is not sufficient if the resulting grasp is weak or poorly distributed. A grasp that barely supports the object may fail when the next target is issued or when a disturbance occurs.

POISE is designed to address these issues through five components: diverse stable-grasp initialization, geometry-conditioned control, an adaptive 6D goal curriculum, a grasp-preserving reward, and domain randomization for sim-to-real transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Task Formulation and Policy

The system uses a fixed-wrist, 22-degree-of-freedom Sharpa Wave hand. The object pose is represented relative to the palm. Position error is the Euclidean distance between the current and target palm-relative positions. Orientation error is the geodesic angle in \(SO(3)\), computed using the matrix logarithm.

The policy is formulated as a goal-conditioned partially observable Markov decision process. The actor receives three-frame histories of current and commanded finger-joint positions, estimated object pose and velocity in the palm frame, gravity direction expressed in that frame, the visual pose-estimation confidence, the target pose, translation and rotation errors, and an object-geometry descriptor. The critic receives additional privileged simulator information, including noise-free states, fingertip states, joint torques, and contact forces. This asymmetric actor-critic arrangement allows training with detailed physical information while restricting deployment to observations available on the real robot.

The actor and critic are multilayer perceptrons with hidden layers of 512, 256, and 128 ELU units. PPO is used for reinforcement learning. Actions are normalized incremental changes to commanded finger-joint positions, which are clipped to the allowable joint limits. Simulation runs at 120 Hz, while policy commands are produced at 20 Hz.

A target is considered reached only when position and orientation errors remain below 10 mm and 10 degrees for 20 consecutive control steps. After reaching, a new target is sampled without resetting the hand-object state, so the policy must support multiple successive reaches within one episode.

#### 3.2. Diverse Stable-Grasp Initialization

To broaden the training distribution, the authors generate stable grasp states rather than relying on a single initial grasp. Object poses are sampled within a feasible palm-relative workspace. Stable contacts and corresponding joint configurations are optimized, and randomized contact searches produce multiple postures for each object pose.

Each candidate is perturbed in object pose, joint configuration, and command state, then simulated for two seconds under gravity and position control. Only candidates that retain the object are placed in a reset cache. This physics-based filtering produces a collection of executable, stable starting states. The reported reset caches contain 6,842 states in the relevant ablation experiments.

This procedure is intended not only to improve generalization across initial grasps but also to expose the policy to states resembling post-drop conditions, in which the object rests on the palm without an established grasp.

#### 3.3. Geometry-Conditioned Control

POISE uses a 64-dimensional Basis Point Set descriptor to represent object geometry. A fixed set of query points is defined in each object’s canonical frame, and each descriptor entry records the normalized distance from a query point to the nearest object surface.

The geometry descriptor is supplied to the actor alongside the state and target. A single policy is trained jointly across objects instead of assigning a separate policy to each object or using only a categorical object identity. Consequently, the policy must infer how geometry affects feasible contacts and finger-object motion.

#### 3.4. Adaptive Goal Curriculum

The full task involves rotation changes up to 180 degrees and translation changes up to 30 mm. Sampling such goals from the beginning makes exploration difficult because large coupled pose changes are unlikely to be achieved by an untrained policy.

The curriculum begins with maximum rotation and translation magnitudes of 5 degrees and 10 mm. These limits are progressively expanded to 180 degrees and 30 mm. Rotation and translation curricula are maintained separately. Target axes and directions remain randomized, and targets that lie outside the palm workspace or intersect the fixed hand base are resampled.

At each stage, targets are sampled near the current frontier, from the already exposed range, or at the current limit with probabilities of 0.6, 0.3, and 0.1. When frontier success reaches 0.4, the corresponding limit increases by 10 degrees or 10 mm. Each object has independent curriculum progress.

#### 3.5. Reward and Sim-to-Real Design

The reward combines dense pose progress, verified goal completion, grasp maintenance, drop penalties, and actuation regularization. The pose reward uses separate translation and rotation terms. When orientation error is large, translation is weighted more broadly and less strongly, allowing the fingers to reposition contacts. As orientation error decreases, the translation term becomes more precise.

A goal-completion reward is issued only after the object remains within both tolerances for 20 steps. This discourages brief, unstable entries into the target region.

The grasp-maintenance reward is based on approximate contact-wrench coverage. Each active contact is represented by friction-cone wrench rays, and six bidirectional force and torque margins are computed. A generalized mean emphasizes the weakest margin, encouraging balanced support rather than excessive force along only one direction. The reward is relative to the quality of the initial stable reset, allowing different object shapes and grasps to retain their own attainable baseline.

Domain randomization includes object mass, scale, inertia, center-of-mass offset, friction, controller gains, wrist orientation, external disturbances, joint-position noise, object-pose noise, observation delay, and pose-estimation dropouts. During hardware deployment, FoundationPose estimates object pose from an RGB-D camera, and the policy runs at 20 Hz using the same observation and action interface as in simulation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

Diverse stable-grasp initialization improved first-target success on independently generated held-out grasps from **40.1% to 51.5%** for targets involving 30 mm translation and 180-degree rotation. The mean number of completed goals per episode increased from **0.69 to 1.00**.

The effect was larger in the recovery experiment, where the object began resting on the palm without an established grasp. Success in reaching a target that required lifting the object increased from **33.8% to 72.9%**. Among successful trials, mean recovery-and-reaching time decreased from **11.63 to 9.45 seconds**. These results indicate that reset-state diversity helped the policy learn contact reconstruction and recovery behaviors, not merely variation around a nominal grasp.

The adaptive goal curriculum had a substantial effect. Without curriculum training, strict full-range first-target success was **6.2%**, while curriculum training increased it to **59.5%**. For maximum-change targets, strict success increased from **3.4% to 55.3%**. Relaxed near-success rates also improved, from 18.8% to 77.0% for full-range targets and from 13.9% to 72.8% for maximum-change targets. Goals completed per episode increased from 0.08 to 1.55 under full-range evaluation.

The grasp-maintenance reward improved simulated grasp-wrench quality from **0.032 ± 0.002 to 0.045 ± 0.005**. Minimum force margin increased from 0.077 to 0.107, and minimum torque margin increased from 0.036 to 0.047. Episode success rose from **53.7% to 59.1%**. The results suggest that balanced contact support improved without reducing pose-reaching performance.

#### 4.2. Hardware Results

The real-world system was evaluated on a cube, hexagonal prism, square bifrustum, and hammer. The first three objects represented multiple training shape families and sizes, while the hammer tested a more elongated geometry and used an expanded translation curriculum of up to 100 mm.

In uninterrupted random-goal rollouts, the system completed at least five successive targets for each object without manual reset. In user-specified sequences, it reached four hexagonal-prism targets with changes up to 35 mm and 100 degrees, and seven hammer targets with changes up to 93 mm and 180 degrees. Mean reaching times were 3.0 seconds and 2.7 seconds, respectively.

Under three fixed wrist orientations, the system completed 7, 5, and 6 successive goals. Mean reaching times ranged from 5.1 to 7.1 seconds. Average minimum errors across conditions were 5.3–7.4 mm in position and 3.2–4.4 degrees in orientation.

The system also recovered from two manually applied disturbances. The first primarily changed orientation, while the second caused a larger displacement and altered contacts. In both cases, the policy used updated visual pose estimates to reorganize contacts and return the object to the unchanged target.

In the hardware reward ablation, the grasp-maintenance reward increased complete three-target sequence success from **2/10 to 8/10** and planned-target completion from **8/30 to 27/30**. Mean targets reached increased from 0.8 to 2.7, while median reaching time decreased from 6.8 to 4.9 seconds. Pose errors remained comparable, although the grasp-reward policy reported somewhat larger mean position and orientation errors in the table.

### 5. Significance, Potential Impact, and Limitations

The work defines palm-relative 6D reaching as a reusable primitive for dexterous manipulation. Its main significance lies in combining translation and rotation control while preserving a grasp suitable for continued operation. The findings indicate that training-distribution design, progressive goal expansion, and explicit grasp-quality incentives are important for this coupled task.

Potential applications include tool reconfiguration, exposing object surfaces, aligning parts for assembly, and preparing objects for interaction with external environments. The reported hardware demonstrations also provide evidence that a single geometry-conditioned policy can operate across several objects and wrist orientations, although the evaluation remains limited in object count and trial scale.

The paper identifies two principal limitations. POISE specifies the desired object pose but not a desired hand posture or contact configuration. It may therefore produce effective but unnatural or task-inappropriate finger arrangements. In addition, the simulation-to-reality gap remains evident because of imperfect contact modeling and visual 6D pose-estimation errors. The authors propose incorporating point-cloud observations, tactile sensing, real-world adaptation, and residual dynamics learning as future directions. These extensions could improve robustness and allow the controller to account for both object-level goals and contact-level manipulation requirements.
