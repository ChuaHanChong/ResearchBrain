# Diffusion-Informed Probabilistic Contact Search for Multi-Finger Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Diffusion-Informed Probabilistic Contact Search (DIPS) integrates a diffusion model and discriminator with A* search and trajectory optimization to plan complex, contact-rich multi-finger manipulation. The approach discovers more efficient and robust contact sequences, outperforming training data baselines in simulation and demonstrating improved performance and reliability on a real robotic hand.

## Problem

- Planning complex, contact-rich interactions in multi-finger manipulation is challenging due to high-dimensionality and hybrid dynamics (making/breaking contact).
- Pure classical planning methods face computational intractability when exhaustively searching discrete contact modes with expensive trajectory optimization.
- Pure learning-based methods are sensitive to training data, often only imitate demonstrations, and struggle to enforce hard physical constraints critical for contact tasks.

## Method

- DIPS is a hybrid framework integrating learned generative models with classical graph search and trajectory optimization for multi-finger manipulation.
- It leverages an offline-trained diffusion model as a fast, approximate trajectory predictor and a discriminator to score trajectory realism within an A* search.
- The online phase performs A* search over contact modes, using a particle-based approach to propagate variability, and executes found sequences with a high-fidelity trajectory optimizer and replanning.

## Results

- DIPS significantly outperformed baselines in simulated card manipulation, achieving distances to goal within 0.6 cm, by planning more efficient contact sequences.
- In simulated screwdriver turning, DIPS increased screwdriver rotation by 12% over fixed sequences and 23% over methods without variability propagation, by planning sequences not present in training data.
- The method successfully transferred to hardware, turning a screwdriver 41% further and demonstrating higher robustness (10/10 successful trials) than baselines by strategically planning contact modes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining learned generative models with classical planning enables efficient exploration of discrete contact sequences while maintaining physical constraint satisfaction.
> - The hybrid approach can discover contact mode sequences that are more efficient and robust than those present in the initial training demonstrations.
> - Propagating uncertainty from the learned diffusion model using a particle-filter inspired method and a discriminator is crucial for robustness in search and execution.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of multi-finger manipulation faces significant challenges due to its high-dimensionality and the hybrid nature of dynamics induced by making and breaking contact. Robots must intelligently sequence discrete contact modes while simultaneously optimizing continuous trajectories. This paper positions itself at the intersection of two prominent paradigms in robotics: **learning-based methods** and **classical planning/optimization methods**.

**Learning-based methods**, particularly recent advances in generative modeling (like diffusion models) and reinforcement learning, have shown promise for learning complex manipulation policies. These methods offer flexibility and can reduce the need for extensive domain knowledge. However, their primary limitations include sensitivity to the quality and diversity of training data, a tendency to generate policies similar to demonstrations, and potential struggles with the sensitive constraints inherent in contact-rich tasks. Diffusion Policy, cited and used as a baseline, exemplifies this data-driven approach.

**Classical planning methods**, such such as trajectory optimization and graph search, excel at incorporating explicit domain knowledge in the form of constraints and can achieve high-quality, optimal trajectories given a predefined contact mode or schedule. However, planning entire contact sequences in high-dimensional, multi-finger scenarios becomes computationally intractable due to the combinatorial explosion of discrete contact modes and the computational cost of solving trajectory optimization problems in a search loop. Prior work, such as that by Natarajan et al., has explored interleaving graph search with trajectory optimization but often without the acceleration provided by learned models for approximating the expensive optimization step.

This paper, "Diffusion-Informed Probabilistic Contact Search (DIPS)," addresses a critical gap by proposing a **hybrid approach that combines the strengths of both paradigms while mitigating their weaknesses**. It leverages data-driven generative models (diffusion models) to *accelerate and inform* the search process of classical planning methods (A* search with trajectory optimization). By using a diffusion model as a fast, approximate proxy for trajectory optimization within the search, DIPS overcomes the computational bottleneck of pure planning. Simultaneously, by embedding this learned model within a structured search (A*) and grounding the final execution in a robust trajectory optimizer (CSVTO), it overcomes the limitations of pure learning in terms of constraint satisfaction and the ability to generalize beyond training data.

Furthermore, a key contribution to the broader landscape is DIPS's explicit mechanism for **reasoning about variability and uncertainty** in the learned diffusion model's output. Learned models, by their nature, can generate unrealistic or low-quality samples. DIPS introduces a particle-filter inspired method with a learned discriminator to estimate trajectory likelihoods and propagate variability estimates through the search, enhancing robustness—a crucial aspect for real-world robotic deployment. This probabilistic reasoning differentiates it from simpler learning-based initializations for planning or purely deterministic model approximations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop a robust and efficient method for planning complex, contact-rich interactions in multi-finger manipulation tasks. The authors identify several specific motivations and objectives that drive the development of DIPS:

1. **Overcome the High-Dimensionality and Hybrid Dynamics of Multi-Finger Manipulation:** Multi-finger hands can make or break contact in numerous ways, leading to discrete changes in system dynamics. Sequencing these contact modes is a central challenge for successful manipulation.
2. **Combine Strengths of Learning and Classical Planning:**
 - **Leverage Learning for Flexibility and Approximation:** Utilize generative models (diffusion models) to learn the distribution of valid trajectories given specific contact modes and initial states. This allows for flexible policy generation and serves as a computationally efficient proxy for expensive trajectory optimization during search.
 - **Incorporate Classical Planning for Constraint Satisfaction and Optimality:** Retain the rigor of model-based trajectory optimization to ensure constraint satisfaction (e.g., contact forces, kinematics, balance) and achieve high-quality, near-optimal trajectories. Use search (A*) to explore and sequence discrete contact modes, providing a structured way to find solutions.
3. **Address Limitations of Pure Learning Approaches:**
 - Learning methods are sensitive to the quality of training data and tend to generate policies similar to demonstrations. DIPS aims to *outperform* the training data by intelligently planning new contact sequences.
 - Pure learning methods often lack explicit mechanisms for enforcing hard constraints, which are critical in contact-rich tasks where minor constraint violations can lead to failure.
4. **Address Limitations of Pure Planning Approaches:**
 - Planning contact sequences with full trajectory optimization in the inner loop is computationally prohibitive for high-dimensional multi-finger systems. DIPS seeks to make this tractable by using the diffusion model as an "accelerator" for evaluating potential contact mode transitions.
5. **Reason About Variability and Model Error in Learned Components:** Acknowledge that diffusion models, as learned systems, can produce unrealistic or suboptimal trajectories. A key objective is to explicitly model and propagate this variability during the planning process to make the search more robust and less susceptible to low-quality samples. This is achieved through a particle-based representation and a learned discriminator.
6. **Demonstrate Real-World Applicability:** Evaluate the proposed method on challenging simulated and hardware tasks (screwdriver turning, card manipulation) to validate its practical utility and robustness in real-world scenarios.

In essence, DIPS is motivated by the desire to build a manipulation system that is both intelligent (leveraging learned priors) and reliable (satisfying physical constraints and robust to model imperfections), bridging the gap between data-driven flexibility and model-based guarantees.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

DIPS (Diffusion-Informed Probabilistic Contact Search) is a hybrid framework that integrates learned generative models with classical graph search and trajectory optimization. Its core principle is to use a diffusion model to efficiently propose and evaluate trajectories within an A* search, while a discriminator helps to filter and score these proposals, and a high-fidelity trajectory optimizer ensures final execution quality.

The methodology can be broken down into offline and online phases:

**A. Offline Phase: Data Generation and Model Training**

1. **Trajectory Optimization (CSVTO):** The foundation is a sophisticated Constrained Stein Variational Trajectory Optimization (CSVTO) framework, building on previous work by Yang et al.. This optimizer can find optimal robot configurations `q` and control actions `u` for a given *fixed contact mode* `c` over a short horizon `H`.
 - **Extension for Regrasping:** The original CSVTO is extended to handle "regrasping" fingers (those not in contact) by optimizing their motion in free space to reach new target contact points, while ensuring they avoid contact until the final timestep.
 - **State and Control Definition:** States `s` include finger configurations and object pose; controls `u` include differential finger movements and contact forces. These are partitioned for contact, regrasping, and object/environment dynamics.
 - **Cost and Constraints:** The optimization minimizes costs for reaching an object goal `J_g`, smooth trajectories `J_smooth`, and regrasping finger targets `J_r`, subject to joint limits, contact constraints (kinematics, balance, friction), and regrasp avoidance/contact.
2. **Dataset Generation (D):** A dataset `D = {τ_i, c_i}` is created by running CSVTO for various initial configurations and contact mode sequences. These sequences are sampled from a "designed prior" `p(C)` that represents reasonable attempts to solve a task (e.g., turning a screwdriver). High optimization budgets are used to generate high-quality `(trajectory, contact_mode)` pairs.
3. **Diffusion Model Training (M):**
 - **Purpose:** To learn a generative model `p(τ|c, s_0)` that can predict valid trajectories `τ` given a contact mode `c` and initial state `s_0`. This model acts as a fast, approximate proxy for CSVTO during search.
 - **Architecture:** A 1-D U-Net (similar to) is used to diffuse trajectories of dimension `H × (d_s + d_u)`.
 - **Conditioning:** Classifier-free guidance conditions on `c`, and an inpainting approach (like) conditions on `s_0`. Random masking during training improves inpainting performance.
4. **Discriminator Training (Ψ):**
 - **Purpose:** To evaluate the "realism" of diffused trajectories, assigning a score or probability `Ψ(τ, c)` that a given `(τ, c)` pair is similar to the high-quality trajectories in `D`.
 - **Training Data:** The discriminator is trained on `D` (labeled "real") and an equal number of trajectories sampled from the diffusion model `M` (labeled "fake").
 - **Architecture:** A U-Net with a Sigmoid output layer.

**B. Online Phase: Probabilistic Contact Sequence Search and Execution**

1. **A* Search for Contact Sequences:** An A* graph search is performed over discrete contact modes. Each node `n` in the search tree represents a partial contact mode sequence `C`.
2. **Particle-Based Variability Propagation (Algorithm 1):**
 - **Node Representation:** Each node `n` stores a set of `k` "particles," `P`, where each particle is a trajectory diffused by `M`. Each particle also has an associated score `S`, derived from the discriminator `Ψ`. A node is defined as `n = (P, S, C)`. This particle set `P` approximates the distribution `p_C(τ)`.
 - **Edge Expansion:** To expand from a parent node `n_p` to a child node `n` with an appended contact mode `c'`:
 - `k` new trajectories `τ_bar` are diffused from `M(c', P_p^H)`, conditioned on `c'` and the endpoints of the parent's trajectories `P_p^H` to ensure continuity.
 - The discriminator `Ψ(τ_bar, c')` assigns scores to these new trajectories.
 - These scores are accumulated with the parent's scores `S_p`, discounted by `γ^d` (where `d` is the depth) to reflect increasing uncertainty or diminishing influence over time.
 - `k` trajectories are then *resampled* from `τ_bar` based on their normalized scores `S_bar`, forming the new particle set `P` for the child node. This step prunes low-quality or unrealistic trajectories.
3. **Cost and Heuristic Functions:**
 - **Cost-to-Come `g(p_C(τ), C)`:** Calculated as the *expected* cost `J(τ, C)` (from CSVTO's cost function) over the `k` particles `τ` in the node's distribution `p_C(τ)`, weighted by their scores.
 - **Heuristic `h(p_C(τ), c_1:N)`:** A non-admissible heuristic designed to guide the search. It combines the expected terminal cost `ϕ(τ)` (e.g., distance to goal object pose) with the negative log-likelihood of the contact sequence `C` under a 1-step Markov approximation of the initial prior `p(C)`. This encourages exploring sequences that are likely to reach the goal and are consistent with the learned prior, while `p_min` ensures exploration of transitions not in the prior.
4. **Online Execution and Replanning:**
 - Once A* finds a promising contact sequence, the first contact mode is executed.
 - For execution, the actual CSVTO is run, initialized by samples from the diffusion model `M` corresponding to the chosen mode. This learned initialization can reduce CSVTO's runtime budget.
 - After executing each contact mode, the system **replans** the subsequent contact sequence from the current state. This allows DIPS to adapt to execution errors and re-evaluate the optimal path forward.

In essence, DIPS uses the diffusion model and discriminator for a "fast but noisy" evaluation of contact transitions during search, allowing exploration of many contact sequences. The explicit variability propagation addresses the "noise," and the final reliance on CSVTO provides the "ground truth" execution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

DIPS was evaluated on three distinct tasks: simulated tabletop card manipulation, simulated screwdriver turning, and real-world screwdriver turning, using an Allegro multi-fingered hand. The method was compared against several ablations and a Diffusion Policy baseline.

**A. Simulated Tabletop Card Manipulation (Goal: slide card -6cm along y-axis)**
- **Performance:** DIPS significantly outperformed all baselines and ablations, achieving a distance to goal within 0.6 cm. In contrast, "DIPS-Sampled Fixed Sequence" (using the prior) achieved 2.6 cm, and "CSVTO-Sampled Fixed Sequence" and "Diffusion Policy" were within 4 cm.
- **Key Insight:** DIPS's planning capability allowed it to avoid unnecessary regrasps present in the uniformly sampled training data. This demonstrated DIPS's ability to generate more efficient contact sequences that *outperform* those in the training dataset, even when the prior for data generation was simple.
- **Computational Cost:** Diffusion model inference for initialization took ~3.6s, CSVTO ~7s per step, Diffusion Policy ~1.1s. A* planning with DIPS took ~65s on average, significantly faster than if CSVTO were in the loop (~18x faster).

**B. Simulated Screwdriver Turning (Goal: turn screwdriver as far clockwise as possible)**
- **Performance:** DIPS again outperformed all ablations and the Diffusion Policy baseline, turning the screwdriver 12% further than "DIPS-Sampled Fixed Sequence" and 35% further than "CSVTO-Sampled Fixed Sequence."
- **Key Insights:**
 - **Outperforming Training Data:** DIPS successfully planned sequences with multiple consecutive turning modes, which were not present in the demonstration data (which enforced a regrasp after each turn). This highlights the power of combining learning with planning to transcend the limitations of the training distribution.
 - **Benefit of Replanning:** "DIPS-No Contact Replanning" performed worse, demonstrating the importance of replanning to adapt to accumulated errors and re-optimize toward the updated goal (turning as far as possible).
 - **Efficacy of Variability Propagation:** DIPS, with its particle-based variability reasoning, turned the screwdriver 23% further than "DIPS-No variability Propagation" (k=1) and exhibited lower variance in the final yaw angle. This confirmed that explicitly accounting for diffusion model unreliability improves performance and robustness.
 - **Value of Discriminator (Ψ):** "DIPS-Max likelihood" (using Ψ to pick the best single sample) outperformed "DIPS-No variability Propagation," indicating Ψ's utility in identifying higher-quality trajectories. However, DIPS (particle-based averaging) still outperformed "DIPS-Max likelihood," suggesting that overly trusting a single "best" sample from a learned model (which itself has error or might pick "data-like" but suboptimal trajectories) is less robust than a particle-based distribution approximation.
- **Computational Cost:** Diffusion initialization ~3.6s, CSVTO ~7.2s per step, Diffusion Policy ~1.1s. A* planning with DIPS took ~47s on average (~20x faster than CSVTO in search).

**C. Real Screwdriver Turning (Hardware experiment)**
- **Performance:** DIPS successfully transferred to hardware, turning the screwdriver 41% further than "DIPS-Sampled Fixed Sequence" over 10 trials, executing 4 modes.
- **Key Insights:**
 - **Real-World Planning Benefits:** DIPS planned 25% more turning modes and strategically reduced unnecessary or problematic regrasps (specifically, index finger regrasps were less frequent in DIPS plans as they were more prone to errors leading to screwdriver drops).
 - **Improved Robustness:** DIPS showed higher success rates (10/10 valid trials) compared to the ablation (8/10 valid trials, dropping the screwdriver twice). This indicates that DIPS's planned contact sequences are more robust to real-world perception and execution errors.
 - **Sim-to-Real Gap:** While successful, the total turning angle in hardware (74% of simulation) highlighted a sim-to-real gap, partly attributed to imperfect force modeling in CSVTO and hardware limitations. Adjustments to CSVTO's force initialization were necessary.
- **Computational Cost:** Diffusion initialization ~3.1s, CSVTO ~16.1s per step (higher budget for hardware). A* planning with DIPS ~10.1s on average (lower due to fewer modes executed).

Overall, the findings consistently demonstrate that DIPS effectively combines the strengths of learning and planning. It allows for the discovery of more efficient and robust contact sequences, outperforming fixed sequences derived from demonstration data and exhibiting better resilience to errors, especially crucial in hardware.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in "Diffusion-Informed Probabilistic Contact Search for Multi-Finger Manipulation" by Kumar et al. represents a significant advancement in robot dexterous manipulation, particularly for tasks involving complex contact dynamics.

**1. A Novel and Effective Hybrid Paradigm:** The paper successfully demonstrates a powerful synergy between data-driven generative models (diffusion models) and classical planning/optimization techniques (A* search and trajectory optimization). This hybrid approach addresses fundamental limitations of both pure learning and pure planning methods, offering a more robust and efficient solution for high-dimensional, contact-rich problems. This integration strategy—using learning to accelerate and inform planning while using planning to refine and constrain learned behaviors—is a major contribution to the field of intelligent robotics.

**2. Outperforming Training Data:** A key finding is DIPS's ability to plan contact sequences that outperform those found in the training dataset. This is crucial because it moves beyond mere imitation, allowing robots to discover novel and more efficient ways to achieve tasks that might not be evident in human demonstrations or pre-defined priors. This capability significantly enhances the robot's autonomy and problem-solving capacity, reducing reliance on extensive and perfectly curated human demonstrations.

**3. Robustness Through Probabilistic Reasoning:** The explicit modeling and propagation of variability in the diffusion model's output using a particle filter-inspired approach and a learned discriminator is a vital contribution. This mechanism allows DIPS to account for the inherent uncertainties and potential inaccuracies of learned models, leading to more robust and reliable planned sequences, as demonstrated by the improved performance and reduced failure rates in both simulation and hardware. This is particularly important for real-world deployment where model imperfections and unexpected events are common.

**4. Real-World Applicability:** The successful transfer and evaluation of DIPS on a real multi-finger robot performing a challenging screwdriver turning task highlights its practical significance. The ability to enhance task performance (e.g., more turns) and improve robustness (e.g., fewer drops) in hardware makes DIPS a promising candidate for real-world applications in manufacturing, service robotics, and assistive technologies where dexterous manipulation is required.

**5. Accelerated Planning:** By leveraging the diffusion model as an approximate, fast evaluator for trajectories within the A* search, DIPS dramatically reduces the computational cost of planning complex contact sequences. This makes tasks that would be intractable for pure model-based planners computationally feasible within practical time limits.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Enhanced Robot Dexterity:** DIPS contributes directly to advancing the dexterity of robotic systems, enabling them to perform more intricate and adaptable manipulation tasks.
- **Reduced Data Requirements:** By generating high-quality training data through trajectory optimization rather than solely relying on human demonstrations, DIPS offers a more scalable and less labor-intensive approach to developing manipulation skills.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The hybrid framework opens avenues for exploring other generative models for planning, more sophisticated ways to bridge the sim-to-real gap, and methods for automatic discovery of task-relevant contact modes, as suggested by the authors. It also sets a benchmark for integrating learned uncertainty directly into planning.
- **Industrial Applications:** The collaboration with Honda Research Institute underscores the industrial relevance. DIPS could enable robots to perform assembly, maintenance, or inspection tasks requiring fine motor skills and precise tool use.

In conclusion, DIPS represents a significant step forward in robotic manipulation, offering a principled way to combine the benefits of learning and planning. Its ability to outperform training data, reason about model uncertainty, and perform robustly in the real world positions it as an impactful contribution with broad potential for advancing autonomous robots.
