# CORE-Bench: Fostering the Credibility of Published Research Through a Computational Reproducibility Agent Benchmark

> [!summary] Summary
> CORE-Bench is a benchmark of 270 tasks from 90 CodeOcean papers across computer science, social science and medicine. It tests whether AI agents can reproduce published results from code and data at three difficulty levels. A task-specific agent, CORE-Agent, built on AutoGPT with GPT-4o solves 60% of easy tasks but only 21% of hard ones.

## Problem

- Computational reproducibility fails widely across 15 fields even when code and data are shared, and checking it by hand takes expert time and hours per paper.
- Claims that agents will automate scientific research lack a grounded test, since reproducing existing results is a necessary but unmeasured step before novel research.
- Existing agent and coding benchmarks cover ML experiments, research programming and scientific discovery but not the full reproduction pipeline of installing dependencies, running code and extracting results, and sequential evaluation takes days.

## Method

- Benchmark construction: 90 verified ==CodeOcean capsules==, filtered by ten criteria and split 45 train and 45 test, with manually written text and figure questions about outputs of a successful run.
- ==Ladder of difficulty==: Easy gives the code output, Medium gives the ==Dockerfile== and README instructions, Hard gives only the README, testing retrieval, terminal use and ==dependency installation== separately.
- **CORE-Agent** is an AutoGPT variant with ==task-specific prompt changes== and ==output format checks==, and a ==parallel evaluation harness== runs each task in an ==isolated VM==, cutting evaluation from over 20 days to hours.

## Results

- CORE-Agent with GPT-4o scores **60.00%** on Easy, **57.78%** on Medium and **21.48%** on Hard, while AutoGPT with GPT-4o scores **35.56%**, **37.78%** and **6.67%**.
- Task-specific tweaks help weaker models most: GPT-4o-mini on Easy rises from **8.89%** with AutoGPT to **44.44%** with CORE-Agent, and GPT-4o rises from **35.56%** to **60%**.
- Raising the cost limit from **$4** to **$10** lifts GPT-4o on Hard only from **26%** to **31%**; successes average **$0.54** versus **$2.59** for failures. Vision questions get **59.26%** versus **87.88%** for written.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Cheap task-specific scaffolding such as prompts and output checks can transform a generalist agent, so evaluate generalist agents with light adaptation rather than out of the box.
> - More compute does not fix agents that are stuck: failures came from repeated failed dependency installs and did not resolve with a larger budget, so better recovery matters more than a bigger budget.
> - Agents are weak on figure interpretation, on multi-file output retrieval and on R environments, so gains need better visual reasoning and long-horizon environment setup, and agents need web-access guardrails.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses the issue of computational reproducibility within scientific research and the emerging capabilities of AI agents in complex problem-solving. Computational reproducibility, defined as the ability to obtain identical results using the original code and data, is recognized as fundamental to the scientific process. However, numerous studies across various disciplines, including psychology, economics, medicine, political science, life sciences, geoscience, and computer science, have documented a widespread lack of reproducibility. Even when code and data are provided, factors such as unspecified software versions, diverse machine architectures, operating systems, library incompatibilities, and inherent result variance often impede reproduction efforts. Table 1 in the paper summarizes evidence indicating that a substantial proportion of studies across 15 fields fail to be computationally reproducible despite the availability of materials. The process of verifying reproducibility is labor-intensive and requires specialized expertise.

Concurrently, AI agents, particularly those based on large language models (LLMs), have demonstrated advancements in coding tasks and complex problem-solving. While LLMs alone show limited success on real-world coding benchmarks like SWE-bench, compound AI systems (agents) have significantly improved performance. These developments have led to discussions regarding the potential for AI to automate various aspects of scientific research, from idea generation to paper writing. However, the quality and reliability of purely AI-generated research remain subjects of evaluation and concern.

This paper positions computational reproducibility as a prerequisite for more ambitious goals of automating novel scientific discovery. It identifies a gap in existing AI benchmarks, which evaluate agents on tasks such as conducting machine learning experiments, research programming, scientific discovery, reasoning, and citation, but not specifically on the process of computationally reproducing published scientific work. CORE-Bench seeks to fill this gap by providing a specialized benchmark to evaluate AI agents on this specific, foundational research task, thereby contributing to the development of agents capable of augmenting scientific workflows and enhancing research credibility.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce CORE-Bench, a benchmark designed to evaluate the accuracy of AI agents in performing computational reproducibility of published scientific research. This objective is motivated by several factors:

First, there is a documented and pervasive crisis of computational irreproducibility across diverse scientific disciplines. Despite the emphasis on open science practices and the sharing of code and data, a significant number of published studies remain challenging or impossible to reproduce. This undermines the credibility and cumulative nature of scientific progress. The authors cite numerous examples and studies (e.g., Trisovic et al., 2022; Belz et al., 2021) highlighting this issue, even within fields like Machine Learning where reproducibility challenges are actively encouraged.

Second, verifying the computational reproducibility of a paper is a labor-intensive task that often requires substantial domain expertise. This high human cost limits the scale at which reproducibility checks can be performed, contributing to the persistence of irreproducibility. The paper posits that automating this process with AI agents could alleviate this burden.

Third, recent advancements in AI agents, particularly their improved performance on coding tasks and their ability to interact with complex environments, suggest their potential applicability to scientific tasks. However, the development of useful AI agents necessitates benchmarks that are challenging and directly align with real-world problems. The authors argue that while AI agents are proposed for automating novel scientific research, a more fundamental and attainable step is for them to demonstrate the ability to reproduce existing research. Such a capability is considered essential before agents can reliably conduct new research or verify the work of other AI research agents.

Therefore, the motivations converge on the need for a robust evaluation tool. CORE-Bench aims to measure agent capabilities in a crucial real-world scientific task, encourage the development of AI agents that can assist in validating scientific claims, and ultimately improve the overall state of computational reproducibility in academic publishing. The overarching goal is to foster the credibility of published research by enabling more efficient and widespread verification.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The development of CORE-Bench involved the construction of a dataset of tasks, the design of a multi-level difficulty structure, the creation of an evaluation harness, and the selection and adaptation of baseline AI agents for testing.

**Benchmark Construction (CORE-Bench):**
The benchmark, named CORE-Bench (Computational Reproducibility Agent Benchmark), consists of 270 tasks derived from 90 scientific papers. The papers were sourced from CodeOcean.com, a platform known for hosting reproducible research "capsules." Using CodeOcean capsules facilitated benchmark construction by providing a baseline of known reproducible environments, making the process of task creation more efficient than curating papers "in the wild." The 90 papers are distributed across three disciplines: computer science, social science, and medicine, and feature codebases primarily in Python or R. The dataset was split into 45 papers for training and 45 for testing agent performance.

**Capsule Selection Criteria:**
A stringent set of ten criteria was applied to filter the initial pool of 5,090 CodeOcean capsules to ensure the selected tasks were realistic, diverse, and feasible for automated evaluation (Table 2). These criteria included:
- Correlation to a publicly accessible research paper.
- Origin from computer science, medical science, or social science.
- Implementation in Python or R.
- Inclusion of a README file.
- Code execution within 45 minutes on CodeOcean’s hardware.
- Requiring a relatively simple Bash command for reproduction.
- Adequately labeled results (figures, tables, file names).
- Low variance in results upon reproduction.
- Capsule size under 10 GB.
- Local reproducibility of capsule results.

**Task Questions and Modalities:**
For each selected paper, a set of task questions (181 in total across the 90 papers) was manually created based on the outputs generated from a successful reproduction. These questions required agents to extract specific results, such as model accuracies, axis labels from figures, or numerical values from tables. Task questions could be either language-only (e.g., from command line text, PDF text) or vision-language (e.g., interpreting figures, graphs, PDF tables using a vision-language model). A task was considered successfully completed only if all its associated questions were answered correctly.

**Difficulty Levels:**
To assess agents across a spectrum of challenges, three distinct difficulty levels were designed for each paper (Table 3):
- **CORE-Bench-Easy:** The agent is provided with the complete code output from a successful run. The task primarily involves information extraction from these outputs.
- **CORE-Bench-Medium:** The agent receives the Dockerfile required to run the code, along with text-based instructions in a README. The agent must execute the Docker command and then perform information extraction. This level tests shell interaction capabilities.
- **CORE-Bench-Hard:** The agent is given only the README file with instructions. It must install all required libraries and dependencies, determine and execute the correct command for code reproduction, and then perform information extraction. This level represents the most realistic and challenging scenario.

**Baseline Agents:**
Two types of agents were evaluated:
1. **AutoGPT:** A general-purpose agent (Significant Gravitas, 2024). It was minimally modified to include a `query_vision_language_model` tool to process images and minor bug fixes for general utility (e.g., truncating long tool outputs, enabling shell-specific commands).
2. **CORE-Agent:** A task-specific variant built upon AutoGPT. This agent incorporated a programmatic check to ensure correct submission of results in a `report.json` file with specified keys. Additionally, it included specific prompting hints tailored to each difficulty level of CORE-Bench, derived from qualitative analysis of common failure modes on the training set (Table 4).

**Language Models and Cost Constraints:**
Both AutoGPT and CORE-Agent were powered by two underlying large language models: GPT-4o-2024-05-13 and GPT-4o-mini-2024-07-18, to assess performance and cost-accuracy trade-offs. A budget constraint of $4 per task was imposed, terminating agent runs exceeding this limit.

**Evaluation Harness:**
To ensure robust and reproducible evaluation, a dedicated evaluation harness was developed (Figure 5). This system operates by:
- Running each task in an isolated virtual machine (Azure VMs, varying types for GPU/non-GPU tasks) with a fresh environment.
- Utilizing a "Manager" machine to create "Worker" VMs for each (agent, task) pair, upload necessary files, initiate agent execution in parallel, download results upon completion, and delete VMs.
- This parallelization significantly reduces evaluation time (from potentially over 20 days to a few hours).
- Enforcing a clear separation between benchmark and agent code, standardizing hardware access, and preventing agents from interfering with the evaluation environment.
The primary metric for evaluation was task accuracy (pass@1), defined as the proportion of tasks for which all task questions were answered correctly. Average API cost per task was also reported.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of baseline agents on CORE-Bench yielded several findings regarding agent capabilities in computational reproducibility:

**Overall Performance and Room for Improvement:**
CORE-Agent, utilizing GPT-4o as its underlying language model, demonstrated the highest accuracy across all difficulty levels. It achieved 60.00% accuracy on CORE-Bench-Easy, 57.78% on CORE-Bench-Medium, and 21.48% on CORE-Bench-Hard (Table 5). The 21% accuracy on the most challenging tasks, CORE-Bench-Hard, indicates that while progress has been made, substantial scope for improvement remains in automating computational reproducibility.

**Impact of Task Difficulty:**
Agent performance consistently decreased with increasing task difficulty, aligning with the design of CORE-Bench. Tasks on CORE-Bench-Easy, which primarily involve information extraction from pre-generated outputs, were solved with higher accuracy than CORE-Bench-Medium, which requires interacting with a shell via Docker. CORE-Bench-Hard, demanding dependency installation and command determination, proved to be the most challenging, resulting in the lowest accuracy for all agents.

**Effectiveness of Task-Specific Modifications:**
The CORE-Agent, which incorporated minimal task-specific modifications and prompting hints, significantly outperformed the general-purpose AutoGPT. For instance, with a GPT-4o backend, CORE-Agent achieved 60.60% accuracy on CORE-Bench-Easy compared to AutoGPT's 35.56%. This performance gain was even more pronounced with the GPT-4o-mini backend, where CORE-Agent scored 44.44% versus AutoGPT's 8.89% on CORE-Bench-Easy. These results suggest that generalist agents can be adapted to specific tasks with relatively minor adjustments, leading to substantial performance improvements, particularly for less powerful LLMs where guidance is critical.

**Influence of Language Model Strength:**
GPT-4o consistently outperformed GPT-4o-mini across all task levels and both agent architectures, despite having a higher per-token cost. While GPT-4o-mini agents were 3-5 times cheaper per task, GPT-4o achieved higher accuracy even within the same $4 cost limit per task. An increased cost limit to $10 on CORE-Bench-Hard tasks on the train set showed only a modest increase in GPT-4o's accuracy (from 26% to 31%) and no change for GPT-4o-mini. This indicates that while cost limits can affect performance, agents often either succeed quickly or get stuck, even with a larger budget.

**Modality-Specific Performance:**
Agents demonstrated higher accuracy on text-based questions compared to vision-based questions. CORE-Agent with GPT-4o achieved 87.88% accuracy on text-based questions versus 59.26% on vision-based questions for CORE-Bench-Easy. Vision tasks are more challenging due to the need to locate relevant figures among multiple output files and accurately interpret visual attributes from graphs or plots.

**Programming Language Discrepancy:**
Performance on Python-based tasks was significantly higher than on R-based tasks. This difference is attributed to R outputs often being more difficult to parse (e.g., generating full PDF manuscripts) and the more complex and time-consuming nature of installing R package dependencies. The higher proportion of Python tasks in Computer Science capsules might contribute to the observed higher reproducibility rates in that discipline.

**Common Failure Cases (Qualitative Analysis):**
- **CORE-Bench-Easy:** Agents struggled with tasks requiring information extraction from multiple output files, frequently selecting and querying incorrect figures.
- **CORE-Bench-Medium:** AutoGPT occasionally deviated from instructions, attempting manual code reproduction instead of utilizing the provided Docker commands. CORE-Agent mitigated this through specific instructions.
- **CORE-Bench-Hard:** The most common failure involved dependency management. Agents struggled to install correct library versions, often getting stuck in repeated installation attempts or hitting cost limits before resolving version conflicts.
- **Safety Concerns:** In one instance, an agent attempted to browse CodeOcean.com to find missing dependency requirements and create an account. Although limited by the inability to render JavaScript, this highlights the need for robust guardrails to prevent unintended or potentially harmful agent actions during open-ended tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The CORE-Bench benchmark and the findings presented in this paper carry several implications for both scientific research practices and the development of AI agents.

**Addressing the Reproducibility Crisis:** The primary significance of CORE-Bench lies in its direct engagement with the computational reproducibility crisis in science. By providing a tool to evaluate and drive the development of agents capable of reproducing research, the work offers a potential pathway to mitigate a long-standing challenge that undermines scientific credibility and efficiency. The automation of this "burdensome yet routine scientific activity" could free up human researchers' time and expertise for novel investigations.

**Foundation for Advanced AI in Science:** The paper frames computational reproducibility as a crucial prerequisite for AI agents to undertake more complex scientific tasks, such as generating novel hypotheses or designing experiments. By demonstrating progress in this foundational area, CORE-Bench contributes to the necessary infrastructure and evaluation methodologies for building robust and reliable "research agents." It establishes a tangible, measurable step towards a future where AI actively participates in the scientific discovery process, including verifying and improving the output of other AI-driven research.

**Practical Utility for the Scientific Community:** The development of agents proficient in computational reproducibility could have immediate practical benefits. Authors could use such agents to verify their work's reproducibility before submission, enhancing the quality of published research. Independent researchers could more easily replicate existing studies, accelerating scientific validation and building on prior work. Additionally, conference organizers and journal editors could integrate automated checks into their peer-review processes, enabling a more efficient and rigorous assessment of submitted research's reproducibility.

**Guidance for Agent Development:** The baseline results, particularly the 21% accuracy on the hardest tasks, underscore the significant room for improvement in current AI agent capabilities for complex, real-world scientific workflows. The qualitative analysis of failure modes—such as challenges with multi-modal information extraction, nuanced dependency management, and adherence to complex instructions—provides specific directions for future research in AI agent design, prompt engineering, and tool integration. The demonstrated effectiveness of even minimal task-specific modifications also offers a pragmatic approach for enhancing generalist agents' performance on specialized tasks.

**Advancement in AI Benchmarking:** CORE-Bench introduces a novel benchmark that is challenging, grounded in real-world scientific tasks, and features a robust, parallelizable evaluation harness. This infrastructure can stimulate further research in AI agents by offering a standardized and efficient way to measure progress, addressing concerns about contamination and saturation that can affect other benchmarks. The inclusion of multi-modal tasks (text and vision) and diverse programming languages (Python and R) also pushes the boundaries of agent evaluation beyond typical code generation tasks.

In conclusion, CORE-Bench represents a contribution to fostering the credibility of published research and advancing the utility of AI agents in the scientific domain. By establishing a benchmark for computational reproducibility, the authors provide a framework that could lead to more robust scientific validation and pave the way for more sophisticated AI-driven research paradigms.
