# In-Context Learning for Robots: Methods and Applications

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey of in-context learning for robots, where demonstrations, corrections and interaction redirect behavior while deployed neural weights stay fixed. It organizes methods into four interfaces (context-conditioned policies, geometric demonstration transfer, world-model-based control, skill- and agent-based execution) linked by correspondence and memory, and synthesizes fourteen reported comparisons into evaluation controls separating context use, physical transfer and retained-experience benefits.

## Problem

- Prior learning-from-demonstration and ICL surveys organize work by teaching interface or context content, not by how contextual evidence survives physical execution into the resulting action.
- Instruction benchmarks like LIBERO let the scene reveal task identity, so high success can hide weak use of teaching; tested VLAs often continue the scene-associated task.
- Reported gains rarely separate context dependence from stronger prior execution, parameter updates, or retained artifacts, leaving unclear what actually drives improvement or transfer.

## Method

- ==Six-horizon framework (S1-S6)==: programmed control, neural policy learning, history-based adaptation, in-context task learning, physical recursive self-improvement, and collective knowledge evolution across embodiments.
- ==Four-family taxonomy==: ==context-conditioned policies==, ==geometric demonstration transfer==, ==world-model-based control==, and ==skill- and agent-based execution==, with shared ==correspondence== and ==memory== mechanisms connecting evidence across situations and time.
- ==Attribution-control protocol==: checkpoint-by-context crossing, teaching replacement, retain-versus-clear history, adapt-versus-restore weights, and object-substitution tests that report task interpretation and physical completion separately.

## Results

- Information content matters more than quantity: LMAct gains little from **0** to **512** context episodes, while NOLO's scene-preview video lifts Habitat success from **33.58** to **43.65**.
- The execution interface drives transfer: part-wise warping raises mug-rack placement from **11/27** to **23/27**, and RAPID's scene-variant verification raises success from **53.2** to **75.9**.
- Retained experience gives modest gains (Zeva **+10-20 pp**, RTCF **86.4** to **88.4**, FARE **91.5** to **93.2**), whereas LMPC successor training lifts held-out teaching success from **39.4** to **66.3**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Contextual learning works only if task-relevant distinctions survive from evidence to execution; loss at selection, interpretation, realization or reuse each demands different data, representations, or control.
> - Evaluate ICL with meaning-changing versus meaning-preserving context interventions; success in scenes that admit only one plausible behavior cannot show that a robot uses teaching.
> - Unclear requirements call for teaching, uncertain conditions for observation or interaction, and missing motor competence for new skills; the real goal is a successor that learns faster.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot may be able to grasp each part of an assembly and still need teaching to know the required order. A correct destination can still violate a required handle grasp. The robot can possess the motions a task needs and still lack the evidence that tells it which motion, order, contact, or remembered event matters now.

The approach the authors review does not update model weights for every new task. It uses evidence at decision time and asks how the robot turns that evidence into action. This is in-context learning (ICL). A demonstration or interaction redirects the robot's existing competence while its neural parameters stay fixed during deployment. In this framing, the practical bottleneck is not simply a shortage of skills. It is getting task-relevant information to the action that executes it.

The review traces intended effects and prescribed order or contact through execution. It does more than check where an object ends up.

Two side-by-side diagrams. (a) Fine-tuning: robot examples adapt the policy, and task data update parameters. (b) In-context learning: a demonstration conditions the policy and supplies context while parameters stay fixed.

### 2. How evidence becomes action

The review classifies methods by the interface that carries context into action. It describes four families, and each has its own transfer condition:

- **Context-conditioned policy:** the policy infers or retrieves actions. Transfer requires preserving the taught distinction in the generated action.
- **Geometric transfer:** the robot aligns and executes a motion or contact reference. Transfer requires retaining matched geometry that still applies.
- **World-model control:** the robot predicts consequences that guide action. Transfer requires predicting futures the robot can realize.
- **Skill- and agent-based execution:** teaching becomes a skill, program, or tool request for an executor. Transfer requires an executor whose available skills preserve task constraints.

These are interfaces, not a ranking. Two roles cut across all of them:

- **Correspondence** determines which part, phase, contact, or event in the teaching matches the current scene.
- **Memory** keeps relevant evidence available.

The review also separates task intent from physical response. A demonstration can establish order, while interaction can reveal how the body or material responds. In the authors' framing, context matters only when it changes a physically executable decision, rather than merely changing an internal description.

### 3. What the comparisons establish

For context to be useful, training must teach the relationship between earlier evidence and a later action. Plentiful examples alone do not guarantee that.

The review's Behavior Prompting Policy (BPP) drawing comparison illustrates this. Performance is measured by Chamfer error, a pixel-distance measure of mismatch between a generated and a reference drawing. The total is fixed at 10,000 demonstrations, and results are averaged over three seeds:

This is evidence that the task and execution relationships represented in the training set matter. Task count and demonstrations per task change together, so the comparison does not isolate task diversity as the sole cause. It also does not prove physical transfer to unfamiliar robots or objects.

A skeptic might ask whether a policy trained to act from demonstrations beats simply using the demonstration directly. KAT offers a qualitative answer. Its context-conditioned inference is competitive with few examples, while policy training becomes stronger with more. The crossover differs against an image-based diffusion policy. Representation and comparison baseline matter, so there is no universal winner.

The authors also propose evaluation controls; these are not new experiments. Evaluations should measure prescribed order and contact as well as terminal success, because reaching the goal can conceal failure to follow the teaching. They should also report success, teaching effort, response delay, and reuse cost separately, since improvement in one does not establish improvement in the others.

### 4. Preserve the lesson

The contribution is a mechanism-oriented literature review. It is not a new robot system, benchmark, or head-to-head experiment establishing one family as best. Transfer remains conditional: the taught distinction must survive evidence selection, interpretation, physical realization, and reuse. The review's central synthesis is that different failures along that path require different data, representations, or control capabilities.
