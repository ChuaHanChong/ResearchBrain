# VR-DAgger: Immersive VR for Dexterous Data Collection and Uncertainty-Guided On-Policy Correction

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at ETH Zurich developed VR-DAgger, a human-in-the-loop framework that enhances robotic manipulation policy performance by efficiently collecting high-quality data. It leverages immersive VR for dexterous teleoperation and an uncertainty-guided mechanism to target and correct informative failure segments from policy rollouts, improving task success rates and generalization while reducing human supervision effort by around 40%.

## Problem

- Collecting sufficient, high-quality task-specific data for imitation learning in robotic manipulation is time-consuming and prone to diminishing returns due to distribution shift for behavioral cloning policies.
- Standard behavioral cloning suffers from compounding errors when robots encounter states not well-represented in initial demonstrations, hindering robust performance.
- Existing human-in-the-loop methods for policy refinement either require continuous human monitoring or complex, separately trained intervention models, adding overhead.

## Method

- Implemented a human-in-the-loop framework utilizing an immersive VR system for dexterous teleoperation and data collection in an Isaac Lab simulation environment.
- Employed ==Diffusion== Policies for learning visuomotor manipulation skills, predicting action trajectories from visual and proprioceptive observations.
- Introduced an uncertainty-guided active relabeling mechanism that uses Monte Carlo dropout to identify high-uncertainty failure segments for human correction.

## Results

- Both on-policy methods (VR-DAgger and HIL) consistently increased task success rates and generalization capabilities, with VR-DAgger achieving up to **97%** success on 'Drawer Default' and **89%** on 'Valve Hard' configurations.
- VR-DAgger's uncertainty-guided active relabeling significantly improved data collection efficiency, reducing human supervision wall-clock time by approximately **40%** per trajectory compared to full human-in-the-loop monitoring.
- On challenging 'Hard' configurations, VR-DAgger (50 initial + 100 corrective segments) outperformed equivalent offline Behavioral Cloning by 6–**14** percentage points in success rate across various tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - On-policy iterative data collection, particularly through human-in-the-loop correction of failures, substantially improves policy success rates and generalization to varied initial conditions compared to offline behavioral cloning.
> - An uncertainty-guided approach, identifying critical failure segments using Monte Carlo dropout, efficiently directs human supervision to the most informative states, reducing the need for continuous oversight.
> - Immersive VR offers an intuitive and efficient interface for dexterous teleoperation, enabling high-quality demonstration and targeted corrective action during policy refinement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research addresses a bottleneck in robotic manipulation: the collection of sufficient task-specific data for imitation learning. While large behavior models like RT-X and Octo leverage extensive multi-task datasets, consistent performance often requires additional task-specific data. The paper identifies two compounding challenges in data collection: the operational burden and time consumption of real-world data collection, and the diminishing returns of simply accumulating more demonstrations due to distribution shift. Behavioral cloning (BC), a common imitation learning technique, performs well within the demonstrated state distribution but suffers from compounding errors when the robot encounters underrepresented states during execution.

Simulation offers a partial solution by providing instantaneous resets and reducing hardware risk. However, it does not inherently resolve the issue of identifying which specific interactions require human correction. Existing VR-based data collection methods often prioritize offline demonstration collection, which may still involve redundant human effort on low-information states. On-policy human-in-the-loop approaches attempt to mitigate compounding errors but frequently necessitate synchronous human oversight or rely on separately trained intervention classifiers, which add complexity and task-specific training overhead.

VR-DAgger positions itself by seeking to decouple policy execution from continuous human attention. It integrates immersive virtual reality (VR) for dexterous teleoperation with an uncertainty-guided mechanism for selecting informative failure segments. This approach aims to streamline data collection and policy refinement by concentrating human supervision on critical failure cases identified during autonomous policy rollouts, without requiring continuous human monitoring or an auxiliary intervention model. The work builds upon advances in diffusion policies for complex action distributions and leverages Monte Carlo (MC) dropout as a lightweight uncertainty estimation technique. It also utilizes established motion retargeting methods for mapping human hand movements to robotic hands in VR environments, particularly within the Isaac Lab simulation framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of VR-DAgger is to develop a human-in-the-loop framework that efficiently collects high-quality data and iteratively refines robotic manipulation policies, specifically addressing the challenges of data scarcity and distribution shift in imitation learning.

The detailed motivations for this work are:

- **Addressing the Data Collection Bottleneck:** The authors observe that while imitation learning is effective for robotic manipulation, collecting sufficient task-specific data remains a significant impediment. This bottleneck is intensified by the operational difficulty and time-consuming nature of real-world data collection, encompassing setup, object placement, and post-failure resets. Furthermore, simply collecting more data often leads to diminishing returns, as behavioral cloning policies struggle with distribution shift, where small errors accumulate and push the robot into states not covered by the original demonstrations.
- **Decoupling Execution from Human Attention:** A core motivation is to create a supervision loop that allows policies to explore autonomously, including encountering failures, without requiring continuous, synchronous human oversight. The goal is for human operators to intervene only for "short, high-value correction episodes," maximizing the efficiency of expert time.
- **Targeting Informative States for Correction:** The paper highlights that current methods either spend operator time on redundant demonstrations or rely on intervention signals (like failure classifiers) that require additional task-specific training. VR-DAgger aims to selectively identify and present the most informative failure segments to the operator, ensuring human effort is concentrated where the policy's uncertainty is highest or where it fails. This avoids the need for full-rollout monitoring or a separate intervention classifier.
- **Leveraging Immersive VR for Dexterous Control:** For dexterous manipulation tasks, immersive VR is motivated as a natural and intuitive interface. It allows tracked human hand motion to be directly mapped to high-degree-of-freedom (DoF) robot hands, preserving intuitive spatial control and enhancing the user's ability to provide precise demonstrations and corrections.
- **Enhancing Generalization and Robustness:** By actively collecting corrections in states where the policy struggles, the framework aims to improve the policy's ability to generalize to new, unseen conditions and recover from errors, thereby increasing its overall robustness. The evaluation on "Hard" initial configurations, where no direct corrective data is collected, implicitly tests this generalization capability.
- **Creating a Generalizable and Accessible Framework:** The authors intend to release the full framework as open source, motivating the development of a modular, task-agnostic VR application that integrates directly with Isaac Lab. This design allows for compatibility with arbitrary USD scenes and various end-effector morphologies, aiming to support broader community adoption and further research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

VR-DAgger implements a human-in-the-loop imitation learning framework based on an iterative process: a visuomotor policy is trained, rolled out in simulation, and if it fails, the most uncertain segments are identified and presented to a human supervisor for correction. These corrective demonstrations are then added to the dataset for subsequent policy retraining.

The system is structured as follows:

**A. VR System Overview:**
- **Client-Server Architecture:** The framework uses a client-server architecture. The client is an OpenXR application running on a Meta Quest headset, responsible for the user interface, hand/head tracking, and synchronized scene visualization. The server runs Isaac Lab, handles retargeting, policy inference, logging, and asynchronous training/relabeling.
- **Scene Synchronization:** Unlike systems that stream full stereo rendering, the VR client ingests and synchronizes USD scenes, linking them to the simulated environment via lightweight object and robot poses. This reduces network and server load.
- **Communication:** ROS 1/UDP serves as the primary data plane for real-time state exchange (tracked poses to server, robot/scene state to client). A REST interface acts as the control plane for simulation commands (reset, pause, record), episode summaries, and training dashboards.
- **Flexibility:** The system is designed to be compatible with any Isaac Lab environment consisting of rigid bodies and supports various end-effector morphologies (e.g., dexterous multi-finger hands, parallel-jaw grippers).

**B. Teleoperation and Data Collection:**
- **Three Modes of Interaction:**
 1. **Teleoperation:** Tracked human hand motion is retargeted to robot commands to collect initial demonstrations.
 2. **Intervention:** The operator temporarily overrides policy control at critical failure points during policy execution.
 3. **Active Labeling:** The system queries the operator for targeted annotations or corrective segments selected from policy rollouts, without requiring synchronous full-episode supervision.
- **Observation Logging:** The backend logs time-aligned observations and executed actions, including RGB views (wrist-mounted and external cameras), robot proprioception (joint positions), commanded end-effector deltas/joint targets, and episode metadata.
- **Alignment Monitoring and Feedback:** To ensure data quality, the system continuously monitors the alignment between the tracked human hand and the robot end-effector. A visual overlay (green/red) indicates alignment, and recording pauses if the mean keypoint error exceeds a threshold (e.g., 15 cm). Optional vibrotactile feedback via a wearable glove maps simulated contact forces to actuator amplitudes.
- **Motion Retargeting:** This is formulated as an optimization problem (Eq. 1) that maps tracked human hand motion to the robot hand, considering kinematic differences and joint limits. It jointly optimizes robot joint positions ($q_t$) and a global wrist pose ($T_t$) by minimizing a weighted sum of:
 - Differences between human and robot relative keypoint vectors.
 - Differences between human and robot anchor keypoints.
 - Regularization terms for joint position increments ($||q_t - q_{t-1}||_2$) and pose increments ($||\xi_t||_2$), where $\xi_t = \log(T_{t-1}^{-1} T_t)$.
 - Joint limit constraints ($q_l \le q_t \le q_u$).
 The optimization is warm-started and solved on a GPU using NVIDIA Warp for real-time performance.

**C. Policy Learning:**
- **Diffusion Policy:** The imitation learning policy is implemented as a Diffusion Policy, predicting a horizon-H trajectory (H=16) of hand joint and end-effector pose commands.
- **Observations:** The policy takes as input a short history ($n_h=2$ steps) of RGB images and proprioception (hand joint positions, 6D end-effector pose).
- **DDPM-style Objective:** Training involves sampling a diffusion timestep, adding Gaussian noise to the ground-truth trajectory, and training the model to minimize the Mean Squared Error (MSE) between the predicted and true noise residual. Orientations are represented using a continuous 6D rotation parametrization.

**D. Uncertainty-Guided Active Relabeling:**
- **MC Dropout for Uncertainty:** For failed episodes, policy uncertainty is estimated at each timestep using Monte Carlo (MC) dropout. This involves performing N=10 stochastic forward passes through the diffusion policy with dropout enabled during inference.
- **Uncertainty Score:** The uncertainty score ($u_t$) at each timestep is calculated as the mean predictive variance across action dimensions in the normalized action space (Eq. 3).
- **Segment Selection:** For failed rollouts, the three peak uncertainty timesteps ($t^*_k$) are identified. A 2-second context window centered at each $t^*_k$ is extracted as a short video snippet.
- **Human Correction:** These three snippets are presented to the supervisor in VR. The supervisor selects the most critical segment and provides a corrective demonstration by taking over teleoperation control from that timestep until the end of the episode.
- **Dataset Update and Retraining:** The resulting corrective segment is appended to the training dataset, and the policy is retrained after a pre-defined number of data samples (e.g., 50) have been added. This focuses human effort on high-uncertainty failure modes without full-rollout monitoring.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of VR-DAgger was conducted on three dexterous manipulation tasks—Pan (pick-and-place), Drawer (drawer opening), and Valve (valve rotation)—using a 10-DoF XHand. Each task was tested under two difficulty levels: "Default" (standard initial configurations) and "Hard" (greater variation in object placement and initial hand pose).

**A. Experimental Setup:**
- **Data Collection Protocol:** An initial 50 demonstrations were collected via VR teleoperation. Subsequently, two rounds of uncertainty-guided active relabeling added 50 corrective segments per round, leading to a maximum total of 150 demonstrations (50 initial + 100 corrective). All data collection and active relabeling occurred under "Default" difficulty, making "Hard" results a measure of generalization.
- **Compared Configurations:**
 - **Behavioral Cloning (BC):** Trained solely on offline demonstrations (50, 100, or 150 demonstrations) collected through VR teleoperation, without active relabeling.
 - **Human-in-the-loop (HIL) Inspection:** Used VR-DAgger for iterative on-policy data collection. Supervisors watched full rollouts of failed episodes and manually decided where to intervene.
 - **Corrective (VR-DAgger):** Used the proposed uncertainty-guided active relabeling to present high-uncertainty snippets for targeted correction.
- **Metrics:** Task Success Rate (%), Subtask Success Rate (%), Task Completion Time (mean simulation time for successful episodes), and Data Collection Time (mean wall-clock time per collected trajectory). All policies were evaluated after 1200 training epochs across 256 parallel environments for three different seeds.

**B. Policy Performance:**
- **Active Relabeling Improves Performance:** Both `Corrective` and `HIL` configurations consistently improved success rates over `Behavioral Cloning` across all tasks and difficulty levels. This improvement is attributed to the on-policy collection exposing the policy to failure regimes and allowing it to learn recovery behaviors, addressing distribution shift.
 - On "Default" difficulty, `Corrective` pushed `Drawer` to 97% (from 87% BC 50 demos), `Pan` to 54% (from 49% BC 50 demos), and `Valve` to 97% (from 84% BC 50 demos).
- **Generalization to Hard Configurations:** The positive effect extended to "Hard" configurations, despite no corrective data being collected under these conditions.
 - On `Valve Hard`, `BC` (50 demos) achieved 54%, while `Corrective` (50 + 50) reached 89% (an increase of 35 percentage points, pp).
 - On `Drawer Hard`, `BC` achieved 53%, and `Corrective` (50 + 50) reached 86% (+33 pp).
 - `Pan Hard` showed a modest lift (18% to 22%) with `Corrective` (50 + 50), increasing to 36% (+18 pp from BC 50) with 100 corrective segments, indicating that long-horizon tasks may require more extensive failure-regime coverage.
- **Corrective vs. HIL:** At lower label budgets (50 initial + 50 corrective), `Corrective` sometimes outperformed `HIL` (e.g., `Drawer Default`: 97% vs. 91%; `Valve Hard`: 89% vs. 83%; `Drawer Hard`: 86% vs. 84%), suggesting that targeted snippet selection can be sufficient. At a larger budget (50 initial + 100 corrective), `HIL` slightly outperformed `Corrective` on `Pan Default` and `Pan Hard`, but the differences were modest. The primary conclusion is that `VR-DAgger` (Corrective) approaches the performance quality of full human oversight while offering significant efficiency gains.
- **Subtask Analysis:**
 - For `Pan`, subtask success rate (grasp success) significantly exceeded final task success rate, indicating that failures often occur during the later placement phase rather than the initial grasp. `Corrective` relabeling helped narrow this gap more effectively than scaling `BC`, implying that uncertainty-guided snippets preferentially identified placement failures for correction.
 - For `Drawer` and `Valve`, subtask and task success rates were closely correlated, suggesting that the main bottleneck was initiating contact or rotation. Once this was achieved, task completion typically followed reliably.
- **Equal Total Sample Budget Comparison (150 samples):** Comparing `BC 150`, `HIL` (50 + 100), and `Corrective` (50 + 100) showed that both on-policy methods (`Corrective`, `HIL`) consistently outperformed `BC` on "Hard" configurations by 6–14 pp, confirming the value of on-policy exposure to failure states. On "Default" difficulty, `Drawer` and `Valve` performance was largely saturated, but `Pan` showed a meaningful improvement for `Corrective` (+22 pp over `BC 150`).

**C. Data Collection Efficiency:**
- `Corrective` and `BC` required significantly less wall-clock time per trajectory compared to `HIL` across all tasks (approximately 40% reduction on average).
 - `Pan`: 12.5 s (Corrective) vs. 20.5 s (HIL)
 - `Drawer`: 9.7 s (Corrective) vs. 17.4 s (HIL)
 - `Valve`: 10.1 s (Corrective) vs. 19.0 s (HIL)
- This efficiency gain for `Corrective` is directly attributed to the active relabeling design, where supervisors review short (2-second) uncertainty-selected snippets rather than monitoring entire rollouts.
- `Corrective` collection times were slightly higher than `Behavioral Cloning` (0.7–2.3 s/sample) due to the overhead of reviewing snippets and taking over from mid-episode states, but this modest overhead was deemed acceptable given the performance improvements.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

VR-DAgger presents a human-in-the-loop imitation learning framework that addresses several key challenges in robotic manipulation data collection and policy refinement. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Improved Data Efficiency and Quality:** The most direct impact is on the efficiency of collecting high-quality data for robotic policies. By using Monte Carlo dropout to identify high-uncertainty failure segments and presenting these as short VR snippets for correction, VR-DAgger concentrates human supervision on the most informative states. This reduces the time burden on human experts compared to methods requiring full-rollout monitoring (approximately 40% less per sample compared to unguided HIL), leading to more targeted and higher-value corrective demonstrations. This efficiency is critical for complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks where failure modes are numerous and difficult to diagnose.
- **Enhanced Policy Robustness and Generalization:** The iterative on-policy correction mechanism allows the policy to learn from its own failures, effectively addressing distribution shift. This leads to policies that are more robust to variations in initial conditions and can recover from errors, as demonstrated by improved success rates on "Hard" difficulty configurations, even without direct supervision in those specific challenging states. This ability to generalize is crucial for deploying robots in varied and unstructured environments.
- **Decoupled Human Supervision:** The framework's ability to decouple policy execution from human attention allows autonomous policy rollouts in simulation without continuous operator oversight. Human intervention is reserved for specific, algorithmically identified moments, which could enable more scalable data collection campaigns. This contrasts with traditional DAgger-like approaches that demand synchronous expert labeling.
- **Generalizable and Accessible Tooling:** The development of a modular, task-agnostic VR application compatible with Isaac Lab and any USD scene provides a flexible platform. This open-source release aims to lower the barrier to entry for researchers and practitioners in robotic learning, enabling easier data collection for diverse robot morphologies and manipulation tasks. Such a tool can accelerate research and development in dexterous manipulation by providing a standardized, efficient method for generating task-specific datasets.
- **Advancement in Active Learning for Robotics:** The application of MC dropout for uncertainty-guided active relabeling in a real-time human-in-the-loop system for dexterous manipulation is a methodological contribution. It offers a lightweight alternative to training separate intervention classifiers, reducing overall system complexity and task-specific overhead.
- **Foundational Work for Sim-to-Real Transfer:** While the current evaluation is limited to simulation, the framework's design within Isaac Lab and its emphasis on efficient, targeted data collection lay groundwork for future work on sim-to-real transfer. The authors indicate future directions include deploying policies on physical hardware and exploiting domain randomization in simulation, which could benefit significantly from this structured approach to data generation.

In summary, VR-DAgger offers a practical and efficient solution for addressing the data bottleneck in imitation learning for robotic manipulation, potentially accelerating the development of more capable and robust autonomous robotic systems.
