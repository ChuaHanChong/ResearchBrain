# PO-PDDL: Learning Symbolic POMDPs from Visual Demonstrations for Robot Planning Under Uncertainty

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University developed PO-PDDL, a symbolic POMDP formulation extending PDDL, and a pipeline to learn these models from visual robot demonstrations. The system enabled 100% task success and superior cumulative rewards on long-horizon real-world manipulation tasks while reducing planning time by over 50% compared to previous methods.

## Problem

- Robot task planning in real-world environments is hindered by pervasive perception and action uncertainties.
- Standard PDDL formulations assume fully observable and deterministic settings, making them inadequate for real-world robotic applications.
- Manually constructing POMDP models is labor-intensive, and learning them from high-dimensional visual robot demonstrations poses significant challenges in state abstraction, stochasticity, and partial observability.

## Method

- A novel symbolic language, **PO-PDDL**, extends PDDL to explicitly model observations, beliefs, stochastic transitions, and partial observability, maintaining LLM-friendly syntax.
- A two-stage learning pipeline constructs complete PO-PDDL domains, including predicates, ==stochastic operators==, and observation functions, directly from visual robot demonstrations.
- Partial observability is identified by analyzing contradictions between inferred symbolic states from reconstructed trajectories and direct visual observations.

## Results

- The system learned complete and correct POMDP models in 291 seconds from 29 visual demonstrations, demonstrating lower learning costs than alternative explicit model-learning approaches.
- PO-PDDL achieved **100%** task success and the highest cumulative rewards across five long-horizon real-world robot manipulation tasks, outperforming all baselines.
- Planning times were reduced by an average of **50.4%** to **85.7%** compared to baselines, attributed to efficient Bayesian belief updates and selective VLM invocations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A unified symbolic POMDP formulation (PO-PDDL) effectively bridges the gap between structured PDDL planning and principled uncertainty modeling, leveraging LLM capabilities.
> - A two-stage learning pipeline, starting with a fully observable domain and then incorporating partial observability based on data-driven inconsistency detection, is effective for learning complex POMDP models from visual demonstrations.
> - Explicitly modeling action constraints and differentiating between passive and active observation types are critical for robust belief-space planning in real-world settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot task planning in real-world environments is inherently subject to various forms of uncertainty, stemming from both perception and action execution. Perception uncertainty arises from factors such as occlusions, limited sensor capabilities, and errors in perception modules. Action uncertainty occurs due to stochastic outcomes of low-level execution policies, including motion planners and vision-language-action (VLA) policies. These challenges necessitate decision-making frameworks that explicitly model uncertainty, such as Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs).

However, the construction of POMDP models for complex, real-world robotics domains is a laborious process, typically requiring significant manual effort to specify transition, observation, and reward functions. Learning such models directly from robot experience, particularly from visual demonstrations, introduces additional layers of complexity, primarily in state abstraction, handling stochasticity, and addressing partial observability inherent in real-world data.

The current research landscape presents a dichotomy. On one side, symbolic planning domains formulated in languages like the Planning Domain Definition Language (PDDL) offer structured reasoning and are increasingly compatible with Large Language Models (LLMs). Approaches like UniDomain have successfully learned PDDL models from demonstrations. However, a significant limitation of standard PDDL is its assumption of fully observable and deterministic environments, which restricts its applicability in uncertain real-world settings.

On the other side, POMDP formulations, expressed in languages like POMDPX or custom Python implementations, explicitly address uncertainty. Recent efforts have explored learning these models, sometimes leveraging LLMs. Nevertheless, these approaches often face challenges in scaling to complex visual robot domains. They are frequently evaluated in simplified, low-dimensional settings and struggle with the high-dimensional visual input, state abstraction, and the pervasive stochasticity and partial observability characteristic of real robot demonstrations.

This work introduces PO-PDDL to bridge this gap. It proposes a symbolic POMDP formulation that integrates the relational structure and LLM-friendly syntax of PDDL with the explicit modeling capabilities of POMDPs for partial observability, stochasticity, and beliefs. By developing a demonstration-driven learning pipeline that reconstructs symbolic trajectories, identifies partial observability from visual inconsistencies, and learns stochastic transition and observation models, this research aims to overcome the limitations of existing PDDL-based deterministic planning and the practical difficulties of scaling traditional POMDP learning or LLM-based POMDP approaches to real-world visual robotics. The proposed method directly tackles the problem of learning explicit, reusable uncertainty models from visual data, which is a key differentiator from prior work that often assumes simplified state abstractions, relies on manual model specification, or uses implicit uncertainty representations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robust and effective robot task planning under the prevalent uncertainties found in real-world environments. This overarching goal is broken down into several specific objectives and is motivated by the limitations of existing approaches:

1. **Propose a Unified Symbolic POMDP Formulation (PO-PDDL):**
 - **Objective:** To introduce PO-PDDL, a novel symbolic language that extends PDDL-style representations to explicitly incorporate observation modeling and belief specifications, thereby supporting stochastic transitions and partial observability while retaining LLM-friendly, structured, and relational syntax.
 - **Motivation:** Existing PDDL formulations assume fully observable and deterministic environments, rendering them inadequate for real-world robotics. Conversely, traditional POMDP representation languages (e.g., POMDPX, RDDL) are often poorly aligned with LLM generation or have compact syntaxes that are difficult for LLMs to reliably synthesize. A unified, LLM-friendly language is needed to facilitate model generation and interpretation in complex domains.

2. **Develop a Demonstration-Driven Learning Pipeline for PO-PDDL Models:**
 - **Objective:** To create an automated pipeline that can construct a complete PO-PDDL domain, including predicate schemas, stochastic operator models, and observation functions, directly from visual demonstrations of robot policy executions.
 - **Motivation:** Manually engineering POMDP models for real-world tasks is excessively difficult and labor-intensive due to the need to specify complex transition, observation, and reward functions. Learning these models from experience, particularly from visual data, is a critical step towards reducing manual effort and enabling wider adoption of POMDP-based planning in robotics.

3. **Implement a Mechanism for Identifying Partial Observability from Data:**
 - **Objective:** To develop a mismatch-based approach for recognizing partial observability by analyzing inconsistencies between inferred symbolic states (reconstructed trajectories) and direct visual observations.
 - **Motivation:** In real-world visual demonstrations, perception modules do not always provide a complete or accurate representation of the underlying ground-truth state. Distinguishing between hidden state variables and observable cues, and quantifying their relationship, is crucial for accurate POMDP modeling and robust belief-space planning. Explicitly identifying such partial observability from data rather than pre-specifying it reduces manual overhead.

4. **Enable Online Belief-Space Planning with Learned PO-PDDL Domains:**
 - **Objective:** To demonstrate that the learned PO-PDDL domains can be used for online belief-space planning, allowing robots to operate effectively under both perception and execution uncertainty in long-horizon tasks.
 - **Motivation:** The ultimate goal of learning these models is to improve robot autonomy and robustness. The ability to perform principled belief-space planning, where the robot actively manages its uncertainty about the environment, is essential for tackling complex, sequential tasks in dynamic and partially observable settings. This contrasts with methods that rely on implicit uncertainty handling or require repeated online inference with large models.

In essence, the research is motivated by the observed gap between the theoretical necessity of POMDPs for real-world robot planning under uncertainty and the practical challenges of creating and utilizing such models, particularly when scaling to complex visual domains and leveraging modern LLM capabilities.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a novel symbolic POMDP formulation, PO-PDDL, and a two-stage learning pipeline that constructs PO-PDDL domains from visual demonstrations. This is followed by a belief-space planning approach utilizing the learned domain.

#### 3.1. PO-PDDL Formulation

PO-PDDL (Partially Observable PDDL) is introduced as a symbolic formulation of POMDPs that extends PDDL's relational structure and LLM-friendly syntax. A PO-PDDL model is defined by $M = \langle P, O, A, Z, b_0, G \rangle$, where:
- **P (Predicate Schemas):** A set of schemas that induce grounded predicates representing the fully observed symbolic state.
- **O (Observable Schemas):** A set of schemas associated with predicates, representing perception outcomes and defining the observation space.
- **A (Stochastic Operators):** A set of operators, each with preconditions (`pre(a)`), stochastic effects (`eff(a)`) over grounded predicates, and a reward (`R(a)`). These induce the POMDP action space and transition function.
- **Z (Symbolic Observation Model):** Specified as conditional symbolic rules mapping logical conditions over predicates to probability distributions over observable values. This represents the POMDP observation function.
- **b_0 (Initial Belief):** A probability distribution over complete symbolic states, rather than direct visual observations.
- **G (Goal Condition):** A logical condition over grounded predicates, defining terminal rewards.

Similar to PDDL, P, O, A, and Z are specified in a domain file (reusable), while b_0 and G are in a problem file (task-specific).

#### 3.2. Learning a PO-PDDL Domain from Demonstrations

The learning pipeline takes a visual-language demonstration dataset $D = \{D_i\}_{i=1}^K$, where each episode $D_i$ consists of visual observations ($V_i$) and language annotations ($L_i$). The goal is to learn the PO-PDDL domain $M = \langle P, O, A, Z \rangle$. The pipeline operates in two stages:

#### 3.2.1. Fully Observable Domain Learning

This stage focuses on learning predicate schemas ($P$) and stochastic operator models ($A$) under the assumption of full observability.

- **State-Action Concept Formulation:** An LLM processes task instructions, subtask instructions, and VLM-generated scene descriptions to generate:
 - **State concepts:** A set of types (static attributes) and predicates ($P$) (variable attributes) necessary for explaining task progression.
 - **Action concepts:** A set of operator interfaces ($\tilde{A}$) (name and parameter signature, without preconditions/effects). Operators are classified as manipulation or active-perception.

- **Stochastic Transition Modeling:**
 - **Goal and Action Grounding:** For each demonstration, an LLM formulates a symbolic goal ($G_i$) and maps subtask instructions to an action sequence ($a_i$).
 - **State Trajectory Reconstruction:** A forward-backward inference approach is used. In the forward pass, an LLM infers symbolic effects for each action. In the backward pass, given actions, effects, scene descriptions, and the goal, the LLM derives a ground-truth initial state ($s_{i,0}$). Applying effects sequentially reconstructs the full trajectory ($\{s_{i,t}\}_{t=0}^{T_i}$). Goal consistency ($s_{i,T_i} \models G_i$) is enforced, with revisions if violated.
 - **Stochastic Operator Modeling:** For each operator $\alpha \in \tilde{A}$, preconditions are identified from consistent predicate assignments in pre-states. Operator effects are clustered into distinct modes, and probabilities are assigned based on empirical frequencies. Rewards are defined as the negative execution cost, proportional to average subtask duration. This transforms $\tilde{A}$ into fully-specified operators $A$.

- **Domain Review:** A VLM-based verification loop refines the learned operators $A$. For each effect mode, a VLM checks consistency between inferred predicates, preconditions, and effects against raw visual observations. Mismatches lead to revisions by the VLM, triggering updates to state trajectories and operator models, iterating until consistency.

#### 3.2.2. Partially Observable Domain Learning

This stage identifies partially observable components ($O, Z$) by analyzing contradictions between reconstructed states and visual observations.

- **Partial Observability Recognition:**
 - **Visual State Parsing:** An LLM parses a symbolic visual state ($\hat{s}_{i,t}$) from scene descriptions, assigning predicate values in {0, 1, ?}.
 - **Contradiction Discovery:** For each grounded predicate $g$, its value in the visual state $\hat{s}_{i,t}(g)$ is compared to its value in the reconstructed state $s_{i,t}(g)$. If $\hat{s}_{i,t}(g) \neq ?$ and $\hat{s}_{i,t}(g) \neq s_{i,t}(g)$, a contradiction is discovered. A VLM verifies contradictions against raw visuals; errors revert to the fully observable learning stage.
 - **Observable Concept Formation:** An LLM proposes an observable schema ($o_p$) for each predicate schema ($p$) with discovered contradictions, forming the observable set $O$. Predicates without associated observable schemas are considered fully observable.

- **Stochastic Observation Modeling:** An observation model $Z$ is estimated for fully observable predicates (identical mapping) and partially observable predicates. The model distinguishes between:
 - **Passive Observation:** For manipulation actions, observable values $o_{i,t}$ are parsed from visual state $\hat{s}_{i,t}$. Data tuples $(o_{i,t}(g), \hat{s}_{i,t}(g), a_{i,t})$ are constructed.
 - **Active Observation:** For active-perception actions, a VLM infers the grounded predicate $g$ of interest and its observation outcome $o_{i,t}(g)$.
 - **Observation Likelihood Estimation:** Empirical frequencies are used to estimate conditional observation likelihoods, $Z(o | p, a)$ and $Z(o | \neg p, a)$, defining the observation rules.

#### 3.3. Planning with a Learned PO-PDDL Domain

Given a learned PO-PDDL domain, a task-specific problem instance is generated, inducing a POMDP solvable via belief-space planning.

- **PO-PDDL Problem Generation:**
 - **Initial Belief Generation:** For fully observable predicates, the initial scene observation provides deterministic assignments. For partially observable predicates, structural dependencies define a feasible space of assignments. The initial belief $b_0$ is a mix of an empirical prior from demonstrations and a uniform prior over feasible assignments.
 - **Goal Specification:** An LLM generates the goal condition $G$ from the task instruction, selecting predicates, grouping mutual exclusives, and enumerating consistent assignments.

- **Belief-Space Planning:** Planning occurs in the belief space over symbolic states. Starting from $b_0$, the agent uses DESPOT (an online POMDP solver). At each step, DESPOT samples state hypotheses from the current belief, performs sparse belief-tree search, and selects the action with the highest estimated value. Beliefs are updated via Bayesian filtering using the learned transition ($T$) and observation ($Z$) models after executing an action and receiving an observation. This process continues in a receding-horizon loop until the goal is met.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of PO-PDDL was evaluated on real-world robotic planning tasks, focusing on its ability to learn reusable symbolic POMDP models that account for action constraints and stochastic action and perception outcomes.

#### 4.1. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted in a real-world tabletop environment featuring drawers, cups, blocks, and a movable black box. The study utilized:
- **Demonstration Dataset:** 29 short-horizon tasks (average 3.4 steps) were rolled out multiple times to collect visual demonstrations for domain learning.
- **Evaluation Tasks:** 5 longer-horizon evaluation tasks, designed with increasing difficulty, requiring compositional reasoning and online planning under uncertainty. These tasks included `push_box`, `block_in_drawer`, `cup_with_water`, `cups_then_blocks`, and `box_cups_blocks`, involving varying degrees of partial observability, action constraints, and stochastic skill execution.
- **Low-Level Stack:** Manipulation was performed by VLA skills based on $\pi_{0.5}$, post-trained on in-domain data. Perception was provided by a VLM operating on RGB observations. 41 manipulation skills and 2 active-perception skills (e.g., "look into a cup") were trained.
- **Metrics:** Task progress (fraction of key task elements accomplished), cumulative reward (from PO-PDDL reward function), and planning time (seconds).
- **LLM/VLM Backend:** GPT-5.4 was consistently used.

#### 4.2. Comparison Results

**Domain Learning Results:**
PO-PDDL learned a correct and complete POMDP model with the lowest learning cost among explicit model-learning approaches. It took 291 seconds to learn from 29 demonstrations (103 execution steps). In contrast, UniDomain$^*$ required 5111 seconds, and pomdp_coder$^*$ took 328 seconds. PO-PDDL maintained a comparable token cost. The significantly slower time for UniDomain$^*$ was attributed to its hierarchical domain merging process, which involves numerous sequential LLM/VLM calls.

**Online Planning Results:**
PO-PDDL consistently outperformed all baselines across all task settings (Figure 3 in the paper).

- **VLM-Planner:** Performed reasonably on simpler tasks (`push_box`, `block_in_drawer`) but struggled with tasks involving action constraints and hidden states (`cup_with_water`), and completely failed on long-horizon tasks (`cups_then_blocks`, `box_cups_blocks`). Its primary limitation was often failing to infer underlying action constraints from visual observations and demonstrations, leading to infeasible actions.
- **UniDomain$^*$:** Performed well on the deterministic `push_box` task. However, its performance significantly degraded on tasks with partial observability and stochastic execution (`block_in_drawer`, `cup_with_water`), achieving only marginal success. It completely failed on long-horizon tasks (`cups_then_blocks`, `box_cups_blocks`) due to stochastic execution outcomes and varying pre-states generating inconsistent symbolic representations, rendering PDDL domains unsolvable.
- **pomdp_coder$^*$:** Demonstrated general incapability for complex visual robot domains, failing on all tasks except the single-step `push_box`. Despite high coverage of demonstrated reality by its learned transition and observation programs, combining them into a globally consistent and solvable POMDP model proved challenging.

In contrast, **PO-PDDL achieved 100% task success** and the highest cumulative reward across all tasks. Its strong performance was attributed to:
1. Explicitly modeling action constraints via symbolic operator preconditions.
2. Explicitly modeling partial observability and stochastic execution.
3. Utilizing an LLM-friendly symbolic formulation for POMDPs.
4. Employing extensive closed-loop verification and revision during domain learning to ensure model correctness and consistency.

Furthermore, PO-PDDL exhibited the shortest planning time, with an average improvement of 50.4% over VLM-Planner, 85.7% over UniDomain$^*$, and 73.2% over pomdp_coder$^*$. This efficiency was largely due to most steps only requiring Bayesian belief updates with learned transition models, with VLM invocations reserved for actions producing informative observations.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

Two key design choices were ablated (Figure 4 in the paper), demonstrating their contributions:

- **Open-Pass:** Joint learning of fully and partially observable components in a single stage resulted in substantially degraded performance. This variant struggled to reliably distinguish predicate types, often introducing redundant or destructive observables, leading to inefficient belief updates and corrupted beliefs, particularly in tasks like `cup_with_water`.
- **No-ActiveObs:** Removing the distinction between manipulation and active-perception operators during observation modeling also led to degraded performance. This variant under-modeled active-perception's information-gathering role, as passive observations often align with inferred hidden states. This prevented correct belief updates regarding newly observed information, leading to poor task performance (e.g., for `cup_with_water`).

#### 4.4. Qualitative Experiment: Online Adaptation of Operator Effect Probabilities (Appendix A.6)

The structured PO-PDDL representation facilitated online adaptation of operator effect probabilities. In a simulated test-time skill degradation (e.g., right-drawer grasping becoming unreliable), the planner initially preferred the right drawer due to learned probabilities. However, after repeated failures, the estimated success probability for right-drawer grasping decreased, prompting the planner to switch its strategy to opening the left drawer and grasping the block from there. This demonstrated the model's ability to recalibrate transition probabilities from real-world feedback and adapt planning strategies accordingly.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents PO-PDDL, a symbolic POMDP formulation, and an associated learning pipeline, which collectively represent a notable advancement in robot task planning under uncertainty.

**Significance:**
The work is significant for several reasons:
- **Unified Formulation:** It introduces a novel framework that bridges the gap between traditional symbolic planning (PDDL) and principled uncertainty modeling (POMDPs). PO-PDDL retains the interpretability, structured reasoning, and LLM-friendliness of PDDL while explicitly incorporating stochastic transitions, partial observability, and belief representations. This unification addresses a long-standing challenge in developing robust robot intelligence.
- **Demonstration-Driven Learning:** The development of a pipeline that learns these complex PO-PDDL domains directly from visual demonstrations of robot executions is a critical contribution. It significantly reduces the manual engineering effort typically required for specifying POMDP models, making advanced planning under uncertainty more accessible for real-world robotics.
- **Data-Driven Partial Observability:** The proposed mismatch-based mechanism for identifying partial observability from discrepancies between reconstructed symbolic states and visual observations is a key innovation. This allows for the data-driven discovery of hidden state variables and their associated observation models, which is essential for accurate belief-space planning in complex environments.
- **Empirical Validation:** The comprehensive experimental evaluation on real-world long-horizon manipulation tasks demonstrates that the proposed method consistently outperforms existing PDDL and POMDP model-learning approaches. The observed improvements in task success rate, cumulative reward, and planning efficiency underscore the practical utility and robustness of PO-PDDL.

**Potential Impact:**
The potential impact of this research extends across several areas:
- **Autonomous Robotics:** PO-PDDL can enable robots to operate more robustly and autonomously in complex, partially observable, and stochastic real-world environments. This is crucial for applications such as household assistance, logistics, and industrial automation, where unforeseen events and incomplete information are common. By reducing the reliance on manually engineered models, it can accelerate the deployment of intelligent robotic systems.
- **AI Planning and Representation:** The PO-PDDL formulation itself contributes to the field of AI planning by providing a structured, LLM-compatible language for POMDPs. It offers a new paradigm for representing and learning complex world models, fostering further research at the intersection of symbolic AI, probabilistic AI, and large foundation models.
- **Model Learning from Data:** The demonstration-driven learning pipeline provides a blueprint for how complex, uncertainty-aware planning models can be acquired directly from raw, multimodal robot experience. This can inspire future work on learning richer and more generalizable models that capture the nuances of real-world physics and perception.
- **Human-Robot Interaction:** The LLM-friendly nature of PO-PDDL could facilitate more intuitive human interaction with robots, allowing humans to describe tasks and provide demonstrations that are more readily translated into actionable, uncertainty-aware plans.
- **Future Research Directions:** The identified limitations, such as the assumption of closed domains, scalability with large datasets, and reliance on VLM capabilities, point towards important avenues for future research, including online model revision, development of modular knowledge bases for domain adaptation, and strategies for explicit human assistance when AI bottlenecks are encountered.
