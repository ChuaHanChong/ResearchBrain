# Motion Priors Reimagined: Adapting Flat-Terrain Skills for Complex Quadruped Mobility

> [!summary] Summary
> A hierarchical reinforcement learning framework adapts pre-trained flat-terrain motion skills for quadrupedal robots, enabling robust and naturalistic locomotion across complex, unstructured environments with reduced reward engineering. The system, demonstrated on the ANYmal-D robot, achieves high success rates in simulation on challenging terrains and performs perceptive navigation in real-world settings while preserving animal-like gaits.

## Problem

- Motion imitation methods for legged robots often struggle with generalization, overfitting to specific training environments (e.g., flat terrain) and failing in novel, complex conditions.
- Achieving expressive, naturalistic robot motions (like animal gaits) while ensuring robust adaptation to diverse terrains typically requires extensive and tedious reward function engineering in reinforcement learning.
- Existing approaches face a trade-off between the expressiveness of learned motions and the policy's ability to generalize and adapt to new, challenging environments.

## Method

- A ==hierarchical reinforcement learning== framework is developed, leveraging animal motion capture data from flat terrain to pre-train a low-level policy as robust "==motion priors==."
- A high-level, goal-conditioned policy is trained to learn ==residual corrections== on top of these frozen low-level priors, allowing adaptation for perceptive locomotion and local navigation in complex environments.
- ==Policy distillation==, incorporating a ==recurrent belief encoder==, transfers the teacher policy's capabilities (trained with ==privileged information==) to a student policy for robust real-world deployment on noisy sensor inputs.

## Results

- The high-level student policy achieved high simulation success rates on challenging terrains, including **84%** on 0.25m stairs up and **75%** on random boxes with high obstacles, demonstrating effective perceptive locomotion.
- The framework was successfully deployed on the ANYmal-D robot in real-world indoor and outdoor environments, showcasing smooth, animal-like gaits for goal-directed navigation and local obstacle avoidance.
- Policies incorporating motion priors exhibited a lower Cost of Transport (CoT) and avoided undesirable 'jumping gaits' compared to a baseline model, indicating improved energy efficiency and more natural movement with less reward engineering.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning joint residuals atop pre-trained motion priors enables dynamic adaptation to varied terrains (e.g., stairs, slopes) while preserving the underlying natural gait, with residual variance increasing proportionally to terrain difficulty.
> - A critical trade-off exists in penalizing residual actions: a moderate penalty is essential to balance robust adaptation with the preservation of the desired animal-like motion style, demonstrating efficient regularization.
> - Integrating motion priors inherently regularizes learned motions, reducing the need for complex reward shaping to achieve smooth, natural gaits compared to training policies from scratch.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged locomotion robotics is characterized by the formidable challenge of enabling robots to move robustly and adaptively across diverse, unstructured terrains, often while performing complex tasks. Reinforcement Learning (RL) has emerged as a dominant paradigm, offering promising avenues for learning intricate motor skills. This paper sits at the intersection of several key trends in RL-based locomotion:

**a. The Dual Approaches to RL Locomotion:**
- **Reward-Engineered RL (Direct Policy Learning):** Early successes in RL-based locomotion primarily relied on meticulously hand-crafted reward functions to achieve stable and velocity-tracking movements. While effective for specific tasks and robust on various terrains, these methods often struggle to produce expressive, animal- or human-like motions and require extensive, tedious reward shaping and tuning.
- **Motion Imitation RL:** This approach addresses the expressiveness limitation by guiding RL policies with reference trajectories derived from sources like teleoperation, motion capture, or trajectory optimization. It has successfully enabled robots to learn complex, agile maneuvers such as jumping and backflips, reducing the need for elaborate reward design for *style*.

**b. The Generalization Gap in Motion Imitation:**
Despite its benefits, motion imitation faces a critical limitation: poor generalization to novel environments. Policies trained strictly to mimic demonstrations tend to overfit to the specific conditions (e.g., terrain type) under which the reference data were collected. This "covariate shift" leads to performance degradation and style inconsistency when deployed in significantly different environments. Prior work has attempted to improve adaptation, but these often rely on specific training data (e.g., motion data from uneven terrains) or are limited to scenarios with only minimal environmental differences. The challenge of extending generalization using readily available, minimally processed references (like raw retargeted animal motion from *flat-terrain*) to highly diverse and challenging scenarios remains largely unaddressed.

**c. Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning and Residual Learning:**
A common strategy to overcome the limitations of monolithic policies and enhance adaptability is **hierarchical RL**. Here, low-level policies (often acting as motion primitives or skills) are pre-trained and then integrated into a higher-level framework. This paper leverages this by pre-training a low-level policy for motion priors. Crucially, it employs **residual learning**, where the high-level policy learns "corrections" on top of the established low-level skills, allowing for adaptation without completely relearning the core motion style. This technique is becoming increasingly popular for balancing learned primitives with task-specific adjustments.

**d. Perceptive Locomotion and Local Navigation:**
Another significant trend is the integration of perceptive capabilities directly into locomotion controllers to enable local navigation without external planning modules. While previous efforts have shown emergent local navigation behaviors with position-based task rewards, these often require extensive reward shaping for smooth motion and are typically applied to less challenging terrains. This work seeks to advance this by combining the naturalness of motion priors with perceptive adaptation for efficient goal-directed navigation in rugged, obstacle-rich environments.

In summary, this paper contributes to the landscape by proposing a robust framework that mitigates the generalization issues of motion imitation by combining hierarchical RL, latent-space motion priors from limited (flat-terrain) data, and residual learning. It aims to bridge the gap between expressive, natural motion styles and robust, adaptive locomotion/navigation in complex, unstructured real-world environments, doing so with reduced reward engineering complexity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this research stems from the acknowledged trade-off in current reinforcement learning approaches for legged locomotion:
- **Expressiveness vs. Generalization:** Reward-engineered RL can be robust but lacks expressive, natural motions. Motion imitation RL excels at naturalness but struggles to generalize to novel environments when the training demonstrations are specific (e.g., collected only on flat terrain).
- **Reward Engineering Complexity:** Both approaches can demand significant effort in reward function design and tuning, especially when aiming for both natural motion and robust adaptation.

Given these challenges, the paper sets forth the following key objectives:

1. **Develop a Hierarchical RL Framework for Adaptive Locomotion:** To create a pipeline that can effectively adapt pre-trained, flat-terrain motion skills for robust locomotion and navigation across complex, non-flat environments.
2. **Establish Robust Motion Priors from Limited Data:** To pre-train a low-level policy using animal motion data collected *exclusively from flat terrain*, thereby establishing a foundational set of "motion priors" that embody natural, animal-like gaits (e.g., walking, pacing, cantering).
3. **Enable Adaptation through Residual Learning:** To train a subsequent high-level, goal-conditioned policy that learns *residual corrections* on top of these frozen low-level motion priors. These residuals should allow the robot to perform perceptive locomotion, local obstacle avoidance, and goal-directed navigation across diverse and rugged terrains, while still preserving the underlying natural locomotion characteristics of the priors.
4. **Achieve Perceptive Locomotion and Local Navigation in a Unified Pipeline:** To demonstrate an integrated policy that can navigate to target goals and avoid local obstacles using animal-like gaits, without requiring an external high-level planning module.
5. **Reduce Reward Engineering Effort:** To show that the incorporation of motion priors inherently regularizes the learned motions, thereby reducing the extensive reward shaping typically needed to achieve smooth and natural gaits in RL-trained controllers.
6. **Validate Sim-to-Real Transferability:** To confirm the policy's capability to generalize these learned animal-like locomotion skills from simulation to real-world deployment on a physical quadrupedal robot (ANYmal-D) in complex, outdoor environments.
7. **Analyze the Impact of Residuals:** To conduct a comprehensive analysis of how motion priors and learned residuals influence overall task performance and locomotion characteristics, particularly with varying penalty weights on the residuals.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed solution is a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework structured into four distinct phases, as illustrated in Figure 2: Motion Processing, Pre-training, Task Training, and Policy Distillation for Sim2Real.

**1. Motion Processing:**
- **Data Source:** Raw animal motion capture data, comprising various gait patterns (walking, pacing, cantering) at different speeds, collected exclusively on flat ground.
- **Retargeting:** Inverse kinematics is used to map these animal motions to the ANYmal-D robot's kinematic configuration (e.g., neck, pelvis, feet positions), preparing the data for robot imitation.

**2. Pre-training (Low-level Policy and Motion Priors):**
- **FLD Encoder Training:** An FLD (Fourier Latent Dynamics) model, comprising an encoder and decoder, is first pre-trained on the retargeted offline motion data. The FLD encoder compresses motion trajectories into low-dimensional latent vectors, capturing global periodic features of the motions (frequency, amplitude, offset) and local phase states.
- **Low-level Policy Training:**
 - A low-level motion imitation policy is then trained on flat terrain, utilizing the latent embeddings generated by the *frozen* FLD encoder.
 - Modifications to the standard FLD pipeline include eliminating the decoder and using ground-truth trajectories instead of reconstructed ones, bypassing phase propagation for improved imitation performance.
 - **Action Space:** 12-dimensional joint actions.
 - **Observation Space:** Proprioceptive signals (base linear/angular velocities, projected gravity, joint positions) and latent encodings.
 - **Reward Function:** Composed of an imitation component (tracking reference velocities, joint positions, projected gravity) and a regularization component (penalizing action rates, collisions, torque limits, etc.).
 - Upon completion, this low-level policy is **frozen** and acts as the "motion prior," providing a command interface for executing fundamental skills.

**3. Task Training (High-level Teacher Policy):**
- **Objective:** To extend the capabilities of the low-level motion priors for locomotion and local navigation in complex, non-flat environments (uneven surfaces, stairs, slopes, high obstacles).
- **Policy Architecture:** A high-level teacher policy is trained on top of the frozen low-level policy.
 - **Outputs:** 16-dimensional latent commands (to select desired gait/speed from the low-level policy) and 12-dimensional joint residuals (`a_res_t`). These residuals are crucial for adapting the low-level skills to challenging terrains.
 - **Observation Space:**
 - Noiseless proprioceptive inputs (same as low-level).
 - Noiseless exteroceptive inputs: Fused elevation scans around robot feet and a downsampled Velodyne LiDAR scan (sparse conical pattern) for comprehensive environmental awareness.
 - Privileged states: Leg contact information, friction coefficients, external disturbances.
 - Task-specific inputs: Goal position (`p_g`) relative to the robot, and a coarse velocity command (`v_cmd`).
- **Reward Function:**
 - Task-specific rewards: `r_reach` (goal-reaching) and `r_vel` (guiding forward motion toward the goal). `r_vel` provides a denser signal for local navigation in cluttered environments.
 - Regularization rewards: Similar to low-level policy (excluding tracking terms), plus a critical `r_res` penalty term to constrain the magnitude of joint residuals, balancing adaptation with prior preservation.

**4. Policy Distillation for Sim2Real:**
- **Bridging the Gap:** Aims to transfer the performance of the high-level teacher policy (trained with privileged, noiseless information) to a student policy suitable for real-world deployment.
- **Student Policy Inputs:** Operates solely on noisy proprioceptive and exteroceptive data.
- **Belief Encoder:** A recurrent belief encoder (implemented with a Gated Recurrent Unit, GRU) is integrated into the student policy. Its role is to reconstruct privileged states and recover noiseless exteroceptive signals from the noisy inputs, mitigating the sim-to-real gap.
- **Distillation Process:** Supervised learning using a behavior loss (`L_0`) quantifies the discrepancy between the teacher's and student's actions, guiding the student to mimic the teacher's robust performance.

**Training Environment:** All simulations are performed in Isaac Gym. Both low-level and high-level policies are trained using the PPO algorithm in parallel environments on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents a comprehensive set of simulation and hardware experiments that validate the effectiveness of their hierarchical RL framework.

**1. Low-Level Policy Performance:**
- The pre-trained low-level policy successfully learned to imitate diverse animal gaits (walking, pacing, cantering) at various speeds on flat terrain.
- Figure 3 illustrates close alignment between reference and actual joint positions, demonstrating strong imitation performance. Footfall patterns (Figure 10) further confirm the learning of distinct gaits.
- Crucially, this low-level policy acts as a robust foundation, providing natural, animal-like motion priors that can be commanded and adapted.

**2. High-Level Policy Adaptation to Complex Terrains:**
- **Simulation Success Rates:** The high-level student policy, when evaluated in simulation with realistic exteroceptive noise, achieved high success rates across a wide array of challenging terrains (e.g., 84% on 0.25m stairs up, 90% on 24-deg slopes up, 75% on random boxes with high obstacles, as detailed in Table 8).
- **Perceptive Locomotion and Navigation:** The robot demonstrated the ability to reach target goals and avoid local obstacles, exhibiting smooth, animal-like gait behaviors without needing an explicit, separate navigation module. This confirms the successful integration of perceptive locomotion and local navigation.
- **Role of Joint Residuals:**
 - Analysis of joint residuals (Figure 4, Figure 5) revealed that the high-level policy dynamically adjusts these residuals based on terrain difficulty.
 - The variance of joint residuals increased significantly as terrain challenges (e.g., stair height, slope steepness) increased, indicating that residuals actively contribute to adapting the base motion priors to new conditions. This provides concrete evidence that the learned residuals are enabling generalization.

**3. Impact of Penalty on Residual Actions:**
- The research demonstrated a critical trade-off when tuning the penalty weight (`w_res`) for joint residuals.
- **High Penalty (e.g., `w_res = -100` to `-10`):** Led to strong adherence to motion priors but limited adaptability to very challenging terrains (e.g., higher steps, steeper slopes), resulting in lower success rates.
- **Low Penalty (e.g., `w_res = -0.001` or `0`):** Enabled greater adaptation and higher success rates on difficult terrains, but allowed residuals to grow unconstrained, causing the gait to deviate significantly from the intended animal-like style (Figure 7).
- **Optimal Balance:** A moderate penalty (e.g., `w_res = -0.1`) was found to balance adaptation capability with the preservation of natural motion characteristics. This highlights the framework's efficient regularization through a single, primary penalty term.

**4. Comparison with Baseline RL Model:**
- A comparative experiment with a baseline RL model trained from scratch (without motion priors) under similar reward conditions revealed a significant advantage for the proposed framework.
- The baseline policy, despite navigating challenging terrains, often exhibited an undesirable "jumping gait" and would typically require extensive additional reward tuning (e.g., on base acceleration or vertical velocity) to achieve smoother, more natural movements.
- In contrast, the proposed model, by integrating motion priors, inherently enforced an animal-like movement style, achieving natural gaits with substantially reduced reward engineering complexity (only requiring a penalty on joint residuals).
- **Energetic Efficiency (Appendix 8.4):** Additional analysis showed that the models incorporating motion priors achieved a lower Cost of Transport (CoT) compared to the baseline, suggesting that natural gaits are also more energy-efficient by avoiding uncontrolled vertical motion.

**5. Real-World Evaluation:**
- The high-level student policy was successfully deployed on the ANYmal-D robot in diverse real-world indoor and outdoor environments, including stairs, random steps, and various obstacles (Figure 1, Figure 8).
- The robot consistently demonstrated smooth, animal-like locomotion while navigating to target goals and performing local obstacle avoidance, without needing explicit waypoint generation.
- These hardware results provide strong empirical evidence for the framework's robustness, transferability, and practical utility in complex, unstructured real-world scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in the field of quadrupedal robotics and reinforcement learning for locomotion, addressing critical limitations of existing approaches. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

**1. Bridging the Gap between Expressiveness and Generalization:**
The most profound significance of this work is its successful mitigation of the long-standing trade-off between learning expressive, natural motions through imitation and ensuring generalization to novel, complex environments. By adaptively learning residuals on top of flat-terrain motion priors, the framework enables robots to retain aesthetic, animal-like gaits while simultaneously demonstrating robust adaptability to highly varied terrains and obstacles. This is a crucial step towards truly versatile and natural-looking robotic locomotion.

**2. Reduced Reward Engineering Complexity:**
The proposed hierarchical framework substantially reduces the burden of meticulous reward function design and tuning, which is a major bottleneck in many RL applications. By leveraging pre-trained motion priors that inherently regularize the movement style, the high-level policy can achieve natural gaits with a minimal set of task-specific and residual penalty rewards. This streamlines the development process for new locomotion skills and tasks.

**3. Unified Perceptive Locomotion and Local Navigation:**
The ability of the learned policy to integrate perceptive locomotion with local obstacle avoidance and goal-directed navigation into a single, end-to-end controller is highly impactful. It eliminates the need for separate, often complex, high-level planning modules to generate waypoints, leading to a more autonomous, responsive, and potentially energy-efficient system. This paves the way for robots that can react instinctively to their immediate surroundings.

**4. Robust Sim-to-Real Transfer for Advanced Skills:**
The successful deployment on the ANYmal-D hardware in diverse real-world settings is a testament to the framework's robustness and its effective sim-to-real transfer strategy (privileged learning and policy distillation). This demonstrates that complex, adaptive locomotion skills learned in simulation can be reliably translated to physical robots, a key challenge in robotics research.

**5. Foundation for More Capable Legged Robots:**
This research contributes directly to the development of more capable and autonomous legged robots for real-world applications. Robots equipped with such adaptive, natural locomotion skills could be deployed more effectively in unstructured environments for tasks like inspection, logistics, search and rescue, and even entertainment, where agile and visually appealing motion is beneficial.

**6. Advancements in Hierarchical RL and Skill Adaptation:**
The work offers a powerful paradigm for hierarchical RL, demonstrating how low-dimensional latent representations of pre-trained skills can serve as powerful motion priors. The method of learning joint residuals on top of these priors provides a scalable and efficient way to adapt a limited set of base skills to an expansive range of downstream tasks and environments. This framework could inspire further research into learning and combining a broader range of motor behaviors (e.g., jumping, crawling) and adapting them to even more extreme terrains (e.g., gaps, stepping stones, overhanging obstacles). The potential for active learning and diversity-driving rewards to overcome current limitations like "mode collapse" (where the policy habitually relies on a single gait) is a promising avenue for future exploration.
