# The Curse of Precision: A Data Scaling Law for High-Precision Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Tsinghua University researchers introduce and validate a novel scaling law that quantifies the data cost of achieving high-precision robotic manipulation, revealing a "Curse of Precision" where data requirements grow super-exponentially as a task's precision approaches a system-specific limit (c). The study establishes c as an intrinsic metric for a system's capability, showing its sensitivity to sensor quality and expert demonstration clarity.

## Problem

- The quantitative data cost required to incrementally increase the precision of a robotic skill was largely unknown, especially for learning-based methods.
- Existing research on robotic scaling laws primarily focused on "breadth" (generalization across diverse tasks and environments), neglecting "depth" (the data cost for achieving higher precision within structured tasks).
- Empirical observations showed standard imitation learning often performs poorly on high-precision tasks, with minor precision relaxations leading to disproportionate success rate improvements, indicating an uncharacterized data bottleneck.

## Method

- The study proposed two novel scaling laws: a **Success Rate Scaling Law** (==power-law relationship== between failure rate and data volume) and a **Precision Scaling Law** (==super-exponential relationship== between data volume and target precision relative to a ==limit precision 'c'==).
- A large-scale empirical study was conducted in the ==ManiSkill3== simulation environment, training ==Diffusion Policy== models on a Franka Emika Panda robot across three high-precision tasks (Peg Insertion, Stack Cuboid, Roll Ball) with varying data volumes and precision targets.
- Systematic ==ablation studies== were performed to investigate how system components, such as observation modalities (e.g., wrist camera) and expert policy strategies, influence the identified limit precision 'c'.

## Results

- Both the Success Rate Scaling Law and the Precision Scaling Law were empirically validated, with the latter showing consistently high R-squared values (greater than **0.968** across tasks) for a shared 'c' parameter.
- Ablation studies on the Peg Insertion task demonstrated that disabling a wrist camera increased 'c' from **2.35 mm** to **3.85 mm**, while utilizing cleaner expert demonstrations reduced 'c' to **1.27 mm**.
- Sufficient policy network capacity was identified as a precondition for the scaling laws to manifest, with increased network capacity enabling a clear scaling law (**R² = 0.99**) for a dynamically complex task where none was initially observed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-precision robotic tasks are subject to a "Curse of Precision," meaning the data required for imitation learning grows super-exponentially as the desired precision approaches a system-specific theoretical limit 'c'.
> - The limit precision 'c' is an emergent, intrinsic property of the entire robotic learning system, encompassing its sensors and expert policy, and serves as a quantifiable metric for the system's ultimate performance ceiling.
> - Improving the clarity and reducing observational ambiguity in expert demonstrations can be more impactful for enhancing a system's limit precision 'c' than solely relying on the expert's raw success rate.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of machine learning, particularly in natural language processing (NLP) and computer vision (CV), has observed the emergence of scaling laws, which describe how model performance predictably improves with increases in data, model parameters, and compute. This paradigm has inspired analogous efforts in robotics to develop more generalized and capable agents.

Existing research in robotic scaling laws has predominantly focused on "Breadth," aiming to improve zero-shot generalization capabilities by increasing data diversity across various objects, environments, tasks, and robot embodiments. Landmark studies have demonstrated that a policy's generalization often follows a power law with the number of training variations, suggesting that diversity is a critical factor for performance in open-world settings. Projects like Open X-Embodiment and models such as RT-X and Octo exemplify this approach, pooling large, heterogeneous datasets to facilitate cross-skill and cross-embodiment transfer. More recent work, like RT-2, integrates pre-trained Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to transfer semantic understanding from web-scale data to robotic control, enabling robots to follow abstract instructions. This collective body of work primarily addresses the challenge of creating robots capable of operating in diverse and unstructured environments.

In contrast, a significant and economically relevant segment of robotic applications, including industrial assembly, electronics manufacturing, and surgical automation, operates within structured, "closed-world" environments. In these contexts, the primary challenge shifts from generalization to novelty to achieving high precision and reliability. Historically, high precision in robotics has been achieved through classical control methods, such as Operational Space Control and Impedance Control, which rely on rigorous analytical models and typically require minimal or "zero-data." The transition to learning-based approaches like imitation learning (IL) and reinforcement learning (RL) introduced a new bottleneck: the high cost, time, and safety risks associated with real-world data collection. This led to a strong emphasis on data efficiency in robotic learning algorithms and system architectures.

This paper addresses a gap in the current scaling law literature by investigating the "Depth" dimension: the quantitative relationship between the data required for learning and the achievement of progressively higher precision and reliability in closed-world robotic tasks. While existing research provides insights into scaling for breadth, the scaling behavior for pushing physical skill precision to its limits remains largely uncharacterized. This work aims to establish a theoretical framework for understanding the data cost of precision, distinct from the data cost of generalization, and to provide a practical methodology for developing and debugging high-precision manipulation systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for this research arose from empirical observations that standard imitation learning techniques often exhibited unexpectedly low performance on high-precision robotic tasks. A minor relaxation of the task's precision requirements frequently led to a substantial improvement in success rates, indicating a disproportionate data cost associated with precision. This sensitivity prompted a series of fundamental questions:
- What is the quantitative data cost required to incrementally increase the precision of a robotic skill?
- Does a predictable scaling law govern this relationship between data volume and target precision?
- How do the intrinsic capabilities of the learning system—specifically, its sensory inputs, the quality of expert demonstrations, and the inherent complexity of the task—modulate or influence this scaling law?

To address these questions, the authors set forth the following key objectives:
1. **Uncover a Novel Scaling Law for Precision:** To empirically identify and model the relationship between the required data volume (N) for imitation learning and the target precision (P) in closed-world robotic manipulation tasks, particularly as precision demands increase.
2. **Characterize the "Limit Precision" (c):** To determine if a theoretical limit precision exists for a given robotic system, and if this limit (denoted as 'c') acts as an emergent property of the entire agent system, including its sensors and expert policy, rather than being a static physical constant of the task itself.
3. **Establish 'c' as a System Capability Metric:** To demonstrate that 'c' can serve as a quantifiable metric for the performance ceiling of a learning system, allowing for systematic evaluation of system capabilities.
4. **Investigate Modulators of 'c':** To systematically analyze how enhancements to system components, such as adding a wrist camera (improved sensors) or utilizing a more effective expert policy (improved expert data quality), can measurably alter and improve the limit precision 'c'.
5. **Provide a Practical Framework:** To offer a new theoretical framework for understanding the data cost of precision in robotics, and to establish a practical methodology that can guide the development, debugging, and engineering of more capable and data-efficient high-precision manipulation systems.

The underlying motivation is to move beyond simply amassing more data to improve performance, particularly when approaching the physical limits of precision, and instead focus on understanding the fundamental data requirements and identifying targeted system-level improvements.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study adopted a large-scale empirical approach conducted within the ManiSkill3 high-fidelity simulation environment. This involved over 100 independent model training runs and tens of thousands of evaluation rollouts under a rigorous experimental protocol.

**A. Scaling Law Formulation for Precision**

The study investigates the relationship between three dimensions: Data Volume (N, number of imitation learning trajectories), Task Precision (P, required tolerance for success), and System Configuration (agent's sensors, expert policy, etc.).

- **Evaluation Metric:** Success Rate (SR) was chosen as the primary metric, a binary measure of task completion. This selection was based on its direct relevance to reliability in high-stakes applications and the feasibility of obtaining statistically robust measures through numerous automated simulation rollouts. Mean Squared Error (MSE) was considered but found not to consistently correlate with task success.

- **Hypothesis 1: The Success Rate Scaling Law:** This hypothesis posits that for a fixed task precision (P), the performance scales with data volume (N) following a power-law relationship. Specifically, the failure rate (1 − SR) is hypothesized to follow:
 `log(1 - SR) = a · log(N) + b`
 where `a` and `b` are fitting parameters.

- **Hypothesis 2: The Precision Scaling Law:** This novel hypothesis describes the data volume (N) required to achieve a given precision (P) for a fixed target success rate. It is hypothesized that this relationship is governed by the proximity of P to a theoretical limit precision `c`:
 `log(N) = m · (1 / (P - c)) + n`
 where `m` and `n` are fitting parameters, and `c` represents the highest possible precision the system can achieve, considered an intrinsic property of the system configuration, independent of the target success rate. As P approaches `c`, the required data volume is expected to grow super-exponentially.

**B. Robotic Learning System**

- **Robot and Environment:** All experiments were conducted in the ManiSkill3 simulation benchmark, built upon the SAPIEN physics engine. The robot used was a Franka Emika Panda 7-DoF arm with a parallel-jaw gripper.
- **Imitation Learning Paradigm:** The task was framed as a behavior cloning problem, where a policy `π(a|o)` learns to map an observation `o` to an action `a`.
 - **Observation Space (o):** Multi-modal input comprising two 256x256 RGB-D images (one from a static third-person camera and one from a wrist-mounted camera) and the robot's proprioceptive state (joint angles, end-effector pose).
 - **Action Space (a):** A 7-DoF end-effector delta pose `(∆x, ∆y, ∆z, ∆roll, ∆pitch, ∆yaw, gripper)`.
- **Learning Algorithm:** Diffusion Policy was used for policy learning. This generative approach was selected for its performance and ability to model complex, multi-modal action distributions relevant to contact-rich precision tasks. The implementation utilized a standard architecture with a ResNet-18 backbone for visual feature extraction and a 1D U-Net as the denoising network, with action inference via 100 DDPM steps.
- **Expert Policy Generation:**
 - **Scripted Policies:** For structured tasks (Peg Insertion, Stack Cuboid), expert trajectories were generated using privileged, scripted policies with access to ground-truth object poses. These scripts defined waypoints, and a motion planner generated collision-free trajectories.
 - **RL-trained Policy:** For dynamically complex tasks (Roll Ball), where scripted policies are intractable, an expert policy trained via reinforcement learning was utilized. This allowed for testing the generality of the scaling laws across different expert generation paradigms.

**C. Experiments and Evaluation Protocol**

- **Tasks:** Three canonical high-precision manipulation tasks with extensive domain randomization were used:
 - **Peg Insertion:** Inserting a square-profile cuboid peg into a square hole. Precision (P) varied from 4mm to 10mm, defined as clearance.
 - **Cuboid Stacking:** Stacking two slender cuboids. Precision (P) varied from 4mm to 10mm, defined as the half-side-length of the cuboids’ square base.
 - **Roll Ball:** Rolling a ball through a circular target region. Precision (P) varied from 35mm to 200mm, defined by the target region's radius.
- **Evaluation Protocol:**
 - **Data Generation:** For each precision level P, a pool of successful expert trajectories was generated.
 - **Policy Training Sweep:** For various data sizes N (e.g., 200, 500, 1000, 2000), N trajectories were sampled to train a Diffusion Policy model. Each data point corresponded to a single training run (approximately 20 A100-hours per run).
 - **Statistical Evaluation:** Each policy was trained to convergence (160k-200k steps) with periodic evaluations. The mean of the top 3 highest SRs achieved during training was reported to provide a stable measure. Each evaluation consisted of M = 100 episodes. Error bars (95% confidence interval) for SR estimates were calculated using the Wilson score interval method over 3M = 300 trials.
 - **Curve Fitting:**
 - **Success Rate Scaling Law:** Parameters `a` and `b` were fitted via linear regression on `log(1-SR)` vs. `log(N)`.
 - **Precision Scaling Law:** For different target success rates (e.g., 0.5, 0.7, 0.9), the required data size N for each P was estimated by interpolating or extrapolating from the fitted Success Rate Scaling Law. A grid search was then performed to find a single `c` value that maximized the sum of coefficients of determination (R²) across all target SR curves, enforcing `c` as a system-intrinsic constant.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The empirical study provided validation for both proposed scaling laws and demonstrated the utility of the limit precision `c` as a metric for system capability through ablation studies.

**A. Validation of the Proposed Scaling Laws**

- **Success Rate Scaling Law:** The experiments confirmed Hypothesis 1, demonstrating that for a fixed task precision, performance scales with data size following a power law. For all three tasks (Peg Insertion, Stack Cuboid, Roll Ball), plots of `log(1 - SR)` against `log(N)` exhibited clear linear relationships (Figure 2a-c). The R-squared values for the fits were consistently high (Table I), supporting the generality of this power-law principle across diverse manipulation tasks and expert generation paradigms (scripted vs. RL-trained).

- **Precision Scaling Law ("Curse of Precision"):** The central finding of the paper is the validation of Hypothesis 2. The relationship `log(N) = m · (1 / (P - c)) + n` accurately modeled the data requirements for achieving increased precision. Plots of `log(N)` versus `1/(P - c)` (Figure 2d-f) showed the data points for all tasks and target success rates aligned on near-perfect straight lines. Notably, for each task, a single, shared value of `c` was identified through grid search that yielded high R-squared values (Table II, R² > 0.968 across all tasks and SR targets). This strongly supports the hypothesis that `c` is an intrinsic property of the system configuration, independent of the desired success rate. The super-exponential growth of `N` as `P` approaches `c` demonstrates the "Curse of Precision."

**B. The Limit Precision `c` as a System Performance Metric (Ablation Studies)**

A series of ablation studies on the Peg Insertion task investigated how `c` is influenced by different system components, confirming its role as a sensitive metric for overall system capability. The Precision Scaling Law remained an excellent fit (R² > 0.97) across all ablated configurations (Table III).

- **Impact of Observation Modality:** Disabling the wrist-mounted camera, a crucial local sensor, degraded the system's capability. The limit precision `c` increased from the baseline of 2.35 mm to 3.85 mm (Figure 3a, 3b). This quantifies the value of high-quality, local observations for high-precision tasks.

- **Impact of Expert Policy Strategy:**
 - The **Baseline** system utilized a **Conservative Expert Strategy (Expert 1)**, which involved multiple corrective alignment maneuvers at the hole's entrance. While this strategy achieved a high raw success rate (e.g., 98% at 5mm clearance), it introduced observational ambiguity for the learning policy.
 - An **Aggressive Expert Strategy (Expert 2)** was designed, performing a more direct, single-shot insertion (Figure 3e). Although this expert had a lower raw success rate (e.g., 50% at 5mm clearance), filtering for its successful trajectories yielded a dataset of cleaner, less ambiguous demonstrations.
 - Training with this cleaner data from the Aggressive Expert significantly improved the limit precision `c`, reducing it from 2.35 mm to 1.27 mm. This indicates that the clarity and lack of observational ambiguity in expert demonstrations can be a more critical factor for achieving high precision than the expert's own raw success rate.

- **Impact of Task Complexity:** Reducing the level of domain randomization (e.g., randomizing only initial XY positions, as opposed to orientations, sizes, and hole positions in the baseline, Figure 3c, 3d) simplified the task. This simplification resulted in a dramatically improved limit precision `c` of 1.00 mm, demonstrating greater data efficiency in a less complex problem space.

These ablation results collectively provided evidence that `c` is a sensitive and informative metric, reflecting the intrinsic capabilities of a robotic learning system and how various system components contribute to its ultimate precision ceiling.

**C. Further Analysis: On the Precondition of Model Capacity**

During validation of the Roll Ball task, an additional insight emerged regarding the necessity of sufficient policy network capacity for the scaling laws to manifest. Initial experiments with a standard-capacity 1D U-Net denoiser failed to show a clear scaling law, with a low R-squared value of 0.22. However, upon substantially increasing the denoiser network's capacity, a distinct scaling law became apparent, yielding an R-squared of 0.99 (Figure 4). This suggests that for certain dynamically complex tasks, the policy network must possess adequate representational power to capture the fundamental task dynamics before predictable scaling relationships between data and precision can emerge.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research shifts the focus of scaling law investigations in robotics from generalization in open-world settings to the challenge of precision and reliability in closed-world manipulation. By systematically characterizing the data cost of high-precision tasks, the work offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

**A. New Theoretical Framework for Precision:**
The paper introduces a novel scaling law that quantifies the super-exponential increase in data requirements as target precision approaches a system-specific limit `c`. This provides a theoretical framework for understanding the "Curse of Precision" and distinguishes the data cost of achieving physical fidelity from that of achieving broad generalization. It suggests that for high-precision tasks, simply accumulating more data eventually yields diminishing returns, implying that "data is not all you need" beyond a certain point.

**B. Quantitative Metric for System Capability (`c`):**
The identification of the limit precision `c` as an emergent, intrinsic property of the entire learning system (including sensors and expert policy) provides a quantifiable metric for a system's performance ceiling. Unlike a static physical constant, `c` is shown to be mutable and sensitive to changes in system components. This metric offers a standardized way to compare and evaluate the intrinsic capabilities of different robotic setups.

**C. Practical Diagnostic and Development Tool:**
The scaling law and the `c` parameter offer a practical methodology for guiding the development, debugging, and engineering of high-precision manipulation systems.
- **Prediction:** Engineers can fit the scaling law parameters with a relatively small number of experiments to predict the ultimate precision limit `c` of their current system and estimate the data cost `N` required to reach a specific target precision.
- **Debugging:** When a high-precision task fails, the law can be used diagnostically. If performance degrades predictably according to the scaling law when evaluating lower-precision versions of the task, it suggests the system is operating near its intrinsic limit `c`. Erratic or unpredicted performance, conversely, would indicate a potential bug in the implementation or an inadequacy in a specific component, thereby guiding engineering efforts.
- **Targeted Improvement:** The ablation studies demonstrate that `c` can be improved through targeted enhancements. For example, the findings highlight that improving sensor quality (e.g., adding a wrist camera) or, critically, enhancing the clarity and reducing ambiguity in expert demonstrations (even if the expert's raw success rate is lower) can significantly lower `c` and expand the system's achievable precision. This encourages a shift from brute-force data collection to more intelligent, system-level solutions.

**D. Implications for Algorithmic Data Curation:**
The finding that demonstration clarity is more impactful than an expert's raw success rate for improving `c` opens a promising research direction in algorithmic data curation. Future work could explore methods to automatically "clean," "edit," or distill large datasets of imperfect demonstrations to extract a smaller set of high-quality, unambiguous trajectories, offering a scalable path to improving precision limits.

**E. Future Research Avenues:**
The work lays the groundwork for further investigations:
- Validation of these laws on physical robotic hardware.
- Exploration of how these scaling laws change with online learning methods like Reinforcement Learning or DAgger, beyond the Behavior Cloning paradigm.
- Expansion of the scope of tasks and system configurations to further validate the universality of the "Curse of Precision."

In conclusion, this research provides both a theoretical foundation and a practical framework for addressing the long-standing challenge of achieving high precision in robotics. It emphasizes that while data scaling is important, understanding and improving the fundamental limits of learning systems through targeted enhancements is crucial for advancing high-precision manipulation capabilities.
