# Revisiting Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning from A Cross-Domain Perspective

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces GURU, a multi-domain reinforcement learning corpus with 92K verifiable examples across six distinct reasoning domains, enabling the training of GURU-7B and GURU-32B models. These models achieve state-of-the-art general reasoning capabilities among open RL-trained LLMs, demonstrating how multi-domain RL can lead to robust, generalized reasoning abilities.

## Problem

- Current reinforcement learning (RL) for large language model (LLM) reasoning predominantly focuses on mathematics and programming code, limiting the generalizability of understanding how RL improves reasoning and hindering the development of versatile models.
- A significant bottleneck exists due to the scarcity of high-quality, diverse, and verifiable datasets suitable for RL training across a broad spectrum of reasoning domains beyond math and code.
- Existing RL-trained LLMs tend to be highly specialized, often exhibiting performance degradation when applied to diverse reasoning tasks outside their specific training distribution.

## Method

- Introduced ==GURU==, a ==multi-domain reinforcement learning corpus== comprising **92K** verifiable examples across six diverse reasoning domains: Math, Code, Science, Logic, Simulation, and Tabular.
- Developed a meticulous ==five-stage data curation pipeline== for GURU, including data sourcing from multiple datasets, conservative deduplication, domain-tailored binary reward design, heuristic filtering, and a crucial difficulty filtering stage.
- Performed large-scale RL training experiments using the ==GRPO algorithm== on single-domain and mixed-domain subsets of GURU, leveraging Qwen2.5-7B-Base and Qwen2.5-32B-Base models, and systematically evaluated them on comprehensive benchmark suites.

## Results

- GURU-7B and GURU-32B models establish new state-of-the-art performance among open models RL-trained with publicly available data, achieving average scores of **43.29%** (**+9.0%** over best baselines) and **54.24%** (**+6.7%** over best baselines) respectively across a 17-task evaluation suite.
- Unlike domain-specific baselines, GURU models demonstrate superior and well-balanced performance across all evaluated domains (Math, Code, Science, Logic, Simulation, Tabular) and unseen generalization tasks.
- Pass@k analysis indicates task-dependent patterns where RL expands the reasoning boundary on complex, less pretraining-exposed tasks like the synthetic Zebra Puzzle, providing evidence of genuine skill acquisition, while on tasks like AIME (Math), it aligns with knowledge elicitation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RL's impact on LLM reasoning is highly domain-dependent: it primarily elicits existing knowledge in domains frequently encountered during pretraining (Math, Code, Science) and facilitates genuine skill acquisition in less-exposed domains (Logic, Simulation, Tabular).
> - Training on a uniformly mixed, diverse dataset is remarkably effective, enabling models to achieve performance comparable to, and often surpassing, those trained exclusively on single-domain data, indicating enhanced general reasoning with minimal apparent interference.
> - Training on difficulty-filtered (harder) in-domain data consistently improves in-domain performance but can lead to degradation on easier cross-domain tasks, suggesting a risk of negative transfer if not carefully managed for beneficial cross-domain transfer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The rapid advancements in large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated impressive reasoning capabilities across various domains. Reinforcement learning (RL) has emerged as a crucial post-training technique for further enhancing these capabilities, as evidenced by frontier models like OpenAI-O3 and DeepSeek-R1. However, the prevailing trend in open-source RL for LLM reasoning has been an exclusive focus on highly specific domains, predominantly mathematics and programming code. This concentration stems from the relative ease of obtaining verifiable ground truth and designing automated reward signals for these structured domains (e.g., correct numerical answers, passing unit tests).

This narrow focus has led to two critical limitations in the broader research landscape:
1. **Limited Generalizability of Understanding**: Current insights into how RL improves LLM reasoning (e.g., whether it teaches new skills or merely elicits existing knowledge) are largely derived from math and code contexts. These findings may not generalize to other, less structured or pretraining-exposed reasoning domains.
2. **Specialized Models with Poor Cross-Domain Performance**: Models trained exclusively on math or code, while excelling in their specialized areas, often exhibit significant performance degradation when applied to diverse reasoning tasks outside their training distribution. This hinders the development of truly general-purpose reasoning LLMs.

This paper directly addresses these limitations by arguing for the necessity of multi-domain RL research for LLM reasoning. It acknowledges the bottleneck of scarce reliable reward signals and curated training data across diverse domains. By introducing GURU, a carefully curated, multi-domain RL corpus, the authors aim to provide a foundational public resource to overcome this data scarcity. The work systematically revisits established hypotheses about RL's mechanisms in LLMs (e.g., the "elicitation vs. skill acquisition" debate) from a cross-domain perspective, offering a more nuanced understanding. Furthermore, it demonstrates that training on a diverse dataset can yield models with robust general reasoning capabilities, thereby pushing the boundaries of open-source LLM development for reasoning. This positions the paper as a crucial step towards developing more versatile and generally intelligent LLMs through RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations driving this research are rooted in the limitations of the current landscape of RL for LLM reasoning:

1. **Address the Scarcity of Reliable Multi-Domain RL Data**: The most significant bottleneck identified is the lack of high-quality, diverse, and verifiable datasets suitable for RL training across a broad spectrum of reasoning domains. Math and code benefit from readily available, verifiable signals, but domains like logic, simulation, and tabular reasoning lack such well-curated resources. The core motivation is to fill this gap by creating **GURU**.
2. **Enable Systematic Re-evaluation of RL's Role in LLM Reasoning**: Current understanding of RL's fundamental mechanisms (e.g., whether it truly teaches new skills or merely refines existing knowledge) is largely based on observations from math and code. The authors are motivated to challenge or confirm these insights by exploring if these mechanisms are *domain-dependent*. This involves systematically performing RL experiments across diverse domains using the GURU corpus.
3. **Promote the Development of General-Purpose Reasoning LLMs**: The existing RL-trained LLMs tend to be highly specialized. A key objective is to demonstrate that multi-domain RL training can lead to models that excel across a wide array of reasoning tasks, fostering more robust and generalized reasoning capabilities, rather than just domain-specific expertise.
4. **Contribute to Open Science**: Motivated by the open-source community's efforts, the authors aim to release GURU, the trained models (GURU-7B/32B), and all associated training and evaluation code. This serves to facilitate future research, foster reproducibility, and accelerate progress in general-purpose reasoning within the broader AI community.

In essence, the paper seeks to broaden the scope of RL for LLM reasoning by providing a much-needed diverse dataset, leveraging it to gain a more comprehensive understanding of RL's impact, and ultimately demonstrating the feasibility and benefits of training LLMs for general reasoning through a multi-domain RL approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers around the creation of the GURU dataset and its subsequent use in large-scale RL training experiments, complemented by a systematic cross-domain analysis.

#### 3.1. GURU Data Construction Pipeline

The GURU corpus, comprising 92K verifiable examples, is built through a meticulous five-stage data curation pipeline (illustrated in Figure 2):

1. **Data Sourcing**: Data is collected from six diverse reasoning-intensive domains:
 - **Math**: Consolidates existing high-quality datasets like OR1, DAPO, and DeepScaler, which primarily derive from competition problems.
 - **Code**: Includes programming problems from LeetCode, TACO-Verified, PrimeIntellect, and LiveCodeBench, covering real-world, curated, and synthetic tasks.
 - **Science**: Primarily sourced from WebInstruct-Verified, focusing on university and PhD-level science questions.
 - **Logic**: A mix of existing datasets (ARC-AGI 1 & 2, BARC) and newly synthesized tasks including Zebra Puzzle, Ordering Puzzles, and Graph Puzzle, focusing on symbolic and constraint-based reasoning.
 - **Simulation**: Uses Code I/O (PyEdu) which involves predicting outputs or inferring inputs from code snippets without execution.
 - **Tabular**: Repurposes HiTab and MultiHierTT, which involve single and multi-table reasoning over structured data.

2. **Data Deduplication**: A conservative deduplication strategy is applied, removing shorter strings if they are strict substrings of longer ones to mitigate content overlap, particularly in Math and Code domains where shared upstream sources are common.

3. **Reward Design**: Binary rewards (1 for correct, 0 for incorrect) are used, tailored to each domain's verification needs:
 - **Rule-Based Matching**: Applied in Math, Logic, Simulation, and Tabular. Requires the model to produce answers in designated formats (e.g., `\boxed{}`, `<answer>`), followed by normalization and strict matching. Math problems additionally use symbolic program verification for algebraic variations.
 - **Execution-Based Verification**: Used for Code. Model-generated functions/scripts are executed against test cases, requiring all tests to pass for a reward of 1. Fuzzy comparison handles formatting inconsistencies for stdin/stdout tasks.
 - **Model-Based Verification**: Employed for Science due to open-ended answers. A 1.5B verifier model (from Ma et al., 2025) assesses semantic entailment between the model's response and the reference answer.

4. **Heuristic Filtering**: Aims to improve reward verifiability and stability. Samples with overly long prompts, invalid reference outputs, or excessively large inputs are removed. Instance difficulty is controlled for complex tasks. Categories exceeding 10k examples are uniformly randomly sampled to ensure manageable size and balanced coverage.

5. **Difficulty Filtering**: This crucial stage identifies and removes overly easy, potentially noisy (consistently failed by strong models), or anomalous samples (weak model outperforms strong model, suggesting label issues). A "difficulty gap" ($P_{strong} - P_{weak}$) is used as a proxy for learnability, with domain-specific criteria (e.g., more aggressive filtering for Math).

#### 3.2. Experimental Approach

1. **Cross-Domain Analysis (Section 3)**:
 - **Dataset**: A subset of 3K training samples from each of the six GURU domains, forming an 18K sample `GURU-18K` mixed dataset.
 - **Models**: Qwen2.5-7B-Base and Qwen2.5-32B-Base.
 - **Training**: RL training (using GRPO algorithm within the verl framework) is performed separately on each single-domain subset and on the combined `GURU-18K` mixed dataset.
 - **Evaluation**: Models are evaluated on a suite of 13 domain-specific benchmarks to observe cross-domain generalization and transferability patterns.

2. **Main Experiment (Section 4)**:
 - **Dataset**: The complete GURU dataset (92K examples).
 - **Models**: GURU-7B and GURU-32B, trained from Qwen2.5-Base-7B/32B.
 - **RL Algorithm**: GRPO, using verl framework. Hyperparameters include AdamW optimizer with learning rate $1 \times 10^{-6}$, batch size 512, 16 responses sampled per prompt (temp=1.0), mini-batch size 64, max 4k input/8k generation tokens, and clipping parameter $\epsilon=0.2$. No KL or entropy loss is applied.
 - **Baselines**: Compared against state-of-the-art open models RL-trained on open data, including General Reasoner, Open-Reasoner-Zero (ORZ), and SimpleRL-Zoo. Baselines using SFT-trained models or RL on top of SFT models are excluded for fair comparison.
 - **Evaluation Suite**: A comprehensive 17-task offline benchmark suite across six domains and two unseen generalization tasks (IFEval, LiveBench), with specific sampling and generation parameters (e.g., temp=1.0, top-p=0.7). Online evaluation is used for monitoring training progress.

#### 3.3. Analysis of Pass@k Curves

The paper conducts a detailed Pass@k analysis to understand the "reasoning boundary" of models, investigating how RL affects the probability of obtaining a correct solution within *k* attempts. This analysis explores task-dependent patterns, the effect of model scale, and the influence of sampling hyperparameters (temperature, top-p) on Pass@k behavior.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings, both from the systematic cross-domain analysis and the performance of the large-scale GURU models:

#### 4.1. Findings from Cross-Domain RL Analysis

- **Differential Transferability**: The most critical finding is that RL's impact on LLM reasoning is **highly domain-dependent**.
 - **Elicitation in Pretrained-Heavy Domains**: Domains frequently encountered during pretraining (Math, Code, Science) readily show performance gains from **cross-domain RL training**. This suggests that RL primarily elicits or refines existing knowledge in these domains, even with data from other domains.
 - **Skill Acquisition in Less Exposed Domains**: Domains with limited pretraining exposure (Logic, Simulation, Tabular) require **in-domain RL training** to achieve meaningful improvements. Cross-domain training provides minimal gains here, indicating that RL is more likely to facilitate genuine skill acquisition or the learning of new reasoning pathways in these novel contexts.
 - **Task Difficulty Matters**: Easier tasks within domains (e.g., MATH500, HumanEval) exhibit positive cross-domain transfer, while more challenging benchmarks (e.g., AIME24, ARC-AGI) show considerably less improvement from cross-domain training, often requiring specific in-domain data.
- **Mixed-Domain Training Effectiveness**: RL training on a uniformly mixed dataset (GURU-18K) is remarkably effective. Models trained on this combined data achieve performance comparable to, and often surpass, those trained exclusively on single-domain data. This suggests that a diverse mixed dataset can significantly enhance general reasoning capabilities with minimal apparent interference between domains.
- **Reward and Response-Length Dynamics**: RL training exhibits strong domain effects on response length. While some domains (Science, Math) lead to more verbose outputs, others (Code, Logic, Tabular) show output contraction. Joint training can reshape these dynamics, though some length priors remain robust. Reward signals generally climb steeply across all domains during joint training, demonstrating positive cross-domain transfer.
- **Effects of Training Data Difficulty**: Training on difficulty-filtered (harder) Math data consistently improves **in-domain performance** (e.g., +6.3% on AMC, +5.9% on AIME24). However, it can lead to **degradation on easier cross-domain tasks** (e.g., -9.2% on HumanEval, -3.0% on HiTab), suggesting a risk of negative transfer if not carefully managed. For beneficial cross-domain transfer, a balanced distribution of training data difficulties or explicit inclusion of diverse domains is more effective.

#### 4.2. Performance of GURU-7B and GURU-32B Models

- **State-of-the-Art General Reasoning**: GURU-7B and GURU-32B establish new state-of-the-art performance among open models RL-trained with publicly available data. GURU-7B achieves an average score of 43.29% (outperforming best baselines by 9.0%), and GURU-32B achieves 54.24% (outperforming best baselines by 6.7%) across the 17-task evaluation suite spanning six domains.
- **Balanced Performance**: Unlike domain-specific baselines that exhibit significant weaknesses outside their specialization, GURU models demonstrate superior and well-balanced performance across all evaluated domains (Math, Code, Science, Logic, Simulation, Tabular, and unseen generalization tasks).
- **Expansion of Reasoning Boundary on Complex Tasks**: On complex, less pretraining-exposed tasks like the synthetic Zebra Puzzle, GURU-7B and GURU-32B effectively expand the reasoning boundary of their base models, providing concrete evidence of genuine skill acquisition.

#### 4.3. Pass@k Analysis Findings

- **Task-Dependent Patterns**: Pass@k curves vary across tasks. On AIME (Math), GURU-7B curves cross the base model's at large *k*, consistent with the "elicitation" hypothesis. However, on Zebra Puzzle (Logic), GURU models consistently expand the reasoning boundary, indicating new capabilities.
- **Effect of Model Scale**: Larger models (32B vs. 7B) show different Pass@k behaviors, with 32B models often showing sustained gains at larger *k*, suggesting that stronger base models might uncover new reasoning trajectories more easily with RL finetuning.
- **Influence of Sampling Hyperparameters**: The study found that RL training can lead to a collapse in output entropy, narrowing Pass@k at larger *k*. Adjusting decoding parameters (e.g., higher sampling temperature, larger top-p) can counteract this, broadening exploration and improving Pass@k at higher *k*. This highlights the sensitivity of Pass@k as an indicator.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research makes several significant contributions and has substantial potential impact on the field of large language models and artificial intelligence:

1. **Creation of GURU, a Foundational Multi-Domain RL Corpus**: GURU addresses a critical data scarcity bottleneck, providing a meticulously curated, diverse, and verifiable dataset of 92K examples across six distinct reasoning domains. By open-sourcing GURU, the authors provide a much-needed public resource that will enable other researchers to systematically investigate and advance general-purpose reasoning with RL.
2. **Nuanced Understanding of RL's Role in LLM Reasoning**: The paper challenges and refines the prevailing hypothesis that RL primarily elicits existing knowledge. By demonstrating the domain-dependent nature of RL's effects—acting as an elicitor in pretraining-heavy domains and facilitating genuine skill acquisition in less-exposed ones—it provides a more comprehensive and actionable understanding of how RL contributes to LLM capabilities. This insight is crucial for designing more effective RL training strategies.
3. **Advancement of General-Purpose Reasoning LLMs**: By successfully training GURU-7B and GURU-32B models, which achieve state-of-the-art performance among open models RL-trained with public data, the research provides a concrete demonstration of how multi-domain RL can lead to robust, general reasoning capabilities. These models are not merely specialists but perform well across a broad range of tasks, pushing the frontier of open-source LLMs towards more versatile intelligence.
4. **Guidance for Future RL Training Strategies**: The detailed analyses, particularly on cross-domain transferability, response length dynamics, and the effects of data difficulty and sampling parameters on Pass@k, offer valuable insights for designing future RL training pipelines. These findings inform how to select and balance training data, interpret model behaviors, and optimize decoding for improved reasoning.
5. **Commitment to Open Science**: The open release of the GURU dataset, the trained GURU models, and the full training and evaluation code (`https://github.com/LLM360/Reasoning360`) is a major contribution. This fosters transparency, reproducibility, and collaborative research, accelerating the development of general-purpose reasoning LLMs across the entire AI community. It aligns with the ethos of projects like LLM360, promoting a more democratic and rapid advancement in AI.

In summary, this work moves beyond the narrow confines of previous RL research for LLMs, providing both a crucial dataset and a deeper understanding of the mechanisms at play, ultimately enabling the development of more capable and generalizable reasoning models for the benefit of the broader research community.
