# MOSAIC: Skill-Centric Manipulation Planning with Physics Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> MOSAIC plans long-horizon manipulation by composing a library of generic, imperfect skills without symbolic abstractions. Generator skills carve out islands of competence as graph nodes, connector skills solve boundary value problems as edges, and a statistical oracle picks which skill to invoke where, with a physics simulator rolling out every candidate. Shortest-path search over this mosaic graph yields physically grounded plans that beat sequential-chaining and roadmap baselines on success rate and planning time.

## Problem

- Policy-learning methods handle short-horizon tasks but fail at long-horizon reasoning and skill composition, needing large data and generalizing poorly to new conditions.
- Task-and-motion planning depends on hand-built symbolic representations that miss geometric and physical detail, making it brittle and laborious to extend to novel tasks.
- Existing skill-chaining planners search goal-directed and sequentially from the start state, forcing skills to declare initiation, termination and effect sets, and missing non-obvious intermediate steps.

## Method

- Skill-centric formulation splits the library into ==generators==, which produce local trajectories unconditioned on start and goal, and ==connectors==, which link them by solving boundary value problems.
- A ==mosaic graph== is built multi-directionally: generator rollouts become nodes carrying skill, parameters and trajectory; connector solutions become edges; Dijkstra search extracts the least-cost skill sequence.
- A domain-independent ==statistical oracle== scores skills by success rate plus a noised exploration bonus, chooses generator-versus-connector by node-to-edge ratio, and penalizes repeatedly failing node pairs in nearest-neighbor selection.

## Results

- Across three plate-transport scenarios of rising difficulty, **MOSAIC** kept high success rates while remaining competitive in plan length and planning time, outperforming all four baselines in the complex settings.
- Sequential Skills-as-Options and cross-entropy-method baselines solved simple tasks but degraded sharply with clutter and movable objects, limited by directional search, local optimization and no backtracking.
- The skill library was deliberately imperfect: the diffusion push policy reached only **70%** success over motions up to 25cm, yet real UR10e runs with RealSense pose estimation tracked simulation trends.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Planning outward from where skills demonstrably work, rather than forward from the start state, finds intermediate steps that goal-directed sequential chaining structurally cannot reach.
> - A physics simulator used as the world model replaces hand-authored preconditions and effects, letting unreliable learned policies compose without symbolic abstraction.
> - Fixed exploration strategies gave uneven success across scenarios; an adaptive statistical oracle needing no task-specific tuning is what made performance robust.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of autonomous robots capable of performing complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks in unstructured environments remains a significant challenge in robotics. Current approaches to this problem typically fall into two categories: policy learning methods and Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) frameworks.

Policy learning methods, often employing techniques like diffusion models or reinforcement learning, have demonstrated success in short-horizon tasks. However, they frequently encounter difficulties with long-horizon reasoning and complex skill composition due to substantial data requirements and limited generalization capabilities without extensive retraining. Their exploration is often localized around current states or immediate goals.

Task and Motion Planning (TAMP) frameworks address long-horizon problems by integrating abstract symbolic task planning with geometric motion planning. While TAMP offers hierarchical reasoning, it relies on explicit symbolic representations that can be labor-intensive to construct, may not adequately capture detailed geometric and physical interactions, and can compromise robustness in real-world scenarios. Approaches to TAMP often involve manual engineering of symbolic representations or learning modules that interface with these symbolic planners, which itself presents challenges in data collection and accuracy.

Skill-based methods aim to compose a library of predefined skills (e.g., learned policies or analytic controllers) to solve longer tasks. These methods often involve selecting skills and parameterizing them. Many existing skill-based and general planning approaches predominantly utilize goal-directed search strategies, either forward from the start state or backward from the goal. This directional search can be inefficient, especially when solutions necessitate non-obvious intermediate steps that do not provide immediate indications of progress toward a distant goal. Furthermore, skills are often imperfect, succeeding only under specific conditions. Prior frameworks have not explicitly focused on reasoning about the conditions under which skills are likely to succeed to guide planning efforts.

MOSAIC positions itself within this landscape by addressing the limitations of existing methods. It proposes a skill-centric, multi-directional planning approach that avoids strict symbolic representations and extends the capabilities of skill-based planning. By integrating high-fidelity physics simulation directly into the planning loop, MOSAIC aims to provide physically grounded solutions without relying on hand-coded preconditions or effects. It distinguishes itself from goal-directed search by focusing on identifying "regions of high skill competence" and orchestrating skill composition in a multi-directional manner, making it suitable for long-horizon tasks with non-obvious intermediate states. The work contributes to the development of more general-purpose robots capable of adapting to novel tasks through flexible skill composition.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient and robust algorithmic framework, named MOSAIC, for planning long-horizon manipulation motions by composing a library of predefined, potentially imperfect skills.

The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges inherent in current robotic manipulation planning:
- **Long-horizon reasoning and skill composition:** Robots operating in unstructured environments, such as homes or offices, require the ability to solve complex tasks that involve sequences of many actions. This necessitates not only sequencing skills but also identifying the specific conditions under which each skill is likely to succeed. Current policy learning methods often struggle with this due to their short-horizon nature and data demands.
- **Limitations of symbolic representations:** Traditional TAMP frameworks rely on symbolic representations that can be brittle, laborious to construct, and may fail to capture the geometric and physical details crucial for robust interaction in the real world. The aim is to move away from hand-coded preconditions and effects.
- **Inefficiency of goal-directed search:** Many existing planning algorithms employ a goal-directed search, exploring outward from the start state or backward from the goal. This strategy can be inefficient for tasks requiring non-obvious intermediate steps that do not immediately appear to advance toward the goal. Such steps, though essential for task completion, may be overlooked by purely goal-directed methods.
- **Imperfect skills:** Learned or analytical skills are typically not universally successful; their efficacy depends on environmental conditions, object configurations, and their inherent design limitations. A robust planning framework needs to explicitly reason about these "islands of competence" where skills are most likely to succeed.
- **Need for physically grounded solutions:** To ensure robustness and reliability in real-world execution, planning decisions must be grounded in physical reality. This necessitates a world model capable of accurately predicting action outcomes, a role for which high-fidelity physics simulators are increasingly suitable.

MOSAIC aims to overcome these issues by developing a framework that:
1. **Positions skills as central to the planning process:** The framework is designed to actively utilize skills to guide the exploration toward states where they are effective.
2. **Leverages high-fidelity physics simulation:** This allows for in-the-loop evaluation of skill feasibility and outcomes, providing physically grounded planning decisions without the need for explicit symbolic definitions of preconditions and effects.
3. **Employs a multi-directional search strategy:** Instead of being constrained by traditional goal-directed search, MOSAIC explores multiple directions simultaneously, anchoring its search in promising regions of the state space identified by skill competence.
4. **Utilizes two complementary skill families:** "Generators" identify "islands of competence" by proposing effective skill trajectories and world configurations, while "Connectors" link these skill trajectories by solving boundary value problems.
5. **Achieves efficient discovery of physically-grounded solutions:** By focusing planning efforts on regions of high competence, MOSAIC seeks to efficiently find solutions for complex, long-horizon manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

MOSAIC is an algorithmic framework designed for long-horizon skill-centric planning, employing a multi-directional search guided by a high-fidelity physics simulator. The core idea is to construct a directed multigraph, termed a "mosaic graph," where nodes represent skill trajectories and edges represent connections between them.

**Problem Definition:**
The planning problem is defined as finding a sequence of parameterized skills from a given library that transforms an initial world state ($x_{start}$) into a state satisfying a goal condition ($\xi_{goal}$).
- **State Space ($X$):** Represents the complete world state, including the robot's configuration ($Q_R$) and the configurations of all movable objects ($Q_{O_i}$). The system is typically underactuated, meaning the robot's degrees of freedom are less than the state space dimension.
- **Skills ($\sigma$):** Temporally extended actions represented as a tuple ($\pi_\sigma$, $\Theta_\sigma$), where $\pi_\sigma$ is the skill policy and $\Theta_\sigma$ are its parameters. Skills produce trajectories ($\tau$).
- **Skill Categories:**
 - **Generators ($G_i$):** Parameterized skills that generate trajectories without requiring specified start and goal states, identifying regions of competence.
 - **Connectors ($C_j$):** Conditional parameterized skills that generate trajectories linking specified start and goal conditions, often between the end of one skill's trajectory and the beginning of another's.
- **Solution Criteria:** A valid skill sequence must satisfy three conditions: the first trajectory starts at $x_{start}$, the last trajectory ends in a state satisfying $\xi_{goal}$, and adjacent trajectories connect continuously. Skills must also produce valid trajectories (e.g., collision-free, respecting robot dynamics).

**MOSAIC Algorithmic Approach (Algorithm 1):**
1. **Graph Initialization:** A directed multigraph (mosaic graph) is initialized.
2. **Initial Generator Invocation:** All available generator skills are invoked. Valid skill trajectories produced by generators are added as disconnected nodes to the mosaic graph, each associated with a cost. The start state is added as a special node with zero cost.
3. **Main Planning Loop:** The algorithm iteratively expands the mosaic graph until a path from $x_{start}$ to a goal-satisfying state is found.
 - **Oracle Guidance:** An "oracle" module orchestrates the search by choosing which skill to invoke next (generator or connector) and sampling its parameters.
 - **Connector Skill Application:** If a connector skill is chosen, the oracle selects two existing skill trajectories (nodes) from the graph (or a node and the goal condition) and attempts to connect them. If a valid connecting trajectory is generated, it is added as an edge to the mosaic graph.
 - **Generator Skill Application:** If a generator skill is chosen, it produces a new trajectory, which is added as a new node to the graph if valid.
 - **Physics Simulation for Validation:** Throughout this process, a physics simulator is used as a world model to forward simulate skill outcomes, verify contact dynamics, and prune infeasible plans. This grounds planning decisions in physical reality. Skill outcomes may be stochastic; MOSAIC can generate multiple trajectories and use the fraction of valid ones to estimate a confidence (reflected in the cost).
4. **Path Retrieval:** Once a path from the start state to a goal state exists within the mosaic graph, the algorithm returns the least-cost path.

**The Oracle Module:**
The oracle is a guidance module that balances exploration (using generators to find new regions of competence) and exploitation (using connectors to link existing regions). The proposed oracle is domain-independent and statistical, making decisions based on the evolving structure of the search graph.
- **Skill Type Selection:** The oracle decides whether to choose a generator or a connector. The probability of selecting a connector increases as the ratio of nodes to edges in the graph grows, promoting connectivity once a sufficient number of islands are discovered.
- **Individual Skill Selection:** A specific skill $\sigma$ is chosen by maximizing a score function that balances its historical success rate ($s_\sigma$) with an exploration bonus based on its invocation count ($t_\sigma$), using a weighting parameter $\alpha$ and a noise term.
- **Node Pair Selection for Connection (for Connectors):** The oracle determines which nodes (or node-to-goal) to attempt connecting. This can involve:
 1. Random selection: Connecting a random node to its nearest neighbor.
 2. Goal bias: Selecting a node connected to a terminal (goal-satisfying) node and attempting to connect it to its nearest neighbor.
 3. Start bias: Selecting a node connected to the start node and attempting to connect it to its nearest neighbor.
 4. Unification bias: Selecting a pair of nodes where one is connected to the start and the other to a terminal node, aiming to bridge the gap.
 - To prevent redundant efforts, the oracle penalizes pairs with repeated failed connection attempts by artificially increasing their distance, discouraging their re-selection.

**Theoretical Analysis:**
The paper provides a proof sketch for MOSAIC's **Probabilistic Completeness**. Assuming deterministic skill-trajectory generation, MOSAIC is probabilistically complete if, as the number of iterations approaches infinity, it can discover any feasible solution. This is ensured because the oracle's sampling strategy guarantees that, given infinite time, all possible generator skills with all parameter configurations will be invoked, and all possible node-pairs will be attempted to be connected using all connector skills.

**Skill Library Implementation:**
For experimental validation, MOSAIC utilized a library of four skills implemented using the Sapien physics engine:
- **Push (Generator + Connector):** Uses a learned diffusion policy (Diffusion Policy) to generate pushing motions. As a generator, it creates new states with local push trajectories. As a connector, it links object poses between world states. It employs a motion planner for pre-push robot configurations. The policy was trained on 10,000 examples of object pushing.
- **Pick (Generator):** Computes antipodal grasp poses based on object geometry and robot kinematics. It executes screw-based approach-grasp-retract trajectories. Grasp selection involves point cloud acquisition, surface normal estimation, sampling antipodal candidates, scoring based on normal alignment, and Inverse Kinematics (IK) verification. Screw motion interpolation is used for smooth trajectory generation.
- **Transport (Connector):** Moves grasped objects to satisfy goal conditions using motion planning (e.g., RRT-Connect). This skill fails if the object is not grasped or the goal is not met.
- **Rearrange (Connector, Scenario 3 only):** Combines pick-and-place and push policies to reposition multiple objects, determining the appropriate policy for each.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of MOSAIC was evaluated through experiments in diverse simulated environments and validated on a real UR10e robot. Three manipulation scenarios of increasing complexity were tested, providing only a goal condition ("plate in bin") and a library of generic skills.

**Experimental Scenarios:**
1. **Basic Transport:** The robot must push a plate to the table edge to enable grasping, then pick it up and place it in a bin. This requires discovering an initial reorientation step.
2. **Transport in Clutter:** Adds static obstacles around the plate, requiring the planner to navigate without collisions.
3. **Transport Among Movable Objects:** Introduces a second movable object that may obstruct plate access. The planner must decide whether to work around it or manipulate it (e.g., by pushing it elsewhere) to clear space for the primary task.

**Evaluation Metrics:**
- **Success Rate:** Fraction of trials where the goal condition was satisfied.
- **Plan Length:** Number of skills in the solution sequence, indicating solution quality and task complexity.
- **Planning Time:** Computational efficiency of the algorithm.

**Baseline Algorithms:**
MOSAIC was compared against four long-horizon planners that compose skill primitives:
1. **Skills as Options:** Implements sequential skill chaining (similar to breadth-first search over world states), where each new skill starts where the previous one ended. It uses modified generator skills that are start-state conditioned.
2. **CEM (Cross-Entropy Method):** A receding horizon planner that samples skill-parameter sequences, selects top candidates, refines the sampling distribution, executes the first skill, and replans. It relies on local optimization.
3. **MOSAIC-Roadmap:** A variant that removes sequential dependencies by freely generating skill trajectories and connecting them via a PRM-inspired roadmap. It has two phases: roadmap generation and path computation using Dijkstra's algorithm.
4. **Incremental MOSAIC-Roadmap:** An extension of MOSAIC-Roadmap that iteratively adds nodes and edges to the roadmap and searches for a path, ensuring continuous progress until a solution is found or deemed impossible. This baseline is conceptually closest to MOSAIC but lacks the sophisticated oracle guidance.

**Experimental Results:**
The results, summarized in Figure 3, indicate that MOSAIC consistently achieved high success rates across all three scenarios while maintaining competitive solution quality and planning times.

- **Success Rates:** MOSAIC demonstrated robust performance, indicating its ability to solve complex long-horizon problems effectively, including those requiring non-obvious intermediate steps (e.g., pushing an object to clear space).
- **Performance of Baselines:**
 - **Skills as Options and CEM** performed adequately on simpler tasks but showed significant degradation as task complexity increased. This was attributed to their directional exploration strategies (sequential chaining, local optimization) and limited scalability in vast search spaces. CEM's lack of backtracking was also a limitation.
 - **MOSAIC-Roadmap and Incremental MOSAIC-Roadmap** showed some benefits from their more flexible exploration (removing sequential dependencies). However, their varying success rates across scenarios highlighted limitations in their fixed search strategies compared to MOSAIC's adaptive guidance.
- **Oracle's Role:** The performance comparison suggests the importance of MOSAIC's adaptive oracle module. The oracle's ability to dynamically adjust the search strategy—balancing exploration (generators) and exploitation (connectors), and intelligently selecting node pairs for connection—enabled MOSAIC to outperform baselines in complex scenarios.
- **Solution Quality and Planning Time:** MOSAIC achieved its high success rates with competitive planning times and reasonable plan lengths, demonstrating efficiency in discovering effective skill sequences.
- **Real-World Validation:** Hardware validation tests using a UR10e robot showed similar trends, supporting the sim-to-real transferability of the approach.

These findings suggest that MOSAIC's feasibility-driven, multi-directional search, coupled with physics-informed reasoning and adaptive oracle-guided exploration, represents an advancement in robust and efficient long-horizon manipulation planning compared to traditional directed or less adaptive roadmap-based approaches.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

MOSAIC introduces a distinct approach to skill-centric planning, characterized by skills actively guiding the planning process toward regions of high competence rather than being constrained by strictly sequential, goal-directed chaining. This framework offers several significant contributions and has potential impacts on robotic manipulation:

**Significance:**
- **Novel Planning Paradigm:** MOSAIC shifts from traditional goal-directed or start-directed search to a multi-directional, feasibility-driven exploration strategy. By explicitly reasoning about where skills are likely to succeed and anchoring its search in these "islands of competence," the framework addresses the challenge of identifying non-obvious intermediate steps in long-horizon tasks, a common pitfall for many existing planners.
- **Physics-Informed Skill Composition:** The modular architecture of MOSAIC, which utilizes "generators" for local trajectory production and "connectors" for linking them through boundary value problems, is critically enabled by high-fidelity physics simulation. This integration allows the framework to evaluate skill execution outcomes in a physically grounded manner, thereby eliminating the need for laborious, hand-coded definitions of skill preconditions and effects that often limit the flexibility and robustness of TAMP systems. This represents a move toward more data-driven and simulation-verified planning.
- **Enhanced Performance, Generalization, and Scalability:** Experimental validation across varied and increasingly complex manipulation tasks in both simulation and the real world demonstrated that MOSAIC consistently achieves high success rates. It also exhibits competitive planning times and solution quality compared to baseline methods, showcasing its robustness, generalization capabilities to novel tasks, and scalability to scenarios involving multiple movable objects and cluttered environments. The adaptive oracle-guided exploration contributes to this improved efficiency and effectiveness.

**Potential Impact:**
- **More Capable and Adaptable Robots:** By enabling the flexible composition of generic, imperfect skills for long-horizon tasks, MOSAIC contributes to the development of more versatile robots that can operate in unstructured and dynamic environments. This could allow robots to autonomously tackle a wider range of novel tasks without extensive retraining or manual engineering for each new scenario.
- **Reduced Engineering Effort:** The reliance on physics simulation to evaluate skill feasibility reduces the need for explicit, hand-coded symbolic task specifications or detailed models of skill effects. This can accelerate the deployment of robotic systems by lowering the burden of manual domain engineering.
- **Advancements in Skill-Based Robotics:** MOSAIC provides a new paradigm for how skills are leveraged in planning. Its focus on skill competence and multi-directional search could inspire further research in designing skill libraries, learning better skill representations, and developing more sophisticated guidance mechanisms for planning.
- **Future Research Directions:** The paper identifies several promising avenues for future work, including developing more sophisticated oracle modules (potentially integrating large language models for high-level reasoning), extending the framework to handle partial observability, and further capitalizing on the parallelization capabilities of physics simulators. These directions suggest that MOSAIC could serve as a foundational framework for subsequent innovations in robotic planning.

Overall, MOSAIC contributes a robust and efficient framework for long-horizon manipulation planning that addresses key limitations of prior approaches by integrating skills and high-fidelity physics simulation within an adaptive, multi-directional search strategy. This work advances the progress toward more general-purpose robotic manipulation in complex, real-world settings.
