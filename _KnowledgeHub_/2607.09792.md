# A Comprehensive Survey and Systematic Real-World Evaluation of Embodied Vision-and-Language Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey of vision-and-language navigation that organizes methods along two orthogonal axes, action paradigm (hierarchical versus monolithic) and model paradigm (discriminative versus generative), and pairs the taxonomy with a physical-robot benchmark over 10 scenes and 200 episodes. A representative monolithic RGB-only system falls from 61% simulated success to 22% real-world, while a hierarchical waypoint system reaches 51%.

## Problem

- Prior VLN surveys cover narrow slices such as foundation-model or aerial navigation, leaving no unified taxonomy spanning both action-space and model-paradigm design choices.
- VLN progress is benchmark-driven and simulation-bound, with reported success rates above 85% in idealized simulators that say little about physical deployment.
- Standard metrics judge success by stopping within a fixed distance, ignoring semantic correctness of the stop and never measuring collisions during execution.

## Method

- A ==two-axis taxonomy== classifying methods by ==action paradigm== (hierarchical waypoint planning versus monolithic end-to-end control) and ==model paradigm== (discriminative candidate selection versus generative autoregressive decoding), plus dataset, simulator, and metric catalogues.
- A ==real-robot evaluation platform==: differential-drive chassis, Jetson Orin Nano, Mid-360 LiDAR, swappable panoramic or RGB-D camera, onboard SLAM and control, VLN inference offloaded to a remote L40 server.
- Two deployment metrics: ==Strict Success Rate==, requiring both 3 m proximity and semantic correctness of the stopping location, and ==Collision Rate==, which terminates an episode on any contact.

## Results

- Across **200** episodes in **10** scenes, the hierarchical system reached **51%** SR, **37%** SSR, **67%** OSR, versus **22%** SR, **17%** SSR, **27%** OSR for the monolithic system.
- Collision rate was **51%** for the RGB-only monolithic system against **7%** for the LiDAR-SLAM hierarchical one, whose only collisions came from stairs below the scan plane.
- On non-standard instructions the hierarchical system led on intention tasks (**35%** versus **25%** SR) but scored **0%** on backtracking against the monolithic system's **20%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Simulation leaderboards do not predict physical performance: a 61% simulated method dropped to 22% on hardware, so real-robot evaluation is not optional.
> - Architecture comparisons confound sensing; panoramic field of view, LiDAR geometry, and topological memory, not hierarchy alone, plausibly explain most of the reported gap.
> - Coarse distance-based success hides semantic stopping failures; the strict metric cut hierarchical success from 51% to 37%, which matters most for downstream manipulation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Navigation is a foundational capability for autonomous systems. Historically, approaches have largely been model-driven, relying on structured models and prior assumptions about environments. This dependency limits their robustness and generalization in unstructured or uncertain real-world settings. Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) addresses these limitations by integrating natural language understanding with visual perception, enabling robots to follow free-form instructions in visually complex, often unseen environments. This approach aligns with human cognitive processes for navigation.

The VLN field has experienced rapid growth, driven by advancements in large-scale datasets, high-fidelity simulators, and sophisticated deep learning, reinforcement learning, and large foundation models. While significant performance gains have been observed in simulated environments, a critical gap exists in systematically evaluating these algorithms on physical robotic platforms and understanding their real-world practical effectiveness. Previous surveys have reviewed specific aspects of VLN, such as foundation models, embodied intelligence, or aerial VLN. This paper distinguishes itself by offering a comprehensive, structured methodological taxonomy of VLN methods (categorizing them by action paradigms – hierarchical vs. monolithic, and model paradigms – discriminative vs. generative) coupled with a systematic real-world evaluation of representative systems. The work aims to bridge the gap between simulation-based benchmarks and practical deployment, specifically addressing the pronounced simulation-to-real (sim-to-real) performance discrepancies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from two observations:
1. **Limitations of Traditional Navigation Systems:** Most existing robotic navigation systems are model-driven, relying on strong prior assumptions about environmental structure and task objectives. This limits their generalization to open-ended scenarios and their ability to cope with complex uncertainty in real-world environments.
2. **Gap in VLN Research:** While Vision-and-Language Navigation (VLN) offers a data-driven paradigm to address these limitations by integrating natural language understanding with visual perception, there is a lack of systematic methodological taxonomy and real-world validation of VLN systems. Despite achieving high success rates in simulation, the practical effectiveness and robustness of current VLN algorithms in physical environments remain largely underexplored. Initial observations indicate a substantial performance degradation when moving from simulation to real-world deployment, coupled with issues like sensitivity to instruction types and inadequate obstacle avoidance.

Based on these motivations, the paper sets out several key objectives:
- **Comprehensive Review and Taxonomy:** To provide a comprehensive review of the current landscape of VLN research. This includes systematically organizing state-of-the-art methods along two orthogonal dimensions: action paradigms (hierarchical and monolithic frameworks) and model paradigms (discriminative and generative approaches).
- **Critical Analysis of Methodologies:** To critically analyze the strengths and limitations of these diverse VLN methodologies.
- **Systematic Real-World Evaluation:** To conduct a systematic real-world evaluation of representative VLN system configurations on a physical robotic platform. This objective specifically aims to quantify the performance gap between simulation and real-world deployment, assess robustness, and identify practical failure modes.
- **Identification of Key Challenges and Future Directions:** To highlight critical challenges in perception, decision-making, and control that must be addressed for future VLN research, thereby guiding the development of more robust and generalizable embodied agents.
- **Reference for the Field:** To serve as a clear and comprehensive reference for understanding the VLN landscape, its evolution, key challenges, and promising directions for future research.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

This work adopts a two-pronged approach: a systematic survey of existing VLN literature and a systematic real-world evaluation of selected VLN system configurations.

**I. Survey Methodology:**
The survey systematically categorizes VLN methods based on two primary dimensions:
- **Action Paradigms:**
 - **Hierarchical Framework:** Decomposes navigation into high-level waypoint planning and low-level control. The agent first selects a high-level waypoint, then a low-level controller executes primitive actions to reach it. This is often used in discrete graph-based environments but has been extended to continuous settings by generating candidate waypoints online.
 - **Monolithic Framework:** Models an end-to-end mapping from perceptual observations to actions within a unified architecture, directly predicting low-level continuous control signals (e.g., translational distance, rotation angle).
- **Model Paradigms:**
 - **Discriminative Methods:** Select actions from a predefined discrete candidate set, typically via a classification head. These models are often task-specific.
 - **Generative Methods:** Produce actions through autoregressive generation in a continuous or open-ended space, commonly implemented with an autoregressive decoder. These methods often leverage large foundation models.

The survey reviews VLN foundations, including problem formulation (as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process, distinguishing between discrete graph-based and continuous Euclidean-based environments), task paradigms (summarizing various datasets like R2R, RxR, VLN-CE, ALFRED, and simulators like Matterport3D, Habitat, AI2-THOR), and a comprehensive suite of evaluation metrics (including Navigation Error, Trajectory Length, Success Rate (SR), Success Rate weighted by Path Length (SPL), Oracle Success Rate (OSR), Normalized Dynamic Time Warping (nDTW), Success-weighted DTW (sDTW), Remote Grounding Success Rate (RGS), and RGSPL). The evolution of VLN models, from early CNN-RNN architectures to Transformer-based and large language/vision-language models (LLMs/LVLMs), is traced, along with training strategies like auxiliary task learning and data augmentation (follower-speaker models, image generation).

**II. Real-World Evaluation Methodology:**
- **Hardware Platform:** A wheeled mobile robot platform with a differential-drive chassis was developed. It features an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano for onboard processing, a Mid-360 3D LiDAR for geometric perception, and either an Insta360 X4 panoramic camera or a RealSense D435i RGB-D camera for visual observations. High-level VLN inference is offloaded to a remote server (NVIDIA L40 GPU) via Wi-Fi.
- **Baseline Selection:**
 - **Hierarchical Configuration:** CLASH was chosen as a representative baseline, known for its strong VLN-CE performance (66% SR on test-unseen) and practical waypoint generation. This configuration utilized panoramic images from the Insta360 X4. SLAM was employed to provide online localization and mapping for point-goal navigation, addressing low-level execution. Panoramic depth information was not used due to real-world sensing limitations.
 - **Monolithic Configuration:** JanusVLN was selected as a representative baseline, achieving 61% SR in simulation. This configuration relied on single-view RGB images from the RealSense D435i.
 - For fair comparison, all experiments maintained identical starting positions and language instructions for both baselines.
- **Evaluation Environments and Instructions:** Experiments were conducted across 10 diverse, previously unseen real-world scenes, including laboratories, lounges, libraries, cafes, residential indoor scenes, and an outdoor garden. A total of 200 navigation episodes were designed (100 per baseline). Instruction types included 70% standard R2R-style step-by-step commands, 20% intent-ambiguous commands, and 10% multi-stage backtracking instructions. Each episode was executed once with a fixed random seed.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** In addition to standard metrics like SR and OSR, two new metrics were introduced for real-world scenarios:
 - **Strict Success Rate (SSR):** An episode is successful only if the robot stops within a 3-meter threshold of the target AND its final location semantically satisfies the instruction. This addresses the semantic correctness of stopping behavior, often overlooked by distance-only metrics.
 - **Collision Rate (CR):** Measures the proportion of episodes in which the robot collides with environmental obstacles. Episodes are terminated immediately upon collision.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The systematic real-world evaluation revealed several key findings regarding the performance and limitations of current VLN systems.

**1. Average Performance Comparison:**
- **Significant Sim-to-Real Gap:** A substantial performance gap between simulation and real-world deployment was observed for both configurations.
- **Hierarchical vs. Monolithic Performance (under tested configurations):**
 - **Monolithic (JanusVLN):** Achieved an average Success Rate (SR) of 22%, Strict Success Rate (SSR) of 17%, and Oracle Success Rate (OSR) of 27%. Critically, it exhibited a high Collision Rate (CR) of 51%.
 - **Hierarchical (CLASH):** Demonstrated higher performance with an SR of 51%, SSR of 37%, and OSR of 67%. It also showed significantly improved safety with a CR of 7%.
- **Influencing Factors:** The hierarchical system's better real-world transfer is attributed to several factors: the use of panoramic visual sensing (providing richer environmental observations), topological graph memory (supporting long-range memory and error recovery), and SLAM-based point-goal navigation (reducing collision risk through explicit spatial modeling). The tested monolithic RGB-only configuration lacked these advantages.
- **Semantic Stopping Discrepancy:** The drop from 51% SR to 37% SSR for the hierarchical configuration indicates that even when physically reaching the target area, agents often fail to stop at the semantically correct location as specified by instructions. This highlights a challenge in grounding instruction semantics for precise task completion.

**2. Per-Scene Performance Comparison:**
- The hierarchical method consistently achieved higher SR and lower CR across most of the 10 diverse scenes. Collisions were entirely avoided in 8 scenes by the hierarchical system, with failures in the remaining 2 due to obstacles (stairs) located below the LiDAR scanning plane.
- Despite residential indoor scenes dominating VLN training datasets, the evaluation did not show a pronounced performance advantage in these environments, suggesting that current VLN models have limited generalization capabilities even within familiar categories in real-world settings.

**3. Intention and Backtracking Performance Comparison:**
- **Intention Tasks:** The hierarchical framework performed marginally better (SR 35% vs. 25% for monolithic).
- **Backtracking Tasks:** The monolithic framework performed better (SR 20% vs. 0% for hierarchical).
- **Stopping Cue Issue:** A significant limitation for both paradigms in these instruction types was the absence of explicit stopping cues. Agents frequently passed the target region and continued navigating until the maximum step limit, leading to task failure despite potentially traversing the correct path. This highlights difficulties in long-term intention tracking and semantic termination.

**4. Computation Time and Inference Step Length:**
- **Hierarchical Framework:** Required significantly fewer inference steps but each step incurred a longer computation time, primarily due to the use of large language models (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-72B) via a remote API. Despite this, its overall inference time was lower due to the reduced number of steps.
- **Network Latency:** Real-world deployments were heavily affected by network latency, especially when inference was offloaded to remote servers, emphasizing the need for lightweight models and edge deployment for future autonomous systems.

**5. Qualitative Analysis and Failure Cases:**
- **Initial Exploration:** The monolithic framework often performed an initial 360-degree rotation to acquire a complete environmental view, a behavior unnecessary for the panoramic-sensing hierarchical framework. The combination of panoramic vision and LiDAR in the hierarchical system contributed to its higher success and safer execution.
- **Primary Failure Modes:**
 - **Incorrect Action Prediction:** Stemming from limited model generalization and robustness, as simulations often fail to capture the ambiguity and diversity of real-world observations and instructions.
 - **Collisions:** Particularly prevalent in the monolithic RGB-only setup due to the lack of explicit geometric sensing and limited field of view. The hierarchical system also experienced collisions when obstacles (e.g., stairs) were below the LiDAR scanning plane and incorrectly classified as traversable.
- **Severity of Collisions:** Collision-related failures, often catastrophic in the real world, receive limited attention in simulation-focused studies where such issues are abstracted or mitigated. Current VLN models struggle with accurate obstacle resolution and lack persistent obstacle-aware memory, leading to unsafe action predictions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of Vision-and-Language Navigation by providing both a structured synthesis of existing knowledge and critical empirical insights from real-world deployments.

**Significance of the Work:**
- **Structured Knowledge Base:** The comprehensive survey and systematic taxonomy of VLN methods (by action and model paradigms) offer a clear and organized reference for researchers, enabling better understanding of the field's evolution, architectural principles, and comparative characteristics.
- **Quantifying the Sim-to-Real Gap:** The systematic real-world evaluation on a physical robot platform is a significant contribution. It empirically quantifies the substantial performance degradation of state-of-the-art VLN systems when deployed outside of simulation, highlighting that high simulation benchmarks do not directly translate to real-world robustness.
- **Identification of Practical Challenges:** The work explicitly identifies critical limitations of current VLN agents in real-world settings, including:
 - **Collision Avoidance Deficits:** The high collision rates, particularly for monolithic systems lacking explicit geometric sensing, underscore the need for enhanced safety mechanisms.
 - **Semantic Stopping and Intention Understanding Gaps:** The discrepancy between SR and SSR reveals that agents struggle with the nuanced semantic interpretation required for precise task completion, especially for ambiguous or backtracking instructions.
 - **Limited Generalization:** Performance issues even in environmentally familiar categories (e.g., residential scenes) suggest that current models generalize poorly to real-world visual and physical variations.
 - **Static World Assumptions:** The paper highlights the fragility of current models in dynamic environments, which are prevalent in the real world but often simplified in simulators.

**Potential Impact and Future Directions:**
The identified limitations directly inform critical future research directions:
- **Lifelong Embodied Memory and Intent Understanding:** Future VLN agents need persistent, adaptive memory structures to reuse knowledge across multiple tasks and environments, moving beyond episodic learning. They also require advanced capabilities to infer underspecified human intent, enabling more natural and robust human-robot interaction.
- **Counterfactual Imagination and Causal Reasoning:** Developing world models that allow agents to internally simulate hypothetical futures under different actions will enable anticipatory behavior, error recovery, and robust decision-making in complex and uncertain environments, shifting from reactive to more intelligent, proactive navigation.
- **Trustworthy and Safe Embodied Navigation:** Greater emphasis is needed on integrating physical constraints (kinematics, dynamics), uncertainty-aware collision avoidance, and risk-sensitive planning into VLN policies. Safety evaluation metrics, including collision rate, entrapment rate, and adherence to social norms, are crucial for deploying robots in shared human environments.
- **Next-Generation Large-Scale Data Engines:** Progress requires moving beyond static datasets to generative data engines capable of producing diverse, physically interactive, and photorealistic environments. This includes developing tools for more accurate and varied instruction generation, along with finer-grained evaluation metrics for safety, physical feasibility, semantic grounding, and decision robustness.

In conclusion, this paper serves as a vital reference by synthesizing the VLN landscape and providing empirical evidence of the challenges in real-world deployment. It sets a clear agenda for future research towards developing more robust, safe, and generalizable embodied agents capable of reliably navigating complex, dynamic real-world environments guided by natural language.
