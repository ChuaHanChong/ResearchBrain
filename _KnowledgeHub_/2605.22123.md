# Beyond Pixels: Learning Invariant Rewards for Real-World Robotics From a Few Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed FLORA (Flow-based Language-driven Offline Reward Adaptation), a framework that learns invariant symbolic reward functions for robotic manipulation from as few as five visual demonstrations. This approach enables robust generalization to out-of-distribution scenarios and significantly accelerates policy learning in real-world tasks, outperforming existing methods in success rates and sample efficiency.

## Problem

- Traditional vision-based reward models for robotic tasks often overfit to pixel distributions, failing to generalize to visually diverse, open-world settings (e.g., varied objects, viewpoints).
- Many programmatic reward learning methods require privileged state information (e.g., 6D object poses) or extensive online interaction, which are impractical for real-world robotics with limited data and safety constraints.
- Designing dense reward functions can inadvertently alter the optimal policy (reward hacking) or suffer from 'potential collapse' in long-horizon tasks when using standard reward shaping techniques.

## Method

- **FLORA** utilizes a Flow-Generator to transform raw visual observations into object-centric motion flow representations, grounding the reward in task-relevant spatial relationships invariant to visual distractors.
- It employs a novel PBRS-MS (Potential-Based Reward Shaping-MileStone) module that augments standard PBRS with a milestone-based progress tracker, providing optimal policy invariance and preventing potential collapse in long-horizon tasks.
- A hybrid symbolic-numerical learning procedure, combining LLM-driven symbolic structure discovery with Bayesian Optimization for continuous parameters, optimizes the potential function from minimal demonstrations.

## Results

- FLORA consistently achieved higher process alignment and discriminative ability than baselines (e.g., 0.97 Spearman's ρ on training tasks) and maintained this performance across various OOD scenarios (position, viewpoint, object changes) in simulation.
- The framework significantly accelerated policy learning, achieving **96%** success rates on simple Meta-World tasks (**22%** improvement) and **42%** on difficult ones (nearly double the best baseline), along with high success rates in OOD policy learning.
- In real-world experiments on a Franka Emika robot, FLORA demonstrated superior sample efficiency and robustness, achieving nearly **100%** success on Peg-Insert and Box-Open tasks, and being the only method to achieve success (**45%**) on the complex Bulb-Unscrew task, while generalizing zero-shot to real-world OOD variants.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Shifting from pixel-based features to object-centric motion flows, coupled with symbolic reward structures, is crucial for learning behavioral invariants and achieving strong out-of-distribution generalization from few demonstrations.
> - Augmenting Potential-Based Reward Shaping with a milestone tracker (PBRS-MS) ensures both optimal policy invariance and stable dense reward signals across multi-stage, contact-rich robotic tasks.
> - A bi-level optimization strategy, where an LLM refines symbolic reward logic and Bayesian Optimization tunes numerical parameters, effectively learns robust reward functions from limited offline data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Developing robust and generalizable reward functions for reinforcement learning (RL) in robotics, particularly for open-world manipulation tasks, remains a significant challenge. Traditional methods often rely on hand-crafted rewards, which are brittle and difficult to scale across diverse task variants (e.g., different object instances, positions, camera viewpoints).

Recent advancements have focused on learning reward functions automatically from demonstrations. This area can be broadly categorized into several approaches:
- **Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for Reward Design**: Some methods utilize general-purpose VLMs to score task progress directly from images and text. While offering broad semantic understanding, these models often lack temporal consistency and numerical calibration, limiting their effectiveness as dense rewards for policy optimization.
- **Dedicated Vision-Based Reward Models**: Another line of research trains specialized reward models on large robotic datasets. These models can produce more accurate scores but are prone to visual overfitting, often requiring in-distribution test tasks and failing to generalize to semantically identical but visually distinct scenarios. They also typically require extensive data.
- **Programmatic Reward Functions**: Approaches like Eureka and Text2Reward leverage Large Language Models (LLMs) to synthesize code-based reward functions, which can explicitly represent physical laws and logical constraints. However, these methods commonly depend on privileged state information (e.g., ground-truth 6D object poses) and extensive online interaction in simulation, requirements often infeasible in real-world robotics.
- **Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) and Preference-Based Learning**: Classical reward learning techniques like IRL infer rewards from expert demonstrations, but often rely on predefined reward features or a large number of demonstrations. Preference-based methods reduce explicit design but require repeated human feedback. Both are challenging in low-data, real-robot settings.
- **Optimality-Preserving Reward Shaping (PBRS)**: PBRS is a framework that can accelerate policy learning by providing dense signals while theoretically guaranteeing optimal policy invariance. However, it typically lacks a mechanism for automatically designing task-specific potential functions and can suffer from "potential collapse" in long-horizon tasks.

This work, FLORA (Flow-based Language-driven Offline Reward Adaptation), positions itself as an advancement that addresses several limitations of existing methods. It aims to learn invariant symbolic reward functions from a minimal number of demonstrations (as few as five), without requiring privileged state information or extensive online interaction. It specifically tackles visual overfitting by shifting from pixel-based features to behavioral invariants derived from object-centric motion flows. Furthermore, it incorporates a novel PBRS-MS (Potential-Based Reward Shaping-MileStone) formulation to ensure optimal policy invariance and prevent potential collapse, thus offering a theoretically grounded and practically robust solution for reward learning in open-world robotic manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework capable of learning robust, dense reward functions for open-world robotic manipulation that generalize effectively from a limited number of visual demonstrations. This overarching goal is driven by several specific challenges and motivations:

1. **Addressing Reward Generalization in Open-World Settings**: In real-world robotic tasks, a single task (e.g., opening a box) can manifest with endless visual variations due to changes in object instances, spatial configurations, and camera viewpoints. Existing vision-based reward models often memorize specific pixel distributions, failing to generalize beyond their training conditions. The motivation is to create a reward function that distills the "behavioral invariants" – task-level properties constant across visual diversity – rather than memorizing visual features.

2. **Overcoming Dependence on Privileged State Information**: Many programmatic reward learning methods require access to privileged state information, such as ground-truth 6D object poses. Such information is typically unavailable in real-world settings. A key objective is to learn rewards directly from raw visual inputs without relying on an oracle state.

3. **Reducing Data and Interaction Requirements**: Current state-of-the-art reward models often necessitate large-scale datasets or extensive online interaction (simulation or real-world) for training and refinement. For real-world robotics, interaction budgets are strictly limited due to hardware safety and time constraints. The motivation is to learn effective reward functions from a very small number of offline demonstrations (as few as five).

4. **Ensuring Optimal Policy Invariance and Preventing Reward Hacking**: Reward functions, especially dense ones, can inadvertently alter the optimal policy, leading to "reward hacking" where an agent optimizes the reward function through unintended or suboptimal behaviors. A critical objective is to provide dense guidance while mathematically guaranteeing that the learned reward function preserves the optimal policy of the original task. This also includes addressing potential collapse issues in long-horizon tasks that can destabilize standard potential-based reward shaping.

5. **Achieving Interpretable and Reusable Reward Representations**: Unlike black-box neural networks, programmatic or symbolic reward functions offer interpretability by explicitly encoding physical laws and logical constraints. The motivation is to synthesize such interpretable functions that can be reused across diverse task variants without retraining, thereby accelerating downstream policy learning.

In summary, the motivation behind FLORA is to enable real-world robots to learn complex manipulation skills efficiently and reliably by providing invariant, theoretically sound, dense reward signals, even when given only a handful of visual demonstrations and no privileged state access.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FLORA (Flow-based Language-driven Offline Reward Adaptation) is a framework designed to learn invariant symbolic reward functions from a minimal set of visual demonstrations. Its methodology is structured around two coupled components: a structural reward formulation and a hybrid symbolic-numerical learning procedure.

#### 3.1. Structural Reward Formulation

This component defines the architecture of the reward model, designed to inherently satisfy behavioral and optimal policy invariance constraints. It consists of three modules:

- **Flow-Generator**: To achieve behavioral invariance and ground the reward model in task-relevant spatial relationships rather than raw pixel intensities, the Flow-Generator transforms high-dimensional visual observations into object-centric motion flow representations ($o_t$). This ensures the potential function perceives the task's logical state regardless of visual distractors or scene variations. The pipeline involves:
 1. **Region of Interest (RoI) Identification**: Task-relevant objects (gripper, manipulated object, target) are identified in a reference image, either manually or via Grounding DINO.
 2. **RoI Relocation**: DINOv2 is used to re-locate these RoIs in the initial frame of each new episode.
 3. **3D Point Tracking**: A 3D point tracker (e.g., TAPIP3D) tracks keypoints within each RoI throughout the episode. All tracked points are expressed in a canonical object-centric coordinate frame for spatial invariance.

- **Symbolic Potential Function ($\phi$)**: This module forms the core of the reward model. It maps the current motion flow ($o_t$) and initial motion flow ($o_0$) to a scalar potential ($p_t$) and an auxiliary task stage ($\hat{\mu}_t$), i.e., ($p_t, \hat{\mu}_t$) = $\phi(o_t, o_0; \theta)$. The potential function is decoupled into two components:
 1. **Symbolic Structure ($\phi$)**: This represents the programmatic logic of the reward, encoding semantic structure and task-level strategy. It is automatically discovered by an LLM.
 2. **Numerical Parameters ($\theta$)**: These are continuous coefficients (e.g., thresholds, weighting factors) embedded within the code, optimized by Bayesian Optimization.

- **PBRS-MS (Potential-Based Reward Shaping-MileStone) Module**: To satisfy optimal policy invariance and prevent potential collapse in long-horizon manipulation tasks, this module augments standard Potential-Based Reward Shaping (PBRS) with a milestone-based progress tracker.
 1. **Milestone Definition**: The potential range [0, $\phi_{max}$] is partitioned into $K$ discrete levels. A state reaches milestone $k$ if its potential $\phi(s)$ exceeds a threshold $\kappa_k \cdot \phi_{max}$. A monotonic milestone index $m_t$ records the highest milestone achieved up to time $t$, augmenting the state space.
 2. **Global Potential and Shaped Reward**: A cumulative potential $\Psi(m)$ is defined over milestones, providing a positive reward bonus ($\hat{R}_k > 0$) for reaching milestone $k$. The final shaped reward ($R'_t$) integrates both local PBRS (difference in $\phi$) and global milestone (difference in $\Psi$) signals: $R'_t = R_t + \gamma\phi(s_{t+1}) - \phi(s_t) + \gamma\Psi(m_{t+1}) - \Psi(m_t)$. This formulation carries a formal guarantee of optimal policy invariance (Theorem 1).

#### 3.2. Hybrid Symbolic-Numerical Learning of Potential Functions

The learning process is a bi-level optimization strategy over a minimal demonstration dataset ($D_{demo}$). The goal is to find an optimal symbolic structure ($\phi$) and parameter vector ($\theta$) that maximize a surrogate performance objective ($J$) while ensuring generalization.

- **Optimization Dataset Preparation**:
 1. Raw observations are converted to motion-flow representations.
 2. GPT-4.1 is used to decompose each task into symbolic subtasks and annotate trajectories with corresponding stage labels ($\mu_t$).
 3. $D_{demo}$ is split 60/40 into training ($D_{train}$) and validation ($D_{val}$) subsets.

- **The Surrogate Objective ($J$)**: $J(\phi, \theta; D_{train})$ is a multi-objective function designed to measure the alignment of the learned potential with an ideal one:
 - **Stage Alignment ($C_{stage}$)**: Measures the accuracy of predicted subtasks ($\hat{\mu}_t$) against VLM labels ($\mu_t$), indicating correct encoding of sequential logic.
 - **Progress Monotonicity ($C_{prog}$)**: Measures Spearman correlation between $\phi(o_t)$ and normalized time ($t/T$), ensuring the potential increases monotonically with progress.
 - **PBRS Positivity ($C_{pbrs}$)**: Measures the frequency of non-negative shaping signals ($\gamma\phi(o_{t+1}) - \phi(o_t) \ge 0$), encouraging consistent progress.

- **Bi-Level Optimization Procedure (Algorithm 1)**:
 1. **Numerical Inner Loop**: For each candidate symbolic structure ($\phi_k$), Bayesian Optimization (BO) with an Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) acquisition function is used to find the optimal continuous parameters $\theta^*_k$ by maximizing $J(\theta_k; \phi_k, D_{train})$. Initial parameters are provided by the LLM for a warm start.
 2. **Symbolic Outer Loop**: An LLM acts as the optimizer over the symbolic space $\Phi$. After each batch of inner-loop optimizations, the top-m candidates based on validation score are selected. Structured feedback (program, optimized parameters, surrogate scores, potential trajectory visualizations) is provided to the LLM, which then uses "reflection" to identify failure causes and revise the generated symbolic functions for improved logical consistency and cross-trajectory invariance in the next iteration.

This hybrid approach iteratively refines both the symbolic structure and numerical parameters, leveraging the strengths of LLMs for symbolic reasoning and BO for continuous optimization, all from a few demonstrations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents extensive experimental results across simulation and real-world settings to validate FLORA's performance, generalization capabilities, and underlying design choices.

#### 4.1. Reward Quality and OOD Generalization (Simulation)

Experiments were conducted on eight Meta-World tasks (four simple, four multi-stage difficult tasks) using only five demonstrations for reward model learning. Baselines included LIV, LIV-FT, VLC, and ReWiND-CT.

- **Process Alignment**: FLORA consistently achieved higher Spearman's rank correlation ($\rho$) between reward signals and actual task progression compared to all baselines (e.g., 0.97 for FLORA vs. 0.89 for ReWiND-CT on training tasks). This indicates that FLORA's rewards accurately track task progress.
- **Policy Rollout Ranking and Discriminative Ability**: FLORA demonstrated a superior ability to differentiate between successful, partial-success, and random trajectories. It achieved a 51% improvement in rank correlation and a 196% increase in reward margin over the strongest baseline, indicating a strong capacity to penalize failures correctly.
- **OOD Generalization**: FLORA's rewards generalized effectively to three Out-of-Distribution (OOD) scenarios: Position OOD (40 cm shift), Viewpoint OOD (30° camera rotation), and Object OOD (changed color and scale). While baselines showed significant performance degradation due to visual overfitting, FLORA maintained high process alignment and discriminative ability across all OOD variants (e.g., 0.85 process alignment for Position OOD vs. 0.61 for ReWiND-CT). Visualizations confirmed FLORA's ability to maintain clear reward separation between trajectories under distribution shift.

#### 4.2. Policy Learning Efficiency (Simulation)

The learned reward models were used to guide policy learning using RLPD.

- **Default Tasks**: FLORA significantly outperformed all baselines in terms of success rates. In simple Meta-World tasks, FLORA achieved a 96% success rate, representing a 22% improvement over the strongest baseline. For difficult tasks, FLORA maintained a 42% success rate, nearly double that of the best baseline. These results align with the observed reward quality, indicating that better reward signals lead to faster policy convergence.
- **OOD Tasks**: When reusing trained reward models in OOD settings (Button-Press-Topdown and Lever-Pull tasks), FLORA achieved high success rates across Position, Viewpoint, and Object OOD variants. Baseline models suffered catastrophic failures in these OOD settings, highlighting FLORA's superior generalization ability in accelerating policy learning under distribution shifts.

#### 4.3. Real-world Experiments

Experiments were conducted on a Franka Emika robot for three challenging manipulation tasks: Peg-Insert, Box-Open, and Bulb-Unscrew, using five demonstrations per task.

- **Policy Learning**: FLORA demonstrated superior sample efficiency and robustness. For Peg-Insert and Box-Open, it achieved nearly 100% success within 20 minutes of training, doubling the learning speed of ReWiND-CT. In the complex Bulb-Unscrew task, FLORA was the only method to achieve success, reaching a 45% success rate within one hour, while baselines failed to achieve any success.
- **OOD Generalization Ability**: For OOD variants of the Box-Open task (Position OOD with two levels of shift, Viewpoint OOD, and Object OOD with two novel boxes), FLORA maintained high success rates (e.g., 19/20 for Position 2 OOD, 15/20 for Viewpoint OOD, 18/20 for Box 1 OOD) within a 30-minute training budget. Baseline models largely failed, confirming that FLORA's learned reward representations can be reused zero-shot across diverse real-world task variants.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

Ablation studies investigated the contributions of FLORA's core components:

- **Flow-Generator**: Object-centric normalization was shown to be a primary driver of OOD generalization, and 3D point tracking outperformed 2D alternatives, aligning better with physical priors.
- **Symbolic Function**: Replacing the symbolic potential function with a transformer network led to a significant drop in OOD reward ranking and ID discriminative ability (0.80 to 0.60 Reward Rank on ID), indicating that symbolic structure is a necessary inductive bias for robust generalization from few demonstrations.
- **Demonstration Numbers**: Performance gains were marginal when increasing from 5 to 10 demonstrations, but a reduction to 3 demonstrations led to a significant drop in generalization, suggesting 5 demonstrations provide a robust balance.
- **Subtask Labels**: VLM-generated subtask labels achieved a mean accuracy of 84.7%, providing a practical and automatic alternative to human labels with acceptable performance impact.
- **PBRS-MS Module**: PBRS-MS significantly improved data efficiency and ensured stable convergence compared to standard PBRS (which suffered from potential collapse) and direct potential as reward, particularly in contact-rich tasks.
- **Hybrid Optimization Method**: The combination of LLM reflection and Bayesian Optimization achieved superior performance across surrogate metrics (Cstage, Cprog, C_pbrs) compared to LLM reflection alone, BO alone, or direct selection, validating its effectiveness in jointly optimizing symbolic structure and parameters.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces FLORA, a framework that addresses several long-standing challenges in learning reward functions for real-world robotics, demonstrating significant contributions and potential impact.

#### 5.1. Addressing Key Challenges in Reward Learning

FLORA successfully tackles the fundamental problem of **reward generalization** by learning behavioral invariants rather than memorizing pixel distributions. By grounding reward learning in **object-centric motion flows**, it moves "beyond pixels" to abstract visual observations into task-relevant spatial relations, which are inherently more robust to variations in object appearance, position, and camera viewpoint. This approach eliminates the dependency on privileged state information, a common limitation of many programmatic reward synthesis methods.

#### 5.2. High Sample Efficiency and Zero-Shot Generalization

A core achievement is the ability to learn invariant symbolic reward functions from **as few as five visual demonstrations**, without requiring extensive online interaction. This drastically reduces the data and interaction burden, which is critical for real-world robotic deployment where data collection is costly and time-consuming. The demonstrated **zero-shot generalization** to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios (position, viewpoint, object variations) in both simulation and real-world experiments is a significant step towards developing reusable reward representations that can be deployed across diverse task variants without retraining. This capability is crucial for scaling robotic learning to open-world environments.

#### 5.3. Theoretical Rigor and Robustness

The introduction of the **PBRS-MS module** is a notable contribution, ensuring **optimal policy invariance** by mathematically guaranteeing that the shaped reward does not alter the original task's optimal policy. This theoretically grounded approach mitigates the risk of reward hacking. Furthermore, PBRS-MS explicitly addresses the issue of "potential collapse" in long-horizon tasks, providing stable and reliable dense reward signals for contact-rich manipulation.

#### 5.4. Interpretable and Accelerating Policy Learning

By synthesizing **symbolic potential functions** through a hybrid LLM-BO optimization, FLORA yields interpretable reward models that explicitly encode task-level strategies and physical constraints. This interpretability allows for better understanding and debugging of the reward logic. The effectiveness of these learned rewards is evident in the downstream policy learning results, where FLORA consistently leads to **faster policy convergence and higher success rates** compared to baseline methods, both in simulation and real-world scenarios. This acceleration in learning is vital for practical robotic system development.

#### 5.5. Potential for Future Robotics Research and Applications

The findings suggest that a combination of object-centric visual representations, symbolic inductive biases, and robust shaping mechanisms provides a promising direction for developing scalable and robust reward functions. The framework's ability to operate with minimal data and generalize broadly could enable robots to learn new skills more efficiently and adapt to novel situations with reduced human intervention. Future work could explore extending PBRS-MS for tasks with reversible subtasks or multiple valid orderings, further automating the object identification process, and optimizing the computational cost of the offline learning stage, thereby broadening the applicability of FLORA to an even wider range of complex robotic manipulation problems.
