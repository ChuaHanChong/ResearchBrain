# Unleashing the Potential of All Test Samples: Mean-Shift Guided Test-Time Adaptation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Xi'an Jiaotong University developed Mean-Shift Guided Test-Time Adaptation (MS-TTA), a training-free method that refines Visual-Language Model feature embeddings using Mean-Shift clustering to leverage all test samples. MS-TTA achieved an average accuracy of 69.48% on cross-domain benchmarks with a ViT-B/16 backbone, outperforming previous methods by 0.81%, and demonstrated improved robustness on out-of-distribution datasets.

## Problem

- Visual-Language Models (VLMs) like CLIP experience significant performance degradation when encountering distribution shifts at test time, despite strong zero-shot generalization.
- Current training-free Test-Time Adaptation (TTA) methods for VLMs rely on CLIP's original feature space and selectively utilize only high-confidence samples, overlooking the potential of "low-quality" data.
- This selective sample usage and dependence on the original feature space limit adaptation effectiveness and can be sensitive to noisy pseudo-labels under strong distribution shifts.

## Method

- Introduce ==MS-TTA (Mean-Shift Guided Test-Time Adaptation)==, a training-free framework that refines feature embeddings and leverages all test samples for adaptation.
- Employ a single-step ==kNN Mean-Shift operator== to refine initial CLIP embeddings, creating "mean-shifted embeddings" that are more compact and discriminative.
- Utilize a ==dynamic cache== to store high-confidence mean-shifted embeddings and their pseudo-labels, which are then combined with original CLIP logits for final, robust predictions.

## Results

- MS-TTA achieved **69.48%** average accuracy on cross-domain benchmarks (ViT-B/16), surpassing BoostAdapter by **0.81%**, with notable gains of **+3.99%** on EuroSAT.
- The method demonstrated robust performance on out-of-distribution benchmarks, achieving **65.65%** average OOD accuracy (ViT-B/16), outperforming previous approaches.
- t-SNE visualizations showed that mean-shifted embeddings have significantly more compact and distinct clusters, improving class separability and resolving ambiguities between challenging classes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Visual-Language Models' original feature space is not always optimal for test-time adaptation and can be enhanced for better class separability and compactness.
> - All test samples, including those initially deemed "low-quality" or low-confidence, can be refined and contribute positively to the adaptation process.
> - Mean-Shift clustering provides an efficient, training-free method to refine feature embeddings, improving their discriminability and robustness to distribution shifts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visual-language models (VLMs) such as CLIP have demonstrated capabilities in various downstream tasks due to their generalization abilities. However, these models frequently encounter performance degradation when exposed to significant shifts in data distribution at test time, a phenomenon known as domain shift. Addressing this challenge is the objective of Test-Time Adaptation (TTA) methods.

TTA approaches are generally categorized into training-required and training-free methods. Training-required methods, including TPT, DiffTPT, HisTPT, and DPE, involve optimizing model parameters or learnable prompts using self-supervised objectives. While these methods can achieve strong adaptation performance, they often incur substantial computational costs, which can limit their applicability in real-time or resource-constrained scenarios.

In contrast, training-free TTA methods aim to adapt models without modifying their parameters, focusing on efficiency. Approaches like Test-Time Adaptation via Dynamic Caching (TDA), BoostAdapter, and BCA leverage feature retrieval and memory-based strategies. These methods typically construct a dynamic cache of high-confidence samples and refine predictions based on similarities to these cached examples.

The current work positions itself within the training-free TTA landscape but addresses a noted limitation of existing methods. Previous training-free approaches operate strictly within the original feature space provided by the VLM (e.g., CLIP) and predominantly rely on "high-confidence" samples for adaptation. This reliance is predicated on the assumption that CLIP's original feature space is already optimal for adaptation. Consequently, these methods often overlook "low-confidence" samples and are constrained by the inherent quality of the initial CLIP features, potentially limiting further performance improvement. The authors identify a gap in exploring whether CLIP's feature space can be further refined and if low-confidence samples can be leveraged effectively for adaptation without additional training. This paper aims to fill this gap by introducing a feature refinement mechanism that operates beyond CLIP's original feature space and utilizes all test samples.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are derived from the identified limitations within existing training-free test-time adaptation (TTA) methods for visual-language models (VLMs) like CLIP. The authors articulate two core questions that motivate their work:
1. **Can CLIP’s original feature space be further optimized for adaptation?** Existing training-free TTA methods largely assume the inherent optimality of CLIP’s feature space, using it directly for adaptation. This approach restricts the potential for feature refinement and limits performance improvements beyond the VLM’s initial generalization ability. The authors aim to explore methods that can enhance these representations.
2. **Can so-called “low-quality” samples be refined rather than disregarded?** Many current training-free TTA techniques prioritize "high-confidence" samples for populating dynamic caches, largely ignoring "low-confidence" examples. These low-confidence samples, which might reside near decision boundaries or represent unique target-domain patterns, are hypothesized to contain valuable information for shaping more accurate decision boundaries and improving adaptation. The motivation is to develop a mechanism that can effectively utilize all test samples, irrespective of their initial confidence, for a more comprehensive adaptation.

To address these objectives, the authors propose MS-TTA, motivated by the properties of the Mean-Shift algorithm. Mean-Shift is an unsupervised clustering technique that shifts data points towards dense regions, enhancing feature compactness and aligning features with underlying data structures. Its appeal for TTA lies in its ability to refine feature representations using only unlabeled test data by moving embeddings towards local density modes, which aligns with the goal of adapting VLMs under distribution shifts without requiring additional training or labels. The key motivations for developing MS-TTA are:
- To **enhance feature representations beyond CLIP’s original feature space** by introducing a self-supervised refinement step.
- To **leverage all test samples**, including both high- and low-confidence ones, for adaptation, thereby overcoming the limitations of methods that rely solely on a subset of confident samples.
- To **improve feature compactness and class separability** through feature refinement, leading to more stable and robust adaptation.
- To achieve these goals in a **training-free, parameter-free, and computationally efficient manner**, making the approach suitable for real-time applications and dynamic environments where model retraining is impractical.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Mean-Shift Guided Test-Time Adaptation (MS-TTA), is a training-free framework designed to enhance feature representations and improve classification performance of visual-language models (VLMs) under distribution shifts. It integrates a single-step k-nearest neighbors (kNN) Mean-Shift operation with a dynamic caching mechanism.

#### 3.1. Training-Free Baseline

The foundation of MS-TTA is a training-free baseline built upon CLIP. Given a test image, its feature embedding (`f_test`) is extracted by CLIP's visual encoder, and initial prediction logits (`logits_CLIP`) are computed by matching `f_test` with pre-encoded text descriptions (`W_t`).
A dynamic queue (cache) stores `Q` representative test examples and their pseudo-labels (`ˆy`) for each class. Pseudo-labels are derived from one-hot encoding the CLIP predictions. Samples are added to the cache based on an entropy minimization criterion: a new sample is inserted only if its prediction entropy is lower than the current highest entropy in its class’s cache. If the cache is full, the highest-entropy sample is replaced.
During inference, for a new test sample, relevant cached samples are retrieved. Cache logits (`logits_cache`) are computed by weighting the pseudo-labels of cached samples by their cosine similarity to the test sample’s feature. The final prediction (`logits_final`) is a linear combination of `logits_CLIP` and `logits_cache`. This baseline operates within CLIP’s original feature space and primarily relies on high-confidence samples.

#### 3.2. Mean-Shifted Embedding

MS-TTA introduces a feature refinement step using a single-step kNN Mean-Shift operation.
- **Initial Feature Extraction:** For an input image `x_i`, its initial `d`-dimensional, `l2`-normalized feature embedding `v_i` is obtained using a pre-trained CLIP visual encoder.
- **Neighborhood Definition:** For each embedding `v_i`, its local neighborhood `N(v_i)` consists of `v_i` itself and its `k` nearest neighbors from a memory `M` (which stores previously seen test sample embeddings). Neighbors are determined based on cosine similarity.
- **Kernel Weighting Strategy:** A kernel function `φ(·)` is applied to assign weights to neighbors. The central embedding `v_i` receives a weight of `(1 - α)`, while each of its `k` neighbors receives a weight of `α/k`. This approximates a uniform kernel and allows balancing the influence of the original embedding and its neighbors via the scaling factor `α`.
- **Mean-Shifted Embedding Calculation:** The refined embedding `z_i` is computed as the `l2`-normalized weighted average of the embeddings in `N(v_i)`, using the defined kernel weights. This process shifts `v_i` towards the local density mode of its neighbors, enhancing feature compactness and robustness. The formula ensures the refined embedding `z_i` remains on the unit hypersphere.

#### 3.3. Mean-Shifted Test-Time Adaptation

The mean-shifted embeddings (`z_test`) are integrated into the TTA process:
- **Feature Refinement:** After `f_test` is extracted for a test image `x_test`, the single-step Mean-Shift operation is applied to `f_test` to obtain `z_test`. The formula explicitly combines `f_test` with the sum of its `k` nearest neighbors, weighted by `α`.
- **Dynamic Cache Update:** If the entropy of the initial CLIP prediction (`logits_CLIP`) for `x_test` is low (indicating high confidence), the *mean-shifted embedding* `z_test` (along with its pseudo-label) is stored in the Mean-Shift dynamic cache. This cache, similar to the baseline, maintains a collection of previously observed, refined embeddings and their pseudo-labels, replacing least confident entries based on entropy minimization.
- **Mean-Shift Logits Computation:** For a new test sample, its `z_test` is matched against the `Z_cache` (stored mean-shifted embeddings) and their `Y` (pseudo-labels) to compute `logits_MS` using a similarity-based approach (`z_test * Z_cache^T * Y`). This step incorporates refined knowledge from the cache.
- **Final Prediction:** The `logits_MS` are linearly combined with the original `logits_CLIP` using a scaling factor `λ` to produce the `logits_final`. The final prediction is then obtained by taking the `argmax` of `logits_final`.

The approach is designed to be training-free, parameter-free (concerning model weights), and self-improving by progressively enhancing the feature space based on the test data distribution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents extensive evaluations of MS-TTA on Out-of-Distribution (OOD) and Cross-Dataset Benchmarks, comparing its performance against various state-of-the-art (SOTA) training-required and training-free TTA methods.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Results

- **Cross-Dataset Benchmark:**
 - **ViT-B/16 Backbone (Table IIb):** MS-TTA demonstrates an average accuracy of 69.48%, outperforming all other training-free methods, including BoostAdapter (68.68%) and TDA (67.53%). It achieved the highest accuracy on 7 out of 10 datasets. Notable improvements over BoostAdapter include +3.99% on EuroSAT, +1.54% on Flowers102, and +0.45% on UCF101, contributing to an overall average improvement of +0.80%.
 - **ResNet50 Backbone (Table IIa):** MS-TTA achieved the highest average accuracy (62.05%) among all compared methods, including training-required approaches. It showed leading performance on EuroSAT (+3.21% over BoostAdapter) and UCF101 (+0.26% over BoostAdapter), affirming its versatility across different backbone architectures.

- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Benchmark:**
 - **ViT-B/16 Backbone (Table III):** MS-TTA achieved an average accuracy of 65.71%, slightly surpassing BoostAdapter (65.57%) and all other training-free methods. It demonstrated superior performance on ImageNet-A (+0.10%), ImageNet-R (+0.13%), ImageNet-S (+0.27%), and ImageNet-V2 (+0.06%) compared to BoostAdapter.
 - **ResNet50 Backbone (Table IV):** MS-TTA yielded an average accuracy of 48.54%, outperforming BoostAdapter (48.20%) and other training-free methods. It consistently led on most datasets within this benchmark.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

- **Effectiveness of MS-TTA (Table VII & VIII):** Comparing MS-TTA to a baseline (cache-based TTA without Mean-Shift) revealed consistent improvements. On the Cross-Dataset Benchmark, MS-TTA improved average accuracy by +0.90%, with significant gains such as +4.19% on EuroSAT and +0.75% on Oxford Pets. On the OOD Benchmark, an overall improvement of +0.84% was observed, including +1.66% on ImageNet-S. These results indicate that incorporating Mean-Shift clustering consistently enhances feature quality and robustness.
- **Impact of kNN numbers `k` (Table V):** An analysis of `k` values (number of nearest neighbors) on Flowers102 and ImageNet-A showed optimal performance at `k=2`. Larger `k` values (e.g., 8 or 16) led to a slight degradation, suggesting that while local context is beneficial, too many neighbors can introduce noise and dilute distinctive features.
- **Effectiveness of MS scaling factor `α` (Table VI):** The `α` factor, which balances the influence of the original feature and its neighbors, showed optimal performance in the range of `0.7` to `0.9`. Specifically, `α=0.8` was optimal for DTD and Flowers102, and `α=0.7` for ImageNet-A. Performance generally improved with increasing `α` up to this range, with slight degradation at `α=1.0`, indicating a balance between retaining original feature information and leveraging local neighborhood refinement.
- **Statistical Robustness over Random Seeds (Table IX):** Experiments with 5 random seeds across six datasets demonstrated that MS-TTA's performance gains were statistically significant and robust to variations in online inference order. Standard deviations remained low (at most 0.22), confirming stability.
- **Effect of Mean-shift Steps (Table X):** Comparing single-step, two-step, and four-step Mean-Shift variants revealed that the single-step approach offered the best balance of accuracy and efficiency. Multiple steps did not yield further accuracy gains and reduced inference speed.
- **Sensitivity of logit scaling factor `λ` (Table XI):** The `λ` parameter, which scales the contribution of Mean-shift logits, showed that moderate values lead to strong performance. Optimal results were achieved at `λ=1`. Extreme values (e.g., `λ=0` or `λ=10`) degraded performance, confirming robustness to this parameter's tuning within a reasonable range.

#### 4.3. Visualization (t-SNE)

t-SNE visualizations on the Flowers102 dataset (Figure 3) qualitatively support the effectiveness of MS-TTA.
- Original CLIP embeddings showed scattered and overlapping clusters, indicating poor class separability.
- MS-TTA refined embeddings produced more clearly defined and compact clusters with enlarged inter-class gaps, reducing ambiguity at decision boundaries.
- Close-up views of specific classes (e.g., 16 and 33) demonstrated that MS-TTA successfully separates classes that were overlapping in the original CLIP embedding space, confirming the hypothesis that Mean-Shift refinement enhances discriminative capability.

#### 4.4. Efficiency Analysis (Table XIII)

MS-TTA operates at an inference speed of 10.05 Frames Per Second (FPS) with a memory consumption of 1.4 GB on an NVIDIA 3090 GPU. This makes it significantly faster than training-required methods like TPT (0.29 FPS) and DiffTPT (0.10 FPS), while offering competitive or superior accuracy. This analysis confirms MS-TTA's efficiency for practical test-time adaptation.

#### 4.5. Plug-and-Play Adaptability (Table XII)

MS-TTA was demonstrated to be compatible with existing training-free TTA frameworks. When integrated with TDA, it improved average performance by +1.24%. Similarly, integration with BoostAdapter resulted in an additional +0.75% improvement. These results confirm MS-TTA's modular design and its ability to serve as a general enhancement module without modifying underlying model architectures or requiring additional training.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of MS-TTA represents a contribution to the field of test-time adaptation for visual-language models, particularly for training-free scenarios.

- **Addressing Fundamental Limitations:** MS-TTA directly confronts two limitations of prior training-free TTA methods: their reliance on the original, unrefined CLIP feature space and their selective use of only high-confidence samples. By demonstrating that Mean-Shift can effectively refine *all* test samples, the work broadens the scope of data utilization in TTA.
- **Enhanced Feature Representation:** The core impact is the demonstration that a simple, single-step kNN Mean-Shift operation can significantly improve feature compactness and class separability. This leads to more robust feature representations that are better suited for accurate classification under distribution shifts, as evidenced by quantitative results and t-SNE visualizations.
- **Improved Generalization and Robustness:** MS-TTA consistently outperforms state-of-the-art training-free methods across diverse OOD and Cross-Dataset Benchmarks. This indicates its strong generalization capabilities and robustness in handling various types of distribution shifts, from natural adversarial examples to entirely different domains.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Operating in a training-free manner and requiring no parameter updates, MS-TTA maintains high inference speed (10.05 FPS) and low memory consumption (1.4 GB). This efficiency makes it practical for deployment in real-world applications where computational resources or real-time adaptation are critical, such as embedded systems or dynamic online environments.
- **Plug-and-Play Modularity:** The demonstrated ability of MS-TTA to integrate seamlessly with existing TTA frameworks like TDA and BoostAdapter highlights its modularity. This suggests it can serve as a general enhancement module, potentially improving the performance of a wider range of TTA methods without requiring complex re-engineering or retraining.
- **Broader Implications for VLMs:** By showcasing a method for refining VLM embeddings without explicit training, MS-TTA offers insights into improving the adaptability of these powerful models beyond their pre-trained capabilities. This could inspire further research into lightweight, unsupervised feature refinement techniques for foundation models.

Potential future work, as indicated by the authors, includes optimizing adaptive neighborhood selection for Mean-Shift and exploring its applicability to other vision-language models, which could further broaden its impact and utility.
