# Move-Then-Operate: Behavioral Phasing for Human-Like Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The Move-Then-Operate framework introduces a structurally disentangled Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model that explicitly separates robotic manipulation into distinct 'move' and 'operate' phases. This approach achieved an average success rate of 68.9% on the RoboTwin2 benchmark, demonstrating a 24.1% absolute improvement over monolithic baselines, alongside enhanced data and training efficiency.

## Problem

- Monolithic Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies conflate coarse relocation movements and fine, contact-critical manipulation within a single objective function.
- The heterogeneous nature of these distinct motor behaviors leads to optimization challenges, instability, and gradient interference.
- Larger action amplitudes from 'move' frames can overwhelm learning signals for finer-grained 'operate' phases, hindering precision in contact-rich interactions.

## Method

- A hierarchically gated dual-expert policy, **Move-Then-Operate**, with disjoint parameters for 'E_Move' (coarse relocation) and 'E_Operate' (fine manipulation).
- A lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) router determines expert activation per action chunk, conditioned on a semantic summary from a shared Vision-Language Model backbone.
- Supervised learning with ground-truth phase labels trains experts via Conditional ==Flow Matching== (CFM) and the router via cross-entropy, ensuring orthogonal parameter updates and expert specialization.
- An automated phase-aware auto-labeling pipeline, using a Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM) with structural constraints and self-refinement, generates high-fidelity ground-truth phase labels.

## Results

- Achieved an average success rate of **68.9%** across 8 RoboTwin2 tasks, representing a **24.1%** absolute improvement over the monolithic π₀ baseline (**44.75%**) with a limited budget of 50 demonstrations per task.
- Demonstrated enhanced data efficiency, outperforming baselines trained on 10 times more data in contact-rich tasks (e.g., 'Press Stapler' at **93%** vs. π*₀.₅ at **80%**, 'Click Bell' at **99%** vs. π*₀.₅ at **75%**).
- Exhibited superior training efficiency, achieving rapid convergence during both multi-task pre-training and task-specific fine-tuning, often reaching near-peak performance in notably fewer training steps.
- Ablation studies confirmed the critical role of the learned routing mechanism; random or reversed expert selection led to severe performance degradation (e.g., from **68.88%** to **25.63%** with random selection).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human motor control naturally segregates into distinct 'move' (rapid approach) and 'operate' (precise adjustments) phases, suggesting an effective strategy for robotic manipulation.
> - Explicitly decoupling these heterogeneous behaviors into specialized experts mitigates optimization instability and gradient interference, allowing each expert to master its specific dynamics.
> - Structural inductive biases, such as phase decomposition, can be more impactful than simply scaling up dataset size for certain contact-rich manipulation tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work operates within the field of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for robotic manipulation. Current VLA systems have demonstrated capabilities across various tasks by learning monolithic policies that generate full action trajectories. These policies often conflate coarse relocation movements with fine, contact-critical manipulation within a single objective function. This approach can lead to optimization challenges due to the heterogeneous nature of these distinct motor behaviors.

The research aligns with observations from human motor control, which often exhibits a two-phase organization: an initial rapid movement to a target's vicinity (move phase), followed by precise, feedback-driven adjustments (operate phase). This organizational principle is supported by classic psychophysical models such as Fitts' Law, which describes how movement time is influenced by both movement amplitude and target tolerance. The paper notes that even modern robotic datasets demonstrate distinct characteristics for actions within these two phases, with move phases exhibiting broader amplitude and operate phases requiring more concentrated, fine-grained control.

Prior research has addressed aspects of trajectory heterogeneity through various strategies, including controller selection (e.g., SP-VLA, Action-Aware Pruning), temporal scaling and credit assignment (e.g., STARE-VLA, Mixture of Horizons), and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures (e.g., FedVLA, AdaMoE, MoE-DP, VER). These approaches primarily focus on reinforcement learning signals, temporal abstraction, or general task heterogeneity. However, the explicit disentanglement of long-range transport motions from precision-demanding interaction phases under imitation-based supervision has remained an area with less dedicated exploration. Existing MoE architectures, while capable of dynamic routing, have not specifically focused on decoupling "move" and "operate" based on their distinct dynamic properties and the potential for optimization interference.

This paper builds on the concept of decoupled architectures, which have been explored in other domains to address conflicting objectives, such as in long-tailed recognition (e.g., BBN) or handling out-of-distribution problems (e.g., DeCoOP). These studies suggest that structural decoupling allows specialized experts to master distinct behaviors without compromising end-to-end performance. By applying this principle to robotic manipulation, Move-Then-Operate aims to introduce a structural inductive bias that mirrors human motor strategies, potentially leading to more effective and efficient learning of complex manipulation skills.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a Vision-Language-Action (VLA) framework, named Move-Then-Operate, that explicitly decouples robotic manipulation into two distinct behavioral phases: coarse relocation (move) and contact-critical interaction (operate).

The motivation for this architectural design stems from several observations and challenges in contemporary robotic manipulation:

- **Mimicking Human Motor Control**: Human manipulation naturally segregates into distinct move and operate phases. The initial rapid approach is followed by precise, feedback-driven adjustments. This natural division in biological systems suggests an effective strategy for complex motor tasks.
- **Addressing Heterogeneous Dynamics**: Current VLA models often employ monolithic policies that learn a single set of parameters for generating full action trajectories. These trajectories encompass both large-magnitude, rapid "move" actions and small-magnitude, precise "operate" actions. The dynamics governing these two phases are fundamentally different, leading to heterogeneous control regimes.
- **Mitigating Optimization Instability and Gradient Interference**: When a single policy attempts to optimize for both move and operate behaviors simultaneously, there is a risk of optimization instability and gradient interference. The paper highlights that "move" frames, often characterized by larger action amplitudes, can overwhelm the learning signal for the finer-grained "operate" phase. This imbalance can hinder the policy's ability to achieve high precision in contact-rich interactions.
- **Improving Data and Training Efficiency**: By structurally isolating the parameter sets for each phase, the framework aims to enable each expert to specialize in the dynamics of its corresponding phase. This specialization is hypothesized to reduce the overall learning complexity, leading to faster convergence and better performance with less training data and fewer training steps compared to monolithic approaches.
- **Leveraging Intrinsic Dataset Structure**: The authors observe that actions within existing robotic datasets already exhibit distinct characteristics for move and operate phases. This empirical evidence supports the hypothesis that explicit phase decomposition can align policy learning with the inherent structure of manipulation tasks, leading to more robust and accurate control.

In summary, the key motivation is to overcome the limitations of monolithic VLA policies by introducing a structural inductive bias that aligns with human motor control principles, thereby enhancing the learning and execution of high-precision robotic manipulation through specialized expert policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Move-Then-Operate framework introduces a hierarchically gated policy designed to explicitly decouple coarse relocation from fine-grained manipulation. The methodology comprises a dual-expert architecture, a routing mechanism, a supervised learning strategy for the router and experts, and an automated phase-aware auto-labeling pipeline.

#### 3.1. Dual-Expert Architecture

The core of the system is a dual-expert policy. At each time step `t`, the policy processes multimodal observations `C_t` (language instruction, visual observation, proprioceptive state) to generate an action sequence `a_t`. The architecture consists of:
- **Shared Vision-Language Model (VLM) Backbone**: This backbone encodes the multimodal context `C_t` into dense hidden states `F_t`. This pre-trained VLM helps in aligning instruction and visual cues.
- **Two Distinct Expert Heads**: `E_Move` and `E_Operate`. These experts are parameterized using Conditional Flow Matching (CFM), a technique that learns a time-dependent vector field to transport a Gaussian prior to the data distribution. Each expert has disjoint parameters, meaning they do not share weights with each other, nor do they directly share parameters with the VLM backbone (though they condition on its output). This parameter isolation is intended to prevent conflicting gradient updates between the coarse transit and fine manipulation phases.
- **Hard-Routing Mechanism**: A discrete latent variable `z_t ∈ {MOVE, OPERATE}` mediates expert selection. When an expert is selected, it becomes exclusively active for the entire flow integration period (`σ ∈`) for generating that action sequence, ensuring temporal consistency.

#### 3.2. Routing Experts Per Action Chunk

A lightweight phase router determines which expert to activate.
- **Semantic Summary**: The VLM backbone first encodes `C_t` into `F_t`. A semantic summary `f_t` is then computed by masked global average pooling over `F_t`. This summary captures global scene dynamics and leverages the VLM's aligned representations.
- **MLP Router**: The `f_t` vector feeds into a lightweight Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) router, which predicts the phase distribution `p_ϕ(z | C_t)`.
- **Greedy Decoding**: During inference, the active expert `ˆz_t` is selected via greedy decoding: `ˆz_t = arg max_z p_ϕ(z | f_t)`. The selected expert `E_ˆz_t` then generates the action trajectory conditioned on the shared representations `F_t`.

#### 3.3. Supervised Routing Learning and Inference

To facilitate expert specialization and prevent gradient conflict, the training process employs a teacher-forcing strategy using ground-truth phase labels `y_t`.
- **Action Optimization (`L_action`)**: The experts are trained using the Conditional Flow Matching (CFM) objective. Crucially, the velocity field `v_pred` is constructed using the ground-truth phase label `y_t` (not the router's prediction), masking out the irrelevant expert. This ensures that parameter updates (`∇_θ_ˆz`) are non-zero only for the matched expert, orthogonalizing the learning processes. The loss is a batch-level masked Mean Squared Error (MSE) against the target vector field.
- **Router Optimization (`L_router`)**: The router is trained separately to predict the ground-truth phase assignments by minimizing a cross-entropy loss on the pooled features `f_t`.
- **Total Objective**: The combined training objective is `L_total = L_action + λL_router`, where `λ` is a weighting factor.
- **Inference**: During deployment, the router's greedy prediction `ˆz_t` directly selects the active expert, which then synthesizes the action trajectory by solving the CFM ordinary differential equation (ODE).

#### 3.4. Phase-Aware Auto-Labeling

To provide high-fidelity ground-truth phase labels (`y_t`) for training, an automated annotation pipeline is developed.
- **Hierarchical Temporal Segmentation**: The labeling task is formulated as a hierarchical temporal segmentation problem, performed by a Multimodal Large Language Model (MLLM). Given a video `V` and an instruction `I`, the MLLM predicts a structured schedule `S` comprising `N` consecutive subtasks. Each subtask `T_i` contains a sequence of atomic phases `p_i = (ϕ_i,1..., ϕ_i,m)` where `ϕ ∈ {MOVE, OPERATE}`.
- **Structural Constraints and Assignment**: Topological constraints are imposed on the phase sequence `p_i` to ensure physical plausibility. Specifically, a subtask can have at most two phases (`|p_i| ≤ 2`), and if two phases exist, they must be of distinct semantic types and follow chronological order. Contextual cues, such as subtask decomposition, phase transition heuristics, and end-effector velocity, are incorporated into the MLLM prompt to align segmentation with human operational patterns. The validated intervals are then aligned with trajectory timestamps to assign a semantic label `y_t` to each control step.
- **Validation and Self-Refinement**: A deterministic validator `V` checks for boundary continuity and structural validity of the MLLM's output. If violations are detected, the validator generates a structured error description `E`. This error `E` is then fed back to the MLLM as part of a new prompt, triggering an iterative self-correction loop until a valid schedule is produced or a maximum query budget is reached. This feedback mechanism ensures high-quality supervision for downstream policy learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the Move-Then-Operate framework on the RoboTwin2 benchmark, focusing on 8 representative tasks that involve diverse contact dynamics and object geometries. The evaluation focuses on task performance, data efficiency, and training efficiency, and includes an ablation study.

#### 4.1. Main Results (Table 1)

On a constrained budget of 50 demonstrations per task and identical training steps, the Move-Then-Operate method achieved an average success rate of 68.9% across the 8 RoboTwin2 tasks. This represents a 24.1% absolute improvement over the monolithic π₀ baseline (44.75%).
- Performance gains were observed across various tasks, with particularly notable improvements in high-precision tasks:
 - Click Bell: 99% success rate, a 55% increase over the next best method (π₀ at 44%).
 - Press Stapler: 70% success rate, an 8% increase over the next best (π₀ at 62%).
- Improvements were also consistent in long-horizon transport tasks, such as Place Cans Plasticbox, where the success rate increased from 64% (RDT) to 79%. These results suggest that explicitly decoupling coarse relocation from fine manipulation effectively mitigates optimization interference and allows for better specialization.

#### 4.2. Data Efficiency (Table 2)

The study investigated the model's ability to compete with baselines trained on significantly larger datasets. Our model, trained on 50 tasks with 50 demonstrations per task (100,000 steps total, no task-specific fine-tuning), was benchmarked against π*₀.₅ and GO-1*, which were trained on 10 times more data.
- Despite the 10x data disparity, Move-Then-Operate demonstrated competitive performance and surpassed baselines in several specific domains.
- In contact-rich tasks:
 - Press Stapler: 93% success rate, outperforming π*₀.₅ (80%) by 13%.
 - Click Bell: 99% success rate, outperforming π*₀.₅ (75%) by 24%.
 - These findings suggest that for tasks heavily reliant on precise physical interaction, architectural specialization can be more impactful than simply scaling up dataset size.
- The model sometimes exhibited a data gap in visually diverse tasks (e.g., Place Cans Plasticbox, where GO-1* achieved 68% vs our 16%), which the authors attribute to the limited visual diversity in the smaller dataset compared to the larger visual exposure of the baselines. However, the overall performance with one-tenth the data indicates both effectiveness and data efficiency.

#### 4.3. Training Efficiency (Figures 5 and 6)

The framework's optimization efficiency was assessed during multi-task pre-training and task-specific fine-tuning.
- **Multi-task Training Performance (Figure 5)**: The model showed rapid convergence during pre-training. For example, in tasks like Click Bell (100%) and Click Alarm (89%), near-peak performance was achieved at 60,000 steps, surpassing the final reported performance of the π₀ baseline (trained for 100,000 steps plus 20,000 fine-tuning steps). Similar efficiency was noted in Press Stapler (91% early). This suggests that the decoupled architecture simplifies joint optimization, leading to faster learning of phase-critical behaviors.
- **Fine-tuning Convergence (Figure 6)**: During task-specific fine-tuning, the model exhibited rapid specialization. For example, in Place Cans Plasticbox, the success rate improved from 16% to 73% within the first 5,000 steps. In Place Burger Fries, it reached 96% success at 15,000 steps. Most tasks reached their performance peak between 10,000 and 15,000 fine-tuning steps, indicating that the decoupled experts rapidly adapt to specific task dynamics within a limited fine-tuning budget.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study (Table 3)

An ablation study investigated the necessity of accurate phase routing and expert specialization by comparing against random and reversed expert selection strategies.
- **Random Selection**: Replacing the learned router with random selection caused the average success rate to drop significantly from 68.88% to 25.63%.
- **Reversal Selection**: An adversarial setting where phase assignments were explicitly inverted led to a further degradation in average success rate, plummeting to 8.88%.
- These results confirm that the experts specialize in distinct, incompatible behaviors. Using the incorrect expert leads to severe failures rather than merely reduced performance, particularly for long-horizon or high-precision tasks (e.g., Place Cans Plasticbox and Move Pillbottle Pad success rates dropped to 2% and 0% respectively under Reversal selection). This underscores the critical role of the learned routing mechanism.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Move-Then-Operate framework introduces a structurally disentangled approach to robotic manipulation within the Vision-Language-Action (VLA) paradigm, offering several significant implications for the field:

- **Improved Manipulation Performance**: By explicitly decoupling coarse relocation ("move") from contact-rich manipulation ("operate") through a dual-expert policy, the framework addresses the long-standing challenge of optimizing for heterogeneous control regimes. This leads to substantial gains in success rates, particularly for complex, high-precision tasks where fine-grained control is critical. The 24.1% average absolute improvement over monolithic baselines demonstrates the efficacy of this architectural bias.
- **Enhanced Data Efficiency**: The specialized nature of the experts allows the model to learn effective policies from comparatively smaller datasets. The ability to rival or surpass baselines trained on 10 times more data is a significant finding, suggesting that structural design can, in certain contexts, be a more effective driver of performance than simply scaling up data. This has practical implications for robotics research and deployment, where large, diverse datasets are often costly and time-consuming to acquire.
- **Accelerated Training Convergence**: The framework demonstrates superior training efficiency, achieving peak performance in a notably fewer number of training steps. Rapid convergence during both multi-task pre-training and task-specific fine-tuning reduces the computational resources and time required for model development and deployment. This efficiency is crucial for iterative research cycles and for adapting robotic systems to new tasks quickly.
- **Robustness in Complex Scenarios**: The explicit separation of move and operate phases mitigates optimization instability and gradient interference, which often arise from the differing scales and dynamics of these behaviors. This leads to more stable and robust learning, particularly for behaviors where small, precise movements can be overshadowed by larger, more frequent transport actions.
- **Human-Inspired Robotics**: The design draws inspiration from human motor control, which naturally partitions complex movements into distinct phases. This biomimetic approach suggests a potentially more intuitive and efficient way for robots to learn and execute manipulation tasks, aligning robotic control strategies with observed biological intelligence.
- **Foundation for Future VLA Designs**: The work establishes that phase-disentangled VLA architectures can serve as a scalable path toward robust, high-precision robotic manipulation, especially under practical data and compute constraints. It opens avenues for future research into finer-grained behavioral decompositions, more sophisticated routing mechanisms, and how such structural biases can be integrated with larger foundation models for broader generalization.

In summary, Move-Then-Operate offers a method to enhance the precision, efficiency, and robustness of robotic manipulation by incorporating a fundamental principle of human motor control into VLA model design.
