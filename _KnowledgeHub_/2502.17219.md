# Humanoid Whole-Body Locomotion on Narrow Terrain via Dynamic Balance and Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Shanghai Jiao Tong University and TeleAI developed Dynamic Balanced Humanoid Locomotion (DBHL), an algorithm that enables full-sized humanoid robots to traverse narrow and irregular terrains using only proprioception. The method integrates Zero Moment Point (ZMP) principles into a reinforcement learning framework, achieving robust balance and adaptable whole-body control for zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a Unitree H1-2 robot.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle with dynamic balance and robust locomotion on extreme terrains (e.g., narrow pathways, uneven surfaces) compared to human capabilities.
- Existing reinforcement learning locomotion algorithms often rely on periodic gaits or external perception (vision, LiDAR), making them vulnerable to unobservable obstacles or sensor failures.
- Current methods frequently fail to effectively leverage crucial proprioceptive information (e.g., contact forces, support polygon, CoM) for fine-grained dynamic equilibrium adjustments.

## Method

- ==Proposes Dynamic Balanced Humanoid Locomotion (DBHL)==, a goal-conditioned reinforcement learning framework using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with an asymmetric actor-critic.
- Integrates the Zero Moment Point (ZMP) concept into the RL reward function, using a custom reward term (`r_zmp`) to encourage the Zero Moment Line (ZML) to remain near the center of the support polygon.
- Employs whole-body control through angular momentum regularization (`r_AM`) and multiplicative action noise to promote coordinated upper and lower limb movements for enhanced stability.

## Results

- DBHL outperformed baseline methods, including the official Unitree RL Gym and a variant without upper body control, across various narrow terrains (flat, slopes, stairs), demonstrating superior success rates and robustness.
- Quantitative comparisons confirmed the ZMP-based reward function enhanced dynamic stability, allowing the robot to maintain balance and manage ZMP-distance under external disturbances in high-fidelity simulations.
- The method achieved successful zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to a Unitree H1-2 robot, which traversed a 25 cm wide track (including slopes, bridges, and stairs), navigated irregular planar terrains, and withstood external pushes and payloads.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating classical Zero Moment Point (ZMP) principles into a modern reinforcement learning framework effectively enhances dynamic balance and robustness for humanoid locomotion on extreme terrains.
> - Whole-body control, particularly coordinated upper and lower limb movements, is crucial for maintaining stability and adaptability in challenging humanoid locomotion tasks.
> - Reward vectorization significantly accelerates reinforcement learning training convergence by preventing critical smaller reward terms from being overshadowed by larger ones.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of humanoid locomotion control has experienced significant advancements, largely propelled by developments in Reinforcement Learning (RL) and the availability of massively parallel simulation environments such as Isaac Gym. Historically, humanoid control relied on classical methods like Whole-Body Control (WBC) and Model Predictive Control (MPC). While these approaches offer theoretical soundness and predictability, they demand precise dynamic modeling and often struggle with the complex structures of modern robots or unexpected environmental interactions.

More recently, RL-based algorithms have emerged as a powerful alternative. These methods, encompassing phase-based gait learning, motor skill control, and motion imitation, have demonstrated impressive adaptability on conventional terrains by leveraging extensive simulated interactions. However, the authors identify a critical gap: despite these achievements, humanoid robots still significantly lag behind human capabilities in dynamic balance, particularly under extreme conditions such as narrow pathways, uneven terrain, or sudden external disturbances. Current RL controllers often fall short because they tend to rely on periodic gaits or motion primitives, which limit their ability to execute rapid, diverse gait adjustments necessary for regaining stability. Furthermore, many contemporary approaches for complex terrain traversal depend on external perception sensors like vision or LiDAR, making them vulnerable to sensor failures or unobservable obstacles.

This paper directly addresses this critical limitation. By integrating the classical concept of the Zero Moment Point (ZMP)—a well-established principle for ensuring dynamic stability in bipedal robots—into a modern RL framework, the authors bridge the gap between model-based analytical control and data-driven learning. The work distinguishes itself by achieving robust locomotion on extreme terrains using *only proprioception*, thereby eliminating the dependency on external perception and enhancing robustness in unobservable or sensor-degraded environments. This focus on "dynamic balance" via "whole-body control" is paramount for humanoid robots, given their inherent instability due to limited support areas and high centers of gravity. By moving beyond rigid, periodic gaits, the proposed method enables more fluid and adaptive movements akin to human locomotion.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation behind this research stems from the observed disparity between human and robotic capabilities in dynamic balance and locomotion under challenging conditions. Humans exhibit remarkable agility and stability across diverse and extreme terrains, rapidly adjusting their posture and foot placement to maintain equilibrium even when faced with unexpected slips or off-edge steps. In contrast, humanoid robots, despite recent progress, struggle in such scenarios.

The authors articulate several specific motivations:
- Existing locomotion algorithms for humanoids often fail to effectively traverse extreme environments, particularly when external perception (e.g., vision or LiDAR) is unavailable.
- Current RL methods, while powerful, typically rely on gait-based or perception-conditioned rewards, lacking effective mechanisms to handle unobservable obstacles or sudden balance loss. They often fail to leverage crucial proprioceptive information such as contact forces, support polygon, and center of mass (CoM) for fine-grained dynamic equilibrium.
- The Zero Moment Point (ZMP) concept, proven effective in classical bipedal locomotion, offers a robust framework for dynamic stability that could significantly benefit learning-based approaches if properly integrated.

Based on these motivations, the paper sets forth the following key objectives:
- To propose a novel whole-body locomotion algorithm, termed Dynamic Balanced Humanoid Locomotion (DBHL), that enables humanoid robots to traverse extreme terrains (specifically narrow pathways and unexpected obstacles).
- To achieve this robust locomotion using *only proprioception*, eliminating the need for external vision or LiDAR sensors during real-world deployment.
- To introduce a dynamic balance mechanism within an RL framework by leveraging a novel ZMP-driven reward function, complemented by task-driven rewards.
- To develop a whole-body actor-critic framework that fosters coordinated actions between the upper and lower limbs, crucial for enhancing overall robot stability.
- To experimentally verify the effectiveness of DBHL on a full-sized Unitree H1-2 robot, demonstrating its ability to maintain balance on extremely narrow terrains and under significant external disturbances.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Dynamic Balanced Humanoid Locomotion (DBHL), formulates humanoid locomotion as a goal-conditioned Reinforcement Learning (RL) task. The core approach revolves around training an end-to-end RL policy using the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm with an asymmetric actor-critic framework.

The key components of the methodology are:

- **ZMP-based Dynamic Balance:** This is the cornerstone of DBHL. The authors integrate the Zero Moment Point (ZMP) concept into the RL reward function.
 - They extend the ZMP concept to form a "Zero Moment Line" (ZML) for non-planar surfaces.
 - A custom reward function, `r_zmp`, is designed to encourage the ZML to be close to the approximated center of the support polygon (`p_csp`). This `p_csp` is dynamically calculated based on the contact status and positions of the robot's feet.
 - Crucially, `r_zmp` calculation uses privileged information (e.g., precise forces) during simulation training, but the learned policy relies solely on proprioception (joint positions, velocities, base orientation, etc.) for real-world deployment, ensuring transferability.

- **Whole-Body Locomotion:** To achieve coordinated movements and enhanced dynamic stability, DBHL employs specific techniques for whole-body control:
 - **Angular Momentum Regularization:** A reward term, `r_AM`, is introduced based on the L2 norm of the total angular momentum about the robot's base. This penalizes excessive angular momentum, thereby promoting counterbalancing movements from the upper limbs and minimizing undesired rotational motion.
 - **Multiplicative Action Noise Injection:** Noise is applied to the policy's output actions before feeding them to the robot's PD controllers. This technique encourages the policy to favor smaller actions for upper-body joints, promoting cautious behavior and enhancing robustness by preventing large, unstable arm swings.

- **Policy Learning Details:**
 - **Task and Command:** The robot is trained across a diverse set of narrow terrains (flat, slopes, stairs) with varying difficulty levels through a curriculum learning mechanism. This includes gradually decreasing path widths, increasing slope gradients, and increasing step heights. Velocity commands (linear and yaw) are sampled to encourage versatile locomotion.
 - **Asymmetric Actor-Critic Framework:** The actor network, responsible for generating actions, receives only proprioceptive information and velocity commands, making it suitable for real-world deployment without external sensors. The critic network, which estimates the value function, additionally receives "privileged information" (e.g., ground truth velocities, contact forces, randomized physical parameters) and a surrounding height map. This privileged information helps guide the learning process in simulation more effectively.
 - **Vectorization of Reward and Value:** Instead of summing all reward terms into a single scalar, DBHL treats rewards as a vector. Each reward term is associated with an independent value function, learned via Temporal-Difference (TD) learning using a neural network with multiple output heads. This "reward vectorization" prevents smaller, but critical, reward terms from being overshadowed by larger ones, leading to more stable and efficient learning. The diverse reward functions are categorized into Task, Gait (including ZMP), and Regularization terms.
 - **Symmetry Regularization:** A symmetry loss term is incorporated to leverage the robot's physical symmetry (across the x-z plane). This technique enhances sample efficiency during training and promotes more harmonious and natural-looking gaits.
 - **Domain Randomization:** To facilitate zero-shot sim-to-real transfer, various physical parameters of the simulated environment and robot (e.g., external pushes, action delays, joint stiffness/damping, friction, link masses, CoM offsets, torque disturbances) are randomized during training.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The authors conducted extensive experiments in both simulation (Isaac Gym and MuJoCo) and on a real-world Unitree H1-2 humanoid robot to validate DBHL. Performance was evaluated using success rate (moving at least 4m) and Mean X-Displacement (MXD).

- **Q1: Outperformance in Various Extreme Terrains:**
 - DBHL consistently and significantly outperformed baselines, including a variant without upper body control (DBHL w/o Upper) and the official Unitree RL Gym (URG) framework, across all narrow flat, slope, and stairs terrains, and varying difficulty levels.
 - The "DBHL w/o Upper" variant showed a drastic drop in performance, quantitatively demonstrating the crucial role of *whole-body control*, especially upper limb movements, in maintaining balance on narrow and challenging terrains.
 - DBHL exhibited superior robustness and adaptability compared to URG, highlighting the benefits of its phase-free locomotion and dynamic balance mechanism over traditional phase-based gait methods.

- **Q2: ZMP-based Reward Contribution to Dynamic Balance:**
 - Quantitative comparison in the high-fidelity MuJoCo simulator revealed that the inclusion of the ZMP-based reward substantially improved the robot's success rate and MXD on narrow and challenging terrains. This indicates that the ZMP reward directly enhances dynamic stability.
 - Qualitative analysis of trajectories under external disturbances further highlighted its importance. The robot without the ZMP reward quickly lost balance, with its ZMP-distance diverging. In contrast, DBHL with the ZMP reward successfully maintained stability by keeping the ZMP-distance constrained, demonstrating its effectiveness in actively managing balance.

- **Q3: Impact of Design Choices (Ablation Study):**
 - **Reward Vectorization:** This technique was shown to significantly accelerate the learning process and policy convergence. By independently learning value functions for each reward term, the method effectively addresses the challenge of small reward magnitudes being overlooked in a summed reward, leading to greater learning efficiency.
 - **Multiplicative Action Noise:** An appropriate scale for action noise (found to be 0.03) was critical. It effectively constrained undesired large-angle movements of the upper body joints, thereby improving overall task performance and robustness. Both excessively low and high noise levels led to instabilities.

- **Q4: Real-World Transferability:**
 - DBHL successfully transferred to a full-sized Unitree H1-2 robot with zero-shot capability, demonstrating remarkable real-world performance.
 - In *narrow terrain* experiments, the robot successfully traversed a 25 cm wide track that included slopes, bridges, and stairs. It robustly handled various external disturbances, such as carrying a 5kg payload, navigating dense conical obstacles, withstanding human pushes, and walking on a levered stick.
 - For *irregular planar terrains*, an additional policy trained with DBHL successfully navigated acupressure plates, wooden blocks, planks, and stone-paved paths, while also enduring external pushes.
 - These real-world results unequivocally demonstrate DBHL's effectiveness in enhancing a robot's adaptability and stability in complex and challenging environments *without relying on external vision or LiDAR sensors*.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work represents a significant advancement in humanoid locomotion, primarily due to its novel integration of the classical Zero Moment Point (ZMP) concept into a modern reinforcement learning framework for whole-body control. Its ability to enable complex, dynamically balanced locomotion on extreme narrow and uneven terrains using *only proprioception* is a particularly noteworthy contribution. This intrinsic sensing capability makes the robot inherently more robust to sensor failures, occlusions, or environments where external perception is limited or unavailable.

The successful demonstration of zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on a full-sized Unitree H1-2 humanoid robot under various challenging real-world disturbances (e.g., carrying payloads, enduring pushes, traversing highly irregular surfaces) underscores the practical utility and robustness of DBHL. This robust transferability is a major hurdle in robotics research, and its achievement here highlights the efficacy of the comprehensive training methodology, including domain randomization and the tailored reward structure.

The significance and potential impact of this research are multi-faceted:
- **Advancing Humanoid Autonomy:** It pushes the boundaries of humanoid robot agility, robustness, and autonomous navigation in complex, unstructured environments that are challenging for traditional methods.
- **Bridging Classical and Modern Control:** The work provides a compelling example of how classical control principles can be effectively re-integrated into deep reinforcement learning to address fundamental challenges in robotics, potentially leading to more physically grounded and stable learning-based policies.
- **Enhancing Real-World Deployability:** By eliminating reliance on external perception during deployment, DBHL makes humanoids more versatile and reliable for real-world applications in diverse and unpredictable scenarios where vision or LiDAR might be compromised.
- **New Reward Design Paradigms:** The "reward vectorization" technique offers a valuable methodology for designing more effective reward functions in complex RL tasks, enabling policies to better capture and optimize for multiple, sometimes conflicting, objectives.
- **Future Applications:** The capabilities demonstrated by DBHL open new avenues for humanoid robots in fields requiring extreme mobility and balance, such as disaster response, search and rescue operations in dangerous or inaccessible areas, construction, logistics in unstructured environments, and even personal assistance in homes with varied floor plans and obstacles.

In conclusion, DBHL contributes a robust and adaptable locomotion framework for humanoids, addressing critical limitations of prior work and paving the way for more capable and reliable robots in real-world applications.
