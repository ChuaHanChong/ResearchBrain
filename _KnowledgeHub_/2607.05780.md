# FORGE: Towards Functional Tool-Use Generalization via Keypoint Trajectory Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> FORGE introduces a two-stage robotic policy that enables functional generalization in tool-use by leveraging function-aware 2D keypoint trajectories as an intermediate representation. The approach improves success rates by over 2x compared to end-to-end baselines on unseen tools in both simulated and real-world environments.

## Problem

- Robots struggle to adapt tool-use to novel objects that were not part of their training, unlike humans who can repurpose various objects for the same task.
- Existing generalization methods (scene, category) primarily tolerate visual variations while maintaining consistent underlying motion, failing to address "functional generalization" where motor patterns must change for different tools performing the same function.
- There is a significant "perception-to-action gap" where functionally equivalent tools share a visually recognizable intent, but this does not directly translate to identical motor patterns in the action space.

## Method

- **FORGE** proposes a two-stage policy architecture that decouples ==functional reasoning== (planning abstract functional intent) from ==action execution== (translating the plan into robot movements).
- It utilizes ==2D keypoint trajectories== as an intermediate representation, hypothesized to balance functional expressiveness and action groundability by capturing function-relevant contact and motion structure while discarding tool-specific appearance.
- Stage 1 (Functional Reasoning) predicts future keypoint trajectories from visual observations using a ==conditional flow-matching model== trained on large-scale action-free data; Stage 2 (Grounded Execution) translates these predicted trajectories into robot actions using a conditional flow-matching model trained on smaller action-labeled data.

## Results

- FORGE achieved an average success rate of **0.36** in simulation, representing over a **2x** improvement compared to the Diffusion Policy baseline (**0.17**) on unseen tools.
- In real-world experiments on a Franka robot, FORGE demonstrated an overall success rate of **0.63**, significantly outperforming the Flow-Matching base policy (**0.22**) on diverse unseen tools.
- Ablation studies confirmed 2D keypoint trajectories as the most effective intermediate representation, yielding a **0.49** success rate, compared to affordance images (**0.20**) or human video prompts (**0.19**) when directly conditioning the execution policy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Functional generalization is a distinct and more challenging problem than traditional generalization types, as it necessitates adapting the robot's motor pattern to a new tool's properties.
> - Function-aware 2D keypoint trajectories are an optimal intermediate representation, effectively bridging the perception-to-action gap by abstracting functional intent while remaining precise enough for action grounding.
> - A two-stage policy design leveraging abundant action-free data for functional reasoning and moderate action-labeled data for grounding provides a practical and data-efficient solution for functional generalization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has significantly advanced, with considerable research focused on enabling robots to generalize learned skills across various conditions. Existing generalization problems are broadly categorized into:

- **Scene-level generalization:** This involves a robot policy maintaining robustness despite variations in visual or physical contexts, such as changes in object layouts, lighting conditions, or camera viewpoints.
- **Category-level generalization:** This focuses on transferring manipulation skills to new objects that belong to the same semantic category but possess differing geometry, size, or texture.
- **Cross-embodiment generalization:** This aims to develop unified policies that can transfer skills across different robot platforms, such as robotic arms and humanoids.

Current robotic systems trained for tool-use often overfit to the specific appearance and geometry of the tools encountered during training. This limitation means they typically fail when presented with a novel tool designed to perform the same function. While humans can readily repurpose everyday objects (e.g., a book or a stone) to accomplish a task like driving a nail, robots currently lack this adaptive capacity.

This paper introduces and formalizes "functional generalization," which represents a distinct and more challenging dimension of generalization. Unlike scene or category generalization, which primarily tolerate visual variations while the underlying motion remains consistent, functional generalization demands a change in the motor pattern itself. Using a novel tool for the same function requires identifying its appropriate contact region, aligning it correctly, and executing a distinct motion pattern, even when the tool's physical characteristics and required trajectory differ entirely from those observed during training. The core difficulty lies in the "perception-to-action gap": functionally equivalent tools share a common functional intent that is visually recognizable, but this perceptual similarity does not directly translate to identical motor patterns in the action space.

Previous efforts to improve generalization in robot policy learning have included scaling up action-labeled robotic data, learning transferable motion priors from large-scale action-free video data, and utilizing structured intermediate representations like interaction heatmaps or object-centric representations. However, these methods primarily focus on standard manipulation tasks or struggle to extract compact, function-relevant intent in complex tool-use scenarios. For instance, dense video representations can entangle function-relevant cues with tool-specific appearance, while overly sparse representations might discard critical geometric and temporal information. Even methods that use keypoint trajectories for general manipulation often rely on generic trackers, which may not capture the specific functional motion required for novel tool-use.

The work presented in this paper, FORGE, positions itself by specifically addressing this functional generalization gap in robotic tool-use. It investigates intermediate representations to bridge the perception-to-action gap and proposes a novel two-stage policy that decouples functional reasoning from action execution. By leveraging function-aware keypoint trajectories, FORGE aims to enable robots to generalize tool-use to unseen objects, thereby advancing robotic autonomy in open-ended, real-world environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable robots to achieve "functional generalization" in tool-use, a capability where an agent can accomplish the same function using diverse, unseen tools. This objective is motivated by a critical gap observed in current robotic systems: while humans effortlessly adapt to repurpose various objects for a given task, robots typically fail to transfer a learned tool function to novel tools that were not part of their training data.

The detailed motivations and objectives are as follows:

1. **Formalize Functional Generalization:** The authors aim to formally define functional generalization in robotic tool-use. They highlight its distinction from conventional generalization types such as scene-level or category-level generalization. Unlike these existing paradigms, which primarily involve robustness to visual variations while maintaining consistent underlying motion, functional generalization necessitates adapting the motion itself to a new tool's properties. For example, striking a target with a novel tool requires identifying its effective contact region, aligning it, and generating an appropriate motion, even if the tool's shape and trajectory differ significantly from known tools.

2. **Address the Perception-to-Action Gap:** A core challenge identified is the fundamental mismatch between perception and action. Functionally equivalent tools share a visually recognizable common intent (e.g., a contact region for striking and a motion to bring it to the target). However, this visual similarity does not directly translate into similar motor patterns in the action space, as each tool demands a distinct manipulation strategy. The motivation is to bridge this gap by finding an effective intermediate representation that can abstract functional intent.

3. **Identify an Optimal Intermediate Representation:** The research is motivated by the need for an intermediate representation that can effectively carry functional intent across different tools. This representation must satisfy two conflicting requirements:
 - **Functional Expressiveness:** It must be sufficiently expressive to capture crucial functional information, such as where contact should be made and how the tool should move onto the target.
 - **Action Groundability:** It must be grounded enough to be reliably predicted from action-free observations without overfitting to specific tool appearances, and then translated into precise robot actions.
 The authors systematically evaluate several candidates, including affordance images, human video prompts, and 2D keypoint trajectories, to determine which best balances these demands. They hypothesize that 2D keypoint trajectories will prove most effective due to their ability to capture function-relevant contact and motion structure while abstracting away tool-specific visual details.

4. **Propose a Decoupled Policy Architecture (FORGE):** Based on the identified optimal representation, the objective is to develop a two-stage policy named FunctiOnal Reasoning and Grounded Execution (FORGE). This architecture is designed to decouple functional reasoning (planning the abstract functional intent) from action execution (translating the plan into robot movements). This decoupling is motivated by the data asymmetry typically encountered in robotics: large quantities of action-free visual data are readily available, while collecting precise action-labeled robot demonstrations is costly and time-consuming. FORGE aims to leverage this data asymmetry by:
 - **Stage 1 (Functional Reasoning):** Predicting generalizable keypoint trajectories for unseen tools from large-scale action-free data.
 - **Stage 2 (Grounded Execution):** Grounding these predicted functional plans into executable robot actions using a smaller, limited set of action-labeled demonstrations.

5. **Benchmark and Validate Performance:** A key objective is to establish a rigorous evaluation framework for functional generalization. The authors introduce a seven-tool hitting-function benchmark in both simulation and the real world. This benchmark is used to systematically evaluate FORGE against state-of-the-art methods on unseen tools, with the motivation to demonstrate significant improvements in success rates and validate the efficacy of the proposed approach.

In summary, the motivation stems from the limitation of current robots in adapting tool-use to novel objects, a capacity critical for open-ended environments. The central aim is to provide a robust, data-efficient solution by identifying and exploiting an abstract functional representation within a decoupled policy architecture.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research addresses the problem of functional generalization in robotic tool-use by proposing a two-stage policy, FunctiOnal Reasoning and Grounded Execution (FORGE), predicated on the use of 2D keypoint trajectories as an intermediate representation.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

The problem is framed as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) for tool-use manipulation tasks. The state $s_t$ at time $t$ comprises visual observation $o_t$, proprioceptive state $q_t$, and optionally a functional intermediate representation $x_t$. The action $a_t$ is the robot's motor command. The task function (e.g., hitting a target) is fixed, but it must be performed using a set of "seen tools" ($T_{seen}$) for training and a disjoint set of "unseen tools" ($T_{unseen}$) for evaluation. This implies that the tool's appearance, geometry, and required contact positions and motions can all change.

Two types of data are utilized:
- **Large-scale action-free dataset ($D_U$):** Consists of visual observations and corresponding functional intermediate representations. This data is abundant as it does not require robot actions.
- **Smaller action-labeled robotic dataset ($D_L$):** Includes visual observations, intermediate representations, proprioceptive states, and ground-truth actions. This data is costly to collect, with $|D_L| \ll |D_U|$.

This data asymmetry, combined with the perception-to-action gap, motivates the two-stage policy decomposition:
$\pi_{FORGE} = \pi_{sys2}(\hat{X}_{t:t+H} | o_t, x_t) \circ \pi_{sys1}(a_{t:t+H} | o_t, q_t, \hat{X}_{t:t+H})$.
Here, $\pi_{sys2}$ performs functional reasoning by predicting future intermediate representations, and $\pi_{sys1}$ performs grounded execution by converting these predictions into robot actions.

#### 3.2. Functional Intermediate Representations Selection

The research systematically evaluates candidate intermediate representations to identify one that balances functional expressiveness and action groundability. The criteria for a suitable representation are: preserving function-relevant structure across tools, being predictable from action-free observations, and being groundable into precise robot actions.

Three candidates were compared:
1. **Affordance Image:** A time-invariant feature vector $x^{(i)}_t = E_{aff}(I^{(i)}_{aff})$ derived from an image encoder applied to an affordance image, which manually annotates the grasp point and hitting region for each tool.
2. **Human Video Prompt:** A time-invariant sequence-level feature $x^{(i)}_t = \text{AvgPool}(E_{vid}(V^{(i)}_{hum}))$ obtained by encoding a human demonstration video with a video encoder and applying temporal average pooling.
3. **Keypoint Trajectory:** A sequence of 2D keypoints $P^{(i)} = \{p^{(i)}_t\}^{T}_{t=1}$, where each $p^{(i)}_t$ contains $K$ 2D keypoints. The keypoint coordinates are flattened and encoded into $x^{(i)}_t = E_{kp}(\text{Flatten}(p^{(i)}_t))$. The authors hypothesize that keypoint trajectories are a strong candidate because they discard tool-specific appearance while retaining compact geometric and temporal information crucial for function.

To evaluate these, a heuristic experiment (Fig. 2a) was conducted. Each representation was pre-extracted for both seen and unseen tools and used as a direct condition for an execution policy ($\pi_{sys1}$). The policy was trained on action-labeled data from seen tools and evaluated on unseen tools, with success determined by whether the predicted hitting point overlapped the target within a tolerance threshold. Keypoint trajectories demonstrated the highest performance among the three.

#### 3.3. Functional Reasoning and Grounded Execution Policy (FORGE)

FORGE adopts a two-stage training strategy:

#### 3.3.1. Stage 1: Action-free Functional Reasoning ($\pi_{sys2}$)

- **Objective:** To predict future functional keypoint trajectories from visual observations.
- **Model:** A keypoint motion predictor $\pi_{sys2}$ is implemented as a conditional flow-matching model in the keypoint space. Given $o_t$ and $x_t$, it generates $\hat{X}_{t:t+H}$.
- **Training:** It is trained on the large-scale action-free dataset $D_U$.
- **Keypoint Extraction:** For training, a hitting point and target point are specified. SAM2 is used to segment the tool mask corresponding to the hitting point, and Farthest Point Sampling (FPS) is applied to sample $N=5$ additional keypoints on this mask. CoTracker then tracks these keypoints to obtain 2D trajectories. This stage leverages action-free data to learn transferable functional intent as keypoint-based motions.

#### 3.3.2. Stage 2: Grounded Execution Policy ($\pi_{sys1}$)

- **Objective:** To ground the predicted functional plans (keypoint trajectories) into executable robot actions.
- **Model:** An execution policy $\pi_{sys1}$ is implemented as a conditional flow-matching model in the action space.
- **Training:** It is trained on the smaller action-labeled robotic dataset $D_L$. The pretrained $\pi_{sys2}$ from Stage 1 is frozen.
- **Input:** Given $o_t$, robot state $q_t$, and 2D keypoint pixels $x_t$, $\pi_{sys2}$ first predicts $\hat{X}_{t:t+H}$. To enhance robustness to prediction errors, random pixel perturbations (1-5 pixels shift with 0.5 probability) are applied to $\hat{X}_{t:t+H}$ during training to yield $\tilde{X}_{t:t+H}$.
- **Output:** $\pi_{sys1}$ then grounds $\tilde{X}_{t:t+H}$ into executable robot actions $\hat{A}_{t:t+H}$.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Simulation Benchmark:**
 - **Tools:** 7 tools (hammer, tobject, pickaxe, mustard, scoop, shoe, book).
 - **Settings:** Each tool has 3 initial settings; 30 demonstrations collected per tool-setting pair using a four-stage Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) planner (630 total demonstrations).
 - **Variations:** Random initialization of target cube position and tool-specific hitting point on the tool boundary.
 - **Training/Evaluation:** Trained on 4 tools (hammer, tobject, pickaxe, mustard) and evaluated on 3 unseen tools (scoop, shoe, book).
 - **Success Metric:** Robot strikes the target cube using the designated hitting point.
- **Real-World Setting:**
 - **Platform:** Franka robot.
 - **Tools:** 3 tools (hammer, mustard, book); each with 2 hitting patterns. 20 expert demonstrations per pattern (120 total trajectories).
 - **Training/Evaluation:** Trained on hammer and mustard, evaluated on the unseen book tool.
 - **Success Metric:** Robot follows the specified hitting pattern and strikes the target.
- **Baselines:**
 - **Flow-Matching (FM) & Diffusion Policy (DP):** End-to-end visuomotor policies for direct observation-to-action mapping.
 - **ATM (Any-point Trajectory Modeling):** Conditions on keypoint trajectories, but uses a generic tracker (pretrained on LIBERO-90) instead of a function-aware predictor.
- **Evaluation Metric:** Success rate (SR).
- **Hypotheses Tested:** The experiments aimed to validate four hypotheses: H1 (need for intermediate representation), H2 (keypoint trajectories are optimal), H3 (function-aware keypoints are essential), and H4 (two-stage design is practical with moderate data).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several key findings based on extensive experiments in both simulation and real-world environments, supported by ablation studies.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

The simulation benchmark involved training on four tools and evaluating on three unseen tools (Scoop, Shoe, Book) across three initial settings for each.

- **Evidence for H1 (Need for Intermediate Representation):** End-to-end policies, Flow-Matching (FM) and Diffusion Policy (DP), achieved average success rates of 0.09 and 0.17 respectively (Table 1). This low performance indicates that without an explicit intermediate representation to convey contact region and target alignment, these policies struggle to generalize motor patterns to novel tools, validating the existence of a perception-to-action gap.

- **Evidence for H3 (Function-Aware Keypoints Required):** ATM, which also uses keypoint trajectories but obtains them from a generic tracker, exhibited a low average success rate of 0.08. This result suggests that generic keypoint tracking is insufficient, and the keypoint trajectories must anticipate function-relevant motion to effectively guide the robot, thereby supporting H3.

- **FORGE's Performance:** FORGE achieved an average success rate of 0.36, representing over a 2× improvement compared to the DP baseline (0.17). This performance was consistent across all three unseen tools. Qualitatively, FORGE's predicted keypoint trajectories were observed to guide the tool-specific contact region directly toward the target before impact (Figure 4a).

- **Observed Failure Modes:** Two primary failure modes for FORGE were identified:
 - **Imprecise Hitting:** The 2D keypoint trajectories sometimes captured the overall motion but lacked fine-grained spatial alignment, suggesting a potential benefit from 3D-aware functional representations.
 - **Tool Dropping:** The execution policy occasionally produced jerky actions, indicating a need for smoothness regularization or trajectory post-processing.

#### 4.2. Real-World Results

Real-world experiments were conducted on a Franka robot, training on two tools (hammer, mustard) and evaluating on an unseen tool (book) under two hitting patterns. Additional experiments involved training on three seen tools and evaluating on three unseen tools (tobject, book, shoe) across two hitting patterns each.

- **FORGE's Superiority:** FORGE consistently outperformed the FM base policy on the unseen book tool for both hitting patterns (Figure 5). In broader real-world evaluations (Table 6), FORGE achieved an overall success rate of 0.63, significantly higher than FM's 0.22 across diverse unseen tools and hitting patterns.
- **Confirmation of H1 in Real-World:** The real-world failures of FM, often failing to align the correct hitting region of the novel tool with the target, further support H1. FORGE's ability to predict function-relevant keypoint trajectories provided explicit guidance for accurate alignment and successful hits, demonstrating its practical applicability.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies: Effect of Functional Intermediate Representations (Table 2)

- **Support for H2:** This ablation compared the effect of different intermediate representations by directly conditioning the execution policy. While affordance images (0.20 SR) and human video prompts (0.19 SR) improved generalization compared to the Flow-Matching baseline (0.09 SR), 2D keypoint trajectories achieved the highest success rate (0.49 SR). This validates H2, indicating that keypoint trajectories provide the most effective balance of capturing fine-grained functional information and structured temporal motion, making them suitable for action grounding.

#### 4.3.2. Effect of Action-Labeled Tool Diversity (Table 3)

- **Support for H4:** The study evaluated FORGE's performance with varying amounts of action-labeled data for fine-tuning. Success rate increased monotonically with the diversity of action-labeled training tools: 0.21 for 1-to-6 tool split, 0.36 for 4-to-3 (standard setting), and 0.52 for 6-to-1. The non-trivial performance in the 4-to-3 setting supports H4. This indicates that FORGE's two-stage design provides a practical recipe where scalable action-free data efficiently supports cross-tool functional reasoning, and only a moderate amount of action-labeled robotic data is sufficient for grounding the predicted trajectories into executable actions.

In summary, the main findings demonstrate that functional generalization in robotic tool-use is a significant challenge for end-to-end policies and generic keypoint-based methods. FORGE, with its two-stage architecture leveraging function-aware 2D keypoint trajectories, consistently and significantly outperforms these baselines, validating its core design principles and hypotheses in both simulated and physical environments.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research addresses a fundamental limitation in robotic manipulation: the inability of robots to functionally generalize tool-use to novel objects. The significance and potential impact of this work can be understood from several perspectives:

1. **Advancement in Robotic Generalization:** The paper formalizes "functional generalization" as a distinct and more challenging problem than existing categories like scene or category generalization. By explicitly tackling this problem, the research pushes the boundaries of how robots can adapt to unforeseen situations and tools. This represents a crucial step towards creating more versatile and autonomous robotic systems that can operate in unstructured, open-ended real-world environments where the "right" tool may not always be available.

2. **Effective Intermediate Representation for Functional Intent:** The identification of 2D keypoint trajectories as an optimal intermediate representation is a key contribution. This representation effectively bridges the perception-to-action gap by capturing function-relevant contact and motion structure while discarding tool-specific appearance, which often causes overfitting. This finding provides a valuable direction for future research in designing robust and generalizable robotic policies for tasks requiring abstract functional understanding.

3. **Practical Two-Stage Policy Design (FORGE):** The proposed FORGE architecture offers a practical and data-efficient approach to achieving functional generalization. By decoupling functional reasoning (predicting keypoint trajectories from abundant action-free data) from action execution (grounding these predictions into robot actions with limited action-labeled data), FORGE addresses the pervasive challenge of data scarcity in robotics. This strategy makes complex tool-use learning more scalable and accessible, reducing the reliance on extensive and costly robot demonstrations. The demonstration that moderate action-labeled data suffices for action grounding further highlights its practical utility.

4. **Improved Performance in Tool-Use Tasks:** FORGE's consistent and significant outperformance (over 2× improvement in average success rate) compared to state-of-the-art methods on unseen tools in both simulation and real-world benchmarks demonstrates its efficacy. This provides empirical evidence for the viability of the proposed approach, indicating that robots can learn to adapt their manipulation strategies to functionally equivalent but visually distinct tools.

5. **Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for future research, which could lead to further improvements and broader applicability:
 - **Automated Keypoint Identification:** Current reliance on specified hitting and target points can be overcome by integrating vision foundation models or pre-training keypoint proposal models to automatically identify function-relevant keypoints, enhancing autonomy.
 - **Integration of Grasping:** Extending FORGE to predict grasp styles from keypoint-based functional trajectories would enable robots to handle a wider range of tools and tasks, potentially including dexterous manipulation with advanced end-effectors.
 - **3D-Aware Representations and Smoothness:** Addressing current limitations such as imprecise 2D hitting and jerky actions (e.g., tool dropping) through 3D-aware functional representations and smoothness regularization or trajectory post-processing could further refine execution.

In conclusion, FORGE represents a significant stride towards enabling robots to exhibit human-like adaptability in tool-use. By providing a principled framework for functional generalization, it lays the groundwork for robots that can more effectively interact with and adapt to the complexities of real-world environments, contributing to advances in fields such as manufacturing, service robotics, and exploration.
