# Which Attention Heads Matter for In-Context Learning?

> [!summary] Summary
> This research from UC Berkeley demonstrates that Function Vector (FV) attention heads are the primary drivers of in-context learning in large language models, rather than induction heads. The study resolves discrepancies in prior work by emphasizing the importance of specific evaluation metrics and controlling for functional correlations between different attention head types, showing that many induction heads develop FV capabilities during training.

## Problem

- Understanding the internal mechanisms enabling in-context learning (ICL) in large language models (LLMs).
- Resolving conflicting hypotheses about whether induction mechanisms or function vector representations are more crucial for ICL.
- Identifying which specific attention heads within transformer models are most responsible for ICL capabilities.

## Method

- Analyzed **12** decoder-only transformer models (**70M** to **7B** parameters) from Pythia, GPT-2, and Llama 2 suites.
- Identified induction heads using an ==induction score== and ==Function Vector (FV) heads== using ==causal mediation analysis==.
- Conducted comprehensive ablation studies (mean ablation and ablation with exclusion) to measure the impact of different head types on ICL accuracy and token-loss difference, while also analyzing training dynamics.

## Results

- Ablating Function Vector (FV) heads consistently led to the largest degradation in ICL accuracy across various model sizes, affirming their primary role.
- When controlling for correlations, induction heads showed a **significantly smaller** impact on ICL performance compared to FV heads.
- Analysis of training dynamics revealed that many induction heads acquire FV functionality over time, and the relative importance of FV heads increases with model scale.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Function Vector (FV) heads are the primary drivers of in-context learning, consistently causing the largest performance drops when ablated.
> - Induction heads play a secondary role in ICL, especially when controlling for correlations with FV functionality.
> - Many attention heads that initially function as induction heads evolve to develop FV functionality during training, suggesting a developmental trajectory from simpler pattern matching to more sophisticated task representation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

In-context learning (ICL) - the ability of large language models (LLMs) to learn new tasks from a few examples provided in the prompt without parameter updates - has become a fundamental capability of modern AI systems. Despite its practical importance, the mechanisms enabling this ability remain incompletely understood. What exactly happens inside these models that allows them to adapt to new tasks on the fly?

In their paper "Which Attention Heads Matter for In-Context Learning?", researchers Kayo Yin and Jacob Steinhardt from UC Berkeley present a thorough investigation into the attention mechanisms that enable in-context learning. The study challenges existing explanations and offers new insights about how large language models accomplish this remarkable feat.

### 2. Understanding In-Context Learning

In-context learning refers to an LLM's ability to perform a new task by conditioning on demonstrations of the task in its context window. For example, when shown a few examples of English-to-French translations followed by an English sentence, the model can translate the new sentence without any fine-tuning. This ability emerged somewhat unexpectedly in large language models and has significantly expanded their practical utility.

Two primary hypotheses have been proposed to explain how models perform ICL:

1. **Induction mechanisms**: The model identifies patterns where similar inputs map to similar outputs and copies relevant output tokens based on matching input patterns
2. **Function vector representation**: The model internally represents the task as a vector that encodes the function mapping inputs to outputs

These competing explanations have led to different conclusions about which components of the model are most crucial for ICL.

### 3. Competing Hypotheses: Induction vs. Function Vector Heads

Previous work has identified specialized attention heads within transformer models that appear to play specific roles:

- **Induction heads**: These attention heads look for repeated patterns in the input and learn to copy tokens that follow similar contexts. For example, if "dog" followed "brown" earlier in the text, an induction head might predict "dog" when seeing "brown" again.

- **Function Vector (FV) heads**: These attention heads appear to encode a latent representation of the task being performed. Rather than simply copying patterns, they encode the underlying function that maps inputs to outputs.

The key question addressed in this study is: which of these mechanisms is more important for in-context learning?

### 4. Methodology

The researchers employed a comprehensive approach to answer this question:

1. **Model Selection**: They analyzed 12 decoder-only transformer models ranging from 70 million to 7 billion parameters, including models from the Pythia suite, GPT-2 variants, and Llama 2.

2. **Identifying Heads**:
 - **Induction heads** were identified using an "induction score" that measures the attention weight placed on tokens following similar contexts.
 - **FV heads** were identified using causal mediation analysis to determine which heads' activations most strongly affected ICL performance.

3. **Ablation Studies**: The researchers systematically disabled different types of attention heads to measure their impact on ICL performance:
 - **Mean ablation**: Replacing head outputs with their average across the dataset
 - **Ablation with exclusion**: Controlling for correlations by excluding heads that score high on both metrics

4. **Metrics**: They measured:
 - **Few-shot ICL accuracy**: The model's ability to perform tasks after seeing demonstrations
 - **Token-loss difference**: The change in prediction loss between early and later tokens in context

5. **Training Dynamics**: They analyzed how induction and FV heads develop throughout training.

### 5. Key Findings

The study yielded several important findings that clarify how in-context learning works in large language models:

1. **FV heads are the primary drivers of ICL**: Ablating FV heads consistently caused the largest drops in ICL performance across model sizes. This effect was especially pronounced when controlling for correlations between induction and FV heads.

 ```python
   # Simplified pseudocode for ablation experiment
   def ablate_heads(model, head_indices, test_examples):
       # Store original head outputs
       original_outputs = model.get_head_outputs(test_examples)

       # Replace target heads with their mean output
       mean_outputs = compute_mean_outputs(model, head_indices, dataset)
       model.set_head_outputs(head_indices, mean_outputs)

       # Measure ICL performance
       ablated_performance = model.evaluate_icl(test_examples)

       # Restore original head outputs
       model.set_head_outputs(head_indices, original_outputs)

       return ablated_performance
   ```

2. **Induction heads play a secondary role**: When controlling for overlapping functionality, induction heads had a significantly smaller impact on ICL performance than FV heads.

3. **Distinct but correlated mechanisms**: Induction and FV heads are largely distinct, with minimal overlap in the top-ranked heads. However, they show some correlation in behavior.

4. **Training dynamics reveal evolution**: Many heads that initially function as induction heads evolve to develop FV functionality during training, but the reverse transition (FV to induction) was never observed.

5. **Scale dependence**: As models grow larger, FV heads become increasingly important relative to induction heads for ICL performance.

### 6. Significance and Implications

These findings significantly advance our understanding of how large language models perform in-context learning:

1. **Mechanism clarity**: By identifying FV heads as the primary drivers of ICL, the research provides a clearer picture of how models adapt to new tasks without parameter updates.

2. **Architectural implications**: Understanding which components drive ICL can inform more efficient model designs that prioritize the development of effective FV mechanisms.

3. **Training implications**: The observation that induction heads often evolve into FV heads suggests that induction might be a stepping stone toward more sophisticated ICL capabilities.

4. **Methodological insights**: The study demonstrates the importance of controlling for correlations between different mechanisms and selecting appropriate evaluation metrics.

### 7. Reconciling Divergent Results

The researchers found that previous studies reached different conclusions about the importance of induction heads due to two key methodological differences:

1. **Evaluation metrics**: Prior work often used token-loss difference rather than direct ICL accuracy. When the authors replicated these methods, they found that token-loss difference can lead to different conclusions than direct task performance.

2. **Failure to control for correlations**: Previous studies did not account for the correlation between induction and FV functionality. When these correlations are controlled for using ablation with exclusion, the primary importance of FV heads becomes clear.

 ```
   Correlation effect example:
   - Head A: Induction score 0.4, FV score 0.02
   - Head B: Induction score 0.1, FV score 0.09

   Without controlling for correlation:
   - Ablating top induction heads → Includes Head A → Also removes some FV functionality
   - This makes induction heads appear more important for ICL than they actually are
   ```

### 8. Evolution of Attention Mechanisms

The authors observed a fascinating pattern in how these specialized heads develop during training:

1. **Early training**: Induction heads emerge first, typically developing rapidly around 1,000 training steps.

2. **Later training**: Many heads that start as pure induction heads gradually develop FV functionality, while maintaining their induction capabilities.

3. **Directionality**: The transition always proceeds from induction → FV, never the reverse.

This suggests that induction may be a simpler capability that serves as a foundation for the more complex FV functionality that drives sophisticated in-context learning.

### 9. Scale Dependence

The study revealed an important relationship between model scale and the mechanisms of in-context learning:

1. As models grow larger, FV heads become increasingly important relative to induction heads.

2. The correlation between induction and FV functionality tends to decrease with scale, suggesting more specialized attention heads in larger models.

3. This challenges the strong version of the "universality hypothesis" in interpretability research, which posits that the same mechanisms appear across different scales.

### 10. Conclusion

The research by Yin and Steinhardt provides compelling evidence that Function Vector heads are the primary drivers of in-context learning in large language models, with induction heads playing a secondary role. The study resolves seemingly contradictory findings in previous work by highlighting the importance of appropriate evaluation metrics and controlling for correlations between different mechanisms.

The evolution of attention heads from induction to FV functionality suggests a developmental trajectory in how models acquire increasingly sophisticated in-context learning capabilities. This insight opens new avenues for research into how to more efficiently train models with strong ICL abilities.

For the broader field of AI interpretability, this work demonstrates the importance of rigorous methodology and careful experimental design when studying the internal mechanisms of neural networks. As large language models continue to evolve, understanding these mechanisms will be essential for building more capable, efficient, and reliable AI systems.
