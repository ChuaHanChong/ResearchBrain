# GAUGE: A Measurement-Grounded Benchmark for Physical Fidelity in Simulation Engines and Video World Models

> [!summary] Summary
> GAUGE is introduced as a measurement-grounded benchmark that evaluates the physical fidelity of both numerical physics engines and generative video world models, leveraging 22 controlled real-world tasks with precisely calibrated physical parameters. The benchmark reveals that current simulation technologies struggle to accurately reproduce fundamental physical principles and parameters, particularly in dynamic contact and high-acceleration deformations, despite often achieving visual plausibility.

## Problem

- Existing benchmarks for physical fidelity often provide isolated evaluations for specific object classes, lacking comprehensive assessment across diverse mechanical regimes.
- Many current evaluation methods rely on subjective perceptual similarity, human judgments, or learned evaluators, which fail to diagnose specific physical principle violations or parameter inaccuracies.
- There is a lack of a unified, real-world-grounded diagnostic framework capable of consistently evaluating both traditional physics engines and emerging generative video world models.

## Method

- **GAUGE** establishes a ==dual-track evaluation framework== comprising 22 controlled real-world task families that cover rigid, textile, and volumetric deformable objects.
- The benchmark integrates ==high-precision motion capture data== with calibrated physical properties for all assets (e.g., friction coefficients, Young's modulus) to provide an objective ground truth.
- It employs distinct evaluation metrics for physics engines (e.g., RMSE, DTW, task-specific physical quantities) and world models (e.g., ==Dynamic Error, R², Quadratic Form Improvement==) to assess both trajectory matching and adherence to physical laws.

## Results

- Physics engines exhibited substantial discrepancies in dynamic contact (e.g., Newton's cradle showed poor momentum transfer), high-acceleration cloth motion, and volumetric deformation, with errors often **an order of magnitude** above real-world baselines.
- World models consistently struggled to recover accurate physical parameters; for instance, inferred free-fall accelerations were often **less than 1%** of gravity, and pendulum periods were significantly longer than measured, despite **high R² values (0.99)**.
- The use of 'negative prompts' in world models yielded inconsistent improvements across tasks and models, suggesting that current prompt formulations do not reliably constrain generated physics.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Physical fidelity is distinct from visual plausibility; current simulators and world models frequently fail to reproduce fundamental physical principles or accurately encode their parameters.
> - No single physics engine demonstrates uniform fidelity across all physical regimes (rigid, textile, deformable), indicating performance is highly domain-dependent and tied to specific solver limitations.
> - World models can generate visually convincing motion that fits the structural form of physical laws (e.g., oscillation) but encode significantly incorrect underlying physical parameters (e.g., period, acceleration).

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of embodied intelligence, particularly in robotics, heavily relies on "real-to-sim-to-real" pipelines for training, evaluation, and data generation. The effectiveness of these pipelines is contingent upon the physical fidelity of the underlying simulation environments. These environments can be traditional numerical physics engines or emerging generative video world models. Prior research has shown that aligned simulation experiments can predict real-world robot policy performance, but this relies on accurate physical dynamics. A significant challenge in this area is that high visual fidelity in simulators does not guarantee accurate physical dynamics, potentially leading to policies that exploit simulation artifacts or incorrect performance rankings.

Existing benchmarks for evaluating physical fidelity often suffer from several limitations:
- **Isolation**: Evaluations are frequently conducted for specific object classes (e.g., rigid, cloth, soft bodies) rather than across diverse mechanical regimes.
- **Subjectivity**: Many benchmarks rely on perceptual similarity, human judgments, or learned evaluators, which primarily indicate visual plausibility or commonsense agreement without pinpointing specific physical principle violations or parameter inaccuracies.
- **Limited Diagnosability**: They often do not provide sufficient insight into *which* physical principles or parameters are violated, hindering targeted improvements.
- **Ground Truth Issues**: Some benchmarks either fit effective simulator parameters from the same observations used for evaluation, or use simulator-derived ground truth, which does not provide an independent real-world reference.
- **Lack of Unified Framework**: There has been a lack of a standardized, real-world-grounded diagnostic benchmark capable of evaluating both numerical physics engines and generative world models within a consistent framework, especially across diverse material properties and physical phenomena.

GAUGE aims to address these gaps by introducing a real-world-grounded diagnostic benchmark that provides calibrated physical metadata, detailed real-world trajectories, and task-specific observables. It offers a unified framework for evaluating both types of simulators, enabling a more diagnostic assessment of physical fidelity beyond mere visual plausibility. It also distinguishes itself by providing experimentally characterized physical parameters spanning multiple mechanical regimes, connected directly to dynamic-fidelity evaluation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the introduction of GAUGE are:

1. **To establish a measurement-grounded benchmark for physical fidelity**: The core motivation is to move beyond subjective or perceptual evaluations by grounding the benchmark in precise real-world experimental measurements. This provides an objective reference for assessing how well simulators replicate physical reality.
2. **To enable joint evaluation of numerical physics engines and generative video world models**: Recognizing that both traditional simulators and emerging AI-driven world models are used as implicit or explicit simulators, GAUGE seeks to provide a unified framework to evaluate both, allowing for direct comparison and understanding of their respective strengths and weaknesses in reproducing physics.
3. **To facilitate diagnostic assessment of physical principle violations**: A key goal is not just to determine *if* a simulator deviates from real-world physics, but *how* and *where* it deviates. This includes identifying which specific physical principles (e.g., collision, friction, momentum transfer, oscillation, deformation) or parameters (e.g., acceleration, period, damping) are violated. This diagnostic capability is crucial for guiding the development of more accurate and robust simulators.
4. **To address limitations of existing benchmarks**: The authors are motivated by the shortcomings of prior work, which often focused on isolated object classes, relied on perceptual similarity, or lacked real-world calibrated physical parameters. GAUGE aims to provide a comprehensive, cross-regime benchmark with high-fidelity real-world data and associated metadata.
5. **To support the development of more physically faithful tools for embodied intelligence**: By providing a rigorous evaluation tool, GAUGE intends to accelerate the creation of simulators and world models that can reliably support robot learning, policy evaluation, and large-scale data generation, thereby improving the robustness and transferability of embodied intelligence systems from simulation to reality.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GAUGE establishes a dual-track evaluation framework based on a shared foundation of real-world experimental data.

#### 3.1. GAUGE Dataset Pipeline: Dataset Design

The benchmark comprises 22 controlled task families designed to evaluate simulators from a fundamental physical perspective, rather than specific manipulation tasks. These tasks cover:
- **Rigid Bodies (8 tasks)**: Evaluate collision, friction, and the conservation of momentum and energy (e.g., slope contact, nonsmooth contact, slope slider, turntable, bouncing ball, Newton’s cradle, wall breaking, pendulum).
- **Deformable Objects (14 tasks)**:
 - **Quasi-one-dimensional (1 task)**: Rope winding (self-collision, stretch modulus).
 - **Quasi-two-dimensional textiles (6 tasks)**: Textile stretching, bending, flinging, funnel, rotating ball, tablecloth pulling (stretch/bending modulus, high-acceleration motion, collision, friction).
 - **Three-dimensional volumetric deformable bodies (7 tasks)**: Foam stretching, compressing, shearing, twisting, bending; stick-stack, cantilever beam (stiffness, elastic modulus, rigid-deformable interaction, friction).
Material variants are introduced to increase diversity: wood, plastic, metal for rigid objects; rayon, satin, uniform cloth, Oxford fabric, synthetic leather, nylon taslan for textiles; and soft/hard foam and rubber for 3D deformable objects.

#### 3.1.2. Data Acquisition

- **Motion Capture**: A millimeter-precision motion capture system (16 NOKOV Mars9H infrared cameras, 180 Hz) tracks object trajectories within a 2 m × 2 m × 2 m volume.
 - **Rigid Objects**: Instrumented with 6mm retroreflective spheres to define a body-fixed frame, recovering 6-DoF pose.
 - **Deformable Objects**: 6mm reflective adhesive markers attached to surfaces, forming triangulated surface meshes, with 3D positions recorded individually.
- **Physical Property Calibration**: Detailed calibration of all assets is provided:
 - **Dimensions, Mass, Density**: Basic measurements.
 - **Friction and Restitution**: Measured for rigid bodies using inclined-plane and collision tests (e.g., inclined air-bearing rail for restitution).
 - **Textiles**: Tensile, shear, and bending stiffness obtained from fabric tests (Style3D instruments). Effective isotropic solver parameters are derived by matching small-strain responses for different simulator constitutive models (Isaac Sim, Genesis, Newton VBD), acknowledging that these are approximations of anisotropic material response.
 - **3D Deformable Objects**: Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio calibrated using compression tests and Digital Image Correlation (DIC), fitting the initial linear region of stress-strain curves for small-deformation regimes.
- **Data Processing**: Each recording is truncated, downsampled to 30 fps, and 20 independent real-world trials are collected per task, stored with calibrated physical properties in JSON format.

#### 3.2. Physics Engines Pipeline: Simulation and Tracking

- **Engines Evaluated**: Isaac Sim (v6.0.0), Genesis (v1.12.0), and Newton (v1.3.0) on 14 representative tasks.
- **Scene Reconstruction**: Scenes are reconstructed using digital assets and initial states from motion capture.
- **Solver Configuration**:
 - **Rigid Bodies**: PhysX (Isaac Sim), native solver (Genesis), MuJoCo (Newton).
 - **Textiles**: Surface FEM (Isaac Sim), PBD (Genesis), VBD (Newton).
 - **Volumetric Deformable Bodies**: FEM (Isaac Sim), explicit and implicit MPM (Genesis), FEM (Newton).
- **Parameter Settings**: Physical parameters are set from GAUGE calibrations; other settings remain at defaults without task-specific fine-tuning.
- **Tracking Simulated Data**: 6-DoF poses for rigid bodies are directly exported. For deformable objects, the Hungarian algorithm assigns simulated vertices/particles to motion-capture marker proxies (matching error < 1 cm).

#### 3.2.2. Evaluation Metrics

- **Generalized Trajectory**:
 - **Rigid Bodies**: Position P(t) ∈ ℝ³
 - **Textiles**: Gaussian curvatures K(t) ∈ ℝ^(Nm) at marker points (angle deficit method, accounting for interior, edge, corner points).
 - **Deformable Bodies**: Areas A(t) ∈ ℝ^(Nf) of triangular faces formed by neighboring markers.
- **General Error Metrics**:
 - **Root Mean Square Error (RMSE)**: Frame-aligned error between simulated (g_sim) and mean real (g_real) trajectories.
 - **Dynamic Time Warping (DTW)**: Allows monotonic temporal alignment to measure error.
 - A baseline of within-trial RMSE and per-trial RMSE standard deviation from real data is provided for normalization.
- **Task-Specific Physical Metrics**:
 - **Newton's Cradle**:
 - **Longest Stationary Duration (LSD)**: Maximum duration of time intervals satisfying stationary conditions (position variation < 0.5 mm, velocity < 5 mm/s), indicating impulse-like motion.
 - **Momentum Transfer Efficiency (MTE)**: Ratio of outgoing to incoming ball momentum, estimated from height differences, evaluating momentum transmission efficiency.
 - **Pendulum**:
 - **Period Duration (PD)**: Time for one full oscillation, estimated via Fourier spectral analysis of the dominant frequency, assessing consistency with expected periodic motion.
 - **Energy Loss (EL)**: Ratio of final to initial mechanical energy loss, evaluating energy conservation; measured at peak oscillation positions.

#### 3.3. World Models Pipeline: Generation and Tracking

- **Models Evaluated**: Cosmos3-Nano, Cosmos3-Super-Image2Video, Wan-2.2, Wan-2.7, Seedance 2.0, and Genie 3.
- **Focus**: Primarily rigid-body scenarios, as current models struggle with 3D consistency and deformable dynamics.
- **Input**: Each model receives the same frontal initial frame (832x480 pixels, or 1300x750 for Genie 3) and a standardized text prompt describing the scene and expected motion. An ablation study is conducted with a generic "negative prompt" to constrain physically implausible behaviors for some models.
- **Motion Recovery**:
 - **Segmentation**: SAM3 (Segment Anything with Concepts) is used to segment and track the target object from generated video sequences, using text and point prompts.
 - **2D Trajectory**: Object's 2D pixel coordinates are approximated from the centroid of each frame's binary segment mask.
 - **Scale Conversion**: For objects with known dimensions and nearly constant depth, an image-to-world scale factor converts 2D pixel trajectories into 2D experimental coordinate system trajectories.

#### 3.3.2. Evaluation Metrics

GAUGE assesses physical laws from two perspectives: structural form of governing equations and accuracy of underlying physical parameters.
- **Dynamic Error (DE)**: For tasks with known external forces, DE measures consistency with Newton's second law. It quantifies the per-frame force exceedance, i.e., how much the estimated force from generated motion deviates from the maximum available physical force (e.g., maximum friction). Lower DE indicates better force-motion consistency.
- **Coefficient of Determination (R²)**: Measures how well an expected physical model (e.g., damped sinusoid for a pendulum) explains the generated trajectory. A higher R² indicates closer agreement with the expected equation form.
- **Quadratic Form Improvement (QFI)**: Complements R² by measuring the additional residual reduction obtained by adding a quadratic term (e.g., 𝑡⁴) to an expected linear relationship (e.g., 𝑠 vs 𝑡² for uniformly accelerated motion). A low QFI suggests the expected linear form is sufficient, while a high QFI indicates additional curvature or deviation from the expected simple form.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation reveals distinct performance characteristics and limitations for both physics engines and video world models.

#### 4.1. Physics Engines

- **No Uniformly Faithful Engine**: No single physics engine consistently outperforms others across all rigid, textile, and volumetric deformable regimes. Performance is strongly domain-dependent.
- **Rigid-Body Dynamics**:
 - Isaac Sim showed lowest errors for slope contact, nonsmooth contact, and turntable motion, with notably low normalized RMSE and DTW for turntable (0.17 and 0.61).
 - Genesis performed best on the slope-slider task (0.58 RMSE, 0.69 DTW). Newton was less accurate in friction-dominated settings.
 - More demanding rigid-body events showed larger discrepancies. Bouncing ball errors were significantly higher (e.g., 15.63 times baseline RMSE for Isaac Sim).
 - Newton's cradle results were poor: Isaac Sim and Newton produced zero longest stationary duration (LSD) and low momentum transfer efficiencies (MTE of 0.20 and 0.26), while Genesis failed to produce a valid rollout. This indicates difficulty with impulsive contact.
 - Pendulum frequency was reproduced reasonably (normalized periods around 1.10), but all engines accumulated energy errors over longer rollouts (raw energy loss from -0.041 to 0.034), indicating issues with long-horizon energy conservation.
- **Textile and Deformable Body Dynamics**:
 - **Textiles**: Performance varied significantly. For textile stretching, Isaac Sim and Newton achieved normalized errors close to or below one. However, for textile bending, RMSE and DTW increased substantially (e.g., Isaac Sim RMSE 7.94, DTW 11.78). Textile flinging saw the largest solver-specific failure, with Isaac Sim reaching 128.26 RMSE, while Genesis achieved 8.54. This highlights that quasi-static agreement does not predict fidelity under rapid, spatially varying deformation.
 - **Volumetric Deformable Bodies**: Genesis showed lowest errors for stretching, shearing, and twisting, while Newton performed best for bending. However, even the best simulations exhibited errors approximately one order of magnitude above real-world baselines.
- **Main Sources of Error**: The results identify dynamic contact, high-acceleration cloth motion, and three-dimensional deformation as primary areas where sim-to-real discrepancies are substantial.

#### 4.2. World Models

- **Law Form vs. Parameter Accuracy**: Satisfying the structural form of a physical law and accurately recovering its parameters are distinct capabilities. A low QFI (good fit to expected form) does not guarantee correct physical parameters.
- **Slope Sliding**: Lowest QFI varied by model and material, indicating limited consistency. Even with low QFI, inferred accelerations were often incorrect (e.g., best estimate for plastic was 0.75 m/s², far from the real 2.57 m/s²). Generated trajectories could appear linear in squared time but have substantially different slopes.
- **Bouncing Ball (Free Fall Segment)**: Cosmos3-Super-I2V with a negative prompt achieved the lowest QFI (12.50), but its inferred acceleration was only 0.088 m/s², less than 1% of gravitational acceleration. Seedance 2.0 provided the closest acceleration (1.84 m/s²), still 81% below gravity. Generated motion could resemble uniformly accelerated trajectories but encode incorrect physical scales.
- **Interaction and Oscillation**:
 - **Newton's Cradle**: Six out of ten model configurations failed to produce a valid sequence. The highest MTE achieved was 0.76 (Wan-2.2 with negative prompt), below the ideal value of approximately one, indicating incomplete momentum transfer.
 - **Pendulum**: Wan-2.2 and Genie3 achieved high R² values (0.99), indicating visually plausible oscillatory fits. However, their fitted periods were 1.93 s and 1.90 s, significantly longer than the measured 1.06 s. The closest period (1.83 s from Wan-2.7) was still about 73% longer. This demonstrates that a generated sequence can appear periodic and achieve a high fitting score while violating underlying temporal parameters and energy behavior.
- **Prompt Sensitivity**: The negative prompt did not yield consistent improvements. Its effect varied in direction and magnitude depending on the model and task, suggesting that prompt formulation can significantly influence generated physics and that evaluations should use fixed templates or report paired results.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GAUGE represents a significant advancement in the evaluation of physical fidelity for simulators and world models. Its key contributions and potential impacts include:

- **Foundational Diagnostic Tool**: By providing a measurement-grounded benchmark with 22 controlled task families across rigid, flexible, textile, and volumetric deformable objects, GAUGE lays the groundwork for developing more physically faithful simulators and world models for embodied intelligence.
- **Enhanced Diagnosability**: Unlike prior benchmarks focused on perceptual plausibility or single metrics, GAUGE enables a diagnostic assessment that reveals not only *whether* a simulator or world model deviates from real-world physics but also *which* specific physical mechanisms (e.g., collision, friction, momentum transfer, oscillation, deformation) it fails to reproduce and with what parameter inaccuracy.
- **Unified Evaluation Framework**: Its ability to evaluate both numerical physics engines and generative video world models using the same real-world experimental foundation, combined with specific metrics for each paradigm (e.g., trajectory errors for engines, law-form consistency and parameter accuracy for world models), offers a comprehensive and consistent evaluation platform.
- **Identification of Current Limitations**: The benchmark's results highlight critical weaknesses in current simulation technologies. For physics engines, it underscores challenges in accurately modeling dynamic contact, rapid textile motion, and volumetric deformation across diverse material properties. For world models, it reveals that visual plausibility does not equate to quantitative physical accuracy, demonstrating issues with recovering correct accelerations, momentum transfer, oscillation timings, and the temporal stability of inferred parameters.
- **Guidance for Future Research**: GAUGE's diagnostic capabilities directly inform future development efforts. It points to the need for:
 - **Physics Engines**: Improvements in specific solver components for challenging physical regimes.
 - **World Models**: Research into learning stronger inductive biases for physical laws, better integration of physical principles into generative models, and improved 3D understanding and consistent physical parameter recovery.
- **Expansion of Material and Physical Regimes**: The identified limitations also outline future work, such as including a broader range of materials, incorporating fluids and coupled processes, and developing advanced 3D metrics for deformable objects in world models (e.g., local strain, curvature, self-contact), alongside explicit modeling of perception uncertainty.

Overall, GAUGE provides a robust, objective, and diagnostic tool critical for advancing the physical realism of artificial intelligence systems, particularly those aimed at operating in the real world.
