# Automating Robot Failure Recovery Using Vision-Language Models With Optimized Prompts

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed a method for automating robot failure recovery by intelligently leveraging existing Vision-Language Models through optimized prompt engineering. The approach significantly improves the VLM's spatial reasoning and generalizability for robot control, achieving superior performance over a VLA model in motion-level tasks and high accuracy in detecting and analyzing task-level failures, without requiring model fine-tuning or extensive data collection.

## Problem

- Robots struggle to autonomously detect and recover from unexpected failures that occur outside their pre-defined Operational Design Domain (ODD) in uncertain real-world environments.
- Traditional failure recovery methods are often labor-intensive, lack generalizability, and require exhaustive enumeration of potential failure cases or extensive data collection for specific policies.
- Existing Vision-Language Models (VLMs), while capable of high-level reasoning, often lack the precise 3D spatial understanding needed for low-level robot control and motion-level error correction without massive dataset collection or fine-tuning.

## Method

- Leveraging powerful pre-trained ==Vision-Language Models (VLMs)== like GPT-4o as ==black-box controllers== for robot failure detection and recovery.
- Optimizing VLM inputs through sophisticated ==prompt engineering==, including incorporating key visual elements (e.g., markers) into images and using ==relative position-focused language prompts==.
- Decomposing the VLM's reasoning process by separating ==failure detection== from ==correction modules== and breaking down complex spatial and task-level queries into multiple simpler, independent steps.

## Results

- The optimized VLM (GPT-4o) significantly outperformed OpenVLA in zero-shot motion-level tasks, achieving **0.005m** 3D spatial distance in Lego Assembly (vs. **0.016m** for OpenVLA) and **65.78%** coverage in Target Reach (vs. **0%** for OpenVLA).
- Ablation studies showed that combining all optimizations yielded the best motion-level performance, improving Target Reach coverage from **0%** (original prompts) to **65.78%** and reducing pixel distance from **215** to **19**.
- For task-level failures, optimized prompts achieved high success rates in failure detection (**116/120**) and analysis (**110/120**), though recovery planning (**88/120**) remained more challenging due to issues with generating precisely executable action sequences.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimizing visual inputs by highlighting key elements and providing reference images significantly enhances VLM's ability to interpret robot states for recovery.
> - Decomposing VLM queries into smaller, focused questions (e.g., 1D movements for spatial reasoning, separate checks for task-level failures) dramatically improves the VLM's precision and interpretability.
> - Framing language prompts to focus on relative positions instead of absolute coordinates mitigates VLMs' inherent difficulty with precise 3D spatial reasoning, making them more effective for robot control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is rapidly advancing towards greater autonomy, yet a significant challenge persists: enabling robots to operate reliably beyond their pre-defined "Operational Design Domain" (ODD). Real-world environments are inherently uncertain, leading to unexpected failures that necessitate human intervention or halt operations. Traditionally, addressing these failures has involved laborious manual intervention, exhaustive enumeration of potential failure cases, and the design of specific, often brittle, recovery policies. These approaches are not scalable and lack generalizability.

The advent of **foundational models**, particularly Large Language Models (LLMs) and Vision-Language Models (VLMs), has introduced a new paradigm to robotics research. These models, trained on vast datasets, demonstrate remarkable common-sense reasoning and generalization capabilities. Early applications in robotics have seen them used for high-level task planning and failure explanations by converting multisensory observations into language summaries. However, this conversion process can introduce compounding errors, and a major limitation for direct robot control remains their struggle with precise **3D spatial reasoning** and low-level motion planning.

Significant efforts have been dedicated to overcoming this spatial reasoning gap by collecting large-scale 3D spatial reasoning training data for **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models** like PaLM-E and RT-2. While promising, these models are often closed systems or require extensive fine-tuning when applied to new scenarios, exhibiting "out-of-distribution" challenges. OpenVLA offers an open-source alternative but still notes the necessity of fine-tuning for new tasks.

This paper positions itself directly within this critical juncture. It aims to bridge the gap between the high-level reasoning prowess of VLMs and the low-level, precise control required for robot failure recovery, *without* the need for massive data collection or model fine-tuning. Instead of developing new VLA models or relying solely on their out-of-the-box performance, Chen et al. propose a novel approach: leveraging existing, powerful, pre-trained VLMs (specifically GPT-4o) as "black-box controllers" and significantly enhancing their performance through **optimized prompt engineering**.

By focusing on prompt optimization—specifically, refining visual and text inputs and decomposing the VLM's reasoning process—this work distinguishes itself from previous efforts that relied on model architecture changes, extensive training data, or fine-tuning. It challenges the assumption that new models or vast datasets are always necessary for improving VLM performance in robotics, instead demonstrating the untapped potential of smart interaction with existing models. This approach contributes to making robots more adaptable and robust by expanding their operational design domains to encompass a wide range of *unknown* failures, moving beyond the limitations of pre-programmed responses or fine-tuned policies. It represents a significant step towards more generalized and resilient robot autonomy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to enable robot systems to autonomously detect and recover from failures that occur outside their predefined Operational Design Domain (ODD). This goal is driven by the fundamental understanding that real-world environments are inherently uncertain and complex, making it impractical to pre-program responses for every conceivable failure scenario.

The specific key objectives and motivations are:

- **Address Limitations of Current Robot Autonomy:** Traditional robot autonomy struggles to handle unexpected events beyond its ODD, leading to system halts or the need for costly human intervention. Existing failure recovery methods (e.g., probability inference, scene graphs, neural-symbolic planners, learning-based policies) are often limited in scope, lack generalizability across diverse failure types, and are labor-intensive due to the requirement of exhaustive enumeration of failure cases or extensive data collection for specific recovery policies.
- **Leverage Foundational Models' Generalization Capabilities:** Vision-Language Models (VLMs) demonstrate remarkable common-sense generalization and reasoning. The authors are motivated to harness these capabilities for robot failure recovery, recognizing their potential to operate within a "broader, potentially unbounded ODD."
- **Overcome VLM Spatial Reasoning Deficiencies for Robot Control:** Despite their high-level reasoning, VLMs frequently struggle with the precise 3D spatial reasoning necessary for low-level robot control and motion-level error recovery. This limitation prevents their direct application as black-box controllers for many robotic tasks. The paper's core objective is to investigate and mitigate this weakness.
- **Develop a Generalizable and Resource-Efficient Recovery Method:** The authors aim to develop a method that eliminates the need to design specific algorithms for every failure, collect failure recovery demonstrations, or fine-tune VLA models. They seek a simpler, more efficient approach that treats VLMs as black-box controllers capable of translating high-dimensional image observations and natural language into executable recovery actions and plans.
- **Enhance VLM Performance through Prompt Optimization:** The central objective is to demonstrate that optimizing VLM "control parameters" (i.e., image and language prompts) and refining the control reasoning process can significantly enhance their spatial reasoning and failure recovery capabilities. This involves identifying key visual elements, highlighting them in text prompts, and decomposing complex reasoning tasks.
- **Improve Interpretability of VLM Decisions:** By decomposing the reasoning process for failure detection and control generation, the authors aim to make the VLM's internal decision-making process more interpretable to humans. This allows for better understanding of why a failure occurred and how a recovery plan was generated, moving beyond simply receiving a structured action.

In essence, the motivation stems from a desire to make robots more resilient and autonomous in unstructured, uncertain environments by intelligently interfacing with powerful, pre-trained VLMs, thus reducing development effort and increasing adaptability.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this research revolves around using a pre-trained Vision-Language Model (VLM) as a black-box controller for robot failure detection and recovery. The key innovation lies not in developing new VLM architectures, but in optimizing the inputs to and reasoning process of existing VLMs through sophisticated prompt engineering. The approach contrasts with traditional methods that rely on specific feature extraction algorithms or extensive policy training.

The proposed pipeline, illustrated in Figure 2 of the paper, integrates the VLM into the robot's control loop. The "Normal Robot Control" (grey box) represents the standard operation. When a failure occurs, the "VLM for Robot Failure Recovery" (blue outer loop) takes over. This VLM is conceptually divided into a "Failure Detection" module and a "Failure Correction Controller or Planner." The critical element is the "Optimized Prompts" (red box), which are human-designed to maximize the accuracy of the VLM's failure detection and recovery.

The methodology is guided by three main principles for optimizing VLM inputs and reasoning:

1. **Visual Prompt Input Optimization - Key Visual Elements on Image Prompt Aligned with Language Prompt:**
 - **Motion-level:** The approach emphasizes making images more "VLM-friendly" by incorporating prominent, easily detectable visual elements. This includes adding physical markers (e.g., colorful tape on the gripper) or using object detectors (e.g., Detic for bounding boxes) to highlight the robot's current state and the target area. These key visual elements are then explicitly referenced in the accompanying language prompt.
 - **Task-level:** For tasks where specific visual elements might not be clear indicators of failure, an "ideal reference image" ($I_{ref}$) is provided to the VLM. This allows the VLM to compare the current state with a successful state and identify deviations.

2. **Language Prompt Input Optimization - Relative Position-Focused Language Prompt at Motion-Level Recovery:**
 - Recognizing VLMs' difficulty with precise 3D world coordinates, the language prompts are crafted to focus on the *relative positions* of the previously defined key visual elements. For example, instead of asking for absolute coordinates, the VLM is queried about "the blue cube's position relative to the red square."
 - This transforms continuous 3D delta movements into a set of discrete, relative actions (e.g., `a_up`, `a_down`, `a_left`, `a_right`, `a_forward`, `a_backward`). Each discrete action is then scaled by a user-defined constant `c` based on the task scenario. This simplifies the VLM's output space and aligns better with its qualitative spatial reasoning.

3. **VLM Reasoning Optimization - Decomposition of VLM Query:**
 - This is a crucial aspect for both accuracy and interpretability, analogous to breaking down a complex problem into smaller, manageable sub-problems.
 - **Separation of Failure Detector and Correction Modules:** To enhance interpretability, the VLM first acts as a "Failure Detector," reasoning about the situation and generating a *textual analysis* of the failure. Only after this analytical step does the "VLM Correction Controller or Planner" summarize and generate the executable recovery actions or plans. This provides human insight into the VLM's reasoning.
 - **Decomposition of Spatial Reasoning (Motion-level):** Instead of asking for a full 3D movement in a single query, the problem is broken down into independent 1D movements (e.g., querying for horizontal movement, then vertical, then forward/backward). This multi-query approach provides the VLM with more resources and focus for each directional decision, improving spatial reasoning accuracy.
 - **Decomposition of Failure Detection (Task-level):** For complex task failures, a single broad query is replaced by multiple fine-grained queries based on human-defined correctness criteria. For example, a "pick up Lego" task failure might be decomposed into separate queries: "Is the block picked up?", "Is it the correct color?", and "Is it a single unit?". This allows for more precise failure identification.

**Implementation Details:** The study utilized **GPT-4o**, a powerful multi-modal VLM, in a zero-shot prompting configuration (i.e., no in-context examples were provided). Experiments were conducted on a 7-DoF Kinova robot for motion-level tasks and in a simulated Lego assembly environment for task-level failures.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling results demonstrating the effectiveness of prompt optimization for robot failure recovery at both motion and task levels.

**A. Motion-level Failure Recovery Experiments:**

- **Setup:** Evaluated GPT-4o with optimized prompts against an open-source Vision-Language-Action (VLA) model, OpenVLA, in a zero-shot setting on a 7-DoF Kinova robot. Four tasks were used: Lego Assembly (position alignment), Rotation (gripper rotation), Target Reach (object placement into a goal area), and Object Grasp (1D, 2D, 3D grasping scenarios). Performance was measured by distance metrics (3D spatial distance, angular difference, pixel distance), coverage, and success rates.
- **Comparison with OpenVLA (Table I):**
 - **Superior Performance of Optimized VLMs:** The approach using GPT-4o with optimized prompts significantly outperformed OpenVLA across all tested tasks. For instance, in Lego Assembly, our method achieved a 3D spatial distance of 0.005m compared to OpenVLA's 0.016m. For Rotation, it achieved 7.4° angular difference versus OpenVLA's 24.3°. In Target Reach, our method achieved 65.78% coverage, while OpenVLA resulted in 0%.
 - **Out-of-Distribution Robustness:** OpenVLA performed relatively better only in grasping tasks, likely because these closely resembled its training settings. This highlights OpenVLA's struggle with out-of-distribution problems, confirming its authors' observation that fine-tuning is often required for new scenarios. In stark contrast, the optimized VLM approach showed strong generalization without any fine-tuning.
 - **Grasping Success Rates:** For Object Grasping, optimized GPT-4o achieved 10/10 (1D), 8/10 (2D), and 4/10 (3D) success rates, compared to OpenVLA's 4/10 (1D), 6/10 (2D), and 0/10 (3D). While 3D grasping remains challenging for both, the optimized VLM significantly improved performance.
- **Contribution of Each Optimization (Table II - Target Reach Task):**
 - This ablation study clearly demonstrated the cumulative benefits of the proposed optimizations.
 - **Original Prompts:** Baseline performance was poor (0% coverage, 215 pixel distance).
 - **Relative Position-Focused Language:** Introducing relative position descriptions improved coverage to 4.99% and reduced pixel distance to 202. This indicated that the VLM could better understand the directional intent.
 - **+ Decomposition of Query:** Adding reasoning decomposition (breaking 3D movements into 1D components) dramatically improved performance, with coverage surging to 31.42% and pixel distance dropping to 34. This shows that giving the VLM more focused queries for each dimension significantly aids spatial reasoning.
 - **+ Full Optimizations (Key Visual Elements):** Incorporating prominent key visual elements into both image and text prompts led to the best performance: 65.78% coverage and 19 pixel distance. This confirms that aligning visual and textual cues optimally guides the VLM.
- **Observations:** Despite significant improvements, the authors noted that GPT-4o occasionally struggled with perfect spatial reasoning or identifying optimal stop conditions, leading to slight overshoots or sub-optimal final steps. Additionally, collision avoidance was not yet integrated, which could lead to failures in complex 3D scenarios.

**B. Task-level Failure Recovery Experiments:**

- **Setup:** Evaluated GPT-4o on Lego assembly (pick and place tasks) with eight diverse failure scenarios (four for pick, four for place). Performance was assessed on three metrics: Failure Detection (identifying failure), Failure Analysis (identifying the correct reason), and Recovery Planning (generating a viable plan using pre-defined robot skills).
- **Results (Table III):**
 - **Overall Improvement:** Optimized prompts achieved higher success rates across all three metrics compared to original prompts. Optimized prompts: 116/120 for detection, 110/120 for analysis, and 88/120 for planning (out of a total 120 attempts for 8 failure types x 3 sets x 5 seeds). This represents an overall 6.4% improvement compared to the original prompts (109/120 D, 103/120 A, 79/120 P).
 - **High Detection and Analysis Success:** The VLM exhibited very high success rates in detecting failures (116/120) and analyzing their causes (110/120), highlighting its strong common-sense reasoning from visual and linguistic inputs.
 - **Challenges in Subtle Visual Failures:** Performance in analysis was slightly lower for visually subtle failures, such as picking two blocks of the same color (Pick Failure 2) or distinguishing between picking multiple blocks including wrong colors (Pick Failure 4). Placing a block in the wrong position (Place Failure 7) was also challenging for analysis due to visual similarity to the correct state.
 - **Lower Success in Recovery Planning:** Recovery planning showed the lowest success rate (88/120). This was primarily due to the VLM generating plans with irrelevant skill actions, omitting necessary pre-skill actions (e.g., placing a block when the gripper is empty), or generating plans that exceeded the immediate task scope. This suggests that while VLMs are good at diagnosing and suggesting high-level plans, translating them into perfectly executable sequences respecting robot preconditions remains a challenge.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a significant step forward in enhancing robot autonomy and robustness, particularly in handling unexpected failures. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Pioneering Prompt Optimization for Robot Control:** The work introduces a novel paradigm of using sophisticated prompt engineering to enhance the spatial reasoning and control capabilities of pre-trained Vision-Language Models for robot failure recovery. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first method to explicitly propose and evaluate such optimization for this specific application, distinguishing it from approaches focused on model architecture or extensive fine-tuning.
- **Expanding Operational Design Domains (ODDs):** By enabling VLMs to function effectively as black-box controllers for unknown failures, the research directly contributes to expanding the ODDs of robotic systems. This reduces the dependency on pre-programmed solutions or laborious, scenario-specific recovery policies, making robots more adaptable to the inherent uncertainties of real-world environments.
- **Generalizability and Resource Efficiency:** A major impact is the demonstration that powerful, general-purpose VLMs like GPT-4o can be effectively utilized for complex robotic tasks (like precise motion-level correction and task-level recovery) without requiring extensive fine-tuning or collecting vast robot-specific demonstration datasets. This significantly lowers the barrier to deploying robust autonomous systems, making advanced robotics more accessible and practical.
- **Enhanced Interpretability of Robot Failures:** The methodology's emphasis on decomposing VLM queries to first generate textual failure analyses before proposing actions improves the interpretability of robot failures. This allows human operators to understand the VLM's reasoning, fostering trust and enabling more effective human-robot collaboration in troubleshooting.
- **Practical Implications for Industry:** The ability to automate failure recovery, especially for unknown failure modes, has profound implications for industrial applications. In manufacturing, logistics, and service robotics, unexpected stoppages due to failures can lead to significant economic losses. This research offers a pathway to more resilient and continuously operating robotic systems, increasing efficiency and reducing human intervention.
- **Future Research Directions:** The findings also highlight areas for future research. While prompt optimization is powerful, further improvements are needed in VLM's precise spatial reasoning (e.g., integrating true 3D scene understanding, depth information), collision avoidance during recovery movements, and more robust action planning that strictly adheres to robot preconditions and logical sequences. The challenge in recovery planning at the task level suggests a need for better grounding of VLM-generated plans into executable, constrained robot actions.

In conclusion, this paper successfully demonstrates that intelligent interaction with existing foundational models, through optimized prompting and reasoning decomposition, can unlock significant capabilities for robot failure recovery. It pushes the boundaries of how general-purpose AI models can be applied to address complex, real-world challenges in robotics, paving the way for more autonomous, robust, and interpretable robotic systems.
