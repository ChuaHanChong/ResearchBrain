# Understanding Reasoning from Pretraining to Post-Training

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from New York University and collaborators established a quantitative framework to understand the interplay between large language model pretraining and reinforcement learning in reasoning tasks, using a controlled chess environment. They developed a joint pretraining-RL scaling law, revealing that lower pretraining loss predicts higher post-RL performance and that optimal RL compute share increases with total compute, while also showing RL reshapes policies through both ground-truth amplification and wrong-mode amplification.

## Problem

- A quantitative understanding of how pretraining choices (model size, data) influence subsequent reinforcement learning (RL) performance and its scaling behavior was largely unexplored.
- The precise mechanistic impact of RL on a pretrained policy was debated, with conflicting views on whether RL primarily sharpens existing reasoning patterns or enables entirely new skill discovery.
- Investigating these questions in large-scale natural language LLMs is challenging due to prohibitive computational costs, difficulty in attributing behaviors in uncontrolled pretraining corpora, and the opaque nature of reasoning processes.

## Method

- The study employed a ==controlled chess testbed== to mirror the standard LLM training pipeline (==pretraining==, ==SFT==, ==RL==), enabling systematic experimentation and detailed analysis at an affordable scale.
- A ==three-stage training pipeline== was used: ==pretraining== on human chess games, ==supervised fine-tuning (SFT)== on Lichess puzzles with synthetic ==chain-of-thought (CoT)== traces, and ==reinforcement learning (RL)== using ==Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)==.
- A **joint pretraining-RL scaling law** was developed to predict post-RL performance and the rate of RL improvement based on pretraining properties, such as validation loss and token count.

## Results

- Lower pretraining loss consistently correlated with higher post-RL `pass@1` performance, and the local slope of the RL reward curve (improvement rate) showed a positive linear correlation with the logarithm of pretraining token count.
- Pareto frontier analysis demonstrated that for a fixed model size, the optimal RL compute ratio increased from **5%** to **32%** as the total compute budget grew for a **20M** parameter model.
- Analysis of Chain-of-Thought reasoning showed that RL primarily expanded search breadth and improved candidate generation and selection within the CoT, with limited improvement in deep, long-horizon search capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A joint pretraining-RL scaling law established that lower pretraining validation loss strongly predicts higher post-RL performance, and the rate of RL improvement is primarily shaped by the quantity of pretraining data exposure.
> - The optimal proportion of compute allocated to RL increases with the total computational budget, suggesting a greater investment in RL becomes more beneficial in higher-compute regimes for single-best answer performance (pass@1).
> - RL reshapes the policy in state-dependent ways, including ground-truth amplification on easier tasks, tail discovery on harder tasks, and concurrently, wrong-mode amplification where incorrect moves are reinforced, rather than simply uniformly sharpening the SFT policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Context

The training pipeline for modern large language models (LLMs) typically involves large-scale pretraining followed by post-training, which often includes supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and reinforcement learning (RL) with verifiable rewards. Within this paradigm, two primary perspectives have emerged regarding the allocation of computational resources: one emphasizes the development of strong base models through extensive pretraining (scaling model size and data), and the other focuses on refining capabilities through environmental interaction and outcome-based feedback via RL.

While scaling laws have been well-established for pretraining (e.g., relating model size, data, and compute to performance), and post-training RL scaling has been studied independently, a quantitative understanding of how pretraining choices specifically influence subsequent RL performance and scaling behavior has been largely unexplored. Furthermore, the precise mechanistic impact of RL on a pretrained policy remains a subject of debate, with some research suggesting RL primarily sharpens existing reasoning patterns, while others propose it enables the discovery or composition of entirely new skills.

Direct investigation of these questions in large-scale natural language LLMs is challenging due to several factors: the prohibitive computational cost of systematic sweeps across both pretraining and RL stages, the difficulty in attributing behaviors in vast and uncontrolled pretraining corpora, and the often opaque nature of reasoning processes in LLMs, which typically provide only final-answer correctness. This research addresses these gaps by introducing a controlled experimental environment to systematically study the pretraining-to-post-training pipeline.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research were to quantitatively characterize the interface between pretraining and reinforcement learning (RL) in reasoning tasks, and to understand the mechanistic changes RL induces in a pretrained policy. Specifically, the authors sought to answer two main questions:

1. **How do pretraining choices (such as model size and pretraining data quantity) influence the effectiveness and returns of subsequent RL computation?** This question is motivated by the need to optimize compute allocation across the entire LLM training pipeline, addressing whether a fixed computational budget should be preferentially invested in improving the pretrained model or in further optimizing it with RL. Existing work has scaled pretraining and RL separately, but a joint scaling law or quantitative characterization of their interaction was lacking.
2. **What specific changes does RL induce in the model's policy, particularly in its reasoning capabilities?** This objective aims to resolve conflicting views in prior literature. Some studies suggest RL primarily sharpens existing reasoning patterns, while others indicate it can foster entirely new skills. Understanding this mechanism has direct implications for compute allocation, guiding decisions on whether to focus more on pretraining for foundational capabilities or on RL for emergent ones.

To overcome the challenges associated with studying these questions in frontier-scale LLMs (e.g., immense computational costs, uncontrolled data, and opaque evaluation), the authors were motivated to use chess as a controlled testbed. Chess offers a compact, explicit action space, allows for exact verification of move quality, provides plentiful and controllable human play data, and enables systematic compute sweeps at an affordable scale, all while mirroring the standard LLM training pipeline. The ultimate motivation was to provide a quantitative account of the pretraining-to-RL interface and a practical framework for reasoning about compute allocation across these critical training stages.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a synthetic framework using chess to mirror the standard LLM training pipeline, enabling systematic experimentation and detailed analysis.

#### 3.1. Controlled Testbed: Chess

Chess was selected as a controlled domain due to its explicit action space, verifiable move quality (via game outcomes or strong engines), and availability of controllable human play data. This environment allowed for varying pretraining data (amount, quality, composition) and performing extensive compute sweeps across different stages. The models were designed not to achieve state-of-the-art chess performance, but to serve as a tractable setup for isolating pretraining-RL interactions.

#### 3.2. Training Pipeline

The training pipeline followed a three-stage approach: pretraining, supervised fine-tuning (SFT), and reinforcement learning (RL).

#### 3.2.1. Chess Representation

Each chess game was represented as an alternating sequence of player moves, serialized into four tokens: `<piece>`, `<source>`, `<destination>`, and `<flag>` (for special cases like promotion). The vocabulary size was 81.

#### 3.2.2. Pretraining

Models were pretrained on a large corpus of human chess games (Blitz and Rapid games from Lichess in 2022), totaling 54 billion tokens. This corpus was filtered for quality and balanced across player Elo ratings (800-3000). The pretraining objective was standard next-token prediction for an autoregressive policy over tokenized games. Models ranged in size from 5 million to 1 billion parameters.

#### 3.2.3. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

For post-training, models were fine-tuned on 156,000 quality-filtered Lichess puzzles.
- **Verifiable Chess Puzzle Environment:** Each puzzle presented an initial board state and a ground-truth solution line. The model, acting as the solver, proposed moves. A mismatch with the ground truth terminated the episode, while a match led to an opponent response and the next solver state. Success required selecting the correct move at every step of the solution line.
- **Synthetic Reasoning Traces (Chain-of-Thought, CoT):** To elicit reasoning in context, models were trained to produce a reasoning trace before committing to a move. This trace was synthesized by sampling multiple game continuations from a pretrained proposal policy (a 200M model), merging them into a prefix tree, and serializing the tree in depth-first order using `<T>` and `</T>` tags and `<sep>` tokens. The model was then trained to commit to the best solution continuation (verified by Stockfish) from this trace. Opponent-move tokens in the solution were masked from the SFT loss.

#### 3.2.4. Reinforcement Learning (RL)

Starting from the SFT policy, models were optimized on the puzzle environment.
- **Reward Function:** A binary outcome reward was used, granting 1 only if every executed move in the solution line matched the ground truth, and 0 otherwise. A single mistake resulted in no reward.
- **Algorithm:** Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) was employed, with hyperparameters including a learning rate of 10^-5, a KL coefficient of 0.001, and a clip ratio of 0.2.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

The study involved sweeping across 36 pretraining-RL combinations, covering 10 model sizes (5M to 1B parameters) and 11 pretraining compute budgets (6.5 x 10^16 to 6.5 x 10^19 FLOPs). Evaluation was performed on a curated benchmark of 1,480 tactical puzzles (B1-B5 difficulty), distinct from training data, measuring `pass@1` and `pass@k` metrics.

#### 3.4. Compute Estimation

Training compute was estimated in FLOPs:
- **Pretraining:** $C_{pt} = 6NT_{pt}$
- **SFT:** $C_{sft} = 6NE_{sft}n_{sft}L_{sft}$ (for $n_{sft}$ examples of length $L_{sft}$ over $E_{sft}$ epochs)
- **RL:** $C_{rl} = 10NT_{rollout}$ (sum of policy rollout generation, reference-model log-probability evaluation, and policy optimization).

#### 3.5. Transfer Case Study: Math

To assess the generalizability of findings, a 1-billion-parameter OLMo-2 model was pretrained on a 200B-token mixed math corpus (70% Nemotron-CC-Math-v1, 30% Dolma3). This model was evaluated at 14 checkpoints (10B to 200B tokens). It then underwent SFT on NuminaMath-CoT, followed by RL on a mix of GSM8K, MATH, and DeepScaler problems. Evaluation used `pass@1` on held-out validation sets.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the interplay of pretraining and RL, the mechanisms of policy change, and the generalizability of these observations.

#### 4.1. Joint Pretraining–RL Scaling Law: Pre-RL Analysis

Pretraining performance was analyzed across varying compute budgets and model sizes. IsoFLOP curves demonstrated that an optimal parameter-token allocation exists, with chess pretraining favoring smaller models trained on more tokens compared to natural language LLMs in the studied compute range. Downstream puzzle benchmark performance (pass@1 and pass@16) consistently improved with additional pretraining within each model size. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) showed that models trained with synthetic reasoning traces achieved substantial improvements across all `pass@k` metrics, unlike SFT without reasoning traces, which primarily improved `pass@1`. Crucially, stronger pretrained checkpoints consistently led to higher post-SFT performance.

#### 4.1.2. Compute Allocation Tradeoff

Across 36 pretraining-RL combinations, RL consistently improved `pass@1` performance. The Pareto frontier analysis for `pass@1` indicated that for a fixed model size, the optimal proportion of compute allocated to RL increased as the total compute budget grew. For example, for a 20M model, the RL compute ratio on the frontier increased from 5% to 32%, suggesting that RL becomes more beneficial in higher-compute regimes after sufficient pretraining. In contrast, `pass@16` improvements from RL were less pronounced, often remaining flat or even degrading slightly, suggesting that for broader sampling diversity, additional pretraining might be more effective than more RL.

#### 4.1.3. Prediction of RL Scaling Behavior

The study established a quantitative scaling law connecting pretraining properties to RL outcomes.
- **Post-RL Performance Level:** The post-RL `pass@1` performance at a given RL compute level ($R_{ref}^{N,T}$) was strongly predicted by the pretraining validation loss ($L_{pt}$). Lower pretraining loss consistently correlated with higher post-RL performance, and this relationship became increasingly monotonic at higher RL compute levels.
- **RL Improvement Rate:** The local slope ($B_{N,T}$) of the RL reward curve (measuring performance gain per decade of RL compute) showed a positive linear correlation with the logarithm of pretraining token count ($\log_{10} T$), with a weaker positive correction from the logarithm of model size ($\log_{10} N$). This indicated that the rate of RL improvement is primarily shaped by the quantity of pretraining data exposure.
- **Joint Scaling Law:** The proposed law is: $R(C_{RL}, N, T) = f(L_{pt}(N, T)) + g(N, T)(\log_{10} C_{RL} - \log_{10} C_{ref})$. This law suggests that pretraining affects RL in two ways: it predicts the ultimate performance level achievable with RL, and it influences how efficiently RL improves performance.

#### 4.1.4. Extrapolated Compute-Optimal Frontier

By combining the joint pretraining-RL scaling law with a Chinchilla-style pretraining loss prediction, the authors extrapolated the compute-optimal frontier across various hypothetical model sizes and total compute budgets. The results indicated that the compute-optimal RL share increased with total compute, while the corresponding pretraining token allocation did not systematically deviate from Chinchilla scaling. This reinforced the idea that as overall computational resources grow, a larger proportion should be allocated to RL.

#### 4.2. Mechanism of RL Policy Change

The research analyzed how RL reshapes the policy, moving beyond a simple sharpening effect.

#### 4.2.1. Reshaping of Move Policy

RL was found to reshape the move policy in state-dependent ways, rather than merely uniformly sharpening the SFT policy. Policy changes were categorized into:
- **Ground-truth amplification:** On easier puzzles (B1-B2), RL primarily reinforced correct moves that the SFT policy already preferred and ranked highly.
- **Tail discovery:** On harder puzzles (B3-B5), RL sometimes promoted correct moves from the low-probability tail of the SFT policy distribution into the top-k set.
- **Wrong-mode amplification:** Concurrently, on harder puzzles, RL was also observed to reinforce incorrect moves that were initially preferred by the SFT policy, while the ground-truth move remained outside the top-k set.
This heterogeneous redistribution of probability mass helps explain why RL improves `pass@1` (the single best move) but does not consistently improve `pass@k` (diversity of good moves), as strengthening incorrect modes can counteract gains from discovering new correct moves.

#### 4.2.2. Dynamics of Chain-of-Thought Reasoning

Analysis of reasoning traces revealed that:
- **Search Shape:** Models primarily expanded search breadth rather than depth during RL training. The width-to-depth ratio and branching factor of the generated prefix trees increased, while the maximum search depth remained relatively flat.
- **Move Quality:** The quality of proposed moves within the CoT improved for both the model's own moves and its predicted opponent responses.
- **Ground-Truth Coverage:** Models became more likely to surface the ground-truth move within their generated CoT and to commit to the best candidate they had considered.
- **Search Behavior:** Generated search traces became less aligned with strict depth-first search (DFS) order, indicating more revisits to previously considered lines.
Despite these improvements, models continued to struggle with recovering continuations requiring more than five moves, suggesting that RL primarily enhances candidate generation and selection rather than significantly improving long-horizon search capabilities.

#### 4.3. Transfer to Text: Math Domain

A qualitative case study involving a 1B-parameter OLMo-2 language model pretrained on a math-domain corpus showed similar patterns to the chess findings. Across checkpoints with varying pretraining token counts (10B to 200B tokens), higher post-RL performance at a fixed RL compute level was consistently predicted by lower pretraining loss. Additionally, the slope of the RL reward curves improved approximately linearly with the logarithm of pretraining tokens. This suggests that the identified scaling structure and interaction dynamics between pretraining and RL may generalize beyond the chess domain to natural language reasoning tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a quantitative framework and empirical evidence for understanding the complex relationship between pretraining and reinforcement learning in the context of reasoning tasks. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

1. **Compute Allocation Guidance:** The established joint pretraining–RL scaling law offers a practical method for reasoning about compute allocation across pretraining, SFT, and RL stages. The finding that the optimal RL compute share increases with the total budget implies that as LLM training scales, a proportionally larger investment in RL may yield better returns for `pass@1` performance. This can inform strategic decisions for future LLM development.
2. **Mechanistic Understanding of RL:** By demonstrating that RL is not simply a uniform sharpening process but rather involves complex, difficulty-dependent policy reshaping (including both "tail discovery" and "wrong-mode amplification"), the study refines the mechanistic understanding of how RL impacts LLM reasoning. This challenges simpler interpretations and highlights the nuanced effects of RL.
3. **Improving RL Effectiveness:** The identification of "wrong-mode amplification" on harder puzzles suggests a critical area for improvement in RL algorithms. Developing methods to mitigate the reinforcement of incorrect modes, perhaps by expanding the support of correct solutions rather than merely sharpening the existing policy, could lead to more robust RL fine-tuning and better performance beyond `pass@1`.
4. **Structured Reasoning Insights:** The analysis of chain-of-thought (CoT) dynamics reveals that current RL primarily improves search breadth and candidate selection over deep, long-horizon search. This insight can guide the design of future SFT data, prompting strategies, or architectural modifications to encourage more systematic and deeper reasoning.
5. **Generalizability of Scaling Laws:** The qualitative transfer of observed patterns to a math-domain language model suggests that the fundamental scaling structure connecting pretraining properties to RL effectiveness may extend to natural language tasks, enhancing the generalizability of the findings beyond the controlled chess environment.
6. **Controlled Testbed for Future Research:** The chess testbed itself provides a valuable, controlled environment for future research on various aspects of LLM development, including synthetic data generation, self-play, transcendence phenomena, and weak-to-strong generalization, allowing for systematic investigation of complex training dynamics at a manageable scale.

In summary, this work provides a foundational quantitative account of the pretraining-to-RL interface, offering both practical guidance for compute allocation and deeper mechanistic insights into how reasoning capabilities evolve throughout the LLM training pipeline.
