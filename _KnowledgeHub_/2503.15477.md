# What Makes a Reward Model a Good Teacher? An Optimization Perspective

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Princeton University analytically and empirically investigated reward model properties for efficient large language model (LLM) alignment through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF). They demonstrated that while accuracy is important, high reward variance – the model's ability to differentiate between probable outputs – is crucial for rapid policy optimization, and a reward model's effectiveness is contingent on the specific language model it guides.

## Problem

- Empirical observations indicated that reward models (RMs) with higher ranking accuracy do not consistently lead to improved downstream language model performance after RLHF.
- The prevailing method for evaluating reward models focuses primarily on accuracy, which is insufficient to capture how well an RM facilitates efficient policy learning within an optimization context.
- A gap existed in understanding what characteristics, beyond mere ranking accuracy, contribute to an effective reward model in an optimization-driven RLHF pipeline.

## Method

- Developed a theoretical framework grounded in an optimization perspective, analyzing the time required for reward maximization during policy gradient optimization, treating it as gradient flow.
- Empirically validated theoretical findings using large language models (Pythia, Llama-3.2) by training and evaluating custom reward models with varying on-policy data mixes, and public RMs, on a simulated ground truth reward.
- Defined and analyzed key properties of reward models, including accuracy and ==reward variance==, and investigated their impact on the rate of policy learning and reward maximization.

## Results

- Theoretically proved that low reward variance implies an extremely slow increase in both proxy and ground truth rewards during policy gradient training (Theorem 1).
- Demonstrated theoretically that a perfectly accurate reward model can lead to arbitrarily slower ground truth reward maximization compared to a substantially less accurate but higher-variance model (Theorem 2).
- Empirical experiments showed a strong positive correlation between reward variance and the rate of increase in both proxy and ground truth rewards across various LLMs and RMs, and confirmed that the 'best' RM varies by initial policy.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Low reward variance within the reward model, even if perfectly accurate, leads to vanishing gradients and a flat objective landscape, causing arbitrarily slow policy optimization.
> - Reward model effectiveness is not universal but is highly dependent on the specific language model (initial policy) it is guiding, challenging the concept of universal RM benchmarks.
> - While accuracy is important for mitigating issues like reward hacking, it is insufficient for efficient RLHF optimization; sufficient reward variance is a necessary, complementary property.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of large language model (LLM) alignment, particularly through Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), is a prominent area of artificial intelligence research. RLHF aims to train LLMs to exhibit desirable behaviors (e.g., helpfulness, harmlessness) that are difficult to specify explicitly. The standard RLHF pipeline involves two main steps: first, training a reward model (RM) using human (or AI) preference data, and second, optimizing the language model (policy) to maximize the learned reward using policy gradient methods such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

The success of RLHF critically depends on the quality of the reward model. However, the prevailing method for evaluating reward models focuses primarily on accuracy, which measures the RM's ability to correctly rank pairs of outputs according to a ground truth preference. While accuracy is a natural metric, quantifying how well the proxy reward aligns with the true preferences, empirical observations have suggested that more accurate reward models do not consistently lead to improved language model performance after RLHF. This disconnect highlights a gap in the understanding of what characteristics, beyond mere ranking accuracy, contribute to an effective reward model in an optimization context. The present work addresses this gap by investigating reward model quality from an optimization perspective, seeking to identify properties that facilitate efficient policy learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to analytically and empirically investigate the properties that render a reward model an effective "teacher" for language models undergoing RLHF, moving beyond the sole reliance on accuracy as an evaluation metric.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several observations and theoretical considerations:

- **Empirical Discrepancy**: Prior empirical studies have indicated that reward models with higher ranking accuracy do not always lead to superior downstream language model performance after RLHF. This suggests that accuracy alone may not capture all critical aspects of a good reward model.
- **Optimization Challenges**: The RLHF process involves optimizing a language model (policy) based on the reward model. Optimization efficiency and the characteristics of the objective landscape are crucial for effective policy learning. The authors hypothesize that certain properties of the reward model might impact the landscape, thereby affecting the optimization rate.
- **Reward Hacking and Instability**: While accuracy is desirable for mitigating issues like reward hacking (where the policy exploits flaws in the RM to maximize proxy reward without increasing true utility), it does not guarantee efficient exploration or optimization. The research aims to understand why accurate models might still underperform in terms of optimization speed.
- **Policy-Reward Model Interaction**: Current reward model benchmarks often evaluate RMs in isolation or based on generalized accuracy metrics. The authors hypothesize that the effectiveness of a reward model might be context-dependent, specifically influenced by the characteristics of the language model it is guiding.
- **Establishing Fundamental Limitations**: By exploring the interplay between reward model properties and optimization dynamics, the authors aim to formalize fundamental limitations of evaluating reward models solely based on accuracy or independently of the language model they are meant to align.

In essence, the research is motivated by a need for a more comprehensive understanding of reward model quality that incorporates their role within the iterative optimization process of RLHF, specifically focusing on how they shape the objective function and influence the efficiency of policy gradient methods.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a dual approach, combining theoretical analysis from an optimization perspective with empirical validation using large language models.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Framework

The theoretical analysis is grounded in an optimization perspective of the RLHF objective.

- **Optimization Paradigm**: The core idea is to analyze the time it takes for the expected ground truth reward to increase by a desired amount during policy gradient optimization of the RLHF objective. Shorter times indicate better teaching.
- **Gradient Flow**: The analysis considers policy gradient in the small learning rate limit, approximating the optimization process as gradient flow. This simplification allows for analytical tractability of the objective function's dynamics.
- **Policy Parameterization**:
 - **General Autoregressive Policies**: Initial theoretical results (e.g., on the impact of low reward variance) are derived for general autoregressive language models, which generate tokens sequentially.
 - **Tabular Policies**: For proving separations in optimization time between different reward models (e.g., more accurate vs. less accurate), the analysis transitions to tabular policies. This simplification assigns a trainable logit to each possible output sequence, which is a common approach in theoretical RL to manage the non-concavity of the objective.
- **Key Concepts and Definitions**:
 - **Reward Model Accuracy (Definition 1)**: Measures the proportion of output pairs that the reward model ranks correctly according to a ground truth reward. The analysis considers accuracy over the policy gradient training set and supports arbitrary output pair distributions.
 - **Reward Variance (Definition 2)**: Measures the variance of rewards assigned by the reward model to outputs sampled from a given policy for a specific prompt. It quantifies how well the reward model differentiates between probable outputs. The authors clarify that this is distinct from gradient estimate variance.
 - **KL Regularization**: The standard RLHF objective includes a KL divergence term to prevent the policy from deviating too much from an initial reference policy. The theoretical analysis incorporates this regularization.
- **Core Theoretical Connection**: The work builds upon a known connection between reward variance and the gradient of the RLHF objective, where low reward variance can lead to vanishing gradients, thereby creating a flat objective landscape that hinders optimization.

#### 3.2. Experimental Design

The empirical section aims to corroborate the theoretical findings using practical LLM setups.

- **Ground Truth Reward Simulation**: Due to the expense of human evaluation, a high-quality, publicly available reward model (ArmoRM) is used to simulate the "ground truth" reward, a common practice in RLHF research. Additional experiments use GRM-gemma2-2B-rewardmodel-ft as an alternative ground truth.
- **Data**: The UltraFeedback dataset is used, with preferences relabeled according to the simulated ground truth. It is partitioned into subsets for reward model training and policy gradient.
- **Initial Policies**: Experiments utilize language models from the Pythia (1B, 2.8B) and Llama-3.2 (1B, 3B, SFT and instruction-tuned versions) families, serving as initial policies after supervised finetuning (SFT).
- **Reward Model Training and Selection**:
 - **Custom-Trained RMs**: Five reward models are trained, each on a different mix of "on-policy" (sampled from initial policy) and "off-policy" (from UltraFeedback) preference pairs (100%, 75%, 50%, 25%, 0% on-policy). This approach is designed to induce variations in reward variance and accuracy.
 - **"Perfectly Accurate, Low Variance" RM**: A specific RM is constructed by taking the ground truth reward and scaling down its reward differences while preserving rankings, to explicitly test the impact of low variance with high accuracy.
 - **Public RMs**: Four publicly available RMs with varying RewardBench scores are also used to assess the findings in a broader context.
- **Reward Normalization**: All reward models, including the simulated ground truth, are normalized to produce rewards on a comparable scale, ensuring that observed differences in reward variance are not merely due to scale variations.
- **Policy Gradient Methods**: RLOO (Reinforced Learning from Optimizer Output) is primarily used due to its computational efficiency, with some experiments using GRPO (Generalized Policy Optimization) for robustness checks.
- **Evaluation Metrics**:
 - Increase in proxy reward (the reward the RM outputs).
 - Increase in ground truth reward (the reward from the simulated ground truth RM).
 - Correlation coefficients (Pearson and Spearman) between reward model properties (variance, on-policy accuracy, off-policy accuracy, and combinations thereof) and reward maximization rates.
 - KL divergence from the initial policy, indicating how much the policy has changed.

This comprehensive methodology, spanning both theoretical proofs and empirical studies with realistic LLMs and datasets, aims to provide robust insights into the multifaceted nature of effective reward models in RLHF.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both theoretical proofs and empirical results that collectively demonstrate the critical role of reward variance, in addition to accuracy, for efficient RLHF optimization.

#### 4.1. Theoretical Findings

1. **Low Reward Variance Implies Slow Reward Maximization (Theorem 1 / Theorem 4)**:
 - The central theoretical finding is that regardless of how accurate a reward model is, if it induces low reward variance (i.e., it assigns very similar rewards to different outputs that are probable under the current policy), the RLHF objective landscape becomes flat.
 - This flatness hinders optimization, causing both the proxy reward and the ground truth reward to increase at an extremely slow rate during policy gradient training. The time required for a desired reward increase is shown to be inversely proportional to the cube root of the average initial reward variance. This occurs because low reward variance leads to vanishing gradients, and higher-order derivatives also vanish, preventing rapid increases in gradient norm.

2. **More Accurate Reward Models Are Not Necessarily Better Teachers (Theorem 2 / Theorem 5)**:
 - Reward variance is distinct from accuracy. A reward model can be perfectly accurate (correctly ranking all output pairs) yet induce very low reward variance.
 - The theory proves that a perfectly accurate reward model can lead to arbitrarily slow ground truth reward maximization compared to a substantially less accurate model, if the accurate model induces low reward variance. Conversely, an inaccurate reward model that sufficiently differentiates between outputs can drive faster optimization.
 - This result formally challenges the sole reliance on accuracy for evaluating reward models, showing it is an insufficient criterion in the context of RLHF optimization. However, it notes that accuracy remains important for preventing issues like reward hacking, which is why RLHF is often run for only a few epochs.

3. **Reward Model Effectiveness is Policy-Dependent (Theorem 3 / Theorem 6)**:
 - Reward variance is a function of both the reward model and the policy it is evaluating. The same reward model can induce high reward variance for one language model (initial policy) but low variance for another.
 - This implies that a reward model deemed "good" for one language model might be ineffective for another, leading to slow optimization. This finding highlights a fundamental limitation of universal reward model benchmarks that evaluate RMs independently of the specific policy they are intended to guide.

#### 4.2. Empirical Corroboration

The experiments, conducted with Pythia and Llama-3.2 models of up to 8B parameters on standard RLHF datasets, largely corroborate the theoretical predictions.

1. **Strong Correlation Between Reward Variance and Maximization Rate**:
 - Across various experimental setups (different initial policies, RM training strategies, and ground truth RMs), reward variance consistently showed a strong positive correlation (high Pearson and Spearman coefficients) with the rate of increase in both proxy and ground truth rewards (e.g., Table 2, 4, 6, 8, 9). This indicates that higher reward variance generally leads to faster reward maximization.

2. **Accuracy Alone is Insufficient**:
 - In line with Theorem 2, the "perfectly accurate reward model with low reward variance" consistently led to significantly slower increases in ground truth reward compared to less accurate reward models that induced higher variance (e.g., Figure 2, 5, 7, 9, 11).
 - Remarkably, in the initial epochs of training, some proxy reward models even outperformed direct optimization using the (simulated) ground truth reward, if the proxy RM induced higher reward variance. This suggests that even a perfectly accurate ground truth reward, if it induces low variance for the current policy, can lead to a flatter landscape and slower progress.
 - The experiments also showed that while on-policy accuracy sometimes correlated with reward increase, off-policy accuracy often showed a negative correlation, indicating that out-of-distribution accuracy might not be a reliable indicator of RM quality for on-policy optimization.

3. **Reward Model Effectiveness Varies with Language Model**:
 - Experiments using different initial language models (Pythia-1B SFT, Llama-3.2-1B SFT, Llama-3.2-1B Instruct, and their 3B counterparts) and publicly available reward models demonstrated that the reward model yielding the highest ground truth reward increase varied depending on the initial policy (e.g., Figure 3, 13, 14). This supports the theoretical finding that optimal reward models are not universally superior but are instead contextually dependent on the language model being aligned.

4. **KL Efficiency**:
 - While high accuracy alone does not guarantee fast optimization, accurate reward models generally lead to policies with lower KL divergence from the initial policy for a given level of ground truth reward increase. This suggests that accurate RMs are more "KL-efficient" (e.g., Figure 4, 6, 8, 10, 12).

Overall, the findings highlight that for efficient RLHF optimization, a reward model must not only be accurate but also induce sufficient variance among the rewards of probable outputs, and its effectiveness is dependent on the specific language model it is guiding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the understanding of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) by identifying reward variance as a critical, yet previously under-recognized, factor influencing the efficiency of language model alignment. Its significance and potential impact are multi-faceted:

- **Refined Understanding of Reward Model Quality**: The work challenges the conventional reliance on accuracy as the sole metric for evaluating reward models. By theoretically proving and empirically demonstrating that low reward variance leads to a flat objective landscape and slow optimization, it introduces a complementary, non-trivial dimension to reward model quality. This implies that "goodness" for a reward model extends beyond merely ranking preferences correctly; it must also provide sufficient signal (variance) for the policy to learn effectively.

- **Improved Reward Model Evaluation Benchmarks**: The findings underscore the limitations of current reward model benchmarks that primarily focus on accuracy and provide universal rankings independent of the specific language model being aligned. The research advocates for more holistic evaluation protocols that consider properties beyond accuracy, such as induced reward variance, and explicitly account for the interaction between the reward model and the language model it guides. This could lead to the development of dynamic or adaptive evaluation methods.

- **Guidance for Reward Model Training and Design**: The insights offer actionable directions for improving reward model training methodologies. Future work could explore techniques to explicitly encourage higher reward variance during reward model training, for example, by incorporating margin-based objectives or designing regularization strategies that promote reward differentiation among diverse outputs. This could involve exploring techniques that optimize for both accuracy and sufficient reward separation.

- **Broader Applicability in Reinforcement Learning**: While focused on RLHF, the theoretical analysis concerning the impact of low reward variance on policy gradient optimization (for policies using softmax distributions) is applicable to general reinforcement learning environments. This opens avenues for investigating how these insights could inform the design of reward functions or data selection algorithms in other domains where outputs are generated via softmax.

- **Informing Alignment Method Selection**: The observation that the importance of reward model properties (like accuracy) can vary across different alignment methods (e.g., RLHF vs. Best-of-N sampling) highlights the need for a nuanced understanding when choosing and designing alignment pipelines. This encourages deeper research into how characteristics of reward models interact with the specifics of various alignment algorithms.

In conclusion, this research significantly advances the theoretical and practical understanding of reward model effectiveness in RLHF. By highlighting reward variance and the policy-dependent nature of reward model quality, it lays a foundation for more robust training, evaluation, and deployment of alignment techniques for large language models.
