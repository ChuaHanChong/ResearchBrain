# Learning Diverse Bimanual Dexterous Manipulation Skills from Human Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Peking University developed BiDexHD, a framework that constructs diverse bimanual dexterous manipulation tasks from human demonstrations and learns policies via a teacher-student reinforcement learning paradigm. The system demonstrated high success rates on trained tasks and competitive zero-shot generalization to tasks involving previously unseen objects, addressing the need for scalable bimanual skill acquisition.

## Problem

- Developing robotic bimanual dexterous manipulation skills faces challenges due to the high-dimensional action space and complex coordination requirements.
- Existing reinforcement learning approaches often rely on manually designed, task-specific reward functions, limiting scalability and generalization across diverse tasks.
- Current benchmarks offer limited task diversity, and teleoperation methods require continuous human intervention, hindering autonomous application.

## Method

- BiDexHD is a three-phase framework: automated task construction from human demonstrations in simulation, state-based multi-task ==reinforcement learning== (teacher policies) using IPPO, and vision-based policy distillation (student policy) with DAgger.
- A unified two-stage reward function (simulation alignment and trajectory tracking) guides teacher policies, leveraging pre-computed functional grasping centers for robust initial interactions.
- The vision-based student policy processes object point clouds and robot proprioception, enabling generalization by learning from expert teacher actions.

## Results

- BiDexHD-IPPO teacher policies achieved **98.71%** Stage 1 success and **78.18%** Stage 2 tracking rate on trained tasks.
- The vision-based student policy, BiDexHD-IPPO+DAgger, demonstrated **99.38%** Stage 1 success and **74.59%** Stage 2 tracking on trained tasks, and notably **94.79%** Stage 1 success and **53.71%** Stage 2 tracking on tasks with entirely new objects.
- Ablation studies confirmed that the explicit alignment stage and functional grasping centers were essential for successful policy learning, showing substantial performance drops without them.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Independent learning for each hand (IPPO) effectively acquires robust bimanual skills and adapts better to diverse tasks compared to a centralized PPO approach.
> - An explicit two-stage reward function with an initial alignment phase is critical for successful bimanual manipulation, particularly when combined with functional grasping centers derived from human demonstrations.
> - Distilling state-based teacher policies into a single vision-based student policy, conditioned on visual features like point clouds, enables competitive zero-shot generalization to tasks involving unseen objects.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Bimanual dexterous manipulation is an area of robotics that involves coordinating two multi-fingered hands to interact with objects. This capability is considered fundamental for robots to perform complex real-world tasks that humans accomplish daily, such as using tools, tying shoelaces, or operating kitchen utensils. It enables both symmetrical collaborative tasks (e.g., carrying a heavy box) and asymmetrical tasks where one hand stabilizes an object while the other manipulates it (e.g., twisting a bottle cap). The development of this capability is relevant to various fields, including manufacturing, healthcare, agriculture, construction, and the tertiary industry.

A significant challenge in robotic bimanual dexterous manipulation is the high-dimensional action space associated with multi-fingered hands and the inherent complexity of tasks requiring coordinated movements. Prior research has addressed bimanual manipulation primarily using low-Degree-of-Freedom (DOF) end-effectors like grippers, as seen in works by Grannen et al. (2023), Yu et al. (2024), Kataoka et al. (2022), and Liu et al. (2024a). For dexterous hands, existing approaches often rely on reinforcement learning (RL) (Lin et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024a). However, these RL methods typically necessitate intricate reward functions specifically designed for a limited set of manually defined tasks, which restricts their scalability and generalizability.

Another line of work utilizes teleoperation for bimanual dexterous manipulation (Sindhupathiraja et al., 2024; Fu et al., 2024; He et al., 2024), but these approaches inherently require continuous human intervention. More recent efforts have explored the application of large language models for system design (Gbagbe et al., 2024), physics-aware learning for grasping (Wang et al., 2024), and keypoint-based visual imitation learning for bimanual coordination (Gao et al., 2024).

The broader field of dexterous manipulation, including single-hand tasks like in-hand manipulation, grasping, and deformable object manipulation, has also seen advancements, often leveraging human demonstrations (Arunachalam et al., 2023; Mandikal & Grauman, 2021; Sivakumar et al., 2022; Qin et al., 2022b; Liu et al., 2023; Shaw et al., 2023b; Chen et al., 2024). These methods often involve human-robot-arm-hand retargeting and imitation learning. However, many focus on single-hand scenarios or tasks like video-conditioned teleoperation, and the application to bimanual dexterous manipulation with diverse tasks remains less explored.

The research context is further defined by the emergence of comprehensive human bimanual manipulation datasets (Zhan et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024b; Fan et al., 2023; Razali & Demiris, 2023), which provide rich sources of dual-hand posture sequences and interaction data. These datasets offer a foundation for defining a wide array of bimanual manipulation tasks automatically. This paper positions its work as a general solution that aims to learn diverse bimanual dexterous skills from these human demonstrations in a unified and scalable manner, contrasting with existing methods that are often limited by task-specific designs or human intervention.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to develop an approach for efficiently learning diverse bimanual dexterous manipulation skills from human demonstrations in a unified and scalable manner. This objective is articulated by the question posed by the authors: "Can we learn diverse bimanual dexterous manipulation skills in a unified and scalable way?"

The motivation behind this research stems from several identified limitations in the current state of robotic manipulation:
- **High-Dimensional Action Space and Task Complexity:** Bimanual dexterous manipulation involves a high number of degrees of freedom, making policy learning inherently challenging.
- **Limited Task Diversity in Benchmarks:** Existing benchmarks often feature a narrow range of tasks, which hinders the development of general-purpose bimanual skills.
- **Reliance on Task-Specific Reward Functions:** Current reinforcement learning approaches for bimanual dexterous manipulation frequently require the manual design of intricate reward functions tailored to specific tasks. This dependency limits scalability and generalizability across a broader spectrum of behaviors.
- **Inevitability of Human Intervention:** Approaches relying on teleoperation, while effective for specific tasks, necessitate continuous human input, which is not scalable for autonomous systems.
- **Advantages of Human Demonstrations:** The authors motivate the use of human demonstrations by noting their relative ease of acquisition (e.g., with haptic gloves or MoCap devices) compared to deploying trained robotic policies. Furthermore, human demonstrations are considered to contain physically compliant and human-aligned behaviors that are valuable for robot learning.

To address these motivations, the authors aim to propose BiDexHD, a framework designed to:
- Automatically construct a series of diverse tasks from existing human bimanual manipulation datasets in simulation.
- Utilize a unified reward function for reinforcement learning, obviating the need for specific reward designs for each individual task.
- Implement a policy learning paradigm that can effectively mimic fine-grained bimanual behaviors, encourage smooth trajectories, ensure synchronous collaboration between hands, and avoid collisions.
By overcoming these challenges, the work seeks to break the "bottleneck of limited tasks and manual designs," which is presented as a significant step toward the development of general-purpose bimanual dexterous manipulation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology, named BiDexHD, is a three-phase framework designed to construct and solve bimanual dexterous manipulation tasks from human demonstrations.

#### 3.1. Overview of BiDexHD

The framework operates in three sequential phases:
1. **Task Construction:** Bimanual manipulation tasks are automatically generated from human demonstration datasets within the IsaacGym simulation environment.
2. **State-Based Multi-Task Reinforcement Learning (Teacher Learning):** Independent state-based teacher policies are trained in parallel using reinforcement learning for subsets of constructed tasks that require similar behaviors.
3. **Vision-Based Policy Distillation (Student Learning):** The learned teacher policies are distilled into a vision-based student policy, designed for inference and capable of generalizing skills across related tasks.

#### 3.2. Task Construction from Bimanual Dataset

This phase involves converting human demonstrations into a set of executable tasks in a simulation environment. The focus is on bimanual tool-using tasks.
- **Data Preprocessing:** Human demonstration trajectories (e.g., from the TACO dataset) are processed. This involves extracting wrist and fingertip poses for both hands at each timestep using MANO parameters. Object position and orientation sequences are also extracted. Human wrist coordinates are aligned with robot palm base coordinates, and a z-axis offset is applied to object poses to standardize initial object heights on the table.
- **Simulation Initialization:** For each preprocessed human demonstration, a corresponding bimanual manipulation task is constructed in IsaacGym. This involves initializing a tool, a target object, and two robotic arms equipped with LEAP Hands. The initial observation space for each robot includes arm-hand joint angles and velocities, wrist poses, fingertip positions, object positions, orientations, linear and angular velocities, and a unique object identifier. By default, the system assumes a right-handed robot setup (left hand handles target object, right hand handles tool).
- **Feasibility Check:** To ensure tasks are viable in the simulation, retargeting optimizers are used to map human hand motions to robot hand joint angles, and inverse kinematics (IK) is solved to determine robot arm joint angles based on palm base poses. Trajectories are replayed in the simulator to identify and remove invalid tasks, yielding a complete set of feasible tasks.

#### 3.3. Multi-Task State-Based Policy Learning (Teacher Learning)

In this phase, state-based policies are learned using reinforcement learning for tasks with similar behaviors, typically divided into two stages within each task. The learning algorithm employed is Independent Proximal Policy Optimization (IPPO), where each hand's policy learns independently.
- **Two-Stage Reward Function:** A general two-stage reward function unifies the learning process:
 - **Stage 1: Simulation Alignment:** The objective is to move the tool and target object from their fixed initial poses to a reference pose derived from the first timestep of the human demonstration. This stage is crucial for establishing initial grasping and object stabilization.
 - **Approaching Reward (r_appro):** Encourages robot palms and fingertips to approach and remain close to a pre-computed "grasping center" (ˆx_gc) on the tool or object. The grasping center is calculated by sampling points from the object mesh and averaging the top L=50 nearest points to the human-demonstrated wrist and fingertip positions at the reference timestep. This reward penalizes distance from these centers.
 - **Lifting Reward (r_lift):** Motivates the robot to hold objects firmly and lift them to their desired reference poses. It consists of a non-negative linear position reward and a negative quaternion distance reward. This reward is activated only when the hands are sufficiently close to the grasping centers.
 - **Bonus Reward (r_bonus):** Provides a positive incentive when the distance between an object's current position and its reference position falls below a success threshold (ε_succ) for a sustained number of consecutive steps (`u`). This signals successful alignment.
 - The total alignment reward (r_align) is a weighted sum of r_appro, r_lift, and r_bonus.
 - **Stage 2: Trajectory Tracking:** Once Stage 1 is successfully completed (i.e., objects are at reference poses), the goal is to maintain the grasp and accurately follow the remaining steps of the human-demonstrated trajectory.
 - **Tracking Reward (r_track):** An exponential reward function that encourages precise tracking of desired object positions at each timestep. It penalizes the squared distance between the current object position in simulation and the corresponding position in the human demonstration trajectory, adjusted for a constant tracking frequency `f`. This reward is active only after Stage 1 success.
- The overall total reward (r_total) for policy learning is a weighted sum of r_align and r_track, enabling unified learning across the two stages.

#### 3.4. Vision-Based Policy Distillation (Student Learning)

This phase transforms the learned state-based teacher policies into a single vision-based student policy, enhancing generalization for deployment.
- **Algorithm:** DAgger (Dataset Aggregation), an on-policy imitation learning algorithm, is used.
- **Student Observation Space:** The student policy receives observations consisting of robot proprioception (joint angles, velocities), wrist poses, fingertip positions, and object information represented as point clouds (P sampled points from the object's surface mesh). Point clouds are pre-sampled from meshes, transformed by current object pose, and augmented with Gaussian noise.
- **Trajectory-Conditioned Policy:** To improve generalization, the student policy is designed as a "trajectory-conditioned in-context policy." It takes K-step future object poses as additional input, providing implicit information about motion direction and speed. The influence of K can be masked by setting K=0.
- **Training Process:** The student policy is trained by minimizing the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss between its actions and actions labeled by the expert teacher policies. During distillation, a mix of expert actions (with probability `p`) and the student's own actions are used to populate the DAgger buffer, which is intended to make the policy more robust to its own errors.
- **Model Architecture:** State-based policies use five-layer Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs). The vision-based policy employs a simplified PointNet backbone (two 1D convolutional layers, max/average pooling) to process object point clouds, with its output feeding into shared MLPs for the actor and critic networks.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of BiDexHD was conducted on the TACO (Liu et al., 2024b) dataset, comprising 141 human demonstrations across six categories of bimanual tool-using tasks. These tasks were categorized into 16 semantic groups. The dataset was split into 80% for training and 20% for testing. Test tasks were further divided into "Test Comb" (seen objects and tools in new combinations) and "Test New" (tasks involving entirely new objects). Performance was measured using the average success rate of Stage 1 (r1) and the average tracking rate of Stage 2 (r2), with default thresholds ε_succ = ε_track = 0.1 for success criteria.

#### 4.1. Teacher Learning (RL Results)

- **Comparison of IPPO vs. PPO:** BiDexHD-IPPO demonstrated higher performance compared to BiDexHD-PPO (centralized PPO). On trained tasks, BiDexHD-IPPO achieved r1 = 98.71% and r2 = 78.18%, while BiDexHD-PPO reached r1 = 90.55% and r2 = 53.88%. This indicated that independent learning for each hand (IPPO) was more efficient in acquiring robust skills and adaptable across diverse tasks, especially for seen objects.
- **Generalization Performance:** Both BiDexHD-IPPO and BiDexHD-PPO showed a noticeable performance decline on "Test New" tasks, particularly in r2 (tracking rate). For BiDexHD-IPPO, r2 dropped from 78.18% on trained tasks to 21.34% on "Test New" tasks. This was attributed to the state-based policies' reliance on one-hot object labels in observations, which are absent or new in unseen scenarios.

#### 4.2. Ablations on Teacher Learning

- **Necessity of Alignment Stage:** A variant without the explicit Stage 1 alignment (BiDexHD-IPPO w/o stage-1) exhibited a substantial drop in performance (r1 = 25.00%, r2 = 17.52% on trained tasks). This demonstrated the critical role of the initial alignment phase and its associated reward components (r_align) in enabling successful manipulation. Many tasks failed to even initiate tracking without this stage.
- **Functional Grasping Center:** Replacing the pre-computed functional grasping center with the geometric center of objects (BiDexHD-IPPO w/o gc) led to a decrease in performance (r1 = 90.53%, r2 = 66.39% on trained tasks). This indicated that a functional grasping center, derived from human demonstrations, was more effective in guiding human-like and stable grasps, particularly for objects with complex geometries or handles.
- **Success Bonus:** Removing the bonus reward (r_bonus) (BiDexHD-IPPO w/o bonus) resulted in a decline in r2 on both training and unseen tasks (r1 = 97.67%, r2 = 66.65% on trained tasks). This suggested that the success bonus played a role in enhancing the policy's awareness of task progression and the transition between stages.

#### 4.3. Student Learning (DAgger Results)

- **Overall Performance and Generalization:** BiDexHD-IPPO+DAgger significantly outperformed BiDexHD-PPO+DAgger and a Behavior Cloning (BC) baseline. On trained tasks, it achieved r1 = 99.38% and r2 = 74.59%. Crucially, it demonstrated competitive zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks, with r1 = 92.85% and r2 = 48.43% on "Test Comb" tasks, and r1 = 94.79% and r2 = 53.71% on "Test New" tasks.
- **Vision-Based Generalization:** The strong performance on "Test New" tasks, which involve previously unseen objects, suggested that the vision-based student policy effectively learned to rely on visual information (point clouds, shapes, local features) rather than explicit one-hot object identifiers. This enabled effective generalization capabilities.
- **Behavior Cloning Baseline:** The BC baseline performed poorly (r1 = 0%, r2 = 0%), highlighting the challenges of learning complex bimanual skills purely from static observation-action datasets and the importance of dynamic interaction and distillation processes in BiDexHD.
- **Future Conditioned Steps (K):** An analysis of the impact of including K-step future object positions in the student policy's input (K ∈ {0, 1, 2, 5}) indicated that while K>0 provided minor additional benefits for precise tracking, the performance did not vary significantly. Even with K=0 (no future steps), the student policy achieved acceptable performance (r1 = 98.01%, r2 = 72.09% on trained tasks; r2 = 49.27% on "Test New" tasks), suggesting that the teacher policies had already acquired robust skills, making pure imitation effective.
- **Threshold Sensitivity:** The performance metrics (r1 and r2) were sensitive to the choice of success and tracking thresholds (ε). Stricter thresholds (e.g., ε=0.05) led to a more pronounced decline in performance, particularly for unseen tasks, underscoring the challenge of precise spatial-temporal tracking in bimanual manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work presents BiDexHD, a framework that addresses several challenges in robotic bimanual dexterous manipulation by offering a novel approach to skill acquisition. The development of BiDexHD marks a step toward more generalized and scalable robotic manipulation capabilities.

The significance and potential impact of this research include:
- **Addressing Scalability and Generality:** By formalizing the problem of learning bimanual dexterous skills from human demonstrations and introducing a method for automatically constructing diverse tasks from existing datasets, BiDexHD aims to overcome the limitations of manually designed tasks and task-specific reward functions. This increases the potential for developing more general-purpose manipulation skills.
- **Efficient Skill Acquisition:** The teacher-student learning paradigm, particularly the use of independent reinforcement learning for teacher policies and subsequent distillation into a vision-based student, contributes to the efficiency of policy learning for high-DOF bimanual robots. This framework simplifies the complexity associated with directly learning multi-task, vision-based policies for dexterous hands.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** The empirical evaluation demonstrates the framework's capacity for competitive zero-shot generalization to unseen tasks and novel objects. The vision-based student policy's reliance on visual features (point clouds) rather than explicit object identifiers is a notable contribution to its ability to transfer skills to new scenarios, which is crucial for real-world deployment.
- **Advancing Embodied AI:** The work contributes to the broader field of embodied artificial intelligence by enabling robots to acquire complex, human-like bimanual manipulation skills. This has potential applications in industries such as manufacturing, healthcare, and services, where dexterous and coordinated robotic actions can enhance automation.
- **Leveraging Human Data:** The approach effectively utilizes the rich information present in human demonstration datasets, offering a pathway for robots to learn physically compliant and human-aligned behaviors that are often difficult to synthesize through purely robotic exploration.

While advancing the field, the authors acknowledge limitations, such as the need for more precise spatial and temporal tracking strategies and the exploration of a wider variety of real-world tasks (e.g., deformable object manipulation, bimanual handover) to further validate the framework's capabilities in dynamic and complex collaborative scenarios. Addressing these limitations in future research could further broaden the impact and applicability of this approach.
