# GTA-2: A Multi-VLM Framework for Synthesizing Robot Manipulation Skills via Grounded Task Axes

> [!summary] Summary
> GTA-2 is a modular framework where four specialized VLM agents turn a language instruction and one RGB-D observation into an executable manipulation skill: a task decomposer, a skill generator composing task-axis controllers over object keypoints and axes, a parameter setter, and a vision grounding module. Across 14 real-robot tasks it reaches 73.9% zero-shot success, rising to 90.7% after targeted per-agent feedback.

## Problem

- Predefined skill primitives stay too coarse, hiding which object parts matter, how the end-effector should orient, and what contact constraints execution must satisfy.
- End-to-end vision-language-action policies keep task decomposition and scene reasoning implicit, so failures are hard to diagnose or adapt when layout, viewpoint, or lighting shifts.
- Earlier task-axis controller systems depended on hand-engineered task decompositions, controller selections, axis definitions, and parameter tuning for every new task.

## Method

- **GTA-2**'s ==task-axis skill representation==: each subtask is a prioritized composition of parameterized controllers (PosAlign, AxisAlign, ForceConstant, PosDMPSpiral, GripperControl) defined over object keypoints and derived axes.
- ==Four-agent abstraction-to-grounding pipeline==: ==Task Decomposer==, ==Skill Generator== producing a lifted skill with parameter placeholders, ==Parameter Setter== assigning offsets and forces, ==Vision Module== grounding keypoints to metric 3D from depth.
- ==Targeted refinement interface==: feedback routes only to the agent that produced the faulty intermediate output, recomputing downstream stages while preserving correct decompositions, controller recipes, and groundings.

## Results

- Zero-shot success averaged **73.9%** over 20 trials per task, beating the strongest baseline CaP-Primitive at **42.5%** by **31.4 points**; refined skills reached **90.7%**.
- Over **210** independent generation runs, **154 (73.3%)** succeeded without feedback; one refinement round gave **181/210 (86.2%)**, two rounds **208/210 (99.0%)**, all within three.
- Feedback shifted from Skill Generator early to Vision Module later; CaP-TAC beat CaP-Primitive on pouring, cutting, and sweeping despite lower overall average.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling reasoning stages, not simplifying the action space, drives the gain: richer controller interfaces help only when a structured pipeline resolves them separately.
> - Exposing explicit intermediate representations converts debugging into localized edits, so a few high-level corrections recover failures without retraining or full regeneration.
> - Grounding once before execution is the residual weakness: object motion or slippage invalidates keypoints, since only robot state and force close the loop.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation often addresses the challenge of enabling robots to perform complex tasks by decomposing them into simpler behaviors or skills. However, current approaches frequently encounter limitations in bridging the gap between high-level natural language instructions and the low-level geometric, control, and scene-dependent decisions required for execution.

This work positions itself within this landscape by addressing two prominent paradigms:

1. **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models:** These models (e.g., Perceiver-Actor, RT-1, RT-2, Octo, π0.5) learn generalist behaviors by mapping visual observations and natural-language commands directly to robot actions. While demonstrating broad task capabilities, their internal reasoning structure is largely implicit. This opacity makes it difficult to diagnose failures, adapt to scene changes, or incorporate targeted human feedback, as the geometric and control details are often encoded within the policy itself.

2. **Language-model-based planning approaches (Code-as-Policies):** These methods (e.g., SayCan, ProgPrompt) generate executable robot programs by composing available APIs and predefined skill primitives. While providing more explicit plans, they typically rely on a pre-existing library of skills that encapsulate substantial perception, grounding, and control logic. This means the system can only solve tasks for which its primitive library already contains suitable behaviors, and the primitives themselves are often still relatively coarse.

Previous work on **Task-Axis Controllers** (e.g., GTA-1,-) introduced a representation that expresses manipulation behaviors as structured compositions of reusable controllers grounded in object-centric coordinate frames. These controllers specify motion, orientation, force, and constraint objectives relative to semantically meaningful keypoints and axes (e.g., handle axis, surface normal). This approach offered a finer granularity than generic skills and made the structure of the skill explicit, allowing for instantiation across new scenes by grounding the required features. However, prior task-axis systems typically relied on manual engineering for task decompositions, controller selections, and parameter tuning.

GTA-2 builds upon the explicit, structured representation of task-axis controllers while addressing the limitation of human-engineered skill construction. It differentiates itself by introducing a modular multi-VLM framework to *automate* the synthesis and grounding of these fine-grained skills directly from a high-level task prompt and visual observation. By factoring the problem into abstract skill generation and scene-specific grounding, GTA-2 aims to overcome the implicit reasoning of VLA models and the reliance on predefined, coarse primitives of LLM-based planning, offering an approach that is both structured and automatically generated for zero-shot application.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the inherent challenge in robotic manipulation: how to translate high-level natural language task instructions into precise, scene-dependent robot actions. Generic manipulation skills, such as "reach," "grasp," or "move," are often too coarse to specify the detailed geometric, control, and interaction decisions required for execution in a dynamic and varied environment. Natural language, while conveying intent, does not inherently encode these scene-specific choices.

The key objectives and motivations driving the development of GTA-2 are:

1. **Bridge the Abstraction Gap:** To develop an intermediate representation that is abstract enough to generalize across different object instances and scene configurations, yet explicit enough for low-level robot controllers to instantiate and execute. This involves decomposing behaviors beyond generic skills into reusable object-centric geometric features and controller-level components.

2. **Automate Skill Construction and Grounding:** To eliminate the reliance on manual engineering for task decompositions, controller selections, and parameter tuning that characterized prior task-axis-based systems. GTA-2 aims to automate the generation of task-bespoke manipulation skills directly from a high-level natural language prompt and a visual observation of the scene.

3. **Enable Zero-Shot Skill Generation:** To synthesize executable skills without requiring task-specific robot demonstrations, extensive policy training, or fine-tuning. This objective targets improving the adaptability and scalability of robotic systems to novel tasks and environments.

4. **Promote Interpretability and Refinability:** To create a framework where intermediate decisions—such as task decomposition, controller composition, parameter assignment, and visual grounding—are explicit, modular, and individually addressable. This design is motivated by the need for a practical interface for human-guided adaptation, allowing targeted feedback to correct specific errors without discarding correct components or retraining an entire end-to-end policy. This contrasts with VLA models where the internal reasoning is opaque, and Code-as-Policies where errors in monolithic code generation can be hard to localize.

5. **Enhance Robustness and Generalization:** To develop a system that generates manipulation skills that are robust to variations in scene configurations and can generalize effectively. By separating abstract skill synthesis from scene-specific grounding, the system can preserve the reusable structure of a skill while adapting its geometric and control details to each new observation.

In summary, GTA-2 is motivated by the need for robotic systems that can autonomously generate, interpret, and adapt complex manipulation skills from high-level commands, moving beyond the limitations of purely end-to-end learning or reliance on pre-defined, coarse primitives.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GTA-2 is structured as a modular, multi-step Vision-Language Model (VLM) framework designed to synthesize executable robot manipulation skills. It takes a natural language instruction ($\ell$), an RGB-D observation ($I$), and a reusable controller library ($C$) as inputs, and produces an executable robot program ($P$). The core of its approach lies in constructing an explicit task-axis skill representation, which separates abstract skill synthesis from scene-specific grounding.

The methodology involves a pipeline of four specialized VLM agents:

1. **Task Decomposer (A_TD):**
 - **Input:** Natural language instruction ($\ell$), scene image ($I$), controller library ($C$).
 - **Function:** Converts the high-level instruction into an ordered sequence of semantic subtasks, $Q = [q_1, \dots, q_T]$. Each subtask describes a meaningful phase of the manipulation task without specifying control details.
 - **Output:** An ordered list of subtasks (e.g., "approach bottle," "grasp bottle," "lift bottle").

2. **Skill Generator (A_SG):**
 - **Input:** A semantic subtask ($q_t$), task instruction ($\ell$), scene image ($I$), controller library ($C$).
 - **Function:** Maps each subtask $q_t$ to a *lifted skill step*, $\tilde{s}_t = [\tilde{c}_{t,1}, \dots, \tilde{c}_{t,M_t}]$. This involves:
 - Selecting appropriate controller types from $C$.
 - Identifying required keypoint and axis definitions (e.g., "scribble center," "board normal").
 - Specifying parameter placeholders (e.g., $\tilde{f}$ for force magnitude, $\tilde{r}$ for spiral radius).
 - Arranging the selected controllers in priority order.
 - **Output:** A full *lifted task-axis skill* $\tilde{S} = [\tilde{s}_1, \dots, \tilde{s}_T]$, which defines controller types, feature relationships, and parameter placeholders, but no metric values.

3. **Parameter Setter (A_PS):**
 - **Input:** Task instruction ($\ell$), scene observation ($I$), the lifted skill ($\tilde{S}$), controller library ($C$).
 - **Function:** Assigns numerical values ($\theta_j$) to the parameter placeholders ($\tilde{\theta}_j$) within the lifted skill. These include quantities like positional/angular offsets, force magnitudes, trajectory scales, and execution timings.
 - **Output:** A set of numerical parameter assignments, $\Theta_{\ell,I}$.

4. **Vision Module (A_VG):**
 - **Input:** RGB-D observation ($I$), robot state ($x$), the lifted skill ($\tilde{S}$).
 - **Function:** Resolves the feature definitions (keypoints $k_j$, axes $a_m$) requested by $\tilde{S}$ to metric 3D values ($p_j \in \mathbb{R}^3$, $v_m \in \mathbb{S}^2$) in the current execution context.
 - Object keypoints are localized in the RGB image by the VLM and converted to 3D positions using depth information.
 - Derived axes (e.g., surface normals, directions between keypoints) are computed from grounded features using point-cloud and geometric operations.
 - Robot- and world-relative features are obtained from forward kinematics and calibrated frames.
 - **Output:** A grounding map, $G_{I,x}$.

**Task-Axis Skill Representation:**
The core representation used by GTA-2 comprises:
- **Keypoints ($k$):** Task-relevant locations on or relative to an object (e.g., `k_object part location`).
- **Axes ($a$):** Task-relevant directions (e.g., `a_surface normal`, `a_along(k_1, k_2)`). This includes robot-relative (`a_eefX`) and world-relative (`a_worldZ`) axes.
- **Lifted Controller ($\tilde{c}$):** Defined by (type $\tau$, control feature $\phi_{ctl}$, target feature $\phi_{ref}$, task axis $a_{axis}$, parameter placeholder $\tilde{\theta}$, priority $\rho$).
- **Grounded Controller ($c$):** A lifted controller where features are resolved to metric values ($G(\cdot)$) and placeholders replaced by numerical values ($\theta$).
- **Controller Library ($C$):** Includes controllers like `PosAlign`, `PosWaypoint`, `AxisAlign`, `ForceConstant`, `GripperControl`, and Dynamic Movement Primitives (`PosDMPSpiral`).

**Robot Script Compilation and Execution:**
A deterministic compiler (Π) converts the fully grounded skill $S = (G_{I,x}, \Theta_{\ell,I})(\tilde{S})$ into an executable robot program $P$. This program activates skill steps sequentially. Within an active step, controllers evaluate objectives, and commands are combined based on priorities, then transmitted via a ROS-based interface.

**Human-Guided Targeted Refinement:**
A crucial aspect of the methodology is the explicit exposure of intermediate outputs, enabling targeted feedback. If an error occurs, human feedback ($h$) can be directed to the specific VLM agent responsible (Task Decomposer, Skill Generator, Parameter Setter, or Vision Module). This allows for revision of only the erroneous component, while preserving other correct parts of the generated skill, avoiding full re-generation or re-training. For example, if a positional offset is incorrect, feedback is sent only to the Parameter Setter.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments were conducted on a real UR5e manipulator with a Robotiq 2F-85 gripper and a fixed ZED 2i stereo camera. Gemini 3.1 Pro was used for all four VLM agents, configured with role-specific instructions and output formats. No task-specific demonstrations or fine-tuning were used for GTA-2.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper evaluates GTA-2 across 14 diverse real-robot manipulation tasks, focusing on zero-shot skill generation, targeted human refinement, and generalization compared to baseline methods.

**A. Zero-Shot Generation and Targeted Refinement:**

- **Initial Success Rate:** GTA-2 achieved an initial zero-shot success rate of **73.3%** across 210 independent runs (15 runs for each of 14 tasks), demonstrating its ability to generate executable skills without task-specific examples or training. This included tasks involving sustained contact and tool use.
- **Effectiveness of Human Refinement:** Targeted human feedback significantly improved success rates:
 - After one round of feedback: **86.2%** cumulative successful runs (181/210).
 - After two rounds of feedback: **99.0%** cumulative successful runs (208/210).
 - All 210 runs succeeded within three rounds of targeted feedback (99.0% after two, and 100% after three rounds as implied).
- **Feedback Distribution:** The analysis of feedback types revealed a clear pattern:
 - **Initial Feedback:** Primarily directed to the **Skill Generator**, indicating that the main challenge in early attempts was translating the task intent into an appropriate task-axis controller composition.
 - **Later Feedback:** Shifted predominantly to the **Vision Module**, once the manipulation structure was largely correct, residual errors were mainly related to scene-specific geometric grounding.
 - No further abstract planning corrections were needed after initial Skill Generator refinements.
- **Implication:** This distribution supports the modularity of GTA-2, showing that different failure modes are independently correctable and correspond to specific agent boundaries. Refinement efficiently corrected reusable components of the skill rather than requiring full regeneration.

**B. Task Generalization and Baseline Comparison:**

GTA-2's performance was compared against three baselines on task generalization across varying scene configurations, with 20 trials per task and method.

- **GTA-2 Performance:**
 - **GTA-2 Zero-Shot:** Achieved an average success rate of **73.9%**.
 - **GTA-2 One-Success:** (after one successful refinement) Further raised the average success rate to **90.7%**. This demonstrated that refinement corrected reusable skill components that generalized to new scenes.

- **Baseline Performance:**
 - **CaP-Primitive (Code-as-Policies with conventional primitives):** Achieved an average success rate of **42.5%**. This baseline, while simplifying planning for some tasks, suffered from the coupling of action sequencing, parameter selection, and visual grounding within a single generated program, making it sensitive to planning errors.
 - **CaP-TAC (Code-as-Policies with task-axis controllers):** Achieved an average success rate of **37.6%**. Its overall performance was lower than CaP-Primitive, which is attributed to the richer action space of task-axis controllers requiring more structured reasoning that CaP-TAC's single-program generation struggled with. However, it outperformed CaP-Primitive on complex tasks (e.g., pouring, cutting, sweeping) where contact constraints, object-relative motion, and tool geometry are critical, highlighting the expressivity of task-axis representations.
 - **π0.5 (Generalist VLA policy):** Achieved an average success rate of **33.1%**. This end-to-end policy, despite its broad capabilities, showed lower performance compared to GTA-2, consistent with the reported sensitivities of VLA policies to scene variations and the implicit nature of their reasoning.

- **Key Findings from Comparison:**
 - GTA-2 Zero-Shot exceeded the strongest baseline (CaP-Primitive) by **31.4 percentage points**, and GTA-2 One-Success demonstrated an even greater advantage.
 - The results indicate that the superior performance of GTA-2 stems from its structured abstraction-to-grounding pipeline and specialized agents. This design effectively reduces the coupling between reasoning stages, leading to more reliable skill generation and robust generalization, especially for complex, contact-rich manipulation.
 - Task-axis representations are shown to be effective for contact-rich manipulation and tool use, but their full potential is realized when combined with GTA-2's multi-agent pipeline for structured decomposition, composition, parameterization, and visual grounding.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GTA-2 framework represents a significant advancement in the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in bridging the gap between high-level language instructions and low-level robot execution. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Enabling Zero-Shot Skill Generation:** GTA-2 demonstrates the ability to construct executable, task-bespoke manipulation skills without requiring task-specific robot demonstrations, extensive policy training, or fine-tuning. This dramatically lowers the barrier for deploying robots in novel scenarios and for executing tasks they have not explicitly learned, fostering greater autonomy and adaptability.

2. **Increased Interpretability and Explainability:** By employing an explicit object-centric task-axis skill representation and factoring skill generation into distinct VLM agent stages (Task Decomposer, Skill Generator, Parameter Setter, Vision Module), GTA-2 makes the robot's reasoning process transparent. This contrasts with opaque end-to-end VLA models and monolithic Code-as-Policies, where internal decisions are difficult to inspect or understand. This interpretability is crucial for trust, debugging, and regulatory compliance in robotic systems.

3. **Efficient Human-Robot Collaboration through Targeted Refinement:** The modular design with explicit intermediate outputs allows for highly efficient and targeted human feedback. Instead of re-training an entire policy or indiscriminately regenerating a full program, users can pinpoint and correct errors at the specific stage responsible (e.g., skill composition, parameter assignment, or visual grounding). This capability preserves correct components of the generated skill, significantly reducing the effort and time required to achieve successful execution, and offers a practical interface for human-guided adaptation.

4. **Enhanced Generalization and Robustness:** The separation of abstract skill synthesis from scene-specific grounding allows for the reuse of the abstract skill structure across varying scene configurations. The findings demonstrate that GTA-2 achieves significantly higher zero-shot success rates and better generalization compared to state-of-the-art VLA and Code-as-Policies baselines, particularly for complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks that benefit from the structured reasoning provided by task-axis controllers.

5. **Leveraging VLM Capabilities for Robotic Control:** GTA-2 effectively harnesses the semantic and visual reasoning capabilities of large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to automate previously manual engineering processes in robotic control. This demonstrates a powerful paradigm for integrating advanced AI models into robot planning and execution.

6. **Foundation for Open-World Manipulation Systems:** The framework contributes a practical step towards the development of open-world manipulation systems. Such systems need to construct, inspect, and adapt new behaviors on the fly, rather than being limited to a fixed set of predefined skills or relying on implicit, uninspectable policies.

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as dependence on the expressivity of the controller library, reliance on single RGB-D observations, and lack of online feature updates, these are presented as avenues for future work that could build upon GTA-2's modular architecture. The core contribution lies in demonstrating a more reliable and interpretable pathway from language to robot execution, fostering a future where robots can more flexibly understand and interact with their environments.
