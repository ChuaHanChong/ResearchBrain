# Geometry-Aware Predictive Safety Filters on Humanoids: From Poisson Safety Functions to CBF Constrained MPC

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Caltech developed a geometry-aware predictive safety filter for legged robots, integrating extended Poisson safety functions with a Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Control Barrier Function (CBF) framework. The approach enables dynamic collision avoidance and navigation in complex environments by explicitly considering robot geometry, environmental dynamics, and real-time perception, validated on quadruped and humanoid robots.

## Problem

- Existing safety-critical control methods often use simplified robot geometry (e.g., point mass) or *ad hoc* obstacle models, leading to conservative or inefficient navigation for complex robots like humanoids.
- Previous Poisson safety functions typically assume static environments, making them unsuitable for dynamic scenarios where obstacles or the environment change over time.
- Many safety-critical control approaches lack a robust, real-time pipeline to synthesize safety constraints directly from raw perception data in unstructured, unknown environments.

## Method

- A hierarchical control architecture combines ==nonlinear MPC== and ==Control Barrier Functions (CBFs)==, using an ==extended Poisson safety function== to generate geometry-aware and time-varying safety constraints.
- Poisson safety functions are extended by reformulating the static Dirichlet problem into a ==parameterized moving boundary value problem== and employing ==Minkowski set operations== to incorporate robot occupancy and rotational degrees of freedom.
- The system uses ==real-time perception== (image segmentation and optical flow) to derive ==2D occupancy maps== and predict boundary evolution for the Poisson safety function, then uses a ==reduced-order model== for the MPC to ensure computational tractability.

## Results

- Both quadruped and humanoid robots successfully performed dynamic collision avoidance, with the Poisson safety function remaining positive throughout maneuvers, confirming the robot's centroid stayed within the buffered safe set.
- The **G1 humanoid**, carrying an oblong payload, navigated a narrow corridor by reorienting its lateral axis to align with the traversal direction, demonstrating the method's ability to prevent navigational deadlock.
- The numerical PDE solution for the safety function typically took **20-100 ms**, and the MPC was solved at **100 Hz**, demonstrating real-time applicability on physical robots in challenging safety-critical scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly accounting for a robot's asymmetric geometry and orientation via Minkowski differences allows for less conservative and more efficient collision avoidance maneuvers, such as reorienting to fit through narrow passages.
> - Extending Poisson safety functions to incorporate both temporal dynamics (moving boundaries) and robot geometry (configuration space lifting) creates a robust, perception-driven mechanism for defining safe operating regions in dynamic, unstructured environments.
> - Integrating these extended Poisson safety functions as CBF constraints within an MPC framework provides both anticipatory optimal planning and instantaneous provable safety guarantees, leading to robust and reactive safety performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Autonomous navigation in unstructured and dynamically changing environments is a complex and ongoing challenge in robotics. This work specifically addresses the need for safe autonomy, particularly for legged robots such as humanoids and quadrupeds, which possess manipulable and often asymmetric geometries. These geometries necessitate careful consideration during safety-critical trajectory planning to prevent collisions, which can have significant consequences.

The field of safety-critical control encompasses various approaches, including Hamilton-Jacobi (HJ) reachability methods, artificial potential fields (APFs), state-constrained model predictive control (MPC), and control barrier function (CBF) methods. This paper focuses on the integration of MPC and CBFs, recognizing the strengths of MPC in finite-horizon optimality and CBFs in enforcing dynamic safety guarantees. The combined MPC+CBF framework has been identified as a robust architecture for safety-critical control, offering provable robustness and recursive feasibility when CBF constraints are applied over the MPC horizon.

A fundamental aspect of safety-critical control involves defining a "safe set" for the robot's state. Traditionally, this safe set is characterized either by *ad hoc* analytical expressions, often representing obstacles with simple geometric shapes (e.g., circles, ellipses), or by signed distance functions (SDFs), which numerically represent the distance to the nearest safe set boundary. This research builds upon a more recent development: Poisson safety functions. These functions numerically characterize safe sets by solving Poisson’s equation in the spatial domain, offering advantages over both *ad hoc* and SDF methods, particularly in handling complex, unknown environmental geometries.

This paper extends the application of Poisson safety functions to address limitations in previous work, specifically their common assumption of static safe sets and their primary focus on spatial dimensions without explicit consideration for robot geometry and orientation. By integrating these extended Poisson safety functions with an MPC+CBF framework, the research aims to provide a more comprehensive and adaptable solution for geometry-aware, predictive safety filtering in dynamic, real-world robotic operations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and experimentally demonstrate a geometry-aware predictive safety filter for robotic systems. This filter is based on nonlinear Model Predictive Control (MPC) and Control Barrier Functions (CBFs), and it specifically utilizes extended Poisson safety functions to address the complexities of robotic operation in dynamic and unstructured environments.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges in current robotic autonomy:
- **Complexity of Unstructured and Dynamic Environments:** Legged robots, especially humanoids, are designed to operate in complex and potentially human-occupied environments that are often unstructured and change dynamically. Ensuring safe interaction with such environments is critical.
- **Limitations of Current Safety Function Characterizations:** Existing methods for defining safe sets, such as *ad hoc* analytical expressions or Signed Distance Functions (SDFs), can be insufficient for complex or unknown environments. *Ad hoc* methods require prior knowledge of obstacle shapes, while SDFs, though numerical, often don't explicitly account for robot geometry or dynamic changes.
- **Neglect of Robot Geometry:** Many safety-critical control approaches treat the robot as a point mass or use a simplified bounding sphere, leading to conservative avoidance maneuvers. Real-world robots, particularly humanoids and quadrupeds, possess asymmetric and manipulable geometries that significantly affect their safe operating space and should be considered for efficient and less conservative navigation.
- **Static Nature of Previous Poisson Safety Functions:** Prior applications of Poisson safety functions typically assumed static safe sets, which is not suitable for dynamically evolving environments where obstacles or the environment itself changes over time.

To address these motivations, the paper outlines several key contributions and objectives:
1. **Temporal Extension of Poisson Safety Functions:** To enable operation in dynamic environments, the researchers aimed to recast the static Dirichlet problem for Poisson's equation as a parameterized moving boundary value problem. This allows for the generation of a time-varying safety function that can extrapolate the evolution of the safe set over a finite future time interval.
2. **Geometric Extension for Robot Configuration Space:** The objective was to lift the domain for Poisson’s equation into a higher-dimensional configuration space. This involves employing Minkowski set operations to incorporate robot occupancy and its corresponding translational and rotational degrees-of-freedom (DOFs), thereby producing a Poisson safety function that is explicitly geometry-aware.
3. **Formulation of a Novel MPC+CBF Predictive Safety Filter:** The researchers sought to integrate these extended Poisson safety functions as CBF constraints within an MPC framework. This new formulation aims to create a predictive safety filter capable of generating safe, geometry-aware trajectories in real-time, initially using a reduced-order, single-integrator model for computational efficiency.
4. **Experimental Validation on Legged Robots:** A crucial objective was to experimentally demonstrate the practical advantages and real-time utility of this online predictive safety filtering method. This involved testing the architecture on both quadruped and humanoid robots in multiple challenging safety-critical scenarios to validate its effectiveness in dynamic collision avoidance and environmental navigation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research integrates Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) with Model Predictive Control (MPC), using an extended formulation of Poisson safety functions to generate geometry-aware and time-varying safety constraints. The overall control architecture is hierarchical, with the predictive safety filter operating at a mid-level to provide velocity references to a low-level locomotion controller.

**A. Safety-Critical Control Foundations:**
- **Control Barrier Functions (CBFs):** The work grounds its safety guarantees in the theory of CBFs. For continuous-time control affine systems, a function `h(x)` is a CBF if its Lie derivative satisfies `L_f h(x) + L_g h(x)u ≥ -γ(h(x))`, ensuring that the safe set `S = {x | h(x) ≥ 0}` remains forward invariant.
- **Discrete-time Control Barrier Functions (DCBFs):** Given that real-world robotic systems operate in sampled data, the paper utilizes discrete-time formulations. A function `h(x)` is a DCBF if `h(F(x,u)) ≥ ρh(x)` for some `ρ ∈`, ensuring that the safety value `h(x_k)` remains lower-bounded by a geometrically decaying curve `ρ^k h(x_0)`.
- **Safety Filter:** DCBFs are employed in a safety filter context where a nominal, potentially unsafe, controller `π_nom(x)` is minimally modified to produce safe control inputs `π_cbf(x)` by solving a quadratic program that minimizes `||u - π_nom(x)||^2` subject to the DCBF constraint.

**B. Model Predictive Control (MPC) Integration:**
- **MPC+CBF Framework:** To address the potential for undesirable equilibria or performance degradation when CBFs are used alone, the researchers integrate DCBF constraints into a finite-time optimal control problem (FTOCP) within an MPC framework. This allows for horizon-based optimization that considers both safety and performance goals.
- **FTOCP Formulation:** The MPC problem minimizes a sum of stage costs `c(ξ_i, ν_i)` and a terminal cost `V(ξ_N)` over a prediction horizon `N`. Constraints include dynamic feasibility (`ξ_i+1 = F(ξ_i, ν_i)`) and the critical DCBF constraint `h(ξ_i+1) ≥ ρh(ξ_i)` for all steps along the horizon. The first optimal control input `ν*_0` is applied to the system.

**C. Extending Poisson Safety Functions:**
- **Spatial Poisson Safety Functions:** Initially, the safe set `C` is defined by the zero superlevel set of a spatial safety function `h_0(y)`. When the environment is unknown *a priori*, real-time occupancy information is used to define the boundary `∂C` where `h_0(y)=0`. Poisson's equation, `Δh_0(y) = f(y)` (where `f(y) < 0` for superharmonicity) with Dirichlet boundary conditions (`h_0(y)=0` on `∂Ω`), is then solved over the domain `Ω` to numerically characterize `h_0`.
- **Temporal Parameterization (Moving Boundary Prediction):** To account for dynamic environments, the safe set `C` is parameterized by time, `C_T(t)`. The evolution of the boundary `∂C_T(t)` is predicted using level set methods and a transport equation, `∂ϕ/∂t + v ⋅ ∇ϕ = 0`, where `v` is the velocity field of the boundary. This creates a non-cylindrical space-time domain `Ω_T`. A modified Poisson's equation, `Δ_y h_T(y,t) = f(y,t)` in `Ω_T` with `h_T(y,t)=0` on `∂Ω_T`, is then solved.
- **Geometric Parameterization (Safe Set Buffering):** To incorporate robot geometry and orientation, the safe set `C` is modified via a Minkowski difference operation: `C_Q(q) = C ⊖ R(q)`. Here, `R(q)` represents the set of points occupied by the robot, relative to its centroid, at a given orientation `q`. This operation reduces the free space `C` to ensure the entire robot is collision-free. The domain for Poisson's equation is lifted into configuration space, `Ω_Q = U_q (C_Q(q) × {q})`. The equation `Δ_y h_Q(y,q) = f(y,q)` in `Ω_Q` with `h_Q(y,q)=0` on `∂Ω_Q` is solved.

**D. Combined Control Architecture:**
- **Unified Poisson Safety Function:** The temporal and geometric extensions are combined to produce a fully parameterized Poisson safety function `h_QT(y,q,t)`. This function is derived by solving `Δ_y h_QT(y,q,t) = f(y,q,t)` over a non-cylindrical domain `Ω_QT` in configuration space and time.
- **Reduced-Order Model (ROM) for MPC:** For computational tractability in real-time, the robotic system is approximated as a fully-actuated single-integrator with two translational DOFs and one rotational DOF. The state is `χ = [x, y, θ]^T` and the control input is `μ = [v_x, v_y, ω]^T`. The MPC problem (Eq. 29) minimizes tracking error to a goal `χ_d` and control effort, subject to discrete-time single-integrator dynamics and `h_QT` as a DCBF constraint.
- **Hierarchical Control:** The output `μ` from the MPC+CBF predictive safety filter (mid-level) serves as a velocity reference for a low-level locomotion controller. For the humanoid, this includes a reinforcement learning (RL) walking policy and a separate optimization-based controller for the waist. The waist controller uses a QP to track the desired rotational velocity `ω` by optimally combining lower-body rotation `ω_α` and relative upper-body rotation `ω_β`, while also reducing upper-body misalignment.

**E. Experimental Implementation:**
- **Robotic Platforms:** Unitree Go2 quadruped and G1 humanoid (with an oblong payload).
- **Perception and Pose:** OptiTrack motion capture for robot pose. Overhead ZED 2i stereo camera processed by an efficient track-anything-model (eTAM) image segmentation for 2D occupancy maps.
- **Environmental Prediction:** OpenCV optical flow estimates a 2D velocity field `v` from the camera stream, used for linear boundary prediction: `∂C_T(t) = {y ∈ R^2: ϕ_0(y - vt) = 0}`.
- **Numerical PDE Solution:** The parameterized Dirichlet problem is solved using a successive overrelaxation (SOR) method with an alternating checkerboard update scheme for parallelization. Typical solve times were 20-100 ms.
- **MPC Solution:** The nonlinear non-convex SQP problem for MPC is solved at 100 Hz using OSQP.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research validated its geometry-aware predictive safety filter through experimental demonstrations on a quadrupedal robot and a humanoid robot in various safety-critical scenarios. The findings consistently indicated the effectiveness of the proposed methodology in maintaining safety while enabling desired robotic behaviors.

**A. Scenario 1: Dynamic Collision Avoidance**
- **Experimental Setup:** Both a Unitree Go2 quadruped and a G1 humanoid were tasked with maintaining a fixed goal state while avoiding an incoming dynamic object (a red dodgeball). The humanoid carried an oblong payload to emphasize rotational considerations.
- **Behavioral Observations:**
 - Both robots successfully avoided the dynamic obstacle by executing simultaneous translational and rotational maneuvers.
 - The quadruped pivoted by aligning its longitudinal axis (its major axis) with the predicted trajectory of the incoming obstacle.
 - The humanoid rotated to align its lateral axis (its major axis) with the predicted obstacle trajectory.
 - These rotational (twisting) actions were crucial in minimizing the amount of translational motion required to avoid collision, thereby demonstrating an efficient use of available space.
- **Quantitative Validation:** The evaluated value of the Poisson safety function `h_QT` remained positive throughout the avoidance maneuvers for both robots. This confirmed that the centroid of the robot remained within the buffered safe set `C_QT`, and consequently, all points on the physical robot remained within the original, unbuffered safe set `C`.
- **Consistency:** The results were reported as consistent and repeatable across multiple trials.

**B. Scenario 2: Environmental Navigation (Humanoid)**
- **Experimental Setup:** The G1 humanoid, carrying an oblong payload, was tele-operated through an unstructured static environment for 100 seconds. The user-provided goal points were passed to the MPC+CBF filter, which then produced safe velocity commands for the low-level locomotion controller.
- **Behavioral Observations:**
 - The humanoid successfully navigated complex sections of the environment, specifically a narrow corridor that initially appeared impassable given the robot's nominal orientation and geometry (as shown in Fig. 6, left image).
 - The geometry-aware safety-critical controller enabled the robot to traverse this corridor by planning a trajectory that involved reorienting the humanoid. It specifically aligned its lateral axis with the corridor traversal direction (as shown in Fig. 6, right image). This "opened" the gap sufficiently for passage.
 - This maneuver demonstrated that the method prevents "navigational deadlock" in situations where a purely translational or simplified geometry approach would fail.
- **Quantitative Validation:** The value of the Poisson safety function `h_QT` was maintained as positive throughout the majority of the 100-second experiment. This confirmed that the robot's 2D footprint remained within the safe configuration space, satisfying the safety requirements.
- **Impact of Reorientation:** The data confirmed that the robot actively maneuvered its heading angle, indicating that reorientation was an integral part of maintaining safety and achieving the navigation goal.

Overall, the experiments demonstrated that the extended Poisson safety functions, integrated with an MPC+CBF framework, enable legged robots to perform robust and efficient collision avoidance and navigation tasks. The approach's ability to incorporate real-time perception data, predict environmental changes, and account for complex robot geometries significantly contributed to its performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents several advancements that hold significance for the field of robotic autonomy and safety-critical control:

- **Enhanced Safety for Legged Robots:** The development of a geometry-aware predictive safety filter directly addresses a critical challenge for legged robots operating in complex and dynamically changing environments. By explicitly considering the robot's asymmetric geometry and orientation through Minkowski set operations, the method allows for more precise and less conservative collision avoidance, which is crucial for humanoid and quadrupedal platforms. This enables these robots to operate more safely in close proximity to obstacles or in human-populated spaces.

- **Novel Extension of Poisson Safety Functions:** The work significantly extends the theoretical framework and practical utility of Poisson safety functions. By reformulating the static Dirichlet problem into a parameterized moving boundary value problem, the method can now account for temporal changes in the environment. This represents a substantial improvement over previous static formulations, making Poisson safety functions applicable to truly dynamic scenarios where obstacles or the environment itself evolves.

- **Perception-to-Control Pipeline:** A key impact is the direct synthesis of Control Barrier Function (CBF) constraints from real-time perception data. This removes the need for *a priori* analytical models of the environment, which are often unavailable or insufficient in unstructured settings. The numerical approach, leveraging image segmentation and optical flow for occupancy mapping and boundary prediction, provides a robust pipeline from sensing to safety-critical control.

- **Integration of Predictive and Reactive Safety:** The combination of Model Predictive Control (MPC) with CBFs, underpinned by these extended Poisson safety functions, offers a powerful safety-critical control architecture. MPC provides anticipatory, optimal trajectory planning over a finite horizon, while CBFs ensure instantaneous, provable safety guarantees. This integration results in a system that can proactively avoid collisions while reacting dynamically to unforeseen changes.

- **Real-time Practicality:** The experimental demonstration on physical quadruped and humanoid robots highlights the real-time applicability and computational efficiency of the proposed architecture. The numerical PDE solution times (20-100 ms) and MPC update rates (100 Hz) suggest that the system is capable of operating effectively in dynamic scenarios requiring rapid decision-making.

- **Reduced Conservatism and Increased Maneuverability:** By providing geometry-aware safety constraints, the method enables robots to execute more efficient and less conservative maneuvers. The ability to reorient the robot to fit through narrow gaps or pivot to avoid dynamic obstacles (as shown in the experiments) demonstrates a higher degree of maneuverability than would be possible with simpler point-mass or spherical approximations. This can prevent "navigational deadlocks" and expand the operational envelope of robotic systems.

The potential impact of this research extends to various applications requiring robust and safe robotic autonomy, including industrial inspection, logistics, service robotics, and autonomous exploration. The capacity for robots to safely navigate complex, dynamic, and partially unknown environments, while accounting for their own physical geometry, contributes to the ongoing development of more capable and reliable autonomous systems.
