# ViPRA: Video Prediction for Robot Actions

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University and Skild AI developed ViPRA, a framework that enables continuous robot control by leveraging large-scale actionless videos. The system uses motion-centric latent actions and multimodal pretraining to achieve high success rates on complex manipulation tasks in both simulation and real-world environments, requiring minimal labeled data for adaptation and generating smooth, high-frequency actions.

## Problem

- Robot learning relies on extensive, costly expert demonstrations, limiting scalability and preventing the use of abundant actionless video data.
- Existing video prediction models and latent action approaches often lack the ability to directly translate visual understanding into precise, high-frequency continuous robot actions or struggle with generalization.
- Prior methods for robot learning from videos often incur high inference costs or fail to explicitly model fine-grained physical dynamics, hindering real-time dexterous control.

## Method

- **ViPRA** introduces a three-stage framework: (1) ==Latent Action Learning== from actionless videos, (2) ==Multimodal Pretraining== that integrates these latents with a video-language model, and (3) ==Continuous Adaptation via Flow Matching==.
- It extracts ==motion-centric latent actions== that encapsulate how an environment changes and jointly predicts future visual states alongside these latent actions to build a robust prior of scene dynamics.
- The framework adapts to continuous robot actions using a ==flow matching decoder==, which, combined with ==action chunking==, generates smooth, high-frequency (up to 22 Hz) control outputs from minimal labeled demonstrations.

## Results

- ViPRA achieved an average success rate of **69.8%** (discrete) and **62.5%** (continuous) on the SIMPLER benchmark, surpassing strong baselines like LAPA, OpenVLA, and UniVLA.
- On the LIBERO Long benchmark, ViPRA attained a **79%** success rate on long-horizon, multi-stage manipulation tasks, demonstrating effective transfer of motion-centric latent pretraining.
- In real-world single-arm manipulation, ViPRA-FM achieved an average success rate of **54.1%**, outperforming π0 and Scratch-FM, and showed effective transfer to challenging bimanual tasks with minimal finetuning data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Jointly predicting both future visual states ("what changes") and motion-centric latent actions ("how the scene evolves") during pretraining is crucial for learning grounded and transferable control priors.
> - Pretraining on a diverse corpus of human and robot videos, rather than solely robot data, enhances generalization capabilities across different robot embodiments.
> - Utilizing flow matching for continuous action generation, coupled with action chunking, provides stable and high-frequency control, essential for dexterous real-world manipulation, outperforming discrete action policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot learning is significantly impacted by the challenge of data acquisition. Training effective robot policies typically requires extensive datasets of expert demonstrations, which are costly, time-consuming to collect, and often limited to specific robot embodiments. In contrast, passive videos—ranging from human activity videos on platforms like YouTube to teleoperated robot logs—are abundant and capture diverse physical interactions, objects, and long-horizon behaviors that are difficult to replicate in controlled settings. However, a primary limitation of these vast video corpora is the general absence of corresponding action labels, which restricts their direct applicability to robot learning.

Recent advancements in video prediction models, such as those based on diffusion and generative architectures, have demonstrated a capacity to understand object dynamics and fine-grained physical interactions, beyond merely preserving high-level task semantics. This capability has opened avenues for leveraging unlabeled videos. The central question in this context is how to transform a video prediction model into a practical control policy for physical robots.

Prior work has explored several related directions:
- **Vision-Language-Action Models (VLAs)**: These models extend vision-language models by integrating imitation learning from action-labeled robot demonstrations. While effective in grounding language in visual semantics, they require substantial amounts of labeled action data, posing a scalability bottleneck. Many existing VLAs also primarily focus on semantic grounding and may lack explicit mechanisms for modeling physical dynamics or temporal structure in action generation.
- **Robot Learning from Videos**: Approaches in this category include visual planning methods that use generative video models to plan in visual or video-language spaces, subsequently relying on inverse dynamics models or pose tracking to translate predicted frames into actions. While suitable for long-horizon reasoning, these methods often incur high inference costs, which can limit their applicability for high-frequency, dexterous control. Other strategies involve using video models as sources of supervision or reward signals for policy training, though evaluations have often been confined to simulation or constrained real-world tasks. Further efforts leverage human videos for pretraining visual representations or extracting intermediate cues like affordances and interaction patterns, but these often depend on structured priors or explicit cue extraction, which can constrain scalability.
- **Latent Action Spaces**: This area aims to improve data efficiency by enabling learning from action-free videos through self-supervised methods. Recent works impose discrete information bottlenecks using vector quantization and predict these tokens during policy learning. Some methods train inverse dynamics models on limited labeled data and apply them to unlabeled videos, or abstract latent actions across embodiments. While these approaches can capture physical dynamics, they sometimes struggle with generalization to novel settings due to insufficient semantic grounding. Video-language models can offer this multimodal grounding, but existing implementations are often computationally intensive and slow for real-time inference.

ViPRA positions itself within this landscape by seeking to address the limitations of existing methods. It aims to bridge the gap between large-scale actionless video data and precise robot control by combining motion-centric latent actions, semantic grounding from a video-language model, and an efficient continuous control adaptation mechanism. Unlike prior latent action approaches that primarily treat pretraining as autoregressive policy learning in latent space, ViPRA explicitly models both future visual states ("what changes") and fine-grained motion-centric latent actions ("how the scene evolves"). This joint prediction, coupled with optical flow consistency and a flow matching decoder for continuous control, is designed to enhance data efficiency, enable cross-embodiment generalization, and support high-frequency control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to investigate whether a video prediction model can be effectively transformed into a control policy for physical robots. This question is motivated by the disparity between the high cost and labor intensity of collecting labeled robot demonstrations and the vast availability of unlabeled video data, encompassing both human activities and teleoperated robot operations. These unlabeled videos are rich in information regarding physical interactions, object dynamics, and complex long-horizon behaviors.

The paper outlines several specific objectives to address this core question:

- **Learning Continuous Robot Control from Actionless Videos**: To develop a scalable pretraining-finetuning framework that enables robots to learn continuous control policies directly from videos that lack explicit action labels. This aims to overcome the "action annotation bottleneck" prevalent in robot learning.
- **Avoiding Expensive Action Annotation**: To reduce the reliance on costly, manually annotated action trajectories by primarily leveraging passive, unlabeled video data during the foundational learning phases.
- **Supporting Generalization Across Embodiments**: To learn a generalist policy that can transfer knowledge between different robot platforms and even from human demonstrations to robots. This is facilitated by training on diverse human and robot videos during pretraining.
- **Enabling Smooth, High-Frequency Continuous Control**: To design a control system capable of generating smooth, continuous robot actions at high frequencies (up to 22 Hz), crucial for dexterous and responsive physical manipulation, through mechanisms like chunked action decoding.
- **Explicitly Modeling Scene Dynamics**: To develop a framework that not only predicts future visual observations ("what happens next") but also the underlying motion ("how the scene evolves") through motion-centric latent actions. This approach contrasts with prior latent action methods that might treat pretraining solely as policy learning in a latent space without explicit video prediction.
- **Integrating Semantic Grounding with Physical Dynamics**: To combine the semantic understanding derived from video-language models with the capture of fine-grained physical dynamics through latent actions, thereby creating a robust prior for effective robot control.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ViPRA proposes a three-stage framework: (1) Latent Action Learning, (2) Multimodal Pretraining with Latent Actions, and (3) Continuous Adaptation via Flow Matching.

#### 3.1. Latent Action Learning from Actionless Videos

This initial phase focuses on extracting motion-centric latent actions from unlabeled human and robot videos.
- **Objective**: To learn discrete latent action sequences `z_t:t+L-1` that encapsulate the dynamics of how an environment changes, from an observation sequence `o_t:t+L`. Each `z_k` is composed of `N_latent` discrete tokens, quantized from a shared codebook `C` of size 8.
- **Inverse Dynamics Encoder (`I_β`)**: This component predicts latent action token `z_t` by conditioning on the full observation sequence `o_t:t+L`. Its non-causal design allows `z_k` to integrate both past and future visual context, enabling it to distinguish nuanced motion intents (e.g., distinguishing a pickup from a putdown). A DINOv2-initialized spatial encoder and spatio-temporal transformer layers are used for feature extraction. Noise-Substitution Vector Quantization (NSVQ) discretizes these features.
- **Forward Decoder (`F_α`)**: This module reconstructs the future frame `ˆo_t+k+1` given the history of observations `o_t:t+k` and latent actions `z_t:t+k`. This causal design ensures that the latent actions contain sufficient information to explain scene dynamics.
- **Loss Functions**: The model is optimized using three components:
 - **`L_rec`**: L1 pixel reconstruction loss for accurate frame prediction.
 - **`L_LPIPS`**: Perceptual loss to capture high-level structural similarity.
 - **`L_flow`**: Optical flow consistency loss, activated after a warm-up period (`α_flow`), which encourages predicted frames to exhibit motion patterns consistent with ground truth, promoting temporally coherent dynamics. It measures the L1 difference between optical flow fields of predicted and ground-truth frame pairs.
- **Data**: The training corpus consists of 198k human videos from Something-Something v2 and a subset of actionless robot videos from the OpenX dataset (87k Fractal, 25.4k BridgeV2, 85.6k Kuka videos).

#### 3.2. Pretraining Multimodal Video Model with Latent Actions

This stage integrates the learned latent actions with a powerful multimodal video prediction model to establish semantic grounding for temporal dynamics.
- **Base Model**: LWM-Chat-1M (Liu et al., 2024), an instruction-tuned video-language model `G_θ`, is used as the backbone. LWM's VQ-VAE encoder `E_VQ` tokenizes visual frames into discrete visual tokens `x_k`.
- **Augmentation**: Two new modules are added:
 - **Latent Action Embedding head (`E_ϕ`)**: Maps each discrete latent token `z_i_t` to a `d_z`-dimensional vector `˜z_i_t` in the model's token space.
 - **Latent Action Token Decoder (`H_ψ`)**: A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) that autoregressively predicts the next latent token `ˆz_i+1_t` from the transformer's hidden state, allowing for sequential latent action generation.
- **Joint Prediction Objective**: Given past observations `(o_t-1, o_t)` and a task description `c`, the model jointly predicts a future frame `o_t+H` (H steps ahead) and the corresponding latent action sequence `z_t:t+H-1`. This multi-step prediction horizon is intended to encourage the learning of meaningful and distinct scene changes.
- **Loss Function**: The pretraining objective `L_pretrain` combines two cross-entropy losses:
 - **`L_img`**: For predicting future visual tokens `ˆx_t+H` against ground-truth `x_t+H`.
 - **`L_act`**: For autoregressively predicting latent action tokens `ˆz_i_k` against ground-truth `z_i_k`. Teacher forcing is applied to both.

#### 3.3. Continuous Adaptation

This final phase adapts the pretrained model to output continuous robot actions with precision and smoothness, using a minimal set of labeled demonstrations.
- **Method**: Flow Matching (Lipman et al., 2022) is employed for continuous action generation.
- **Augmentation**: The video model `G_θ` is augmented with:
 - **Action Encoder (`E_γ`)**: Embeds continuous noisy actions `u_s` (interpolated between Gaussian noise and target action) into the model's token space.
 - **Flow Decoder (`H_η`)**: Predicts a flow field over the action chunk.
- **Training Process**:
 1. A target action sequence `a_t:t+H-1` is sampled from expert demonstrations.
 2. A noise sample `u_0` is drawn, and a time-interpolated noisy action `u_s` is created using `s ∼ Beta(1.5, 1.0)`.
 3. `u_s` is encoded by `E_γ` along with `s`, and this embedding is passed into the transformer along with VQ-encoded image tokens (`x_t-1, x_t`) and language prompt `c`.
 4. The model predicts a flow field `ˆg = H_η(G_θ(...))`.
 5. **Loss Function**: `L_FM` is minimized, which is the L2 distance between the true flow direction (`a_t:t+H-1 - u_0`) and the predicted velocity scaled by `(1-s) · ˆg`.
- **Inference**: Given visual history and task instruction, the continuous action chunk `a_t:t+H-1` is iteratively solved using forward Euler integration (10 steps with `Δs = 0.1`) of the predicted flow field from `s=0` to `s=1`. This amortizes inference latency through action chunking (producing multiple low-level actions in one pass) and enables high-frequency control.
- **Data**: Only 100 to 200 teleoperated demonstrations are used for finetuning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of ViPRA covered both simulated and real-world environments, addressing questions regarding the leverage of physical dynamics and semantic understanding, data efficiency, effectiveness of multimodal pretraining, and adaptation to high-frequency continuous control.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **SIMPLER Benchmark**: ViPRA demonstrated substantial performance gains.
 - In the discrete setting, **ViPRA-AR** achieved an average success rate of **69.8%**, surpassing LAPA (53.1%), OpenVLA (38.6%), and VPT (51.0%). It showed strong performance on precision-heavy tasks like StackG2Y (66.7%) and Eggplant2Bask (83.3%).
 - In the continuous setting, **ViPRA-FM** achieved an average success rate of **62.5%**, outperforming Scratch-FM (41.7%), π0 (27.1%), and UniVLA (42.7%).
 - ViPRA also exhibited better conversion of grasps into full task completion compared to baselines, indicating more stable transport and release.
 - UniPI and VPT, which rely on inverse dynamics models or video diffusion models, showed limited gains or divergence, suggesting sensitivity to environment shifts compared to ViPRA’s joint prediction approach.
- **LIBERO Long Benchmark**: On long-horizon, multi-stage manipulation tasks, ViPRA achieved a **79% success rate**, notably higher than OpenVLA (54%) and π0-FAST (60%), and competitive with UVA (90%). This demonstrated effective transfer of motion-centric latent pretraining to complex, sequential tasks, although it showed some limitations with precision grasps for cylindrical objects due to delta-EEF drift.

#### 4.2. Real World Manipulation Results

- **Single-Arm Tasks**: On a bimanual Franka Panda setup, evaluating three multi-instruction tasks (Cover-Object, Pick-Place, Stack-Cups) with a single arm, **ViPRA-FM** achieved an average success rate of **54.1%**. This outperformed π0 (40.1%) and Scratch-FM (23.8%), despite using fewer labeled demonstrations for finetuning.
 - The policy demonstrated robust retry behavior following failed grasp attempts, particularly in tasks like Cover-Object.
 - Discrete policies were excluded from real-world evaluation due to instability and safety concerns (abrupt spikes under physical noise triggering safety stops).
- **Bimanual Tasks**: ViPRA-FM also showed effective transfer to challenging dual-arm settings, such as Place-in-Bowl and Mix-with-Whisk.
 - In Place-in-Bowl, it completed 10 out of 18 trials, with failures primarily linked to gripper limitations rather than coordination.
 - In Mix-with-Whisk, it completed 8 out of 12 trials, demonstrating coordinated tool use and sustained curved motions. This suggests that the motion-centric latent pretraining provides embodiment-agnostic priors suitable for adaptation to multi-arm scenarios with minimal finetuning.

#### 4.3. Ablations and Analysis

- **Effect of Future State and Latent Prediction**: Ablation studies confirmed that both future state prediction (FS) and latent action prediction (L) are critical. Adding state prediction to a 1-step latent baseline (ViPRA–AC, 59.2% AR) improved performance over latent-only (LAPA, 53.1%). Removing state prediction from the full model (ViPRA–SP2) caused significant drops (69.8% to 59.4% AR, 62.5% to 53.2% FM). A state-only variant (ViPRA–LA, 60.7%) was comparable to ViPRA–AC but inferior to the full model, indicating that state prediction alone is insufficient. Joint prediction during pretraining was found to be crucial, as adding it during finetuning (ViPRA+SP3) degraded performance due to compounding errors.
- **Effect of Action Chunking**: Removing action chunking (ViPRA–AC) reduced performance (59.2% AR, 44.8% FM), indicating its importance for capturing fine temporal dynamics. The combination of chunking and future state prediction yielded the best results. Action chunking also enabled high-frequency control, with the flow matching decoder running at 1.95 Hz per chunk, supporting effective control rates up to 22 Hz on hardware with KV caching.
- **Action Smoothness**: Comparison with discrete policies like LAPA revealed that ViPRA-FM's flow matching decoder produces smooth, continuous trajectories, which is essential for real-world deployment. LAPA's discrete outputs exhibited sharp, localized spikes, particularly during contact events, which can lead to instability and trigger safety mechanisms on physical robots.
- **Latent Action Analysis**: Cross-video rollouts demonstrated that latent actions captured transferable, dynamics-aware semantics. Positional codebook analysis showed that both the codebook entry and its position within the sequence encoded structured information about motion direction and dynamics. The optical flow consistency loss (`L_flow`) was found to be crucial for encouraging the latent space to encode motion dynamics rather than just background appearance, as its removal increased perplexity, entropy, and reduced action probe accuracy.
- **Human and Robot Data Composition**: Co-training on both human and robot videos yielded the best results (79% success, 0.84 Action MSE on LIBERO-10), surpassing robot-only (72% success, 0.91 MSE) and human-only (69% success, 0.99 MSE) training. This suggests that human videos contribute to motion diversity, while robot videos ground the latents in robot-specific dynamics.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ViPRA represents an advancement in robot learning by demonstrating a framework that effectively bridges the gap between large-scale actionless video data and precise, continuous robot control. Its primary significance lies in the following contributions:

- **Leveraging Unlabeled Data at Scale**: The method enables robots to learn from abundant actionless human and robot videos, drastically reducing the reliance on costly, meticulously labeled robot demonstrations. This scalability has the potential to accelerate the development of more capable and generalist robot policies.
- **Unified Modeling of "What" and "How"**: By jointly predicting future visual observations ("what changes") and motion-centric latent actions ("how the scene evolves"), ViPRA constructs a richer, more grounded understanding of scene dynamics. This integrated approach, in contrast to methods focusing solely on latent action prediction or inverse dynamics, appears to yield more robust and transferable control priors.
- **Cross-Embodiment Generalization**: The pretraining on diverse human and robot videos facilitates the learning of embodiment-agnostic priors. This enables effective transfer to different robot platforms, including bimanual setups, with minimal task-specific finetuning, expanding the applicability of learned policies.
- **High-Frequency, Smooth Continuous Control**: The integration of flow matching with action chunking provides a mechanism for generating smooth, continuous robot actions at high frequencies (up to 22 Hz effective control rate). This precision and responsiveness are critical for dexterous manipulation tasks in real-world environments, where discrete action policies often exhibit instability.
- **Empirical Performance Improvements**: ViPRA demonstrates consistent and notable empirical gains over strong baselines in both simulation benchmarks (SIMPLER and LIBERO Long) and real-world manipulation tasks, validating the effectiveness of its architectural and training design choices.

**Potential Impact:**
The ability to learn from passive, unlabeled videos could transform how robot skills are acquired, making robot learning more scalable and accessible. It could lead to robots capable of performing a wider range of tasks in unstructured environments by drawing on the vast amount of visual information available online. The framework's emphasis on generalizable dynamics and semantic understanding lays groundwork for more adaptive and intelligent robotic systems.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The paper also identifies several limitations and areas for future research:
- **Embodiment and Dexterity**: While ViPRA generalizes to different arm configurations, extending it to more dexterous domains (e.g., multi-fingered hands, humanoids) or scenarios requiring precise contact dynamics and inter-arm coordination remains a challenge. Incorporating modalities like tactile feedback or learning embodiment-specific adapters could enhance these capabilities.
- **Data Scope and Generalization**: Despite using large-scale videos, the diversity of current datasets may still be limited. Expanding to richer ego-centric datasets with human-hand interactions, tool use, or navigation could improve embodiment invariance and temporal grounding. Integrating additional sensing modalities (wrist cameras, proprioception, depth) could further strengthen perception-control alignment.
- **Scaling Behavior and Data Efficiency**: Further research is needed to characterize the scaling behavior of passive video pretraining and to determine the optimal balance between unlabeled video data and labeled demonstrations for efficient large-scale robot learning.
- **Predictive Modeling for Reinforcement Learning**: The latent action decoder could be further interpreted and utilized as a predictive world model. This could enable sampling of latent actions to generate visual rollouts, supporting reinforcement learning-based alignment or planning at test time with VLM-based reward models.
