# Filling the Pareto-Optimal Front for Affordance Segmentation on Embedded Devices Using RGB-D Cameras

> [!summary] Summary
> Two distinct strategies enable efficient affordance segmentation on embedded devices using RGB-D cameras, delivering real-time inference at 14-16 FPS on a Jetson Nano with system power consumption below 11W. These approaches improved segmentation accuracy by up to 31.6% for certain objects compared to RGB-only methods, especially under suboptimal visual conditions.

## Problem

- Deploying complex Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) for Affordance Segmentation (AS) on resource-constrained portable wearable devices is challenging due to strict limits on weight, size, and energy consumption.
- Existing "tinyML" approaches often lack robustness and generalization for real-world AS scenarios, while high-performance solutions are too demanding for embedded use.
- Effectively integrating depth information with RGB data for AS on embedded devices, while managing increased computational complexity, remains an underexplored area.

## Method

- Implemented a reformulated ==Hardware-Aware Neural Architecture Search (HW-NAS)== to automatically design RGB-D DNNs with multi-branch processing and multi-resolution feature aggregation, optimized for embedded constraints.
- Developed a fine-tuning approach that adapts existing pre-trained RGB networks for RGB-D input by adding a new convolutional layer to process concatenated RGB and depth channels, minimizing additional computational cost.
- Utilized an ==evolutionary algorithm== for HW-NAS to identify Pareto-optimal architectures by balancing segmentation accuracy against hardware requirements like FLOPs, model size, and inference time.

## Results

- RGB-D inputs consistently improved affordance segmentation accuracy, with HW-NAS RGB-D increasing 'Grasp'/'Don't Grasp' accuracy by **2.8%** and fine-tuned RGB-D by **2.6-3.6%**, leading to gains up to **31.6%** for specific objects.
- The proposed models successfully achieved real-time inference (**14-16 FPS** for optimized versions) on a Jetson Nano with total system power consumption under **11W**, compatible with portable battery operation.
- Both HW-NAS and fine-tuning approaches generated solutions that frequently resided on or near the Pareto optimal front, effectively balancing segmentation performance with embedded hardware requirements (FLOPs, memory).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Depth information significantly improves affordance segmentation generalization, especially under visually challenging conditions, and can be beneficial even for objects without complex 3D geometry.
> - Hardware-Aware Neural Architecture Search (HW-NAS) can systematically generate Pareto-optimal RGB-D DNN architectures that effectively balance segmentation accuracy with tight hardware constraints.
> - A simple fine-tuning method can efficiently adapt pre-trained RGB networks to integrate depth information with negligible increases in computational overhead, offering a practical alternative to full NAS.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Wearable robots, including prosthetics and robotic arms for industrial tasks, rely on semi-autonomous control to reduce user effort and enhance overall experience. A critical component for achieving such autonomy is advanced sensing capabilities, with non-contact sensing (e.g., cameras and depth sensors) being particularly important for tasks like grasping. The processing of these sensory signals on-site to extract high-level information is crucial for control pipelines.

Affordance Segmentation (AS) is identified as a valuable input method for these control pipelines. AS involves partitioning an object into its functional parts, enabling fine-grained control strategies, especially for objects with complex geometries. Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have emerged as a viable approach for the complexity of AS. However, deploying DNNs on portable wearable devices presents a significant challenge due to stringent constraints on weight, size, and energy consumption of embedded electronic systems.

Existing solutions for DNN deployment on constrained hardware generally fall into two categories: "tinyML" paradigms for highly resource-limited microcontrollers, and high-performance accelerators. The paper notes that mid-range, application-specific DNN architectures, which could effectively address real-life scenarios by balancing resilience to environmental variations (framing, background, illumination) with acceptable hardware requirements for portable devices, remain relatively underexplored.

Depth sensors offer geometric information that can complement RGB data, potentially providing insights into object shape critical for AS. Prior studies on integrating depth data with RGB for AS suggested that a straightforward combination might yield marginal improvements, but hinted that specific processing could make depth information beneficial. The increased computational complexity associated with processing depth data necessitates efficient design strategies compatible with evolving hardware accelerators. Hardware-aware Neural Architecture Search (HW-NAS) is a methodology that tailors network architectures to target hardware platforms, typically driving the design of tiny, hardware-efficient DNN models. This work positions itself within the context of leveraging depth information and HW-NAS to develop efficient AS solutions for embedded accelerators, addressing the gap in mid-range hardware-efficient solutions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop efficient near-sensor inference strategies for affordance segmentation on portable embedded devices using RGB-D data. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and aims:

- **Enhancing Affordance Segmentation with Depth Information:** The paper aims to effectively integrate depth (D) information with conventional RGB data to improve the generalization performance of AS. This is motivated by the understanding that while depth sensors provide valuable geometric cues, their straightforward integration might not yield optimal results, requiring dedicated processing chains. The objective is to harness the full potential of depth data to provide robust insights into object shapes for more effective control pipelines in wearable robots.

- **Addressing Hardware Constraints of Wearable Devices:** A core motivation is to overcome the significant challenges associated with deploying complex DNNs on resource-constrained portable hardware. Wearable robots demand minimal weight, size, and energy consumption, which often limit the computational power available. The research aims to design DNNs that achieve real-time performance within a power budget compatible with standard portable batteries (e.g., smartphone batteries), thus enabling practical deployment.

- **Generating Pareto-Optimal Solutions:** The work seeks to generate solutions that identify the Pareto optimal front, effectively balancing generalization performance (accuracy in AS) with critical hardware requirements (model size and computational cost, such as FLOPs). This involves developing design strategies that allow for suitable trade-offs between accuracy and resource usage, moving beyond approaches that prioritize either high accuracy at high cost or extreme efficiency at significant accuracy loss.

- **Developing Novel Hardware-Aware Design Strategies:** To achieve the aforementioned balance, the paper proposes two distinct yet complementary design strategies:
 - **Reformulated Hardware-Aware Neural Architecture Search (HW-NAS):** The motivation here is to automate the design of specialized DNN architectures that are inherently optimized for RGB-D inputs and specific hardware constraints. This includes developing a novel search space that integrates depth information and is designed to scale effectively when hardware constraints are relaxed, allowing for multi-resolution feature extraction and multi-branch architectures.
 - **Dedicated Fine-Tuning Approach:** Recognizing the computational cost and data requirements of full HW-NAS, an alternative motivation is to provide a more accessible method for integrating depth. This approach aims to extend existing pre-trained, optimized RGB networks to handle RGB-D inputs with minimal additional computational overhead, thereby leveraging established models and potentially reducing training time and data needs.

- **Overcoming Limitations of Existing "Tiny-like" Approaches:** The research is motivated by the observation that many existing tiny models, while efficient, often fail to tackle critical scenarios in AS due to severe hardware constraints leading to reduced robustness and generalization. The objective is to provide solutions that offer improved resilience to variations in framing, background, and illumination, making them more robust for real-world applications in wearable robotics.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes two distinct end-to-end pipeline design strategies for processing RGB-D inputs to generate affordance maps for control logic on small-size, portable circuitry.

**A. HW-NAS for Embedded AS**
This approach aims to automatically design a DNN architecture ($a^*$) by searching a defined space ($A$) under specified constraints, primarily inference time ($\phi(a) < Th$) and memory footprint (flash and RAM).

1. **Search Space Definition ($A$)**:
 - **Multi-branch Architecture**: The search space is designed to handle RGB-D inputs with separate processing branches for RGB and depth (D) data, which then merge into a CNN-backbone module.
 - **Encoder Block**: The base building block for encoders is a refined MobileNetV3 block, specifically excluding Squeeze-and-Excite layers. It consists of a sequence of Conv2D, BatchNormalization, Activation, DepthwiseConv2D, BatchNormalization, Activation, and two Conv2D layers, with a skip connection from the input to the output of the block (Table I). The search space allows stacking these blocks to optimize RGB and D backbone structures independently.
 - **Backbone Structure**: The RGB and D branches process inputs separately. The RGB-D backbone (purple block in Fig. 1a) combines these outputs, typically starting with a concatenation layer, and includes a stack of decoder blocks.
 - **Aggregator Network**: This module (green block in Fig. 1a) functions as a stack of decoder blocks, each composed of a Conv2DTranspose layer followed by a non-linearity. It combines the output of the RGB-D backbone with intermediate representations extracted from multiple layers of the architecture, allowing for multi-resolution feature sets via skip connections (green arrows). A connection to the topmost block of the RGB-D backbone is always included. The number of kernels and the activation function's shape are tunable parameters.
2. **Optimization Algorithm**: A conventional evolutionary approach is employed. The iterative algorithm generates candidate architectures ('children') by applying random mutations to a 'parent' architecture. Each child is trained, and the most promising one (based on a predefined metric balancing generalization performance and hardware cost) is selected as the new parent. This process continues until a stopping criterion is met.
3. **Constraints**: The inference time ($\phi(a)$) on the target device is estimated, and architectures are constrained to meet a real-time threshold ($Th$). The FLOPS threshold for NAS was set to 528 KFLOPS, matching MobileNetV3 inference on a 128x128 image.

**B. Fine-tuning-based Approach**
This alternative strategy addresses the computational cost and data demands of HW-NAS by adapting pre-trained RGB networks to RGB-D inputs.

1. **Input Preprocessing Layer**: RGB and D channels are concatenated to form a four-channel tensor $X \in R^{W \times H \times 4}$. This tensor then passes through a new convolutional layer with 3 filters and a stride of 1, producing an output tensor $X' \in R^{W \times H \times 3}$.
2. **Leveraging Pre-trained Networks**: The $X'$ tensor is then fed directly into an existing architecture optimized for RGB data (e.g., MobileNetV3 pre-trained on ImageNet).
3. **Fine-tuning**: The filter values of the newly introduced convolutional layer are learned through conventional backpropagation, while preserving the pre-existing weights of the main RGB architecture. This approach aims to integrate depth information with minimal impact on the original network configuration, assuming structural similarities between depth maps and RGB data allow for effective transfer of low-level features.

**C. Experimental Setup**

- **Software**: Python, TensorFlow, and Keras libraries.
- **Datasets**:
 - **UMD**: 28,843 RGB-D images across 7 object categories, varying framing angles.
 - **IIT**: 8,835 images with variations in framing, lighting, occlusion, and resolution.
 - **Data Preparation**: For both datasets, foreground objects were isolated using segmentation masks and contained within the smallest square bounding box. The task was a three-class, pixel-wise classification ('grasp,' 'do not grasp,' 'background').
- **Training**:
 - NAS ran for 100 generations. Candidate children were trained for 10 epochs with early stopping. The final selected NAS architecture was re-trained for 100 epochs with an initial learning rate of $10^{-3}$, learning rate reduction on plateau, and early stopping.
 - For fine-tuning, MobileNetV3 (pre-trained on ImageNet) was used as the backbone, combined with the segmentation head described in. Training settings were identical to the re-training of the NAS-selected architecture.
- **Evaluation Metrics**: Generalization performance was assessed using a held-out test set. Hardware requirements were estimated using parameters and FLOPs.
- **Deployment Prototype**: A Jetson Nano board interfaced with a RealSense RGB-D camera was used for real-time inference and energy profiling. Models were tested in plain TensorFlow and optimized versions (using TRT optimizer with float16 quantization) under 5W and 10W power configurations. Memory usage was monitored via system utilities and Tracemalloc. Power consumption was measured with a wattmeter.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. Efficient Usage of Depth Information**
Experiments on the UMD dataset demonstrated that the inclusion of depth information consistently improved generalization performance for both proposed approaches.
- **HW-NAS**: RGB-D input led to a 2.8% increase in accuracy for both 'Grasp' and 'Don't Grasp' classes compared to RGB-only.
- **Fine-tuning**: The RGB-D network outperformed the RGB baseline by 2.6% for 'Grasp' and 3.6% for 'Don't Grasp', with a negligible 0.3% deterioration for the 'Background' class.
Regarding computational requirements, the NAS RGB-D model showed increased parameters (more than doubled) and FLOPs (from 257.1M to 393.2M) compared to NAS RGB, while staying within the set optimization threshold. The fine-tuned RGB-D model exhibited only a minor increase (0.1%) in FLOPs and negligible change in parameters compared to its RGB counterpart, indicating minimal additional computational cost.

**B. Detailed Analysis of Generated Architectures**
An inspection of the HW-NAS-generated architectures revealed design choices:
- **RGB Architecture (Table III)**: Featured skip connections from the first input block to two of the three decoders, which is consistent with maintaining geometric information in segmentation. It utilized relatively large kernel sizes (up to 9x9) and a stride of 1 in most blocks, likely due to the small input resolution.
- **RGB-D Architecture (Table IV)**: Included two blocks for RGB input and one for depth input before early merging of information. This was followed by a long branch (eight blocks) processing the aggregated information, aligning with the fine-tuning approach's immediate concatenation of RGB-D inputs. Skip connections to early layers were also present.

**C. Comparison with State-of-the-Art**
The proposed models were compared against seven baseline architectures.
- **Performance Comparison**: Proposed RGB-D models consistently outperformed "tiny" models (TinySeg, HW-0.5, HW-1s). While larger models (EfficientNetB0, VGG16) and transformer-based models (SegFormer MiT-B0) often achieved higher absolute accuracy, depth information significantly closed this gap for the proposed models on the UMD dataset.
- **Dataset Specifics**: On the more challenging IIT dataset (variations in background, illumination, occlusion), the fine-tuning approach generally surpassed the NAS-based approach. The benefit of depth information on accuracy was less pronounced on IIT, potentially due to the need for more robust features that pre-trained large models (from ImageNet) provided.
- **Pareto Optimal Front**: On the UMD dataset, both proposed RGB-D models (especially NAS-generated) frequently resided on the Pareto optimal front for average classwise accuracy and the 'Grasp' class, demonstrating an effective balance between accuracy and computational requirements. The Custom-MobileNetV3 performed better for the 'Don't Grasp' class. On the IIT dataset, the proposed RGB-D models consistently occupied or were close to the Pareto optimal front.

**D. Impact of Input Size on Performance**
Reducing input resolution significantly lowered MFLOPS. The NAS RGB-D model achieved an average accuracy above 0.9 even with tight MFLOPS constraints, and its accuracy increased with higher MFLOPS, indicating effective exploitation of the depth channel. In contrast, the NAS RGB model's average accuracy remained nearly independent of FLOPs.

**E. Output Analysis**
Visual analysis and quantitative results on the UMD test set showed that depth information improved average classification accuracy for 12 out of 16 objects, with substantial gains in 7 cases (e.g., +31.6% for 'scoop'). Depth proved particularly crucial when lighting and color conditions were suboptimal or when object colors matched the background. Even for some flat objects, depth information was beneficial. A synthetic test set generated with Unreal Engine 5 also showed an 11.5% improvement in average accuracy with depth, which reduced to 4.6% after data augmentation, confirming depth's role in generalization to unseen objects.

**F. Deployment on Embedded Accelerators**
The two proposed RGB-D models were deployed on a Jetson Nano with a RealSense RGB-D camera.
- **Frame Rate (Table V)**: Optimized versions (TRT, float16 quantization) significantly reduced inference time. NAS opt achieved 15-16 FPS at 10W (0.06 seconds per frame), and Mob3 opt achieved 14-15 FPS at 10W (0.07 seconds per frame), supporting real-time operations.
- **Memory Analysis (Table VI)**: NAS architectures consistently used less RAM (approx. 300 MB average Python memory) compared to MOB3 (approx. 400 MB). Optimized versions used slightly more memory due to the TRT engine. Total system RAM usage was over 2.5 GB due to the operating system and background processes.
- **Power Consumption (Table VII)**: The average power consumption of the entire system (Jetson + camera) remained below 11 W in all configurations, with many scenarios around 7-8W. This consumption profile is compatible with standard portable lithium batteries, providing an estimated 3.5 hours of operation with a 5000 mAh smartphone battery.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents practical and efficient solutions for affordance segmentation on embedded devices, with implications for wearable robotics and human-robot interaction.

- **Enabling Robust Semi-Autonomous Control**: By demonstrating that RGB-D sensors significantly enhance the generalization performance of affordance segmentation models, especially in visually challenging conditions, the work contributes to the development of more robust and reliable semi-autonomous control pipelines for wearable robots. This could translate to improved functionality and user experience in applications like prosthetics and industrial exoskeletons, reducing cognitive load on users.

- **Optimized Hardware-Software Co-Design**: The proposed HW-NAS framework provides a systematic method for designing lightweight DNNs specifically tailored for RGB-D inputs and embedded accelerators. This approach generates architectures that occupy the Pareto optimal front, effectively balancing model accuracy with hardware constraints such as FLOPs and parameter count. This capability is crucial for the deployment of intelligent functionalities on resource-constrained devices, offering a pathway for developing efficient solutions without extensive manual engineering.

- **Practical Deployment on Portable Devices**: The successful deployment and validation on a Jetson Nano and RealSense RGB-D camera prototype confirm the feasibility of real-time affordance segmentation within acceptable power and memory budgets. The achieved frame rates (14-16 FPS) and power consumption (below 11W) are compatible with portable battery operation, making these solutions suitable for actual wearable applications.

- **Flexible Design Strategies**: The provision of two distinct design strategies—a comprehensive HW-NAS and a more lightweight fine-tuning approach—offers flexibility depending on computational resources and data availability. The fine-tuning method allows for the efficient reuse of existing pre-trained RGB-based networks, accelerating development and deployment in scenarios where extensive NAS training is not feasible.

- **Advancement in Sensing for Robotics**: The findings challenge some conventional assumptions about the utility of depth data, showing its benefits even for objects not typically considered to have complex 3D structures. This broadens the understanding of how depth information can be leveraged for perception tasks in robotics.

- **Future Research Directions**: The work lays groundwork for future advancements, including the integration of object localization capabilities for simplified user interaction, the extension of these techniques to transformer models, and the crucial step of conducting clinical trials to quantify user-perceived benefits in real-world semi-autonomous systems.
