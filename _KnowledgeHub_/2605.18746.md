# ESI-Bench: Towards Embodied Spatial Intelligence that Closes the Perception-Action Loop

> [!summary] Summary
> The ESI-BENCH benchmark introduces a new paradigm for evaluating embodied spatial intelligence, requiring AI agents to actively interact with a simulated environment to acquire information and resolve perceptual ambiguities. Evaluations show that current multimodal large language models (MLLMs) achieve significant accuracy gains through active exploration but exhibit 'action blindness' and a 'metacognitive gap' compared to human performance.

## Problem

- Existing spatial intelligence benchmarks primarily rely on passive observation, assuming all necessary information is pre-given.
- Real-world spatial understanding often requires uncovering hidden physical properties and latent scene states that cannot be resolved from static, pre-provided visual data.
- Current AI models, including MLLMs, lack frameworks to evaluate their ability to actively acquire information by interacting with an environment.

## Method

- ESI-BENCH, a new benchmark built on the OmniGibson simulator, features 3,081 tasks across 10 categories requiring active interaction.
- Agents must employ high-level discrete actions (locomotion, perception, manipulation) to resolve spatial ambiguities and uncover hidden information within the environment.
- The benchmark's tasks are grounded in cognitive science, assessing abilities like object representation, layout, number representation, and agent-driven actions, specifically when passive sensing is insufficient.

## Results

- Active exploration yielded substantial accuracy improvements for models; for instance, Gemini 3.1's performance on View Hallucination increased from **39.9%** (Passive Single-View) to **68.1%**.
- Providing optimal ground-truth views significantly boosted MLLM accuracy (e.g., GPT-5 on Rigid Containment improved from **42.5%** to **95.0%**), indicating action selection, not perception, is a primary bottleneck.
- Humans dramatically outperformed MLLMs in active tasks (e.g., **88.3%** vs. **64.2%** for Physical Contact), attributed to more diverse evidence gathering and belief revision strategies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Active exploration by agents leads to significant accuracy gains over passive observation, demonstrating emergent spatial strategies for resolving ambiguities.
> - Current MLLMs exhibit 'action blindness,' where the inability to select informative actions is a greater limitation than their perceptual capacity for many tasks.
> - A substantial 'metacognitive gap' separates human and MLLM performance in active settings, with humans showing greater epistemic caution and belief revision.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the field of artificial intelligence, specifically at the intersection of computer vision, natural language processing, and embodied agents. The paper addresses a limitation in existing benchmarks for spatial intelligence, which often assume a passive observer model. Traditional benchmarks like VSR, BLINK, 3DSRBench, VSI-Bench, and MMSI-Bench primarily evaluate spatial reasoning from fixed, pre-given observations, such as single images, multiple views, or videos. While some recent benchmarks, including MindCube, PhysBench, and CausalSpatial, have begun to incorporate reasoning about latent physical structures or object dynamics from partial observations, the observation acquisition process itself typically remains fixed. ESI-BENCH distinguishes itself by explicitly requiring agents to actively acquire information through interaction within a simulated environment.

Current multimodal large language models (MLLMs) have shown improvements in spatial understanding, but largely operate under the paradigm of fixed inputs. Approaches such as SpatialVLM, SpatialBot, SpatialRGPT, Spatial-MLLM, and VLM-3R inject 3D priors into 2D backbones or use depth-oriented question answering. Reasoning-based methods like SpatialCoT and VILASR focus on enhancing inference over given visual inputs. ESI-BENCH challenges these models by necessitating that they select the observations needed to solve a task, rather than merely processing provided data.

Furthermore, ESI-BENCH contributes to the growing area of embodied AI, which evaluates models as agents situated in interactive environments. Existing embodied benchmarks, including EmbodiedBench, EmbodiedEval, OpenEQA, and EXPRESS-Bench, assess capabilities like navigation, interaction, and embodied question answering. Works like EmbSpatial-Bench and ESPIRE diagnose egocentric spatial reasoning, and CHAIN evaluates closed-loop physical reasoning in specific tasks such as mechanical puzzles. ESI-BENCH complements these efforts by offering a broader suite of embodied spatial faculties, encompassing object representation, layout and geometry, number representation, physical reasoning, and agent-driven actions. A key distinction is its focus on "hidden" states—properties like occlusion, containment, transparency, reflection, and unobserved scene changes—that cannot be resolved from passive sensing alone and require an agent to actively close the perception-action loop. By doing so, ESI-BENCH aims to push research towards agents that strategically interact with their environment to gain information, rather than passively interpreting static data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to advance the evaluation of spatial intelligence in AI by moving beyond passive observation to an active, embodied paradigm. The motivation stems from a cognitive science perspective that characterizes perception as perceptually guided action, where understanding how observations change as a function of action is as crucial as sensing itself.

The paper identifies several critical limitations in prior formulations of spatial intelligence, which assume "oracle observations," meaning all necessary information is readily available. In contrast, real-world spatial intelligence often requires uncovering "unseen" latent physical properties such as:
- **Occluded structure:** Objects hidden behind others.
- **Dynamics:** How objects move or react to forces.
- **Containment:** Whether an object is inside another.
- **Functionality:** The purpose or use of an object.

These properties are inherently inaccessible through passive sensing and necessitate active interaction with the environment. To address this, the ESI-BENCH benchmark was developed with the following key objectives:

1. **Recast the observer as an actor:** The benchmark requires agents to actively engage with the environment to acquire necessary observations, rather than relying on pre-provided visual input. This shifts the focus from merely "seeing" to "acting to see."
2. **Evaluate spatial competence, not just sensing:** Agents are assessed not only on their ability to perceive visual information but also on their capacity to determine which embodied abilities (perception, locomotion, manipulation) to deploy and how to sequence them effectively to solve spatial tasks.
3. **Promote selective sensing:** Agents must identify and prioritize task-relevant information, acquiring only observations that are diagnostically useful, rather than processing redundant or uninformative inputs.
4. **Enable resolution of perceptual ambiguities:** The benchmark includes tasks where agents must reason through misleading observations to infer hidden spatial structures and underlying physical constraints that are not directly observed.
5. **Ground tasks in cognitive science theories:** The design of ESI-BENCH's task categories follows Spelke’s four core knowledge systems (object representation, layout and geometry, number representation, and agents & goal-directed actions), providing a theoretically informed framework for spatial intelligence evaluation.
6. **Provide a comprehensive and challenging benchmark:** ESI-BENCH offers a diverse set of 10 task categories and 29 subcategories, comprising 3,081 instances. These tasks are specifically designed to be meaningful only when an agent possesses a body, a belief state, and the ability to interact physically, thereby creating "physical stakes" in the outcome.

The ultimate motivation is to create a testbed for studying agents that can effectively close the perception-action loop, thereby fostering the development of more robust, adaptive, and human-like embodied AI systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ESI-BENCH benchmark is designed to evaluate embodied spatial intelligence through interactive tasks within a simulated environment.

**Benchmark Setup:**
Each task instance is formally defined as a tuple `(S, p0, q, y*)`, representing a 3D scene, the agent’s initial pose, a natural-language question about a spatial property, and the ground-truth answer. The environment is modeled as `E = ⟨S, A, O, T⟩`, where `S` is the scene, `A` is the action space, `O` is the egocentric observation space, and `T` governs scene transitions. An agent receives an observation `o_t` at each timestep, executes an action `a_t`, and iteratively builds a trajectory `τ` until it commits to a final answer `ŷ` with a confidence `c` within a maximum budget of 30 steps.

The action space is high-level and discrete, encompassing three main types:
- **Locomotion:** `move_forward`, `move_backward`, `move_left`, `move_right`, `move_up`, `move_down`.
- **Perception:** `turn_left`, `turn_right`, `turn_up`, `turn_down` (for viewpoint rotation).
- **Manipulation:** `pick_up`, `put_obj_inside_obj`, `put_obj_on_obj`, `fill_obj_with_water`, `pour_obj_from_obj_to_obj`.
- **Terminal:** `answer(ŷ, c)` to submit a final answer.

The benchmark is built on the **OmniGibson simulator**, which leverages NVIDIA Isaac Sim and PhysX 5. This environment provides realistic rigid-body contact physics, particle-based fluids, transparency rendering, and lighting. It uses the **BEHAVIOR-1K scene pool**, featuring 51 interactive 3D scenes with over 300 rooms and 9,000 object instances with physical properties. For each task, a scene graph is extracted, providing object bounding boxes, categories, spatial relationships, and states (e.g., fill levels, contact flags) to facilitate scenario construction and ground-truth label derivation.

**Task Construction:**
The task construction pipeline involves several stages:
1. **Task Proposal:** GPT-4o is used as a proposal engine. Given a scene graph and task category requirements, it selects task-relevant objects from a random sample of 200 BEHAVIOR-1K categories, determines their initial positions, and proposes the agent's initial pose. It also generates a ground-truth action trajectory, outlining the optimal sequence of actions to resolve the task.
2. **Scene Instantiation:** Proposed objects are loaded into the scene. Conflicts with existing content are checked using bounding box intersection. Objects are placed on supporting surfaces via physics-based kinematic sampling and allowed to settle. Configurations are validated for stability, per-view object existence (via segmentation masks), and contact flags. Invalid configurations are rejected.
3. **Agent Trajectory Collection:** The agent is initialized at the GPT-4o-proposed pose. The proposed ground-truth actions are executed step by step, with each step rendered as an egocentric observation and verified. Trajectories failing verification are discarded.
4. **Metadata Saving:** Each verified task instance is saved as a JSON file, containing comprehensive metadata including scene, room, object details, initial poses, question, ground-truth answer, and action trajectory.
5. **Human Verification and Generator Bias Audit:** All generated tasks undergo a three-criteria review by human annotators:
 - **Correctness:** Physical and semantic consistency with simulator state.
 - **Answerability:** Question has a unique answer obtainable through available actions.
 - **Non-triviality:** Requires embodied evidence acquisition, not solvable from initial observation or priors.
 Each instance is reviewed by three annotators, with disagreements resolved by majority vote. Additionally, a bias audit evaluates GPT-4o's generated tasks for linguistic or object-category biases through shortcut baselines (question-only, metadata-only performance) and comparison with human-generated tasks.

**Task Categories and Statistics:**
ESI-BENCH comprises 3,081 tasks across 10 categories and 29 subcategories. These categories are organized around Spelke's core knowledge systems: Object Representation (e.g., Physical Structure, Physical Dynamics), Layout and Geometry (e.g., Specular Reflection, Spatial Relations), Number Representation (Enumerative Perception), and Agents & Goal-Directed Actions (e.g., Temporal Understanding, Action Sequencing, Cognitive Mapping, Perceptual Grounding, Metric Comparison). Each category demands a distinct combination of locomotion, perception, and manipulation abilities.

**Evaluation Paradigms:**
Four paradigms are used to evaluate models:
1. **Passive Single-View:** A single fixed observation from the initial pose, serving as a baseline for existing spatial benchmarks.
2. **Passive Multi-View:** 30 randomly sampled views along a random trajectory, matching the active exploration budget, to simulate exhaustive passive scene coverage without active viewpoint selection.
3. **Active Exploration:** The agent interacts with the full action space to gather evidence before answering. This is the core paradigm for ESI-BENCH.
4. **Ground-Truth Passive:** Views rendered along the ground-truth action trajectory, acting as an oracle to separate perception errors from action selection errors.

**Models and Evaluation:**
Two families of models are evaluated:
- **2D Vision-Language Models (VLMs):** GPT-5 and Gemini 3.1, taking egocentric visual observations.
- **3D-Augmented Models:** VGGT+Gemini (using 3D scene representations reconstructed from multi-view observations) and Ground-Truth 3D+Gemini (using perfect point clouds from simulator state as an oracle).
Human performance is also collected as an upper bound. All evaluations are conducted zero-shot, and results are reported as accuracy on matched subsets for fair comparison.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on ESI-BENCH yielded several findings regarding the capabilities and limitations of current MLLMs in embodied spatial intelligence.

**Active Exploration and Emergent Strategies:**
Active exploration demonstrated a substantial performance advantage over passive observation paradigms. Models, without explicit instruction, developed emergent spatial strategies to resolve ambiguities. For instance, to determine if a chestnut was inside a glass, agents spontaneously employed diverse approaches such as moving behind the object, repositioning for a top-down view, picking up the object, or pouring out its contents. This active engagement led to significant accuracy gains; for example, Gemini 3.1's performance on View Hallucination improved from 39.9% (Passive Single-View) to 68.1% (Active Exploration), and on Rigid Containment, it increased from 47.5% to 67.5%. In contrast, passive multi-view, despite providing significantly more images, showed negligible or even negative gains, suggesting that observation quantity without selective action often introduces noise rather than signal. Active exploration achieved correct answers with a relatively small number of task-conditioned steps, indicating the benefit stems from targeted evidence acquisition.

**Action Blindness vs. Perceptual Limitations:**
The comparison between active exploration and ground-truth passive trajectories indicated that the primary bottleneck for most tasks was not the perception module itself, but rather the agent's ability to select informative actions. GPT-5, for example, improved from 42.5% in active exploration to 95.0% when provided with ground-truth trajectory views for Rigid Containment, and from 64.2% to 90.0% for Physical Contact. This suggests that with optimal viewpoints, current MLLMs possess sufficient perceptual capabilities for many tasks. However, exceptions were identified, such as Geometric Configuration (GPT-5 26.0% with ground-truth passive views) and Specular Reflection, where models consistently struggled even with perfect views, indicating hard perceptual ceilings for certain types of spatial reasoning. Furthermore, the results demonstrated that failures often cascade: suboptimal actions lead to uninformative observations, which in turn trigger worse subsequent actions, creating a chain of errors that cannot be recovered within the step budget.

**The Role of 3D Representations:**
Explicit 3D grounding proved beneficial when the underlying representation was accurate. Ground-Truth 3D+Gemini showed substantial improvements on depth-sensitive tasks, with a 43.3-point gain on Geometric Configuration (from 27.5% to 70.8% compared to Gemini 3.1) and a 30.0-point gain on Counting w Occlusion (from 3.3% to 33.3%). This indicates that precise 3D geometry can resolve ambiguities inherent in 2D observations. However, imperfect 3D reconstruction, exemplified by VGGT+Gemini, was found to be actively detrimental. VGGT+Gemini's performance dropped to 9.9% on Geometric Configuration (from Gemini 3.1's 27.5%) and to 0.0% on Counting w Occlusion (from 3.3%). Imperfect reconstructions introduced geometric artifacts, such as object duplication, hallucination, and spatial-relation corruption, which actively misled the language model reasoning over a corrupted scene graph.

**Human-Model Metacognitive Gap:**
Human performance studies revealed a significant "metacognitive gap" between humans and MLLMs. While human and model performance were often comparable under fixed observation paradigms, humans dramatically outperformed models in active exploration. For example, on Physical Contact, humans achieved 88.3% compared to GPT-5's 64.2%, and on Material Transparency, humans reached 93.6% versus Gemini 3.1's 52.3%. Human agents exhibited stronger epistemic caution: they gathered more diverse observations, actively sought viewpoints that could falsify their hypotheses, and revised their beliefs under contradiction, resulting in performance much closer to the ground-truth passive oracle. In contrast, models tended to commit prematurely with uniformly high confidence, often after only a few steps, regardless of evidence quality. They exhibited a directional bias in action selection, repeatedly seeking confirming views rather than probing orthogonal angles or disproving initial impressions. This anchoring to first impressions and lack of belief updating highlights a fundamental metacognitive failure distinct from perceptual ability, which neither better perception nor more embodied interaction alone could fully close.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ESI-BENCH contributes to the field of embodied AI by providing a diagnostic tool for evaluating a critical aspect of intelligence: the ability to actively acquire and reason about information within a physical environment. Its primary significance lies in shifting the paradigm from passive observation to active engagement, thereby enabling researchers to probe deeper into the mechanisms of spatial intelligence in AI systems.

The benchmark's comprehensive design, spanning 10 categories and 29 subcategories grounded in Spelke's core knowledge systems, provides a structured framework for identifying specific strengths and weaknesses of MLLMs. By focusing on tasks that require agents to infer hidden physical properties and overcome perceptual ambiguities through locomotion and manipulation, ESI-BENCH highlights the importance of the perception-action loop.

The findings, particularly the observed "action blindness" and "metacognitive gap" in current MLLMs, have several potential impacts:
- **Advancing Embodied AI Development:** ESI-BENCH offers concrete targets for future research in embodied AI. Addressing action selection failures, improving belief revision mechanisms, and instilling appropriate confidence calibration are identified as crucial areas for development.
- **Enhancing Safety and Reliability:** The finding that models commit prematurely with high confidence despite insufficient evidence poses practical risks for embodied systems deployed in real-world settings (e.g., homes, hospitals, warehouses). ESI-BENCH makes this failure mode measurable, facilitating the development of safer and more reliable AI agents that can accurately assess their own uncertainty and gather necessary evidence before acting.
- **Bridging the Human-AI Gap:** By systematically comparing human and model performance, ESI-BENCH clarifies where current AI systems diverge from human-level spatial reasoning. The detailed analysis of human exploratory behaviors (e.g., seeking falsifying viewpoints) provides insights that can guide the design of more human-like AI cognitive architectures.
- **Guiding 3D Representation Research:** The results on 3D-augmented models underscore that while accurate 3D information is beneficial, imperfect reconstructions can be actively harmful. This emphasizes the need for robust 3D reconstruction techniques that not only generate geometry but also provide uncertainty-aware scene graphs to avoid misleading downstream reasoning.
- **Open-Source Contribution:** The public availability of all data, dataset construction codes, and evaluation scripts supports reproducible research and encourages broader community participation in developing embodied spatial intelligence.

In summary, ESI-BENCH serves as a critical testbed for understanding and improving the capabilities of AI agents in actively navigating, perceiving, and interacting with complex physical environments, moving closer to the goal of truly embodied spatial intelligence.
