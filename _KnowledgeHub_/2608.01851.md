# Weights or Skills? A Survey of Robot-Learning Techniques: from Action-Predicting Weights to Robots that Write their Own Skills

> [!summary] Summary
> A survey by Jena et al. categorizes robot learning techniques into "weights" (Vision-Language-Action models) and "skills" (code-as-policy agents), establishing a five-rung self-improvement ladder for the latter and classifying 77 representative systems. It highlights the current frontier of integrated self-improvement and the disconnect between research advancements and commercial robot skill marketplaces.

## Problem

- The robot learning field lacked a clear, unified framework to understand diverging paradigms, particularly between end-to-end action prediction and programmatic skill generation.
- There was an ambiguous and overloaded usage of the term "skill" across different research communities, hindering precise communication and progress.
- A structured taxonomy was missing for code-as-policy methods, especially one that systematically organized them by their capacity for self-improvement and autonomy.

## Method

- Proposed a **"weights vs. skills"** dichotomy to categorize robot learning approaches, with "skills" specifically defined as executable, editable code.
- Developed a five-rung **"self-improvement ladder"** for code-as-policy methods, based on the hierarchical integration of ==Feedback, Memory, and Search== mechanisms.
- Constructed a detailed taxonomy by classifying ==77 representative robot learning systems== and cataloging an additional 225 related works from 2016-2026.

## Results

- A comprehensive taxonomy was established, classifying **77** representative systems and cataloging **225** additional works, providing a structured overview of the robot learning field.
- The survey clarified **five** distinct senses of "skill" in the literature, identifying the "code" definition as uniquely enabling continuous self-improvement and inspectability.
- A future research agenda was proposed, featuring **four** measurable quantities (e.g., success-vs-interactions curve, skill-library reuse rate) to guide progress in physical AI and bridge gaps between research and industry.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Robot learning exhibits a fundamental dichotomy between VLA models (competence in frozen weights) and code-as-policy agents (competence in executable skills).
> - The "full self-improving loop" (integrating feedback, memory, and search) represents a sparsely populated and critical frontier in robot learning, achieved by only a few recent systems.
> - Only the "code" sense of "skill" inherently supports human-inspectability, on-device adaptability, symbolic composability, and self-improvement without requiring gradient updates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This paper functions as a focused survey, positioning itself within the rapidly evolving field of robot learning. It addresses a perceived fundamental divergence, or "fault line," in contemporary approaches: one paradigm relies on large, end-to-end models that predict actions from "frozen weights" (Vision-Language-Action, or VLA, models), while the other focuses on "code-as-policy" agents that generate, refine, and execute their own programmatic "skills."

The survey distinguishes itself from prior related works by its unique organizing axis. While existing surveys cover areas such as world models, embodied intelligence, language-model navigation, VLA models, and foundation models for manipulation (as indicated in Table 1 and Figure 3 of the paper), this work specifically organizes code-as-policy methods by their *degree of self-improvement*. It also explicitly names and characterizes the most advanced "full self-improving loop" cell, which is largely unaddressed by other surveys. Furthermore, a unique contribution is the integration of the "skill economy" perspective, linking academic research to emerging commercial robot skill marketplaces.

By classifying 77 representative systems into a detailed taxonomy and cataloging an additional 225 related works, the survey provides a structured overview of the field from 2016 to 2026, emphasizing key trends, capabilities, and limitations along its central "weights versus skills" dichotomy.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations underpinning this survey are multifaceted:

1. **To articulate the fundamental dichotomy in robot learning:** The paper's central motivation is to organize the field around the "fault line" separating methods where competence is "baked into frozen weights" (VLA models) from those where agents "write and refine their own executable skills as code." This aims to provide a clear analytical axis for understanding current research trends.

2. **To establish a detailed taxonomy for code-as-policy methods:** A core objective is to arrange code-as-policy techniques based on their capacity for self-improvement. This involves defining a ladder of increasing autonomy, ranging from zero-shot program synthesis to closed-loop self-repair, persistent skill memory, evolutionary program search, and culminating in a "full self-improving loop" that integrates all these mechanisms. The motivation is to explicitly identify the mechanisms enabling greater agent autonomy and highlight the current sparsity of systems achieving the highest rung of self-improvement.

3. **To clarify the various interpretations of "skill":** The survey aims to address the ambiguous and overloaded usage of the term "skill" within the robot learning literature. It seeks to delineate at least five distinct senses in which "skill" is employed, emphasizing that only the "code" definition inherently supports self-improvement without relying on gradient updates. This clarification is motivated by the need for precise communication and understanding of capabilities.

4. **To characterize the strengths and limitations of distinct technique families:** By comparing different robot learning paradigms (VLA, code-as-policy, reward synthesis, skill libraries) across various operational axes (e.g., data needs, task horizon, transfer, interpretability, safety, failure modes), the survey intends to provide a balanced assessment of their respective advantages and disadvantages. This comparative analysis is motivated by the need to inform researchers about the trade-offs inherent in each approach.

5. **To connect research to the emerging robot skill economy:** A significant motivation is to link academic advancements in robot learning to practical, commercial developments, specifically the advent of marketplaces for robot skills. The survey highlights the discrepancy between the current offerings of these marketplaces (static playback skills) and the potential for adaptive, self-improving capabilities being explored in research.

6. **To identify and articulate open problems and a future research agenda:** Building on the identified gaps between current research capabilities and the demands of a functional skill economy, the survey aims to formulate a set of pressing open problems. These include challenges related to adaptation, cross-embodiment portability, provenance, safety verification, skill composition, and standardization. The motivation is to provide a structured agenda that can guide future research efforts toward actionable and measurable progress in physical AI.

The authors explicitly state that this is a "deliberately focused survey" designed to examine 77 representative systems through its specific taxonomy and a set of contrast tables, rather than attempting an exhaustive catalog of the entire field.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The survey employed a structured methodology for corpus construction and analysis, adhering to principles inspired by the PRISMA 2020 style.

**Corpus Construction:**
The research corpus was assembled in two distinct passes over a time window spanning January 2016 to July 2026:
1. **Taxonomy Systems (77 works):** This primary set of systems, featured in the main taxonomy, was curated using a "seeding and snowballing" approach. For each of the six main branches of the taxonomy, landmark systems were identified, and then citations were tracked both forward and backward until new candidate papers ceased to appear.
2. **Landscape Corpus (225 additional works):** This broader set of representative works, detailed in an appendix, was gathered through five structured web-search sweeps. Each sweep targeted specific thematic areas (e.g., generalist policies, language-model planning, skill discovery) using area-specific search strings across academic databases such as arXiv, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar, and major conference proceedings (CoRL, RSS, ICRA, IROS, NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, CVPR).

**Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria:**
- **Inclusion:** A work was included if it concerned how an embodied agent's skill or policy is authored, learned, transferred, evaluated, or distributed. Each included work required verifiable metadata (exact title, first author, year, and an arXiv identifier or DOI).
- **Exclusion:** Works primarily focused on pure perception, navigation, or locomotion were excluded from the main taxonomy unless they directly addressed skill authoring or improvement; otherwise, they were relegated to the landscape appendix. Unverified metadata and duplicate entries were also excluded.

**Taxonomy Placement Criteria (for the 77 systems):**
For a verified work to be promoted from the general corpus to the detailed taxonomy, it had to pass three specific tests:
1. **Axis-placeable:** It needed to occupy an unambiguous position on the "weights versus skills" question and, for skill-authoring systems, on the defined self-improvement ladder.
2. **Distinct:** It had to contribute a novel mechanism, rung exemplar, or branch exemplar not already represented in the taxonomy, avoiding near-variants or reimplementations.
3. **Fully characterizable:** Its primary source had to provide sufficient detail regarding what it "ships," how it "learns," where it is "evaluated," and whether it "self-improves" to fill all comparative columns without inference.

Works failing these criteria were still retained in the landscape appendix to ensure comprehensive coverage.

**Analytical Framework - Self-Improvement Ladder:**
The central analytical contribution involved arranging "code-as-policy" methods into a five-rung self-improvement ladder. This arrangement is based on the presence or absence of three key operational mechanisms, defined unambiguously:
- **Feedback (F):** An execution-grounded signal used to revise code *within* a task (e.g., failure detection, sensory summary, execution-derived reward).
- **Memory (M):** Run-time generated content (e.g., validated code, episodic text) stored *across* task boundaries and retrieved for later tasks.
- **Search (S):** Maintenance and selection among multiple candidate programs (e.g., sampled-program selection, evolutionary mutation, beam search), rather than a single sequential repair chain.
The rungs progressed from zero-shot synthesis (no F, M, S) to closed-loop self-repair (F present, M, S absent), skill-library accumulation (M present), evolutionary program search (S present), and finally, the "full self-improving loop" (F, M, and S all present).

**Analysis of "Skill" Definition:**
The survey systematically identified and categorized five distinct senses of the word "skill" used in the literature, evaluating each sense based on its representation (e.g., latent policy, executable program) and properties such as inspectability, adaptability, composability, and distributability.

**Comparative Analysis and Future Agenda:**
The methodology included generating several comparative tables (e.g., Table 2 for system details, Table 4 for self-improvement mechanisms, Table 6 for skill-building systems, Table 8 for family comparisons) to highlight capabilities, limitations, and characteristic failure modes of different approaches. Finally, the survey outlined a future agenda by proposing four measurable quantities (success-vs-interactions curve, skill-library reuse rate, cross-embodiment transfer drop, provenance check) with concrete testbeds, aiming to translate aspirational goals into actionable research directions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The survey's analysis yielded several key findings that structure the current landscape of robot learning:

1. **Dichotomy of "Weights" vs. "Skills":** The field is characterized by a fundamental split. On one side are **Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models** (e.g., RT-1, RT-2, Octo, OpenVLA), which predict actions directly from observations and language using large, pre-trained neural networks. Their competence resides in "frozen weights," relying on data scaling and interpolation for generalization, but lacking inspectable, editable code and explicit self-improvement loops. On the other side are **code-as-policy agents**, which generate and refine executable programs. This survey positions these as the "skills" pole.

2. **The Self-Improvement Ladder for Code-as-Policy:**
 - **Zero-shot synthesis:** This foundational rung involves LLMs emitting executable control code without any execution feedback loop. Systems like Code-as-Policies, ProgPrompt, and VoxPoser demonstrate the ability to generate programs from natural language instructions and APIs. Their limitation is a lack of adaptation during execution.
 - **Closed-loop self-repair:** This level incorporates feedback, allowing agents to revise code within a task based on execution-grounded signals (e.g., Inner Monologue, DoReMi, REFLECT). However, these corrections are typically discarded after the task, preventing cumulative learning.
 - **Skill-library accumulation:** Systems at this rung feature persistent memory, storing validated code across task boundaries for later retrieval and composition (e.g., Voyager, LRLL, Uni-Skill). This enables competence to compound over time.
 - **Evolutionary program search:** This rung introduces explicit search, where agents maintain and evaluate multiple candidate programs, mutating and selecting the best ones based on execution feedback (e.g., CaP-X, RoboEvolve, GEAR). This offers a more robust exploration strategy than sequential repair.
 - **Full self-improving loop:** This highest rung combines feedback, memory, and search into one continuous, open-ended loop. The survey identifies this cell as "sparsely populated," with only a few very recent systems such as ASPIRE, ENPIRE, and RoboClaw demonstrating this full integration on real hardware. This finding underscores a significant frontier in robot learning.

3. **Overloaded Definition of "Skill":** The term "skill" is used in at least five distinct senses across the literature: latent policy, option/primitive, code (executable program), robot app (packaged behavior), and market product. A key finding is that *only the "code" sense of skill inherently allows for human-inspectability, adaptability, and symbolic composability, enabling self-improvement without requiring gradient updates.* Other senses often involve opaque representations or static playback.

4. **The Nascent Skill Economy and its Limitations:** Commercial robot skill marketplaces (e.g., Unitree UniStore) are emerging, but currently only distribute "static playback" skills. These skills lack on-device adaptation, cross-embodiment portability, and mechanisms for self-improvement, representing the "least-capable point in the taxonomy." This highlights a significant gap between commercial offerings and research advancements in adaptive capabilities.

5. **Comparative Strengths and Weaknesses of Technique Families:** The survey's matrix comparison (Table 8) reveals that no single technique family is optimal across all six examined axes (data needs, task horizon, transfer, interpretability, safety, characteristic failure mode).
 - **Code-as-policy** systems are favorable for interpretability and safety verification due to readable, editable code.
 - **VLA models** demonstrate strengths in cross-task and cross-embodiment transfer by leveraging web-scale data.
 - **Reward synthesis** can achieve long task horizons and real-world transfer but carries risks of reward hacking.
 - **RL skill discovery** produces temporally extended primitives but often involves opaque latent codes and high data needs.
 - The "static playback" nature of current **market skills** results in weaknesses across most adaptive dimensions.

6. **Absence of Standardized Self-Improvement Benchmarks:** A critical observation is the lack of standardized benchmarks that measure competence as a function of *accumulated interaction* (i.e., charting a success-vs-interactions curve). Existing benchmarks primarily assess one-shot task success, failing to capture the crucial dimension of on-device self-improvement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This survey carries several significant implications and points toward potential future impacts on both the research community and the nascent robot industry:

1. **Reframing Research Agendas:** By articulating the "weights versus skills" dichotomy and, more specifically, the self-improvement ladder for code-as-policy, the survey provides a novel analytical framework. This framework can help researchers categorize, evaluate, and strategically plan their work, offering a clearer understanding of how different approaches contribute to increasingly autonomous and adaptive robot behavior. It highlights the "full self-improving loop" as a critical, yet sparsely populated, research frontier, thereby guiding future efforts.

2. **Driving Foundational Research in Self-Improvement:** The explicit definition of Feedback, Memory, and Search mechanisms, and their hierarchical arrangement, offers a structured roadmap for developing truly self-improving robots. This could stimulate foundational research into how these mechanisms can be robustly integrated and scaled, particularly for physical AI applications where continuous learning and adaptation are essential.

3. **Informing the Development of the Robot Skill Economy:** The survey directly links academic progress to the commercialization of robot skills. By identifying the limitations of current static "robot app" marketplaces, it highlights the urgent need for research that enables downloaded skills to adapt on-device, transfer across embodiments, and possess verifiable provenance. This analysis is crucial for transforming the "skill economy" from a distribution channel for animations into a genuine marketplace for adaptive capabilities.

4. **Promoting Standardized Evaluation and Benchmarking:** The proposed actionable evaluation protocol, including metrics like the success-vs-interactions curve, skill-library reuse rate, cross-embodiment transfer drop, and provenance checks, has the potential to standardize how self-improvement and transfer are measured. Adopting such benchmarks would allow for more rigorous comparison of methods and accelerate progress by clearly quantifying the benefits of adaptive robot learning.

5. **Enhancing Interpretability and Safety in Robotics:** The emphasis on "code-as-policy" inherently promotes systems that are more interpretable, editable, and potentially safer than opaque, end-to-end VLA models. By advocating for approaches where competence is transparently expressed as code, the survey underscores a pathway toward more accountable and verifiable robotic systems, which is critical for their deployment in human environments.

6. **Facilitating Cross-Paradigm Integration:** By mapping both gradient-trained and LLM-authored skill acquisition methods, the survey suggests a future direction of bridging these "RL and code flavors." A unifying interface that allows code to wrap, call, and refine learned policies, and vice-versa, could unlock synergistic benefits, enabling the self-improvement machinery to operate over a broader range of learned competences.

In conclusion, this survey provides a timely and focused analysis that not only synthesizes the current state of robot learning along a critical analytical dimension but also charts a clear course for future research, particularly at the intersection of self-improving agents and the emerging commercial landscape of robot skills.
