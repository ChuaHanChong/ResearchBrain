# TrojanTO: Action-Level Backdoor Attacks against Trajectory Optimization Models

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers developed TrojanTO, an action-level backdoor attack framework that effectively injects malicious behaviors into Trajectory Optimization models for offline reinforcement learning, achieving high attack success rates with minimal data poisoning while preserving benign task performance. This work highlights a new vulnerability in TO models, which are immune to traditional reward-manipulation backdoor attacks due to their sequence modeling objective.

## Problem

- Trajectory Optimization (TO) models in offline reinforcement learning, widely used in control tasks like robotics, have largely uninvestigated security vulnerabilities.
- Existing backdoor attacks for traditional RL agents rely on reward manipulation, a method ineffective against TO models which minimize reconstruction loss rather than maximizing rewards.
- Achieving precise action-level manipulation in high-dimensional continuous action spaces for specific malicious behaviors is technically challenging.

## Method

- Introduces **TrojanTO**, an ==action-level backdoor== attack framework designed specifically for Trajectory Optimization models (e.g., Decision Transformer, Decision ConvFormer).
- Employs trajectory filtering to select high-quality data, ensuring the backdoored model maintains benign task performance.
- Uses batch poisoning to stealthily inject triggers and target actions into training data, and an alternating training scheme to co-optimize the trigger and model parameters effectively.

## Results

- TrojanTO achieved an average composite performance (CP) of **0.701**, significantly outperforming baseline attacks (Baffle **0.342** CP, IMC **0.551** CP) across various D4RL environments and TO model architectures.
- The attack demonstrated high efficiency and stealth, achieving an Attack Success Rate (ASR) of **0.719** with a poisoning rate as low as **0.3%** of trajectories while maintaining a Benign Task Performance (BTP) of **0.914**.
- Ablation studies confirmed that each component of TrojanTO (Trajectory Filtering, Batch Poisoning, Alternating Training) critically contributed to the attack's overall effectiveness, efficiency, and stealth.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Trajectory Optimization models are fundamentally immune to traditional reward-manipulation-based backdoor attacks due to their sequence modeling objective and reconstruction loss optimization.
> - The choice of target action (e.g., boundary actions) and the design of learnable triggers are critical factors for successfully implanting action-level backdoors in TO models.
> - A combination of data quality control, partial data poisoning, and alternating optimization can enable effective and stealthy backdoor implantation in continuous, high-dimensional action spaces.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work is situated within the field of offline reinforcement learning (offline RL), where policies are learned from pre-existing datasets without requiring real-time interaction during training. Trajectory Optimization (TO) models, such as Decision Transformer (DT) and Decision ConvFormer (DC), have demonstrated capabilities in this domain by reframing RL as a sequence modeling problem. These models are increasingly applied in areas like embodied intelligence and robotic control, particularly for tasks involving high-dimensional continuous action spaces.

Despite the growing adoption of TO models, their security vulnerabilities to backdoor attacks have remained largely unexplored. Existing backdoor attacks in traditional RL typically rely on manipulating reward signals during the agent's training process. This paradigm is observed to be ineffective against TO models due to their fundamental nature as sequence models that minimize reconstruction loss rather than maximizing rewards. Additionally, the large network size and computational cost associated with training TO models make traditional training-time attacks less practical. The complexities of injecting precise manipulations into high-dimensional continuous action spaces further exacerbate this challenge.

This paper addresses these gaps by introducing TrojanTO, which is presented as the first action-level backdoor attack specifically designed for TO models. It operates as a post-training attack, meaning it modifies a pre-trained model rather than interfering with the initial training phase. This approach positions the work as relevant to supply-chain security concerns, particularly as large-scale pre-trained models become widely shared and deployed. The research explores a new threat vector within the context of offline RL and TO models, aiming to establish their susceptibility to sophisticated, targeted manipulation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and evaluate an effective action-level backdoor attack against Trajectory Optimization (TO) models. This encompasses several specific goals:

- **Address the limitations of existing backdoor attacks for TO models:** The authors identify that conventional RL backdoor attacks, which often rely on reward manipulation during training, are not well-suited for TO models. This is primarily because TO models operate by minimizing reconstruction loss over action-state-Return-to-Go (RTG) sequences, rather than directly optimizing policies based on reward signals. Their large scale also makes retraining for attack impractical.
- **Propose a post-training attack paradigm:** Given the computational expense and scale of modern TO models, the research aims to develop a backdoor attack that can be applied to already pre-trained models. This post-training approach is motivated by its practicality in real-world scenarios, particularly within supply-chain vulnerabilities where compromised models might be shared.
- **Achieve action-level control in continuous action spaces:** The objective is to compel the victim TO model to output specific, predetermined target actions when a trigger is activated. This fine-grained control is considered challenging in high-dimensional continuous action spaces, which are common in TO model applications.
- **Maintain attack effectiveness and stealthiness:** The proposed attack aims to achieve a high Attack Success Rate (ASR) under trigger conditions, while simultaneously ensuring that the model's performance on benign, untriggered inputs (Benign Task Performance, BTP) remains largely undisturbed. This balance is crucial for an attack to remain undetected in practical deployment.
- **Demonstrate broad applicability and low attack budget:** The research aims to show that the proposed method is effective across diverse TO model architectures (DT, GDT, DC), various reinforcement learning tasks, and different types of target actions. Furthermore, it seeks to achieve this with a minimal poisoning rate, such as 0.3% of the total trajectories.

The motivation for this work stems from the increasing deployment of TO models in critical applications like robotic control and embodied intelligence. Understanding and demonstrating their security vulnerabilities, particularly to action-level backdoors, is considered essential for developing robust defenses and raising community awareness about these emerging threats.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology of TrojanTO involves an empirical investigation into factors influencing backdoor efficacy in TO models, followed by the development of a three-component post-training attack framework.

#### 3.1. Preliminary Investigations

The authors conducted empirical studies to understand the fundamental elements influencing backdoor attacks against TO models:
- **Target Action Selection:** Prior work often uses fixed boundary actions. This study investigated how the choice of target action (e.g., '0', '1', '-1', 'fixed random', 'arithmetic', '0.5staggered') impacts ASR in high-dimensional continuous action spaces. Results showed that boundary actions (e.g., '1', '-1') consistently yielded high ASR, while internal actions (e.g., '0' in Walker2d) significantly reduced ASR. This finding motivated the evaluation of TrojanTO against a diverse set of target actions.
- **Trigger Design Parameters:** The impact of trigger dimensions and values on backdoor efficacy was examined. Experiments demonstrated that the choice of trigger dimensions (e.g., (1,2,3) vs. (1,10,14) in HalfCheetah) critically affects ASR. Learnable trigger values, optimized using Momentum Iterative Fast Gradient Sign Method (MI-FGSM), were found to be more effective than static or empirically defined triggers.
- **Reward Manipulation:** The study empirically validated whether reward manipulation, a common technique in traditional RL backdoors, influences TO models. Results indicated that varying reward signals (e.g., -4, 4, 8, 16) during backdoor training had a negligible impact on both ASR and BTP across DT, DC, and GDT models. This confirmed that TO models' objective of minimizing reconstruction loss makes them largely insensitive to reward-based manipulation for backdoor injection, thus guiding the design to exclude RTG modification.

#### 3.2. TrojanTO Framework

TrojanTO is designed as a post-training attack method to efficiently modify pre-trained TO models. It comprises three core components:
- **Trajectory Filtering (TF):** To maintain the Benign Task Performance (BTP) and prevent overfitting to suboptimal behaviors, this module filters the dataset. It retains only trajectories whose sequence length exceeds a predefined threshold, under the assumption that longer trajectories are more representative of successful behavior. The filtered set ($F_\tau$) is then exclusively used for subsequent backdoor training and trigger optimization.
- **Batch Poisoning (BP):** This component ensures consistent trigger association and preserves BTP with a low poisoning rate. For each training batch, a duplicate is created. One copy serves as the clean batch ($B_c$), used for maintaining benign performance. In the other copy, a *single, randomly selected transition* has its state perturbed by the trigger ($\delta$), and its corresponding action is set to the predefined target action ($a^\dagger$). The Return-to-Go (RTG) in this poisoned transition is not modified, consistent with the preliminary findings on reward manipulation. The training objective minimizes a combined loss $L = L_p + \lambda L_c$, where $L_p$ is the backdoor loss (mean squared error between predicted and target action for the poisoned transition) and $L_c$ is the clean loss (reconstruction loss for the entire benign batch). $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter to balance these objectives.
- **Alternating Training (AT):** Inspired by Input Model Co-optimization (IMC), this module jointly optimizes the trigger $\delta$ and the model parameters $\tilde{\pi}$ to enhance backdoor efficacy. This is framed as a bi-level optimization problem and solved through an alternating procedure:
 - **Trigger Learning:** The trigger $\delta$ is updated using MI-FGSM to minimize the backdoor loss ($L_p$). This phase involves multiple inner iterations ($N_1$).
 - **Parameter Updating:** The model parameters $\tilde{\pi}$ are updated using gradient descent to minimize the combined loss $L$. This phase also involves multiple inner iterations ($N_2$).
 The alternating optimization is performed for the first half of the total training budget, after which only model parameter updates are conducted for the remainder of the training, keeping the trigger fixed. This strategy is intended to mitigate training instability in RL contexts.

#### 3.3. Threat Model and Experimental Setup

- **Threat Model:** A post-training supply-chain attack where an adversary modifies a pre-trained TO model without access to the original training dataset, using a minimal set of poisoned trajectories (e.g., 0.3%). The adversary can inject a trigger into the agent's input observation during inference.
- **Environments:** Experiments were conducted on D4RL benchmark tasks: Hopper-Medium-Expert-v2, HalfCheetah-Medium-v2, Walker2D-Medium-v2 (MuJoCo locomotion), AntMaze-Umaze-v2 (Navigation), Kitchen-Partial-v0, and Pen-Cloned-v1 (Manipulation).
- **Victim Models:** Decision Transformer (DT), Graph Decision Transformer (GDT), and Decision ConvFormer (DC).
- **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Attack Success Rate (ASR):** The proportion of evaluation episodes where the model's output action for a triggered step is within a threshold $\epsilon$ of the target action $a^\dagger$.
 - **Benign Task Performance (BTP):** The average return of the backdoored policy, normalized by that of the clean policy. A BTP near 1 indicates minimal performance degradation.
 - **Composite Performance (CP):** The harmonic mean of ASR and BTP, balancing attack effectiveness and stealthiness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Preliminary Research Findings

- **Target Action Impact:** The choice of target action significantly affected ASR. For instance, '1' and '-1' (boundary actions) yielded ASRs approaching 1.0, while '0' for Walker2d resulted in an ASR of 0.11. This underscored the necessity for evaluating backdoor attacks against a diverse set of target actions.
- **Trigger Design Impact:** Trigger dimensions critically influenced ASR. For HalfCheetah, dimensions (1,2,3) yielded an ASR of 0.915, whereas (1,10,14) resulted in 0.000. Optimized, learnable trigger values (using MI-FGSM) were observed to be more effective than non-learnable alternatives.
- **Reward Manipulation Irrelevance:** Empirical evidence showed that variations in reward signals (e.g., -4, 4, 8, 16) during backdoor training did not substantially alter ASR or BTP trends across DT, DC, and GDT models. This confirmed that reward manipulation is largely ineffective for backdooring TO models due to their reconstruction loss objective.

#### 4.2. TrojanTO Attack Performance

- **Overall Efficacy:** TrojanTO achieved an average Composite Performance (CP) of 0.701 across diverse tasks and models. This represented a 105.0% improvement over Baffle (0.342 CP) and a 27.2% gain over IMC (0.551 CP).
- **High ASR with Low Budget:** TrojanTO achieved an average ASR of 0.719 while utilizing a data poisoning rate of approximately 0.3% (10 trajectories). In comparison, Baffle only reached an ASR of 0.369 with a 10% poisoning rate. This demonstrates a higher attack efficiency and stealth for TrojanTO.
- **Benign Performance Preservation:** TrojanTO maintained an average Benign Task Performance (BTP) of 0.914, which was higher than Baffle (0.792) and IMC (0.853).
- **Robustness Across Models and Tasks:** TrojanTO exhibited consistent performance across DT, GDT, and DC architectures and various D4RL environments. Baseline methods, Baffle and IMC, showed instances of performance degradation or collapse in specific settings, such as IMC's CP of 0.013 in Hopper (DT model) or Baffle's 0.000 CP in Walker2d (DT model).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Component Contributions:** All three core components—Trajectory Filtering (TF), Batch Poisoning (BP), and Alternating Training (AT)—were found to contribute positively to TrojanTO's performance.
 - Removing AT or BP led to substantial decreases in ASR (from 0.719 to 0.507 and 0.528, respectively), indicating their importance for attack effectiveness.
 - Removing TF or BP resulted in BTP reductions (from 0.914 to 0.850 and 0.836, respectively), highlighting their role in maintaining stealth.
 - These findings indicate that AT enhances attack effectiveness, while TF and BP contribute to stealth and efficiency through precise poisoning.
- **Attack Budget:** An increase in the attack budget (e.g., from 0.31% to 3.1%) generally improved CP, but performance plateaued beyond a certain point (e.g., 3.1% to 16% showed marginal gains).

#### 4.4. Persistent Attack and Robustness

- **Persistent Backdoor:** TrojanTO was demonstrated to implant persistent backdoors, forcing the model to consistently execute the target action for several consecutive time steps (up to the context window limit of the TO model, e.g., less than 20 steps) after a single trigger activation. This sustained malicious behavior was maintained with minor CP degradation as persistence duration increased.
- **Trigger Perturbations:** The backdoor exhibited gradual performance degradation under multiplicative noise applied to the trigger. For example, a 10% noise level still resulted in ASRs of 0.870 (Hopper), 1.000 (HalfCheetah), and 0.777 (Walker2d). This suggests robustness to environmental uncertainties, which could increase real-world threat potential.

#### 4.5. Defense Assessment

Several baseline defense methods were evaluated against TrojanTO:
- **Fine-tuning:** Demonstrated effectiveness, reducing ASR to near zero while largely preserving BTP with a minimal portion of the original training steps (10%).
- **Weight Pruning (Activation-based and Magnitude-based):** Found largely ineffective. Magnitude-based pruning did not reduce ASR, and activation-based pruning showed only marginal ASR reduction at the cost of significant BTP degradation.
- **Provable Defense:** Successfully reduced ASR to zero but rendered the agent unusable by severely degrading BTP. This was attributed to the defense's underlying assumptions being unsuitable for continuous state spaces.
- **Spectral Analysis:** Deemed impractical due to inconsistent True Positive Rate (TPR) across environments and hyperparameters.
- **Activation Clustering (AC):** Entirely ineffective, yielding 0% Precision and Recall. t-SNE visualizations of internal activations revealed that the variance between different input modalities (state, action, RTG) in TO models significantly masked the subtle changes introduced by the trigger, preventing detection.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research has several implications for the security of machine learning systems, particularly in the context of offline reinforcement learning:

- **Identification of a Novel Threat:** The paper identifies and systematically investigates action-level backdoor attacks as a significant and previously underexplored threat vector specifically targeting Trajectory Optimization (TO) models. This contributes to a broader understanding of vulnerabilities in modern decision-making AI.
- **Practical Post-training Vulnerability:** By demonstrating an effective post-training attack (TrojanTO), the work highlights a practical supply-chain vulnerability. As large-scale, pre-trained TO models become more common and are shared or deployed without full access to original training data, the ability to covertly implant backdoors post-training poses a substantial risk.
- **High Efficiency and Stealth:** TrojanTO's capability to achieve a high attack success rate with a remarkably low data poisoning rate (0.3%) and minimal impact on benign task performance signifies a potent and stealthy attack. This level of subtlety could make such compromises difficult to detect in real-world deployments.
- **Insights into TO Model Security:** The preliminary investigations provide empirical insights into the unique security characteristics of TO models. The findings regarding the critical roles of target action selection and trigger design, as well as the irrelevance of reward manipulation, distinguish TO models from traditional RL agents and inform future defense strategies.
- **Benchmark for Defense Development:** By establishing a robust attack framework, the research provides a concrete benchmark for the development and evaluation of countermeasures. The assessment of existing defense methods, highlighting the effectiveness of fine-tuning and the limitations of others like activation clustering for TO models, guides future research directions in securing these systems.
- **Real-world Implications:** Given the application of TO models in sensitive domains such as autonomous vehicles, robotics, and embodied intelligence, successful action-level backdoors could lead to critical system failures, safety hazards, or enable malicious control over autonomous agents. The work therefore raises awareness about potential risks that require proactive mitigation.
