# Can We Detect Failures Without Failure Data? Uncertainty-Aware Runtime Failure Detection for Imitation Learning Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> The Toyota Research Institute developed FAIL-Detect, a framework for runtime failure detection in imitation learning robot policies that operates without requiring explicit failure data. It uses uncertainty quantification with Conformal Prediction and a novel `logpZO` score, achieving average balanced accuracies up to 78% in simulation and 72% on robot hardware with practical detection times.

## Problem

- Existing failure detection methods for robot policies, especially those learned via imitation, largely require extensive and costly datasets of explicit failure examples for training.
- Complex, long-horizon imitation learning tasks often lead to diverse, unpredictable, and 'out-of-distribution' failure modes that are difficult to anticipate or enumerate beforehand.
- Ensuring trustworthy and safe deployment of autonomous robots in safety-critical environments necessitates robust, real-time failure detection without introducing prohibitive computational latency.

## Method

- **FAIL-Detect** introduces a ==two-stage framework==: first, learning or computing a scalar score indicative of successful trajectory characteristics using only in-distribution data, and second, designing a sequential threshold.
- Proposes ==logpZO==, a novel ==flow-based density estimator==, which computes the likelihood in the latent noise space (==log p(Z_O_t')==) to identify Out-of-Distribution events indicating potential failures.
- Leverages ==functional Conformal Prediction== to construct time-varying thresholds with statistical guarantees on the false positive rate, calibrating solely on successful rollouts to adapt to changing task dynamics.

## Results

- **FAIL-Detect** achieved average best balanced accuracies of approximately **78%** in simulation and **72%** on robot hardware tasks for failure detection without using failure data.
- The novel ==logpZO== score emerged as the top performer, securing highest balanced accuracy in **10 out of 16** simulation scenarios and **8 out of 12** hardware scenarios, while being significantly faster than baselines like STAC.
- Detected failure alerts from FAIL-Detect show a strong correlation with observable physical indications of failure, providing diagnostic value for pinpointing critical moments of failure.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learned scalar scores, particularly the novel flow-based `logpZO` method, consistently outperform post-hoc analytical metrics for discriminating between successful and failing robot executions.
> - Uncertainty quantification using time-varying Conformal Prediction thresholds is crucial for reliable failure detection, providing statistical guarantees on the false positive rate and adapting to task dynamics.
> - Framing failure detection as an Out-of-Distribution (OOD) problem, relying solely on successful demonstration data, effectively circumvents the significant practical challenge of acquiring explicit failure data.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research addresses a critical and long-standing challenge in the deployment of autonomous robotic systems: robust and reliable runtime failure detection, especially for policies learned through imitation learning. The field of robotic manipulation has seen rapid advancements, driven by sophisticated techniques like diffusion and flow-based generative models, enabling robots to learn complex skills from human demonstrations. However, as robot policies become more capable and handle longer, more intricate tasks, they also encounter unexpected and diverse failure modes that are difficult, if not impossible, to predict and enumerate beforehand.

Existing approaches to failure detection often rely on having prior knowledge of failure modes or requiring explicit failure data for training. For instance, many methods frame failure detection as a binary classification problem, necessitating the collection and annotation of failed trajectories. This presents a significant practical limitation: acquiring comprehensive failure datasets is time-consuming, expensive, and often infeasible in real-world scenarios, particularly because failure modes are highly varied and can be "out-of-distribution" themselves. Furthermore, classifiers trained on specific failure examples may not generalize well to novel or unseen failure types.

This work directly addresses these limitations by proposing **FAIL-Detect**, a novel framework that can detect failures without needing failure data during its training or calibration phase. It reframes the problem as sequential **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) detection**, where policy failures are identified as deviations from the distribution of successful executions. This paradigm shift represents a significant advancement, making failure detection more practical and scalable for real-world robotic deployment.

The paper integrates several key areas of research:
- **Imitation Learning (IL):** It builds upon state-of-the-art generative imitation learning policies, specifically Diffusion Policy (DP) and Flow Matching (FM) based architectures, which are becoming standard for learning visuomotor skills.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection:** It leverages principles from OOD detection, but critically, adapts them to a "one-class" setting where only in-distribution (successful) data is available. It explores various categories of OOD detection methods, including density-based, second-order (evidential), and one-class discriminators, and proposes a novel method (`logpZO`) within this context.
- **Uncertainty Quantification (UQ):** A central contribution is the robust quantification of uncertainty using **Conformal Prediction (CP)**. Unlike traditional heuristic thresholds, CP provides statistical guarantees on false positive rates, which is vital for safety-critical applications. The use of *time-varying* CP bands is a notable improvement over static thresholds used in some prior work (e.g., the STAC baseline).

By offering a lightweight and efficient runtime monitoring solution that boasts statistical guarantees and does not rely on costly failure data, FAIL-Detect significantly advances the trustworthiness and deployability of complex AI-driven robotic systems, bridging the gap between impressive research results and practical, safe real-world application.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a reliable and practical runtime failure detection system for robotic manipulation policies, particularly those learned through imitation learning. The core motivation stems from several critical challenges inherent in deploying such advanced robotic systems:

- **Growing Complexity and Diverse Failure Modes:** As imitation learning policies become more sophisticated and capable of handling complex, long-horizon tasks, the potential for unexpected and diverse failure modes increases significantly. These failures are difficult to predict or enumerate *a priori* (as illustrated by the diverse failure types in Fig. 2), making traditional, pre-defined error handling insufficient.
- **Lack of Failure Data:** Most existing failure detection methods require access to and training on explicit failure data. This is a severe practical impediment because:
 - Collecting and annotating a comprehensive set of failure examples is incredibly time-consuming, expensive, and often dangerous or impractical in real-world scenarios.
 - Failure distributions are often high-dimensional and complex, meaning any collected failure data might only cover a small fraction of possible failure modes, leading to poor generalization to unseen failures.
- **Need for Trustworthy Deployment in Safety-Critical Environments:** For robots operating in human environments (e.g., manufacturing, healthcare, logistics), reliable failure detection is paramount for safety and preventing undesirable or harmful behaviors. A system that can flag potential issues quickly and accurately is essential for enabling human intervention or system recovery.
- **Computational Efficiency for Real-Time Operation:** Any failure detection mechanism must be lightweight and fast enough to operate in real-time during policy inference, without introducing prohibitive latency to the robotic system.

In response to these motivations, the key objectives of FAIL-Detect are:
1. **Develop a "failure-free" failure detection approach:** The central aim is to detect policy failures effectively by relying solely on data from *successful* trajectories for training and calibration, circumventing the need for explicit failure examples. This is framed as sequential out-of-distribution (OOD) detection.
2. **Incorporate uncertainty awareness with statistical guarantees:** Employ Conformal Prediction (CP) to provide statistically rigorous thresholds for failure detection. This ensures control over the false positive rate (i.e., successfully executed tasks being incorrectly flagged as failures), enhancing the trustworthiness and reliability of the detection system.
3. **Ensure practical applicability:** Design the system to be modular, computationally lightweight, and capable of fast inference to facilitate real-time robot manipulation.
4. **Thoroughly evaluate diverse scoring mechanisms:** Investigate both learned and analytically derived ("post-hoc") scalar signals that can effectively discriminate between successful and failing policy executions, even without explicit failure data. This includes proposing a novel flow-based density estimator (`logpZO`).

By achieving these objectives, FAIL-Detect aims to significantly enhance the safety, reliability, and practical deployability of advanced imitation learning-based robotic systems, paving the way for their broader adoption in complex real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FAIL-Detect proposes a modular, two-stage framework for uncertainty-aware runtime failure detection in generative imitation learning-based robotic manipulation policies. The core idea is to identify deviations from expected successful behavior, treating failures as out-of-distribution (OOD) events, without requiring explicit failure data during training.

**A. Two-Stage Framework:**

The decision function `D(τ_t; θ)` that flags a failure (1) or success (0) is constructed in two sequential stages:

1. **Stage 1: Score Learning (or Computation):**
 The goal is to distill high-dimensional policy inputs (observations `O_t`, e.g., image features, robot states) and/or outputs (generated future actions `A_t`) into a scalar score `D_M(A_t, O_t; θ)`. This scalar score is designed to be indicative of successful trajectory characteristics, meaning values outside a certain range would suggest a failure. Crucially, the score network `D_M` is trained *only on successful demonstration data*. The framework investigates several categories of scalar score candidates, each with specific desiderata:
 - **One-class:** Must not require failure data for training.
 - **Lightweight:** Must allow for fast inference for real-time operation.
 - **Discriminative:** Must yield a clear separation in scores between successful and failed rollouts.

 The paper explores various score candidates (summarized in Table I):
 - **Learned Data Density:**
 - `lopO`: Uses a continuous normalizing flow (CNF) to estimate the likelihood `log p(O_t')` of an observation. Low likelihood indicates OOD.
 - **Novel `logpZO`:** This is a key contribution. It also uses a CNF but evaluates likelihood in the *latent noise space* (`log p(Z_O_t')`), where `Z_O_t'` is a noise estimate conditioned on `O_t'`. The squared norm `||Z_O_t'||^2_2` serves as the score; higher values indicate lower likelihood (OOD) and thus potential failure. This is hypothesized to be more robust than `lopO` as it avoids difficult high-dimensional divergence integration.
 - **Second-Order (Evidential Deep Learning):**
 - `NatPN`: Adapts a method that learns parameters for second-order distributions (Dirichlet prior on class probabilities) to estimate uncertainty. It discretizes observations using K-means.
 - `DER`: Multivariate Deep Evidential Regression, which models the distribution of actions given observations using a Gaussian with a Wishart prior.
 - **One-Class Discriminator:**
 - `RND` (Random Network Distillation): Trains a predictor network to mimic a fixed, random target network. Deviations between predictor and target outputs signal OOD inputs.
 - `CFM` (Consistency Flow Matching): Measures the curvature of the observation-to-noise forward flow; high curvature indicates OOD data.
 - **Post-hoc Metrics (computed analytically without learning):**
 - `SPARC`: Measures the smoothness of predicted actions, useful for detecting jitter-related failures.
 - **Baselines:** `STAC` (Statistical Temporal Action Consistency, which uses MMD on batches of predicted actions) and `PCA-kmeans` (distance of observation features to centroids of PCA-reduced training data). These baselines are integrated into FAIL-Detect's two-stage structure for fair comparison, though `STAC` originally uses a static CP threshold.

2. **Stage 2: Sequential Threshold Design with Conformal Prediction (CP):**
 Once scalar scores `D_M(A_t, O_t; θ)` are obtained, a time-varying threshold `η_t` is constructed. This stage leverages **functional Conformal Prediction (CP)** to provide statistical guarantees on the false positive rate.
 - **Calibration:** A set of successful rollouts, distinct from the training data for `D_M`, is used as calibration data.
 - **One-Sided Band:** Since only high score values indicate failure, a one-sided CP band `C_α = {[lower_t, upper_t]}` is constructed. `lower_t` is the minimum observed score.
 - **Time-Varying Threshold:** `upper_t` is set as `μ_t + h_t`, where `μ_t` is the time-varying mean of scores from calibration rollouts, and `h_t` is an adaptively computed bandwidth. This adaptive `h_t` allows the threshold to adjust to the changing dynamics and inherent variability of manipulation tasks over time, a significant improvement over static thresholds.
 - **Statistical Guarantee:** With a user-specified significance level `α` (e.g., 0.05), the CP framework guarantees that a new in-distribution (successful) trajectory's scores will fall within the computed band `C_α` with probability at least `1-α`.
 - **Decision Rule:** A failure is flagged at time `t` if `D_M(A_t, O_t; θ)` exceeds the time-varying threshold `η_t = upper_t`. This directly controls the false positive rate at level `α`.

**B. Experimental Validation:**

The methodology is thoroughly tested in diverse environments:
- **Simulation:** Four tasks from the Robomimic benchmark (Square, Transport, Can, Toolhang). OOD conditions are simulated by a camera bump.
- **Robot Hardware:** Two complex bimanual manipulation tasks on a Franka Emika Panda robot (FoldRedTowel, CleanUpSpill). OOD conditions include human disturbances, crumpled towels with distractors, and novel towel colors (Fig. 3, 8).
- **Policy Backbones:** Both Diffusion Policy (DP) and Flow Matching (FM) are used as underlying generative policy architectures to test the framework's versatility.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Standard metrics are used: True Positive Rate (TPR), True Negative Rate (TNR), Balanced Accuracy, Weighted Accuracy, and crucially, *Detection Time*, which measures the average time from rollout start to failure detection.
- **CP Band Calibration Modes:** Two modes for CP band calibration are compared: "setting-dependent" (calibrated on successful trajectories specific to the test environment, including OOD settings) and "ID-only" (calibrated only on successful trajectories from the nominal in-distribution environment).

This comprehensive approach allows for a rigorous evaluation of FAIL-Detect's ability to detect failures without failure data, its performance across different score types, its real-time feasibility, and its statistical robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive empirical evaluation of FAIL-Detect across diverse simulation and robot hardware tasks yields several significant findings:

**A. High Performance Without Failure Data:**
FAIL-Detect demonstrates impressive capabilities in detecting failures despite never being explicitly trained or calibrated on failure data.
- **High Accuracy:** The average best balanced accuracy across FAIL-Detect's score candidates is approximately 78% in simulation (Fig. 4) and 72% on robot hardware tasks (Fig. 5). This indicates that the framework can reliably distinguish between successful and failing executions in various scenarios.
- **Fast Detection:** Crucially, FAIL-Detect maintains viable detection times across different score designs. The best detection times are consistently faster than the average successful trajectory completion time, making the system practical for real-time robotic deployment.

**B. Superiority of Learned Scores Over Post-hoc Methods:**
A key insight from the experiments is the consistent outperformance of learned scalar scores compared to post-hoc, analytically computed metrics.
- **`logpZO` as the Top Performer:** The novel `logpZO` score, derived from a flow-based density estimator, emerges as the most consistent and robust performer across all experiments.
 - In simulation (Fig. 4), `logpZO` achieved top-1 performance in terms of combined accuracy (balanced and weighted) in 10 out of 16 cases and was in the top-3 for 14 out of 16 cases. It also frequently achieved the fastest detection times.
 - On robot hardware (Fig. 5), where data is scarcer and conditions more diverse, `logpZO` remained robust, securing top-1 highest balanced and weighted accuracy in 8 out of 12 scenarios. Its detection times remained practical, well below the average success trajectory time.
- **`RND` as a Strong Contender:** `RND` (Random Network Distillation), another learned one-class discriminator, also showed strong performance, consistently ranking among the top methods, though it never reached top-1 in hardware.
- **Underperformance of Baselines:**
 - **`STAC`**, a state-of-the-art baseline, generally exhibited lower accuracy compared to the best learned methods and suffered from critically slow detection times in simulation, often exceeding the average success trajectory duration. This limitation led to its exclusion from real robot hardware evaluations due to prohibitive latency.
 - **`PCA-kmeans`** also showed less consistent performance than the top learned methods, particularly in simulation accuracy.
- **Qualitative Evidence:** Visualizations of detection scores (Fig. 6, 9) corroborate these quantitative findings, showing that learned methods (like `logpZO` and `RND`) produce clearer separation between scores of successful and failed trajectories, unlike post-hoc approaches which often struggle to distinguish them effectively. The static threshold of `STAC` was also identified as a weakness.
- **Computational Efficiency:** Learned scores, particularly `logpZO`, offer a significant computational advantage over methods like `STAC` (e.g., 36-44 times faster in simulation), which require extensive sampling from the stochastic policy.

**C. Alignment with Human Intuition and Diagnostics:**
- **Physical Correlation:** The detected failure alerts from FAIL-Detect show a strong correlation with observable physical indications of failure in the environment (Fig. 7). Score spikes consistently align with critical moments when the robot loses grip, drops an object, or performs an action incorrectly.
- **Diagnostic Value:** This strong correspondence between quantitative scores and real-world events provides invaluable diagnostic capabilities. Instead of relying on an *a priori* enumeration of failure modes, researchers can use FAIL-Detect to identify *when* and *where* a failure occurs, enabling targeted analysis and more efficient iteration for policy improvement.

**D. Importance of Environment-Dependent Thresholds on Hardware:**
While "ID-only" calibrated CP bands (using only successful rollouts from nominal in-distribution settings) performed adequately in simulation, hardware experiments revealed their limitations. For complex OOD initial conditions on real robots, most methods showed degraded performance with ID-only bands. This is because even "successful" rollouts in challenging OOD environments might exhibit degraded quality (e.g., slower execution, jitter) that results in higher scores than ideal ID successes. "Setting-dependent" CP bands (calibrated on successful rollouts from the specific OOD environment) proved more effective on robot hardware tasks, adjusting the threshold to reflect the acceptable range of behaviors in the altered environment.

In summary, FAIL-Detect, particularly with the `logpZO` score, provides a robust, efficient, and practical solution for runtime failure detection in imitation learning-based robotics, fundamentally addressing the challenge of operating without explicit failure data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of FAIL-Detect holds significant implications for the field of robotics and the broader deployment of AI-driven autonomous systems:

1. **Enabling Trustworthy and Safe Robot Deployment:** This is arguably the most critical impact. By providing a reliable runtime failure detection mechanism with statistical guarantees, FAIL-Detect significantly enhances the trustworthiness and safety of robotic systems operating in safety-critical human environments. Early and accurate detection of failures allows for timely intervention (human or automated recovery), preventing undesirable behaviors, damage to equipment, or harm to humans. This is a crucial step towards broader acceptance and integration of autonomous robots in real-world applications.

2. **Overcoming the "No Failure Data" Bottleneck:** The paper's core contribution—detecting failures without requiring prior failure data for training or calibration—addresses a major practical limitation in robotics research and development. Collecting comprehensive failure datasets is notoriously difficult, expensive, and often infeasible given the vast and unpredictable space of potential failure modes. FAIL-Detect's "failure-free" approach liberates developers from this constraint, making robust failure detection more scalable and accessible for diverse robotic tasks and environments.

3. **Advancing Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection in Robotics:** By framing failure detection as a sequential OOD problem and successfully applying conformal prediction, this work contributes to the broader field of OOD detection. The introduction of the `logpZO` score, a novel flow-based density estimator that consistently outperforms other methods, provides a valuable new tool for identifying anomalous conditions in high-dimensional robot data.

4. **Improving Diagnostic Capabilities and Policy Iteration:** The strong correlation observed between FAIL-Detect's alerts and real-world physical failure events offers significant diagnostic value. Instead of relying on laborious manual analysis to understand *why* a policy failed, researchers can use the system's precise timing of alerts to quickly pinpoint the critical moments and associated environmental states. This can drastically accelerate the feedback loop for policy improvement, enabling more efficient debugging, data collection for specific failure modes (if desired), and overall policy refinement.

5. **Practical Real-Time Applicability:** The emphasis on lightweight models and fast inference speeds ensures that FAIL-Detect is suitable for real-time runtime monitoring. This is a non-negotiable requirement for practical robotic systems that need to react instantaneously to unfolding situations. The demonstrated computational advantage over state-of-the-art baselines like STAC further solidifies its utility for immediate deployment.

6. **Modular and Flexible Framework:** The two-stage, modular design of FAIL-Detect makes it highly flexible. It can readily integrate new types of scalar scores or advanced conformal prediction techniques as they emerge, ensuring its adaptability to future advancements in both robot learning and statistical methods.

While the paper acknowledges limitations, such as the potential for scores to over-rely on simple robot state information or the need for more advanced adaptive CP techniques in highly dynamic OOD settings, these points also serve as clear avenues for future research, indicating the fertile ground this work has opened up. Overall, FAIL-Detect represents a significant step forward in making advanced robot policies more reliable, safer, and ultimately, more deployable in complex and unpredictable real-world environments.
