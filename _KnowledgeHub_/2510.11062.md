# Stronger-MAS: Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning for Collaborative LLMs

> [!summary] Summary
> STRONGER-MAS, from UCSD and Intel, introduces an algorithm and system for training collaborative Large Language Model (LLM) agents using multi-agent reinforcement learning. This approach enables LLMs to learn specialized roles and coordinate effectively, achieving up to 99.5% accuracy in long-horizon planning tasks and consistent performance gains across various agentic domains.

## Problem

- Current Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) for LLMs primarily rely on 'prompt-only' designs, lacking mechanisms for iterative improvement through training.
- Applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) to LLM agents is largely confined to single-agent scenarios, without robust frameworks for training multi-agent policies.
- Standard group-based RL objectives assume identical prompts for candidate actions, which is often violated in MAS due to role-specific and turn-dependent prompts.

## Method

- Developed **AT-GRPO** (==Agent- and Turn-wise== Grouped RL), an algorithm that adapts group-based RL for MAS by using tree-structured sampling and agent/turn-wise grouping.
- Designed a novel distributed training system with independent LLM resource pools and CPU-based environment execution, supporting concurrent on-policy training for multiple LLM policies.
- Implemented agent-wise credit assignment using a mixed global and local reward design to balance individual specialization with overall team objectives.

## Results

- MAS + AT-GRPO achieved substantial performance gains, increasing accuracy from 14.0–**47.0%** to 96.0–**99.5%** in long-horizon planning tasks and delivering 3.87–**7.62%** gains in coding.
- Role-specialized policies learned distinct functions, evidenced by a performance drop from **96.0%** to **6.0%** when trained agents' roles were swapped.
- The framework demonstrated scalability, showing continuous performance gains (from **18.2%** to **47.7%** on AIME24) as more collaborative agents were added to the system.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - RL training within a MAS environment robustly reinforces and leads to the emergence of specialized, non-interchangeable roles among LLM agents.
> - The choice between a single role-sharing policy and multiple role-specialized policies for MAS depends on the functional distinctness and overlap of roles within a given task.
> - Directly applying single-agent group-based RL to multi-agent settings typically leads to performance degradation due to state heterogeneity and biased advantage estimations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The research on "STRONGER-MAS" is positioned at the intersection of three highly active and promising areas within artificial intelligence: Large Language Models (LLMs) as agents, Multi-Agent Systems (MAS), and Reinforcement Learning (RL).

**LLM Agents:** The foundational idea is that LLMs can serve as intelligent agents capable of decision-making, action-taking, and tool use within specific workflows. This concept has shown significant promise across diverse domains, from embodied control to software engineering and scientific discovery. The current challenge lies in enhancing their reliability, reasoning abilities, and capacity for long-horizon planning.

**Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) for LLMs:** A prevalent approach to improving LLM agent performance is to orchestrate multiple LLMs in a collaborative system, often assigning specialized roles (e.g., AutoGen, MetaGPT). This "prompt-only" augmentation typically leverages a single, shared LLM policy. More recent work has begun exploring "role-specialized policies," where distinct models are used for different roles, recognizing that a single LLM's performance can vary significantly across domains. However, the existing research in MAS for LLMs predominantly focuses on *inference-time* design, leaving the potential of *training* these multi-agent policies with RL largely underexplored.

**Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLMs:** RL treats the LLM as a policy and iteratively updates its parameters based on environmental rewards to strengthen decision-making. Frameworks like VERL and AReaL have enabled scalable rollouts and online updates for single LLM agents, demonstrating success in improving reasoning, planning, and tool use. However, these frameworks are primarily designed for single-agent scenarios.

**The Research Gap and STRONGER-MAS's Contribution:** The paper identifies a critical underexplored area: combining the power of RL training with the collaborative benefits of MAS. This combination, while intuitively appealing, poses significant challenges:
1. **Algorithmic Mismatch:** Standard group-based RL objectives (e.g., Group Relative Policy Optimization, GRPO) assume identical prompts for candidate actions. In MAS, prompts inherently differ by agent role and interaction turn, making direct application problematic.
2. **Systemic Limitations:** Existing LLM-RL training systems are typically tailored for single models, lacking the infrastructure to support concurrent multi-model rollouts, inter-agent environment interactions, and independent on-policy parameter updates for MAS workflows.

STRONGER-MAS directly addresses these challenges by introducing a novel algorithm, AT-GRPO (Agent- and Turn-wise Grouped RL), specifically adapted for MAS, and a new training system designed to support complex MAS workflows and multi-policy training. By bridging the gap between single-agent LLM-RL and multi-agent LLM systems, this work aims to unlock the synergistic potential of both paradigms, leading to more robust, capable, and collaborative LLM agents. It offers a "more comprehensive solution" compared to prior limited attempts at multi-agent RL for LLMs, which often focused on specific interaction settings, fixed roles, or narrow domains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation behind STRONGER-MAS is to harness the complementary strengths of Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) and Reinforcement Learning (RL) to enhance the performance and capabilities of Large Language Model (LLM) agents. While MAS offers role-based specialization and collaborative problem-solving, and RL provides a powerful mechanism for iteratively refining LLM policies based on environmental feedback, their integration for *training* truly collaborative LLMs remains largely uncharted territory.

The authors explicitly articulate several key objectives to address this gap:

1. **Develop an RL Algorithm for MAS:** The primary algorithmic objective is to design a Reinforcement Learning algorithm that can effectively adapt group-based policy optimization (like GRPO) to the unique dynamics of Multi-Agent Systems. This specifically entails addressing the challenge that prompts in MAS vary by agent role and interaction turn, invalidating the identical-prompt assumption of standard group-based RL.
2. **Design a Robust MAS Training System:** A crucial systemic objective is to build a training infrastructure capable of supporting the complex requirements of on-policy RL for MAS. This includes facilitating scalable rollouts based on diverse MAS workflows and enabling efficient on-policy updates for both single (role-sharing) and multiple (role-specific) policy models concurrently.
3. **Demonstrate Comprehensive Performance Gains:** The research aims to empirically validate the effectiveness of the proposed approach by demonstrating substantial and consistent performance improvements across a wide array of representative agentic domains, including game playing, long-horizon planning, code generation, and mathematical reasoning.
4. **Analyze Role Specialization and Policy Choice:** A deeper objective is to investigate how RL training within a MAS context influences role-specific specialization among agents. Furthermore, the work seeks to provide insights and guidance on when to opt for a single role-sharing policy versus multiple role-specialized policies, based on task characteristics.

In essence, the motivation stems from the observed limitations of current LLM agent approaches—either MAS workflows lack the benefits of learned policies, or RL training is confined to single agents. STRONGER-MAS seeks to overcome these limitations by providing both the algorithmic and systemic foundation for training collaborative LLM agents that are not only specialized but also continuously improve through interaction with their environment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in STRONGER-MAS is twofold, addressing both the algorithmic challenges of applying RL to MAS and the systemic requirements for efficient training.

#### 3.1. AT-GRPO Algorithm Design

The core of the algorithmic contribution is AT-GRPO (Agent- and Turn-wise Grouped RL), which adapts the concept of group-relative advantages from single-agent GRPO to the multi-agent setting. It incorporates three key ideas:

1. **Tree-structured Sampling:** To ensure that candidate actions within a group originate from an *identical prompt* (a prerequisite for valid GRPO advantage calculations), the method employs tree-structured sampling. At each turn (*t*) and for each agent (*i*) within an environment instance (*e*), *K* candidate actions are sampled from the current state. These *K* candidates form a valid comparison group because they share the same prompt (agent, turn, and interaction history). After advantages are computed for this group, the candidate action with the highest reward is greedily selected to proceed with the environment rollout, balancing exploration for learning with stable, high-quality trajectory generation.
2. **Agent- and Turn-wise Grouping:** Experiences are explicitly grouped based on the acting agent (*i*) and the turn number (*t*) within each parallel environment instance (*e*). A unique group key (e.g., a hash of `(e, i, t)`) is used to store the observation, *K* actions, and their *K* advantages. This ensures that the context (including role-specific prompts and interaction history) is identical for all samples within a group, making the group-relative advantage calculation meaningful.
3. **Agent-wise Credit Assignment:** To balance individual agent learning with the overall team objective, a mixed-reward design is utilized. Each agent *i* at turn *t* receives a reward `r_t,i` that is a convex combination of a global `r_team` (reflecting overall task success) and a local `r_loc,i` (evaluating its subtask performance). A hyperparameter `α` controls the balance between these two components. This encourages both specialized skill development and effective collaboration towards shared goals.

#### 3.2. MAS Training System

Recognizing the limitations of existing single-agent RL frameworks, the authors designed a novel distributed training system specifically for MAS:

1. **LLM Resource Pools (GPU):** Each policy (whether shared or role-specific) is managed within its own independent resource pool, typically pinned to a GPU group. Each pool contains a `RolloutWorker` for inference (generating actions) and an `UpdateWorker` for optimization (computing losses and updating parameters). This architecture enables concurrent on-policy training of multiple policies without interference.
2. **Environment Execution (CPU) and Data Flow:** Environment steps, which involve executing agent actions and computing rule-based rewards, are run on a fleet of CPU `EnvWorkers`. These `EnvWorkers` operate in sandboxed instances for safety and reproducibility, allowing thousands of concurrent rollouts. A central `Router` dispatches collected experience tuples (group key, observation, actions, advantages) to the appropriate `UpdateWorker` based on the policy (`σ(i)`) assigned to the agent that generated the experience. This ensures that each policy's training data remains strictly on-policy.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup

The methodology includes extensive empirical validation:

- **Models:** Qwen3 models (1.7B and 8B parameters) were used, operating in a "no-thinking" mode.
- **Tasks:** A diverse set of agentic tasks across four domains:
 - **Game:** 4x4 Sudoku, 6x6 Sokoban.
 - **Planning:** 10x10 grid-based Plan-Path.
 - **Coding:** LiveCodeBench, APPS, CodeContests.
 - **Mathematical Reasoning:** AIME24, AIME25, OlympiadBench.
- **Baselines:** A comprehensive comparison against five variants: single-agent (prompt-only), single-agent + GRPO, MAS (prompt-only, role-sharing backbone), MAS + GRPO (direct application), and the proposed MAS + AT-GRPO (with either shared or per-role policies).
- **Workflows:** Domain-specific MAS workflows were designed (e.g., Coder-Tester loop for code, Tool-User/Reasoner for math, Tool-User/Executor for game/plan) to facilitate collaborative interaction.
- **Ablation Studies:** Further analyses explored the importance of on-policy MAS training, the reinforcement of role specialization, the trade-offs between shared vs. role-specific policies, the limitations of direct GRPO application to MAS, and scalability with increasing agents. The impact of reward shaping was also ablated by using outcome-only rewards.

This comprehensive methodology ensures that the algorithmic contributions are theoretically sound, practically implementable, and rigorously evaluated across a broad spectrum of challenges relevant to LLM agents.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted by STRONGER-MAS yielded several significant findings, demonstrating the efficacy and robustness of their proposed AT-GRPO algorithm and MAS training system.

1. **Substantial Performance Gains, Especially in Long-Horizon Planning:**
 - MAS + AT-GRPO consistently delivered significant performance improvements across all tested domains.
 - On **long-horizon planning tasks** (game and planning), it showed a dramatic boost, increasing accuracy from a 14.0–47.0% single-agent RL baseline to an impressive 96.0–99.5%. This improvement is attributed to the emergence of effective collaboration, where specialized agents (e.g., a tool agent generating algorithms and a plan agent providing oversight) learn to coordinate.
 - For **coding tasks**, it achieved average gains of 3.87–7.62% over baselines.
 - For **mathematical reasoning tasks**, average gains ranged from 9.0–17.93%. The authors hypothesize that the slightly lower relative gains in code and math (compared to planning) might be due to base LLMs already being extensively trained on these common domains, leading to performance saturation, and the inherent diversity of problems posing a greater challenge for RL improvement.

2. **RL Training in MAS Reinforces Role-Specific Specialization:**
 - Analysis confirmed that training within the MAS environment with AT-GRPO leads to agents developing distinct and complementary skills.
 - For instance, on the Plan-Path task, swapping the trained role-specialized policies (e.g., putting the Planner in the Tool Agent's role and vice versa) resulted in a catastrophic performance drop from 96.0% to 6.0%. This definitively shows that agents learned highly specialized, non-interchangeable functions critical for collaboration.
 - The standardized rewards for both planning and tool-using agents consistently increased throughout training, indicating co-evolution and adaptation to each other's improving policies.
 - In coding and math workflows, the average number of turns required to solve tasks decreased over training, directly evidencing tighter alignment and more efficient collaboration.

3. **Choice Between Role-Sharing and Role-Specialized Policies Depends on Task Characteristics:**
 - The research explored the trade-off of training separate policies for each role versus a single shared policy.
 - For **coding tasks**, where roles like "Coder" and "Tester" are functionally distinct, role-specialized policies showed clear benefits (e.g., 3.05 points higher average accuracy for Qwen3 1.7B).
 - For **math tasks**, where roles such as "Tool-User" and "Reasoner" might have greater functional overlap (e.g., a tool agent often needs to reason to execute tool calls), a shared policy could sometimes be superior (e.g., 39.60% vs. 35.20% on OlympiadBench for Qwen3 1.7B). This suggests that sharing data across roles can be beneficial when the underlying skills are transferable.
 - For **game/planning tasks**, both shared and specialized policy configurations achieved near-optimal, saturated performance, making the policy choice less critical in these highly optimized scenarios.

4. **Limitations of Directly Applying GRPO to MAS:**
 - Empirical results showed that directly applying existing single-agent GRPO to MAS often led to performance degradation. For instance, Qwen3-8B's performance on CodeContests dropped from 17.60% to 10.30% with MAS + GRPO.
 - This degradation is attributed to the violation of the "identical-state" assumption in GRPO. In multi-turn MAS, interaction histories diverge across different policy rollouts, leading to heterogeneous states and biased advantage estimations, which destabilizes optimization. AT-GRPO specifically addresses this by enforcing agent- and turn-wise grouping.

5. **Scalability of MAS + AT-GRPO:**
 - The framework demonstrated successful scalability with an increasing number of collaborative agents (Reasoners and Tool-Users) in a modular MAS architecture.
 - On AIME24, MAS + AT-GRPO achieved continuous performance gains (from 18.2% to 47.7%) as more agents were added, whereas a direct MAS + GRPO baseline quickly saturated (at 34.1%), highlighting AT-GRPO's ability to effectively leverage increased ensemble size without hitting coordination bottlenecks.

6. **Superiority Over Other MARL Frameworks:**
 - Comparison with existing MARL frameworks like MAPORL (homogeneous agents), MARFT (single-turn), and CURE (single-turn code generation) using identical base models confirmed AT-GRPO's advantages.
 - STRONGER-MAS's untrained MAS outperformed trained MAPORL (84.4% vs 81.0% on math) due to heterogeneous agent roles and multi-turn interaction.
 - It also significantly outperformed MARFT (84.4% untrained vs 78.7% trained on math) and CURE (30.3% untrained vs 25.9% CURE baseline on CodeContests), underscoring the critical efficacy of iterative alignment and self-refinement cycles enabled by multi-turn, heterogeneous agent workflows.

7. **Robustness to Reward Shaping:**
 - An ablation study showed that AT-GRPO's effectiveness is largely independent of dense, task-specific heuristic rewards. Even when using only sparse, outcome-only rewards, the method maintained high performance and significantly outperformed baselines, demonstrating that the cooperative group optimization mechanism itself is robust and capable of solving complex tasks without explicit "oracle" guidance.

Overall, the findings strongly validate AT-GRPO and its associated training system as a highly effective approach for developing collaborative LLM agents, particularly for challenging long-horizon tasks, while also providing valuable insights into the dynamics of multi-agent RL for LLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The STRONGER-MAS paper represents a significant step forward in the field of Large Language Model (LLM) agents, offering both an innovative algorithmic solution and a practical system for training truly collaborative LLM multi-agent systems (MAS) using reinforcement learning (RL). Its potential impact spans several dimensions:

1. **Advancing LLM Agent Capabilities:** By effectively integrating on-policy RL with MAS, STRONGER-MAS unlocks a new paradigm for enhancing LLM agents. It demonstrates that LLMs can learn to collaborate more effectively, specialize their roles through training, and tackle complex, long-horizon tasks that are intractable for single-agent LLMs or prompt-only MAS. The dramatic performance gains in planning tasks (up to 99.5% accuracy) underscore this capability.

2. **Addressing Key Research Bottlenecks:** The work directly tackles and solves fundamental challenges in multi-agent LLM-RL: the breakdown of standard RL assumptions (like identical prompts for group-based optimization) in MAS, and the lack of systemic infrastructure for multi-policy, multi-agent training. The AT-GRPO algorithm and the MAS training system provide a robust blueprint for future research in this domain.

3. **Guiding MAS Design Principles:** The findings offer crucial insights into the design of effective LLM-based MAS. The nuanced conclusion that the choice between role-sharing and role-specialized policies depends on task characteristics (e.g., distinct roles benefiting from specialization, overlapping roles from sharing) provides valuable guidance for practitioners and researchers creating multi-agent architectures.

4. **Enhancing Reliability and Efficiency:** The demonstrated reduction in "turns to alignment" during training signifies that RL-trained MAS not only perform better but also become more efficient in their collaborative problem-solving. This improved reliability and efficiency are critical for deploying LLM agents in real-world applications.

5. **Scalability for Complex Problems:** The scalability analysis shows that AT-GRPO can effectively leverage an increasing number of agents to achieve continuous performance gains, overcoming the coordination bottlenecks observed in baseline methods. This suggests its applicability to increasingly complex problems requiring large-scale agent collaboration.

6. **Broader Applications and Societal Impact:** The ability to train highly collaborative and specialized LLM agents has profound implications across various sectors:
 - **Software Engineering:** More reliable code generation and testing, automated bug fixing, and complex project management.
 - **Scientific Discovery:** Accelerated hypothesis generation, experimental design, and data analysis in fields like drug discovery and materials science.
 - **Complex Planning and Logistics:** Improved autonomous systems for supply chain optimization, disaster response, and robotic coordination.
 - **Education and Training:** Development of more intelligent tutoring systems and collaborative learning environments.

7. **Foundation for Future Research:** This paper lays a strong foundation for future exploration into emergent behaviors in LLM-based MAS, more sophisticated credit assignment mechanisms, adaptation to dynamic environments, and the development of even more general-purpose multi-agent RL algorithms. The open-sourcing of code and environment (PettingLLMs) will further accelerate research in this area.

While acknowledging the dual-use risks inherent in autonomous systems, the authors emphasize ethical considerations and safety measures, ensuring that the advancements are developed responsibly. Overall, STRONGER-MAS positions itself as a pivotal contribution, pushing the boundaries of what collaborative LLM agents can achieve and paving the way for a new generation of intelligent, adaptive, and highly capable AI systems.
