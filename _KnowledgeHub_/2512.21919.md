# SWE-RM: Execution-free Feedback For Software Engineering Agents

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology and Alibaba Group developed SWE-RM, an execution-free reward model that provides reliable, fine-grained feedback for software engineering agents. This model substantially improves agent performance, boosting accuracy by 7.6 to 10.4 absolute percentage points in test-time scaling and increasing resolve rates by 3 absolute percentage points in reinforcement learning on SWE-Bench Verified.

## Problem

- Traditional execution-based feedback for software engineering agents (via unit tests) is sparse and binary, providing insufficient granularity for efficient reinforcement learning.
- The reliance on unit tests for feedback is often hindered by their unreliability, limited coverage, and high computational cost for large-scale training.
- Existing execution-free reward models for software engineering tasks primarily focus on test-time scaling, lacking a comprehensive understanding of their suitability and properties for reinforcement learning.

## Method

- Formulated reward modeling as a ==generative classification== task, where the model outputs  or  tokens indicating task resolution, with continuous scores derived from token log probabilities.
- Utilized the Qwen3-30B-A3B model as the backbone and trained it with supervised fine-tuning on over 400k diverse, multi-turn agent trajectories, featuring a crucial 256k context length.
- Established a comprehensive evaluation framework including Test-Time Scaling (TTS), Area Under the Curve (AUC) for discriminative ability, and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) for confidence reliability, to assess reward models' versatility for both ranking and reinforcement learning.

## Results

- SWE-RM achieved state-of-the-art Test-Time Scaling performance on SWE-Bench Verified, improving Qwen3-Coder-Flash accuracy by 10.4 absolute percentage points (to **62.0%**) and Qwen3-Coder-Max accuracy by 7.6 absolute percentage points (to **74.6%**).
- Integrating SWE-RM's execution-free feedback with execution-based signals for reinforcement learning improved resolve rates by 3 absolute percentage points (**54.8%** vs. **51.8%**) on SWE-Bench Verified, demonstrating enhanced training efficiency and stability.
- Extensive ablation studies showed that factors like increased data scale, a 2:1 positive-to-negative training ratio, and a 256k context length are critical for building robust reward models with superior discriminative ability (AUC) and confidence reliability (ECE).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Test-Time Scaling (TTS) performance alone is insufficient for evaluating reward models, as high TTS does not guarantee effective or stable Reinforcement Learning (RL); AUC and ECE are critical for assessing discriminative ability and confidence reliability.
> - Training data scale and the ratio of positive-to-negative examples significantly impact reward model quality, with larger datasets and a 2:1 positive-to-negative ratio yielding better calibration and discrimination.
> - Employing a 256k context length in the reward model is vital for processing long, complex agent trajectories, enabling 100% scoring coverage and substantially boosting overall performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The automation of software development tasks using large language models (LLMs) represents a rapidly evolving and critical frontier in AI research. This field encompasses the development of "coding agents" that can understand, debug, and even implement software changes. A cornerstone of developing effective coding agents is the feedback mechanism used during their training and evaluation, particularly in paradigms like reinforcement learning (RL) and test-time scaling (TTS).

Historically, feedback for coding agents has primarily relied on **execution-based verifiers**, which typically involve running unit tests to determine if a generated code solution passes or fails. Examples include Agentless (Xia et al., 2024), R2E-Gym (Jain et al., 2025), and DeepSWE (Luo et al., 2025). While providing concrete binary outcomes, this approach suffers from several fundamental limitations:
1. **Sparsity:** Unit tests yield a sparse, binary (pass/fail) signal, offering limited granularity. This makes it difficult to differentiate between varying degrees of correctness or progress for unsuccessful attempts.
2. **Reliability and Coverage:** The effectiveness of execution-based feedback is highly dependent on the quality and comprehensive coverage of unit test suites. Real-world or model-generated unit tests can often be overly specific, irrelevant, or simply unreliable, leading to noisy or misleading feedback.
3. **Scalability:** Collecting and running extensive unit tests for every generated trajectory, especially in multi-turn interactive environments, can be computationally expensive and slow, hindering large-scale RL training.

In contrast, **execution-free verifiers**, often implemented as model-based reward models, aim to provide more continuous and fine-grained feedback without requiring actual code execution in a sandbox environment. Early explorations in this area include SWE-Gym (Pan et al., 2025) and OpenHands Critic (Team, 2025). However, as the paper highlights, existing work on execution-free verifiers for software engineering tasks has largely focused on their performance in test-time scaling (TTS) scenarios, with limited attention paid to their effectiveness in reinforcement learning (RL) or a comprehensive understanding of what constitutes a "versatile" reward model.

This paper directly addresses these gaps by:
- **Challenging the sole reliance on TTS:** It empirically demonstrates that high TTS performance alone does not guarantee effective RL training, revealing a crucial blind spot in prior research.
- **Introducing a comprehensive evaluation framework:** It expands beyond TTS to incorporate metrics crucial for RL, namely Area Under the Curve (AUC) for discriminative ability and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) for confidence reliability.
- **Systematically investigating reward model training:** It provides the first large-scale ablation study on factors influencing the quality of execution-free reward models for SWE, offering a practical recipe for their development.
- **Pioneering execution-free feedback in agentic RL for SWE:** It is among the first to successfully integrate execution-free feedback into multi-turn, agentic RL settings for SWE, demonstrating significant improvements in training efficiency and performance.

By providing a systematic study and a new state-of-the-art reward model (SWE-RM), this work significantly advances the understanding and practical application of execution-free feedback, aiming to overcome the limitations of traditional execution-based approaches and unlock the full potential of LLMs for software engineering automation. It positions itself as a foundational step toward building more robust, efficient, and intelligent SWE agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **develop a versatile and effective execution-free reward model that can significantly enhance the performance of software engineering (SWE) agents across both test-time scaling (TTS) and reinforcement learning (RL) paradigms.**

The motivations for pursuing this objective stem from the inherent limitations of current feedback mechanisms in SWE agent development:

1. **Sparsity and Lack of Granularity in Execution-Based Feedback:** Traditional execution-based feedback, primarily relying on unit tests, provides only a sparse, binary (pass/fail) signal. This makes it challenging for RL agents to learn effectively, as it cannot distinguish between different unsuccessful trajectories, nor can it provide fine-grained signals about the quality or proximity to a correct solution for successful ones. This sparse signal hinders efficient exploration and reward shaping in RL.
2. **Unreliability and Limited Coverage of Unit Tests:** The accuracy of execution-based feedback hinges on the quality and comprehensiveness of unit test cases. In real-world software development, unit tests might be incomplete, overly specific, or even unrelated to the actual issue being addressed, especially when tasks are extracted from real GitHub repositories or tests are model-generated. Such unreliable feedback can lead to misleading reward signals, undermining RL training.
3. **Underexplored Potential of Execution-Free Feedback:** While execution-free reward models offer the promise of continuous, fine-grained scores across entire trajectories, their application and properties within the context of realistic SWE agents, particularly for RL training, remain largely underexplored and poorly understood.
4. **Discrepancy Between TTS Performance and RL Effectiveness:** A critical observation by the authors is that two reward models showing nearly identical performance in Test-Time Scaling (TTS) – which measures the ability to select the best solution among candidates – can exhibit drastically different behaviors when used for RL training. This highlights that TTS alone is an insufficient metric for evaluating a reward model's suitability for RL. This disparity motivates a deeper investigation into what truly defines a "versatile" reward model.

To address these motivations, the authors set out to answer a fundamental research question: "What properties determine a reward model’s effectiveness in RL training, and how can we develop an all-round SWE reward model that performs well in both TTS and RL?" This leads to the key objectives of:
- Identifying additional, crucial metrics beyond TTS (specifically, AUC for discriminative ability and ECE for calibration) that are essential for evaluating reward models for RL.
- Conducting comprehensive controlled experiments to understand how various training factors (data scale, positive/negative ratios, policy mixtures, data sources, context length) impact these newly identified metrics and overall reward model robustness.
- Developing **SWE-RM**, an accurate and robust execution-free reward model based on these findings, aiming to set a new state-of-the-art in both TTS performance and RL effectiveness for SWE agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors adopted a comprehensive methodology to develop and evaluate SWE-RM, focusing on identifying crucial properties for versatile reward models and systematically investigating their training.

#### 3.1. Reward Model Formulation and Training

- **Generative Classification Task:** Following previous work like SWE-Gym (Pan et al., 2025), reward modeling is framed as a generative classification task. The reward model takes a full multi-turn agent trajectory (including user prompts, assistant actions, and tool responses) as input. It then outputs a special token, either `<YES>` (indicating a resolved task) or `<NO>` (unresolved).
- **Score Calculation:** During inference, the final continuous reward score $r \in$ is derived from the log probabilities of the `<YES>` and `<NO>` tokens using the softmax function: $r = \exp(l_y) / (\exp(l_y) + \exp(l_n))$.
- **Supervised Fine-tuning:** The model is trained using standard next-token prediction loss on this special classification token, employing supervised fine-tuning.
- **Base Model:** The **Qwen3-30B-A3B** model (a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture with 30 billion total parameters and 3 billion activated during inference) is used as the backbone for reward model training. This choice is driven by its balance of efficiency and strong coding capabilities, aligning with prevalent practices in state-of-the-art coding agents.
- **Context Length:** The models are trained to support a **256k context length**, a significant advancement over previous verifiers (typically 32k), enabling the scoring of complex and long trajectories often encountered in challenging SWE tasks.

#### 3.2. Data Collection and Labeling

- **Large-Scale Trajectory Collection:** Over 400k multi-turn agent trajectories (up to 100 iterations) are collected using various policy models (Qwen3-Coder-Max, Qwen3-Coder-Flash, Claude-4-sonnet) and agent scaffolds (OpenHands, SWE-Agent).
- **Diverse Data Sources:** The data is gathered from a mixture of prominent SWE datasets, including SWE-Gym, SWE-rebench, SWE-smith, and R2E-Gym.
- **Ground Truth Labeling:** Trajectories are labeled as "positive" (resolved) or "negative" (unresolved) based on their execution results using provided fail2pass unit tests. Data cleaning, such as filtering instances without successful trajectories, is applied to enhance label quality, acknowledging potential noise in unit test-based supervision.

#### 3.3. Evaluation Metrics Beyond TTS

Recognizing the limitations of solely relying on Test-Time Scaling (TTS), the authors introduce a more comprehensive evaluation toolkit:
- **Test-Time Scaling (TTS):** Measured as "RM@K," representing the resolve rate of the top-ranked trajectory chosen by the reward model from $k$ sampled trajectories. This assesses the model's ability to rank the correct solution highest. The primary evaluation is on SWE-Bench Verified using 32 independent runs per instance.
- **Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC):** This metric evaluates the model's **discriminative ability** by measuring how well it separates resolved from unresolved trajectories across the entire distribution, rather than just the top-1. A higher AUC indicates better separation.
- **Expected Calibration Error (ECE):** This metric quantifies the **confidence reliability** or **calibration** of the reward model. It measures the alignment between the model's predicted confidence scores and the empirical correctness. A lower ECE signifies that the model's scores reliably correspond to the true probability of correctness.

#### 3.4. Ablation Studies

Extensive, large-scale ablation studies are conducted to understand the impact of various training factors on TTS, AUC, and ECE:
- **Training Data Scale:** Investigating the effect of dataset size (e.g., 500 to 100k samples) on all three metrics.
- **Positive-to-Negative Sample Ratio:** Analyzing how different ratios (e.g., 2:1, 1:1, 1:2) influence performance.
- **Policy Mixtures:** Comparing models trained on on-policy (generated by the agent itself), off-policy (generated by other agents like Claude-sonnet-4), and mixed-policy data.
- **Data Source Composition:** Examining the impact of using single data sources (SWE-rebench, SWE-smith, SWE-Gym) versus various mixtures of these sources.
- **Context Length Constraint:** Evaluating the effect of increasing context window from 16k to 256k tokens on score rate and TTS performance.

#### 3.5. Reinforcement Learning Setup

- **Agent Scaffold:** OpenHands is used for multi-turn tool interactions in a sandbox environment.
- **RL Algorithm:** GSPO (Group Sequence Policy Optimization) is adapted for stability, especially for Mixture-of-Experts RL training.
- **Reward Formulation:** A hybrid reward function is proposed, combining execution-free feedback from SWE-RM ($Score_{EF}$) with sparse execution-based signals:
 $r(q, \tau_i) = \begin{cases} 1 + Score_{EF}(q, \tau_i, \text{patch}_i), & \text{if issue resolved} \\ -0.5 + Score_{EF}(q, \tau_i, \text{patch}_i), & \text{if unfinished} \\ 0 + Score_{EF}(q, \tau_i, \text{patch}_i), & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$
- **Baselines for RL:** Comparisons are made against execution-based only feedback, execution-free only feedback (from SWE-RM), and feedback from a poorly calibrated execution-free reward model.

This systematic and multi-faceted approach allowed the authors to not only build a high-performing reward model but also to thoroughly understand the underlying factors contributing to its versatility and effectiveness for advancing SWE agents.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields several significant findings, demonstrating the effectiveness and versatility of SWE-RM across both test-time scaling (TTS) and reinforcement learning (RL).

#### 4.1. Limitations of TTS and the Importance of AUC and Calibration

- The initial findings reveal that two verifiers with nearly identical TTS performance can lead to drastically different outcomes in RL training. Verifier A, with good AUC and calibration, enables smooth RL improvement, while Verifier B, despite similar TTS, causes RL training instability and collapse.
- This discrepancy is explained by the introduction of **AUC** (discriminative ability) and **ECE** (calibration). Verifier A (AUC = 0.764, ECE = 0.077) significantly outperforms Verifier B (AUC = 0.669, ECE = 0.210) in these metrics, even with comparable TTS. This highlights that AUC and ECE provide crucial, complementary information about a reward model's quality, particularly for providing reliable signals in RL. Poor calibration leads to misleading confidence scores and unstable training.

#### 4.2. Impact of Training Factors on Reward Model Performance (Ablation Studies)

- **Data Scaling:** Increasing training data scale from 500 to 25k samples substantially improves TTS performance and, crucially, discriminative ability (clearer separation of scores for resolved/unresolved trajectories) and calibration (ECE drops from 0.481 to 0.137). Further scaling to 100k shows diminishing returns for TTS but continues to improve calibration (ECE of 0.067). This underscores that larger datasets lead to more reliable and robust reward models.
- **Positive-to-Negative Ratio:** A **2:1 ratio of positive to negative trajectories** consistently yields the best overall performance across AUC, ECE, and RM@32 for both Qwen3-Coder-Flash and Qwen3-Coder-Max. This ratio effectively balances the utilization of scarce positive data with sufficient negative examples for robust learning.
- **Context Length:** Scaling the context length of the reward model from 16k to **256k tokens** dramatically increases the "score rate" (percentage of trajectories successfully scored) from 0.5% to 100%. This directly translates to significant improvements in RM@32 performance (from 66.8% to 74.4%), as the model can now process and score complex, long trajectories that would otherwise be truncated or ignored.
- **Policy and Source Ablation:**
 - **Mixed-Policy** training (combining on-policy and off-policy data) generally offers a better balance across AUC, ECE, and ranking compared to solely on-policy or off-policy data, indicating improved generalization.
 - While **SWE-rebench** data alone yields strong results, combining it with **SWE-smith** and **SWE-Gym** further improves calibration (lower ECE) and enhances data scaling effects, leading to a more robust final model.

#### 4.3. SWE-RM Performance in Test-Time Scaling (TTS)

- **State-of-the-Art:** SWE-RM achieves new state-of-the-art performance among open-source models on SWE-Bench Verified.
 - It boosts Qwen3-Coder-Flash accuracy from 51.6% to **62.0%** (+10.4 absolute points).
 - It raises Qwen3-Coder-Max accuracy from 67.0% to **74.6%** (+7.6 absolute points).
- **Superior Versatility:** SWE-RM consistently outperforms all execution-based (Agentless, DeepSWE-EB) and existing execution-free verifiers (SWE-Gym Verifier, DeepSWE-EF) across all three metrics (AUC, ECE, RM@32) and across different base models (OpenHands-LM-32B, Qwen3-Coder-Flash, Qwen3-Coder-Max). It demonstrates superior discrimination (higher AUC) and calibration (lower ECE) compared to all baselines.

#### 4.4. SWE-RM Performance in Reinforcement Learning (RL)

- **Enhanced RL Training:** Using **hybrid feedback** (combining SWE-RM's execution-free signals with execution-based outcomes) yields the best RL performance and efficiency. It improves the resolve rate by **3 absolute points** (54.8% vs. 51.8%) over execution-based only feedback on SWE-Bench Verified, showing faster and smoother improvements.
- **Benefits of Continuous Signals:** Execution-free feedback alone shows faster initial progress than execution-based feedback due to its continuous nature, though it may converge less strongly without verified signals.
- **Consequences of Poor Calibration in RL:** Reinforcement learning with a poorly calibrated reward model (even one with comparable TTS) leads to significant performance degradation, highlighting the critical role of calibration for stable and effective RL.
- **Generalization Across Tasks:** Hybrid feedback consistently achieves better RL performance across a broader suite of SWE tasks, including SWE-Bench Live (Lite), SWE-Bench Multilingual, Multi-SWE-Bench Mini, and Terminal Bench, demonstrating the generalizability of the approach.

In summary, the main findings conclusively demonstrate that for developing robust SWE agents, execution-free reward models need to be evaluated and trained not just for top-1 ranking ability (TTS), but also for their discriminative power (AUC) and confidence reliability (ECE). SWE-RM, developed following these principles, significantly advances the state-of-the-art in both evaluating and training SWE agents.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research marks a substantial contribution to the field of large language models for software engineering (SWE) agents, with significant implications and potential impact:

1. **Redefining Reward Model Evaluation for SWE Agents:** The most immediate and profound impact is the shift in how reward models for SWE agents are evaluated. By empirically demonstrating the inadequacy of Test-Time Scaling (TTS) alone and introducing Area Under the Curve (AUC) and Expected Calibration Error (ECE) as crucial, complementary metrics, the paper provides a more holistic and robust framework for assessing verifier quality. This new understanding will guide future research to build reward models that are not only good at picking the best solution but also provide reliable and nuanced feedback essential for stable reinforcement learning.

2. **Unlocking the Potential of Execution-Free Feedback for RL:** The paper pioneers the systematic integration of execution-free feedback into agentic reinforcement learning (RL) for SWE tasks. Historically, RL for coding has been hampered by sparse and noisy execution-based signals. SWE-RM demonstrates that well-calibrated, continuous execution-free rewards can significantly accelerate and stabilize RL training, leading to substantial performance gains (e.g., +3 absolute points over execution-based counterparts). This breakthrough opens new avenues for developing more sophisticated and generalizable SWE agents.

3. **Providing a Practical Recipe for Robust Reward Model Training:** Through extensive, large-scale ablation studies, the authors distill a practical and comprehensive recipe for training versatile execution-free reward models. Key insights regarding optimal data scale, positive-to-negative sample ratios (2:1), mixed policy data, diverse data sources, and the critical importance of a long context window (256k tokens) offer invaluable guidance for researchers and practitioners. This reduces the trial-and-error often associated with developing such models.

4. **Advancing State-of-the-Art in SWE Agent Performance:** The developed SWE-RM achieves new state-of-the-art performance among open-source models on SWE-Bench Verified for TTS, boosting accuracies of leading models like Qwen3-Coder-Flash and Qwen3-Coder-Max by over 7-10 absolute points. This directly translates to more capable and reliable software engineering agents. The MoE architecture (30B total, 3B activated) combined with a 256k context length demonstrates that efficient, powerful reward models are achievable.

5. **Addressing Real-World Challenges in Software Development:** By improving the ability of LLMs to automatically resolve complex GitHub issues and other software engineering tasks, this research moves closer to real-world deployment of AI in software development. More reliable and efficient agents could reduce developer workload, accelerate debugging cycles, and improve software quality.

6. **Broader Impact on LLM-based Agents:** The principles and methodology established for training and evaluating versatile reward models are not limited to SWE tasks. The insights into discriminative ability and calibration are likely transferable to other complex, multi-step reasoning tasks where LLMs act as agents, such as scientific discovery, complex problem-solving, or general-purpose AI assistants.

In conclusion, "SWE-RM" is a foundational work that not only pushes the technical boundaries of software engineering agents but also provides critical methodological advancements for the broader field of LLM-based agent development. Its comprehensive approach to feedback mechanism design and evaluation will likely set new standards and inspire future innovations in autonomous AI agents.
