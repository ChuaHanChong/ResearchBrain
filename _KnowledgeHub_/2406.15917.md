# To Err is Robotic: Rapid Value-Based Trial-and-Error during Deployment

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Stanford University developed Bellman-Guided Retrials (BGR), an algorithm that enables pre-trained robot policies to rapidly adapt to novel situations by systematically detecting and recovering from mistakes during deployment. BGR achieves over 20% increased success rates in simulation and over 50% in real-world tasks by intelligently biasing policy sampling away from failed strategies, without requiring new data or expert supervision.

## Problem

- State-of-the-art imitation learning policies struggle when confronted with novel, out-of-distribution scenarios not covered in their training data.
- Existing error recovery mechanisms in robots are often implicit, undirected, and unreliable, leading to compounding errors or unrecoverable states.
- Robots lack an explicit mechanism for systematically trying different strategies, evaluating their effectiveness, and intelligently switching plans when initial attempts fail.

## Method

- ==Bellman-Guided Retrials== (**BGR**) is introduced, enhancing a base robot policy with explicit trial-and-error capabilities during deployment, utilizing only the original expert demonstration data.
- A ==value function== is trained on expert demonstrations to continuously monitor the robot's progress and detect suboptimality by comparing expected progress rates to achieved rates.
- Upon detecting suboptimality, the robot recovers to a neutral state and resamples a new strategy, non-parametrically biasing policy execution away from previously failed states or behaviors.

## Results

- BGR consistently and significantly increased success rates, showing over **20%** improvement in simulation (e.g., CaddyGrasp, SimObjectLift) and over **50%** improvement in real-world tasks (e.g., RealObjectLift, DoorOpening).
- The strategy evaluation method demonstrated timely and effective detection of suboptimality, often within a few frames of an object slipping, enabling rapid recovery and preventing progression into unrecoverable states.
- Non-parametric behavior skewing contributed to improved strategy diversity, allowing robots to explore different approaches and avoid repeating past failures in challenging adversarial scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Value functions, trained solely on expert demonstrations, can effectively serve as self-monitoring tools for detecting suboptimal robot progress and task failure during deployment, without needing external reward signals.
> - Non-parametric behavior skewing, by avoiding states associated with past failures, encourages diverse exploration of strategies and prevents repeated mistakes, leading to more robust adaptation.
> - An explicit try-evaluate-retry loop, guided by value function predictions, allows robots to learn from their deployment-time errors and adapt to hidden environmental parameters.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

#### 1.1. Limitations of Existing Robot Learning

The field of robot learning, particularly through imitation learning (learning from demonstrations), has made remarkable strides in enabling robots to acquire complex skills. State-of-the-art methods, often leveraging diverse datasets and advanced policy architectures (e.g., Diffusion Policy, Robotics Transformers), can learn sophisticated real-world strategies for tasks like object manipulation. However, a significant limitation persists: these learned policies often struggle when confronted with *novel scenarios* or *out-of-distribution states* that deviate from their training data.

When faced with unforeseen circumstances, current robot policies, even those trained on diverse data, tend to exhibit several shortcomings:
- **Implicit vs. Explicit Recovery:** While some degree of error recovery might emerge implicitly from diverse training data, it is typically undirected and unreliable for genuinely novel situations. The robot might repeat the same suboptimal strategy or drift into unrecoverable states.
- **Compounding Errors:** A small initial mistake in a novel scenario can lead the robot into an increasingly unfamiliar situation, further impairing the policy's performance and making recovery even harder, as the policy operates further from its familiar data distribution.
- **Lack of Systematic Trial-and-Error:** Existing methods generally lack an explicit mechanism for systematically trying different strategies, evaluating their effectiveness, and intelligently switching plans when current attempts fail.

The related work section explicitly discusses these limitations under "Learning From Demonstrations," highlighting that expressive policies are not sufficient to guarantee success in new scenarios, especially when encountering out-of-distribution error states. It also notes that many fast adaptation methods rely on external supervision or well-crafted data distributions for meta-learning, which can be restrictive for real-world deployment.

#### 1.2. The Niche of this Work

"To Err is Robotic" directly addresses these critical limitations by proposing a framework that endows expert-trained robot policies with an *explicit and systematic strategy retrying behavior*. It carves out a niche by focusing on *post-deployment adaptation without additional expert supervision or data*.

This work distinguishes itself by:
- **Moving Beyond Implicit Recovery:** Instead of hoping recovery behavior emerges from data, it instills an active "try, evaluate, and retry" loop.
- **Leveraging Existing Data for Value Estimation:** It intelligently re-uses the *same expert demonstration data* (used to train the base policy) to train a value function. This value function then serves as a self-monitoring tool to detect suboptimal progress. This is a significant advantage as it avoids the need for new reward signals or human intervention during adaptation.
- **Non-Parametric Strategy Skewing:** The method introduces a novel way to adapt by biasing the base policy's sampling process *away from previously failed states*, without retraining the policy itself. This allows for rapid, flexible adaptation to new environmental parameters.
- **Robustness to Novelty:** By constantly monitoring progress and adapting strategies, the robot becomes more robust to novel objects, obstacles, and dynamic changes that might cause initial strategies to fail.

The paper fits into the growing research area of "Single-Life RL" and adaptation without supervision, which aims to make robots robust enough to operate autonomously in real-world scenarios where constant resets or human interventions are impractical. It offers a practical and effective solution for improving the deployment-time robustness and success rates of powerful, pre-trained robot policies.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The core objective of this research is to **enable robots to rapidly and systematically adapt to novel scenarios by learning from their mistakes during deployment, without requiring additional external supervision or new data collection.**

The authors are motivated by the fundamental challenge that real-world robots frequently encounter situations not explicitly covered in their training data. While modern imitation learning policies can be highly skilled, they often fail catastrophically when faced with novelty because they lack a robust mechanism for trying different approaches, evaluating their effectiveness, and switching plans. The current reliance on implicitly learned recovery mechanisms from diverse data is deemed insufficient, as it may lead to undirected retrying, compounding errors, or reaching unrecoverable states.

Specifically, the paper seeks to address:
- **The need for explicit trial-and-error:** Moving beyond passive imitation to active exploration of strategies when initial attempts falter.
- **Efficient mistake detection:** Developing a reliable mechanism to identify when a current strategy is suboptimal before it leads to irreversible failure or excessive time waste.
- **Intelligent replanning:** Ensuring that subsequent attempts are not mere repetitions of previous failures but are informed by past experiences, thereby enabling faster convergence to a successful strategy.
- **Leveraging existing resources:** Designing a solution that builds upon existing trained policies and utilizes the already collected expert demonstration data, minimizing additional overhead.

The overarching goal is to make robots more resilient and practical for deployment in unstructured human environments, where perfect first attempts are rare and the ability to learn from errors is paramount.

### 3. Methodology and Approach: Bellman-Guided Retrials

The authors introduce **Bellman-Guided Retrials (BGR)**, an algorithm designed to enhance a base robot policy with explicit trial-and-error capabilities during deployment. BGR operates without external reward signals, expert interventions, or additional data beyond the initial expert demonstrations used for policy training.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation: Hidden-Parameter MDPs

The problem is framed within a Hidden-Parameter Markov Decision Process (HiP-MDP) (S, A, Z, p_s(s_0), p_z(z), p(s'|s, a, z), r(s, a, z)). Here, 'z' represents a hidden variable that influences the dynamics and rewards, effectively defining a novel scenario. During training, expert demonstrations cover various 'z's. During test time, the robot encounters a *new* 'z' and must infer its properties by interacting with the environment to solve the task.

#### 3.2. Overview of the Bellman-Guided Retrials Algorithm

BGR consists of three main stages (illustrated in Figure 1):
1. **Training Time (A):** A base policy ($\pi_\theta$) and a value function ($V_\phi$) are trained using a dataset of expert demonstrations. The base policy captures a repertoire of strategies, while the value function learns to estimate task completion time.
2. **Deployment in Novel Situation (B & C):**
 - **Evaluate Progress (B):** During task execution, BGR monitors the robot's progress by comparing the expected rate of progress (from $V_\phi$) to the achieved rate.
 - **Detect Suboptimality:** If the current strategy fails to make progress at a reasonable rate (i.e., it's slower than expected), BGR detects suboptimality.
 - **Recover and Retry (C):** Upon detection, the robot is recovered (e.g., arm retraction). A new strategy is then sampled from the base policy, but with a crucial modification: the sampling is *skewed* to avoid behaviors that have recently led to failure. This process implicitly helps the robot discover aspects of the hidden parameter 'z' and adapt.

#### 3.3. Strategy Evaluation with Value Functions

This is a critical component of BGR.
- **Value Function Training:** $V_\phi$ is trained on the same expert demonstrations as $\pi_\theta$. Assuming the last state ($s_T$) in an expert trajectory is a success state, a sparse reward is assigned ($r_+$ for success, $r_-$ for others). $V_\phi(s_t)$ is trained to estimate the Monte Carlo return from state $s_t$, effectively predicting the remaining "time to success" (or cost to go). Using a categorical distribution for $V_\phi$ (inspired by Distributional RL) is noted for robustness.
- **Suboptimality Detection:** During deployment, BGR monitors the consistency of $V_\phi$'s predictions. If the policy is executing optimally, $V_\phi(s_{t-1})$ should be approximately equal to $r + \gamma V_\phi(s_t)$ (Bellman equation). However, in novel scenarios, a suboptimal strategy might lead to states where the *true* future value is much lower than $V_\phi(s_{t-1})$ initially predicted. Specifically, if $V_\phi(s_{t-k})$ significantly overestimates the return ($r + \dots + \gamma^k V_\phi(s_t)$) after 'k' steps, it indicates the current strategy is suboptimal.
- **Practical Formulation:** With $r_- = -1$ for non-success and $r_+ = 0$ for success, and $\gamma=1$, the condition for suboptimality simplifies to $V_\phi(s_{t-k}) > V_\phi(s_t) - k$. This checks if the robot has progressed by less than 'k' units of "cost" in 'k' steps. The use of k-step returns makes the detection robust, as $s_{t-k}$ is more likely to be in-distribution even if $s_t$ is not. Distributional value functions allow for a statistical comparison against the expected progress, accounting for uncertainty.

#### 3.4. Non-Parametric Behavior Skewing

Once suboptimality is detected:
- **Recovery:** A simple recovery policy is executed (e.g., retracting the robot arm). This brings the robot back to a "neutral" or safe state, ideally within the distribution of states where the base policy is competent.
- **Replan and Skew:** The robot then needs to sample a *new* strategy from $\pi_\theta$. To avoid repeating past mistakes, BGR maintains a set $S_{avoid}$ of proprioceptive states ($s_{prop}^-$) that were associated with suboptimal attempts.
- **Biased Sampling:** When sampling action sequences ($a_1, \dots, a_k$) from $\pi_\theta$, BGR samples multiple proposals. From these proposals, it selects the action sequence $a^*$ that *maximizes* the minimum L2 distance of any of its states from any state in $S_{avoid}$. This non-parametric approach effectively biases the policy *away* from behaviors leading to previously failed states, encouraging exploration of genuinely different strategies without modifying the underlying $\pi_\theta$.

The overall framework is a "drop-in replacement" that works on top of any existing base policy, providing an online, adaptive wrapper for robustness.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were conducted across simulated (CaddyGrasp, SimObjectLift) and real-world (RealObjectLift, DoorOpening) environments, designed to assess the effectiveness of Bellman-Guided Retrials (BGR) in boosting base policy performance, especially in novel and adversarial scenarios.

#### 4.1. Qualitative Assessment of Suboptimality Detection

- **Timely Mistake Detection:** Visualizations (Figure 3) show that BGR's strategy evaluation method (monitoring $V_\phi(s_t) - V_\phi(s_{t-k}) - k$) effectively detects suboptimality. For instance, when an object slips out of the robot's grasp, the value expression drops below zero almost immediately (within a few frames). This rapid detection allows the robot to recover quickly, saving time and preventing progression into unrecoverable error states.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Performance Improvements

- **Significant Success Rate Boost:** BGR consistently and significantly increases the absolute success rates of base policies:
 - **Simulation:** More than 20% average increase (e.g., CaddyGrasp, SimObjectLift with train, test, and adversarial objects).
 - **Real-World:** More than 50% average increase (e.g., RealObjectLift, DoorOpening).
- **Robustness in Challenging Scenarios:** The performance gap between BGR and the base policy often widened in more difficult situations (e.g., adversarial objects with low friction, invisible obstacles in CaddyGrasp). This demonstrates that BGR enhances the robustness of policies when faced with distribution shifts.
- **Generality Across Tasks:** BGR's effectiveness was shown across various tasks, including different grasping scenarios (SimObjectLift, RealObjectLift) and a longer-horizon, articulated manipulation task (DoorOpening), suggesting broad applicability.

#### 4.3. Analysis of Skewing and Early Termination

- **Improved Strategy Diversity (Skewing):** In environments like CaddyGrasp where optimal strategies are constrained by hidden obstacles, the non-parametric behavior skewing component was crucial. Without it, the robot often repeatedly tried the same blocked regions. With skewing, the robot's grasp attempts were more diverse, spanning different handles and avoiding past failure points (Figure 8). This qualitative observation was supported by quantitative measures of grasp diversity.
- **Reduced Timesteps to Success and Early Termination:** BGR not only increased success rates but also generally reduced the number of steps required until success (Table 1). The ability for "early recovery" was observed, where BGR would trigger a recovery before the robot fully committed to a bad grasp or attempt. This conserves time per trial and reduces the risk of irrecoverably altering the environment (e.g., knocking an object off the table). Many recoveries occurred *before* the average expert grasp time, indicating quick mistake detection (Figure 9).

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

- **Importance of Strategy Evaluation:** An "Interval Recovery" baseline (which triggers recovery regularly based on average expert completion time) performed worse than BGR. This highlights that simply resetting after a fixed interval is insufficient; intelligent, value-function-guided *detection of suboptimality* is crucial for effective recovery.
- **Contribution of Skewed Sampling:** The "Ours w/o Skew" ablation showed reduced performance compared to the "Ours (Full)" method, particularly in challenging adversarial scenarios. This confirms that the non-parametric behavior skewing contributes significantly to performance by encouraging diverse exploration and preventing repeated mistakes, especially when environmental dynamics change.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

#### 5.1. Contributions to Robot Learning

"To Err is Robotic" makes several significant contributions to the field of robot learning:
- **General Framework for Explicit Trial-and-Error:** The paper introduces a novel and generalizable framework for endowing pre-trained robot policies with explicit trial-and-error capabilities during deployment. This moves beyond passive imitation and towards active, informed adaptation.
- **Effective Use of Value Functions for Self-Monitoring:** It demonstrates an elegant and practical method to leverage a value function, trained solely on expert demonstrations, for self-monitoring robot progress and detecting suboptimality without external reward signals. This highlights the underutilized potential of value functions in a self-supervised adaptation context.
- **Non-Parametric Behavior Skewing:** The proposed method for skewing policy sampling away from past mistakes is simple, effective, and non-parametric, allowing rapid adaptation with minimal interaction data and without requiring fine-tuning of the base policy.
- **Drop-in Enhancement:** A key advantage is that BGR acts as a "drop-in replacement" or wrapper for any base policy trained via imitation learning, requiring no modifications to the base policy itself or additional training data for adaptation.

#### 5.2. Practical Implications

The practical implications of this work are substantial:
- **Increased Robot Robustness and Autonomy:** BGR significantly improves the robustness of robots when operating in novel, unstructured, or adversarial environments. This is crucial for real-world deployment in areas like logistics, domestic assistance, and manufacturing, where unforeseen situations are common.
- **Reduced Reliance on Perfect Initial Execution:** By allowing robots to learn from errors, BGR reduces the need for policies to succeed on the first attempt in every scenario, making robot skill acquisition more forgiving and adaptable.
- **More Efficient Adaptation:** The quick detection of mistakes and intelligent replanning (skewing) leads to faster adaptation and reduced task completion times, improving overall efficiency.
- **Lower Deployment Costs:** By enabling adaptation without requiring human intervention or additional data collection during deployment, BGR could lead to lower operational costs for robotic systems.

#### 5.3. Limitations and Future Directions

The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point towards exciting avenues for future work:
- **Strategy Repertoire:** BGR does not *create* new strategies; it only helps the robot judiciously select and avoid strategies from its pre-trained base policy. Future work could explore integrating BGR with methods that *generate* novel behaviors.
- **Robust Recovery Policy:** The framework assumes access to a robust recovery policy (e.g., retracting the arm). While simple for some tasks, designing such policies for more complex, long-horizon, or safety-critical tasks could be non-trivial.
- **Dynamic Environments and Skewing Metric:** The current proprioception-based distance metric for skewing might be less effective in highly dynamic environments where visual context is more important for defining "mistake states." Exploring more expressive skewing methods that operate in a robot's *strategy space* (perhaps using latent representations of actions or goals) rather than just proprioceptive states could be beneficial, potentially enabling more conceptually different retries, much like humans.

Overall, "To Err is Robotic" presents a promising and practical framework that enhances the adaptive capabilities of robots, bringing them closer to robust and autonomous operation in complex, real-world settings.
