# Exploring Vision Transformers for Fine-grained Classification

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Universidad de Valladolid and University of San Francisco developed a multi-stage Vision Transformer framework for fine-grained image classification. This method leverages ViT's self-attention for intrinsic discriminative region localization and employs attention-guided data augmentations, achieving accuracy of 93.2% on Stanford Dogs and 91.0% on CUB-200-2011.

## Problem

- Fine-Grained Visual Classification (FGVC) is challenging due to low inter-class variance (subtle differences between categories) and high intra-class variance (large variations within a single category).
- Existing CNN-based FGVC methods often rely on complex, multi-stage pipelines with non-differentiable components for explicit region localization, complicating end-to-end optimization.
- Limited training data in FGVC datasets frequently leads to overfitting and reduced generalization capabilities in models.

## Method

- A **multi-stage Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16)** framework is proposed, using a shared encoder across three sequential stages: full image, localized object, and discriminative sub-parts.
- The inherent ==multi-head self-attention== mechanism of the ViT is utilized to generate ==attention maps== for intrinsically localizing the primary object and fine-grained discriminative parts, without requiring explicit detection heads.
- ==Attention-guided data augmentations==, including selective erasing of high-attention pixels, are introduced to improve model robustness, encourage feature diversity, and mitigate overfitting in data-limited scenarios.

## Results

- The method achieved an accuracy of **93.2%** on Stanford Dogs and **91.0%** on CUB-200-2011, outperforming baseline ViT and several established CNN-based approaches.
- Competitive performance was demonstrated on Stanford Cars (**94.3%**) and FGVC7 Plant Pathology (**92.4%**), showing broad applicability across different fine-grained domains.
- Qualitative visualizations confirmed the model's ability to generate interpretable attention maps that effectively localize primary objects and pinpoint discriminative sub-regions within them.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Vision Transformers' self-attention mechanism can be intrinsically leveraged for both feature extraction and discriminative region localization in FGVC, simplifying architectural complexity compared to explicit region proposal networks.
> - A multi-stage, multi-scale processing approach, where the model hierarchically focuses from full image to object to fine-grained parts, effectively enhances the ViT's ability to discern subtle visual cues.
> - Targeted data augmentation guided by attention maps enables the model to learn from a broader and more diverse set of discriminative features, improving generalization and robustness on fine-grained datasets.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Fine-Grained Visual Classification (FGVC) constitutes a challenging area within computer vision. The core difficulty in FGVC tasks lies in distinguishing between sub-categories that often exhibit minor visual differences (low inter-class variance) while also accounting for significant variations within the same sub-category (high intra-class variance). For instance, classifying different breeds of dogs or specific car models demands a nuanced understanding of subtle visual cues that traditional coarse-grained classification might overlook.

Prior research in FGVC predominantly relied on Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), which, while effective for general image classification, have encountered limitations when applied to the minute distinctions required by FGVC. A common approach to mitigate these challenges in CNN-based methods has involved the identification and extraction of highly informative image regions. This often entailed complex architectural pipelines, which could sometimes be difficult to optimize end-to-end due to non-differentiable components. Furthermore, the creation of FGVC datasets is resource-intensive, requiring domain expertise for accurate labeling, resulting in datasets with limited training samples.

The emergence of Vision Transformers (ViTs) marked a notable shift in the landscape of computer vision. Originally developed for natural language processing, transformers were adapted for image recognition, demonstrating strong performance across various classification tasks. The self-attention mechanism, a fundamental component of transformers, enables the model to weigh the importance of different parts of the input, which presents a potential avenue for addressing the region-localization challenge in FGVC without necessitating complex, specialized architectures. This work positions itself by exploring and leveraging the inherent capabilities of ViTs for FGVC, aiming to overcome some of the limitations of prior CNN-based and multi-stage approaches by utilizing the transformer's self-attention for both feature extraction and discriminative region localization.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate and enhance the applicability of Vision Transformers (ViTs) for fine-grained image classification tasks. The authors identify several motivations driving this investigation:

1. **Addressing FGVC Challenges with ViTs**: The inherent difficulties of FGVC, characterized by high intra-class and low inter-class variances, often lead to suboptimal performance with traditional CNNs. Existing state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods frequently involve complex pipelines for localizing informative regions. The authors are motivated to explore whether the self-attention mechanism within ViTs can naturally identify these discriminative regions, thereby simplifying the architectural complexity.
2. **Leveraging Inherent Multi-Head Self-Attention for Localization**: A central motivation is to exploit the multi-head self-attention mechanism of ViTs. This mechanism allows the model to capture relationships between different parts of an image. The authors propose using this inherent capability to localize informative image regions without requiring explicit architectural modifications, such as additional detection heads or region proposal networks. This aims to create a more integrated and potentially more efficient approach to regional feature extraction.
3. **Enhancing Model Robustness and Feature Diversity through Attention-Guided Augmentations**: Fine-grained datasets often suffer from limited training data, which can lead to overfitting and reduced generalization capabilities. The authors are motivated to introduce attention-guided data augmentations. By selectively cropping or erasing regions identified as important by the model's attention mechanism, the goal is to compel the model to focus on a broader spectrum of discriminative features, thereby improving its robustness and generalization.
4. **Achieving State-of-the-Art Performance**: Ultimately, the research is motivated by the objective of demonstrating the competitive potential of Vision Transformers in the context of FGVC. By combining the multi-stage framework, inherent attention-based localization, and attention-guided augmentations, the authors aim to achieve classification accuracy that is comparable to or surpasses existing state-of-the-art methods on established fine-grained benchmarks.
5. **Improving Model Interpretability**: The ability to visualize and understand *why* a model makes a certain prediction is valuable. By using attention maps to localize regions, the authors are motivated to provide interpretability to their ViT-based FGVC model, offering insights into which image parts are deemed critical for classification.

In summary, the authors seek to develop an interpretable, multi-stage ViT framework that effectively addresses the challenges of FGVC by intrinsically leveraging the self-attention mechanism for region localization and enhancing learning through attention-guided data augmentation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a multi-stage, multi-scale Vision Transformer (ViT) framework designed for fine-grained image classification, drawing inspiration from MMAL-Net. The core of the approach revolves around utilizing the ViT's inherent multi-head self-attention mechanism for both feature extraction and discriminative region localization.

#### 3.1. Vision Transformer Encoder and Multi-Stage Framework

- **Encoder**: A Vision Transformer (ViT-B/16) serves as the primary encoder (E). This ViT variant processes images by dividing them into non-overlapping patches, linear projecting them into tokens, adding positional embeddings, and then feeding them through a series of transformer encoder layers. A learnable class token (CLS token) is appended to the sequence of patch tokens, and its final representation is used for classification.
- **Classification Head**: For the downstream classification task, the output of the CLS token from the ViT encoder is fed into a two-layer Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with ReLU activation.
- **Multi-stage Processing**: The framework operates in three sequential stages, with all ViT parameters shared across these stages:
 - **Main Stage (a)**: The full input image is processed by the encoder E. Attention maps are extracted using the attention flow method, which recursively multiplies self-attention weights across all transformer layers. A bounding box is generated around the most salient object using an object localization method. The full image feature (from the CLS token) is used for an initial classification. Concurrently, the localized object and its corresponding attention map are cropped from a higher-resolution version of the full image.
 - **Object Stage (b)**: The cropped object image from stage (a) is passed through the same shared encoder E. Its feature representation is used for a second classification task.
 - **Parts Stage (c)**: The attention map of the localized object from stage (b) is used to identify multiple fine-grained sub-regions (parts) within the object. These discriminative parts are cropped as independent inputs and passed through the shared encoder E. The features from these individual part crops are then used for a third classification task.
- **Joint Loss Function**: The entire framework is optimized simultaneously using a joint loss function, L_joint. This function is a sum of the Cross-Entropy losses from each stage: L_a (for the main stage), L_b (for the object stage), and L_c (an aggregation of losses from the multiple part crops in stage c).

#### 3.2. Attention Map Generation

The attention map (AM) is crucial for localization. It is obtained by recursively multiplying the self-attention weights (a0...aN) from each transformer layer (L0...LN). This approach is intended to capture more comprehensive information about token interactions compared to using only the last layer's attention.

#### 3.3. Object Localization

This method aims to identify the primary object in the full image.
1. **Binarization**: The attention map of the full image is binarized using a histogram-based thresholding algorithm (mean thresholding was noted as effective).
2. **Morphological Closing**: A grayscale morphological closing operation is applied to the binary map to smooth and connect regions.
3. **Bounding Box Generation**: The bounding box that encompasses the binary foreground region with the maximum attention value is then extracted, providing the crop for the object stage.

#### 3.4. Discriminative Regions Localization

This step focuses on identifying important parts within the localized object.
1. **Scoring Candidate Regions**: The attention map of the cropped object (from stage b) is analyzed. Candidate object part regions are scored using an Average Pooling (AP) layer with a specific kernel size (e.g., 0.3 of the cropped object area, around 112x112 pixels for a 384px attention map) and a stride of S=1 to search all possible regions. Max Pooling was found to be too localized, while Generalized-mean (GeM) pooling was suggested as an alternative.
2. **Non-Maximum Suppression (NMS)**: After scoring, Non-Maximum Suppression is applied to the candidate regions to select the top non-overlapping regions (e.g., top 2 were used in experiments) that represent the most discriminative parts of the object. These regions are then cropped for the parts stage.

#### 3.5. Attention-based Augmentations

To improve generalization and prevent overfitting, the authors introduce attention-guided data augmentations:
- **Cropping**: An attention-based cropping mechanism is implied by the multi-stage localization, focusing on salient regions.
- **Erasing**: Inspired by WS-DAN, RandomErasing is applied selectively. In a given batch, an image is randomly selected with probability P. For this image, all pixels with an attention value higher than a threshold T are erased. This destructive augmentation forces the model to learn from alternative discriminative regions, thereby encouraging feature diversity.
- **Standard Augmentations**: These attention-based augmentations are combined with standard techniques such as horizontal flipping and additive noise.

#### 3.6. Experimental Setup

Experiments were conducted on four fine-grained benchmark datasets: CUB-200-2011, Stanford Cars, Stanford Dogs, and FGVC7 Plant Pathology. Full images (stage a) were resized to 384 px. Objects (stage b) and crops (stage c) were first cropped from a higher-resolution version of the full image and then resized to 384 px before being fed into the subsequent stages.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrate the potential of Vision Transformers for fine-grained classification, particularly when integrated into the proposed multi-stage framework with attention-guided augmentations.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Performance

- **Stanford Dogs**: The proposed method achieved an accuracy of 93.2% on the Stanford Dogs dataset. This result surpassed the performance of several established methods, including API-Net (90.3%), ViT (baseline, 91.7%), Cross-X (88.9%), RA-CNN (87.3%), FDL (84.9%), and MaxEnt (83.6%). The authors state this as a state-of-the-art performance on this benchmark.
- **CUB-200-2011**: On the CUB-200-2011 dataset, the model achieved 91.0% accuracy. This also surpassed the reported accuracy of previous methods such as API-Net (90.0%), WS-DAN (89.4%), MMAL-Net (89.6%), and the baseline ViT (89.4%), positioning it as a state-of-the-art result for this dataset according to the paper.
- **Stanford Cars**: For the Stanford Cars dataset, the method achieved 94.3% accuracy. While demonstrating competitive performance, it was slightly below the reported 94.6% of Cross-X and 95.3% of API-Net, but above the baseline ViT (92.8%) and other methods like DCL (94.5%), MMAL-Net (95.0%), WS-DAN (94.5%), and MaxEnt (93.0%).
- **FGVC7 Plant Pathology**: The model obtained 92.4% accuracy on the FGVC7 Plant Pathology dataset. This result improved upon the baseline ViT (91.7%), EfficientNet B0 (90.1%), EfficientNet B2 (90.4%), and ResNet50 (89.2%).

These results collectively indicate that the multi-stage ViT framework, combined with attention-driven augmentations and region detection, can improve the performance of ViT-based models for fine-grained classification tasks.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Results and Interpretability

Figures 4 and 5 provide qualitative visualizations that aim to demonstrate the interpretability and effectiveness of the proposed method.
- **Attention Map Visualization**: The visualizations illustrate that the Vision Transformer generates attention maps (Figure 4b, 5 central column) that highlight informative regions within the input image. These maps effectively delineate the primary object of interest.
- **Object Localization**: The red bounding boxes in Figures 4a and 5 (left column) indicate that the object localization method successfully identifies and crops the main object based on the attention map.
- **Discriminative Part Localization**: Figures 4e and 5 (rightmost column) show the attention maps of the cropped regions (parts), further indicating that the model can pinpoint highly discriminative sub-regions within the main object.
- **Attention-based Augmentation**: Figure 5 (middle-right column) visually demonstrates the attention-based erasing augmentation, where high-attention pixels are removed. This aims to qualitatively support the claim that the model is encouraged to learn from diverse features.

These qualitative results suggest that the model not only performs classification but also provides insights into its decision-making process by visually highlighting the relevant image areas.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of fine-grained visual classification by demonstrating an effective approach to leverage the Vision Transformer architecture. The significance of this work stems from several aspects:

Firstly, the development of a multi-stage ViT framework for FGVC provides an alternative to previous CNN-based methods that often required complex, specialized region proposal or localization networks. By utilizing the inherent multi-head self-attention mechanism of the Vision Transformer for identifying discriminative regions, the proposed method offers a more integrated and potentially simpler pipeline for feature extraction and localization within the same architecture. This intrinsic use of attention, without requiring architectural changes, represents a noteworthy advancement in applying transformers to FGVC challenges.

Secondly, the introduction of attention-guided augmentations has implications for improving model generalization, particularly in data-scarce FGVC environments. By selectively removing or focusing on highly attended regions, the model is compelled to learn from a broader set of discriminative features, potentially leading to more robust and less overfitted representations. This augmentation strategy offers a targeted approach to data enrichment, contrasting with purely random augmentations that might inadvertently discard crucial fine-grained information.

Thirdly, the reported performance gains on multiple established fine-grained benchmarks, including achieving state-of-the-art results on Stanford Dogs and CUB-200-2011, indicates the practical effectiveness of the proposed framework. These quantitative improvements suggest that ViTs, when appropriately configured and supplemented, can be highly competitive for complex categorization tasks that demand attention to subtle details.

Finally, the qualitative results showcasing the model's ability to localize objects and discriminative parts contribute to the interpretability of deep learning models. Understanding which specific regions of an image contribute to a fine-grained classification decision can be crucial for debugging models, gaining scientific insights into visual recognition, and building trust in AI systems. This interpretability, derived directly from the attention maps, is a significant benefit in domains where model transparency is valued.

The potential impact of this work lies in encouraging further exploration of self-attention mechanisms for object localization and feature learning in fine-grained tasks. It suggests a pathway towards more unified and inherently interpretable deep learning architectures for visual recognition, potentially reducing the need for explicit region proposal networks. Future work addressing the identified limitations, such as achieving full end-to-end differentiability and optimizing computational efficiency, could further broaden the applicability and impact of such ViT-based FGVC systems across various domains requiring precise visual differentiation, from biological classification to industrial quality control.
