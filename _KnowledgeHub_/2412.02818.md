# RoboMD: Uncovering Robot Vulnerabilities through Semantic Potential Fields

> [!summary] Summary
> ROBOMD enables the diagnosis of robot manipulation policy vulnerabilities by training a reinforcement learning agent to actively search for failure-inducing environmental variations within a learned semantic embedding space. The approach achieved an 80.7% average failure diagnosis accuracy and improved policy robustness by 92.83% with 85% less fine-tuning data compared to untargeted methods.

## Problem

- Identifying vulnerabilities in robot manipulation policies often requires costly, slow, and potentially unsafe physical trials.
- The full range of relevant environmental variations that can cause policy failures is frequently unknown and diverse, making exhaustive testing impractical.
- Existing methods, such as querying large language models or using other DRL approaches, struggle to reliably predict specific manipulation failures or generalize beyond a fixed set of known issues.

## Method

- **ROBOMD** formalizes failure diagnosis as an ==active search problem== within a continuous ==vision-language embedding space==, which functions as a ==semantic potential field== of success/failure likelihoods.
- A separate ==vulnerability prediction policy== (π_MD) is trained using ==Deep Reinforcement Learning (PPO)== to navigate this embedding space and proactively discover failure-inducing environmental conditions.
- The embedding space is constructed using a ==dual-backbone architecture== (==ViT== and ==CLIP==) trained with a ==joint binary cross-entropy and contrastive loss==, allowing for generalization to unseen environmental variations.

## Results

- ROBOMD achieved an average failure diagnosis accuracy of **80.7%** across four manipulation tasks (lift, stack, threading, pick & place), outperforming state-of-the-art VLMs (e.g., GPT-4o below **60%**) and other RL baselines.
- The framework demonstrated generalization by accurately ranking failure likelihoods for entirely unseen environmental variations in both real-world and simulated settings.
- Using RoboMD's prioritized failure modes for targeted fine-tuning improved a pre-trained BC-lift policy's accuracy by **92.83%** while requiring only **1.3 GB** of data, an **85%** reduction compared to **9.0 GB** for untargeted fine-tuning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A semantically rich embedding space can effectively capture the continuous relationship between environmental variations and robot policy success/failure, enabling diagnosis without direct physical interaction.
> - Active exploration guided by reinforcement learning in this learned continuous space allows for systematic and sample-efficient discovery of novel and subtle failure modes.
> - The theoretical underpinnings of advantage invariance and sample-efficient boundary exploration ensure that the DRL agent can find optimal failure-inducing conditions and converge quickly.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust robot manipulation policies is a central challenge in physical artificial intelligence, with applications ranging from industrial automation to assistive robotics. A significant obstacle in deploying these policies is their vulnerability to variations in real-world environments. Identifying these vulnerabilities is challenging due to two primary factors: the unknown nature of relevant environmental variations to test against and the inherent cost and safety concerns associated with direct physical robot trials.

Existing approaches to addressing robot policy robustness and failure detection include:
- **Foundation Models for Failure Characterization:** Some research uses vision-language foundation models (VLMs) to query for failures. However, these models often lack iterative interaction capabilities with the robot policy and may not provide accurate quantitative predictions, such as failure probabilities. The paper argues that VLMs alone are insufficient for reliable failure diagnosis in high-stakes robotic tasks.
- **Active Failure Search:** Outside of robotics, deep reinforcement learning (RL) has been applied to identify errors in classification and generation tasks. Similarly, Markov Decision Processes have been utilized to explore challenging conditions in areas like autonomous vehicles. These methods, however, have not typically been demonstrated on complex physical systems like robotic manipulators, nor do they often generalize beyond a fixed set of known failure modes.
- **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Detection:** OOD methods aim to identify unseen inputs, a related but distinct problem from failure detection. Not all OOD samples lead to policy failure, and failures can occur within the training distribution. Therefore, OOD methods do not provide a comprehensive characterization of policy weaknesses.
- **Uncertainty Quantification:** This area seeks to quantify epistemic uncertainty, particularly in robot perception and deep RL. While valuable for understanding model confidence, these techniques typically do not directly inform engineers about *where* models fail, thus hindering targeted policy improvement.
- **Generalization in Robotics:** Extensive work focuses on making policies more generalized and less prone to failures through large-scale simulation, vision-language-action models, curriculum learning, and domain randomization. These methods aim to improve policy robustness during training.

ROBOMD positions itself as a diagnostic tool that is complementary to these generalization-focused methods. It addresses the limitations of existing approaches by providing a systematic, active exploration framework for uncovering policy vulnerabilities *pre-deployment*. By reformulating vulnerability search as an RL problem within a learned continuous semantic embedding space, ROBOMD aims to overcome the challenges of unknown variations and costly physical trials, offering a scalable and safe diagnostic solution. It distinguishes itself from VLMs by actively interacting to find failures and from OOD detection by characterizing failures both within and beyond the training distribution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The motivation for ROBOMD stems from two principal challenges in ensuring the robustness of robot manipulation policies:

1. **Unknown Relevant Variations:** Robot policies must operate reliably in diverse and unpredictable real-world environments. However, the specific environmental variations that might lead to failure are often unknown or difficult to anticipate. Traditional testing methods might only cover a limited, pre-defined set of variations, risking the oversight of critical failure modes that emerge under novel or subtle conditions. For instance, a robot grasping a bottle needs to generalize across various attributes (color, shape, size, material) and environmental factors (lighting, background, physical layouts). Naive testing with a small set of known variations may fail to expose vulnerabilities arising from complex or unseen combinations of these factors.

2. **Costly and Unsafe Direct Testing in the Real World:** Unlike perception or language tasks where vulnerabilities can often be identified through large datasets with minimal computational cost, diagnosing failures in robot manipulation necessitates physical trials. These trials are inherently slow, expensive, and can pose safety risks to the robot, the environment, and human operators. This makes exhaustive heuristic testing or trial-and-error approaches impractical and economically prohibitive for comprehensive vulnerability analysis before deployment.

To address these challenges, the key objectives of the ROBOMD framework are:

- **Develop a scalable and safe vulnerability exploration method:** The framework aims to enable efficient discovery of failure modes without requiring extensive, costly, or unsafe physical trials.
- **Systematically uncover vulnerabilities in unknown variation spaces:** By operating in a continuous semantic embedding space, the framework seeks to move beyond discrete, hand-specified variations to identify subtle and unforeseen failure conditions.
- **Learn a dedicated vulnerability prediction model:** This model, formulated as a deep reinforcement learning (deep RL) policy, is designed to actively search for and predict failure-inducing scenarios.
- **Provide actionable insights for policy improvement:** The discovered vulnerabilities should translate into a prioritized list of failure modes that can guide targeted fine-tuning of the manipulation policy, thereby improving its robustness with reduced data collection costs.

The paper summarizes its main contributions as:
1. Proposing a deep RL-based framework that operates in a semantically rich embedding space for diagnosing failures in pre-trained manipulation policies.
2. Providing experimental validation on simulated and real-world setups, supported by theoretical guarantees.
3. Demonstrating systematic improvement of robot policies using the failures diagnosed by the framework.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The ROBOMD framework operates by training a separate vulnerability prediction policy, denoted as πMD, to diagnose failures in an existing pre-trained manipulation policy, πR. A key aspect is that πMD is designed to be agnostic to the specific architecture or training method of πR, requiring only rollouts of πR to function. The methodology involves three primary stages:
1. **Formalizing Failure Diagnosis as a Search Problem in a Semantic Space:** The core idea is to transform raw manipulation data into a semantically meaningful, continuous vision-language embedding space, E, which captures the success or failure outcomes of πR. This embedding space serves as a "potential field" for the RL agent. The search for vulnerabilities by πMD is formalized as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) ⟨S, A, P, R, γ⟩:
 - **State Space (S):** States st ∈ S ≡ E are 512-dimensional vectors representing continuous semantic embeddings of hypothesized environment variations. This includes both physically plausible and unviable variations.
 - **Action Space (A):** Actions at ∈ A ≡ E are also 512-dimensional vectors that introduce variations to the current state st. Taking an action corresponds to moving to a different hypothesized environment variation.
 - **Transition (P):** The transition function P(s t+1
 |st, at) determines the next state based on applying the action (variation) at to the current state st.
 - **Reward (R):** The agent πMD is rewarded for efficiently discovering failure conditions.
 - **Discount Factor (γ):** Set to 0.99. The objective for πMD is to learn a sequence of actions (environmental variations) that maximizes the probability of πR failing.

2. **Building the Semantic Embedding as a Potential Field of Success-Failures:** To enable generalization to unseen environments, a multimodal embedding space E is constructed from a limited dataset D = {(xvisioni, xlangi), yi} of labeled success-failure rollouts. xvision is an image input, xlang is a textual description of the environmental variation, and y indicates success or failure. The textual description is automatically generated based on the applied variation. This dataset is initially populated with variations chosen based on prior knowledge of common policy vulnerabilities. The embedding architecture features a dual backbone (Figure 3):
 - **Vision Transformer (ViT):** A ViT-B/16 backbone, pre-trained on ImageNet-1k, extracts visual features, aiding in understanding semantic relationships between everyday environments.
 - **CLIP Encoder:** The CLIP text transformer processes natural language task descriptions, helping focus on relevant visual features and improving generalization. The outputs from both backbones are projected, concatenated, and passed through a 512-unit MLP layer, followed by a classification head. The output of the MLP layer forms the embedding vector ei. The model is trained with a joint objective: binary cross-entropy for the success/failure classification (y) and a contrastive loss objective (L) at the MLP layer. The contrastive loss (Eq. 1) minimizes the distance between embeddings of rollouts with similar outcomes and maximizes it for different outcomes, thereby structuring E as a potential field where proximity correlates with outcome similarity.

3. **Deep Reinforcement Learning in the Continuous Semantic Space:** Once the semantic embedding space E is constructed, the πMD agent, implemented using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), navigates this space. It is guided by a set of known embeddings, Eknown, derived from the initial dataset D. The process (Algorithm 1) involves:
 - πMD sampling an action a t+1 from the continuous embedding space.
 - Identifying the closest known embedding a\* t+1 ∈ Eknown to the sampled action.
 - The state s t+1 is updated, representing a hypothesized environmental variation. Crucially, these steps are "virtual rollouts," meaning they do not require physical interaction with the robot, significantly reducing cost and time. The reward function (Eq. 2) is designed to encourage discovery of failure regions and discourage large deviations from Eknown (uncertain regions) and repetitive actions (stalled search). The frequency penalty N(a) addresses the latter. PPO's entropy regularization is important for promoting broad exploration across the continuous space. A special case is handled for when candidate variations are explicitly known, where πMD searches directly over A ≡ Eknown.

4. **From Predicting Vulnerabilities to Improving Robot Policy Performance:** The trained πMD policy outputs a probability distribution over variations (actions), which is interpreted as a map of failure likelihoods. In the continuous space, this is modeled as a Gaussian density, and likelihood ratios indicate which variations are more failure-prone. For a finite set of known candidates, it becomes a categorical distribution. These identified and prioritized failure modes provide actionable insights for improving πR. Practitioners can target data collection on the highest likelihood failures (e.g., specific lighting or object variations) and then fine-tune πR on this compact, targeted dataset. This process allows for systematic patching of vulnerabilities.

**Theoretical Underpinnings:** The paper provides three theorems:
- **Theorem 1 (Advantage Invariance):** States that the potential-based reward shaping introduced by the semantic embedding does not alter the optimal policy of the original MDP and preserves the advantage function, ensuring the correctness of the learning process.
- **Theorem 2 (Sample-Efficient Boundary Exploration):** Asserts that the reward function, including a term for predictive uncertainty (entropy), is Lipschitz continuous, leading to stable PPO gradients. Furthermore, it guarantees that exploration concentrates near the success/failure boundary, identifying it to a given precision with a polynomial number of rollouts.
- **Theorem 3 (Convergence Acceleration):** Demonstrates that potential shaping can lead to a transformed critic with smaller initialization and approximation errors, thereby accelerating the convergence of PPO.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental validation of ROBOMD addresses three research questions concerning its diagnostic capabilities and utility for policy improvement. Experiments were conducted in simulated environments (RoboSuite, RoboMimic, MimicGen) and with a physical UR5e robot arm, across tasks such as lift, stack, threading, and pick & place, using various manipulation policies (BC, HBC, BC-Transformer, BCQ, Diffusion).

#### 4.1. Benchmark Comparisons

- **Performance against Baselines:** ROBOMD's vulnerability prediction policy (πMD), leveraging PPO, was benchmarked against alternative RL algorithms (A2C, SAC) and state-of-the-art Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like GPT-4o, Gemini 1.5 Pro, and Qwen2-VL, as well as lightweight CNN and ResNet models.
 - ROBOMD demonstrated higher ranking accuracy across all tasks, consistently outperforming other methods in identifying failure likelihoods (Table 1).
 - VLMs, even with in-context learning (GPT-4o-ICL), generally performed below 60% accuracy, indicating limitations in reliably predicting environmental configurations leading to failures.
 - Lightweight visual models (CNN, ResNet) exhibited low true-positive and high false-positive rates for fine-grained perturbations, suggesting they lack the sensitivity for subtle failure cues (Appendix B, Figure 11).
- **RL Algorithm Exploration:** Analysis of action distributions across RL algorithms indicated that PPO achieved the highest entropy (2.8839), supporting its suitability for the broad exploration required in failure discovery (Figure 5, Table 4).
- **Generalization across Policies:** ROBOMD's failure detection accuracy generalized across diverse manipulation policies and tasks, maintaining performance regardless of the underlying policy architecture (Table 2).

#### 4.2. Analyzing Diagnostic Capabilities

- **Unseen Environments:** A key strength of ROBOMD is its ability to generalize. It correctly ranked the failure likelihood of previously unseen environmental variations (e.g., "Bread" for a UR5e robot, "Black Table" in simulation) relative to known items. This consistency confirms that the embedding space effectively captures semantic relationships governing policy failures (Table 3).
- **Embedding Quality Ablations:** Experiments evaluating the embedding quality demonstrated that multimodal inputs (Image+Text) combined with both Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) and Contrastive Loss yielded the most effective embeddings. This configuration resulted in the lowest Mean Square Error (0.1801) and Frobenius distance (7.6387) from an ideal identity matrix, indicating strong separability and a robust diagonal structure in the similarity matrix (Figure 8, Table 9). This validates the choice of multimodal inputs and contrastive loss for creating a locally consistent semantic space.
- **Seen Environments:** Visualizations of failure likelihoods generated by πMD for different policies showed varied failure patterns, reflecting the unique inductive biases and weaknesses of each architecture (Figure 6). For instance, the HBC policy demonstrated high robustness, which was quantitatively supported by its low Failure Severity Index (FSI) and a small number of failure modes (NFM) (Table 4).

#### 4.3. Ablation: Embedding Quality and Semantic Continuity

- **Embedding Space Structure:** The learned embedding space formed well-separated and compact clusters for success and failure modes across various simulated tasks and real-world policies (Table 6). This was indicated by high separation ratios and silhouette scores, coupled with low Davies–Bouldin indices.
- **Semantic Continuity:** Under controlled variations, such as a lighting intensity sweep, the embedding space exhibited semantic continuity. The cumulative embedding distance increased monotonically as illumination decreased (Kendall’s τ = 1.000, Pearson’s r = 0.982), indicating a smooth transition from failure to success clusters (Table 7, Figure 10). These results support the conceptualization of the embedding space as a continuous potential field for PPO-based exploration.

**Actionable Insights: Failure-Guided πR Policy Improvement:**
- **Targeted Fine-tuning:** The top-ranked failures identified by ROBOMD were used to construct a targeted dataset for fine-tuning a pre-trained BC-lift manipulation policy.
- **Performance Improvement:** This ROBOMD-guided fine-tuning significantly mitigated vulnerabilities of the pre-trained policy (Figure 9). Quantitatively, it achieved a 92.83% accuracy, surpassing fine-tuning on randomly selected failures (85.48%) (Table 5).
- **Data Efficiency:** A notable finding was the data efficiency of the ROBOMD-guided approach. It required only 1.3 GB of targeted failure data, compared to 9.0 GB needed for fine-tuning on all known failures (Table 13). This indicates that ROBOMD not only identifies failure modes but also prioritizes the most informative ones for policy adaptation, enabling efficient robustness improvements under data collection constraints.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ROBOMD framework represents a methodological shift in the diagnosis of robot manipulation failures. By recasting the problem from heuristic-based approaches to an active, sequential search driven by deep reinforcement learning, the framework offers a principled method for uncovering policy weaknesses.

The integration of a continuous vision-language embedding space is a key innovation, enabling the RL agent to generalize its diagnosis beyond pre-defined variations. This capability allows for the identification and ranking of subtle failure modes in both known and previously unseen environmental conditions. The empirical evidence supports that ROBOMD surpasses static VLM baselines, lightweight models, and alternative RL methods in diagnostic accuracy.

The practical impact of ROBOMD is notable in its ability to generate actionable insights. The prioritized list of failure modes facilitates targeted fine-tuning of manipulation policies, which has been shown to measurably enhance policy robustness. Critically, this improvement is achieved with a substantially reduced data cost compared to indiscriminate fine-tuning with all known failures. This efficiency has implications for resource-constrained development and deployment scenarios.

Beyond its debugging capabilities, ROBOMD offers a foundation for integration into continuous testing pipelines, potentially enabling automated regression testing as robot policies evolve. This capability contributes to the development of more reliable and trustworthy robotic systems, addressing a fundamental need in physical AI. Future research could explore scaling this approach to create generalist diagnostic agents capable of evaluating multi-task policies across broader and more unstructured environment distributions, further contributing to the safety and robustness of real-world robotic deployments.
