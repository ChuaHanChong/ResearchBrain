# Robust Finetuning of Vision-Language-Action Robot Policies via Parameter Merging

> [!summary] Summary
> RETAIN, a method for Vision-Language-Action robot policies, employs linear interpolation of policy weights to robustly finetune new skills with limited data while preserving generalist capabilities. This approach achieved approximately 40% higher average success rates on real-world out-of-distribution tasks, facilitating continuous learning of new behaviors.

## Problem

- Generalist robot policies (VLA models) suffer from severe overfitting when finetuned with limited task-specific demonstrations.
- This overfitting leads to catastrophic forgetting of the broad generalist skills learned during pretraining.
- Finetuned policies often lack robustness, failing to generalize to out-of-distribution variations of the new target task.

## Method

- The **RETAIN** (Robust finE-tuning wiTh pArameter mergINg) method linearly interpolates parameters between a pretrained generalist policy and a policy finetuned on a specific target task.
- The approach allows for modality-specific merging, using different interpolation weights for the vision, language, and action components of the VLA model.
- RETAIN is applied to continual learning scenarios, enabling sequential acquisition and merging of multiple new skills into a single, evolving policy checkpoint.

## Results

- RETAIN achieved approximately **40%** higher average success rates on real-world out-of-distribution target tasks compared to existing finetuning baselines.
- The method successfully preserved the generalist capabilities of the pretrained policy, demonstrating performance on generalist tasks on par with the original model.
- RETAIN enabled robust sequential acquisition of multiple robot skills, outperforming baselines in maintaining performance on both previously learned and new tasks in continual learning settings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Linear interpolation in parameter space effectively mitigates overfitting in low-data regimes by combining new task-specific knowledge with the broad generalization abilities of the pretrained model.
> - The robustness benefits of RETAIN scale positively with the amount of data used to train the initial generalist policy, leveraging stronger base models more effectively.
> - Merging primarily the language model parameters is often sufficient for achieving robust out-of-distribution generalization, highlighting the critical role of the language component in VLA models.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of "generalist robot policies," often referred to as Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, represents a significant paradigm shift in robotics. These policies, trained on vast and diverse datasets, aim to enable a single robot to perform a wide array of tasks in various environments, generalizing across objects, scenes, and language instructions. Landmark works like RT-2, Gato, and more recently, policies such as $\pi_0$ and $\pi_0.5$, exemplify this trend. However, despite their impressive zero-shot generalization capabilities, these generalist policies frequently require adaptation or "finetuning" to achieve high performance on new, specific tasks not adequately covered in their pretraining data or to function optimally on different robot systems.

The standard approach to adaptation is finetuning, where the pretrained model is updated using a small dataset of demonstrations for the target task. The critical challenge, which this paper addresses, is that in low-data finetuning regimes (typically less than 100 demonstrations), these policies tend to *overfit* severely. This overfitting manifests in two primary ways: (1) **loss of generality**: the policy forgets its previously acquired broad skills and performs poorly on non-target tasks (catastrophic forgetting), and (2) **poor generalization on the target task**: it fails to generalize to unseen variations (e.g., different object instances, lighting, viewpoints) of the *new* task itself, operating effectively only in scenarios identical to the finetuning data.

Existing methods to mitigate overfitting during finetuning include mixing target and pretraining data (co-finetuning), using parameter-efficient adaptation techniques like LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), or freezing parts of the network (e.g., the language model backbone). While these methods offer some improvements, the paper argues they still struggle to retain the full generality of the pretrained model and robustly generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) variations of the target task in low-data settings.

The concept of "model parameter merging" has gained traction in vision and language domains, where interpolating weights of different models (e.g., pretrained and finetuned, or multiple finetuned models) has shown promise in combining capabilities or improving robustness. This paper extends this idea to the complex, multimodal domain of robot VLA policies, making it the first to thoroughly investigate and analyze parameter merging for this specific application. Furthermore, its exploration into modality-specific merging and its application to continual learning for robotics marks a novel contribution to the field, offering a simple yet powerful strategy to bridge the gap between generalist capabilities and specific task mastery in robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the critical limitations of current finetuning approaches for generalist robot policies, particularly in scenarios with limited target task demonstrations. While large-scale generalist policies excel at diverse tasks, their deployment into new environments or for novel skills often necessitates adaptation. The prevailing methods for this adaptation – primarily finetuning – suffer from severe overfitting when data is scarce. This overfitting leads to two undesirable outcomes:
1. **Loss of Generalist Capabilities**: Finetuned policies often forget the broad range of skills they learned during pretraining, sacrificing their "generalist" nature.
2. **Lack of Robustness on New Tasks**: Even on the target task, finetuned policies frequently fail to generalize to slight variations (out-of-distribution scenarios) not explicitly present in the small finetuning dataset.

Given these challenges, the key objectives of this work are to:
- **Develop a robust finetuning method**: The central aim is to create an approach that allows generalist robot policies to incorporate new skills effectively without succumbing to overfitting in low-data regimes.
- **Preserve generalization capabilities**: Ensure that the finetuned policy retains the broad competencies and generalist abilities acquired from large-scale pretraining.
- **Enhance generalization on the target task**: Enable the adapted policy to robustly perform the new skill across a wide range of unseen variations (e.g., different objects, environments, viewpoints, lighting) of that specific target task.
- **Enable continual skill acquisition**: Explore if the proposed method can facilitate the sequential learning and merging of multiple new skills into a single, evolving policy checkpoint, paving the way for lifelong robot learning.

Ultimately, the goal is to achieve a single, unified policy that is both specialized for a new task and broadly competent across its original repertoire, thereby expanding the practical usability and deployability of advanced generalist robot policies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed method, **RETAIN (Robust finE-tuning wiTh pArameter mergINg)**, is a surprisingly simple yet effective strategy: **linear interpolation of policy weights in parameter space**.

#### 3.1. The RETAIN Principle

Given a pretrained generalist policy with weights $\theta_{pre}$ and a finetuned policy (trained on a new target task) with weights $\theta_{ft}$, RETAIN constructs a final merged policy $\tilde{\theta}$ using the formula:
$\tilde{\theta} = (1 - \alpha) \cdot \theta_{pre} + \alpha \cdot \theta_{ft}$
Here, $\alpha$ is a tunable merging weight, typically ranging between 0 and 1. An $\alpha=0$ corresponds to the original pretrained policy, while $\alpha=1$ represents the fully finetuned policy. The intuition is that finetuning moves the policy parameters from a generalist solution towards a task-specific optimum. This movement, especially with limited data, can lead to overfitting. Merging with the original generalist weights effectively pulls the parameters back towards the robust, generalist basin, combining the new task-specific knowledge with the broad generalization abilities of the pretrained model.

#### 3.2. Finetuning Settings

The paper explores two common finetuning settings to obtain $\theta_{ft}$:
- **Task-finetuning (Task-FT)**: The policy is finetuned exclusively on the target task dataset ($D_\eta$). This is common when pretraining data is proprietary or too large to easily access.
- **Co-finetuning (Co-FT)**: The policy is finetuned on a mix of the target task dataset ($D_\eta$) and a subset of the pretraining dataset ($D_{pre}$). Co-finetuning is known to help preserve generalist abilities but can still suffer from OOD generalization issues on the target task. RETAIN can be applied to policies obtained from either Task-FT (RETAIN-task-FT) or Co-FT (RETAIN-co-FT).

#### 3.3. Modality-Specific Merging

Recognizing that modern generalist robot policies are typically multimodal Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models (consisting of a vision encoder, a language model backbone, and an action expert), RETAIN extends its approach to allow for different merging weights for each modality. This means separate $\alpha$ values ($\alpha_v, \alpha_l, \alpha_a$) can be used for the vision encoder parameters ($\theta_v$), language model parameters ($\theta_l$), and action expert parameters ($\theta_a$), respectively. This allows for fine-grained control over which parts of the model retain more of their pretrained or finetuned characteristics.

#### 3.4. Continual Task Adaptation

RETAIN's ability to preserve generalist skills naturally lends itself to continual learning. The methodology proposes a sequential merging process where, after learning a new task $\tau_n$ and obtaining a merged policy $\tilde{\theta}_n$, this $\tilde{\theta}_n$ then serves as the "pretrained" base model for finetuning on the next task $\tau_{n+1}$. This iterative process allows a single policy to accumulate multiple skills over time without significant degradation of previously learned abilities.
$\tilde{\theta}_n = (1 - \alpha) \cdot \tilde{\theta}_{n-1} + \alpha \cdot \theta_{ft,n}$

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

The methodology is rigorously tested across both **real-world (DROID)** and **simulated (LIBERO)** environments.
- **DROID experiments**: Utilize a 7-DoF Franka robot arm with a wrist-mounted camera, evaluating on two challenging real-world finetuning tasks (wiping a whiteboard, putting plates in a rack) with limited demonstrations (50-100). Evaluations include in-distribution (ID) and out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios for the target task, as well as performance on generalist tasks from the pretraining distribution. The pretrained policy is $\pi_0$-FAST-DROID.
- **LIBERO experiments**: Conducted in a multi-task robotic manipulation simulator, using three target tasks from LIBERO-10, with approximately 45 demonstrations per task. The pretrained policy is a $\pi_0$ variant finetuned on a broad set of LIBERO tasks. OOD evaluations involve variations in object positions, distractors, and backgrounds.

Baselines include standard Task-FT, Co-FT, LoRA, Freeze-FT (freezing language model backbone), and training from Scratch. Hyperparameter tuning for $\alpha$ is done on a "validation" OOD scene, with results reported on separate "test" OOD scenes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on both real-world (DROID) and simulated (LIBERO) environments consistently demonstrate the effectiveness of RETAIN for robust finetuning and continual skill acquisition.

#### 4.1. Robust Generalization to Unseen Target Task Variations

- **Superior OOD Performance**: RETAIN significantly outperforms all baseline methods in out-of-distribution (OOD) evaluations for target tasks. For instance, on real-world DROID tasks, RETAIN achieves approximately 40% higher average success rates than the best prior finetuning methods (e.g., Co-FT, LoRA) on unseen variations of the target task. This suggests that RETAIN successfully transfers the pretrained policy's generalization ability to the new skill.
- **Competitive ID Performance**: While excelling in OOD scenarios, RETAIN also maintains competitive performance on in-distribution (ID) target task evaluations, matching or slightly exceeding most baselines. This indicates that the method effectively learns the target skill without compromising performance in familiar contexts.

#### 4.2. Retention of Generalist Capabilities

- **Preserves Prior Knowledge**: Crucially, RETAIN policies demonstrate performance on generalist tasks (from the pretraining distribution) that is on par with the original pretrained model. This indicates that RETAIN effectively mitigates catastrophic forgetting, allowing the policy to integrate new skills without losing its broad competencies. RETAIN-co-FT, in particular, shows the best retention of generalist abilities.

#### 4.3. Scaling with Pretraining Data

- **Benefits from More General Base Models**: The effectiveness of RETAIN in improving OOD performance scales positively with the amount of data used to train the initial generalist policy. Policies pretrained on larger and more diverse datasets (e.g., DROID-ALL + PI) yield substantially greater OOD generalization benefits when merged with RETAIN, sometimes achieving OOD performance nearly identical to ID performance. This underscores that RETAIN effectively "transfers" the inherent generalization power of a stronger generalist base model.

#### 4.4. Modality-Specific Merging Insights

- **Language Model Parameters are Key**: Surprisingly, modality-specific merging experiments reveal that the language model backbone parameters have the most significant influence on the merged policy's OOD performance. It is often sufficient to only merge the language model parameters (i.e., set $\alpha_v = \alpha_a = 1$ and $\alpha_l < 1$) while leaving vision encoder and action expert parameters at their finetuned values, achieving similar performance to merging all parameters. This provides a crucial insight into the architecture of VLA models and the role of different components in generalization and task understanding.

#### 4.5. Enabling Continual Skill Acquisition

- **Sequential Learning Without Forgetting**: RETAIN effectively enables the sequential acquisition of multiple robot skills. In a two-task sequential learning experiment (plates then whiteboard on DROID), the final RETAIN policy significantly outperforms the Co-FT baseline on both tasks, across both ID and OOD evaluations. This demonstrates RETAIN's ability to robustly "merge" successive skills into a single policy checkpoint while retaining performance on previously learned tasks.

In summary, RETAIN is a straightforward yet powerful method that addresses key limitations of finetuning generalist robot policies, significantly improving their robustness to task variations and enabling continuous learning of new skills without sacrificing prior knowledge.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of RETAIN represents a significant step forward in the practical deployment and scalability of generalist robot policies, offering several profound implications for the field of robot learning:

#### 5.1. Enhanced Real-World Applicability and Robustness

One of the most critical challenges in robotics is bridging the gap between controlled lab environments and the unpredictable real world. RETAIN directly tackles this by enabling policies to generalize robustly to unseen variations of a task—such as changes in object instances, lighting, or camera angles—even when finetuned on limited data. This dramatically increases the reliability and adaptability of robots in diverse real-world scenarios, making it more feasible to deploy robots for new tasks with modest data collection efforts. The demonstrated 40% higher success rate on real-world OOD tasks for DROID is a powerful testament to this improved robustness.

#### 5.2. Efficient Skill Acquisition in Low-Data Regimes

Collecting vast amounts of high-quality robot demonstration data is expensive, time-consuming, and often impractical. RETAIN's effectiveness in low-data finetuning regimes (e.g., under 100 demonstrations) is a game-changer. It means that leveraging powerful generalist policies for new tasks no longer necessitates prohibitive data collection efforts, democratizing access to advanced robot capabilities and accelerating the development cycle for new robotic applications.

#### 5.3. Facilitating Lifelong and Continual Learning for Robots

The ability of RETAIN to enable sequential skill acquisition without catastrophic forgetting is crucial for the vision of lifelong learning robots. Instead of retraining from scratch or managing a collection of task-specific policies, robots can incrementally learn new skills and integrate them into a single, evolving generalist policy. This allows robots to continually adapt and expand their repertoire of behaviors over their operational lifetime, moving closer to human-like learning capabilities.

#### 5.4. Simplicity and Computational Efficiency

RETAIN's core mechanism—linear interpolation in weight space—is remarkably simple to implement and introduces virtually no additional computational overhead during inference. This makes it an easily adoptable technique for researchers and practitioners alike, requiring minimal changes to existing finetuning pipelines. The method's effectiveness scales with the quality of the pretrained model, meaning investments in more general foundation models will yield increasing returns when combined with RETAIN.

#### 5.5. Insights into Multimodal Robot Policy Architectures

The discovery that merging primarily the language model parameters is often sufficient for robust generalization provides valuable mechanistic insight into how VLA models encode and transfer knowledge. This finding could guide future research in designing more efficient finetuning strategies or architectural improvements, allowing for targeted updates to the most critical components of multimodal policies.

While RETAIN's empirical success is strong, the authors acknowledge a limitation in fully understanding the theoretical underpinnings of *why* parameter merging works so well. This opens up avenues for future theoretical work, alongside practical efforts to develop heuristics for automatically determining the optimal merging coefficient $\alpha$. Nevertheless, RETAIN offers a powerful, practical solution to a pressing problem in robot learning, pushing the boundaries of what generalist robots can achieve in complex, dynamic environments.
