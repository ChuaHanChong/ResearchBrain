# WaveForward: An Omnidirectional Passive Wheeled Quadruped Robot with Casters

> [!summary] Summary
> The WaveForward robot, an omnidirectional passive wheeled quadruped, leverages 2-DOF casters on each leg and an asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning framework to achieve multi-directional movement. It demonstrated an 89.1% reduction in Cost of Transport compared to point-foot trotting, alongside high-speed capabilities up to 4.1 m/s.

## Problem

- Existing passive wheeled-legged robots lack omnidirectional mobility, being restricted by non-holonomic constraints to mainly forward/backward motion due to single-DOF wheels.
- Actuated wheeled-legged robots offer enhanced mobility but incur increased complexity, weight, and cost due to their active wheel components.
- Passive caster-based propulsion is strongly underactuated, since directional force generation depends on an indirect geometric coupling between swivel-axis inclination, caster deflection, and no-slip contact.

## Method

- Introduces **WaveForward**, a passive wheeled quadruped robot featuring 2-DOF casters on each of its four legs to enable omnidirectional movement.
- Develops a control framework based on asymmetric actor-critic ==reinforcement learning== (PPO) that dynamically adjusts the caster base posture using actuated leg joints.
- Implements a caster base posture adjustment strategy that aligns the caster swivel axis and deflection angle with commanded velocities to generate directional propulsive force.

## Results

- Demonstrated omnidirectional mobility through successful slalom navigation in real-world tests and achieved high-speed motion up to **4.1** m/s on flat terrain.
- Reduced the mechanical Cost of Transport (COT) by **89.1%** compared to traditional point-foot trotting of the same robot, consuming 41 Wh over a **1.1** km trajectory versus 91 Wh.
- Validated the capability to smoothly switch between different propulsion modes, including four-wheel, front-wheel, and rear-wheel drive, by dynamically controlling caster engagement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Propulsive force in passive casters can be generated through a geometric-constraint coupling involving swivel-axis inclination, caster deflection, and no-slip conditions, requiring both non-zero inclination and deflection.
> - An asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning framework can effectively control strongly underactuated systems by allowing the critic to access privileged information about passive joint states during training.
> - Dynamically adjusting the caster base posture using actuated leg joints provides a viable method for achieving omnidirectional propulsion in passive wheeled systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic locomotion research broadly categorizes robots into legged and wheeled systems, each with inherent advantages and disadvantages. Legged robots, such as the MIT Cheetah 3 or ANYmal, demonstrate high adaptability to complex and rough terrains but typically exhibit lower energy efficiency for long-distance travel on flat surfaces. Wheeled robots, conversely, offer high efficiency and speed on smooth terrains but are limited by their ability to traverse obstacles or uneven ground.

Wheeled-legged robots aim to combine the strengths of both locomotion types. These hybrid robots can be further divided into two main categories: actuated wheeled-legged robots and passive wheeled-legged robots.

- **Actuated Wheeled-Legged Robots:** These robots integrate powered wheels onto their legs, allowing them to switch between walking and driving modes. Examples include Boston Dynamics' Handle, ETH Zurich's ANYmal (when equipped with actuated wheels), DRC-HUBO+, and the BHR-WI. While these systems offer enhanced mobility and efficiency, they incur increased complexity, weight, and specialized hardware/electrical design due to the active wheel components.
- **Passive Wheeled-Legged Robots:** These robots utilize unactuated, or "passive," wheels on their feet. This design choice aims to reduce hardware complexity, weight, and cost, making them suitable for energy-efficient transportation on relatively flat or gentle slope terrains. Existing passive wheeled-legged robots, such as those employing single-degree-of-freedom (DOF) fixed wheels or skateboards, often face a significant limitation: a lack of omnidirectional mobility. Their non-holonomic constraints restrict movement primarily to forward or backward directions, making lateral motion difficult or impossible without complex body maneuvers.

This paper addresses the omnidirectional mobility limitation of passive wheeled-legged robots. By incorporating 2-DOF casters on each leg, the proposed robot aims to achieve omnidirectional movement capability while retaining the high efficiency and low-cost benefits associated with passive wheel designs. The work thus contributes to the field of hybrid locomotion by exploring a novel mechanism for propulsion and control in strongly underactuated systems, specifically focusing on expanding the maneuverability of passive wheeled-legged platforms beyond traditional forward-only motion. The method introduces a control strategy that leverages leg actuation to manipulate the passive casters for directional propulsion, moving beyond simple rolling or fixed-direction skating.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are:

- **Develop a novel passive wheeled-legged robot with omnidirectional mobility:** The main motivation is to overcome the limitation of existing passive wheeled-legged robots, which typically lack omnidirectional movement due to their single-DOF wheels and non-holonomic constraints. The authors propose a new mechanical design that incorporates 2-DOF casters on each leg to enable movement in any direction.
- **Achieve high efficiency and high-speed motion:** While omnidirectionality is a primary goal, the inherent advantage of wheeled locomotion—energy efficiency on flat terrain—is also a key objective. The authors aim for the robot to operate at high speeds with reduced energy consumption compared to legged gaits.
- **Propose a control method for strongly underactuated caster-based systems:** The use of passive casters results in a strongly underactuated system, posing a significant control challenge for generating directional propulsion. A key objective is to develop a robust control strategy that can effectively utilize the passive casters for movement.
- **Implement a caster base posture adjustment strategy:** To address the propulsion generation issue, the authors aim to develop a method where the robot's actuated joints modify the caster base's joint axis posture. This adjustment is intended to regulate the propulsive force direction generated by the passive casters, enabling controlled omnidirectional movement.
- **Introduce multiple propulsion modes:** The research aims to explore the redundancy of four casters by designing multiple drive modes (e.g., four-wheel, front-wheel, rear-wheel drive). This is intended to offer versatility in locomotion and potentially optimize energy usage for different scenarios.
- **Utilize an asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning framework:** A specific objective is to leverage reinforcement learning (RL) with an asymmetric actor-critic structure. This structure allows the critic to access "privileged information" (such as passive caster angles and velocities) during training, which is not available to the actor in deployment, thereby enhancing the learning process for the underactuated system.
- **Verify the proposed robot and controller through various experiments:** The research aims to validate the effectiveness of the design and control strategy through empirical tests, including omnidirectional movement (slalom test), high-speed motion, mode switching, and a quantitative comparison of energy efficiency using the Cost of Transport (COT) metric against traditional legged locomotion.

In summary, the motivation stems from the desire to create a cost-effective, energy-efficient, and highly maneuverable wheeled-legged robot that overcomes the directional limitations of previous passive wheeled designs, bridging the gap between efficient wheeled transport and agile, adaptable locomotion.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research methodology encompasses mechanical design, a detailed analysis of the propulsion mechanism, and a reinforcement learning-based control framework.

**A. Mechanical Design and Driving Mechanism**
The robot's mechanical design features a floating base, twelve actuated revolute joints, and eight passive revolute joints. Each of the four legs has three actuated DOFs: hip abduction/adduction, hip flexion/extension, and knee flexion/extension. Crucially, each leg is equipped with a replaceable 2-DOF passive caster. These casters consist of a swivel axis (for fork rotation) and a wheel axle (for wheel rotation). The three actuated joints per leg allow the robot to actively adjust the pose of the caster base. This reconfigurability of the caster base posture is central to achieving omnidirectional mobility.

The paper provides a detailed force analysis for a single passive caster with an inclined swivel axis, assuming pure rolling and no lateral slip.
1. **Coordinate System:** A body-fixed coordinate frame (i, j, k) is defined, where **i** is forward, **j** is lateral, and **k** is vertical.
2. **Wheel Axle Projection:** The unit vector **a** along the wheel axle is projected onto the ground plane as **a'**. This projection determines the non-rolling direction **n'** at the contact point.
3. **Allowable Rolling Direction:** The unit vector **t'** along the allowable rolling direction is perpendicular to **n'** in the ground plane.
4. **Moment Equilibrium:** By neglecting friction and rotational inertia about the swivel axis, the moment component of the ground reaction force **F_Λ** about the swivel axis is assumed to vanish. This leads to the derivation of the magnitude of the no-slip constraint reaction **F'**:
 `F' = (N_o * sin(α) * sin(φ)) / sqrt(cos^2(α) * sin^2(φ) + cos^2(φ))`
 where `N_o` is the vertical normal force, `α` is the inclination angle of the swivel axis, and `φ` is the caster deflection angle.
5. **Propulsive Force:** The forward propulsive force `F` is the component of `F'` along the forward direction **i**:
 `F = (N_o * sin(α) * cos(α) * sin^2(φ)) / (cos^2(α) * sin^2(φ) + cos^2(φ))`
 This derivation indicates that propulsive force is generated through a geometric-constraint coupling involving the swivel-axis inclination (`α`), caster deflection (`φ`), and the no-slip constraint. Both `α ≠ 0` and `φ ≠ 0` are necessary for propulsion. Periodic variation of `φ` is required for continuous propulsion.

**B. Control Framework**
The control framework utilizes an Asymmetric Actor-Critic structure based on Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).
- **Actor-Critic Asymmetry:** During training, the Critic receives "privileged information" which includes the passive caster deflection angles (`φ`) and velocities (`φ̇`). This information is not available to the Actor during deployment.
- **Actor Input (Deployment):** The Actor's input consists of velocity commands (linear velocities in x, y, and angular velocity around z), Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU) data (angular velocities, quaternion), and actuated joint encoder data (joint angles and velocities). The previous action is also included.
- **Policy Output:** The policy outputs actuated joint position offsets. These offsets are added to nominal joint angles to determine target joint positions, which are then converted to joint torques via PD controllers.

**C. Caster Base Posture Adjustment Strategy**
This strategy is crucial for directing the propulsive force. The robot dynamically adjusts the caster base's posture based on reference velocity commands.
- **Swivel Axis Orientation:** The angle `θ_swivel_axis` between the horizontal component of the caster swivel axis vector and the linear velocity vector in the world frame is constrained to satisfy `|θ_swivel_axis| < 90°`. This ensures that the swivel axis has a positive component along the desired motion direction, biasing the policy towards generating propulsion in that direction.
- **Caster Deflection Alignment:** An angle deviation constraint `θ_deviation` between the caster base deflection angle (`φ`) and a command angle (`θ_cmd`, derived from `atan2(v_y_cmd, v_x_cmd)`) is introduced. This constraint helps align the caster's orientation with the commanded velocity.

**D. Observation Definition**
- **Actor Observations (`s_actor`):** Includes velocity commands, angular velocity in robot's base frame, gravity vector in robot's base frame, actuated joint positions, actuated joint velocities, and the previous action.
- **Critic Observations (`s_critic`):** Includes all Actor observations plus privileged observations: z-axis of the caster base coordinate frame, caster swivel axis joint positions (`φ`), caster swivel axis joint velocities (`φ̇`), and linear velocity of the torso in the world coordinate frame.

**E. Reward Function Design**
The reward functions guide the robot to learn the posture adjustment strategy and utilize periodic caster oscillations for propulsion.
- **Velocity Tracking:** Rewards for tracking linear (x, y) and angular (yaw) velocity commands.
- **Caster Base Posture Tracking:** A reward term `max(cos(π/2) - cos(θ_swivel_axis), 0)` is active when the robot's speed is above 0.2 m/s, encouraging the swivel axis to align with the commanded direction.
- **Caster Base Joint Angle Tracking:** A penalty term `min(cos(θ_deviation) - cos(π/2), 0)` discourages large deviations between caster deflection and command angle.
- **Caster Oscillation Mask:** A Boolean mask `M` is introduced. For `b_i = 1`, it penalizes oscillation of caster `i`. For `b_i = 0`, it encourages oscillation. This allows for different propulsion modes.
- **Other Rewards/Penalties:** Body height tracking, torso z-direction and roll-pitch velocity penalties, collision penalties, termination penalties, actuated joint acceleration, position, velocity, and torque limit penalties, standing joint positions penalty, and action rate penalty.

**F. Domain Randomization**
To enhance the sim-to-real transferability and adaptive performance, domain randomization is applied during training. This includes randomizing:
- Caster joint damping (range [0.01, 0.1]).
- Joint proportional gain (Kp) and derivative gain (Kd) (range [0.9, 1.1]).
- Torso center of mass position (range [-0.05, +0.05]).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several experiments to validate the proposed design and control strategy.

**A. Caster Base Posture Adjustment Strategy Simulation**
A simulation experiment was conducted to evaluate the dynamic response of the caster base posture adjustment to varying velocity commands.
- **Observation:** Before 6 seconds (no command), casters moved unordered for stability. From 6 to 10 seconds (backward command, -0.6 m/s), `cos(φ)` remained near -1 (caster base angle ~ ±180°), and error angles `θ_swivel_axis` and `θ_deviation` maintained positive values (< 90°). At 10 seconds, a step change to forward command (+0.6 m/s) caused the caster base angle to transition from ~±180° to near 0°.
- **Result:** The error angles `θ_swivel_axis` and `θ_deviation` briefly exceeded 90° upon the step change but were rapidly reduced to below 90° within approximately 0.5 seconds by the controller. This demonstrated the responsiveness and effectiveness of the caster base angle adjustment strategy in controlling the propulsive direction.

**B. The Slalom Test in the Real World**
A real-world slalom test with 1.8m spacing between poles was performed to assess the robot's omnidirectional mobility.
- **Observation:** When a rightward (positive y-direction) command was given, the lateral swing joints (abduction/adduction) of the left legs (LF and FR) opened. Conversely, for leftward commands, the lateral swing joints of the right legs opened. The hip and knee joints also coordinated their movements.
- **Result:** The robot successfully navigated the slalom course, indicating that the combined action of lateral swing joints, hip joints, and knee joints effectively adjusted the caster base posture and swing, enabling stable omnidirectional motion in a real-world setting.

**C. High-Speed Motion Experience**
A high-speed backward motion test was conducted on flat terrain.
- **Observation:** The robot covered a distance of 8.4 meters in 2.05 seconds.
- **Result:** This yielded an average speed of approximately 4.1 m/s. During the constant-velocity phase, the robot demonstrated low energy consumption, attributed to the use of passive casters.

**D. Mode Switch of Passive Wheeled Quadruped Robots**
The ability to switch between different propulsion modes (four-wheel drive, front-wheel drive, rear-wheel drive) was validated.
- **Observation:** When specific propulsion legs were disabled using a mask, the linear velocity at the foot end of those legs was correspondingly reduced.
- **Result:** The robot demonstrated the capability to smoothly switch between these modes, confirming that the masking mechanism effectively controls which casters contribute to propulsion.

**E. Cost of Transport Comparison**
The mechanical Cost of Transport (COT) was used to compare the energy consumption of the passive caster locomotion with traditional point-foot trotting. The experiment involved traversing a 1.1-kilometer trajectory on campus, including downhill, flat, and uphill segments.
- **Point-foot Trot:** Average Battery Output Power: 334.93 W; Average Velocity: 1.124 m/s; Mechanical COT: 0.648.
- **Caster Foot:** Average Battery Output Power: 192.69 W; Average Velocity: 1.414 m/s; Mechanical COT: 0.0705.
- **Driving Wheel (from literature, not the robot in question):** Average Battery Output Power: 229.26 W; Average Velocity: 0.842 m/s; Mechanical COT: 0.0637.
- **Result:** The passive wheeled quadruped robot achieved an approximately 89.1% reduction in COT compared to the point-foot trotting mode of the same quadruped robot (Unitree Go2). The total energy consumption for the 1.1 km trajectory was 41 Wh for the passive wheeled mode, significantly lower than 91 Wh for the point-foot mode.
- **Power Consumption Analysis:** Power consumption for the passive wheeled quadruped decreased significantly on downhill terrain, an advantage not observed with point-foot robots. Even on uphill segments, the passive wheeled-legged robot maintained substantially lower energy consumption.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of mobile robotics by presenting a functional design and control strategy for an omnidirectional passive wheeled-legged robot.

The primary significance lies in demonstrating a method to achieve omnidirectional mobility with passive casters, which addresses a key limitation of previous passive wheeled-legged platforms that were often restricted to linear or limited directional movement. By leveraging the robot's actuated leg joints to adjust the caster base posture, the system can dynamically modify the propulsive direction, effectively converting static friction and vertical load into directed forces for multi-directional movement.

The achieved energy efficiency, quantified by an 89.1% reduction in Cost of Transport compared to traditional legged locomotion (trotting), positions this approach as a potentially viable solution for long-distance transportation tasks on relatively smooth or gentle terrains. The capability for high-speed motion (up to 4.1 m/s) further enhances its utility in scenarios requiring rapid deployment or traversal.

The integration of an asymmetric actor-critic reinforcement learning framework, which utilizes privileged information during training, highlights a successful application of this control paradigm to strongly underactuated systems. This approach allows for the development of complex, dynamic gaits and behaviors without direct measurement of passive joint states in real-world deployment, simplifying hardware requirements.

The development of multiple drive modes (e.g., four-wheel, front-wheel, rear-wheel) provides versatility, allowing the robot to adapt its propulsion mechanism based on terrain, desired maneuverability, or energy optimization goals. This modularity in propulsion could extend the robot's operational envelope.

Potential impacts include:
- **Cost-effective and Energy-efficient Robotics:** The use of passive casters reduces the complexity and cost associated with actuated wheels, making omnidirectional hybrid robots more accessible for various applications, particularly those requiring long-duration operations.
- **Enhanced Logistics and Transportation:** For tasks involving material transport or surveillance in large, relatively flat environments (e.g., warehouses, factory floors, campuses), this robot could offer a more efficient and agile solution than traditional legged or single-direction wheeled platforms.
- **Further Research in Underactuated Systems:** The findings can inform future research in controlling strongly underactuated robotic systems, particularly those relying on environmental interactions for propulsion without direct actuation of the contact elements.

A noted limitation is the suboptimal velocity tracking performance due to the lack of caster angle sensors, suggesting future work in integrating external sensing (e.g., visual odometry) to enhance precision. Despite this, the current work demonstrates a significant step towards practical and versatile passive wheeled-legged locomotion.
