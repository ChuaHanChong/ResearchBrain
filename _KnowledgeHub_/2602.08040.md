# FIRE: Frobenius-Isometry Reinitialization for Balancing the Stability-Plasticity Tradeoff

> [!summary] Summary
> FIRE (Frobenius–Isometry REinitialization) introduces a method for deep neural networks to manage the stability–plasticity tradeoff by reinitializing weights through a constrained optimization. This approach minimizes changes to existing knowledge while enforcing an isometric property for adaptability, leading to improved performance in continual visual learning, language modeling, and reinforcement learning with negligible computational overhead.

## Problem

- Deep neural networks struggle with the stability–plasticity tradeoff when learning continuously from nonstationary data, often leading to catastrophic forgetting or an inability to adapt to new information.
- Existing reinitialization methods face a 'tuning dilemma': aggressive resets erase valuable knowledge, while conservative resets fail to restore sufficient plasticity, lacking a principled way to balance these.
- Prior metrics for plasticity (e.g., sharp loss curvature, dormant neurons) are often data-dependent and non-differentiable, making them unsuitable as direct optimization objectives for reinitialization strategies.

## Method

- Proposes **FIRE**, a ==reinitialization== method that formulates the stability–plasticity tradeoff as a constrained optimization problem.
- Quantifies stability using Squared Frobenius Error (SFE) to measure the similarity between original and reinitialized weights and defines plasticity loss by Deviation from Isometry (DfI) of the weight matrix.
- Solves the optimization problem (minimizing SFE subject to an isometry constraint) efficiently using the Newton–Schulz iteration to approximate the polar decomposition, driving singular values towards one.

## Results

- Achieved consistent performance gains across continual visual learning benchmarks (CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, Tiny-ImageNet) in warm-start, continual, and class-incremental settings.
- Mitigated severe plasticity degradation in continual pretraining of GPT-**0.1B** from later, more rigid checkpoints, and achieved superior or competitive performance in reinforcement learning (DQN on Atari, SAC on HumanoidBench) under high replay ratios.
- FIRE achieved the lowest DfI while maintaining the lowest SFE compared to other reinitialization baselines, produced smoother loss landscapes, and incurred negligible computational cost (less than **1%** training time increase).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Stability, defined as the preservation of learned information, can be effectively bounded by the Squared Frobenius Error (SFE) between weight matrices, preserving feature similarity.
> - Plasticity can be quantified by Deviation from Isometry (DfI), which correlates with a smoother loss landscape, higher effective rank of feature matrices, and increased neuron activity.
> - The stability–plasticity tradeoff can be systematically balanced by projecting network weights onto the isotropy manifold while minimizing their change from original values, providing a principled reinitialization strategy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep neural networks, while highly effective in many domains, encounter a fundamental challenge when tasked with learning continuously from nonstationary data distributions. This scenario requires models to adapt to new information while simultaneously retaining previously acquired knowledge, a problem often termed the "stability–plasticity tradeoff." This tradeoff implies that excessive stability can prevent a model from adapting, while too much plasticity can lead to "catastrophic forgetting" of past learning.

The existing research landscape addresses this challenge through two primary categories of methods: regularization-based and reinitialization-based. Regularization methods aim to constrain parameter updates, often by keeping them close to their initial values or enforcing specific geometric properties like orthogonality. While these methods can promote favorable learning geometries, their effectiveness is limited by the strictness of the constraints; overly strong constraints impede new learning, and overly weak ones fail to prevent plasticity degradation.

Reinitialization-based methods periodically reset or partially reset network weights to earlier checkpoints when new data becomes available. These methods offer advantages in terms of faster adaptation and lower computational overhead during optimization, as they avoid interfering with the ongoing training process. However, they face a "tuning dilemma": aggressive reinitializations can erase valuable learned knowledge, leading to instability, while conservative reinitializations may not sufficiently restore plasticity.

This work specifically operates within the regime where access to past data is assumed during continual learning, distinguishing it from classical continual learning that often assumes limited or no access to previous data. In this context, the focus shifts from mitigating catastrophic forgetting (a stability issue under data scarcity) to addressing plasticity loss, where models struggle to adapt to new tasks even with access to prior information. The paper aims to provide a principled solution to the tuning dilemma inherent in reinitialization methods, thereby advancing the field's ability to maintain adaptability in dynamic, real-world environments such as autonomous driving, large language model updates, and robotic control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to develop a principled reinitialization method that explicitly balances the stability–plasticity tradeoff in deep neural networks trained on nonstationary data. This objective is motivated by several identified limitations and challenges within current continual learning paradigms:

1. **Addressing the Tuning Dilemma of Reinitialization Methods:** Standard reinitialization strategies, such as resetting weights to an earlier checkpoint, face a dilemma. Aggressive resets can erase useful knowledge and lead to instability, effectively restarting the learning process unnecessarily. Conversely, conservative resets may fail to adequately restore the model's capacity to learn new information (plasticity), leaving it rigid. The paper aims to overcome this by introducing a method that inherently navigates this balance without extensive manual hyperparameter tuning.

2. **Developing Quantifiable and Optimizable Metrics for Stability and Plasticity:** Prior work has linked plasticity loss to phenomena like sharp loss curvature, dormant neurons, and low-rank features. However, these indicators are often data-dependent and non-differentiable, making them unsuitable as direct optimization objectives for reinitialization strategies. A key motivation is to define complementary, optimizable metrics that accurately capture the essential dimensions of stability and plasticity:
 - **Stability:** Quantifying the retention of prior knowledge, or how close the model remains to its past representations.
 - **Plasticity:** Quantifying the model's readiness to learn new information, including properties like a smooth loss landscape, active neurons, and high-rank features.

3. **Formulating Reinitialization as a Principled Constrained Optimization Problem:** Instead of relying on heuristic or empirical tuning, the authors propose to frame the reinitialization process as a constrained optimization problem. This mathematical formulation is intended to provide a systematic and theoretically grounded approach to finding an optimal reinitialization point that simultaneously promotes stability and restores plasticity. This is presented as an advancement over existing methods that often involve trial-and-error hyperparameter searches.

4. **Enhancing Performance Across Diverse Continual Learning Domains:** The ultimate motivation is to improve the performance and robustness of deep neural networks in real-world applications characterized by nonstationary data. This includes computer vision systems (e.g., autonomous driving), large language models (requiring continuous updates), and robotic agents (adapting to dynamic environments). The goal is to provide a unified solution applicable across these varied domains, demonstrating its generalizability and effectiveness.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, **FIRE (Frobenius–Isometry REinitialization)**, addresses the stability–plasticity tradeoff by formulating reinitialization as a constrained optimization problem. This approach relies on two metrics: Squared Frobenius Error (SFE) for stability and Deviation from Isometry (DfI) for plasticity, and leverages an efficient approximation technique to solve the underlying optimization.

#### 3.1. Measure for Stability: Squared Frobenius Error (SFE)

Stability, defined as the preservation of learned information, is quantified by the Squared Frobenius Error (SFE) between the original weight matrix $W$ and its reinitialized counterpart $\tilde{W}$:
$\text{SFE}(W, \tilde{W}) = \lVert W - \tilde{W} \rVert_F^2$.
A lower SFE indicates greater similarity between the weights, implying better retention of prior representations. The paper provides theoretical justification (Theorem 1), demonstrating that SFE bounds the discrepancy between the normalized feature covariances of two deep neural networks. This theorem establishes that minimizing SFE can effectively preserve feature similarity, with the sensitivity of this bound being proportional to the spectral norms of the weight matrices.

#### 3.2. Measure for Plasticity Loss: Deviation from Isometry (DfI)

To address the limitations of prior plasticity metrics (e.g., data-dependent, non-differentiable measures like sharp loss curvature or dormant neurons), the authors propose Deviation from Isometry (DfI):
$\text{DfI}(W) = \lVert W^T W - I \rVert_F^2$.
DfI measures how close a weight matrix is to being orthonormal. Theoretical analyses (Theorems 2, 3, and 4) support DfI as a plasticity measure:
- **Theorem 2:** Minimizing DfI results in a smoother loss landscape curvature by bounding the Hessian spectral norm.
- **Theorem 3:** DfI controls the effective rank of feature matrices, where lower DfI corresponds to higher effective rank.
- **Theorem 4:** Minimizing DfI increases neuron activity scores and reduces dormant neurons by tightening the bounds on relative neuron activations within a layer.
These theoretical connections establish DfI as an optimizable proxy for maintaining favorable properties associated with high plasticity and good initialization.

#### 3.3. Balancing Stability and Plasticity: Constrained Optimization

FIRE frames the stability–plasticity tradeoff as a constrained optimization problem:
$\min_{\tilde{W}} \lVert W - \tilde{W} \rVert_F^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \tilde{W}^T \tilde{W} = I$.
This objective minimizes the SFE (stability) while enforcing perfect isometry (DfI = 0) as a constraint (plasticity). This formulation is equivalent to the Orthogonal Procrustes Problem, which has a closed-form solution derived from the polar decomposition:
$\tilde{W}^\star = W (W^T W)^{-1/2}$. This solution projects the weights onto the isotropy manifold while remaining as close as possible to their original subspace.

#### 3.4. Approximating the Solution: Newton–Schulz Iteration

Directly computing the inverse square root of $W^T W$ can be computationally intensive for large networks. FIRE approximates this solution efficiently using the Newton–Schulz iteration. For a weight matrix $X$, the iteration is defined as:
$X_{k+1} = a X_k + b X_k (X_k^T X_k)$, with constants $a=1.5$ and $b=-0.5$, initialized with $X_0 = W / \lVert W \rVert_F$. This iterative process drives the singular values of $W$ towards 1, approximating orthonormality with minimal computational overhead (reported as less than 1% increase in training time). The paper clarifies that this choice of coefficients prioritizes accuracy of convergence to an orthogonal matrix over faster, potentially less accurate, approximations used in some other methods.

#### 3.5. Implementation Details (Appendix C)

- **Linear Layers:** Weights $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$ are normalized, orthogonalized via Newton–Schulz, and then scaled by $\sqrt{d_{\text{out}}/d_{\text{in}}}$ to preserve stable signal variance, drawing inspiration from Modular Duality.
- **Convolutional Layers:** Filters $W \in \mathbb{R}^{C_{\text{out}} \times C_{\text{in}} \times k_h \times k_w}$ are orthogonalized kernel-wise along spatial dimensions. The scaling factor for convolutional layers is $\sqrt{C_{\text{out}} / (C_{\text{in}} k_h k_w)}$.
- **Attention Modules (ViTs):** Orthogonalization is applied specifically to the query (Q) and key (K) projections due to their sensitivity to conditioning, while value (V), output, and MLP weights are left unchanged.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

FIRE was evaluated across continual visual learning, language modeling, and reinforcement learning domains, consistently demonstrating its effectiveness in balancing the stability–plasticity tradeoff.

#### 4.1. Continual Visual Learning

Experiments were conducted on CIFAR-10 (ResNet-18), CIFAR-100 (ViT-Tiny), and Tiny-ImageNet (VGG-16) under warm-start, continual, and class-incremental protocols. Baselines included naive training, reinitialization methods (S&P, DASH), regularization methods (Parseval regularization, L2init), node-resetting methods (CBP, SNR, ReDo), and the Muon optimizer.

- **Warm-Start Setting (10% initial data, then full dataset):** FIRE achieved consistent performance gains across all benchmarks. It outperformed most baselines on CIFAR-10 and Tiny-ImageNet, showing a strong ability to recover plasticity. On CIFAR-100 (ViT-Tiny), it was competitive with S&P and outperformed all baselines except DASH, which employs a data-dependent strategy. Parseval regularization and L2init showed slower convergence.
- **Continual Setting (data expanding from 10% to 100% in 10 stages):** FIRE delivered consistent gains across all datasets. It exhibited only slight or negligible performance drops after reinitialization, unlike full reset and DASH which suffered sharp drops. S&P avoided sharp drops but remained suboptimal.
- **Class-Incremental Setting (new classes introduced over 20 phases):** FIRE maintained strong performance without significant drops, consistent with its balanced approach. Full reset and DASH showed sharp drops, while S&P performed suboptimally.
- **Node-resetting methods (CBP, SNR, ReDo) and Muon optimizer:** These baselines generally showed poor overall performance across visual learning tasks.

#### 4.2. Continual Pretraining of LLMs

A GPT-0.1B model was pretrained on WikiText-103 and then continually trained on a mixture of OpenWebText and WikiText-103, using checkpoints from various pretraining durations (best, 30k, 60k iterations).

- Plasticity loss was observed to worsen with longer pretraining (validation loss increased for the base model initialized from later checkpoints).
- FIRE maintained strong performance even when initialized from the 60k checkpoint, demonstrating its ability to mitigate severe plasticity degradation.
- S&P, despite careful tuning, remained suboptimal compared to FIRE.
- Full reset performed poorly, indicating that erasing all past information introduced instability that outweighed plasticity restoration in this context.

#### 4.3. Reinforcement Learning

FIRE was evaluated in high Replay Ratio (RR) settings, where plasticity loss is significant, using DQN on Atari games (Asterix, BeamRider, DemonAttack) and SAC on HumanoidBench tasks (balance, walk, run). Baselines included full reset, S&P, and Plasticity Injection.

- **DQN on Atari:** FIRE consistently outperformed S&P, surpassed full reset in Asterix, and remained competitive in other environments. S&P provided minor convergence speed improvements but was suboptimal overall.
- **SAC on HumanoidBench:** FIRE achieved superior or competitive performance across tasks compared to baselines. Plasticity Injection, which uses additional parameters, showed poor performance.
- The results suggest that FIRE's principled approach is more effective than manually tuned hyperparameters in mitigating severe plasticity loss in visual reinforcement learning.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

An ablation study further analyzed FIRE's performance and properties:

- **Balancing Stability and Plasticity:** FIRE achieved the lowest DfI while simultaneously maintaining the lowest SFE compared to other reinitialization baselines. This indicates that FIRE effectively balances both objectives in practice.
- **Loss Landscape Curvature:** FIRE produced a smoother loss landscape (lower maximum eigenvalue of the Hessian) compared to S&P, while also preserving lower SFE. DASH, while effective at smoothing the landscape, exhibited the highest SFE, suggesting potential erasure of useful knowledge.
- **Robustness to Iterations:** FIRE demonstrated high robustness to the number of Newton–Schulz iterations. Strong performance gains were observed even with as few as five iterations, with minimal impact from increasing iterations, indicating practical efficiency. However, using very few iterations (e.g., one) could introduce instability and performance degradation despite quickly reducing DfI.

#### 4.5. Computational Efficiency (Appendix B.1)

FIRE incurs negligible computational cost (0.06 seconds) and GPU memory usage (55 MB) compared to baselines like DASH (69 seconds, 2834 MB), similar to Shrink & Perturb.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of FIRE represents a significant contribution to the field of continual learning and the broader application of deep neural networks in dynamic environments. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Principled Resolution of the Stability–Plasticity Tradeoff:** By framing reinitialization as a constrained optimization problem, FIRE offers a theoretically grounded and systematic approach to managing the inherent conflict between retaining prior knowledge (stability) and adapting to new information (plasticity). This moves beyond heuristic tuning methods, providing a more robust and predictable mechanism for adaptation.

2. **Introduction of Effective and Optimizable Metrics:** The paper's contribution of Squared Frobenius Error (SFE) for stability and Deviation from Isometry (DfI) for plasticity, along with their theoretical connections to key network properties (feature similarity, loss landscape smoothness, effective rank, neuron activity), provides valuable tools for both analysis and optimization in continual learning. DfI, in particular, addresses the need for a differentiable, data-independent plasticity measure.

3. **Demonstrated Generalizability Across Diverse Domains:** The consistent outperformance or competitiveness of FIRE against various baselines across a wide range of tasks—including continual visual learning, large language model pretraining, and reinforcement learning (both discrete and continuous control)—underscores its general applicability. This suggests that the underlying principle of balancing weight stability with isotropy is fundamental to effective continual adaptation in deep learning.

4. **Enhanced Practicality and Efficiency:** The efficient approximation of the constrained optimization solution via the Newton–Schulz iteration ensures that FIRE can be integrated into existing training pipelines with negligible computational overhead and memory footprint. This practicality, combined with its reduced reliance on extensive hyperparameter tuning, makes it a viable tool for real-world deployments.

5. **Advancing Foundation Model Lifecycles and Robotic Autonomy:** The demonstrated effectiveness in continual pretraining of LLMs and in reinforcement learning tasks suggests that FIRE can play a crucial role in maintaining the relevance and adaptability of large foundation models and autonomous agents in rapidly evolving environments. This can lead to more resilient and efficient systems in areas like AI-driven content generation, autonomous driving, and robotics.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The primary limitation acknowledged by the authors is the assumption of access to past data during continual learning. Future work could investigate FIRE's performance under restricted data access scenarios, which are common in some continual learning settings. Additionally, while promising results were shown for LLMs, the evaluations were conducted on relatively smaller models (GPT-0.1B); testing FIRE on larger-scale language models and exploring its application in continual fine-tuning scenarios could be a valuable direction for future research.
