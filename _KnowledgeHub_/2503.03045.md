# ArticuBot: Learning Universal Articulated Object Manipulation Policy via Large Scale Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> ArticuBot presents a single, vision-based robotic policy capable of robustly manipulating diverse, unseen articulated objects in the real world. By training on a large-scale simulated dataset and employing a hierarchical policy architecture, it achieves effective zero-shot transfer for tasks like opening drawers and cabinets with standard parallel-jaw grippers.

## Problem

- Robotic manipulation of articulated objects faces challenges in generalizing across diverse geometries, sizes, and articulation types due to vast variations.
- Prior methods often rely on simplifying assumptions like pre-existing knowledge of articulation parameters, object-specific policies, or specialized grippers, limiting their real-world scalability and universality.
- Bridging the simulation-to-real-world gap remains difficult for complex manipulation tasks, often requiring extensive real-world data or fine-tuning.

## Method

- The approach leverages large-scale, automated demonstration generation (42.3k trajectories on 322 objects) in a physics-based simulator (Pybullet) using a sampling-based grasping and motion planning pipeline.
- A vision-based hierarchical policy is learned via imitation: a high-level weighted displacement model predicts sub-goal end-effector poses, which then conditions a low-level 3D diffusion policy to generate delta end-effector actions.
- Zero-shot transfer to real robots is enabled by using point cloud observations (with real-world segmentation and filtering), end-effector space actions, and targeted sim augmentations to bridge the sim2real gap.

## Results

- ArticuBot's hierarchical policy achieved **0.7**-**0.75** normalized opening performance in simulation, significantly outperforming non-hierarchical baselines (below **0.25**).
- It demonstrated robust zero-shot sim2real transfer, achieving **0.63** normalized opening on a Franka arm and **0.54** on an X-Arm on 20 diverse, unseen articulated objects across multiple real-world environments.
- ArticuBot outperformed existing methods like FlowBot3D (**0.86** vs. **0.57** normalized opening after grasp) and a leading generalist robotic foundation model, OpenVLA (which failed to open any objects).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A hierarchical policy, decoupling 'where to go' from 'how to move', is crucial for achieving object-level generalization across diverse articulated objects.
> - The weighted displacement model in the high-level policy enables robust predictions by grounding them in the 3D scene structure and implicitly focusing on task-relevant regions like handles.
> - A learned low-level policy is essential for handling minor collisions and accurately following object kinematic constraints during real-world opening, outperforming traditional motion planning and IK controllers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

ArticuBot addresses the long-standing and challenging problem of **robotic manipulation of articulated objects** (e.g., cabinets, drawers, fridges). This task is inherently difficult due to the vast variations in geometry, size, and articulation types of such objects (e.g., hinge joints, prismatic joints, different handle shapes and positions).

Historically, prior work in articulated object manipulation has faced several limitations:
- **Limited Real-World Generalization:** Many methods show promising results primarily in simulation, with real-world deployments often restricted to a few specific objects or highly controlled lab settings.
- **Simplifying Assumptions:** Some approaches rely on suction grippers (simplifying the grasping problem), pre-existing knowledge of articulation parameters, or object-specific policies. These assumptions limit their scalability and universality.
- **Modular Systems:** Traditional approaches often involve complex pipelines with separate modules for perception, planning, and control. While robust in some cases, these can be brittle when errors propagate between modules and are hard to generalize across diverse objects.

ArticuBot positions itself by tackling these limitations head-on, aiming for a *single, generalist policy* that can manipulate *diverse unseen articulated objects in the real world* using *standard parallel-jaw grippers* and *purely visual observations*, without explicit articulation parameter inference.

This work is heavily influenced by the recent success of **sim2real policy learning** and the trend towards **robotic foundation models**.
- **Sim2Real Policy Learning:** The paradigm of training policies extensively in simulation and transferring them zero-shot to the real world has proven effective in various domains like grasping, locomotion, and deformable object manipulation. ArticuBot extends this success to the complex domain of generalizable articulated object manipulation, a task where sim2real transfer for universal policies has not been widely demonstrated. Unlike many sim2real works that use reinforcement learning, ArticuBot employs imitation learning from expert demonstrations generated in simulation.
- **Robotic Foundation Models:** A recent surge in robotics research focuses on creating "foundation models" – single, large models capable of performing multiple tasks or generalizing across diverse settings. Projects like RT-1, RT-2, and OpenVLA demonstrate remarkable generalism by training on vast real-world datasets. ArticuBot aligns with this philosophy by aiming for a *universal* policy. However, it distinguishes itself by:
 - **Domain Specialization:** While RT-X models are broad, ArticuBot deeply specializes in articulated object manipulation, showing that focused, scaled-up learning can achieve robust performance on specific, complex tasks where generalist models might still struggle (as evidenced by OpenVLA's poor performance in the paper's comparison).
 - **Data Source:** ArticuBot leverages *simulation-generated data* for scale, contrasting with the predominantly real-world data collection strategy of many generalist foundation models. This highlights the power of high-fidelity simulation and automated data generation for specific robotic challenges.
 - **Practicality:** The focus on standard parallel-jaw grippers and deployment on commercial manipulators (Franka, X-Arm) in diverse real-world settings (labs, kitchens, lounges) emphasizes the practical applicability of the learned policy.

In essence, ArticuBot represents a significant step towards practical and universal articulated object manipulation, leveraging the scalability of simulation and imitation learning to overcome challenges that have previously limited real-world generalization in this domain.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of ArticuBot is to develop a single, learned robotic policy that can robustly open a wide variety of *unseen* articulated objects in the *real world*, solely based on visual observations and without any prior knowledge of the objects' articulation parameters.

The key motivations driving this research include:

- **Ubiquity and Practical Demand:** Articulated objects are pervasive in both industrial and household environments (e.g., factory cabinets, kitchen drawers, refrigerators, microwaves). Automating their manipulation is crucial for various applications, from smart homes to automated warehouses.
- **Addressing Generalization Challenges:** Existing robotic manipulation systems often struggle with the inherent diversity of articulated objects in terms of their geometry, shape, size, and articulation mechanisms (e.g., revolute doors, prismatic drawers). Learning a single policy capable of handling such vast variations without explicit object models is a significant challenge.
- **Overcoming Simplifying Assumptions:** Many prior works circumvent the complexity of articulated object manipulation by making simplifying assumptions, such as using specialized grippers (e.g., suction cups) or assuming access to object CAD models or articulation parameters. ArticuBot aims to develop a solution that works with standard parallel-jaw grippers and real-world visual input, making it more broadly applicable.
- **Leveraging Data-Driven Paradigms:** Inspired by the success of large-scale data-driven approaches in other fields (e.g., large language models) and recent advancements in robot learning, the authors hypothesize that generating massive, diverse datasets in simulation and distilling them into a generalizable policy through imitation learning can unlock unprecedented generalization capabilities for this task.
- **Enabling Zero-Shot Sim2Real Transfer:** To bypass the high cost and time of real-world data collection, the project aims for zero-shot transfer from simulation to reality. This requires careful design of observations (e.g., point clouds) and actions (e.g., end-effector space control) to minimize the sim2real gap.

In summary, ArticuBot is motivated by the need for a truly universal and practical solution for articulated object manipulation, achieved by scaling up robot learning through large-scale simulation and novel policy architectures designed for generalization and robust sim2real transfer.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ArticuBot's methodology is structured around a three-stage pipeline: large-scale demonstration generation in simulation, hierarchical policy learning via imitation, and zero-shot sim2real transfer.

#### 3.1. Large-Scale Demonstration Generation in Simulation

The core idea is to automatically create a massive dataset of successful articulated object opening trajectories in a physics-based simulator (Pybullet), using the PartNet-Mobility dataset for object assets. The process is decomposed into two sub-steps: grasping and opening.

- **Simulation Initialization:** Franka arms are used, with randomization of object position, orientation, size, and initial robot joint angles to enhance data diversity.
- **Sampling-Based Grasping:** For a target object and its handle (using dataset annotations), the system generates numerous candidate grasping poses. This involves farthest point sampling on the handle for positions and aligning the end-effector's z-axis with the handle's normal, with small angular perturbations to increase diversity.
- **Motion Planning for Reaching Grasp:** For each grasp candidate, Inverse Kinematics (IK) is used to compute target joint configurations. Collision-free solutions are preferred, and the one closest in joint space is selected. Multiple motion planning algorithms (RRT*, BIT*, ABIT*) are run, and paths are smoothed (B-spline) to find the shortest end-effector movement path.
- **Generating Opening Actions:** Once grasped, the system leverages ground-truth articulation information from simulation. It computes an "idealized" end-effector trajectory that maintains the relative pose between the gripper and the opening link as the object opens. IK is performed at each step to determine the necessary robot poses.
- **Filtering and Selection:** Trajectories failing to open the object beyond a threshold are filtered out. From successful trajectories, the single best one is chosen based on grasp stability (number of handle points between fingertips) and the length of the motion-planned trajectory. This efficient, parallelized pipeline generated 42.3k successful demonstrations for 322 objects.

#### 3.2. Policy Learning with a Hierarchical Policy Representation

The generated demonstrations are distilled into a vision-based neural policy using imitation learning. The policy takes point clouds (segmented to isolate the object) and robot proprioception as input, and outputs delta end-effector transformations.

- **Hierarchical Policy (Key Innovation):** To achieve object-level generalization, the authors propose a hierarchical policy comprising a high-level and a low-level component.
 - **High-Level Policy (Weighted Displacement Model):** This policy `π_H` predicts "where" the robot should move, specifically the *sub-goal end-effector pose*. Instead of predicting in free SE(3) space, it grounds its prediction in the observed 3D structure. The sub-goal end-effector pose is represented by 4 key points (robot hand root, two gripper fingers, grasping center). The policy learns to predict:
 1. The 3D *displacement* from each point in the input scene point cloud to each of the 4 sub-goal end-effector points.
 2. A *weight* for each scene point, indicating its importance (e.g., handle points would have higher weights).
 The final predicted sub-goal end-effector points are computed as a *weighted average* of (scene point + predicted displacement) over all scene points. PointNet++ is used as the backbone, making the model translation-invariant. The loss function supervises both per-point displacement and the final weighted average prediction.
 - **Low-Level Policy (Goal-Conditioned 3D Diffusion Policy):** This policy `π_L` predicts "how" to move, i.e., the delta end-effector transformation (translation, 6D rotation, finger movement). It is *conditioned* on the current observation and the sub-goal end-effector pose predicted by the high-level policy. It modifies the 3D Diffusion Policy (DP3) architecture. Its encoder uses cross-attention between current end-effector points, scene points, and the sub-goal end-effector points. Features include displacement to goal points and displacement to closest scene points (for collision avoidance). A UNet diffusion head generates the action, which is robust to multi-modal action distributions arising from motion planning.

#### 3.3. Zero-shot Transfer to Real Robotic Systems

The simulation-trained hierarchical policy is directly deployed on real robots without any fine-tuning.

- **Inference Pipeline:** At each timestep, the high-level policy predicts a sub-goal, which then conditions the low-level policy to produce an action. This loop continues until the object is opened or episode limits are reached.
- **Robust Sim2Real Transfer:**
 - **Observation Processing:** Point clouds are used as sensory input. In the real world, open-vocabulary segmentation (Grounded SAM, EfficientSAM) is used to isolate the target object, and the robot's own point cloud is removed using its URDF and current joint angles. Additional filters (radius, statistical outlier removal) are applied to clean noisy depth sensor data.
 - **Simulation Augmentations:** To bridge the sim2real gap, training data in simulation includes augmentations (edge artifacts, random holes) on depth maps, mimicking real-world sensor noise.
 - **Cross-Embodiment Transfer:** The policy learns actions in the robot's end-effector space (pose transformations) rather than joint space, which enables zero-shot transfer from a Franka arm (sim training) to a Franka arm in different labs and an X-Arm on a mobile base.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

ArticuBot's evaluation spans extensive simulation experiments and zero-shot real-world deployment across multiple robot setups.

#### 4.1. Simulation Results

- **Effectiveness of Hierarchical Policy:** A core finding is that the hierarchical policy significantly outperforms non-hierarchical baselines (e.g., standard DP3) for object-level generalization. While non-hierarchical policies showed normalized opening performance below 0.25, ArticuBot consistently achieved around 0.7-0.75. Crucially, non-hierarchical policies showed little improvement with increasing training data, underscoring that the hierarchical structure is vital for handling diverse objects.
- **Performance of High-Level Policy Architectures:** The proposed Weighted Displacement Model demonstrated strong performance. When trained with camera randomizations (crucial for sim2real), it consistently outperformed other baselines like DP3-UNet Diffusion and DP3-Transformer Diffusion, especially when tested under unseen camera randomizations. This highlights its robustness to sensor noise and viewpoint changes. Without camera randomizations, DP3-Transformer Diffusion sometimes achieved slightly higher peak performance, but ArticuBot's weighted displacement model showed better overall scaling and consistency across data sizes.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - Using a Point Transformer instead of PointNet++ in the high-level policy slightly decreased performance, supporting the chosen PointNet++ backbone.
 - The "unweighted displacement model" (predicting displacements without per-point weights) performed worse, validating the importance of the learned weighting mechanism, which implicitly focuses on task-relevant regions like the handle.
 - Representing the sub-goal end-effector as a 6D orientation (instead of 4 points) and averaging them led to a significant performance drop, confirming the effectiveness of the proposed 4-point representation, which simplifies aggregation across points.
 - Replacing the learned low-level policy with traditional motion planning and an IK controller resulted in very poor performance (0.24), indicating that a learned low-level policy is critical for handling minor collisions and accurately following object kinematic constraints during opening.
- **Comparison with Prior Methods:**
 - **AO-Grasp (Grasping):** ArticuBot achieved a much higher grasping success rate (0.88 vs. 0.11). AO-Grasp frequently failed by proposing grasps at "fake" edges or corners in partial point clouds, which were not actual graspable features, especially for initially closed objects.
 - **FlowBot3D (Opening):** When compared in an "after grasping" scenario (where ArticuBot's grasp was successful), ArticuBot achieved 0.86 normalized opening performance, significantly outperforming FlowBot3D (0.57 with mask, 0.2 without mask). FlowBot3D often predicted incorrect flow directions (e.g., upwards for a microwave) or failed to follow the correct articulation path.

#### 4.2. Real-World Experiments (Zero-Shot Transfer)

ArticuBot successfully transferred zero-shot to three different real-world robot setups, demonstrating its ability to open 20 diverse, unseen articulated objects (cabinets, drawers, microwaves, ovens, fridges) across two labs and real kitchens/lounges.

- **Table-top Franka Arm (Lab A & B):**
 - Lab A: Grasping Success Rate: 0.78, Normalized Opening Performance: 0.63.
 - Lab B: Grasping Success Rate: 0.85, Normalized Opening Performance: 0.59.
 - The policy successfully manipulated challenging objects with small knobs (e.g., A2, A8 in Lab A).
 - Visualizations confirmed the high-level policy's learned weights focused on the handles, and accurately predicted different opening poses for various articulation types.
- **Mobile X-Arm (Real Kitchens/Lounges):**
 - Grasping Success Rate: 0.90, Normalized Opening Performance: 0.54.
 - Showed generalization to real-world environments beyond controlled labs, including kitchens and lounges.
- **Comparison to OpenVLA:** OpenVLA, a leading open-source robotic foundation model, failed to open any test objects (0 performance), suggesting its generalist training did not provide sufficient expertise for this specific, complex manipulation task.
- **Common Failure Cases:**
 - **Sim2Real Gap:** A noticeable performance drop from simulation to real-world due to noisier real-world point cloud observations.
 - **Robot Constraints:** Robot running into joint limits due to limited workspace or imprecise movements.
 - **Grasping Failures:** Incorrect end-effector pose predictions for grasping, particularly on objects with very small handles.
 - **Slippage:** Gripper slipping off the handle during opening if the grasp was not sufficiently stable.
 - **X-Arm Specific:** Lack of impedance control on the X-Arm led to motor protection stops if the policy predicted motions that resulted in excessive force (partially mitigated by a soft gripper).
 - **Occlusion and Ambiguity:** Real-world objects being occluded by surroundings, and inherent ambiguity in articulation type from single-view point clouds (e.g., discerning a prismatic dishwasher from a revolute one).

Despite these limitations, ArticuBot demonstrated a remarkable level of generalization and robustness in real-world articulated object manipulation for a single, simulation-trained policy.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ArticuBot represents a significant advancement in the field of robot manipulation, particularly for complex and unstructured environments. Its key contributions and potential impact are multifaceted:

- **Pioneering Generalization in Articulated Object Manipulation:** ArticuBot is among the first to demonstrate robust zero-shot sim2real transfer for *diverse, unseen articulated objects* using a *single learned policy* and *standard parallel-jaw grippers*. This moves beyond previous works that relied on simplifying assumptions, object-specific policies, or extensive real-world fine-tuning. It fundamentally shifts the paradigm for this challenging task.
- **Validation of Large-Scale Simulation and Imitation Learning:** The work powerfully demonstrates that collecting massive datasets of expert demonstrations purely in simulation, combined with sophisticated imitation learning, can unlock high levels of generalization for complex manipulation skills. This approach significantly reduces the prohibitive cost and time associated with real-world data collection, offering a scalable path towards advanced robotics capabilities.
- **Novel Architectural Contributions:**
 - The **hierarchical policy representation** is a crucial innovation for achieving object-level generalization. By decoupling the "where to go" (high-level sub-goal) from the "how to move" (low-level delta actions), the system becomes more adaptable to varied object geometries and articulation types.
 - The **weighted displacement model** for the high-level policy is particularly impactful. By grounding predictions in the observed 3D scene structure and learning per-point weights, it improves robustness to visual variations and sensor noise, which is vital for effective sim2real transfer. The 4-point end-effector representation avoids the complexities of averaging rotations, leading to more stable predictions.
- **Bridging the Sim2Real Gap:** The comprehensive strategy for sim2real transfer, including the use of point cloud observations, end-effector space actions, and targeted point cloud augmentations in simulation, is a valuable blueprint for future work in robust real-world robot deployment. The successful cross-embodiment transfer (Franka to X-Arm) further underscores the generality of the learned policies.
- **Practical Applicability and Accessibility:** Demonstrating performance on commercial Franka arms and a mobile X-Arm in real kitchens and lounges shows the practical potential for deployment in everyday environments. This pushes robot manipulation closer to real-world utility in household and service robotics.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** ArticuBot provides a large, openly available simulation dataset (42.3k demonstrations on 322 objects) and a fast data generation pipeline. This is a valuable resource for the robotics community to build upon. Furthermore, the identified limitations (handling multi-modal outputs, user-specified doors, transparent/reflective objects, lack of force sensing in X-Arm, interaction history) clearly chart important directions for future research in generalist manipulation.
- **Implications for Robotic Foundation Models:** The paper implicitly argues for the value of "specialist" foundation models. While generalist models like OpenVLA aim for breadth, ArticuBot shows that deep, focused learning on a specific, complex task like articulated object manipulation can yield superior performance where generalist models might still fall short. This suggests a future where specialized, high-performance models could complement broader foundation models for specific demanding tasks.

In conclusion, ArticuBot makes a significant stride in universal articulated object manipulation by combining large-scale simulated data with innovative hierarchical policy learning. Its strong real-world generalization capabilities set a new benchmark and pave the way for more capable and robust robotic systems in diverse, unstructured environments.
