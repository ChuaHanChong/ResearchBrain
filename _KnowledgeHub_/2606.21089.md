# Repeated post-training is not Self-improving: Diagnosing Scientific Amnesia in Continual DPO Pipelines

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper defines scientific amnesia: a continual-DPO pipeline keeps updating checkpoints yet fails to accumulate reusable training knowledge for later campaigns. The authors ship a five-metric diagnostic suite, an FSDP checkpoint-chaining testbed over Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and a 30-campaign HumanEval benchmark, then diagnose five strategy proposers. Four of five, including the memory-augmented reasoner, degrade across a three-step chain.

## Problem

- Continual post-training research centers on catastrophic forgetting of task accuracy, leaving unmeasured whether the training system itself accumulates reusable methodological knowledge across successive campaigns.
- Meta-learning and warm-start Bayesian optimization transfer hyperparameters across related problems, but were never evaluated inside production-like chains where each checkpoint is both initialization and DPO reference policy.
- No prior diagnostic suite or reproducible multi-step real-LM testbed existed for this failure, so practitioner claims of self-improving repeated post-training went untested.

## Method

- Five amnesia diagnostics: ==repeated-failure rate==, ==regret slope==, ==transfer-decay matrix==, ==hyperparameter-rediscovery cost==, and ==curriculum sensitivity==, each asking whether earlier campaign evidence improves later training choices.
- **MAST** pipeline chains ==propose, generate_pairs, train, evaluate, memory_update== per campaign, passing ==FSDP-sharded trainer checkpoints== from step t as initialization and reference policy for step t+1.
- **MSCL** proposer: ==triple-scale memory== (short, mid, long) with ==surprise-driven replay== plus a reasoner LM trained by a ==Scientific DPO loss== over experimental outcomes with ==EWC regularization==.

## Results

- Homogeneous single-seed three-step chain: four of five proposers lose peak pass@4 (delta **-0.062 to -0.125**); only the conservative rule-based schedule gains **+0.125**.
- Heterogeneous chain crossing control, numeric and strings: MSCL alone improves (**+0.250**) while random-memory and rule-based both drop **-0.250**; two of five chains did not complete.
- Multi-seed sweep (**13 of 15** chains done): retrieval-only best mean delta **+0.167**, MSCL **-0.125**; on the synthetic five-seed pilot MSCL scored **0.862** pass@1 versus **0.772** random.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Improving a checkpoint is not learning how to improve; measure whether an update makes the next campaign easier to train.
> - Highest single-campaign peak does not imply better transfer: the most expressive reasoner started strongest and still declined across the chain.
> - Conservative fixed schedules and plain retrieval of prior recipes can match or beat adaptive proposers; rankings flip with regime, evaluator and seed count.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Industrial LLM teams often update a deployed model by running preference training again on a related campaign. A pipeline can keep producing updated checkpoints yet fail to carry forward useful lessons about how to train the next campaign. The authors call this system-level failure **scientific amnesia**, which is distinct from simply forgetting an earlier behavior. They track whether training recipes and lessons carry forward from one campaign to the next. In a small, single-seed chain, four of five strategy conditions ended with lower peaks than they started with. A fixed conservative rule-based schedule was the exception.

### 2. A chain that can learn, or repeat

The question is whether today's training makes tomorrow's campaign easier to train. To study this, the authors build a chain with four stages:

1. A strategy proposer chooses a training recipe.
1. The model is trained with DPO, a form of preference-based training.
1. The trained model is evaluated.
1. The resulting checkpoint is passed to the next campaign.

In the real-LM setup, the previous FSDP-sharded checkpoint initializes the next step and also serves as its DPO reference policy.

Five proposers choose recipes:

- **Random memory** samples a recipe.
- **Rule-based** applies a fixed conservative schedule.
- **Retrieval-only** reuses the best recipe from similar prior campaigns.
- **Warm-start BO** searches over observed strategies.
- **MSCL** uses short-, mid-, and long-term memories plus a small reasoner trained on experimental preferences.

None of these is treated as the paper's solution. Each is a candidate mechanism to be diagnosed.

The paper defines five ways to look for reusable methodological learning:

- repeating known failures
- accumulating regret
- losing transfer of good strategies
- rediscovering known-good hyperparameters
- sensitivity to campaign order

This shifts the diagnostic level. Zheng et al. argue that a drop in an old task's score may reflect a loss of task alignment rather than erased knowledge ([Zheng et al., *Spurious Forgetting in Continual Learning of Language Models*](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2501.13453)). This paper asks a different question: what does the *training system* learn?

The authors' diagnostic framework. It lists five diagnostics (Repeated failure, Regret slope, Transfer decay, HP rediscovery, Curriculum sensitivity) and the question each addresses. Not every row was empirically measured in the reported chains.

### 3. What the chain measured

The strongest evidence is a single-seed, three-step homogeneous chain on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct.

**The metric.** Table 3 reports peak per-batch pass@4/mean, an execution-based CodeGrader training-time signal. Each step's value is the maximum over batches during that step, and the reported change is step 2 minus step 0. This is a rolling training-time signal, not a held-out end-of-step evaluation. The authors discuss this as a limitation.

**The result.** Four of five conditions have lower step-2 than step-0 peaks. MSCL Reasoner starts highest at 0.688 and ends at 0.625 (−0.062). The exception is the fixed conservative rule-based schedule, which rises from 0.562 to 0.688 (+0.125). The authors interpret this as chains failing to convert earlier outcomes into better later training choices. The table establishes a pattern in this small, specific experiment. It does not establish a universal cause or an intervention winner.

**The follow-ups.** Two further experiments qualify the result.

- *Heterogeneous chain (control-easy → numeric-easy → strings-easy):* only three of five chains completed. Among the completed chains, MSCL is the only candidate with positive change, and the authors state this is not a statistical win claim.
- *Separate homogeneous multi-seed sweep:* 13 of 15 condition-seed chains completed. Retrieval-only has the best mean change, but there are at most three seeds per condition and the values are quantized. No candidate pair is statistically distinguishable.

The authors' summary of evidence strength. The initial 20 real-LM jobs completed, the homogeneous chain degrades in four of five conditions, and rankings vary by regime. Intervention superiority is not claimed because comparisons are small-n, partly incomplete, and not statistically distinguishable.

### 4. The next campaign is the test

The headline is diagnostic. The pipeline makes scientific amnesia measurable and shows it in a production-like chain. The authors recommend that industrial post-training teams track chain-level transfer, rediscovery, regret, and curriculum sensitivity. That means asking whether a campaign makes the next one easier to train, not only whether it improves the current checkpoint. Intervention rankings depend on regime, and the evidence does not establish that MSCL, or any candidate, solves the problem.
