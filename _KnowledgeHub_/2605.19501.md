# CANINE: Coaching Visually Impaired Users for Interactive Navigation with a Robot Guide Dog

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the National University of Singapore developed CANINE, an automated coaching system that uses foundation models to provide personalized, adaptive verbal feedback to visually impaired users, improving their learning efficiency and navigation performance with a robot guide dog. The system decomposes complex interactive navigation tasks into manageable sub-skills and tracks user proficiency to deliver targeted training.

## Problem

- Visually impaired individuals face challenges in independent navigation, with existing robot guide dogs requiring complex human-robot coordination that is difficult to master.
- Coaching for physical human-robot interaction is difficult due to the multi-phase, heterogeneous nature of navigation tasks and significant variability across users.
- Visually impaired users' lack of visual feedback prevents self-correction, necessitating a system that can infer latent error causes from noisy interaction signals and provide meaningful, in-situ guidance.

## Method

- A **two-level coaching system** is introduced: "inter-skill" coaching for dynamic curriculum sequencing and "intra-skill" coaching for adaptive feedback generation.
- Inter-skill coaching employs a ==Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP)== and ==Bayesian knowledge tracing== to model learner proficiency and dynamically schedule practice on the user's weakest sub-skills.
- Intra-skill coaching uses a ==four-stage pipeline== leveraging Vision-Language Models (VLMs) and Large Language Models (LLMs) to extract states from visual observations, summarize episodes, diagnose error causes, and generate personalized verbal feedback and robot behavior adjustments.

## Results

- A simulated learner model showed CANINE's adaptive curriculum achieved a **93.6%** success rate for 15 sub-skills, outperforming uniform (**90.2%**) and greedy (**66.0%**) baselines.
- In a controlled human study, CANINE users significantly improved task completion times for complex sub-skills like "Open Door" (**8.64s** vs. **4.53s** improvement in baseline) and "Enter Room" (**6.96s** vs. **2.27s** improvement in baseline).
- Participants rated CANINE's coaching system as significantly more useful (mean **13.10** vs. **10.80** for baseline) and demonstrated skill retention over a two-week period, with some skills showing further improvement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An adaptive curriculum, which prioritizes a user's weakest sub-skills, leads to faster learning and higher mastery rates in complex human-robot interaction tasks compared to uniform or simple greedy approaches.
> - Foundation models (VLMs and LLMs) can effectively serve as reasoning tools to diagnose human coordination errors from raw visual interaction data and generate actionable, personalized coaching for physical tasks.
> - Coaching systems for visually impaired users require multimodal feedback (e.g., haptic cues) and user control over the coaching process to manage cognitive load, highlighting critical differences from sighted proxy studies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated at the intersection of assistive robotics, human-robot interaction (HRI), and intelligent tutoring systems (ITS), specifically focusing on enhancing the independent mobility of visually impaired (VI) individuals through robotic assistance.

In the domain of **assistive robotics**, significant advancements have been made in developing robotic navigation systems for visually impaired users. While these systems demonstrate technical feasibility in controlled environments, they typically lack structured learning support, requiring users to acquire complex coordination skills through trial and error. This paper addresses this gap by integrating a systematic coaching framework.

Within **physical Human-Robot Interaction**, research explores paradigms such as assistance, collaboration, and teaching. Prior work in robotic teaching has focused on curriculum design and feedback generation to improve human capabilities. However, these often do not specifically address the unique challenges of learners lacking critical perceptual channels (e.g., vision) who cannot visually self-correct. CANINE contributes by combining proficiency-based curriculum scheduling with diagnostic verbal feedback tailored for such users.

Regarding **Intelligent Tutoring Systems**, traditional ITS primarily target cognitive skills (e.g., mathematics, language). More recent developments have explored large language model (LLM)-based coaching for physical activities, such as GPTCoach, which provides feedback based on self-reported performance and sensor data. CANINE extends this by diagnosing coordination errors from raw visual observations of human-robot interaction and generating actionable corrections for spatial and temporal coordination failures that users cannot directly perceive.

Finally, the work leverages **foundation models for robotics**, including LLMs and Vision-Language Models (VLMs). Most applications of these models in robotics have focused on enabling robots to execute tasks more effectively through language instruction or scene understanding. CANINE inverts this paradigm by employing VLMs and LLMs as reasoning tools specifically to coach *humans* in interacting with robots.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges faced by 2.2 billion visually impaired individuals worldwide in navigating unfamiliar environments, often due to limited access to consistent human assistance. Robot guide dogs offer a promising solution for independent mobility, but their effective utilization necessitates precise human-robot coordination that is difficult for users to acquire.

The research identifies two fundamental challenges in coaching humans to master these complex physical interaction tasks:
1. **Task Complexity and Heterogeneity:** Interactive navigation tasks are composed of distinct phases, each requiring different coordination strategies (e.g., locating a door handle versus passing through a door). Treating the task as a monolithic process is inefficient due to the heterogeneity of these phases and significant variability across users. Users may understand high-level goals but struggle with physical execution, leading to diverse error patterns.
2. **Perceptual Limitations of Visually Impaired Users:** The absence of visual feedback prevents users from observing their environment to self-correct errors. This necessitates a coaching system that can infer latent user states (e.g., distinguishing between spatial misalignment and misinterpretation of robot cues) solely from noisy interaction signals and provide meaningful, in-situ guidance. Traditional methods rely on human Orientation and Mobility (O&M) specialists, which are effective but unscalable and limit opportunities for repetitive skill refinement.

To address these challenges, the core objectives of CANINE are:
- **To develop an automated coaching system** that trains visually impaired users for interactive navigation with robot guide dogs.
- **To provide personalized and adaptive verbal feedback** to users.
- **To decompose complex coordination tasks into manageable sub-skills** to enable targeted training.
- **To dynamically schedule the training curriculum** by tracking learner proficiency across sub-skills using knowledge tracing and prioritizing training on weaker areas (inter-skill coaching).
- **To generate targeted, adaptive verbal corrections** by observing human practice episodes, inferring underlying causes of errors using foundation models, and adapting robot behavior accordingly (intra-skill coaching).
- **To improve both learning efficiency and final navigation performance** compared to generic verbal instructions.
- **To investigate the retention of learned skills** over time.
- **To explore the system's feasibility and design considerations** with a visually impaired user and its extensibility to other interactive robotic tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

CANINE operates as a two-level coaching system designed to facilitate visually impaired users' learning of coordination with robot guide dogs. The overall navigation task (e.g., doorway navigation) is decomposed into discrete sub-skills (e.g., Navigate to Door, Open Door, Enter Room) that can be practiced independently. User behavior is observed via a chest-mounted camera providing a first-person view. Feedback is delivered verbally through a speaker after each practice episode.

**A. Inter-skill Coaching: Curriculum Sequencing**
This layer addresses the "what to train" problem, formulating it as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP).
- **Sub-skill Decomposition:** For doorway navigation, the task is broken into three sequential sub-skills. The robot can be configured to start from an appropriate initial state for each sub-skill.
- **Knowledge Tracing:** The system models the learner's latent proficiency for each sub-skill using a Gaussian distribution, extending Bayesian knowledge tracing. Performance measurements (e.g., completion time) from practice episodes are incorporated via a Bayesian update, and the posterior mean is mapped to a normalized proficiency score between 0 and 1. Initial proficiency beliefs are personalized using a participant's initial trials, and hyperparameters are calibrated from pilot study data.
- **Curriculum Policy:** At each step, CANINE employs a greedy policy, selecting the sub-skill with the lowest estimated proficiency for the next practice session. This strategy aims to maximize learning gain by focusing on the learner's weakest areas, and the curriculum dynamically adapts as proficiency estimates evolve.

**B. Intra-skill Coaching: Feedback Generation**
This layer addresses the "how to train" problem within a selected sub-skill, also formulated as a POMDP. It processes raw visual observations into structured state histories, summarizes the episode, diagnoses error causes, and generates verbal feedback and robot parameter adjustments. This occurs in a structured four-stage process:
1. **Frame-level State Extraction (f_frame):** A Vision-Language Model (VLM) processes uniformly sampled key frames (e.g., every 0.5 seconds) from the chest-mounted camera video (o_1:T). It extracts a symbolic representation (h_t) encoding the user's physical state, robot state, environment state, task progress, and safety flags. This transforms raw visual data into a structured format for subsequent reasoning.
2. **Timeline Summarization (f_time):** A Large Language Model (LLM) takes the sequence of frame-level states (h_1:T) and generates a compact episode summary (τ). This summary includes episode statistics (duration, outcome), performance metrics (efficiency, safety scores), and a discrete event sequence with timestamps. This step makes behavioral patterns explicit.
3. **Feedback Generation (f_coach):** An LLM receives the episode summary (τ) and any optional user verbal feedback (transcribed). It infers the likely latent cognitive state (e.g., spatial misalignment, cue misinterpretation) as the cause of errors. Based on this diagnosis, it generates a coaching action (c) comprising an error diagnosis and an actionable verbal instruction tailored to the inferred error cause.
4. **Robot Adaptation (f_param):** An LLM, informed by the coaching action (c) and the user's proficiency belief, determines and generates robot behavior adjustments (u). These adjustments implement scaffolding by modulating task difficulty; for instance, if spatial misalignment is diagnosed, the robot's stopping position might be adjusted closer to the door.

To ensure robustness and reduce hallucinations, each stage is prompted to emit a constrained JSON object matching a predefined schema, with outputs parsed and validated. GPT-5.1 is used zero-shot with fixed prompts for all stages. Feedback is delivered post-episode to avoid interfering with safety-critical maneuvers.

**C. Hardware Platform**
The system uses a Unitree Go2 quadruped robot equipped with a Jetson AGX Orin for onboard computation and a Hesai JT16 LiDAR for environmental sensing. Users interact via a rigid leash. A DJI Action 6 camera mounted on the user's chest provides the first-person view, and a microphone records user feedback, while instructions are delivered through a speaker.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research addressed four main questions through a multi-stage evaluation.

**A. Study 1: Feasibility Evaluation (RQ1)**
This study evaluated the technical feasibility of CANINE's components.
- **Simulated User Evaluation:** An adaptive curriculum sequencing strategy (CANINE's approach) was compared against Uniform and Greedy baselines using a simulated learner model. The adaptive curriculum achieved faster learning, requiring fewer teaching actions to reach mastery, and demonstrated higher success rates across varying numbers of sub-skills (e.g., 93.6% success for 15 sub-skills compared to 66.0% for Greedy and 90.2% for Uniform). The performance advantage of the adaptive curriculum increased with task complexity.
- **Frame Description Quality:** Human judges, evaluating 100 frames, showed a preference for GPT-5.1 generated VLM descriptions over other models, indicating its suitability for accurate state extraction.
- **VLM Pipeline Comparison:** Comparing CANINE's decomposed pipeline against direct VLM prompting and structured full-video VLM approaches on 10 navigation videos, CANINE's pipeline was preferred in 51.2% of comparisons. It also achieved a lower average latency of 20.62 seconds, compared to 44.36 seconds and 42.15 seconds for the baseline VLM approaches. This indicated that the staged representation preserved sufficient context while reducing generation time.

**B. Study 2: Human Subject Evaluation (RQ2)**
A controlled study with 20 blindfolded sighted participants evaluated CANINE against a baseline providing generic verbal instructions.
- **Objective Performance:** CANINE significantly improved learning rates and final navigation performance for interaction-intensive sub-skills. For the "Open Door" sub-skill, the CANINE group showed significantly greater improvement (mean improvement of 8.64s vs. 4.53s in baseline, p = .012) and faster final completion times (11.07s vs. 14.54s, p = .045). Similarly, for "Enter Room," the CANINE group achieved greater improvement (mean improvement of 6.96s vs. 2.27s, p = .019) and faster completion times (11.36s vs. 14.42s, p = .006). No significant difference was observed for the simpler "Navigate to Door" sub-skill.
- **Subjective Experience:** Participants rated CANINE as having significantly higher Perceived Usefulness (M = 13.10 vs. M = 10.80 for baseline, p = .036). General usability (SUS) and workload (NASA-TLX) metrics did not show significant differences. Participants identified dedicated sub-skill training, personalized feedback, and adaptive curriculum as the most helpful features.
- **Human Expert Comparison (Post-hoc):** A post-hoc comparison with the CANINE group showed that 70% of participants preferred a human expert, primarily citing the ability for physical corrections and concurrent, real-time feedback. Conversely, 20% preferred the robot coach due to its consistent feedback and perceived autonomy/privacy.

**C. Study 3: Retention Analysis (RQ3)**
A retention study with N=5 participants from the CANINE group, conducted two weeks after their initial training, provided preliminary evidence of skill persistence.
- **Results:** Skills learned with coaching largely persisted over two weeks. The "Open Door" task showed substantial improvement, which the authors hypothesize may be due to an incubation effect for complex motor skills. Simpler skills like "Enter Room" and "Navigate to Door" remained relatively stable or showed minor performance degradation. All participants demonstrated confident navigation.

**D. Study 4: Case Study with Visually Impaired User (RQ4)**
An exploratory case study with a totally blind participant (Male, 70, no residual vision) evaluated CANINE's usability and extensibility.
- **Convergence with Study 2 Findings:** The participant showed clear learning progression, improving "Open Door" completion time from 22.4s to 9.1s, consistent with Study 2. Preferences for sub-skill practice, personalized feedback, and adaptive curriculum also aligned with the controlled study.
- **VI-Specific Design Needs:** The study highlighted critical distinctions from blindfolded proxies, specifically the need for multimodal grounding (haptic cues to supplement verbal feedback) and greater user control over the coaching system (e.g., ability to pause, replay, skip feedback) to manage cognitive load.
- **Extensibility to Robotic Handover:** CANINE was applied to a robotic handover task. This demonstrated that the coaching pipeline could be instantiated for a different interaction task. It also revealed task-dependent coaching needs: concurrent (real-time) feedback was required for hand positioning during handover, whereas terminal feedback was sufficient for navigation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

CANINE represents an advancement in the field of assistive robotics and human-robot interaction by providing an automated and personalized coaching system for visually impaired users.
The system's **primary significance** lies in addressing the critical gap in existing robot guide dog systems that lack structured learning support. By offering personalized, adaptive verbal feedback and decomposing complex tasks into sub-skills, CANINE demonstrates the potential to significantly improve both the learning efficiency and the final navigation performance of visually impaired individuals. This could expand independent mobility and enhance the safe and effective utilization of robotic assistance.

The application of **foundation models (VLMs and LLMs) as reasoning tools for coaching humans** rather than solely for robot control is a notable contribution, showcasing a new paradigm for intelligent tutoring in embodied HRI. The decomposed, four-stage pipeline for intra-skill coaching provides a structured and interpretable approach to error diagnosis and feedback generation.

The **multi-stage evaluation**, including simulated users, controlled human studies with blindfolded participants, a retention study, and an exploratory case study with a visually impaired individual, provides a comprehensive assessment. The quantitative evidence from the controlled study on performance improvement and perceived usefulness, combined with the longitudinal data on skill retention, supports the efficacy of the proposed methods. Insights from the visually impaired participant underscore the importance of multimodal feedback (e.g., haptic cues) and user control in real-world deployment, offering valuable design considerations for future systems. The demonstration of CANINE's extensibility to a robotic handover task suggests its architectural generalizability across different interactive assistance domains, while also highlighting the need for task-dependent adaptation of feedback timing.

**Limitations** of the current work include the reliance on terminal feedback, which cannot provide in-the-moment corrections, potential issues with chest-mounted camera occlusion, and the preliminary nature of the visually impaired case study and retention study. Additionally, the system currently relies on expert-defined skill decomposition and requires human oversight for physical safety and task resetting.

**Future work** is directed towards longitudinal deployment with the visually impaired community to validate real-world efficacy, developing adaptive feedback mechanisms that can transition between terminal and concurrent guidance, exploring multi-camera sensing or explicit body-orientation guidance, automating skill discovery to reduce reliance on expert-defined sub-skills, and extending CANINE to more complex interactive navigation scenarios. Overall, CANINE offers a foundational step towards scalable, personalized, and effective automated coaching for human-robot collaboration in assistive contexts.
