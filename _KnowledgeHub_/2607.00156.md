# Dual-Informed Vertical Expansion for Multi-Objective Node Selection in Anytime Conflict-Based Search

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from MIT developed Dual-Informed Vertical Expansion (DIVE), a new high-level node selection policy for Conflict-Based Search (CBS) in Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF). This policy allows exact CBS solvers to operate in an "anytime" mode, providing early, high-quality feasible solutions with certified optimality gaps. DIVE reduces dive breaks by 6-51% and maximum queue size by 44-86% compared to standard Best-First Search, while maintaining robust performance on complex benchmarks.

## Problem

- Standard Conflict-Based Search (CBS) uses Best-First Search (BFS) for node selection, which, despite being node-efficient for optimality proof, suffers from high memory consumption and frequent interruptions of parent-child expansion sequences.
- Traditional exact CBS methods, including BFS and Iterative Deepening CBS (IDCBS), typically do not provide feasible solutions until global optimality is certified, limiting their utility in time-constrained robotic applications requiring "anytime" capabilities.
- There is a need to explore multi-objective trade-offs in exact CBS, balancing solution quality, memory use, and the ability to find early feasible solutions, rather than solely focusing on minimizing expanded nodes.

## Method

- Introduces **Dual-Informed Vertical Expansion (DIVE)**, a high-level node selection policy that combines ==best-bound reanchoring== with depth-oriented exploration within "dives" to balance multiple search objectives.
- **DIVE** initiates a dive from the globally best-bound node and preferentially expands the most promising child within that dive, using an ==incumbent cutoff== to prune unproductive branches in the primal zone.
- The framework formally models ==Conflict-Based Search (CBS)== as a ==Branch and Bound== algorithm, proving that **DIVE** maintains exactness, and proposes a memory-constrained variant (**MC-DIVE**) to dynamically adapt to memory limits.

## Results

- DIVE reduces dive breaks by **6-51%** and maximum queue size by **44-86%** compared to BFS, while increasing expanded nodes by **18-57%** on average across standard benchmarks, demonstrating its distinct performance profile.
- DIVE provides early feasible incumbent solutions, often within the initial dive, enabling "anytime" exact CBS with certified primal-dual gaps typically ranging from **0.0-0.6%** by the time BFS would complete.
- Warm starts, such as from PIBT, significantly improve DIVE's performance on dense instances, allowing it to solve **35 out of 40** instances in a challenging stress test where it otherwise struggles, matching BFS's solution count.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-level node selection in CBS can be treated as a modular, tunable policy, analogous to Branch and Bound algorithms, enabling tailored trade-off profiles for different operational contexts.
> - DIVE establishes a unique Pareto point in the multi-objective design space for CBS, offering a balance between node efficiency, reduced memory footprint, and improved parent-child continuity (fewer dive breaks).
> - The combination of incumbent cutoffs and diversified best-bound restarts is crucial for DIVE's robustness, preventing the search from becoming trapped in unproductive deep branches while consistently progressing toward optimality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) is a core problem in robotics, involving the coordination of multiple agents to navigate a shared environment while avoiding collisions. It finds applications in diverse domains such as warehouse automation and airport surface operations. Optimal MAPF is a computationally challenging problem, and Conflict-Based Search (CBS) is a prominent exact algorithm used to solve it.

Standard CBS employs a high-level search over a Constraint Tree (CT), where nodes represent subproblems with agent-specific constraints. The conventional approach for selecting the next node to expand in this high-level search is a best-first search (BFS) strategy, prioritizing nodes with the minimum lower-bound key. While this BFS approach is effective for minimizing the total number of expanded nodes and for certifying optimality, it presents several operational limitations. These limitations include maintaining a large open frontier, frequent interruptions of parent-child expansion sequences (which can hinder efficiency improvements based on locality), and typically providing no feasible solution until the algorithm has completed its search and certified optimality.

Existing advancements in exact CBS, such as improved heuristics or conflict selection strategies, generally refine the BFS paradigm rather than altering its fundamental trade-off profile. Iterative Deepening CBS (IDCBS) addresses the memory consumption issue of BFS by employing repeated depth-first passes under increasing cost thresholds. While IDCBS significantly reduces explicit queue memory, it incurs costs in terms of re-expanding nodes across passes and also typically does not yield intermediate feasible solutions. Suboptimal CBS variants exist that prioritize speed or bounded suboptimality, sometimes using greedy or learning-based node selection, but these approaches often sacrifice the guarantee of optimality.

This work positions high-level node selection as a modular design choice within the CBS framework, drawing parallels to the established practice in Branch and Bound (B&B) algorithms, such as Mixed Integer Programming (MIP) solvers. In B&B, node selection is recognized as a key dimension for balancing objectives like solution quality, memory use, and proof time. By treating node selection as a tunable policy, the paper aims to explore the multi-objective trade-offs inherent in exact CBS, seeking a previously unaddressed operating point that offers advantages in terms of parent-child continuity, memory efficiency, and the provision of early feasible solutions, while maintaining optimality guarantees.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to formally analyze and demonstrate the impact of high-level node selection as a modular policy in exact Conflict-Based Search (CBS), rather than treating it as an inherent, fixed part of the algorithm. This perspective allows for the exploration of diverse behavioral profiles and trade-offs beyond the traditional focus on minimizing expanded nodes.

The motivation behind this work stems from several limitations observed in standard CBS implementations and the need to adapt exact solvers for practical robotic deployment contexts:

1. **Addressing Limitations of Best-First Search (BFS):** The standard best-first node selection in CBS, while node-efficient and effective for dual-bound progress, exhibits significant drawbacks. It can maintain a large open frontier, leading to high memory consumption. It also frequently interrupts parent-child expansion sequences, potentially reducing the efficiency gains from data reuse between closely related nodes. Crucially, BFS typically does not provide any feasible, conflict-free solution until the entire optimality certificate is closed, limiting its utility in time-constrained scenarios where an intermediate, high-quality solution might be required.

2. **Improving upon Iterative Deepening CBS (IDCBS):** IDCBS offers a solution to the memory problem by performing repeated depth-first passes. However, this comes at the cost of re-expanding nodes across passes, increasing the total work performed. Similar to BFS, IDCBS generally does not yield intermediate feasible solutions, meaning its "anytime" capabilities are limited to providing a solution only upon full completion of a pass, which may still be far from optimal or not guaranteed to be produced early.

3. **Recognizing Multi-Objective Trade-offs:** The authors argue that the quality of a node-selection policy should be evaluated across multiple competing objectives rather than solely by the number of expanded nodes. These objectives include:
 - **Expanded Nodes:** A measure of the total high-level search effort.
 - **Dual-Bound Progress:** How quickly the optimality certificate is advanced.
 - **Queue Size:** The memory required to store open nodes.
 - **Dive Breaks / Parent-Child Continuity:** The frequency with which the search switches away from a parent-child sequence, impacting the potential for locality-based optimizations.
 - **Incumbent Discovery:** The ability to find feasible solutions before global optimality is certified.
 These objectives often trade off against each other, implying that different policies might form a Pareto front of optimal behaviors for different contexts.

4. **Enabling Anytime Capabilities for Exact CBS:** Many real-world robotic applications, such as real-time replanning, require a feasible plan within a bounded time window, even if it is not yet proven optimal. The ability to provide early, high-quality incumbents with certified gaps (a measure of remaining possible improvement) would transform exact CBS into a more robust "anytime" algorithm.

5. **Leveraging Branch and Bound Principles:** Drawing inspiration from general Branch and Bound (B&B) literature, where node selection is a dynamic and context-dependent decision, the authors seek to bring similar flexibility to CBS. This includes exploring strategies like best-bound search with "plunging" into deeper branches, which are common in solvers for Mixed Integer Programming (MIP).

By addressing these motivations, the paper aims to introduce a new node-selection policy, Dual-Informed Vertical Expansion (DIVE), that occupies a "missing middle ground" in the multi-objective design space. DIVE is designed to preserve the strengths of best-bound reanchoring while integrating depth-oriented exploration for earlier incumbent discovery and improved parent-child locality, making exact CBS more adaptable to various deployment requirements.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research develops a systematic framework for analyzing high-level node-selection policies in CBS, abstracting CBS as a Branch and Bound (B&B) algorithm. This abstraction formally separates the correctness of the solver from the specific node traversal order.

**Formalization and Correctness:**
The paper models CBS as a generic B&B algorithm (Algorithm 3), where each node represents a relaxed subproblem. A node key `ℓ(P)` provides a lower bound, and branching refines the problem. The core argument for correctness (Proposition 2) states that any policy selecting a node from the `OPEN` set preserves exactness, provided standard B&B conditions (monotone lower bounds, proper branching to cover all feasible solutions, valid incumbent pruning) are maintained and the induced search tree is finite. This proof confirms that DIVE, which only modifies node selection and uses a valid incumbent cutoff (Proposition 1), is an exact algorithm (Proposition 4). This also highlights that warm starts, which only improve the initial primal bound, maintain exactness (Proposition 5).

**Search Tree Framework:**
A conceptual search tree `T` is defined, induced by the MAPF instance and a deterministic branching policy. Nodes in this tree are characterized by their children `C(n)`, a lower-bound key `ℓ(n)`, and a conflict-free indicator `f(n)`. The paper defines three zones based on the optimal objective value `z*`:
- **Dual Zone (`N_D`):** Nodes `n` where `ℓ(n) < z*`. These must be explored to prove optimality.
- **Optimal Zone (`N_O`):** Nodes `n` where `ℓ(n) = z*`. At least one feasible node from this zone must be found.
- **Primal Zone (`N_P`):** Nodes `n` where `ℓ(n) > z*`. These are not required for optimality proof but can provide feasible solutions.
An exact solve requires finding an optimal feasible node and resolving all dual-zone nodes.

**Node-Selection Objectives:**
Five route-dependent quantities are defined to evaluate policies:
1. **Expanded Nodes (`|E_T|`):** Total processed high-level nodes, a measure of search effort.
2. **Dive Breaks (`B_T`):** Number of times the search jumps away from a parent-child sequence, indicating loss of parent-child locality.
3. **Maximum Queue Size (`Q_max`):** Peak memory required for the `OPEN` set.
4. **Primal-Bound Progress:** Rate of discovering and improving feasible solutions (`z_P_t`).
5. **Dual-Bound Progress:** Rate of advancing the optimality certificate (`z_D_t`), often expressed via the primal-dual `Gap_t`.

**DIVE Policy Definition (Algorithm 2):**
Dual-Informed Vertical Expansion (DIVE) is designed to operate best-bound between dives and depth-oriented within a dive.
- A "dive" is a maximal contiguous subsequence of node expansions where each subsequent node is a child of the previous one.
- DIVE starts a new dive from the globally best-bound node in `OPEN` (selected using `π_BFS`).
- Within a dive, DIVE preferentially expands the most promising child (smallest `ℓ(c)`) of the currently processed node.
- A dive terminates if the current node is conflict-free, no feasible children exist, or if the best child's lower bound `ℓ(c)` is not strictly better than the current primal bound `z_P`. This `z_P` acts as an **incumbent cutoff** (Proposition 1), pruning unproductive excursions into the primal zone.
- This strategy of periodically returning to the best-bound frontier is termed **diversified search**.

**Theoretical Comparison:**
The framework is used to compare BFS, DFS (Depth-First Search), ID (Iterative Deepening), and DIVE, particularly on a stylized "perfect search tree" model:
- **BFS:** Proven to be optimal for expanded-node count (Proposition 6) and frontier-bound progress (Proposition 7) when the optimal zone is a singleton. However, it incurs high `Q_max` (Proposition 8) and many `B_T` (Proposition 9).
- **DFS:** Achieves minimal `Q_max` (Proposition 11) and `B_T` (Proposition 10), but is not guaranteed to be exact without modifications and can explore the primal zone extensively.
- **ID:** Reduces `Q_max` to `d+1` (Proposition 12) like DFS, but its repeated passes lead to high `B_T` (Proposition 13) and repeated work in `N_D`.
- **DIVE:** Proven to minimize `B_T` for the critical subtree `N_D U N_O` (Proposition 15) and for the processed subtree (Proposition 14). It has intermediate `Q_max` (Proposition 16, typically `2^(d-1)+1` on a perfect tree), positioning it as a distinct Pareto point. DIVE's bound progression is better than plain DFS due to best-bound reanchoring (Fig. 3).

**Responsive Node Selection (MC-DIVE):**
The concept of responsive node selection is introduced, where `π` adapts based on real-time solver state. Memory-Constrained DIVE (MC-DIVE, Equation 10) serves as an example, switching from DIVE to DFS behavior if the `OPEN` queue exceeds a soft memory threshold. This demonstrates dynamic trade-off adjustment.

**Experimental Protocol:**
- All policies (BFS, ID, DIVE) are implemented in a shared CBS code base, ensuring consistent low-level search, conflict selection, heuristics, and pruning logic.
- Standard MAPF benchmarks (empty, random, room, warehouse maps) are used, along with a dense `test-5-5` stress test.
- Instances are filtered to exclude trivial cases (<100 nodes) and truncated after a 5-minute wall-clock time limit.
- Metrics focus on expanded nodes, dive breaks, maximum queue size, and primal/dual bound progress, along with incumbent quality.
- Ablation studies investigate the individual contributions of incumbent cutoff and diversified search. Warm starts (e.g., from PIBT) are also tested to assess their impact on DIVE's performance in dense regimes.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation on standard MAPF benchmarks supports the theoretical predictions regarding the multi-objective trade-offs inherent in node selection.

**Complete-Solve Structural Trade-Offs:**
- **BFS:** Consistently minimizes the number of expanded nodes, validating its theoretical node-efficiency. However, it incurs the largest maximum queue size and a high number of dive breaks.
- **IDCBS:** Achieves the smallest explicit queue size, demonstrating 89-97% reductions compared to BFS. This memory efficiency, however, comes at the cost of significantly more expanded nodes (13-134% more than BFS) and a higher number of dive breaks than both BFS and DIVE in many cases, reflecting repeated work across passes.
- **DIVE:** Occupies a distinct Pareto point. It consistently reduces dive breaks (6-51% fewer than BFS), indicating improved parent-child continuity. DIVE also substantially reduces maximum queue size (44-86% less than BFS) while keeping node overhead controlled (18-57% more nodes than BFS on average for these benchmarks, though it can match BFS on symmetric maps). This positions DIVE as a middle-ground policy, sacrificing some node efficiency for better dive continuity and memory.

**Zone Accounting:**
- Analysis of node expansions across dual, optimal, and primal zones reveals distinct behaviors. BFS processes the dual zone once and minimizes work in other zones. IDCBS also avoids the primal zone but shows repeated work in the dual zone due to its restart mechanism. DIVE processes the dual zone once, similar to BFS, and its additional node expansions are concentrated in the optimal and primal zones. This indicates that DIVE's overhead comes from controlled exploration for incumbents rather than redundant work in the dual zone or uncontrolled search.

**Incumbents and Anytime Behavior:**
- DIVE frequently finds its first feasible incumbent solution early in the solve, often within the initial dive. This enables an "anytime" mode for exact CBS.
- For complete benchmark instances, DIVE's first incumbents are typically found after tens to hundreds of high-level nodes, providing a certified primal-dual gap (ranging from 0.2% to 1.5% for complete benchmark densities).
- At the point where BFS completes an instance, DIVE has already found a feasible incumbent in 100% of cases, with very low average certified gaps (0.0-0.6%) and an optimal solution found in 47-100% of instances, depending on the map. This implies that DIVE often identifies the optimal solution well before the complete optimality certificate is closed.

**Primal-Dual Bound Progression:**
- BFS exhibits the most direct dual-bound progress, as expected from its node selection strategy.
- IDCBS initially tracks BFS but slows down as repeated restarts accumulate.
- DIVE maintains a dual bound close to BFS, as its best-bound reanchoring mechanism prevents prolonged excursions from the certificate frontier. Simultaneously, DIVE's primal bound progresses, showing its ability to improve feasible solutions while also advancing the optimality certificate.

**Warm Starts:**
- Warm starts, such as from PIBT, are beneficial for DIVE, particularly in dense problem instances. In a dense `test-5-5` stress test (33% occupancy), DIVE without a warm start failed most instances, but with a PIBT warm start, it solved 35 out of 40 instances, matching BFS. This suggests warm starts are a valuable safeguard, activating incumbent pruning earlier and improving DIVE's scalability in challenging scenarios, while still preserving its characteristic trade-off profile.

**Ablation Study: Diversified Search:**
- An ablation comparing DIVE with an incumbent-pruned depth-first search (DFS) that lacks diversified best-bound restarts demonstrates the necessity of diversified search. The ablated DFS policy, despite using incumbent pruning, required substantially more expanded nodes and dive breaks (126-157% more nodes, 187-244% more dive breaks than DIVE) and significantly more work in the primal and optimal zones. This confirms that diversified reanchoring is crucial for DIVE's robustness, preventing the search from becoming trapped in unproductive deep branches.

**Responsive Enhancements (MC-DIVE):**
- Memory-Constrained DIVE (MC-DIVE), an example of a responsive policy, successfully controls the maximum queue size. When the queue exceeds a soft threshold, MC-DIVE switches to depth-first behavior, demonstrating its ability to adapt to memory constraints. This reduction in queue size comes at the cost of some increase in expanded nodes and dive breaks compared to DIVE, but it remains a less conservative approach than always using DFS.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant re-evaluation of high-level node selection in Conflict-Based Search (CBS), moving it from a fixed implementation detail to a modular and strategically tunable policy. This shift in perspective has several implications for the design and deployment of exact Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) solvers.

**Paradigm Shift in Exact CBS Design:** The work formalizes node selection as a distinct design choice, provably separate from the core correctness of the CBS algorithm. This opens avenues for more sophisticated, context-aware, and multi-objective solver designs, aligning CBS with advanced Branch and Bound methodologies prevalent in other optimization fields like Mixed Integer Programming.

**Enhancing Practical Applicability:** DIVE's most significant contribution is its ability to transform exact CBS into an "anytime" algorithm. By consistently providing early, high-quality feasible solutions alongside a certified primal-dual gap, DIVE addresses a critical need in real-time robotic systems. In scenarios requiring rapid replanning or operation under strict time limits, a feasible plan (even if not yet proven optimal) is often more valuable than waiting for a full optimality certificate. The certified gap provides a quantifiable measure of the potential for further improvement, allowing decision-makers to assess the quality of the incumbent.

**Improved Resource Management:** DIVE offers a novel balance of computational resources. It significantly improves parent-child continuity by reducing dive breaks, which can lead to efficiency gains in implementations that exploit data locality. Furthermore, DIVE manages memory more effectively than standard best-first search, providing a substantial reduction in queue size without resorting to the repeated work characteristic of iterative deepening approaches. This makes exact CBS more viable for deployment on platforms with limited memory resources.

**Robustness through Diversified Search and Warm Starts:** The research demonstrates that the combination of incumbent cutoffs and diversified best-bound restarts is crucial for DIVE's robust performance, preventing unproductive deep dives while maintaining progress toward optimality. The natural integration with warm starts further enhances DIVE's ability to tackle dense and challenging instances, where an initial feasible solution can dramatically accelerate the search by pruning large portions of the primal zone.

**Foundation for Future Research:** This work lays a foundation for several future directions:
- **Adaptive and Hybrid Policies:** The concept of responsive node selection, exemplified by MC-DIVE, suggests designing more dynamic policies that can continuously adjust their behavior (e.g., between best-bound, depth-oriented, and DIVE-like modes) based on real-time solver statistics like queue growth, incumbent quality, and dual-bound progress.
- **Learning-Based Node Selection:** The modular nature of node selection makes it amenable to machine learning approaches. Policies could be trained to predict the most efficient traversal order given an instance or solver state, leveraging techniques from reinforcement learning or supervised learning.
- **Advanced Analysis and Integration:** Extending the theoretical analysis beyond stylized models to stochastic search tree models or empirical studies of real CBS trees could provide deeper insights. Combining DIVE with state-of-the-art CBS enhancements (e.g., advanced heuristics, conflict prioritization) could yield even more powerful and versatile exact MAPF solvers.

In conclusion, this paper shifts the understanding of CBS node selection from a monolithic component to a flexible policy that can be tailored to specific operational requirements. DIVE serves as a concrete example of this new paradigm, offering a valuable trade-off profile that enhances the practical utility and versatility of exact MAPF algorithms in robotics and beyond.
