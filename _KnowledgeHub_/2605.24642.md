# Understanding the Impact of Geometric Foundation Models on Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> This study quantitatively assessed the geometric understanding of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, finding them deficient in dense depth and surface normal estimation compared to Geometric Foundation Models (GFMs). Researchers systematically analyzed three GFM integration strategies within VLAs and demonstrated that a "mid-training" phase with larger datasets and GFM integration significantly improved manipulation performance, particularly in single-camera settings.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, while effective for semantic understanding and task generalization, often lack precise spatial and geometric reasoning crucial for robotic manipulation.
- A quantitative understanding of the 'geometric gap' in modern VLAs was missing, as was a systematic comparison of architectural strategies for integrating Geometric Foundation Models (GFMs).
- The impact of non-architectural design choices, such as training data size, number of cameras, and GFM reconstruction quality, on geometric VLA performance remained underexplored.

## Method

- The team employed linear probing to quantitatively measure the geometric understanding (depth and surface normal estimation) of the GR00T-N1.5 VLA's internal representations compared to the VGGT GFM.
- Three GFM integration strategies were implemented and compared: Early Fusion, Late Fusion, and Spatial Forcing, all building upon GR00T-N1.5 and VGGT while ensuring consistent low-level details.
- A comprehensive experimental protocol evaluated architectural and non-architectural factors (training data scaling, camera count, GFM reconstruction quality) across simulated (RoboCasa, LIBERO) and real-world (Unitree G1) manipulation benchmarks.

## Results

- Linear probing showed GR00T-N1.5 had 0.73m RMSE depth error and 0.63 δ1 score from its VLM output, while VGGT achieved 0.41m RMSE and 0.89 δ1, quantitatively confirming the geometric deficiency of the VLA.
- On the Unitree G1 humanoid, Early Fusion modestly increased overall success rate to **27.78%** (vs. **22.22%** for baseline) and achieved a statistically significant improvement in the 'Approach' stage (**84.44%** vs. **57.78%**).
- A 'mid-training' phase with Early Fusion on RoboCasa resulted in a **75.2%** average success rate, statistically better than the GR00T-N1.5 baseline's **71.7%**, demonstrating the benefit of data scaling for geometric VLAs.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - VLAs exhibit a significant 'geometric gap', with internal representations demonstrating substantially higher depth errors (e.g., 0.73m RMSE for VLM output) compared to GFMs (0.41m RMSE), confirming their deficiency in explicit geometric knowledge.
> - Despite being frozen, the VLM's plasticity allows it to effectively leverage new GFM token inputs to generate dense depth predictions (0.44m RMSE with Early Fusion), indicating its capacity to assimilate geometric information.
> - Larger-scale training (mid-training) unlocks the potential of geometric VLAs, yielding statistically significant performance improvements on manipulation tasks, especially in sensor-limited, single-camera environments where GFMs provide a 21.5% improvement over the VLA baseline.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in this paper is situated at the intersection of Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) and Geometric Foundation Models (GFMs), two significant and rapidly evolving areas in robotics and computer vision.

Vision-Language-Action models have gained prominence in robotics for their capacity to facilitate generalist manipulation behaviors from limited expert demonstrations. These models leverage vision-language models (VLMs) to interpret visual inputs and natural language instructions, coupled with an action expert to generate robot actions. While VLAs demonstrate proficiency in semantic understanding and task generalization (e.g., executing commands like "pick up the red mug"), they have been observed to exhibit limitations in precise spatial understanding and geometric reasoning, which are critical for successful physical interaction in manipulation tasks. Early attempts to address this often involve augmenting VLA policies with explicit depth information from RGB-D cameras or learning implicit spatial structures.

In parallel, computer vision research has seen the development of Geometric Foundation Models. These models, often based on feed-forward transformer architectures, represent a departure from traditional multi-stage 3D reconstruction methods (e.g., SLAM or Structure from Motion). GFMs directly reconstruct camera poses, depth maps, and point maps from image collections, offering advantages in simplicity, zero-shot generalization, and compatibility with uncalibrated cameras. Notable examples include the Visual Geometry Grounded Transformer (VGGT). These models provide powerful geometric representations that generalize across environments without scene-specific training.

The current research is motivated by a recent trend to enhance VLAs with geometric understanding by integrating GFMs, leading to what are termed "geometric VLAs." Prior works have explored various strategies for this integration, reporting improvements in performance. However, several fundamental questions have remained unaddressed in this nascent field. This paper aims to systematically investigate these questions, thereby providing a more rigorous understanding of the true impact and optimal integration strategies of GFMs within VLAs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized deficiency of current Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) in robust geometric understanding, which is a critical capability for successful robotic manipulation. While VLAs excel at semantic grounding and generalization from natural language instructions, their reliance primarily on RGB inputs often leaves them struggling with tasks requiring precise spatial reasoning, such as estimating distances, relative poses, or object placement constraints. Geometric Foundation Models (GFMs) offer a potential solution by providing explicit 3D scene structure directly from images, without requiring additional data collection.

Despite promising initial results from combining VLAs and GFMs in "geometric VLAs," the authors identify several specific open questions that motivate their systematic investigation:

1. **Quantify the "Geometric Gap" in Modern VLAs:** It is intuitively understood that VLAs lack sufficient geometric understanding, but a rigorous, quantitative assessment of this deficiency has been absent. The first objective is to formalize this intuition and quantify the extent to which modern VLAs (specifically GR00T-N1.5) possess or lack geometric understanding compared to GFMs (specifically VGGT). This involves assessing the VLA's ability to infer dense depth information from its internal representations.
2. **Identify and Compare Optimal Architectural Strategies for GFM Integration:** Existing research has proposed diverse architectural approaches for injecting 3D information from GFMs into VLAs. However, a clear comparative analysis of these strategies, or an understanding of why one might be more effective than another, is lacking. The second objective is to identify, implement, and rigorously compare different architectures for integrating GFM tokens into a VLA, while minimizing low-level implementation differences to ensure a fair comparison.
3. **Analyze the Impact of Non-Architectural Design Choices on Geometric VLA Performance:** Beyond architectural considerations, various external factors related to geometry can influence the performance of geometric VLAs. The third objective is to systematically analyze the effect of these non-architectural choices, including the size of the training dataset, the number of cameras used for input, and the quality of the reconstruction provided by the GFM, on the overall performance of the integrated geometric VLA.

The paper aims to provide empirical evidence and insights into these questions, focusing on the GR00T-N1.5 VLA and the VGGT GFM.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a rigorous experimental analysis approach to address its objectives, centered on a specific VLA (GR00T-N1.5) and GFM (VGGT).

#### 3.1. Formalizing Geometric Understanding (Section 4 & Appendix E.4)

To quantify the geometric understanding inherent in VLAs, the authors utilize **linear probing**. This technique involves attaching a simple Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), acting as a "linear probe," to specific layers of a frozen, pre-trained network. The MLP is then trained to perform a downstream task to infer the information content of that network layer.

- **Probing Task:** Monocular depth estimation is chosen as the primary task, with surface normal estimation as an additional task (Appendix E.4).
- **Dataset:** NYU Depth V2 dataset, comprising 24,000 training pairs and 645 validation pairs of RGB and depth images.
- **Metrics:** For depth, Scale-Invariant Logarithmic (SILog) loss is used for training, and RMSE depth error (meters, lower is better) and δ1 score (fraction of pixels with predictions within 25% of ground truth, higher is better) for evaluation. For surface normals, cosine similarity loss is used, and mean/median angular errors (degrees, lower is better) and fraction of normals within 30 degrees (higher is better) are reported.
- **Probed Layers:** Probes are attached to the output of the GR00T-N1.5 vision encoder, the output of the GR00T-N1.5 VLM, and the output of the VGGT backbone. For geometric VLA architectures, probes are attached to the VLM output after fusion.

#### 3.2. Architectural Strategies for GFM Integration (Section 3)

The authors identify and implement three distinct strategies for integrating GFM tokens into the VLA architecture, ensuring low-level implementation details are kept consistent for fair comparison. All implementations build upon GR00T-N1.5 as the base VLA and VGGT as the GFM.

- **Early Fusion (Fig. 1b):** In this strategy, the GFM (VGGT) processes the same input images as the VLA, generating geometric tokens. These geometric tokens are then fused with the visual tokens produced by the VLA's vision encoder *before* being passed to the VLA's LLM backbone. The GFM effectively acts as an additional encoder.
- **Late Fusion (Fig. 1c):** Here, GFM tokens are generated similarly. However, they are fused with the visual tokens *outputted by the VLA's LLM* (Vl) just before these tokens are fed into the action expert. This approach aims to enrich the VLM's output with geometric information directly preceding action generation.
- **Spatial Forcing (Fig. 1d):** This strategy does not alter the VLA's architecture. Instead, during training, an alignment loss is introduced. This loss encourages internal tokens of the VLA's LLM to align (via cosine similarity) with the GFM tokens, thereby implicitly forcing the VLM to retain geometric information.

**Fusion Module Implementation (for Early and Late Fusion):** A cross-attention mechanism with attention gating is used.
 - VLA visual tokens (X) and GFM tokens (G) are projected into a common space.
 - Cross-attention is computed (Q=XW_Q, K=GW_K, V=GW_V, then Y=softmax(QK^T/√d)V).
 - The result (Y) is projected back to the VLA feature space (Z=YW_O).
 - A learned attention gate (A) is applied element-wise as a residual correction: ˜X = X + A ⊙ Z. The gate is initialized close to zero to facilitate gradual integration of geometric tokens, which is important given the pre-trained nature of the VLA. LoRA layers with rank 8 are used for projections, and modality-specific positional encodings are added to tokens to retain spatial information.

#### 3.3. Impact of Key Design Choices (Section 5)

A comprehensive experimental protocol is established to evaluate the impact of architectural and non-architectural factors on geometric VLA performance.

- **Benchmarks (Section 5.1):**
 - **Simulated:** RoboCasa (8 pick-and-place tasks, 600 total experiments, 3 cameras: left, right, wrist) and LIBERO (LIBERO-SPATIAL, LIBERO-OBJECT, LIBERO-100, evaluated over 500 trials).
 - **Real-world:** Unitree G1 humanoid (pick-and-place tasks for 3 object classes, 90 tests, randomizing object location/orientation/instances).
- **Performance Metrics:** Success rate is the primary metric. For Unitree G1, success is broken down by stages (Approach, Grasp, Lift, Placement).
- **Evaluation Protocol:** Models are evaluated at various training checkpoints (1, 5..., 100 epochs), and the best success rate is reported. Statistical significance is assessed using the two-sided McNemar’s test with respect to the GR00T-N1.5 baseline, addressing the issue of inherent randomness in VLA results (Appendix C and D). Random seeds are fixed for repeatability.
- **Training Details:** Finetuning typically involves training the action expert and the fusion module (for Early/Late Fusion), or a linear projection and alignment loss (for Spatial Forcing), while keeping other VLA components frozen. An additional "mid-training" phase on the entire RoboCasa dataset is explored for data scaling experiments.
- **Factors Investigated:**
 - **Fusion Strategy (Section 5.2):** Direct comparison of Early Fusion, Late Fusion, and Spatial Forcing against GR00T-N1.5.
 - **Training Data Scaling (Section 5.3):** Effect of mid-training Early Fusion on a larger dataset before task-specific finetuning, and comparison against a mid-trained baseline (Appendix E.2).
 - **Number of Cameras (Section 5.4):** Performance of Early Fusion vs. GR00T-N1.5 in a single-camera setup (left camera only).
 - **VGGT Reconstruction Quality (Section 5.5):** Correlation between VGGT's average RMSE depth error per episode and the Early Fusion model's success rate on RoboCasa.
 - **Invariance to Object Appearance (Appendix E.5):** Robustness of Early Fusion and GR00T to randomized object appearances in RoboCasa.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the geometric understanding in VLAs and the impact of integrating Geometric Foundation Models.

#### 4.1. Geometric Understanding Gap (Section 4 & Appendix E.4)

- **VLA Deficiency:** Linear probing consistently demonstrated that GR00T-N1.5, in its original form, exhibited limited geometric understanding. Probes attached to both the vision encoder output and the VLM output showed substantially higher RMSE depth errors (0.92m and 0.73m respectively) and lower δ1 scores (0.51 and 0.63) compared to VGGT (0.41m RMSE, 0.89 δ1). This quantitatively validates the intuition that VLAs lack explicit geometric knowledge, with significant information loss occurring even after the vision encoder.
- **GFM Efficacy:** VGGT tokens, when probed, yielded high performance in monocular depth estimation and surface normal prediction, confirming their rich geometric information content.
- **Geometric Injection:** Both Early Fusion and Late Fusion architectures, when combined with a trained probe (and the fusion module itself trained), were able to inject and retain geometric information effectively. Early Fusion achieved depth performance (RMSE 0.44m, δ1 0.88) and surface normal performance (Mean Error 41.29, Within 30° 0.44) close to that of VGGT, even with a frozen LLM, indicating the VLM's plasticity in leveraging new token inputs. Late Fusion also showed significant improvement over the VLA baseline.

#### 4.2. Impact of Fusion Strategy on Manipulation Performance (Section 5.2 & Appendix E.1)

- **Simulated Benchmarks (RoboCasa, LIBERO):** When geometric VLAs (Early Fusion, Late Fusion, Spatial Forcing) were finetuned on specific tasks, they generally did not show a statistically significant increase in overall success rate compared to the GR00T-N1.5 baseline. In some cases, average performance was slightly lower, although p-values often indicated these differences were not statistically significant and could be due to randomness (e.g., Early Fusion average 69.7% vs. GR00T-N1.5 71.7% on RoboCasa, with p=0.399).
- **Real-World Benchmark (Unitree G1):** On the Unitree G1 humanoid, both Early Fusion (27.78%) and Late Fusion (25.56%) showed a modest increase in overall success rate compared to the GR00T-N1.5 baseline (22.22%), though this difference was not statistically significant (p>0.1). However, Early Fusion demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in the "Approach" stage (84.44% vs. 57.78% for GR00T-N1.5, p<0.001), suggesting better object identification and gripper movement precision. The VLM's flexibility in processing geometric information through Early Fusion was noted as a potential factor for its stronger real-world performance.
- **Spatial Forcing (Appendix B.3):** Fine-tuning the LLM alongside the alignment loss in Spatial Forcing led to significantly worse results compared to only fine-tuning the projection module, suggesting that too many trainable parameters can hinder performance.

#### 4.3. Impact of Training Data Scaling (Section 5.3 & Appendix E.2)

- **Mid-training Advantage:** When the Early Fusion model underwent "mid-training" on the entire RoboCasa dataset before task-specific finetuning, its performance substantially improved. It achieved an average success rate of 75.2% on RoboCasa, which was statistically better (p=0.104 average, but some tasks with p<0.05) than the GR00T-N1.5 baseline (71.7%). This indicates that larger-scale training can unlock the potential of geometric VLAs and that the advantage is attributable to VGGT integration, not merely mid-training alone, as confirmed by comparison to a mid-trained GR00T baseline (75.2% vs. 72.2% with p=0.168 average, and statistically significant improvements on specific tasks).

#### 4.4. Impact of the Number of Cameras (Section 5.4)

- **Single-Camera Benefit:** In a single-camera setup on RoboCasa, both GR00T-N1.5 and Early Fusion showed a drastic drop in overall performance, confirming the importance of multi-view inputs. However, in this constrained setting, Early Fusion achieved a statistically significant improvement (21.5%) over the GR00T-N1.5 baseline (17.2%, p<0.05). This suggests that GFMs offer a viable solution to boost performance in sensor-limited scenarios where multi-view geometry cannot provide dense geometric understanding.

#### 4.5. Impact of VGGT Reconstruction Quality (Section 5.5)

- **Correlation with Success Rate:** An analysis correlating the average RMSE depth error of VGGT reconstruction with the success rate of the Early Fusion model across RoboCasa episodes revealed a mild negative correlation (Spearman coefficient ρ = -0.202). This indicates that higher quality VGGT reconstructions (lower depth error) tend to be associated with higher manipulation success rates, suggesting that improving GFM accuracy could further enhance geometric VLA performance.

#### 4.6. Invariance to Object Appearance (Appendix E.5)

- The study found that randomizing only the appearance of the target object in RoboCasa did not pose a particular challenge for either the Early Fusion approach or the GR00T-N1.5 baseline. Both models maintained similar relative performance, indicating their vision encoders abstract low-level pixel appearance into robust feature spaces.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a systematic and quantitative analysis of the integration of Geometric Foundation Models (GFMs) into Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs), offering several contributions and potential impacts on the field.

Firstly, the work formally quantifies the "geometric gap" in modern VLAs (specifically GR00T-N1.5) using linear probing for depth and surface normal estimation. By comparing the VLA's internal representations with those of a dedicated GFM (VGGT), the authors rigorously demonstrate the VLA's inherent deficiency in geometric understanding. This provides empirical grounding for prior intuitions and establishes a baseline for future improvements. The surprising finding that a frozen LLM can still generate dense depth predictions when fed GFM tokens highlights the plasticity of these large models.

Secondly, the paper systematically compares three distinct architectural strategies—Early Fusion, Late Fusion, and Spatial Forcing—for injecting GFM-derived geometric information into VLAs. By developing prototype implementations and meticulously controlling low-level details, the study provides a fair comparison of these approaches. While direct task-level finetuning did not yield statistically significant gains in simulation, the analysis in real-world experiments, particularly the significant improvement in the "Approach" stage for Early Fusion, suggests its practical relevance. This comparative analysis offers guidance for researchers on architectural choices for integrating geometric priors.

Thirdly, the research extends beyond architectural comparisons to investigate the influence of non-architectural design choices on geometric VLA performance. The finding that scaling up training data through "mid-training" can unlock statistically significant performance improvements for geometric VLAs indicates a pathway for realizing their full potential. Furthermore, demonstrating that GFMs provide statistically significant benefits in single-camera, sensor-constrained environments highlights their utility in scenarios where traditional multi-view geometry is not available. The observed correlation between GFM reconstruction quality and manipulation success rate suggests that efforts to improve GFM accuracy could directly translate to better robotic performance.

The emphasis on statistical significance testing (McNemar's test) and careful control of randomness addresses a critical need for rigorous evaluation in VLA research, offering a methodological example for the community. The insights derived from this work can inform the design of future generalist robot policies, potentially leading to more robust and precise manipulation capabilities. Specifically, the findings encourage future research into large-scale training of geometric VLAs, adaptation or finetuning of GFMs for specific domains, and the development of architectures that optimally leverage the inherent plasticity of VLM components to fully exploit geometric priors. This research contributes to a deeper understanding of how to bridge semantic and geometric reasoning for more capable robotic systems.
