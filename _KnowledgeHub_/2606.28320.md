# WARP-RM: A Warp-Augmented Relative Progress Reward Model for Data Curation

> [!summary] Summary
> WARP-RM is a self-supervised method that learns a dense, signed relative task progress signal by applying time-warp augmentations to human demonstrations. This enables WARP-BC, a behavior cloning strategy, to effectively curate imperfect human data, leading to policies with significantly improved success rates (e.g., from 0/20 to 14/20 on the lowest quality T-shirt folding dataset) and up to 18x higher throughput on physical robot manipulation tasks compared to vanilla behavior cloning.

## Problem

- Imitation learning policies trained on human teleoperation often inherit inefficiencies (hesitations, fumbles) from mixed-quality demonstration data, leading to degraded performance.
- Existing data curation methods are either too coarse (trajectory-level filtering, discarding valuable segments) or suffer from label noise (absolute frame-level progress proxies equating time to task progression) or high annotation costs (human-annotated progress models).
- Scaling imitation learning for complex robot manipulation is hampered by the difficulty of obtaining perfectly curated, efficient, and large datasets.

## Method

- Developed **WARP** (Warp-Augmented Relative Progress), a ==self-supervised algorithm== that generates synthetic progress labels by applying ==time-warp augmentations== (varying speeds, reversals) to successful demonstrations.
- Trained **WARP-RM**, a vision-based reward model, using a ==categorical objective== to predict local, signed relative task progress (like velocity) from visual observations.
- Introduced **WARP-BC**, a ==behavior cloning strategy== that leverages WARP-RM's per-frame progress velocities to selectively weight and filter action chunks, prioritizing segments with high forward progress and discarding suboptimal ones.

## Results

- WARP-BC significantly improved policy robustness and success rates, achieving **14/20** successes on the lowest quality T-shirt folding dataset (D3) where Vanilla BC failed (**0/20**), and **19/20** on a medium quality dataset (D2) where Vanilla BC achieved **2/20**.
- Policies trained with WARP-BC demonstrated substantially higher throughput (e.g., **1.78x** on D1, ~**18x** on D2 T-shirt folding, and **1.6x** on bottle-in-bin) and reduced execution times compared to baselines.
- The method efficiently curated data, training on only ~**22-35%** of action chunks while consistently outperforming other baselines (SARM, DemInf, SCIZOR) on challenging, noisy datasets.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning a *relative*, signed progress signal from time-warped demonstrations effectively overcomes the label noise inherent in absolute progress proxies and avoids costly human annotations.
> - Focusing on the *terminal frame's velocity* within an action chunk provides a robust and sharp signal for identifying efficient task progression, outperforming averaging or future-offset methods.
> - Continuous weighting of the imitation loss based on progress magnitude, rather than simple binary filtering, further enhances policy performance by differentiating between varying degrees of efficiency.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of robot imitation learning, particularly focusing on methods for scaling policy training using large datasets of human demonstrations. Imitation learning (IL) has demonstrated efficacy in enabling complex visuomotor behaviors for long-horizon robot manipulation tasks. However, its effectiveness is often contingent on the quality of the demonstration data. Human teleoperation frequently introduces mixed-quality demonstrations, which may include hesitations, fumbles, recoveries, and suboptimal pauses. Policies trained on such data can learn these inefficiencies, leading to degraded performance.

Previous approaches to address this issue have largely fallen into two categories:
1. **Trajectory-level data curation**: Methods like DemInf, and those based on influence functions, filter or discard entire episodes deemed suboptimal. A limitation of this approach is that it may inadvertently remove valuable segments (e.g., recovery behaviors) embedded within otherwise less efficient demonstrations. Simultaneously, it fails to prune localized inefficiencies within the retained, seemingly good, trajectories.
2. **Frame-level progress reward models**: These methods aim to learn a dense quality signal at a finer granularity.
 - **Absolute progress models**: ReWiND uses normalized episode duration, while VIP and LIV employ temporal contrastive learning to align visual observations. A significant drawback here is that equating elapsed time or global temporal alignment with task progression introduces label noise. Different demonstrations, even at the same normalized time index, can represent vastly different task states due to variations in operator speed, pauses, or recovery attempts.
 - **Human-annotated progress models**: SARM and ARM utilize human annotations to define subtask boundaries or assign progress scores. While these can provide accurate signals, they are costly to obtain, difficult to scale, and can suffer from annotation inconsistencies.
 - **Self-supervised progress estimators**: SCIZOR estimates progress by predicting the time between two frames.
 - **Vision-Language Model (VLM) based reward models**: Recent efforts explore using VLMs (e.g., Robometer, TopReward, Robo-Dopamine) to provide rewards, leveraging their semantic generalization capabilities. However, these often operate at a coarser temporal resolution and can still be susceptible to cross-demonstration temporal alignment issues or require specific VLM queries that limit their sensitivity to high-frequency temporal variations crucial for dynamic control.

The WARP-RM work distinguishes itself by proposing a fully self-supervised approach that learns a *local relative progress signal*. This formulation aims to overcome the label noise inherent in absolute progress proxies and the cost associated with human annotations. By predicting velocity-like magnitudes from time-warped demonstrations, WARP-RM focuses on *how fast and in which direction* the task is progressing locally, rather than an absolute completion percentage. This enables fine-grained data curation that can identify and leverage efficient segments while suppressing suboptimal ones, without requiring external supervision beyond the successful demonstrations themselves.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges associated with scaling imitation learning for complex robot manipulation tasks. While large datasets are necessary for advanced policy modeling, human teleoperation inherently produces data of mixed quality, containing segments of efficient execution alongside hesitations, fumbles, and recovery attempts. Training policies directly on such mixed-quality data can lead to the robot mimicking suboptimal behaviors, thereby reducing task success rates and execution efficiency.

The paper identifies several limitations in existing data curation strategies:
- **Episode-level filtering**: Discards entire demonstrations if they fall below a certain quality threshold. This approach is coarse, potentially removing valuable, high-advantage segments (e.g., recovery strategies) embedded within otherwise lengthy or inefficient episodes. Simultaneously, it fails to address localized suboptimal actions within the retained "good" demonstrations.
- **Absolute frame-level progress models**: These models supervise on proxies like normalized episode duration. However, temporal progression does not always equate to task progression. Variations in human operator speed, pauses, or failed attempts mean that the same normalized frame index across different demonstrations can correspond to widely different task states. This introduces significant label noise, making it difficult for models to accurately learn true task progress.
- **Human-annotated progress models**: These models require costly and time-consuming human annotations to define subtask boundaries or evaluate progress. Such annotations can also be inconsistent across different human annotators, further limiting scalability and reliability.

Given these limitations, the key objectives of this work are:
1. **Develop a novel self-supervised algorithm (WARP)**: The goal is to learn a dense, signed relative task progress signal directly from successful human demonstrations, eliminating the need for human annotations or absolute temporal proxies.
2. **Train a relative progress reward model (WARP-RM)**: This model should be capable of generating a per-frame signal indicating local task progress, specifically distinguishing decisive forward progress, stagnation, and regression, based on visual observations.
3. **Introduce WARP-BC for data curation**: Design a behavior cloning strategy that leverages WARP-RM's frame-level progress estimates to selectively filter and reweight action chunks, thereby upweighting high-advantage segments and downweighting or removing suboptimal ones.
4. **Evaluate robustness and efficiency**: Empirically demonstrate that WARP-BC can improve policy robustness, reduce execution time, and increase task throughput on physical robot manipulation tasks, especially when trained on datasets containing significant inefficiencies.

By achieving these objectives, the research aims to provide a more effective and scalable solution for data curation in imitation learning, enhancing the ability of robots to learn efficient and robust behaviors from imperfect human demonstrations.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology revolves around WARP (Warp-Augmented Relative Progress), a fully self-supervised algorithm designed to learn a dense, signed relative progress signal, and WARP-RM, a vision-based reward model trained by this algorithm. This signal is then used in WARP-BC for data curation in behavior cloning.

#### 3.1. WARP Algorithm for Self-Supervised Progress Learning

The WARP algorithm trains WARP-RM to predict the normalized time delta between input frames, effectively learning progress velocity. It constructs per-frame cumulative progress labels by applying time-warp augmentations to successful demonstrations.

- **Time-Warp Sampler (Section 3.2)**: This component generates sequences of `N` frame indices (`i_0, ..., i_{N-1}`) from a demonstration. The key steps are:
 - **Log-Velocity Generation**: `N-1` relative log-velocities (`z_k`) are drawn from a stationary AR(1) process. This process, characterized by an autocorrelation `α` and marginal standard deviation `σ_∞`, ensures smooth and correlated changes in velocity across frames, modeling natural variations in execution speed. Operating in log-space keeps velocities positive.
 - **Path Length Rescaling**: The log-velocities are exponentiated to `˜v_k`. The total path length `ℓ` (representing the total temporal displacement of the window) is sampled uniformly within a range (`[1/3 L, 5/3 L]`), allowing the model to see both slow-motion and fast-forwarded views. The `˜v_k` values are then rescaled to sum to `ℓ`, yielding `˜u_k`.
 - **Reversals for Negative Progress**: To simulate non-monotonic progress (regression), `R` reversal points are sampled from a Poisson distribution (`λ_rev`). At each reversal point, the sign of all subsequent velocities is flipped, causing the trajectory to alternate between forward and backward playback. Additionally, the entire trajectory is reversed with a 0.5 probability. This generates a sequence of signed, normalized playback velocities (`u_k`).
 - **Index Generation**: Cumulative signed displacements (`c_j`) are calculated from `u_k`. A starting index `i_0` is uniformly sampled such that all subsequent indices `i_j = round(i_0 + c_j)` fall within the original demonstration's bounds.
- **Relative Cumulative Progress Targets (Section 3.3)**: For the sampled indices, the per-frame cumulative progress pseudo-labels (`y_j`) are computed as the signed displacement from the window start, normalized by a constant `C_norm`: `y_j = (i_j - i_0) / C_norm`.
- **Categorical Objective (Section 3.3)**: Instead of direct regression on `y_j`, WARP-RM predicts `N` separate probability distributions over evenly spaced categorical bins. The model is trained with a cross-entropy loss against a two-hot target encoding that linearly interpolates between the two nearest bin centers. This categorical approach is stated to mitigate optimization instabilities and improve capacity utilization compared to direct regression.

#### 3.2. WARP-RM Model Architecture (Section 3.4)

The WARP-RM architecture (Figure 3) uses a visual backbone and a temporal aggregator:
- **Visual Backbone**: A frozen DINOv3 ViT-B/16 model (`ϕ`) extracts 768-dimensional feature vectors for each frame.
- **Input Tokens**: Each input token to the temporal aggregator is formed by concatenating the frame embedding with its temporal difference: `[ϕ(o_i_j), ϕ(o_i_j) - ϕ(o_i_{j-1})]`. The difference is zero for the first frame.
- **Temporal Aggregator**: A bidirectional Transformer encoder processes the projected input tokens, augmented with fixed sinusoidal positional embeddings.
- **Output Head**: A linear layer maps each output token from the Transformer to separate probability distributions over the 30 cumulative-progress bins. At inference, the predicted `ˆy_j` is the expectation of the distribution.

#### 3.3. Warp-Augmented Relative Progress Behavior Cloning (WARP-BC) (Section 3.5)

WARP-BC integrates the WARP-RM's progress signal into behavior cloning.
- **Velocity Aggregation**: WARP-RM operates in overlapping windows shifted by a single source frame. Within each window, consecutive expected cumulative progress predictions (`ˆy_j`) are differenced and scaled to produce intra-window velocities (`v_j`). For each interior source-frame `t`, the final per-frame progress velocity (`ˆv_t`) is computed as the mean of all `v_j` predictions from overlapping windows covering `t`. This `ˆv_t` signifies how fast and in which direction the task is progressing, with `≈ 1` for average reference pace, `≈ 0` for stagnation, and `< 0` for regression.
- **Action Chunk Weighting**: The behavior cloning policy predicts 1-second action chunks. For each chunk, a weight `w(s, a)` is computed based on the progress velocity at the *final frame* of the chunk (`ˆv_end`): `w(s, a) = ˆv_end * 1_{ˆv_end > τ}`. Here, `τ` is a filtering threshold (e.g., `τ=1.0` to reweight chunks above the reference expert pace). Chunks with `w(s, a) = 0` are filtered out before training.
- **Behavior Cloning Objective**: The policy is trained using a standard flow-matching loss, with each sample weighted by `w(s, a)`: `L_BC = E_{(s,a)~D} [w(s, a) * L_flow(π_θ; s, a)]`.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Robot Platform**: Physical bimanual I2RT YAM robot arm.
- **Tasks**:
 - Long-horizon deformable object manipulation: T-shirt folding from a crumpled start.
 - Pick-and-place: Bottle-in-bin placement.
- **Datasets**: Subsets of human-teleoperated demonstrations, length-filtered to create tiers of varying quality (D1, D2, D3 for T-shirt folding) as a proxy for sub-optimality. WARP-RM is trained on a fixed reference subset (D_RM) of the shortest, most efficient demonstrations.
- **Evaluation Metrics**: Success rate, mean time-to-completion (TTC) for successful trials, throughput (successful tasks per hour), and percentage of action chunks retained for training.
- **Baselines**: Vanilla Behavior Cloning, SARM, DemInf, and SCIZOR.
- **Ablation Studies**: Conducted on the D2 dataset to analyze the impact of weighting function, aggregation strategy, and time-warp sampler variants.
- **Simulation**: Bottle-in-bin task evaluated in a GPU-parallel MuJoCo simulation to corroborate real-world results at a larger scale.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented several findings across various tasks and experimental setups:

#### 4.1. T-shirt Folding: Cross-tier Evaluation (Table 1, Figure 4)

- **Robustness against Suboptimal Data**: WARP-BC demonstrated superior robustness when trained on datasets of progressively lower quality (i.e., admitting more inefficient demonstrations).
 - On the most efficient dataset (D1, ≤ 60s), both Vanilla BC and WARP-BC achieved 20/20 success. However, WARP-BC yielded 1.78x higher throughput (56.3 vs. 31.6 folds/hr) and lower mean time-to-completion (TTC).
 - As dataset quality decreased (D2, ≤ 90s, and D3, ≤ 120s), Vanilla BC's performance deteriorated significantly, collapsing to 2/20 success on D2 and 0/20 on D3. Policies often exhibited repetitive, localized adjustments, leading to timeouts.
 - In contrast, WARP-BC maintained high success rates (19/20 on D2, 14/20 on D3) and substantially higher throughput (e.g., ~18x improvement on D2 compared to Vanilla BC).
- **Data Curation Efficiency**: WARP-BC selectively retained a smaller fraction of action chunks for training (e.g., 35.7% on D1, 34.4% on D2, 22.5% on D3) compared to Vanilla BC, which used 100%. This indicates effective pruning of suboptimal segments.

#### 4.2. T-shirt Folding: Baseline Comparisons (Table 2, Figure 10)

- **Comparative Performance**: WARP-BC was compared against SARM, DemInf, and SCIZOR on augmented datasets (D4 and D5) which included expert annotations for SARM.
 - SARM and SCIZOR performed well on D4 (19/20 success) but experienced a substantial drop on D5 (2/20 success). These methods retained a larger fraction of action chunks (e.g., 66.6-77.9% on D5), suggesting their filtering might be insufficiently selective for broader training distributions.
 - DemInf remained comparatively robust on D5 (18/20 success) with a similar retained-data budget to WARP-BC but yielded lower throughput.
 - WARP-BC achieved 20/20 success on both D4 and D5, consistently outperforming baselines in throughput (e.g., 44.6 folds/hr on D5 compared to 1.55-25.3 for others), while retaining a moderate fraction of chunks (33.7-45.6%).

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies (Table 3)

Conducted on the D2 dataset, ablations investigated the impact of different algorithmic components:
- **Weighting Function**:
 - Using no threshold (τ=0) resulted in poor performance (3/20 success).
 - Binary weighting (τ=1, filtering only) achieved 16/20 success.
 - Continuous weighting (WARP-BC, τ=1, scaling by magnitude) yielded 19/20 success, demonstrating that scaling the imitation loss by progress magnitude is beneficial beyond simple filtering.
- **Aggregation Strategy**:
 - Gating by the terminal frame's velocity (`ˆv_end`, as in WARP-BC) was superior (19/20 success) to averaging `ˆv` over the entire action chunk (15/20) or using a future-offset chunk mean (14/20). Terminal aggregation was found to better isolate the leading edges of task progress and provide a sharper cutoff for segments decelerating into hesitation.
- **Time-Warp Sampler**:
 - The AR(1) process sampler (WARP-BC, 19/20 success, 27.4 thrput) showed marginal success improvement and clearer gains in throughput compared to an IID log-normal sampler (18/20 success, 22.8 thrput). The AR(1) process, which models smoothly correlated velocity changes, likely better aligns with the characteristics of natural execution speeds encountered during inference.

#### 4.4. Bottle-in-Bin Placement Task (Real World & Simulation)

- **Real-World (Table 4, Figure 5)**: WARP-BC placed more bottles (74/80 vs. 59/80 for Vanilla BC), reduced mean per-bottle placement time (11.3 s vs. 15.9 s), and achieved a 1.6x throughput improvement (237.8 vs. 147.8 bottles/hr).
- **Simulation (Table 5, Figure 6)**: These findings were corroborated in a larger-scale simulation. WARP-BC placed more bottles (1326/1536 vs. 1205/1536), reduced mean per-bottle placement time (8.3 s vs. 10.0 s), improved throughput (310.8 vs. 282.4 bottles/hr), and completed all six bottles in 53.9% of scenes compared to 30.5% for Vanilla BC. Statistical tests confirmed the significance of these improvements (p < 10^-6 for bottles placed, p < 10^-10 for placement time).
- **Consistency**: Across both real-world and simulation, WARP-BC consistently demonstrated faster, more successful execution by retaining approximately 30-31% of action chunks.

In summary, the main findings indicate that WARP-RM effectively learns a relative progress signal, enabling WARP-BC to perform robust and efficient data curation. This approach significantly enhances policy performance, particularly when faced with suboptimal demonstrations, improving task success rates, execution speed, and overall throughput on various robot manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces WARP-RM, a self-supervised method for learning dense, signed relative progress signals, and demonstrates its utility through WARP-BC for data curation in imitation learning. The work addresses a persistent challenge in scaling robot learning from human demonstrations: the inherent presence of mixed-quality data containing suboptimal behaviors.

The significance and potential impact of this work are multifaceted:

- **Enhanced Policy Robustness**: The most immediate impact is the significant improvement in policy robustness. WARP-BC enables policies to learn efficient behaviors even when trained on datasets containing substantial inefficiencies, hesitations, or fumbles, as evidenced by its sustained high performance across various quality tiers of T-shirt folding demonstrations. This directly contributes to making imitation learning more practical for real-world robot deployment, where perfectly curated expert demonstrations are often unavailable or prohibitively expensive to collect.
- **Increased Throughput and Efficiency**: By filtering out or downweighting suboptimal action chunks, WARP-BC leads to policies that execute tasks faster and with higher success rates, thereby increasing the effective throughput of robotic systems. This was consistently observed across T-shirt folding and bottle-in-bin placement tasks in both real-world and simulated environments. Such efficiency gains are critical for industrial or service robotics applications.
- **Scalability through Self-Supervision**: A key contribution is the fully self-supervised nature of WARP. By generating progress labels through time-warp augmentations rather than relying on human annotations or absolute temporal proxies, the method bypasses a major bottleneck in data curation. This significantly reduces the cost and effort associated with preparing large, high-quality datasets, making the approach highly scalable for larger and more diverse robot learning scenarios.
- **Fine-Grained Data Curation**: Unlike coarse episode-level filtering, WARP-BC operates at the action-chunk level, allowing for precise identification and weighting of high-advantage segments. This means valuable recovery behaviors or efficient sub-task completions embedded within otherwise suboptimal demonstrations can be retained, while localized inefficiencies are pruned. This intelligent utilization of demonstration data maximizes the learning potential from any given dataset.
- **Generalizability Across Tasks**: The successful application of WARP-BC to two distinct real-world manipulation tasks—a long-horizon deformable object task (T-shirt folding) and a pick-and-place task (bottle-in-bin)—suggests a degree of generalizability for the method across different types of robot manipulation challenges. The consistent performance in simulation further corroborates its potential for broader application.

**Limitations and Future Directions**:
The paper acknowledges certain limitations, which also highlight avenues for future work:
- **Behavioral Scope**: The resulting policy remains confined to the behaviors observed in the training set. Future research could explore integrating WARP-RM with methods like DAgger, offline/online reinforcement learning, or iterative self-improvement to enable generalization beyond initial offline demonstrations and improve exploratory capabilities.
- **Realism of Negative Progress**: The negative progress supervision derived from reversed playback is an approximation. While demonstrated to be effective, physically implausible reversals may introduce a distribution gap between synthetic training data and real-world regressions. Further investigation into generating more realistic negative progress signals or validating the approach across a wider range of tasks could be beneficial.
- **Embodiment and Task Diversity**: The current experiments are limited to two bimanual manipulation tasks on a single robot embodiment. Extending evaluations to diverse robot platforms, task complexities, and sensory modalities would further validate the robustness and generalizability of WARP-RM.

In conclusion, WARP-RM represents a methodological advancement in imitation learning data curation, offering a practical, scalable, and effective solution for training robust robotic policies from imperfect human demonstrations. Its self-supervised approach and demonstrated empirical gains position it as a significant contribution to the field of robot learning.
