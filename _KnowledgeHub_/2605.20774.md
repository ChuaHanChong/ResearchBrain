# VLA-REPLICA: A Low-Cost, Reproducible Benchmark for Real-World Evaluation of Vision-Language-Action Models

> [!summary] Summary
> VLA-REPLICA introduces a low-cost, reproducible real-world benchmark for evaluating Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in diverse manipulation tasks. It demonstrates that VLA models generalize effectively to visual appearance changes but show limitations in tasks requiring robust memory, while yielding consistent evaluation results across independently constructed instances.

## Problem

- Existing real-world robotics benchmarks often require expensive hardware and complex setups, limiting accessibility and widespread adoption by researchers.
- The "sim-to-real gap" means that policies performing well in simulations frequently do not translate effectively to physical environments, leading to overestimates of real-world capabilities.
- Many current real-world evaluation platforms suffer from limited task diversity or hinder standardized comparative studies due to poor reproducibility across different laboratories.

## Method

- Developed **VLA-REPLICA**, a real-world benchmark using off-the-shelf hardware with a total cost of approximately $1050, emphasizing low cost and ease of reproduction.
- Implemented a standardized physical platform setup, including a controlled lighting environment, custom 3D-printed mounts, and a camera calibration procedure using AprilTags and visual overlays.
- Introduced a universal action space for the SO-101 robotic arm and a calibration process to ensure consistent action interpretation and reproducibility across different arm units.

## Results

- VLA models achieved higher average success rates on in-distribution tasks (e.g., π0.5 at 0.54) compared to imitation learning policies (e.g., ACT at 0.18), demonstrating the benefit of pre-training.
- Models demonstrated generalization to visual attribute changes in out-of-distribution tasks but showed a significant decline in performance for tasks requiring internal state tracking or numerical repetition.
- The benchmark's reproducibility was validated by consistent success rates between the original and an independently constructed setup (e.g., average ID success rates of 0.49 vs. 0.48, and OOD rates of 0.55 vs. 0.50).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pre-trained VLA models generally outperform imitation learning policies on in-distribution tasks, indicating the value of pre-training for real-world adaptation with limited data.
> - VLA models exhibit generalization capabilities for out-of-distribution tasks involving visual attribute changes (e.g., object color or shape), effectively reusing learned manipulation skills.
> - Current VLA models demonstrate limitations in out-of-distribution tasks requiring robust memory, internal state tracking, or precise numerical counting, often failing to track action repetitions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models indicate their potential for developing general-purpose robotic manipulation systems that can interpret natural language instructions and execute corresponding physical actions. Despite demonstrated capabilities in controlled settings, the reliable real-world evaluation of these models remains a challenge, limiting their development and deployment.

Current evaluation methodologies primarily consist of simulation-based and real-world benchmarks. Simulation platforms, such as Meta-World and LIBERO, offer scalable and standardized environments but frequently encounter the "sim-to-real gap," where policies performing well in simulation do not translate directly to physical environments. This discrepancy can lead to overestimates of real-world capabilities.

Conversely, existing real-world benchmarks provide a more accurate assessment of robotic performance but introduce their own limitations. Some, like REPLAB and SceneReplica, offer limited task diversity, focusing on specific actions such as object grasping. Others, including FurnitureBench and ManipulationNet, necessitate expensive hardware setups, meticulously engineered environments, and considerable human effort for replication across laboratories. Furthermore, certain evaluation platforms, such as RoboArena and RoboChallenge, rely on centralized or remote evaluation, which can restrict accessibility, prolong iteration cycles, and reduce transparency for individual researchers.

The absence of a widely adopted benchmark that is simultaneously grounded in real-world conditions and easily reproducible across independent research facilities represents a gap in the current robotics research landscape. VLA-REPLICA positions itself to address this deficit by offering a low-cost, easily reproducible real-world benchmark that combines diverse manipulation tasks with a standardized evaluation protocol for local, distributed execution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research originates from identified challenges in reliably evaluating Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models in real-world scenarios. Despite the capabilities demonstrated by VLA models in controlled settings, their practical deployment is constrained by the limitations of existing evaluation protocols.

The specific problems that motivated this work include:
- **Sim-to-Real Gap**: Simulation benchmarks often provide an optimistic assessment of model performance that does not fully transfer to real-world conditions due due to physical discrepancies.
- **High Cost and Complexity**: Many existing real-world benchmarks require expensive hardware, complex environmental engineering, and significant human expertise for setup and maintenance, limiting their widespread adoption.
- **Limited Reproducibility**: The specialized nature of some real-world setups impedes consistent replication across different laboratories, hindering standardized comparative studies.
- **Limited Task Diversity**: Some real-world benchmarks focus on a narrow range of tasks, failing to cover the broad spectrum of manipulation behaviors required for general-purpose VLA models.
- **Centralized Evaluation Constraints**: Benchmarks relying on centralized or remote evaluation mechanisms can slow down research iteration, reduce transparency, and limit immediate, hands-on experimentation.

To address these limitations, the key objectives of the VLA-REPLICA project are:
- **Introduce VLA-REPLICA as a Low-Cost and Reproducible Benchmark**: To establish an accessible real-world benchmark with a total hardware cost of approximately $1050 USD, designed for straightforward replication.
- **Design a Standardized Hardware and Environment Setup**: To develop and document a standardized physical platform and environmental configuration that can be consistently built and calibrated by non-expert users across various laboratories with minimal effort and expense.
- **Propose a Comprehensive Evaluation Protocol**: To establish an evaluation framework that assesses both in-distribution performance (how effectively models adapt to a target environment from limited data) and out-of-distribution generalization (how robustly models perform under novel variations in object properties or task requirements).
- **Provide Empirical Evaluation of VLA Models**: To conduct extensive experiments with a range of state-of-the-art VLA models, offering data-driven insights into their real-world capabilities, limitations, and generalization behaviors.
- **Demonstrate Reproducibility**: To empirically validate that the benchmark yields consistent evaluation results across independently constructed instances of the platform, thereby confirming its design for reproducibility.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The VLA-REPLICA benchmark was designed with principles of accessibility, standardization, and local executability to facilitate widespread adoption and consistent evaluation of VLA models in real-world settings.

#### 3.1. Physical Platform and Hardware Configuration

The benchmark system is constructed from off-the-shelf components, designed for a low total cost of approximately $1050 USD. The core hardware includes:
- **Robotic Arm**: A 6-Degrees-of-Freedom (DoF) SO-101 follower arm (approximately $200).
- **Cameras**: An Intel RealSense D455 RGB-D camera (approximately $425) serves as the top-view camera, and a Vinmooog RGB webcam ($13.98) is mounted on the end-effector as a wrist camera.
- **Controlled Environment**: A 32-inch photography light box (approximately $153) is utilized to ensure consistent illumination (around 5600K) and reduce environmental variations across setups.
- **Object Set**: A curated set of commodity objects (approximately $216) is used for the manipulation tasks.
To ensure physical reproducibility, custom 3D-printed mounts are designed for both the top and wrist cameras. Detailed assembly instructions facilitate quick setup. Camera views are standardized using a camera overlay program, which allows users to visually match their camera's live feed to a reference image. Precise alignment of the top camera relative to the robot base is further achieved using an AprilTag, whose 6-DoF pose is recorded at a fixed position.

#### 3.2. SO-101 Action Reproducibility

The SO-101 arm's construction from 3D-printed parts introduces potential variability in servo motor offsets between different units. To address this, a standardized action space is implemented:
- **Arm Calibration**: Each SO-101 arm undergoes a calibration process where it is set to a standard "L" shape, defining a universal "zero position." Raw encoder values for offset (`a_offset`) and joint limits (`a_min`, `a_max`) are recorded for each of the arm's six servo motors.
- **Action Normalization**: A universal action space (ˆu) representing joint angles in degrees (ranging from -180 to 180) is defined. A diagonal calibration matrix (D) is computed from `a_min` and `a_max` to linearly scale raw encoder values. Policy outputs in this universal action space (ˆu) are then converted back to arm-specific raw encoder values (ˆa) during policy execution, ensuring consistent action interpretation across different SO-101 arms.

#### 3.3. Task Suite and Evaluation Protocols

VLA-REPLICA includes a diverse suite of ten real-world manipulation tasks, categorizing them into pick-and-place, object interaction, and memory-dependent tasks (e.g., "Put bread on the red plate," "Fold the pink towel in half," "Pour pepper n times").
- **Demonstration Data**: A dataset of 500 expert demonstrations (50 per task) was collected using a teleoperated SO-101 leader arm controlling the follower arm, with varied scene configurations and consistent robot actions.
- **Evaluation Scenarios**: The benchmark supports two evaluation settings:
 - **In-Distribution (ID) Evaluation**: Measures a model's ability to learn and execute tasks within the distribution of the training data after fine-tuning. It consists of 50 unique test scenes (5 per task) with object placements within the observed range of the demonstration dataset.
 - **Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Evaluation**: Assesses a model's generalization capabilities to novel variations not seen during training. It includes 40 test scenes across eight tasks, featuring changes in object attributes (e.g., color, shape) or task requirements (e.g., unseen repetition counts for memory tasks).
- **Test Scene Design**: Object placements for test scenes were designed based on an analysis of object center distributions from the demonstration data, ensuring either adherence to the training distribution for ID evaluation or controlled deviations for OOD evaluation. A live image overlay method is used for consistent physical scene reconstruction.

#### 3.4. Benchmarking Experiments

The study evaluates both imitation learning methods (ACT, Multi-task Diffusion Transformer (DiT-D), Flow-Matching DiT (DiT-F)) and state-of-the-art VLA models (SmolVLA, X-VLA, π0, π0.5). All models were trained or fine-tuned for 40,000 steps using the 500 collected expert demonstrations, utilizing LeRobot implementations. Each policy was evaluated for five runs per task variant, with 90 seconds allocated per task. Success was determined by predefined task-specific criteria. A second, independently constructed instance of the VLA-REPLICA setup was built by a non-expert user at a different location. A subset of ID and OOD tasks were re-evaluated on this new setup to verify consistent performance across physical instantiations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

Benchmarking experiments on VLA-REPLICA yielded insights into the performance and generalization capabilities of various robot learning models in real-world manipulation tasks.

#### 4.1. In-Distribution Policy Evaluation

Evaluation of models on in-distribution (ID) tasks, which involve object configurations and task requirements similar to the training data, produced the following observations:
- **VLA Models vs. Imitation Learning**: Fine-tuned VLA models generally achieved higher average success rates on ID tasks compared to imitation learning (IL) policies trained from scratch. For example, π0.5 achieved an average success rate of 0.54, while ACT averaged 0.18. This suggests that pre-training in VLA models provides a beneficial initialization for adapting to specific target tasks and environments with limited real-world data.
- **Task Performance Variation**: Policies performed more effectively on tasks involving deformable objects or simpler pick-and-place motions. However, tasks requiring precise manipulation of rigid objects, such as "Stack block on block," showed lower success rates across models, attributed to sensitivity to gripper pose inaccuracies.

#### 4.2. Out-of-Distribution Policy Evaluation

Testing policies on out-of-distribution (OOD) tasks, designed to assess generalization to unseen variations, showed a general decline in performance compared to ID tasks:
- **Generalization to Visual Attributes**: VLA models like SmolVLA, π0, and π0.5 demonstrated comparable success rates on OOD pick-and-place and object-interaction tasks involving variations in object color or shape. This indicates that these pre-trained VLA models can effectively reuse learned manipulation skills and adapt to new visual appearances.
- **Limitations in Memory-Counting Tasks**: All evaluated methods, including IL baselines and VLA models, showed a significant drop in success rates for memory-counting OOD tasks that required generalizing to unseen repetition counts. Policies frequently failed to track the number of actions executed, often leading to continuous, unprompted actions. This outcome highlights a limitation in current VLA models regarding tasks that demand internal state tracking, sequential memory, or generalization of numerical repetition.

#### 4.3. Reproducibility Analysis

To validate the benchmark's reproducibility, a new, identical setup was constructed at a different location by an inexperienced user. A subset of ID and OOD tasks with relatively higher success rates from the original evaluations were re-tested:
- **Consistent Performance**: The success rates achieved on the independently constructed platform were comparable to those obtained on the original setup across all tested models and tasks. For example, average ID success rates were 0.49 (original) and 0.48 (reproduced), while OOD tasks averaged 0.55 (original) and 0.50 (reproduced). This consistency in performance across different physical instantiations validates VLA-REPLICA's design for reproducibility and its utility for distributed research.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

VLA-REPLICA addresses a previously identified gap in the robotics research landscape by providing a low-cost, reproducible real-world benchmark for Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. Its significance and potential impact span several areas:

- **Democratization of Real-World Robotics Research**: By offering a hardware setup costing approximately $1050 USD and detailed, accessible assembly instructions, VLA-REPLICA lowers the financial and technical barriers to entry for real-world robot manipulation research. This enables a broader range of academic institutions and individual researchers to conduct experiments in physical environments.
- **Standardization of Evaluation**: The benchmark introduces a comprehensive set of standardization protocols, including a controlled lighting environment, precise camera calibration using AprilTags and visual overlays, and a universal action space for the SO-101 arm. These measures aim to minimize environmental and hardware inconsistencies, enabling more consistent and comparable evaluations of VLA models across different laboratories worldwide.
- **Acceleration of Research and Development**: By enabling local, on-site evaluation, VLA-REPLICA facilitates faster iteration cycles for model development and refinement. Researchers can deploy and test their policies directly, accelerating the empirical understanding of VLA model performance and generalization capabilities in complex, real-world scenarios.
- **Identification of Model Strengths and Limitations**: The empirical evaluations conducted using VLA-REPLICA have provided insights into current VLA model capabilities. The findings suggest that these models generalize effectively to visual appearance changes but demonstrate limitations in tasks requiring robust memory, internal state tracking, or precise numerical action counting. These identified limitations highlight specific areas for future research and development in VLA model architectures and training methodologies.
- **Foundation for Future Benchmarking Efforts**: VLA-REPLICA serves as a practical foundation for the development of future real-world benchmarks. Its emphasis on low cost and reproducibility could inspire similar initiatives, contributing to a more robust and accessible ecosystem for evaluating general-purpose robotic systems. While currently focused on tabletop manipulation with a single robot type, the principles established by VLA-REPLICA may be extended to more diverse robotic platforms and environments.

The paper acknowledges certain limitations, including its focus on tabletop manipulation with a single low-cost robot embodiment, and a task scale that is smaller than large-scale simulation benchmarks. Additionally, minor environmental variations may persist despite standardization efforts. Nevertheless, VLA-REPLICA represents a step towards establishing a more accessible and consistent framework for real-world VLA model evaluation, with the goal of fostering progress towards versatile and capable robotic systems.
