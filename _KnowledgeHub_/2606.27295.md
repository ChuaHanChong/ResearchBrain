# LA4VLA: Learning to Act without Seeing via Language-Action Pretraining

> [!summary] Summary
> LA4VLA introduces a pretraining framework that decouples language-action learning from visual grounding to develop robust, vision-independent action priors for robot policies. This approach, which includes the LA4-33K dataset and various pretraining paradigms, improves manipulation performance by up to 45.0 percentage points in real-world tasks and enhances robustness to visual perturbations.

## Problem

- Existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models often over-rely on visual observations, leading to "visual shortcuts" and fragile instruction following.
- The inherent asymmetry in VLA training datasets, with dense vision-action supervision but sparse, high-level language-action instructions, causes language grounding to be underdeveloped.
- VLA policies struggle with visual variations and conflicting visual inputs, indicating that their instruction-following behavior is not robustly grounded in language.

## Method

- The **LA4VLA** framework performs ==vision-agnostic language-action pretraining== by explicitly masking visual observations during this phase.
- A specialized dataset, LA4-33K, is constructed from existing VLA demonstrations by segmenting them into 33,116 atomic action episodes, each paired with a low-level, vision-agnostic language description via VLM and human verification.
- Various pretraining paradigms (e.g., LA-only, sequential LA-VLA, mixed LA-VLA) are explored to effectively integrate this vision-independent language-action supervision into VLA policy learning.

## Results

- LA pretraining consistently improved downstream success rates, with LA4VLA-1B achieving **83.00%** on MetaWorld and **95.30%** on LIBERO, surpassing no-pretraining and VLA-only baselines.
- The mixed pretraining (MixPT) paradigm yielded the highest real-world success rate of **83.3%** on an xArm6 across three tasks, marking a **45.0** percentage point improvement over the no-pretraining baseline (**38.3%**).
- LA-pretrained policies demonstrated enhanced robustness to visual perturbations, achieving **70.0%** success under visual noise with MixPT, compared to **27.5%** for the no-pretraining baseline, and exhibited strong instruction following (DCS = **0.85**) even with masked visual input.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Standard VLA training results in policies whose apparent instruction following is fragile, significantly degraded when visual input is removed or conflicting (e.g., directional alignment rate dropped from 0.98 to 0.35 with conflicting visuals).
> - Explicitly decoupling language-action learning from visual input during pretraining enables policies to acquire robust, vision-independent action priors, preventing reliance on visual shortcuts.
> - The generated dense, localized language-action supervision is complementary to standard VLA training, leading to stronger instruction grounding and more resilient policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represents a notable paradigm for general-purpose robot manipulation. These models typically operate by learning from large-scale robot demonstrations that incorporate visual observations, action sequences, and high-level language instructions. Current VLA models, such as OpenVLA, $\pi^0$, GR00T N1, SmolVLA, Evo-1, StarVLA, and Qwen-VLA, have demonstrated capabilities across various simulation and real-world tasks, making VLA pretraining a central research direction.

However, the existing joint VLA training paradigm exhibits an inherent asymmetry that this work seeks to address. At the data level, a single robot trajectory often contains numerous image-action pairs but is typically associated with only one high-level task instruction. This leads to dense and dynamically varying vision-action supervision, contrasting with sparse language-action supervision that lacks explicit alignment to local action stages. At the model-input level, visual observations are commonly encoded into significantly more tokens than language instructions. This asymmetry can bias the learning process towards visual signals, potentially diluting the influence of language on action execution. Consequently, policies might develop "visual shortcuts," relying more on visual cues than on robust language-action grounding, which can render them sensitive to visual variations (e.g., changes in viewpoint, lighting, object appearance).

This research identifies a gap in existing VLA methodologies, specifically the challenge of isolating and strengthening language-action grounding independently of dense visual input. While some recent efforts have explored more explicit language-to-action signals, they have largely remained within the joint VLA training framework or have not thoroughly investigated vision-agnostic language-action supervision as a primary pretraining signal. This work positions itself as a complementary approach to existing VLA learning pipelines by explicitly decoupling language-action learning from visual grounding during pretraining, with the objective of fostering vision-independent action knowledge and reusable language-conditioned action priors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the robustness and generalizability of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies by addressing the limitations of current VLA pretraining, specifically the observed over-reliance on visual cues and insufficient language-action grounding. The authors hypothesize that explicitly removing visual observations during pretraining can compel a policy to focus on the inherent relationship between language and action, thereby preventing the formation of visual shortcuts.

The motivation for this approach is supported by an initial diagnostic study detailed in Section 3 of the paper. This study probed the instruction-following capabilities of a standard VLA policy by manipulating its visual input while keeping the language instruction fixed. The diagnostic revealed that:
- Under standard paired input (language and aligned visual observations), the policy exhibited apparent instruction following.
- However, when the visual input was removed, unaligned (replaced with a different observation from the same scene), or conflicting (replaced with an observation associated with an opposite motion direction), the policy's instruction following significantly deteriorated. The predicted action directions became scattered, poorly separated for opposite instructions, or even biased towards the conflicting visual cue, overriding the language instruction. For instance, the Directional Alignment Rate (DAR) dropped from 0.98 with standard input to 0.35 with conflicting visual input, indicating a strong dependence on visual cues.

These findings motivated the development of the LA4VLA framework, which aims to:
1. **Develop a vision-agnostic language-action pretraining framework**: This framework would enable policies to acquire language-conditioned action priors without simultaneously processing visual observations, thereby encouraging the learning of intrinsic action dynamics, temporal consistency, and language-conditioned motion patterns.
2. **Construct a dedicated Language-Action (LA) dataset**: This dataset, named LA-33K, would be created by segmenting existing VLA demonstrations into atomic action intervals and pairing each segment with a low-level, vision-agnostic action description. The goal is to transform sparse, high-level language supervision into dense, localized language-action supervision directly aligned with action execution.
3. **Investigate various pretraining paradigms**: The research sought to systematically evaluate how language-action supervision can be incorporated into VLA learning, including LA-only pretraining, sequential LA-to-VLA pretraining, and mixed LA-VLA pretraining.
4. **Assess downstream performance, transferability, and robustness**: A key objective was to determine if LA pretraining improves VLA policies' performance on various simulation and real-world manipulation tasks, generalizes across different VLA architectures, and enhances robustness under visual perturbations.
5. **Analyze instruction-following behavior and internal representations**: The authors aimed to examine whether LA pretraining leads to more reliable instruction-conditioned action prediction and more distinct, instruction-aligned internal representations within the policy.

By pursuing these objectives, the research seeks to establish language-action pretraining as a valuable and complementary strategy for building VLA policies that are more robust, generalize better across varying visual conditions, and are more reliably grounded in language intent.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The LA4VLA framework introduces a language-action pretraining strategy that decouples language-conditioned action learning from visually grounded VLA training. The core methodology involves constructing a vision-agnostic Language-Action (LA) dataset and integrating this supervision into various pretraining paradigms for VLA models.

#### 3.1. LA-33K Dataset Construction

The LA-33K dataset is derived from existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) demonstrations, specifically the DROID dataset, without requiring additional robot data collection. The construction pipeline involves reorganizing these demonstrations into short, atomic, vision-agnostic language-action episodes:

1. **Keyframe Detection**: The process begins by extracting temporal cues from robot state trajectories.
 - **Static Keyframes**: Identified as short intervals with low arm-state variation, often corresponding to alignment, grasping, or phase transitions. These are detected by analyzing smoothed and normalized arm-state differences.
 - **Gripper-Transition Keyframes**: Mark frames where the gripper changes state (open/close), indicating grasp or release phases.
 - These keyframes are used as soft timestamped hints within the segmentation prompt for the VLM.
2. **Atomic-Action Vocabulary**: A predefined taxonomy of common manipulation primitives is established, organized by interaction context (object manipulation, contact interaction, free-space motion) and motion type (translation, rotation). Examples include `grasp`, `place`, `lift`, `lower`, `transport`, `push`, `pull`, `press`, `move`, `rotate`, `reorient`. Each category is defined by its dominant physical effect and corresponding end-effector motion, gripper state, object status, and contact context.
3. **Prompt Construction**: For each original demonstration, a structured prompt is created for a Vision-Language Model (VLM). This prompt includes:
 - The original task instruction.
 - Sampled multi-view video frames (used only during construction, not in the final LA data).
 - Detected keyframe events as temporal hints.
 - The atomic-action vocabulary and primitive definitions.
 - Category assignment rules.
 - A required JSON output schema specifying structured primitive labels and natural-language instructions.
 - The instructions are designed to be compact and vision-agnostic, describing action type, direction, and optional object status, while avoiding appearance or scene-specific details.
4. **VLM-based Temporal Segmentation**: Qwen-3-VL-Plus is utilized as a proposal generator. Given the prompt and video inputs, the VLM outputs a JSON list of candidate segments, each containing a primitive label, a vision-agnostic instruction, and start/end timestamps. Visual observations are explicitly removed from these segments to form the LA dataset.
5. **Human Verification**: The VLM-generated candidates undergo a human verification process. Annotators inspect the video clip, primitive label, and instruction for each segment, assigning a quality score (0-3). Segments with a quality score of 2 or higher are retained, ensuring high-quality, mutually consistent segments. This process filtered 56,899 candidates to 33,116 final LA episodes (LA-33K), reducing the average episode length from 287.83 to 46.05 frames, thereby increasing the temporal density of language supervision. Inter-annotator agreement measured by Krippendorff’s alpha was 0.7794.

#### 3.2. LA4VLA-1B Model and Pretraining Paradigms

The study uses LA4VLA-1B, a 1B-parameter VLA model based on InternVL3-1B with a flow matching action head for continuous action prediction. To evaluate the impact of LA pretraining, five distinct pretraining paradigms are investigated:

1. **Base (No Pretraining)**: The policy is directly finetuned on downstream task data without any prior pretraining.
2. **LA Pretraining**: The model is pretrained solely on the LA-33K dataset, where visual observations are masked out. It predicts action trajectories from language instructions and proprioceptive robot states. This is followed by downstream finetuning.
3. **VLA Pretraining**: The model is pretrained on LA-33K-V, a VLA-format counterpart of LA-33K that includes the original visual observations for each atomic episode. This is followed by downstream finetuning.
4. **LA-VLA Pretraining**: A sequential approach where the model first undergoes LA pretraining on LA-33K, then proceeds to VLA pretraining on LA-33K-V, before final downstream finetuning.
5. **MixPT Pretraining**: In this paradigm, LA-33K (vision-agnostic) and LA-33K-V (VLA format) data are jointly used within a single mixed pretraining stage, followed by downstream finetuning.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup and Evaluation

The efficacy of LA4VLA is assessed across several dimensions:

- **Simulation Benchmarks**: Evaluated on MetaWorld (easy, medium, hard, very hard tasks) and LIBERO (spatial, object, goal, long-horizon tasks).
- **Real-World Manipulation**: Experiments conducted on an xArm6 robot with a parallel-jaw gripper and two RGB cameras. Three language-conditioned tasks are designed: Press Button, Place Book, and Place Drink, with 100 teleoperated demonstrations per task for finetuning.
- **Robustness Evaluation**: Policies finetuned for real-world tasks are re-evaluated under visual perturbations (Gaussian noise added to input images) to assess their stability.
- **Generalization across Architectures**: The LA pretraining protocol is also applied to StarVLA, another VLA architecture, to determine transferability of benefits.
- **Instruction-Following Behavior and Representation Analysis**: The initial diagnostic protocol from Section 3 is reapplied to LA-pretrained policies to quantitatively measure instruction following (Directional Alignment Rate, Direction Consistency Score, Separability Ratio, Silhouette Score) under masked visual input and conflicting robot states. t-SNE visualizations of the policy's internal representations are used to analyze instruction-conditioned clustering.

Throughout all experiments, efforts are made to ensure fair comparisons by keeping model architecture, training budget, optimization hyperparameters, downstream data, and finetuning protocols consistent, varying only the pretraining paradigm under investigation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding the effectiveness and characteristics of language-action (LA) pretraining:

#### 4.1. Initial Instruction-Following Diagnostic (Section 3)

A diagnostic study with a standard VLA policy (built on InternVL3-1B) demonstrated its sensitivity to visual input changes.
- **Standard paired input (L, V+)**: Achieved high Directional Alignment Rate (DAR) of 0.98 and Direction Consistency Score (DCS) of 0.95, with clear endpoint separation (SR 2.35, SS 0.55).
- **Visual-removed input (L, ∅)**: DAR dropped to 0.63, DCS to 0.16, and SS to 0.04, indicating poor instruction following without visual cues.
- **Visual-unaligned input (L, ˜V)**: DAR 0.66, DCS 0.37, SS 0.05, showing disruption even with same-scene, unaligned visuals.
- **Visual-conflict input (L, V−)**: DAR dropped to 0.35 (below random choice), DCS to 0.03, indicating that conflicting visual cues biased predictions away from the language instruction.
These results confirmed that standard VLA training could lead to reliance on visual shortcuts rather than robust language grounding.

#### 4.2. Effect of Language-Action Pretraining on Downstream Performance (Section 5.2)

LA pretraining consistently improved downstream manipulation success rates for LA4VLA-1B compared to no-pretraining (Base) and VLA-only pretraining (using LA-33K-V data).
- **MetaWorld**: LA pretraining achieved an average success rate of 83.00%, surpassing VLA pretraining (79.78%) and the Base policy (69.73%). This represents a 13.27 percentage point (pp) gain over Base.
- **LIBERO**: LA pretraining achieved 95.30%, exceeding VLA pretraining (94.40%) and Base (92.85%), with a 2.45 pp gain over Base.
These results indicate that vision-agnostic LA supervision is more effective than VLA pretraining under the matched atomic-action pretraining setting.

#### 4.3. Integrating Language-Action Supervision with VLA Pretraining (Section 5.3)

Combining LA supervision with VLA pretraining further enhanced performance.
- **MetaWorld**: Both LA-VLA (sequential) and MixPT (mixed) paradigms improved upon LA pretraining alone. MixPT achieved the highest average success rate at 87.53%, followed by LA-VLA at 86.75%. MixPT showed a 17.80 pp gain over the Base policy.
- **LIBERO**: LA-VLA achieved 96.28% and MixPT achieved 95.75%, both outperforming LA pretraining alone (95.30%). LA-VLA showed a 3.43 pp gain over Base.
This demonstrates that LA supervision is complementary to VLA pretraining, with both sequential and mixed integration strategies being effective.

#### 4.4. Generalization to Different VLA Architectures (Section 5.4)

The benefits of LA pretraining extended to the StarVLA architecture.
- **MetaWorld (StarVLA)**: LA pretraining increased the average success rate from 58.39% (no pretraining) to 69.91%, an 11.52 pp gain, with notable improvements in hard and very hard tasks.
- **LIBERO (StarVLA)**: LA pretraining increased the average success rate from 93.70% to 94.85%, a 1.15 pp gain, particularly in long-horizon tasks.
These findings suggest the transferability of LA supervision across different VLA model architectures.

#### 4.5. Real-World Manipulation Performance (Section 5.5)

LA pretraining significantly improved real-world manipulation capabilities.
- **Average across three tasks (Press Button, Place Book, Place Drink)**: The MixPT paradigm achieved an average success rate of 83.3%, followed by LA pretraining at 81.7%, VLA pretraining at 48.3%, and no pretraining at 38.3%.
- MixPT resulted in a 45.0 pp improvement over the no-pretraining baseline.
These results demonstrate that LA pretraining translates into stronger performance on physical robots.

#### 4.6. Robustness to Visual Perturbations (Section 5.6)

LA pretraining enhanced policy robustness when visual inputs were degraded with Gaussian noise.
- **Average across two tasks (Press Button, Place Drink)**: MixPT achieved 70.0% average success, and LA pretraining achieved 67.5%. These compare to VLA pretraining at 42.5% and no pretraining at 27.5%.
LA and MixPT policies maintained substantially higher success rates under visual noise, supporting the hypothesis that language-conditioned action priors reduce over-reliance on visual cues.

#### 4.7. Instruction-Following Behavior and Representation Analysis (Section 5.7)

Analysis of LA-pretrained policies revealed improved instruction-following and distinct internal representations.
- **Direction-following**: With masked visual input, the LA-pretrained policy exhibited robust instruction following. Under the "LA-pretrained" condition (original instruction and robot state), DCS was 0.85 and DAR was 0.99. Even under the "Conflicting-state" condition (robot state from an opposite-direction action), DCS remained high at 0.80 and DAR at 0.95. This indicates that the policy strongly adheres to language instructions, even when other non-visual inputs might be conflicting.
- **Representation Analysis**: t-SNE visualizations of action-decoding representations showed that LA-pretrained policies formed clearer clusters aligned with commanded directions, with opposite commands falling into distinct regions. In contrast, VLA-trained policies' representations were more grouped by robot state, with mixed command directions. This suggests that LA pretraining induces a more instruction-conditioned action representation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents LA4VLA, a framework for language-action pretraining, which contributes to the field of robotic manipulation by addressing a recognized limitation in existing Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models: the entanglement and dilution of language-action supervision within visually dominant training paradigms.

The significance of this work can be understood through several points:

1. **Enhanced VLA Policy Robustness**: The framework facilitates the development of VLA policies that exhibit greater robustness to visual variations and degraded visual observations. This is crucial for real-world robot deployment, where environmental conditions can be unpredictable and visual inputs imperfect. The demonstrated resilience under visual noise indicates a more stable foundation for robot control.
2. **Improved Language-Action Grounding**: By explicitly removing visual observations during pretraining, LA4VLA encourages policies to learn intrinsic action dynamics, temporal consistency, and language-conditioned motion patterns. This leads to a stronger grounding of actions in language, making policies less susceptible to "visual shortcuts" and more reliably guided by instructions.
3. **Cost-Effective Dataset Creation**: The LA-33K dataset is generated by reorganizing and re-annotating existing robot demonstrations, rather than requiring new, expensive data collection. This approach provides a practical and scalable method for creating high-quality, dense language-action supervision without incurring significant additional resource costs.
4. **Complementary Pretraining Strategy**: The findings establish language-action pretraining as an effective and complementary strategy for VLA model development. Its benefits are evident whether applied alone, sequentially before VLA pretraining, or mixed with VLA data. This suggests that future VLA architectures could integrate LA pretraining to yield further performance gains.
5. **Generalizability Across Architectures and Tasks**: The demonstrated improvements generalize across different VLA architectures (LA4VLA-1B and StarVLA) and a range of simulation benchmarks (MetaWorld, LIBERO) and real-world manipulation tasks. This indicates the broad applicability and potential for widespread adoption of the LA pretraining paradigm.
6. **Insights into Internal Representations**: The analysis of internal policy representations and instruction-following behavior provides evidence that LA pretraining fosters a more instruction-conditioned understanding of actions. This contributes to a deeper understanding of how VLA models process and integrate multimodal information.

The potential impact of LA4VLA lies in its capacity to advance the development of more capable and reliable general-purpose robot manipulation systems. By fostering policies that better understand and respond to language instructions independently of specific visual cues, this work paves the way for robots that are more adaptable, robust, and intuitive to control in complex and dynamic environments. It suggests a fundamental shift in how language supervision can be leveraged to build stronger foundations for robot learning.
