# Generalized Semantic Segmentation by Self-Supervised Source Domain Projection and Multi-Level Contrastive Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The Domain Projection and Contrastive Learning (DPCL) framework enables generalized semantic segmentation by projecting target images into the source domain's appearance space and learning discriminative features through a multi-level contrastive approach. This method consistently outperforms state-of-the-art techniques on synthetic-to-real segmentation benchmarks without requiring target domain data during training.

## Problem

- Deep learning models for semantic segmentation suffer significant performance degradation when deployed in environments (target domains) that differ from their training data (source domain), a phenomenon known as domain shift.
- Domain shift manifests as differences in appearance (e.g., lighting, weather) or content (e.g., object layouts), leading to inaccurate dense pixel predictions.
- Existing domain generalization methods often struggle with severe domain shifts and may require complex training procedures or additional data.

## Method

- The ==Domain Projection and Contrastive Learning (DPCL)== framework is introduced, combining ==Self-Supervised Source Domain Projection (SSDP)== and ==Multi-Level Contrastive Learning (MLCL)==.
- SSDP learns to project images from any domain into the appearance space of the source domain through a self-supervised reconstruction task, trained using augmented source images.
- MLCL employs three complementary contrastive learning objectives (==pixel-to-pixel, pixel-to-class, and instance-to-class==) to learn discriminative and generalizable features at multiple levels of granularity.

## Results

- The DPCL framework consistently outperformed state-of-the-art domain generalization methods on **synthetic-to-real** semantic segmentation tasks, specifically when trained on GTAV and tested on Cityscapes, BDD100K, and Mapillary.
- Qualitative results demonstrate improved segmentation quality, producing more accurate and refined boundaries compared to baseline methods.
- Ablation studies confirmed that both SSDP and each component of MLCL contribute to the overall performance, with SSDP alone providing significant improvements and the full MLCL yielding the best results.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-Supervised Source Domain Projection (SSDP) effectively reduces the domain gap by transforming target domain images to match the appearance of the source domain while preserving semantic content.
> - Multi-Level Contrastive Learning (MLCL) with pixel-to-pixel, pixel-to-class, and instance-to-class objectives learns discriminative and generalizable features crucial for accurate segmentation across domains.
> - The combination of appearance projection (SSDP) and feature discrimination (MLCL) is essential for achieving superior generalization performance in semantic segmentation across diverse unseen domains.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Semantic segmentation is a fundamental computer vision task that involves classifying each pixel in an image into a predefined category. While deep learning models have achieved impressive results in this area, they typically suffer from significant performance degradation when deployed in environments that differ from their training data. This phenomenon, known as domain shift, presents a major challenge for real-world applications.

To address this issue, researchers have developed various domain adaptation and domain generalization techniques. Domain adaptation methods require access to target domain data during training, which is often impractical. Domain generalization approaches aim to create models that can generalize to unseen target domains without requiring target data during training, but current methods still struggle with significant domain shifts.

This paper introduces a novel Domain Projection and Contrastive Learning (DPCL) framework for generalized semantic segmentation. DPCL consists of two key components: Self-Supervised Source Domain Projection (SSDP) and Multi-Level Contrastive Learning (MLCL). Together, these components enable semantic segmentation models to achieve superior generalization performance across diverse unseen domains.

### 2. Domain Shift Challenge in Semantic Segmentation

Domain shift occurs when the distribution of test data differs from the training data. In semantic segmentation, this shift can manifest in several ways:

1. **Appearance shift**: Differences in lighting conditions, weather, camera settings, or image quality
2. **Content shift**: Variations in object appearances, layouts, or scene compositions
3. **Label space shift**: Discrepancies in the label definitions or annotation styles

These shifts are particularly problematic for semantic segmentation models, which need to make dense predictions for every pixel in an image. When a model trained on a source domain (e.g., synthetic images) is applied to a target domain (e.g., real-world images), the performance often degrades significantly.

Existing approaches to address domain shift include:

- **Normalization-based methods**: These standardize feature distributions to remove domain-specific information
- **Generalizable feature learning**: These aim to learn domain-invariant representations using techniques like attention mechanisms
- **Domain randomization**: These increase training data diversity by synthesizing images with different styles

While these approaches show promise, they often struggle with severe domain shifts and may require additional data or complex training procedures.

### 3. Self-Supervised Source Domain Projection

The first key component of DPCL is Self-Supervised Source Domain Projection (SSDP), which aims to reduce the domain gap by projecting target domain images into the source domain's appearance space.

SSDP works by learning a mapping function that transforms images from any domain to match the appearance of the source domain while preserving their semantic content. This is achieved through a self-supervised training approach:

1. Source domain images are augmented with various transformations (color jittering, blurring, etc.)
2. An encoder-decoder network learns to reconstruct the original source images from their augmented versions
3. The network uses instance normalization and affine transformations to transfer style information

The detailed architecture of SSDP is shown in Figure 3:

During training, SSDP learns to map augmented source images back to their original appearance using an L1 reconstruction loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{recon} = \|D(AdaIN(E(A(x)), G(f_x))) - x\|_1$$

where $x$ is a source image, $A(x)$ is its augmented version, $E$ is the encoder, $D$ is the decoder, $AdaIN$ is adaptive instance normalization, and $G(f_x)$ extracts style information.

At test time, when faced with an image from an unseen target domain, SSDP uses cluster centers of source data features to approximate the style information needed to project the target image into the source domain. This results in target images that have the appearance of source domain images while preserving their semantic content.

The visual results of this projection can be seen in Figures 6 and 7, which show how target domain images are effectively transformed to match the appearance of source domain images.

### 4. Multi-Level Contrastive Learning

While SSDP helps reduce domain shift, it does not explicitly address feature discrimination, which is crucial for accurate segmentation. The second key component of DPCL, Multi-Level Contrastive Learning (MLCL), addresses this by learning discriminative and generalizable features at multiple levels.

MLCL consists of three complementary contrastive learning objectives:

#### 4.1. Pixel-to-Pixel Contrastive Learning

This objective ensures that pixels of the same class have similar features, while pixels of different classes have distinct features. MLCL reformulates traditional contrastive learning using transition probability matrices:

The Jensen-Shannon divergence is used to measure the difference between the predicted transition probability matrix $\tilde{W}$ and the ground truth matrix $\tilde{L}$:

$$\mathcal{L}_{p2p} = JS(\tilde{W}, \tilde{L})$$

This approach is more efficient and effective than traditional contrastive learning methods, as shown in the analysis in Figure 5:

#### 4.2. Pixel-to-Class Contrastive Learning

This objective encourages pixel features to be similar to their corresponding class prototype (centroid in feature space):

$$\mathcal{L}_{p2c} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \log \frac{\exp(f_i \cdot \mu_{y_i} / \tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp(f_i \cdot \mu_j / \tau)}$$

where $f_i$ is the feature of pixel $i$, $\mu_{y_i}$ is the prototype of its class, and $\tau$ is a temperature parameter.

#### 4.3. Instance-to-Class Contrastive Learning

This objective preserves instance-level feature diversity by ensuring that instance prototypes (computed from connected regions) are correctly classified by class prototypes:

$$\mathcal{L}_{i2c} = \frac{1}{N_I} \sum_{i=1}^{N_I} \max(0, m + d(p_i, \mu_{y_i}) - \min_{j \neq y_i} d(p_i, \mu_j))$$

where $p_i$ is an instance prototype, $\mu_{y_i}$ is its class prototype, and $m$ is a margin parameter.

The complete MLCL loss combines these three objectives:

$$\mathcal{L}_{MLCL} = \mathcal{L}_{p2p} + \lambda_1 \mathcal{L}_{p2c} + \lambda_2 \mathcal{L}_{i2c}$$

Additionally, a divergence loss is applied to encourage class prototypes to be distinct:

$$\mathcal{L}_{div} = -\frac{1}{K(K-1)} \sum_{i=1}^{K} \sum_{j \neq i}^{K} \|cos(\mu_i, \mu_j)\|_2^2$$

### 5. Implementation Details

The complete DPCL framework combines SSDP and MLCL in a two-stage training process:

1. **Pre-training SSDP**: The SSDP module is pre-trained using the reconstruction loss.
2. **Training the segmentation model**: With SSDP parameters frozen, the segmentation model is trained using a combination of task loss (cross-entropy), MLCL loss, and divergence loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{CE} + \alpha \mathcal{L}_{MLCL} + \beta \mathcal{L}_{div}$$

During training, a warm-up period is used to avoid feature mode collapse. Class prototypes are updated using a moving average strategy.

At test time, target images are first projected using SSDP, and then segmented using the trained model. The final prediction combines the results from the classifier and the class prototypes:

$$P_{\text{final}} = \frac{1}{2}(P_{\text{classifier}} + P_{\text{prototype}})$$

Additionally, a test-time adaptation scheme is proposed, which iteratively refines the predictions by minimizing the pixel-to-pixel contrastive loss.

### 6. Experimental Results

DPCL was evaluated on the domain generalization task of semantic segmentation, specifically on the synthetic-to-real scenario:

- **Source domain**: GTAV (synthetic driving scenes)
- **Target domains**: Cityscapes, BDD100K, and Mapillary (real driving scenes)

The model was trained only on GTAV and tested on the three real-world datasets without any fine-tuning or access to target data during training.

DPCL consistently outperformed state-of-the-art domain generalization methods across all target domains. Qualitative results show significantly improved segmentation quality:

The results demonstrate that DPCL effectively addresses domain shift in semantic segmentation by:
1. Reducing appearance differences through source domain projection
2. Learning discriminative and generalizable features through multi-level contrastive learning

### 7. Ablation Studies

Comprehensive ablation studies were conducted to analyze the contribution of each component:

1. **Effect of SSDP**: SSDP alone significantly improved performance by reducing domain shift.
2. **Effect of MLCL**: Each level of contrastive learning contributed to the overall performance, with the best results achieved using all three levels.
3. **Effect of prototype-based prediction**: Combining classifier and prototype predictions provided more reliable results.
4. **Effect of test-time adaptation**: The proposed adaptation scheme further improved performance by iteratively refining predictions.

The studies also compared different contrastive learning formulations and verified the effectiveness of the approach across different network backbones (ResNet50, ShuffleNetV2, MobileNetV2).

### 8. Conclusion

This paper presents Domain Projection and Contrastive Learning (DPCL), a novel framework for generalized semantic segmentation. DPCL addresses domain shift through two complementary components:

1. **Self-Supervised Source Domain Projection (SSDP)**: Reduces domain gap by projecting target images to match source domain appearance
2. **Multi-Level Contrastive Learning (MLCL)**: Learns discriminative and generalizable features at pixel, instance, and class levels

Extensive experiments demonstrate that DPCL significantly outperforms existing methods on challenging synthetic-to-real segmentation tasks. The approach does not require auxiliary datasets or complex training procedures, making it practical for real-world applications.

The DPCL framework represents an important advancement in domain generalization for semantic segmentation, enabling models to maintain high performance across diverse unseen domains. This capability is crucial for deploying segmentation models in real-world scenarios where the test environment may differ significantly from the training data.
