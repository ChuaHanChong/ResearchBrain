# Static In, Dynamic Out: Counterfactual Action Augmentation for Moving Object Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Institute of Technology developed SIDO, a counterfactual action augmentation method that enables visuomotor policies trained solely on static object demonstrations to manipulate moving objects robustly. This approach significantly improves success rates on dynamic tasks, achieving 0.90 on a simulated Stack task and 0.60 on a real-world Peachtree task, by generating anticipatory actions and maintaining in-distribution observations without requiring costly dynamic data collection.

## Problem

- Visuomotor policies trained on readily available static object demonstrations fail when target objects are in motion during real-world deployment.
- Collecting extensive, high-quality dynamic object manipulation demonstrations is expensive, time-consuming, and often limited to specific motion profiles, hindering generalization.
- Existing policies struggle with dynamic environments due to action mismatch for moving targets and out-of-distribution observations caused by robot-object misalignment.

## Method

- **SIDO** (STATIC IN, DYNAMIC OUT) is introduced as a ==counterfactual action augmentation== technique that transforms static demonstrations into data suitable for training policies that anticipate object motion.
- The problem is factorized into an object pose prediction module and a ==goal-conditioned manipulation policy==, allowing each component to be trained with less expensive data.
- Counterfactual training samples are generated by virtually displacing objects to a 'counterfactual future position' and morphing expert actions to maintain a ==hand-object relative pose invariant==, using either a heuristic (H-SIDO) or a dynamics-aware (DynaSIDO) approach.

## Results

- SIDO consistently improved success rates on moving object tasks (e.g., **0.90** on simulated Stack x-axis, **0.60** on real-world Peachtree dynamic) while preserving or enhancing performance on static tasks.
- SIDO-trained policies effectively preserved the hand-object relative pose at the action terminus, leading to superior object tracking (e.g., **0.42** tracking slope on Gantry x-axis vs. **0.10** for baselines) and in-distribution observations.
- The method demonstrated transferability across different policy architectures (Diffusion Policy, Equivariant Diffusion Policy) and successfully leveraged more accurate object pose predictors to further improve task success (e.g., **0.40** success rate with FERNN predictor on Gantry random task).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Moving object manipulation can be effectively decoupled into predicting future object pose and executing an action to achieve a specific hand-object relative pose at that predicted future location.
> - Policies can learn anticipatory behavior from static data by providing temporal variation through an explicit geometric prior (hand-object relative pose invariant) during training.
> - The modular design, separating policy training from object pose prediction, enables plug-and-play integration of various predictors and allows for policy-agnostic application.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of visuomotor manipulation has advanced considerably through the application of imitation learning, particularly with the emergence of diffusion- and transformer-based policies. These methods have demonstrated proficiency in solving complex, contact-rich tasks when trained on extensive datasets of robot demonstrations. A foundational assumption underlying much of this success is that the target object remains stationary throughout the robot's execution of a task.

However, real-world deployment scenarios frequently violate this assumption. Objects requiring manipulation may be in motion due to external factors, such as parts drifting on conveyor belts in industrial settings or fruits swaying in the wind in agricultural contexts. Existing research has explored several avenues related to this challenge, but without comprehensively addressing the specific problem of adapting policies trained solely on static demonstrations to dynamic environments.

Reactive policies aim to reduce latency between perception and action, thereby enabling quicker responses to environmental changes. However, these policies typically assume that their training data already encompasses the motion regimes they will encounter at deployment. Counterfactual data augmentation methods generate synthetic training samples along various axes (e.g., spatial or semantic) to enhance robustness, but generally do not account for the forward-in-time object displacement characteristic of moving targets. Similarly, equivariant policies encode spatial symmetries to improve generalization to varying object poses, yet they do not inherently address the temporal motion observed in dynamic scenes.

A more direct approach, exemplified by collecting large-scale dynamic demonstrations and training dynamics-aware policies, has proven effective. Nevertheless, this strategy is constrained by the high cost and safety concerns associated with collecting moving object data, and the resulting policies are often limited to the specific motion profiles present in the collected demonstrations. In contrast, vast datasets of static object demonstrations are already available across the research community, representing a valuable resource if they can be leveraged for dynamic tasks.

This work positions itself to bridge the gap between policies trained on readily available static object demonstrations and the demands of real-world dynamic manipulation. It proposes a method that allows policies to adapt to unseen object motion at test time without requiring new, expensive dynamic data collection, thereby extending the utility of existing static datasets.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable visuomotor policies, which have been trained exclusively on static object manipulation demonstrations, to effectively operate on target objects that are in motion during execution. This adaptation is intended to occur without requiring additional demonstrations of moving objects, whose motion patterns may be entirely new and unseen during the initial training phase.

The motivation for this objective stems from several practical and research-oriented considerations:

1. **Mismatch between Training Assumptions and Real-World Conditions:** A significant disparity exists between the controlled, static environments often used for robot training and the dynamic nature of many real-world applications. Industrial assembly lines, agricultural harvesting, and household assistance frequently involve objects that are not perfectly stationary. Policies trained under static assumptions are prone to failure when deployed in these common dynamic scenarios.

2. **Cost and Difficulty of Dynamic Demonstration Collection:** Collecting teleoperated demonstrations of robots manipulating moving objects is inherently more complex, time-consuming, and potentially less safe than collecting static demonstrations. Such data collection often requires specialized setups to induce and control object motion, and human operators must precisely anticipate object trajectories, which is a demanding task. Furthermore, any collected dynamic dataset is typically limited to a finite set of motion profiles, restricting the policy's generalizability to novel movements.

3. **Abundance of Static Demonstration Data:** In contrast to dynamic data, there is an increasing availability of large-scale datasets comprising static object manipulation demonstrations. These datasets are comparatively easier and cheaper to acquire. The ability to leverage this existing resource for dynamic tasks would significantly enhance data efficiency and reduce the overall cost of developing robust robotic systems.

4. **Addressing Core Failure Modes in Dynamic Environments:** Standard behavior cloning policies fail in dynamic environments for two main reasons:
 - **Action Mismatch:** Actions learned from static demonstrations are designed for a stationary target. When the object moves, these actions will target the object's past or current position, leading to a miss by the time the action chunk is executed. Anticipatory actions are absent in static training data.
 - **Out-of-Distribution Observations:** Following an action mismatch, the robot's end-effector will often be misaligned with the moving object, causing subsequent observations (e.g., camera views of the object and gripper) to deviate significantly from the training distribution. This can compound errors and lead to further policy failures.

To address these points, the authors propose a core insight: moving object manipulation can be factorized into two distinct sub-problems. First, predicting the object's future pose a short time horizon ahead, which is a pose-forecasting problem that can be solved with object trajectory data (without robot teleoperation). Second, executing an action chunk to achieve a specific hand-object relative pose at that predicted future position, which is a goal-conditioned reaching problem that the static object demonstrations already cover. By decoupling these problems, each component can be trained with data that is inexpensive to collect in isolation, thereby avoiding the need for costly moving object teleoperation.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, named STATIC IN, DYNAMIC OUT (SIDO), is a counterfactual action augmentation technique designed to transform static object demonstrations into data suitable for training policies that can adapt to unseen object motion at deployment. SIDO operates by factorizing the problem of moving object manipulation into an object pose prediction module and a goal-conditioned manipulation policy.

**Problem Setup:**
The robot's state includes end-effector position, orientation, and gripper state ($s_t$). Observations ($o_t$) comprise agent view images ($I_{agent_t}$), wrist view images ($I_{wrist_t}$), robot proprioception ($s_t$), and the object's position ($P_t$). A behavior-cloning policy $\pi_\theta$ maps $o_t$ to a chunk of $T_p$ actions. During training, $N$ static object demonstrations $D_{train}$ are provided, where the object position $P_t^{(i)}$ is constant ($P_{static}^{(i)}$) before grasping. At deployment, the object follows an unknown trajectory $\xi(t)$, meaning $P_t = \xi(t)$, and deployment observations $o_t^{dep}$ are likely out of the training distribution.

**Hand-Object Invariant:**
A core concept in SIDO is the "hand-object relative pose" at offset $k$, defined as $r_t(k) = p_{eef_{t+k}} - P_{t+k}$. In static demonstrations, this relative pose remains constant throughout a pre-grasp window. SIDO's augmentation aims to preserve this invariant constraint at two critical horizons: $T_a$ (the action horizon, when re-planning occurs) and $T_p$ (the prediction horizon, the terminus of the current action chunk).

**Counterfactual Augmentation (Training Time):**
For each static training transition, SIDO generates a counterfactual pair $(\tilde{o}_t, \tilde{a}_{t:t+T_p-1})$ that satisfies the invariant constraint.
1. **Counterfactual Displacement and Observation:** A displacement $\delta$ is sampled (e.g., uniform direction, magnitude $vT_p$). With probability $\alpha$ (e.g., 0.2), the original static sample is used to preserve static object performance. Otherwise, the object is virtually displaced by $\delta$ to a "counterfactual future position" $\tilde{P}_{t+T_p} = P_{static} + \delta$. The observation $\tilde{o}_t$ is then constructed by keeping the original images ($I_{agent_t}, I_{wrist_t}$) but replacing the object's static position in the dedicated observation channel with $\tilde{P}_{t+T_p}$. This simulates the deployment scenario where a predictor provides a future pose.
2. **Counterfactual Action Generation:** The expert action chunk $a_{t:t+T_p-1}$ is "morphed" into $\tilde{a}_{t:t+T_p-1}$ to maintain the hand-object relative pose given the displaced object. Two variants are proposed:
 - **Heuristic SIDO (H-SIDO):** For absolute actions (world-frame waypoints), each waypoint $a_{t+k}$ is shifted by $\rho_k \delta$, where $\rho_k$ is a linear ramp function increasing from 0 at $k=0$ to 1 at $k \ge T_a-1$. This distributes the displacement across the chunk. For relative actions (step increments), the ramp is applied to the cumulative path (waypoints) indexed by arc length, and then differenced back to relative actions, preserving trajectory shape.
 - **Dynamics-aware SIDO (DynaSIDO):** This method refines the heuristic chunk to account for the robot's closed-loop controller dynamics.
 - **Dynamics Model:** A residual MLP $f_\psi$ is trained on expert transitions to predict the next robot state $\hat{s}_{t+1}$ given current state $\bar{s}_t$ (augmented with velocity/acceleration) and action $a_t$. This model captures the robot's controller dynamics, as the object is static during training.
 - **Targets and MPPI Refinement:** The invariant constraint defines target end-effector positions for the displaced object: $p_{tgt_{t+T_a}} = p_{eef_{t+T_a}} + \delta$ and $p_{tgt_{t+T_p}} = p_{eef_{t+T_p}} + \delta$. Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control is then used to search for an action chunk $\tilde{a}$ whose $f_\psi$-rollout minimizes a cost function $J(\tilde{a})$ that penalizes deviations from these targets. The H-SIDO chunk serves as an initial seed for the MPPI search.

**Object Pose Predictor (Deployment Time):**
At deployment, the policy requires the object's predicted future position $\hat{P}_{t+T_p}$. SIDO is designed to be plug-and-play with any suitable object pose predictor. This decoupling means the policy is trained once, and different predictors can be swapped in based on the deployment regime without retraining the policy. The paper evaluates:
- **Finite-difference predictor:** Extrapolates future position based on recent pose history (last three observations).
- **MLP predictor:** A learned model regressing future position from a history of 8 object pose observations.
- **Flow-Equivariant Recurrent Network (FERNN):** A learned model that incorporates equivariance to object motion for improved generalization across velocities, also using an 8-step history.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Policy Classes:** SIDO was evaluated with two policy architectures to test its policy-agnostic nature: Diffusion Policy (DP) and Equivariant Diffusion Policy (EquiDP).
- **Tasks:**
 - **Simulation:** Three MimicGen tasks (Mug, Square, Stack) in robosuite environment, using five object motion patterns (static, x-axis, y-axis, circle, random) at 2 cm/s.
 - **Real-world:** Two tasks on DROID setup (Franka Emika Panda arm): Gantry (pick and place cylindrical capsule) and Peachtree (pick peach from swaying tree). Objects moved at 1.5 cm/s with various motion patterns.
- **Baselines:** Standard DP (no object pose input), DP (current object pose as input), and DP+AC (test-time action compensation by shifting commanded action by object's current displacement). EquiDP variants of these baselines were also tested.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments were designed to test three main hypotheses, with results reported across simulated and real-world tasks.

**H1: SIDO improves moving object success while preserving static object performance.**
Across all evaluated simulated tasks (Mug, Square, Stack) and real-world tasks (Gantry, Peachtree), SIDO consistently demonstrated improved success rates on tasks involving moving objects compared to the tested baselines. Simultaneously, its performance on static object tasks was maintained or closely matched that of the baselines.

- **Simulation:** For example, on the Stack task under x-axis motion, DP+SIDO achieved a success rate of 0.90, significantly higher than DP (cur. pose) at 0.15. Similar gains were observed across other motion patterns and tasks.
- **Real-world:** On the Gantry x-axis dynamic task, DP+SIDO reached 0.55 success, outperforming the best baseline (DP cur. pose) at 0.35. On Peachtree dynamic, DP+SIDO achieved 0.60 success, compared to 0.45 for DP (cur. pose).
- **Baseline Analysis:** The baselines exhibited limitations: DP without object pose input failed significantly in dynamic settings (e.g., 0.10 on Gantry x-axis dynamic). Providing the current object pose (DP cur. pose) improved performance but was insufficient for anticipatory action. DP+AC (action compensation) often performed poorly, sometimes even worse than plain DP, suggesting that a simple test-time correction is not effectively integrated by the policy's controller.
- **Performance Preservation:** SIDO maintained static object performance, often matching baseline success rates, and even topped them in some cases (e.g., Gantry random at 1.00). This is attributed to the inclusion of unchanged static samples (with probability $\alpha$) during augmentation, ensuring the policy remains proficient in static conditions. SIDO's benefit was particularly pronounced in scenarios where objects might move out of the wrist camera's field of view, as SIDO's anticipation helps keep the object centered.

**H2: SIDO preserves the hand-object relative pose at the action terminus.**
This hypothesis asserts that SIDO's training methodology leads the policy to command gripper movements that track the moving object, preserving the critical hand-object relative pose. This was verified by analyzing the correlation between the commanded end-effector (EEF) position and the observed object position.

- **Tracking Slope:** On the Gantry x-axis task, the least-squares slope ($\beta_x$) of the commanded EEF x-position relative to the object's x-position at action terminus $T_a$ was calculated. An ideal tracking slope of 1 indicates perfect one-for-one tracking. Expert static demonstrations showed a $\beta_x$ of 0.62. DP+SIDO achieved 0.42, which was the closest to the expert reference among all methods. Baselines trailed, with DP at 0.31, DP+AC at 0.24, and DP (cur. pose) lowest at 0.10. This indicates that SIDO's augmentation effectively imbues the policy with anticipatory behavior, unlike baselines that either ignore motion or react too slowly.
- **In-Distribution Observations:** t-SNE embeddings of pre-grasp wrist camera frames showed that DP+SIDO's frames overlapped well with the static training distribution, and its grasp-attempt view closely matched the training reference. In contrast, baseline methods lagged the object, causing their observations to drift to distinct regions of the encoder space, leading to misses.

**H3: SIDO transfers across policy classes and predictor choices.**
SIDO was designed to be policy-agnostic and modular with respect to the object pose predictor.

- **Policy Agnosticism:** SIDO demonstrated consistent benefits for both Diffusion Policy (DP) and Equivariant Diffusion Policy (EquiDP) variants. It was the strongest DP variant across all dynamic conditions on all three simulation tasks and the strongest EquiDP variant on Square and Stack. An exception was noted for EquiDP+SIDO on Mug, where the commanded-vs-executed trajectory gap might still impact precision-critical grasps.
- **Predictor Plug-and-Play:** On the real-world Gantry random task, DP+SIDO was evaluated with three different object pose predictors while keeping the policy fixed: Finite-difference, MLP, and FERNN.
 - FERNN, which attained the lowest Final Displacement Error (FDE) of 1.77 cm, also achieved the highest success rate (0.40). The MLP and Finite-difference predictors had higher FDEs (2.21 cm and 2.97 cm, respectively) and lower success rates (0.25 for both). This correlation demonstrates that SIDO effectively leverages a more accurate object pose prediction to improve task success.

**Comparison of H-SIDO and DynaSIDO:**
An analysis of the two action generation methods revealed that at low object speeds (e.g., 2 cm/s deployment speed), H-SIDO's heuristic ramp closely approximated DynaSIDO's performance in terms of achieving the invariant constraint, trajectory smoothness, and end-to-end task success. However, as object speed increased, DynaSIDO significantly outperformed H-SIDO, maintaining higher success rates (e.g., a +0.20 gain at 6 cm/s on Stack x-axis). DynaSIDO's dynamics-aware refinement helps maintain trackability of commanded chunks at higher speeds.

**SIDO vs. Training on Moving Object Demonstrations:**
Comparing SIDO (trained on 64 static demonstrations) against a policy trained directly on 64 moving object demonstrations for the Gantry random task showed SIDO to be superior in both collection efficiency and task performance.
- **Collection Cost:** Moving object demonstrations were more expensive to collect (lower teleoperation success rate of 59.8% vs. 98.5% for static, and longer wall clock time).
- **Performance:** SIDO achieved higher success rates (1.00 static, 0.25 dynamic) than the moving object baseline (0.80 static, 0.05 dynamic). The moving object baseline's lower static performance was attributed to the anticipatory offset learned, which overshoots static targets. Its lower dynamic performance was linked to limited coverage of motion directions and workspace extent in the collected dynamic demonstrations.

**SIDO under Varying Object Velocity:**
SIDO demonstrated extensibility to scenarios with varying object velocities. By training the policy with a range of displacement magnitudes (sampling uniformly from $[0, \delta]$) and employing FERNN as the predictor, a single DP+SIDO policy could handle multiple speeds (1.5, 3.0, 4.5 cm/s) and a condition where velocity changed mid-rollout, performing comparably or better than single-speed conditions. This suggests SIDO induces an approximate Galilean equivariance, adapting to changes in the object's inertial reference frame through predicted goals and morphed actions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces STATIC IN, DYNAMIC OUT (SIDO), a method that addresses a notable limitation in current visuomotor manipulation policies: their reliance on static target objects during training and deployment. The significance and potential impact of this work are manifold:

1. **Enabling Dynamic Manipulation with Static Data:** SIDO demonstrates a method to leverage readily available and cost-effective static object demonstrations for robust manipulation of moving objects. This directly tackles the prohibitive expense, safety concerns, and inherent limitations of collecting extensive dynamic demonstration datasets. By transforming static data into anticipatory supervision via counterfactual action augmentation, SIDO significantly improves data efficiency for dynamic tasks.

2. **Increased Robustness and Generalizability:** The ability of SIDO-trained policies to adapt to unseen object motion patterns at test time enhances the robustness and generalizability of robotic systems. This is crucial for deploying robots in less structured and more dynamic real-world environments, where object movements are common and often unpredictable.

3. **Modular and Flexible Architecture:** The factorization of the problem into an object pose predictor and a goal-conditioned policy creates a modular system. The policy, once trained with SIDO's augmentation, can be paired with various plug-and-play object pose predictors without requiring retraining. This flexibility allows for easy adaptation to different sensing capabilities or prediction algorithms as they evolve, reducing development overhead.

4. **Conceptual Contribution to Imitation Learning:** SIDO introduces the concept of providing temporal variation through an explicit geometric prior (the hand-object relative pose invariant) at training time, rather than solely through dynamic demonstrations. This re-casts the dynamic problem as one of goal displacement within the policy's action space, offering a generalizable framework for handling moving targets. The work shows that the gap between static demonstrations and dynamic deployment can be closed by enriching the training data with carefully constructed counterfactuals.

5. **Practical Applications:** The implications of SIDO extend to various practical domains:
 - **Manufacturing and Logistics:** Robots on assembly lines or in warehouses could more effectively pick and place parts moving on conveyor belts.
 - **Agriculture:** Harvesting robots could manipulate swaying fruits or vegetables more reliably.
 - **Service Robotics:** Household robots could handle objects in dynamic environments, such as items being moved or falling.

6. **Advancement in Visuomotor Control:** By preserving the hand-object relative pose and enabling the commanded gripper to track moving objects, SIDO helps maintain the policy's observations within its training distribution during dynamic rollouts. This avoids compounding errors and enhances the stability of visuomotor control in dynamic settings. The demonstrated ability to handle varying object velocities further underscores its potential for real-world scenarios.

In summary, SIDO represents a significant step towards practical and adaptable robot manipulation in dynamic environments, offering a strategy to bridge the gap between ideal training conditions and the complexities of real-world deployment through intelligent data augmentation and problem factorization.
