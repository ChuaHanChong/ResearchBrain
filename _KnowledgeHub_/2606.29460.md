# Understanding LLM Intervention Explanations in Multi-Party Human-Robot Interaction

> [!summary] Summary
> This study from Politecnico di Milano and ETH Zurich characterizes the textual explanations generated by an LLM orchestrator for its intervention decisions in multi-party human-robot interactions. It identifies five recurring themes in these explanations, such as "Managing Agreement" and "Participation Balancing," and reveals how differentiated robot roles influence the specific intervention types deployed by the LLM.

## Problem

- The application of LLMs in multi-party human-robot interaction (HRI) settings, involving multiple humans and robots, is underexplored despite being common in real-world scenarios.
- The decision-making mechanisms of LLM-driven systems regarding when, how, and why to intervene in conversations often remain opaque, hindering interpretability in real-time HRI.
- There is limited understanding of how varying robot social configurations (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous roles) influence the types of intervention explanations produced by an LLM orchestrator.

## Method

- An experimental setup was designed where an ==LLM-based orchestrator== controlled two robots (Furhat and Nao) interacting with three human participants in a collaborative discussion task.
- Two experimental conditions were implemented: a ==Homogeneous (HOM)== condition where both robots were "movers," and a ==Heterogeneous (HET)== condition where one robot was a "mover" and the other an "opposer."
- Conversational logs were collected over 30 turns, including the LLM's ==binary intervention decision== and its generated textual explanation for each decision, forming a dataset of 610 instances.

## Results

- The most frequent explanation themes were "Managing Agreement" (**202** instances) and "Participation Balancing" (**167** instances) out of **610** total intervention explanations.
- The overall intervention explanation patterns and the orchestrator's likelihood to intervene did not significantly differ between the homogeneous and heterogeneous robot role configurations.
- In heterogeneous configurations, the "mover" robot was selected more frequently for interventions (**158** instances) and primarily for "Managing Agreement" explanations (**85** instances), while the "opposer" (**83** instances) was more often linked to "Highlighting Negotiation Goals" (**58** instances), with these differences being statistically significant (**p < .001**).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - LLM orchestrator intervention explanations in multi-party HRI predominantly revolve around five functional themes: Managing Agreement, Participation Balancing, Highlighting Negotiation Goals, Maintaining Interaction, and Understanding Conversation State.
> - The LLM orchestrator primarily acts as a facilitator of group processes, focusing on promoting consensus, ensuring balanced participation, and guiding discussion toward negotiation goals.
> - Role differentiation in heterogeneous configurations leads the LLM to strategically deploy robots for complementary functions, with the 'mover' handling coordination and the 'opposer' challenging proposals and driving goal-oriented discussion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into social robots has enabled more naturalistic and dynamic human-robot interactions (HRI). Previous research has shown progress in dyadic HRI, where one human interacts with one robot. However, the application of LLMs in multi-party settings, involving multiple human participants and multiple robotic agents, remains an underexplored area. These complex environments, common in real-world scenarios such as classrooms or workplaces, introduce challenges related to robot coordination, conversational role distribution, turn-taking management, and collective decision-making. Empirical studies focusing on LLM-powered autonomous robots in such multi-agent interactions are limited.

Concurrently, the increasing use of LLMs in interactive systems has amplified concerns regarding interpretability and transparency. While LLM-based agents can generate sophisticated conversational behaviors, the internal mechanisms governing their decisions—specifically when, how, and why to intervene in a conversation, and which conversational goals to prioritize—often remain opaque. This opacity hinders the assessment of whether the system's behavior is aligned with user expectations or appropriate for socially sensitive contexts. Despite a broader interest in explainable AI (XAI), there is a limited body of work examining how LLM-driven systems justify their intervention decisions in real-time, multi-party HRI.

This research addresses these identified gaps by investigating the intervention explanations generated by an LLM-based orchestrator in a multi-party human-robot interaction scenario. It bridges the fields of LLM interpretability and multi-party HRI, contributing to the understanding of how autonomous, embodied LLM-driven systems articulate their decision-making processes in complex social environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this study was to characterize the intervention explanations generated by an LLM-based orchestrator in a multi-party interaction involving three human participants and two robots. The authors specifically focused on the textual explanations produced by the orchestrator for its decisions regarding whether and how to intervene at each conversational turn. The term "intervention explanations" was used to denote these generated justifications, distinguishing them from claims of accessing the model's internal reasoning processes.

The motivation for this investigation stems from several factors:
- **Addressing the multi-party HRI gap:** Real-world social interactions often involve multiple participants. Understanding how LLM-driven robots navigate and contribute to these complex dynamics is crucial for their effective deployment.
- **Improving LLM interpretability in embodied systems:** The opacity of LLM decision-making is a significant challenge. By analyzing generated explanations, the study aims to shed light on the system's operational logic in a dynamic, embodied context. This interpretability is vital for assessing system behavior, particularly in socially sensitive applications.
- **Investigating the impact of robot social configurations:** The study sought to determine whether varying the social roles assigned to robots (homogeneous vs. heterogeneous) influences the types of intervention explanations produced by the LLM orchestrator. This helps understand the system's adaptability and role-awareness.

To achieve these objectives, the study posed the following research questions:
- **RQ1: What types of intervention explanations characterize LLM-driven decisions in multi-party HRI?** This question aimed to inductively identify recurring patterns and themes within the LLM's justifications for intervening.
- **RQ2: How does the social configuration of robots influence the intervention explanations generated by the LLM orchestrator?** This overarching question was further broken down into two sub-questions:
 - **RQ2a: How do explanations differ between homogeneous (HOM) and heterogeneous (HET) configurations?** This aimed to compare explanation patterns when robots shared the same role versus when they had differentiated roles.
 - **RQ2b: Within HET configurations, how do explanations differ between the mover and opposer roles?** This focused on understanding whether distinct roles elicited different types of justifications from the orchestrator.

By addressing these questions, the research intended to contribute to the study of LLM-powered social robots by characterizing intervention explanation patterns in complex conversational settings and to advance research on explainability in interactive AI by demonstrating the utility of generated justifications for examining the behavior of embodied LLM-driven systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study employed a between-subjects experimental design to collect data on multi-party interactions.

**Experimental Setup and Participants:**
- **Participants:** 24 groups, totaling 66 university students, participated in the study.
- **Robots:** Each group interacted with two robotic agents: a Furhat robot and a Nao robot.
- **LLM Orchestrator:** An LLM-based orchestrator controlled both robots. At each conversational turn, it analyzed the group context, decided whether to intervene, selected which robot role to activate (mover or opposer), generated a textual response, and determined the addressee. For every decision, the orchestrator logged a textual explanation along with metadata (condition, role configuration, intervention status).
- **Task:** Groups were assigned a collaborative discussion task requiring them to reach a consensus on allocating rooms and associated costs in a shared apartment, with a total budget of 2,000 CHF.
- **Interaction Duration:** The conversation length was fixed at 30 turns, which corresponded to approximately 15 minutes. Groups unable to reach a decision within this timeframe were considered not to have achieved consensus.

**Experimental Conditions:**
Two experimental conditions were implemented to investigate the influence of robot social configurations:
1. **Homogeneous (HOM) Condition:** In this condition, both the Furhat and Nao robots were assigned the same conversational role: "mover." The mover role was designed to facilitate progress toward consensus in the group discussion.
2. **Heterogeneous (HET) Condition:** In contrast, this condition assigned differentiated roles to the robots. One robot acted as a "mover," encouraging agreement and convergence, while the other robot acted as an "opposer," challenging proposals and introducing alternative perspectives. Robot roles were counterbalanced across groups, and groups were randomly assigned to either the HOM or HET condition.

**Data Collection:**
The study collected conversational logs from the 24 groups, resulting in a dataset of 610 intervention decision instances generated by the LLM orchestrator. Each instance in the dataset comprised:
1. The conversational context at the point of decision.
2. The binary decision made by the orchestrator (intervene vs. not intervene).
3. The LLM-generated textual explanation justifying that decision.
These logs served as the primary data for the thematic analysis.

**Data Analysis:**
The collected data underwent two main types of analysis:

1. **Thematic Analysis (for RQ1):**
 - Thematic analysis was conducted on the 610 intervention explanations to identify and quantify prevalent explanation patterns.
 - A coding scheme was developed iteratively using a combination of inductive and deductive strategies.
 - An initial subset of explanations was reviewed to identify recurring patterns, which were then translated into fine-grained codes (e.g., "encouraging agreement," "balancing participation," "proposing solutions").
 - These codes were refined, merged, and operationally defined with inclusion and exclusion criteria.
 - One researcher coded the entire dataset, and a second researcher independently reviewed 20% of the data. An inter-rater agreement of 77% and a Cohen’s Kappa of 0.75 were achieved, with discrepancies resolved through consensus.
 - The 22 fine-grained codes were then consolidated into five broader themes representing overarching intervention explanation orientations.

2. **Dialogue Analysis (for RQ2a and RQ2b):**
 - **For RQ2a (HOM vs. HET comparison):** The thematic distributions identified in the thematic analysis were compared between the HOM and HET conditions. Additionally, the frequencies of intervention versus non-intervention decisions were compared across these conditions.
 - **For RQ2b (Mover vs. Opposer comparison in HET):** This analysis focused specifically on the HET condition. Intervention explanations and the characteristics of the generated utterances were examined for both the mover and opposer roles.
 - LLM utterances were characterized using a taxonomy of dialogue acts, adapted from existing taxonomies. This taxonomy distinguished between:
 - **Initiative acts:** Introducing new topics (e.g., factual questions, opinion questions, small talk, statements).
 - **Responsive acts:** Continuing existing conversation threads (e.g., acknowledgments, follow-ups, answers).
 - Addressees of the robot utterances were coded as either the whole group or a single individual.
 - Statistical comparisons, specifically two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests, were used to identify significant differences.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

**A. What types of intervention explanations characterize LLM-driven decisions in multi-party HRI? (RQ1)**
The thematic analysis of 610 coded instances identified 22 fine-grained codes, which were consolidated into five broader themes:

1. **Managing Agreement (n = 202):** This was the most frequent theme, largely comprising codes such as "proposing a final solution with wrapping up," "proposing a new solution," and "move on." It reflected the orchestrator's efforts to guide the discussion toward consensus, consolidate proposals, and signal readiness for resolution.
2. **Participation Balancing (n = 167):** The second most frequent theme, primarily driven by "wait for finishing thoughts," along with "encourage sharing/clarity" and "let discussion finished." This theme indicated the orchestrator's concern with regulating conversational pace and ensuring equitable participation among human interactants.
3. **Highlighting Negotiation Goals (n = 152):** This theme encompassed goal-directed explanations, including "spark discussion/question opinion," "pushing for equity/price fairness," and "encourage agreement." It highlighted the orchestrator's aim to influence the group's decision-making process by emphasizing fairness and eliciting opinions.
4. **Maintaining Interaction (n = 59):** This theme included explanations related to "echoing/summarizing" and "positive note." It represented structural, non-strategic contributions intended to sustain the conversational flow without directly influencing the discussion's content or participation dynamics.
5. **Understanding Conversation State (n = 30):** The least frequent theme, involving codes like "discussion initiation," "no assistance needed," and "clarification needed." These explanations indicated moments when the orchestrator focused on the meta-level state of the conversation, initiating discussions or signaling whether intervention was required.

Overall, the findings indicated that the LLM orchestrator's intervention explanations primarily revolved around promoting group consensus, ensuring balanced participation, and encouraging discussions on relevant topics, suggesting an orientation towards facilitating the negotiation process in multi-party HRI.

**B. How do intervention explanations differ between homogeneous and heterogeneous robot role configurations? (RQ2a)**
The analysis compared thematic distributions and intervention frequencies between the HOM and HET conditions:

- **Thematic Distribution:** "Managing Agreement" remained the most frequent theme in both HET (n = 107) and HOM (n = 95) conditions. "Participation Balancing" showed similar frequencies (HET: n = 83, HOM: n = 84). "Highlighting Negotiation Goals" appeared slightly more often in HET (n = 92) compared to HOM (n = 60), although this difference was not statistically significant. "Understanding Conversation State" also showed similar frequencies (HET: n = 16, HOM: n = 14).
- **Intervention Likelihood:** The orchestrator's propensity to intervene was similar across conditions. In the HOM condition, it intervened 226 times and refrained 65 times. In the HET condition, it intervened 241 times and refrained 78 times.

These results suggest that altering the robot role configuration (HOM vs. HET) did not significantly change the overall intervention explanation patterns or the orchestrator's likelihood to intervene.

**C. In heterogeneous configurations, how do intervention explanations differ between distinct robot roles (mover vs. opposer)? (RQ2b)**
This analysis focused on the HET condition to examine the differences between the mover and opposer roles:

- **Robot Selection for Intervention:** The mover role was selected more frequently for interventions (158 instances) compared to the opposer role (83 instances).
- **Theme Distribution by Role:**
 - The **mover** was predominantly selected for "Managing Agreement" (85 vs. 9 for opposer), "Participation Balancing" (18 vs. 11 for opposer), and "Maintaining Interaction" (16 vs. 4 for opposer).
 - The **opposer** was more frequently selected for "Highlighting Negotiation Goals" (58 vs. 29 for mover).
 - All these differences were statistically significant (p < .001, according to two-sided Mann–Whitney U tests).
- **Dialogue Acts and Addressees:**
 - Both roles primarily generated responsive utterances, but this tendency was significantly stronger for the opposer (57 out of 83 utterances) than for the mover (88 out of 158 utterances) (p = .0494).
 - The **mover** exhibited a relatively balanced distribution across dialogue act categories, contributing both initiative and responsive statements.
 - The **opposer** showed a clear skew towards responsive acts, particularly within "Managing Agreement" and "Highlighting Negotiation Goal" explanations.
 - The opposer utilized "Follow-Up" questions (questions extending an existing topic) more frequently, in 32.5% (27/83) of its utterances, compared to 20% (30/153) for the mover.

These findings indicate that the LLM orchestrator assigned complementary functions to the robots in the heterogeneous condition. The mover was primarily involved in coordination-oriented behaviors (e.g., managing agreement, balancing participation), while the opposer was deployed for goal-oriented interventions that challenged and refined the discussion. This specialization was evident in both the chosen themes for intervention and the specific dialogue acts performed by each robot.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This study makes several contributions to the fields of Human-Robot Interaction (HRI) and Explainable AI (XAI), particularly concerning LLM-driven systems in multi-party contexts.

**Significance and Contributions:**
- **Characterizing LLM Intervention Explanations:** The research systematically characterized the types of intervention explanations generated by an LLM orchestrator in a multi-human, multi-robot conversational setting. By identifying five key themes (Managing Agreement, Participation Balancing, Highlighting Negotiation Goals, Maintaining Interaction, and Understanding Conversation State), the study provides empirical evidence of the functional orientations underpinning an LLM's decision to intervene in social group dynamics.
- **Advancing Explainability in Embodied AI:** This work demonstrates that the interpretability of embodied LLM systems can be empirically examined through the analysis of generated decision justifications. This offers a concrete methodology for understanding the behavior of complex AI systems in real-time, socially rich environments, moving beyond merely observing output to understanding the purported rationale.
- **Understanding LLM as a Facilitator:** A central finding is the LLM orchestrator's dominant role as a facilitator of group processes, emphasizing agreement, participation balance, and progress rather than active content contribution. This clarifies the current operational mode of such systems in negotiation tasks. The recurring emphasis on fairness in goal-directed explanations suggests an implicit normative anchor, leveraging shared social norms for interpretability.
- **Insights into Role Differentiation:** While the overall explanation patterns remained stable across homogeneous and heterogeneous robot configurations, the study revealed that the LLM orchestrator strategically leverages differentiated robot roles. The mover was associated with coordination and facilitation, while the opposer was tasked with challenging proposals and driving goal-oriented discussions. This indicates that role specialization influences *how* interventions are performed (via dialogue acts) more profoundly than *what* is broadly explained (via high-level themes).

**Potential Impact:**
- **Design of More Transparent HRI Systems:** The findings provide insights for designing more transparent and accountable multi-robot conversational orchestration systems. Developers can use the identified explanation themes to engineer LLM prompts or architectures that align with desired interactional behaviors and provide legible justifications to human users.
- **Informing Future LLM Development for Social Robotics:** Understanding the LLM's inherent biases (e.g., toward consensus-seeking) and its capabilities (e.g., managing participation) can inform the development of more nuanced and robust LLMs for social robots, potentially allowing for finer control over their influence on group dynamics.
- **Guidance for Human-Robot Teamwork:** By revealing how LLM-driven robots justify their actions, the study offers a foundation for humans to better understand, predict, and ultimately trust robotic agents in collaborative settings. This understanding is critical for effective human-robot teamwork, particularly in scenarios requiring collective decision-making.
- **Addressing Ethical Considerations:** The discussion about fairness as an implicit normative anchor highlights the importance of how values are internalized and expressed by LLMs. This has implications for the ethical deployment of AI in socially sensitive domains, prompting further investigation into value alignment and potential subtle influences on group outcomes.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge several limitations that also point to future research directions:
- **User Perception of Explanations:** The study did not evaluate whether the generated explanations were understandable to users or improved their perceived system transparency. Future work should assess the human-centered aspects of these explanations.
- **Linking Behavior to Objective Outcomes:** The research did not correlate robot intervention behaviors with objective measures of conversational success (e.g., task completion rate, decision quality, group satisfaction). Future studies could investigate the causal impact of different intervention patterns on group outcomes.
- **Generalizability and Theoretical Claims:** The study is exploratory, and its findings need to be tested for generalizability across different tasks, environments, and system configurations. Future research should aim to develop more robust theoretical models of orchestrator decision-making in multi-party HRI.

In conclusion, this research significantly contributes to the empirical understanding of how LLM-driven systems articulate their intervention decisions in complex social human-robot interactions, paving the way for more interpretable and effective multi-robot conversational orchestration.
