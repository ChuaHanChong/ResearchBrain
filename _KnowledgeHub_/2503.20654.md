# AccidentSim: Generating Vehicle Collision Videos with Physically Realistic Collision Trajectories from Real-World Accident Reports

> [!summary] Summary
> AccidentSim, developed by researchers from Beijing Technology and Business University and The University of Sydney, generates physically realistic vehicle collision videos by integrating real-world accident reports with a physics simulator and a fine-tuned large language model. This framework produces accurate pre- and post-collision trajectories, addressing the scarcity of safety-critical data for autonomous driving systems.

## Problem

- Autonomous driving systems suffer from the "Curse of Rarity," lacking sufficient real-world data for rare, safety-critical scenarios like vehicle collisions.
- Current video generation models fail to produce physically realistic vehicle collision dynamics, particularly post-collision trajectories, as they do not account for physical constraints.
- Physical simulators for collision scenarios are computationally intensive, require precise parameter specification, and cannot easily interpret natural language descriptions for diverse scenario generation.

## Method

- A multi-stage framework that first plans pre-collision trajectories by extracting parameters from accident descriptions and identifying valid lane paths.
- Predicts physically realistic post-collision trajectories using AccidentLLM, a large language model fine-tuned on data generated from real-world accident reports within a ==physics simulator==.
- Synthesizes complete collision videos by integrating the generated pre- and post-collision trajectories with rendered vehicle models and dynamic backgrounds.

## Results

- Generated trajectories demonstrated lower L2 errors and significantly smaller MAE/RMSE for impulse and momentum changes, indicating higher physical consistency compared to existing methods.
- Videos achieved the highest VBench Motion Smoothness (**0.997**) and superior scores in human and GPT-4o evaluations for Physical Realism and Semantic Consistency.
- Training autonomous driving systems with AccidentSim-generated videos reduced the average collision rate to **0.073**, a substantial improvement over baselines (e.g., **0.552** without this data).

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Physically consistent collision dynamics, especially post-collision trajectories, can be effectively learned and generated by an LLM (AccidentLLM) when trained on data distilled from a high-fidelity physics simulator.
> - Real-world accident reports provide rich, underutilized physical cues and contextual information that can be extracted to ground and diversify synthetic collision scenarios.
> - The fusion of natural language understanding, robust physical simulation, and generative AI enables the creation of diverse and controllable physically realistic collision videos.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust autonomous driving systems critically depends on comprehensive and diverse training data that encompasses a wide range of driving scenarios, including rare but safety-critical events like vehicle collisions. Existing large-scale datasets, such as KITTI, Cityscapes, nuScenes, Waymo Open Dataset, and BDD100K, primarily document normal driving conditions. The scarcity of data for safety-critical scenarios, termed the "Curse of Rarity" (CoR), poses a significant challenge for training deep learning models to respond effectively to such situations, potentially compromising the reliability and safety of autonomous systems.

Recent advancements in video generation models offer an alternative to expensive real-world data collection. However, current state-of-the-art models (e.g., Kling, Gen-4, Jimeng) and specialized driving-scenario generators (e.g., DriveDreamer, Panacea, Cosmos, HoloDrive) are often limited in their ability to produce physically realistic vehicle collisions. Specifically, these methods frequently fail to generate accurate post-collision trajectories that adhere to real-world physical constraints and dynamics, as they are not explicitly designed to incorporate essential physical cues such as vehicle momentum, impulse, and mass distribution. Consequently, the generated videos may be visually plausible but lack physical realism.

Previous efforts to address safety-critical scenarios include specialized accident datasets (e.g., CADP, ISSAFE, CCD) and advanced paradigms like EQ-TAA, which synthesizes pseudo-accident pairs. These approaches are often constrained by fixed viewpoints and high acquisition costs, and they typically focus on observational anticipation rather than dynamic synthesis of novel crash scenarios with accurate physics. Adversarial scenario generation (e.g., AdvSim, Learning-to-Collide, CAT) and trajectory optimization (e.g., STRIVE) aim to create challenging pre-collision scenarios but generally do not simulate actual physical impacts or post-collision dynamics.

In the domain of trajectory prediction, models typically rely on historical states, latent sequence modeling, and high-definition maps. More recently, language-guided traffic generation using large language models (LLMs) (e.g., LCTGen, CTG++, TrafficGen, Traj-LLM, MotionLM, MotionCLIP) has emerged, enabling control of traffic simulation via natural language. However, these often focus on general traffic flow rather than specific, physically realistic collision dynamics.

Physical simulation, using tools like CARLA, LGSVL, and UniSim, provides a means to enforce strict physical constraints for vehicle dynamics. Some research, such as SoVAR and AC3R, has attempted to reconstruct accidents from police reports within simulators. While physically rigorous, pure physical simulations are computationally intensive, require precise parameter tuning, and cannot readily generate diverse, novel scenarios from under-specified natural language prompts.

AccidentSim positions itself at the intersection of these research areas, aiming to bridge the gap by combining natural language processing of real-world accident reports, high-fidelity physical simulation, and large language models for video generation. It seeks to produce physically realistic vehicle collision videos that overcome the limitations of existing methods by explicitly incorporating physical cues from real-world data and distilling complex physics into an adaptable language model for efficient, context-aware trajectory generation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized "Curse of Rarity" in autonomous driving data. Autonomous driving systems require extensive and diverse training data to ensure reliable operation across all possible scenarios, including rare safety-critical events like vehicle collisions. However, collecting real-world collision data is inherently difficult, costly, and ethically complex due to the low frequency and unpredictable nature of these events. Existing datasets predominantly focus on normal driving conditions, leaving a significant gap in data for training and validating autonomous systems in critical situations.

While current video generation models can produce visually convincing content, they generally lack physical realism when it comes to vehicle collisions. These models often fail to accurately reproduce post-collision trajectories because they do not explicitly account for fundamental physical principles such as conservation of momentum, impulse, and mass distribution. This limitation means that even visually realistic generated videos may not accurately reflect the true dynamics of a real-world collision, diminishing their utility for safety-critical training.

Real-world accident reports, such as those from the National Motor Vehicle Crash Causation Survey (NMVCCS), contain a wealth of physical cues and contextual information—including vehicle speeds, collision angles, masses, road types, and collision types. This rich, real-world data is currently underutilized in existing video generation frameworks.

Furthermore, while dedicated physical simulators (e.g., CARLA) can generate physically accurate collision dynamics, they are computationally expensive, require a full specification of physical parameters, and cannot directly interpret or respond to user-provided natural language accident descriptions to generate varied scenarios efficiently.

Against this backdrop, the key objectives of the AccidentSim framework are:

1. **To generate physically realistic vehicle collision videos:** The core objective is to produce synthetic collision videos where vehicle trajectories, especially post-collision movements, faithfully adhere to physical laws and real-world dynamics.
2. **To leverage real-world accident reports:** To extract and utilize the detailed physical cues and contextual information embedded in real-world accident reports to ground the simulations in actual incident data.
3. **To overcome simulator limitations through physics distillation:** To use a reliable physical simulator to create a high-quality dataset of physically constrained post-collision trajectories. This dataset is then used to fine-tune a large language model, AccidentLLM, to predict novel, physically consistent trajectories directly from user-provided accident descriptions, thereby bypassing the computational expense and parameter specification requirements of direct simulation.
4. **To enable language-driven scenario generation:** To develop AccidentLLM, a language model capable of interpreting natural language accident descriptions and translating them into physically consistent pre- and post-collision trajectories.
5. **To synthesize complete collision videos:** To integrate the predicted physically realistic trajectories with rendered backgrounds and vehicles to create comprehensive collision videos according to user-specified viewpoints.
6. **To demonstrate practical utility:** To validate that incorporating these generated videos into the training of autonomous driving systems can effectively reduce collision rates, thereby enhancing system safety and reliability.

In essence, AccidentSim aims to provide a scalable and efficient solution to the scarcity of physically accurate collision data, improving the ability of autonomous systems to navigate and respond to safety-critical events.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AccidentSim is designed as a multi-stage framework that integrates natural language understanding, physical simulation, and video synthesis to generate physically realistic vehicle collision videos from user-provided accident descriptions.

The overall methodology comprises three main phases: Pre-Collision Trajectory Planning, Post-Collision Trajectory Prediction (driven by AccidentLLM), and Collision Video Generation.

**I. Pre-Collision Trajectory Planning**
This module generates the vehicle trajectories leading up to the point of collision, along with associated parameters such as vehicle speed. It consists of three phases:

1. **Initial State Extraction:** A large language model first extracts essential vehicle and collision parameters from the user-provided accident description. This includes the collision point (C), number of vehicles (n), distances (D), velocities (V), accelerations (A), and orientation angles (θ) for all involved vehicles. Starting points (P0) for each vehicle are determined based on these dynamics and directional properties.
2. **Lane and Path Selection:** The algorithm parses a scene map (M) to identify available lanes (L) that are compatible with the vehicles’ current orientations and collision constraints. It then assembles a subset of valid lanes and generates various lane combinations (G) by analyzing lane connectivity and spatial feasibility relative to the extracted vehicle parameters.
3. **Trajectory Generation and Validation:** For each lane combination, the algorithm checks if it meets specific collision criteria defined by the collision parameters (C). Once a valid lane combination (Gs) is identified, it generates dynamically feasible and collision-aware trajectory paths (T) using the starting points (P0) and Gs. These trajectories are validated against pre-collision scenario criteria. The output includes the validated trajectories (T) and the imminent collision information (S), which serves as input for the subsequent Post-Collision Prediction module.

**II. Post-Collision Trajectory Prediction**
This module addresses the challenge of generating physically realistic post-collision trajectories by leveraging real-world accident reports and a fine-tuned language model, AccidentLLM. It involves three stages:

1. **Accident Report Extraction:** The framework utilizes a Llama model with a few-shot learning strategy to automatically extract detailed information from real-world accident reports (sourced from NMVCCS). This extraction captures pre-accident environmental conditions, road features, driver behaviors, in-collision details (vehicle positioning, speed, collision type), and post-accident information. This approach is designed to overcome the variability and complexity of natural language in accident reports, providing a comprehensive data foundation for simulations.
2. **Collision Scenario Simulation:** A scenario reconstruction pipeline is developed to recreate real-world collision scenarios within a physical simulator. Extracted accident report information is partitioned into vehicle-specific (e.g., velocity, location) and road-related details. High-fidelity 3D road models are constructed using RoadRunner and integrated into the CARLA simulation environment. CARLA’s physics engine, combined with extracted vehicle dynamics and physical parameters (friction, gravity), ensures realistic motion and collision behavior. As vehicles approach the collision point, critical impact information (speed, position, orientation) is captured, and the resulting post-collision trajectories are recorded, forming a high-quality collision dataset.
3. **Fine-tuning AccidentLLM:** The generated collision dataset is used to fine-tune a Llama model, resulting in AccidentLLM. Supervised fine-tuning is performed using LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), optimizing the model for precise collision trajectory prediction in complex scenarios based on user-provided accident descriptions. An L1 loss function (Ltraj) is employed, measuring the absolute discrepancy between predicted and ground-truth trajectories across both spatial (position: ˆpi, p∗i) and rotational (orientation: ˆθi, θ∗i) dimensions. This joint optimization ensures that AccidentLLM captures complex post-collision dynamics and produces physically consistent trajectory predictions.

**III. Collision Video Generation**
This final phase integrates the generated trajectories with visual elements to produce the complete collision video.

1. **Foreground Processing:** The complete vehicle motion paths, comprising pre-collision trajectories (from Pre-Collision Trajectory Planning) and physically realistic post-collision trajectories (inferred by AccidentLLM), are combined with user-specified vehicle models. From these complete trajectories, foreground images depicting the physically realistic vehicle collision are rendered.
2. **Background Processing:** This involves viewpoint adjustment and background rendering. User-provided intended video viewpoint movements are parsed into positional and angular shift parameters, converted into an extrinsic transformation matrix, and combined with initial parameters to define the updated viewpoint. For novel viewpoints, ChatSim's scene reconstruction method (incorporating multi-camera alignment and luminance consistency) is applied to generate the background. If the viewpoint is not novel, frames from the Waymo Open Dataset are used.
3. **Video Composition:** Alpha channel compositing techniques are utilized to blend the generated foreground images with the rendered backgrounds, adjusting transparency to create a visually and physically cohesive collision video.

This comprehensive methodology allows AccidentSim to generate diverse, controllable, and physically realistic vehicle collision videos by effectively translating semantic descriptions into dynamic simulations.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of AccidentSim focused on three core aspects: trajectory generation quality and physical consistency, quality evaluation of generated videos, and collision reduction capabilities in autonomous driving systems.

**I. Trajectory Generation Quality and Physical Consistency**

1. **Physical Consistency and Accuracy:** AccidentSim's physically plausible post-collision trajectories were compared against CARLA-simulated ground truth. The system demonstrated lower average L2 errors in trajectory prediction and significantly smaller Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) for impulse and momentum changes compared to the baseline method, AutoVFX. This indicates a stricter adherence to physical laws, such as momentum conservation, in AccidentSim’s generated trajectories. Qualitative visualizations in Figure 3 further supported that AccidentSim produced smoother and more realistic dynamics.
2. **Text-to-Trajectory Semantic Consistency:** Human A/B tests, following the LCTGen protocol, were conducted to assess how well generated trajectories aligned with crash reports and attribute descriptions. AccidentSim achieved higher preference percentages and higher alignment scores (out of 5) compared to baselines such as TrafficGen, MotionCLIP, and LCTGen. This suggested that AccidentLLM, fine-tuned on simulator-calibrated data, effectively mapped complex textual narratives to kinematically consistent trajectories.

**II. Quality Evaluation for Generated Video**

The visual fidelity and physical realism of the generated videos were assessed using VBench metrics for motion smoothness and imaging quality, along with human and GPT-4o evaluations for Physical Realism (PhysReal), Photorealism (PhotoReal), and Semantic Consistency (Align).

1. **Quantitative Evaluation (Table III):** AccidentSim achieved a VBench Motion Smoothness score of 0.997, which was the highest among all compared methods, including Kling 2.0, Gen-4, Jimeng 3.0, Cosmos, and AutoVFX. For Imaging Quality, AccidentSim scored 0.718, which was competitive with the top-performing Jimeng 3.0 (0.727). In human evaluation, AccidentSim secured the highest scores for Physical Realism (3.906), and Semantic Consistency (4.186), while its Photorealism score (3.622) was competitive. Similarly, in GPT-4o evaluation, AccidentSim achieved the highest scores in Physical Realism (0.839) and Semantic Consistency (0.910), and a competitive Photorealism score (0.873). These results collectively indicate that AccidentSim achieves state-of-the-art performance in motion smoothness, physical realism, and semantic consistency, while maintaining a high level of imaging quality.
2. **Qualitative Evaluation (Figures 3 and 4):** Qualitative comparisons illustrated AccidentSim's ability to generate visually high-fidelity and physically realistic vehicle collisions that accurately followed user descriptions across diverse road types and collision scenarios.

**III. Collision Reduction by AccidentSim**

This experiment assessed the practical utility of AccidentSim's generated videos in reducing collision rates (CR) for autonomous driving systems. The collision rate was measured after augmenting the training of an autonomous driving model (fine-tuned from a Soft Actor-Critic model from SafeBench) with AccidentSim’s generated videos, compared to various baselines.

1. **Quantitative Collision Rate Reduction (Table IV):** AccidentSim achieved an average collision rate of 0.073 across diverse dual-vehicle, triple-vehicle, and multi-vehicle scenarios. This represented a significant reduction compared to the "Pre Fine-tuning" baseline (0.552, without AccidentSim videos) and other mainstream adversarial and knowledge-driven methods for scenario generation, including Learning-to-Collide (0.304), AdvSim (0.320), CARLA Scenario Generator (0.258), Adversarial Trajectory Optimization (0.179), and ChatScene (0.112). These results demonstrate that incorporating AccidentSim's generated collision videos into autonomous system training can effectively improve the systems' ability to avoid collisions, thereby enhancing safety.

In summary, the main findings indicate that AccidentSim successfully generates vehicle collision videos with high physical and visual realism, accurately adheres to semantic descriptions, and provides a valuable data augmentation strategy that measurably reduces collision rates in autonomous driving systems.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AccidentSim framework addresses a fundamental challenge in autonomous driving research: the scarcity of diverse and physically realistic data for safety-critical scenarios, particularly vehicle collisions. By effectively mitigating the "Curse of Rarity," this work holds several significant implications and potential impacts:

1. **Enhanced Safety and Reliability of Autonomous Driving Systems:** The primary impact is on the training and evaluation of autonomous vehicles. By providing a scalable source of physically realistic collision videos, AccidentSim enables deep learning models to learn robust responses to rare but critical situations. This improved training can lead to autonomous systems that are better equipped to anticipate, avoid, or mitigate the effects of collisions, thereby enhancing their overall safety and reliability. The demonstrated reduction in collision rates in experimental settings directly supports this potential.
2. **Reduced Dependence on Costly Real-World Data Collection:** Collecting real-world accident data is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and ethically complex. AccidentSim offers a cost-effective and efficient alternative, reducing the need for extensive real-world crash testing or data harvesting for rare events. This allows researchers and developers to iterate faster on safety features.
3. **Advancement in Language-Guided Simulation and Video Generation:** The integration of real-world accident reports with physical simulation and large language models represents a notable advancement. AccidentLLM's ability to translate under-specified natural language descriptions into physically consistent trajectories demonstrates effective "physics distillation." This approach bridges the gap between high-level semantic instructions and low-level physical dynamics, pushing the boundaries of controllable and physically grounded video generation.
4. **Improved Generalization Across Diverse Scenarios:** By extracting contextual information and physical cues from a wide range of real-world accident reports, AccidentSim can generate diverse scenarios spanning various vehicle models, collision types, road types, and traffic conditions. This generalizability is crucial for developing autonomous systems that can perform reliably across the myriad conditions encountered in the real world.
5. **New Tools for Research and Development:** AccidentSim provides a valuable tool for researchers and developers to generate custom, on-demand collision scenarios. This can facilitate targeted testing of specific safety algorithms, exploration of edge cases, and development of defensive driving strategies.
6. **Potential for Broader Applications:** While focused on autonomous driving, the methodology of combining real-world textual reports with physical simulation and LLM-driven generation could extend to other domains requiring physically accurate, scenario-based simulation. Examples include forensic accident reconstruction, training for emergency services, or simulating complex physical interactions in robotics and virtual reality.
7. **Foundation for Future Realism Enhancements:** The paper explicitly mentions future work involving vehicle deformation and additional physical cues. The current framework provides a robust foundation upon which such advanced realism features can be built, further blurring the line between synthetic and real-world collision data.

In conclusion, AccidentSim represents a significant step towards enabling the development of safer and more reliable autonomous driving systems by offering a scalable, physically realistic, and language-controllable solution for generating critical collision data.
