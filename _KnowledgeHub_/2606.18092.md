# EAGG: Embodiment-Aligned Grasp Generation via Geometry-Aware Graph Conditioning

> [!summary] Summary
> A unified generative model synthesizes functional grasps for diverse robotic end effectors and novel objects by explicitly aligning embodiment structure through a low-dimensional control basis, a topology-aware graph, and dynamic geometry-aware conditioning. The model achieved an average success rate of 56.17% across six training end effectors, comparable to specialized models, and demonstrated successful real-world execution on three distinct robot platforms.

## Problem

- Most successful grasp generators are designed for specific end effectors, struggling to generalize to heterogeneous robotic hands without extensive re-training.
- Existing cross-end-effector approaches often use static embodiment descriptors or abstract away embodiment, limiting their ability to account for dynamic geometric interactions during grasp closure.
- There is a challenge in representing diverse end-effector geometries and actuation logic within a single, unified framework while ensuring broad transferability.

## Method

- Developed **EAGG**, a time-conditioned generative model that predicts a compact grasp state comprising a low-dimensional control code, wrist translation, and rotation.
- Integrated an ==embodiment-aligned Principal Component Analysis== (PCA)-based low-dimensional control basis for each end effector and a topology-aware graph representation derived from its URDF structure.
- Introduced Iterative Geometry Injection (IGI) for dynamic geometry-aware conditioning, which recomputes end-effector surface-anchored latent tokens at each step of the iterative sampling process.

## Results

- EAGG (Unified) achieved an average success rate of **56.17%** across six training end effectors, within **1.10** percentage points of specialized models and outperforming non-EAGG baselines by up to **35.46** points for articulated grippers.
- Demonstrated efficient transfer to unseen end effectors, achieving an average success rate of **36.68%** after finetuning with **5%** of their data and **33.25%** with zero-shot adaptation using seed grasps.
- Hardware experiments on three distinct robot platforms (UR5 + FreedomHand, UR5 + DaHuan AG95, SOARM101) showed high average success rates, ranging from **89.06%** to **95.00%**, validating real-world applicability and robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly aligning embodiment structure using a combination of a low-dimensional PCA-based control basis and a topology-aware graph is crucial for effective cross-end-effector grasp generalization.
> - Dynamically updating geometry-aware conditioning during the iterative grasp sampling process, through Iterative Geometry Injection (IGI), significantly improves grasp quality and reduces penetration, particularly for highly articulated hands.
> - The learned latent space effectively separates different morphology classes, suggesting that an embodiment-aligned representation facilitates structured knowledge transfer and adaptation across diverse robotic hands.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic grasping is focused on enabling robots to interact with objects effectively. A significant challenge within this field is the development of robust and generalizable grasp generation models. Historically, most successful grasp generators have been designed for specific end effectors, such as parallel-jaw grippers or particular dexterous hands. Methods like Dex-Net, PointNetGPD, 6-DoF GraspNet, S4G, and Contact-GraspNet have demonstrated strong performance in this fixed-end-effector setting. However, their implicit embodiment-specific parameterization means that they typically generalize well across objects for a given end effector but struggle to transfer to heterogeneous end effectors without extensive re-training or new data.

The broader research landscape has recently shifted towards cross-end-effector grasp generation, aiming for a unified model that can synthesize grasps for diverse robotic hands, including those with varying topologies, actuation mechanisms, and contact geometries. This shift is motivated by the increasing availability of large-scale datasets that encompass multiple end effectors, such as DexGraspNet, UniDexGrasp, and the MultiGripperGrasp (MGG) benchmark used in this study. These benchmarks highlight the need for models that can handle both object novelty and end-effector novelty within a single framework.

Existing approaches to cross-end-effector transfer can be categorized into several strategies:
1. **Object-centric abstractions:** These define a grasp intent (e.g., contact targets or maps) independent of the end effector, which is then instantiated for different hands. However, this separation can become insufficient as morphology diversity increases, as a contact plan suitable for a dexterous hand might be difficult for a simpler gripper.
2. **Learning combined with optimization or structured interaction models:** These methods enforce contact or physics consistency during adaptation, but often incur higher computational costs during inference that scale with embodiment complexity.
3. **Shared policies or action spaces:** These are learned across multiple dexterous embodiments, but are generally best suited for hands with broadly similar actuation richness and kinematic structures.
4. **Direct grasp distribution translation:** This involves translating grasp knowledge directly between heterogeneous hands.

A common limitation across many of these approaches is that they either partially abstract away the embodiment or use static descriptors (e.g., as tokens), which can limit their ability to account for the evolving contact opportunities and collision patterns that occur as an end effector closes around an object. This is particularly problematic for unseen end effectors or those with complex articulation.

The present work, EAGG, addresses this limitation by integrating concepts from structured embodiment representation and advanced generative modeling. Previous research has explored low-dimensional grasp subspaces or postural synergies to compress articulation, and graph-based models to represent kinematic structure. While powerful for specific aspects, neither alone provides a complete unified embodiment representation. EAGG combines these by using a Principal Component Analysis (PCA)-based low-dimensional control space and a topology-aware graph representation. Furthermore, EAGG leverages continuous-time generative models (flow matching) which have shown strong capabilities in sampling multimodal continuous actions, but extends them by integrating dynamic, geometry-aware conditioning. This positions EAGG as an attempt to bridge the gap by providing a unified, embodiment-aligned generative model that explicitly accounts for an end effector's structure and its evolving geometry during grasp formation, rather than treating embodiment identity as an auxiliary label or abstracting it away.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a single, unified grasp generation model capable of synthesizing functional grasps for a wide range of heterogeneous robotic end effectors, while simultaneously generalizing across novel objects. This goal is more demanding than traditional fixed-end-effector grasping, as the model must address two coupled problems: discerning how an object should be grasped and articulating that grasp for a specific end effector.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and observations:

1. **Limitations of Fixed-End-Effector Models:** Existing grasp generators typically achieve strong performance for a fixed end effector, but they lack a unified mechanism for transferring knowledge across different hardware. This necessitates re-parameterization, new data collection, or full retraining when a new gripper or hand is introduced, which is inefficient in dynamic manipulation systems.
2. **Restrictive Static Embodiment Descriptors:** Many cross-end-effector approaches inject embodiment information as a static token or latent descriptor. This static conditioning is restrictive because the geometric interactions—such as contact opportunities and collision patterns—evolve significantly throughout the grasp sampling process, especially for end effectors with complex articulation that undergoes substantial geometric changes during closure. A static descriptor cannot capture this dynamic evolution.
3. **The Representation Challenge:** The feasible grasp postures for different end effectors occupy distinct, structured, low-dimensional subsets within their respective wrist-and-joint spaces. These subsets do not collapse into a single common manifold due to differences in joint coordinates, actuation couplings, and closure patterns. A robust unified generator needs to preserve these embodiment-specific actuation logics and geometries, yet also expose sufficient common structure to enable statistical transfer across objects and embodiments. Simply forcing all embodiments into one raw joint coordinate system discards crucial structural information.
4. **Complementary Nature of Control Basis and Topology:** Low-dimensional control spaces (e.g., postural synergies) effectively describe how an end effector *may* move compactly but do not convey how it is *organized* (kinematic chains, joint couplings). Conversely, graph structures can encode topological organization but do not define an actuation-aligned coordinate system. The motivation is to combine these complementary representations for a comprehensive embodiment description.
5. **Practical Deployment Needs:** In shared manipulation stacks, hardware swaps should ideally not require relearning grasp generation from scratch. The research aims to develop a reusable generator that is both effective and efficient in such scenarios.

Therefore, EAGG is motivated by the need for a generative model that explicitly aligns embodiment structure—through a topology-aware graph and a low-dimensional control space—and dynamically conditions the generation process with evolving geometry-aware information. This design is intended to enable stronger generalization by embracing embodiment differences rather than attempting to suppress or abstract them entirely.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

EAGG (Embodiment-Aligned Grasp Generation) is designed as a time-conditioned generative model that predicts a compact grasp state `x = [s; t; r]`. Here, `s` is a low-dimensional control code, `t` is the wrist translation, and `r` is a 6-dimensional rotation representation. The model operates on an object point cloud `P_O` and an end-effector embodiment `h`, which is characterized by its actuated joint dimension `q_h` and kinematic graph `G_h`.

The methodology integrates three core components: an embodiment-aligned control basis, a topology-aware graph representation, and geometry-aware dynamic conditioning through Iterative Geometry Injection (IGI).

#### 3.1. A. Problem Formulation

The grasp state `x` is decomposed into a wrist pose `(t, r)` and an embodiment-specific low-dimensional control code `s`. This separation allows the generator to learn global object interaction patterns that can be shared, while local articulation is handled through an embodiment-aligned interface. The model is trained on tuples `(P_O^(n), h^(n), θ_h^(n), t^(n), r^(n))`, where `θ_h^(n)` is the full joint configuration.

#### 3.2. B. Embodiment-Aligned Control Basis and Topology-Aware Graph

1. **Embodiment-Aligned Control Basis:**
 - For each end effector `h`, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) model is fitted to a collection of feasible grasp postures `X_h`.
 - This PCA identifies a mean posture `μ_h` and a set of `d` principal directions `U_h` (where `d=4` in experiments), forming an embodiment-specific low-dimensional subspace.
 - Given a control code `s`, the full joint configuration `θ_h(s)` is reconstructed as `θ_h(s) = μ_h + U_h s`.
 - Conversely, an observed joint configuration `θ_h` can be projected into this low-dimensional space as `s = U_h^T (θ_h - μ_h)`.
 - This approach ensures that each embodiment retains its own motion basis, and cross-end-effector transfer is performed in the standardized coefficient space `s`, rather than forcing all hands into a single raw joint parameterization.

2. **Topology-Aware Graph:**
 - Each end effector `h` is represented as a graph `G_h` derived from its URDF structure. Nodes correspond to the virtual base and actuated joints, and edges connect kinematically linked joints.
 - Each node `i` has a static feature vector `h_static_i` comprising kinematic properties (joint axis, type, limits, local transform, kinematic depth), geometric statistics from the URDF, the mean joint value `μ_h,i`, and the corresponding row of the PCA basis `U_h,i,:`.
 - At each sampling step `t`, the current normalized joint angle `θ_h,t,i` (derived from the evolving grasp state `x_t`) is appended to `h_static_i` to form a dynamic node feature `h^(0)_i,t`.
 - Graph propagation is performed using row-normalized graph convolutions (Eq. 8) to capture relational inductive biases and propagate information across the end effector's kinematic structure.
 - This combination ensures that the model understands both *how* an embodiment moves (control basis) and *how* it is organized (graph topology).

#### 3.3. C. Geometry-Aware Dynamic Conditioning (Iterative Geometry Injection - IGI)

1. **Frozen End-Effector-Cognition Backbone:**
 - A neural network backbone is pretrained independently to map an articulated state into geometry-aware point tokens.
 - This backbone takes the topology-aware graph `G_h`, a canonical surface point cloud `P^(h)_E,can` (sampled at the mean posture), and the current articulation induced by the low-dimensional control state.
 - It is trained to reconstruct the posed surface cloud from the canonical cloud, conditioned on the graph and articulation (Eq. 9).
 - Once trained, this backbone is frozen and integrated into EAGG, acting as a stable morphology prior that converts current articulated states into surface-anchored latent tokens `Z^(h)_E,t`.

2. **Dynamic Conditioning during Grasp Generation:**
 - During grasp generation, the current wrist pose `(t_t, r_t)` from the evolving grasp state `x_t` transforms the canonical end-effector cloud to `~P^(h)_E,t`.
 - The frozen end-effector-cognition backbone then generates dynamic end-effector tokens `Z^(h)_E,t` using `G_h`, the normalized current joint angles `θ_h,t`, and `~P^(h)_E,t` (Eq. 11). Each token summarizes local geometry and motion context.
 - **IGI Mechanism:** These `Z^(h)_E,t` tokens are recomputed *after every update* in the iterative sampling process. This ensures that the conditioning provided to the generator remains synchronized with the end effector's evolving articulated geometry, allowing the model to adapt to changing contact opportunities and collision patterns.

#### 3.4. D. Object Encoding and Token Fusion

1. **Object Encoding:** The input object point cloud `P_O` is processed by a PointNet++-style backbone `Φ_O` to generate object tokens `Z_O`, which are augmented with positional embeddings.
2. **Token Fusion:** EAGG fuses three token families:
 - A single grasp-state token `z_x` (encoded from `x_t`).
 - The dynamic end-effector tokens `Z^(h)_E,t`.
 - The object point tokens `Z_O`.
 - These tokens are augmented with sinusoidal time embeddings `e_t` and learned type embeddings (`e_g, e_e, e_o`) before being passed to the main transformer backbone `Ψ` (Eq. 13).
 - The transformer's first output token predicts the clean grasp state `^x_0`.

#### 3.5. E. Training Objective and Inference

1. **Training:**
 - A clean grasp state `x_0` is interpolated with Gaussian noise `ε` along a linear path `x_t = (1-t)x_0 + tε`.
 - The network `f_Θ` is trained to predict the clean state `^x_0` directly from the noisy state `x_t`, time `t`, and the time-varying conditioning bundle `C_t = {Z_O, Z^(h)_E,t}` (Eq. 17).
 - The optimization uses a component-wise Huber objective `L` (Eq. 18), where `ω(t) = exp(-2t)` weights lower-noise states more heavily. This emphasizes precise final articulation and contact formation.

2. **Inference:**
 - The generator iteratively refines the grasp state from noise to a clean grasp. Crucially, IGI recomputes `Z^(h)_E,t` at each step `t'` using the current predicted state `x_t'` to provide updated geometry-aware conditioning for the next prediction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents an extensive evaluation of EAGG across various regimes, including jointly trained, finetuned, zero-shot, and real-world scenarios, utilizing the MultiGripperGrasp (MGG) dataset.

#### 4.1. A. Cross-End-Effector Benchmark Results

- **Overall Success Rate:** EAGG (Unified) achieved an average success rate (SR) of 56.17% across the six training end effectors. This performance was within 1.10 percentage points of the average SR (57.27%) achieved by EAGG (Specialized) models, which were trained independently for each end effector. This indicates that the unified model largely preserved performance comparable to specialized models while sharing knowledge.
- **Performance vs. Baselines:** EAGG (Unified) demonstrated improved SR compared to strong non-EAGG baselines (NS, GPG, UDG, DexDiffusion variants) on articulated end effectors. For WSG-50, it improved SR by 4.22 points; for Robotiq 3F, by 17.70 points; and for Allegro, by 35.46 points relative to the strongest non-EAGG baseline. The magnitude of gain increased with the articulation complexity of the end effector.
- **Contact Geometry Metrics:** While EAGG (Unified) exhibited strong task-level completion, specialized training showed some advantages in refining local contact geometry, reducing pooled median Contact Distance (CD) from 0.56 cm to 0.40 cm and Penetration Depth (PD) from 1.04 cm to 0.79 cm, and increasing Contact Count (CC) from 2.081 to 2.254. This suggests that embodiment-specific specialization primarily sharpens the final contact state.
- **Grasp Diversity:** The Repeated-Grasp Ratio (RGR) indicated that EAGG could generate diverse grasps, with lower RGR for more articulated hands like Allegro and HumanHand, and higher RGR for simpler grippers like WSG-50, where feasible closure families are narrower.

#### 4.2. B. Transfer to Held-Out End Effectors

- **Finetuning End Effectors (Sawyer, Jaco):** After 10 epochs of adaptation with only 5% of their grasp data, EAGG achieved an average SR of 36.68%. Jaco adapted quickly, with most SR gains appearing in early epochs, and significant reductions in CD and PD. Sawyer also improved but more gradually.
- **Zero-Shot End Effectors (FreedomHand, DexHand):** With lightweight adaptation using seed grasps from SynergyGrasp, EAGG achieved an average SR of 33.25%. Both FreedomHand and DexHand showed steady improvement, though their contact geometry metrics remained more sensitive to embodiment-specific contact ordering.
- **Adaptation Dynamics:** SR generally improved faster than fine contact geometry metrics (CD, PD, CC) during adaptation, suggesting that executable grasps can emerge before contact geometry is fully calibrated.

#### 4.3. C. Representation Structure and Geometry Diagnostics

- **Latent Space Diagnostic:** A projection of end-effector embeddings from the frozen end-effector-cognition backbone showed that two-finger grippers were separated from multi-finger embodiments, and structure was preserved within each group. The mean between-end-effector centroid distance (16.41) was significantly larger than the mean within-end-effector spread (0.57), indicating a morphology-aware organization of the latent space.
- **Impact of Iterative Geometry Injection (IGI):** IGI improved grasp quality across all 10 end effectors. It lowered the pooled median CD from 0.406 cm to 0.350 cm and the median PD from 0.731 cm to 0.700 cm, and reduced initial overlap. The gains from IGI were more pronounced for highly articulated end effectors (e.g., Allegro, Jaco, Robotiq 3F, HumanHand) where geometry changes significantly during closure, while less articulated grippers (e.g., Franka Panda, WSG-50) showed smaller effects.

#### 4.4. D. Component Analysis (Ablation Study)

- The ablation study demonstrated the critical contributions of EAGG's core components. Removing topology-aware graph conditioning (`w/o Graph`) resulted in the largest SR drop (from 56.17% to 37.77%). Removing end-effector geometry (`w/o EndEffGeom`) also led to a significant SR reduction (to 38.75%). These two factors were identified as dominant contributors to performance.
- Removing the PCA-based basis prior (`w/o BasisPrior`) reduced SR to 43.48%, indicating its role in aligning heterogeneous embodiments.
- Other components, such as local object features and absolute pose, also showed substantial contributions to overall performance.

#### 4.5. E. Efficiency and Real-World Evaluation

- **Efficiency:** EAGG exhibited competitive efficiency, with a latency of 9.68 seconds, peak GPU memory of 1986.44 MB, and a parameter footprint of 52.95 MB (at batch size 64). It was faster and more memory-efficient than several baselines (e.g., 28.2x faster than UDG, 4.4x faster than DD (pn2)). The graph and geometry branches contributed targeted computational costs corresponding to their performance gains.
- **Real-World Execution:** Hardware trials on three distinct platforms (UR5 + FreedomHand, UR5 + DaHuan AG95, SOARM101) across five object groups demonstrated high average success rates: 91.94%, 95.00%, and 89.06%, respectively. These results indicate that EAGG's learned policies transfer effectively to physical robots, tolerating real-world sensing noise and actuation uncertainties.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents EAGG as a significant contribution to the field of robotic manipulation, particularly in the domain of grasp generation for heterogeneous end effectors. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Advancement in Unified Grasp Generation:** EAGG demonstrates that a single generative model can effectively synthesize high-quality grasps across a diverse range of robotic end effectors, from simple parallel grippers to complex dexterous hands, without requiring individual models for each. This addresses a fundamental challenge in robot grasping and provides a more scalable approach than previous embodiment-specific or coarsely abstracted methods.
2. **Validation of Embodiment Alignment as a Principle:** The work establishes "embodiment alignment" as a viable design principle for cross-end-effector transfer. By explicitly representing and aligning an end effector's structure (via a low-dimensional control basis and a topology-aware graph) and dynamically updating its geometry-aware conditioning (via IGI), EAGG shows that preserving and integrating embodiment-specific differences within a shared framework can lead to robust generalization. This contrasts with approaches that suppress or abstract away embodiment details.
3. **Improved Grasp Quality and Robustness through Dynamic Conditioning:** The introduction of Iterative Geometry Injection (IGI) enhances grasp quality by ensuring that the generator's conditioning remains synchronized with the evolving articulated geometry of the end effector during the sampling process. This leads to cleaner contact formation and reduced penetration, which is crucial for the physical reliability of generated grasps. The differential impact of IGI on highly articulated versus simpler grippers further highlights the importance of dynamic geometric awareness for complex morphologies.
4. **Efficient Transfer and Adaptation:** EAGG's ability to transfer effectively to finetuned and zero-shot end effectors with limited adaptation data suggests a potential for significantly reduced development and deployment costs for new robotic hardware. The structured latent representation, which inherently segregates morphology classes, likely contributes to this efficient adaptation by providing a semantically meaningful starting point.
5. **Practical Applicability in Real-World Scenarios:** The successful execution of generated grasps on three distinct physical robot platforms under realistic conditions validates the practical utility of EAGG. This indicates that the learned policies are robust enough to handle the complexities of real-world sensing noise, calibration errors, and robot control dynamics, making EAGG a candidate for deployment in versatile robotic manipulation systems.
6. **Guidance for Future Multi-Embodiment Systems:** The findings suggest a useful design principle for broader multi-embodiment generative policies. Instead of treating embodiment identity as a secondary factor or a constraint to be overcome, EAGG demonstrates the benefits of making it a first-class variable within the generation process. This perspective could inform the design of future learning systems that need to generalize across a variety of robot morphologies for diverse manipulation tasks.
