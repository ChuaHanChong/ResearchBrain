# Where Should I Join? Robot Group Joining via Language-Guided Goal Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> A two-stage pipeline for language-grounded robot group joining: recursive spectral partitioning proposes candidate person subsets that a language-conditioned SigLIP2 image-geometry ranker scores to ground the described group, then a goal predictor trained on simulated F-formation attention topologies outputs a multimodal energy-orientation map over feasible joining poses. It reaches 89.76% pose validity at ~1.1s per query and 18/20 real-robot successes.

## Problem

- Social navigation methods assume the goal position is already specified, focusing only on reaching it politely rather than inferring where to join from group activity.
- Group detection work estimates who belongs together among observed people, but never grounds a language-specified target group or yields a task-relevant navigation target.
- F-formation joining work mostly targets conversations, assumes the interaction is already identified, and uses rules or regression that miss multimodal valid poses.

## Method

- ==Hypothesis generation==: ==recursive divisive spectral clustering== with local scaling sweeps six Fiedler-vector thresholds between the 10th-90th percentiles, producing compact structured candidate subsets instead of exhaustive enumeration.
- ==Hypothesis ranking==: **SigLIP2** fine-tuned with ==dynamic-margin pairwise ranking==, where a **GeoEncoder** applies person-person self-attention and person-language cross-attention to ==FiLM==-modulate image embeddings against text.
- ==Goal prediction==: **People Encoder** plus FiLM language/scale conditioning feeds a transposed-convolutional **Pose Decoder** emitting a 3-channel energy plus sine/cosine orientation map, trained on simulated sequential, mutual and directed-shared attention topologies.

## Results

- Grounding hits **0.8125** agreement-weighted F1 in **0.936s**, versus **0.8311** for the best few-shot VLM needing **5.48s** and **8.7s** for the slowest zero-shot model.
- End-to-end pose validity is **89.76%** (**95.71%** position with oracle groups) against **59.76%** for few-shot GPT-5.5, **64.29%** for handcrafted F-formation rules, and **35.71%** for regression.
- Real-robot trials succeed **18/20** with zero collisions, beating NaVILA (**0/20**) and OmniVLA (**3/20**); the threshold-sweep ablation saturates at six intervals and human-click KL divergence is lowest at **14.87**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structured candidate generation plus a small learned ranker can match giant VLMs on semantic grounding at a fraction of latency, which is what makes online goal updates viable.
> - VLMs infer orientation from visible body pose far better than metric position, so image-space predictions stay geometrically invalid even when depth is supplied.
> - Predicting a distribution rather than one waypoint matters because socially valid joining is genuinely multimodal; deterministic regression collapses and handcrafted rules break on noisy poses.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A guide dog, mobility scooter, or other mobile robot can be told to “join that queue.” The robot must then determine which people belong to the named group and where a socially acceptable place to join is. That target emerges from people’s activity and arrangement in a cluttered scene, not from a coordinate given in advance. The authors therefore frame group joining as a goal-inference problem rather than ordinary navigation toward a supplied point. Their approach has two stages: use language to identify task-relevant people, then infer a joining position and heading from the group’s formation.

The authors’ project-page teaser: grounding language descriptions to group members and predicting joining poses for static and dynamic interactions.

### 2. From words to a joinable group

The task is formulated for an RGB-D observation and an instruction that unambiguously specifies the intended group. The system starts with detected people and generates structured candidate subsets. It uses recursive spectral partitioning, sweeping several thresholds rather than relying on one cut, to create alternatives with different group boundaries. A language-conditioned image-and-geometry ranker then selects the best match. This avoids enumerating all possible subsets.

Grounding is task-oriented. The output need only preserve enough interaction context to predict a joining pose, not necessarily recover every semantic member. The same design has a limit: the ranker cannot select a correct group that is absent from its candidate set.

### 3. From formation to plausible poses

An F-formation is a recurring pattern in how people position and orient themselves around shared attention. The pose predictor learns from simulated formations organized into three attention topologies:

- sequential attention, such as a queue;
- mutual attention, such as a conversation around shared space;
- directed shared attention, such as viewers facing a speaker or exhibit.

The predictor’s inputs are the grounded group, nearby bystanders, language, and an occupancy map. From these, it predicts a spatial energy map and two heading channels. Lower energy means a more likely position, and the heading channels encode orientation. At inference, the system selects a minimum-energy location and its corresponding heading. The occupancy map rules out occupied space. It does not directly tell the model to use a particular chair or other environmental affordance.

### 4. What the evaluations establish

The offline benchmark has 42 scenes: 15 conversations, 15 queues, and 12 audiences. A pose counts as valid only when both position and heading are valid. Position must be 0.5–1.2 m from the relevant group under interaction-specific rules. Heading must be within ±30° of the queue direction, conversation-group centroid, or audience focal entity. These are rule-based validity rates, not a direct measure of universal social acceptability.

On this benchmark, the full pipeline reports **89.76±4.21% pose validity** at **1.108 seconds per query**. The strongest reported few-shot GPT-5.5 comparison reports **59.76±8.73%** at **49.25 seconds**. VLM predictions are projected from image space. Points landing on people are snapped to the nearest free ground position when possible, a correction that favors those baselines.

Grounding is competitive, not the highest-scoring. Measured against 27 human annotators’ group-member judgments, its agreement-weighted F1 is **0.8125** in **0.936 seconds**. The strongest few-shot VLM in that table reaches **0.8311** in **5.48 seconds**.

On a Spot robot, the method succeeds in **18/20 trials** across seated and standing conversations and static and dynamic queues. The paper attributes the two failures to controller and detection errors. In the tested medium-density scenes, with fewer than 10 people, full inference takes under 0.7 seconds. This is a small set of robot trials.

### 5. The goal is the group

The contribution links two predictions: which people the instruction refers to, and where and which way to join them. The main boundary is the environment’s role. The environment is modeled mainly as a constraint on infeasible poses, not as a source of joining affordances such as chairs.
