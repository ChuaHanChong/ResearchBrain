# Faster-WAM: Do World Action Models Need Deep Action Modules?

> [!summary] Summary
> Faster-WAM introduces the Dock of Transformers (DoT) framework, enabling World Action Models to use a single-layer action head that efficiently accesses multi-layer representations from a video backbone. This approach achieves a 3.2x speedup over Fast-WAM, reducing inference latency to 66.5 ms, and improves out-of-distribution generalization by 23.5 percentage points on LIBERO-Plus.

## Problem

- World Action Models (WAMs) often suffer from high inference latency compared to Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models due to deep, computationally intensive action modules.
- Existing WAM architectures, particularly Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) designs, typically couple the depth of the action module to the video backbone, leading to redundant computation and slow inference.
- Prior attempts to reduce WAM latency often compromise control performance by using compact video backbones or simplified action decoding.

## Method

- The **Dock of Transformer (DoT)** framework treats a pretrained video world model as a "representation hub" that lightweight, task-specific action heads can "dock" into through explicit interfaces.
- ==KV-Fusion==, a head-specific module, maps and aggregates keys and values from all layers of the video backbone into the action head's feature space, enabling multi-layer representation access.
- ==Video–Action RoPE alignment== addresses positional encoding mismatches by undoing 3D RoPE from cached video keys, fusing them, and then applying 1D RoPE (matching the action head) before ==mixed-context attention==.

## Results

- Faster-WAM achieved an end-to-end inference latency of **66.5 ms** per action chunk, representing a **3.2x** speedup over Fast-WAM (**211.7 ms**) and matching or surpassing the speed of several VLA models.
- It demonstrated strong control performance, achieving **98.5%** average success on LIBERO and competitive results (**89.17%**) on the RoboTwin 2.0 bimanual manipulation benchmark.
- The model showed enhanced out-of-distribution generalization, improving success rates on LIBERO-Plus by **23.5 percentage points** over Fast-WAM (**75.0%** vs. **51.5%**), with significant gains in camera, sensor noise, and language robustness.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - High-performance WAMs can be achieved with a lightweight, single-layer action head by effectively leveraging multi-layer representations from a pretrained video world model through a dedicated docking interface.
> - Proper alignment of positional encodings across different modalities (e.g., 3D video features and 1D action features) is critical for maintaining temporal consistency and effective cross-modal attention.
> - Routing instruction conditioning through the language-conditioned video backbone is more effective for a lightweight action head than having the head process text cross-attention separately.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot action models is increasingly leveraging large pretrained foundation models. Within this domain, two primary architectural paradigms have emerged: Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models and World Action Models (WAMs). VLA models, typically built upon pretrained large language models (LLMs), directly map visual observations and language instructions to robot actions. In contrast, WAMs center action prediction on video or visual world model backbones that explicitly model temporal evolution and physical dynamics. The extensive video pre-training of WAMs offers advantages in understanding physical interactions, making them appealing for robot control tasks.

However, existing WAM architectures often face limitations related to inference efficiency. Representative low-latency WAMs can exhibit significantly higher inference latency compared to VLA models. Current WAM designs generally fall into two categories:

1. **Shared-backbone methods:** These fine-tune a single video world model backbone to process both video and action tokens. This approach leads to high computational costs for action prediction due to the full denoising steps of the heavy video backbone.
2. **Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) methods:** These pair a video backbone (often a Diffusion Transformer, DiT) with a separate action DiT. The action DiT predicts actions conditioned on representations from the video DiT. While this design allows for some decoupling during inference, many existing MoT-based WAMs maintain an explicit, ordered correspondence between video and action layers. This often results in the action DiT mirroring the depth of the video DiT, leading to substantial inference latency. Some asymmetric designs (e.g., DiT4DiT, MotuBrain’s H-Bridge) have attempted to reduce action layers or restrict cross-stream interaction to intermediate layers. However, these connections remain predetermined and ordered, making it difficult to significantly reduce action expert depth while accessing representations distributed across the entire video backbone.

The presented work, Faster-WAM, addresses the issue of computational overhead and inference latency in WAMs, particularly those based on the MoT paradigm. It proposes a video-centric architectural framework, Dock of Transformers (DoT), which reconfigures the relationship between the video world model and task-specific action prediction. Instead of mirroring deep action modules, Faster-WAM aims to leverage the rich representations already present across all layers of a pretrained video backbone, enabling a lightweight action head without compromising control performance. This approach distinguishes it from prior low-latency WAM efforts (e.g., Light-WAM, Efficient-WAM) that often achieve speedup at the cost of control performance by using compact video backbones or simplified action decoding. By focusing on efficient utilization of a full-scale pretrained video backbone, this research seeks to bridge the latency gap between WAMs and VLA models while maintaining high control fidelity.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from the observed trade-off between the performance benefits of World Action Models (WAMs) and their associated computational overhead and inference latency. While WAMs, built on pretrained video world models, excel at capturing physical dynamics for robot control due to extensive video pre-training, their architectural designs often lead to slow inference.

Specifically, existing WAM architectures, particularly those based on the Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT) paradigm, typically employ action modules whose depth is tied to that of the video backbone. This design choice, common for enabling single-pass co-training through shared attention, results in substantial action-specific computation and high inference latency. For instance, a representative low-latency WAM, Fast-WAM, was noted to require approximately 211.7 ms per action chunk, significantly slower than some VLA models. This latency can be a bottleneck for real-time robotic applications.

The key objectives of this work are articulated as follows:

1. **Develop a WAM that matches the inference speed of VLA models without sacrificing control performance.** This overarching goal motivates the architectural redesign proposed in the paper.
2. **Introduce a video-centric design principle for WAMs.** The premise is that a pretrained video world model already possesses rich representations of physical dynamics. Therefore, instead of duplicating this knowledge in a separate, deep action expert, a more efficient approach would be to treat the video world model as a central "representation hub."
3. **Enable lightweight, task-specific output heads to directly access and aggregate representations from all layers of a central video backbone.** This objective aims to decouple the depth of the action module from the video backbone, allowing for substantially shallower action heads. This contrasts with existing MoT architectures where an explicit, ordered correspondence between video and action layers is maintained.
4. **Design an effective "docking interface" to facilitate this multi-layer representation access.** This interface must address technical challenges, such as reconciling different positional encoding schemes (e.g., 3D RoPE for video and 1D RoPE for actions) that arise when fusing representations from distinct modalities or processing streams.
5. **Validate the proposed architecture on standard robot control benchmarks** to demonstrate that reduced action-specific computation can still yield competitive control performance and robust generalization, particularly under out-of-distribution conditions.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to improve the practical applicability of WAMs by making them more computationally efficient while retaining their performance advantages.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology introduced in this paper is the **Dock of Transformer (DoT)**, an architectural framework based on a video-centric design principle. This principle posits that a pretrained video world model functions as a "representation hub," from which lightweight, task-specific output heads can predict actions.

#### 3.1. Dock of Transformer (DoT) Framework

DoT treats a pretrained video Transformer as a central hub with `L_v` layers. Task-specific modules, such as an action head with `L_a` layers, "dock" into this hub through explicit docking interfaces, denoted `g(·)`. This design allows the head to access multi-level representations from the hub (specifically, `{K_v^(ℓ), V_v^(ℓ)}` for `ℓ=1, ..., L_v`) without requiring `L_a = L_v` or a predetermined layer mapping. For any head layer `j`, the docking mechanism aggregates these multi-level video-hub representations into `(eK_v^(j), eV_v^(j))`. The action head then performs mixed-context attention by concatenating its own action KV cache with the docked video KV cache.

#### 3.1.1. KV-Fusion Docking Mechanism

The docking mechanism `g(·)` is instantiated as **KV-Fusion**, which is a head-specific module designed to map video backbone keys and values into the action head's feature space and fuse them across all video backbone layers.
- **Channel Mixing:** As the video hub and action head operate in different feature spaces, separate projections (`W_K`, `W_V`) are applied to the cached video keys and values to remap them into the action-head feature space. This results in channel-mixed `K̄_v` and `V̄_v`.
- **Layer Mixing:** Following channel mixing, representations are aggregated along the video-layer axis. For each attention head `h` in the action module, a learnable cross-layer aggregation matrix `A_h` is used to compute the fused keys `eK_v^h` and values `eV_v^h`. The same coefficients from `A_h` are applied to both keys and values to maintain consistency.

#### 3.1.2. Video–Action RoPE Alignment

A critical component of the docking interface is **video–action RoPE alignment**. This mechanism addresses a mismatch in positional encoding. In conventional MoT-based WAMs, directly reusing video keys (encoded with 3D Rotary Positional Embeddings, RoPE) in attention calculations with action queries (encoded with 1D RoPE) leads to an invalid cross-modal product. The relative-position property of RoPE, which is fundamental to its effectiveness, is undermined when query and key use different rotary bases (e.g., 1D temporal vs. 3D temporal-width-height).

DoT mitigates this by separating the hub's self-attention from the head's mixed attention. The procedure involves:
1. Undoing the 3D RoPE from the cached video keys, recovering their canonical, unrotated representation.
2. Performing KV-Fusion in this canonical feature space.
3. Applying key normalization.
4. Applying the 1D RoPE (used by the action head) to the fused video keys before mixed-context attention. This ensures that the fused video keys share the same rotary basis as the action queries, restoring the relative-position consistency.

#### 3.2. Instantiation of DoT: Faster-WAM

Faster-WAM is the specific instantiation of DoT for WAMs. It adopts the training objectives and inference procedure of Fast-WAM.
- **Architecture:** It utilizes Wan2.2-TI2V-5B, a 30-layer video DiT, as the representation hub (`L_v = 30`). A single-layer action DiT (`L_a = 1`) serves as the action head.
- **Instruction Conditioning:** Following the video-centric design, text cross-attention is removed from the action head. Language information is instead incorporated into the video backbone (hub) and then exposed to the action head through the docking interface. This allows the action head to focus primarily on action prediction.
- **Training Details:** The video backbone, single-layer action head, KV-Fusion module, and proprioceptive encoder are jointly optimized, while the video VAE remains frozen. An action horizon of H=32 is used. Video sequences are temporally downsampled, and multiple camera views are spatially concatenated before VAE encoding. The flow-matching timestep `t` is sampled from a logit-normal distribution. AdamW optimizer with a cosine-annealing learning-rate schedule is employed.
- **Inference Procedure:** During inference, the video backbone processes only the observed conditioning frame. The action module then generates future actions conditioned on the resulting video key–value representations via the docking interface, using 10 denoising steps with a classifier-free guidance scale of 1.0. End-to-end latency is measured from input reception to complete action output generation.

#### 3.3. Experimental Evaluation

Faster-WAM was evaluated on three benchmarks:
- **LIBERO (Liu et al., 2023):** For standard control tasks.
- **RoboTwin 2.0 (Chen et al., 2025):** For bimanual manipulation, including clean and randomized scenes.
- **LIBERO-Plus (Fei et al., 2026):** For assessing generalization under various distribution shifts (e.g., background, camera, language, lighting, object, robot, sensor perturbations).

Baselines included various VLA and WAM models, both with and without embodied pretraining. Performance metrics included success rates and end-to-end inference latency on a 24 GB consumer GPU. Ablation studies were conducted on LIBERO-Plus to quantify the contribution of each design component (final-layer access, KV-Fusion, RoPE alignment, removal of text cross-attention).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of Faster-WAM yielded several findings regarding its performance, efficiency, and generalization capabilities.

#### 4.1. Performance on LIBERO and Inference Efficiency

- **Control Performance:** Faster-WAM achieved an average success rate of 98.5% on the LIBERO benchmark, which exceeded Fast-WAM (97.6%) by 0.9 percentage points. It matched or outperformed Fast-WAM across all individual suites, with a notable gain of 2.6 percentage points on LIBERO-Long. Despite using a single-layer action head and no additional embodied pretraining, Faster-WAM's performance was comparable to embodied-pretrained models like LingBot-VA and surpassed π0.5, X-VLA, and Motus.
- **Inference Latency:** Faster-WAM demonstrated reduced end-to-end inference latency. It generated a complete 32-step action chunk in 66.5 ms on a 24 GB consumer GPU. This represents a 3.2× speedup compared to Fast-WAM (211.7 ms) under controlled conditions. Faster-WAM also exhibited lower latency than representative VLA models such as π0 (68.2 ms), π0.5 (71.4 ms), and X-VLA (105.3 ms). Other WAMs like LingBot-VA and Motus could not be tested under the same hardware constraints due to higher memory requirements.

#### 4.2. Performance on RoboTwin 2.0

- On the RoboTwin 2.0 bimanual manipulation benchmark, Faster-WAM achieved an average success rate of 89.17%, with similar performance in clean (89.70%) and randomized (88.64%) scenes.
- This performance surpassed the embodied-pretrained Motus (87.84%) and approached that of LingBot-VA (92.20%).
- Faster-WAM trailed Fast-WAM by 2.47 percentage points on average, demonstrating that the reduction to a single-layer action expert maintained competitive performance on complex bimanual tasks and under scene randomization.

#### 4.3. Generalization on LIBERO-Plus

- When evaluated on LIBERO-Plus for generalization under distribution shifts, Faster-WAM achieved an overall success rate of 75.0%. This represented a 23.5 percentage point improvement over Fast-WAM (51.5%).
- Performance improved across all seven perturbation categories, with substantial gains observed in camera perturbations (from 16.4% to 67.9%) and sensor noise (from 37.7% to 82.7%).
- Faster-WAM also exhibited the highest language robustness among the compared methods in this category, improving from 68.9% to 92.1%.
- Despite the absence of embodied pretraining, Faster-WAM outperformed embodied-pretrained models like π0 (53.6%) and X-VLA (71.4%) on this benchmark. The remaining performance gap to GE-ACT and π0.5 suggests that large-scale embodied pretraining can provide complementary benefits under more significant distribution shifts.

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies

A sequential ablation study on LIBERO-Plus quantified the contribution of Faster-WAM's design elements:
1. **Simple DoT (final-layer access only):** A single-layer action head accessing only the final layer of the video hub improved the success rate from a baseline of 49.5% (conventional layerwise coupling) to 60.3%. This indicated that a compact, video-centric design can offer better generalization even with basic information access.
2. **Adding KV-Fusion:** Incorporating KV-Fusion, which allows the action head to access representations from all video hub layers, further improved performance to 66.8%. Analysis of learned layer-mixing signals revealed that while the strongest fusion signals were concentrated in intermediate video layers, substantial signals were distributed across the entire backbone, indicating multi-depth representation usage.
3. **Adding Video–Action RoPE Alignment:** Applying cross-module RoPE alignment increased the success rate to 71.3%. This demonstrated the importance of correcting the positional-basis mismatch between fused video keys and action queries for temporally consistent attention.
4. **Removing Text Cross-Attention from Action Head:** Eliminating text cross-attention from the action head further enhanced the success rate to 75.0%. This result suggested that routing instruction conditioning through the language-conditioned video hub was more effective than having the lightweight action head model it separately.

These ablation results confirm the individual and cumulative benefits of the proposed DoT architecture and its specific instantiation in Faster-WAM.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a set of findings that hold implications for the design and deployment of robotic control systems based on large foundation models.

**Reduced Computational Overhead and Enhanced Efficiency:** The primary significance of Faster-WAM lies in its demonstration that high-performance World Action Models (WAMs) do not necessarily require deep, computationally intensive action modules. By replacing a deep action expert with a single-layer action head and an efficient docking interface, the work achieves a 3.2× speedup over a representative low-latency WAM (Fast-WAM) and matches or surpasses the inference speeds of some Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models. This substantial reduction in end-to-end latency can enable real-time robot control in latency-sensitive applications, making WAMs more practical for deployment in physical robots.

**Improved Generalization Capabilities:** The results on LIBERO-Plus indicate that Faster-WAM exhibits robust out-of-distribution generalization. The architecture's ability to significantly outperform Fast-WAM and even some embodied-pretrained VLA models under various perturbations (e.g., camera, sensor noise, language) suggests that effective utilization of a pre-trained video world model's representations, rather than duplicating task-specific computation, can lead to more generalizable policies. This has implications for developing robots that can operate reliably in diverse and unstructured environments without extensive task-specific data collection for every new scenario.

**Flexible Architectural Design Paradigm (DoT):** The introduction of the Dock of Transformers (DoT) framework offers a new architectural paradigm for robot learning. By treating a pretrained video world model as a "representation hub" and allowing lightweight task-specific heads to "dock" into it through flexible interfaces, DoT opens a design space for efficient and modular robot policies. This approach shifts focus from scaling up task-specific models to more effectively exploiting the rich, pre-existing knowledge embedded in general-purpose world models. This modularity could simplify the development and integration of new robotic capabilities.

**Revalidation of World Model Utility:** The work reinforces the central premise of WAMs: that video world models, pre-trained on extensive temporal data, capture comprehensive representations of physical dynamics. Faster-WAM's performance, despite its lightweight action head, suggests that these intrinsic dynamics are a potent source of information that, when accessed effectively, can drive robust robot control. The success of the video-centric design supports continued investment in powerful video foundation models as a core component for generalist robot intelligence.

**Broader Applicability and Future Research Directions:** The DoT framework is presented as generalizable beyond action prediction. The authors suggest its potential for other robot learning tasks such as navigation and affordance prediction. This implies that the principles of efficient multi-layer representation access and alignment could be applied to a wider array of robotic tasks, potentially fostering new avenues for research in efficient, modular, and generalizable robot learning. Future work evaluating Faster-WAM on real robots will be a critical step in validating its practical impact.
