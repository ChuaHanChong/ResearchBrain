# SPARK: Safe Protective and Assistive Robot Kit

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Carnegie Mellon University developed SPARK, a modular benchmark and toolbox for safe control of high-dimensional humanoid robots. It provides a framework for synthesizing, testing, and deploying safe controllers in simulation and on real hardware, demonstrating robust safety enforcement and real-time performance on a Unitree G1 humanoid.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots pose significant safety risks due to their physical complexity and potential for interaction with dynamic environments, requiring robust safety solutions.
- Existing safe control toolboxes and benchmarks often lack modularity for high-dimensional robots and do not explicitly prioritize or benchmark safety aspects for humanoids.
- Synthesizing and deploying safe controllers for diverse humanoid applications is difficult and inefficient for non-experts, as it often requires re-synthesis for each unique scenario and lacks standardized evaluation.

## Method

- **SPARK** introduces a ==modular framework== decomposing control systems into five core components (Configuration, Agent, Task, Policy, Safety modules) supporting both simulation and real-world deployment.
- It implements five ==energy-function-based safe control algorithms== (SSA, CBF, SSS, PFM, SMA) and provides a suite of configuration options for robot models, dynamics, task objectives, and constraints.
- The framework automates ==hierarchical computation of safety index derivatives== to efficiently support model-based safe control algorithms, enabling real-time operation for complex systems.

## Results

- SSS achieved the highest success rate for whole-body motion tasks, while SMA performed best for fixed-base tasks, with PFM consistently showing the lowest success rates across all benchmarks.
- Dynamic obstacles and increased obstacle density negatively impacted both arm tracking scores and environmental safety scores, demonstrating performance degradation with higher task complexity.
- SPARK achieved real-time operation on a laptop CPU, with control loop times around **0.011s to 0.015s** on the real Unitree G1 robot, demonstrating feasibility for real-time deployment in various scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimization-based safe control methods (SSA, CBF, SSS) generally offer a better balance between motion accuracy and environmental safety for humanoids compared to projection methods like PFM.
> - The framework effectively translates safe control performance from simulation to real-world humanoid robots, showing strong correlations in safety and goal tracking metrics.
> - Integrating real-time human perception (e.g., via Apple Vision Pro) enables humanoid robots to prioritize collision avoidance over task goals in dynamic human-robot interaction scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of the Safe Protective and Assistive Robot Kit (SPARK) addresses a critical area within robotics: ensuring safety in autonomous and teleoperated systems, particularly for high-dimensional and complex platforms such as humanoid robots. This work is positioned at the intersection of safe control, humanoid robotics, and benchmarking frameworks.

Existing research has explored various aspects of safe control. Modular safe control toolboxes, such as the Benchmark of Interactive Safety (BIS) and Control Barrier Function (CBF) toolboxes, offer foundational algorithms. However, these often lack the modularity for seamless integration with other frameworks or do not cater to the specific complexities of high-dimensional robots like humanoids.

In the domain of benchmark platforms for safety-critical tasks, advancements in safe reinforcement learning (RL) have led to the creation of environments like GUARD, Safety-Gymnasium, and Safe Control Gym. These platforms are primarily tailored for learning-based policies and often do not emphasize model-based methods, which are crucial for analytical rigor and interpretability in safety-critical applications. Furthermore, these benchmarks generally do not address the unique challenges presented by high-dimensional robotic systems such as humanoids.

Concurrently, significant progress has been made in developing benchmark platforms specifically for humanoid robots, focusing on areas such as whole-body locomotion and manipulation (HumanoidBench, Humanoid-Gym), human-scene interaction (Mimicking-Bench), in-home manipulation (MS-HAB, BiGym), and teleoperation (H2O, OmniH2O). While these platforms facilitate the evaluation of advanced control strategies, including reinforcement learning-based approaches (HumanPlus, ExBody, ExBody2, HOVER), they generally do not prioritize or specifically benchmark the *safety* aspects of humanoid operation.

SPARK aims to bridge these gaps by providing a unified, modular framework that explicitly focuses on synthesizing, testing, benchmarking, and deploying *safe* controllers for complex humanoid robots. By supporting both simulation and real-world deployment, integrating with advanced sensing, and emphasizing model-based control while allowing for extensibility to data-driven methods, SPARK contributes a platform designed to accelerate research and development in safe humanoid autonomy and human-robot interaction. It provides a standardized environment for comparing safe control algorithms and addressing the unique safety challenges of humanoid systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of SPARK is to provide a comprehensive benchmark and toolbox for ensuring safety in humanoid robot autonomy and teleoperation. This overarching goal is motivated by several key challenges inherent in the deployment of complex robotic systems, particularly humanoids.

Firstly, humanoid robots pose significant safety risks due to their physical capabilities and their potential for interaction with dynamic and complex environments. Their intricate physical structures and high degrees of freedom (DoFs) introduce substantial complexity in designing general safety solutions. The paper highlights that current model-free data-driven approaches often lack robust safety assurance in high-dimensional systems, and formal verification of these methods typically requires an accurate system model. Therefore, the work focuses on model-based approaches for their analytical rigor and interpretability.

Secondly, a fundamental challenge identified is the inefficiency and difficulty for non-experts to synthesize and deploy safe controllers in diverse applications. This process requires:
- **Easy specification** of both task objectives and safety constraints.
- **Informed selection** of appropriate safe control approaches from a range of options.
- **Tuning-free deployment** on real hardware with minimal friction.

The paper elaborates on the limitations of current practices. Synthesizing safe controllers from scratch for every unique scenario (e.g., a differential-drive vehicle versus a humanoid, navigation versus manipulation, autonomous versus teleoperated control, or varying environments with static versus dynamic obstacles) is time-consuming and resource-intensive. Each change in robot dynamics, task requirements, or environmental conditions often necessitates a complete re-synthesis by experts. Conversely, designing a single, universal safe controller capable of handling all possible scenarios is considered highly challenging, if not impossible. This inherent inefficiency hinders the practical development and deployment of safe controllers across real-world robotic applications.

To address these motivations, SPARK was designed with three core principles:
- **Composability:** To enable users to assemble modular components for custom task goals and safety requirements, facilitate large-scale benchmark generation, and allow easy scenario switching by module replacement.
- **Extensibility:** To permit users to customize individual modules (e.g., developing new controllers, modifying robot dynamics), integrate external sensors or task planners, and seamlessly bridge with other benchmarks.
- **Deployability:** To support the deployment of synthesized safe control algorithms on real robotic hardware by wrapping hardware SDKs with SPARK interfaces, ensure compatibility with real-time middleware like ROS and DDS, and maintain robustness in diverse real-world task settings, including human-robot interaction and teleoperation.

Ultimately, the motivation behind SPARK is to lower the barrier for safe controller synthesis and deployment, thereby accelerating research and enabling robots to operate reliably and safely in varied real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPARK employs a modular framework to facilitate the synthesis, testing, benchmarking, development, and deployment of safe controllers for robotic systems. The methodology is structured around decomposing complex robotic control systems into distinct, interacting components, which are then implemented as modular Python class templates.

**A. Components of a Safety-Critical Robotic Scenario**
The framework identifies five essential components in synthesizing a robotic control system:
- **System State:** Encompasses both the robot's internal state (e.g., joint positions, velocities) and the external state (e.g., obstacle positions, human presence). This can be from simulation or the real world.
- **System Dynamics:** User-defined models (analytical or data-driven) approximating the robot's internal dynamics and external environmental dynamics, tailored for the controller.
- **System Objectives:** Includes task-specific goals (e.g., trajectory following, target reaching) and safety constraints (e.g., collision avoidance). These can originate from pre-coded programs, user-defined criteria, teleoperator inputs, or AI planners.
- **System Measurements:** Information obtained from external sensors (e.g., LiDAR, odometry, Apple Vision Pro) to inform the controller about the system state.
- **System Controller:** Algorithms that optimize control inputs based on dynamics, objectives, and measurements to achieve tasks while maintaining safety.

**B. SPARK's Modular Framework**
SPARK organizes these components into five core modules:
- **Configuration Module:** Defines robot-specific details (DoFs, motor interfaces) and system models, typically using MuJoCo XML files, and provides essential context for other modules.
- **Environment (Agent and Task Modules):**
 - **Agent Module:** Handles interaction with the robot, either simulated (MuJoCo-based) or real (Unitree G1 SDK). It receives control commands and modifies the robot's state.
 - **Task Module:** Gathers and integrates information including system state, robot state, and user-specified goals. This module decouples information collection from the control algorithms, enhancing modularity and reusability.
- **Algorithm Module (Policy and Safety Submodules):**
 - **Policy Module:** Generates nominal control actions focused on performance objectives (e.g., reaching a goal) without considering safety constraints.
 - **Safety Module:** Refines the control actions from the Policy module to ensure adherence to safety constraints, aiming to follow nominal actions as closely as possible. It supports both model-based and data-driven controllers.

**C. SPARK Suite Options for Benchmarking and Deployment**
SPARK provides a comprehensive suite of options for configuring and evaluating safe control systems:

- **Configuration Options:**
 - **Robot Models:** Four types based on the Unitree G1 humanoid: G1RightArm (7 DoFs), G1FixedBase (17 DoFs, fixed pelvis), G1MobileBase (20 DoFs, floating base for locomotion), and G1SportMode (20 DoFs, bipedal locomotion via learned policy).
 - **Dynamic Order:** Two models: Dynamic1 (single integrator, control is joint velocity) and Dynamic2 (double integrator, control is joint acceleration).
- **Agent Options:**
 - **Simulation Agent:** MuJoCo-based for algorithm evaluation, allowing access to full state information.
 - **Real Robot Agent:** Interface for the Unitree G1 humanoid, utilizing its high-level control API.
- **Task Options:**
 - **Task Objectives:** Arm Goal (manipulation, static/dynamic 3D targets) and Base Goal (navigation, static/dynamic 2D targets). Whole Body Goal (WG) combines both.
 - **Task Constraints:** Primarily collision avoidance, including self-collision and environmental collision. Collision volumes are approximated using analytic geometric primitives (Spheres, Boxes) for efficient signed distance and gradient computation (O(1) per pair). Obstacles can be static or dynamic, and their number is configurable (e.g., v0 with 10 obstacles, v1 with 50).
 - **Task Interface:** Keyboard for simulation-based obstacle control; Apple Vision Pro for real-time human hand tracking (used as dynamic obstacles or teleoperation goals) in real robot scenarios.
- **Policy Options:** Default policies include PID control and inverse kinematics (IK) for locomotion and manipulation. The framework is designed to support user-defined and data-driven policies.
- **Safety Options:** SPARK implements five energy-function-based safe control algorithms:
 - **Safe Set Algorithm (SSA):** An optimization-based method that extends to multi-constrained cases, using slack variables to manage feasibility.
 - **Control Barrier Function (CBF):** An optimization-based method that enforces continuous safety constraints by ensuring the time derivative of the energy function remains below a certain bound.
 - **Sublevel Safe Set Algorithm (SSS):** Combines SSA and CBF, applying control correction only when the safety index indicates an unsafe state.
 - **Potential Field Method (PFM):** A projection method that adds a repulsive "force" in Cartesian space to avoid obstacles.
 - **Sliding Mode Algorithm (SMA):** A projection method that corrects control input by focusing on the most critical safety index to maintain the system state around a sliding layer.
 - The framework automates the hierarchical computation of the time derivative of the safety index (˙ϕ) from Cartesian to configuration space, including analytical gradient derivation, to efficiently support these algorithms.
- **Evaluation Metrics:** Four metrics are provided for assessing performance: step-wise average arm goal tracking score (J_arm), base goal tracking score (J_base), self-safety score (M_self), and environment safety score (M_env). These scores are derived from distances to goals or violated distances, transformed by an exponential function for scaling.

The methodology emphasizes the modularization of value function computation and its time derivative, allowing users to focus on safety index design and control law implementation. This structure supports several use cases: benchmarking, simulated teleoperation, real robot autonomy, and real robot teleoperation, by simply reconfiguring modules.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SPARK focused on benchmarking various safe control algorithms under diverse conditions and demonstrating its utility in simulated and real-world teleoperation and autonomous tasks.

**A. Overall Benchmark Results (Q1)**
Experiments across eight benchmark tasks, combining robot configurations (G1FixedBase, G1MobileBase), dynamics (D1), task objectives (Arm Goal, Whole Body Goal), and constraints (Static Obstacles, Dynamic Obstacles, varying numbers of obstacles), yielded differential performance among the safe control algorithms:
- **PFM** exhibited the weakest overall performance in terms of both motion accuracy (J_arm, J_base) and environmental safety (M_env). Its reliance on repulsive forces in low-dimensional Cartesian space was found to be insufficient for handling complex, high-dimensional joint space constraints and multiple obstacles.
- **Optimization-based methods (SSA, CBF, SSS)** generally demonstrated a superior balance between safety and efficiency.
- **SSA** showed stable environmental safety and strong adaptability to external constraints. However, its motion accuracy was somewhat reduced in fixed-base tasks.
- **CBF** excelled in environmental safety, minimizing collisions, but sometimes exhibited slightly lower motion accuracy compared to SSA.
- **SSS** consistently showed stable safety and efficiency across tasks. In some mobile robot tasks, its motion accuracy was comparable to SSA and CBF, with environmental safety performance sometimes exceeding other methods. In fixed-base tasks, SSS achieved higher motion accuracy among optimization methods.
- **SMA** achieved a commendable trade-off between safety and efficiency despite not being an optimization-based method, often showing higher motion accuracy and stable environmental safety in most tasks.

**B. Trade-off between Safety and Efficiency (Q2)**
Tuning the parameters of each algorithm revealed characteristic trade-off curves. SMA and PFM, which act as projection methods, could achieve a better balance by reverting closer to the nominal controller when their respective parameters approached zero. However, PFM's trade-off curve was less favorable due to its limitations in complex environments. Optimization-based methods (SSA, SSS, CBF) prioritized safety. CBF, in particular, showed a higher concentration of parameter settings near the origin on the trade-off plot, indicating scenarios where it struggled to achieve both safety and efficiency, likely due to its continuous enforcement of safety constraints which can unnecessarily impact efficiency if not tuned properly.

**C. Impacts of Constraint Types and Task Difficulty (Q3, Q4)**
- **Dynamic obstacles** resulted in decreased Arm Tracking Scores and Environment Safety Scores compared to static obstacles.
- **Increased obstacle density** (e.g., v1 with 50 obstacles vs. v0 with 10) further reduced both Arm Tracking Score and Environment Safety Score, indicating a negative impact on performance with higher complexity.
- **Task difficulty** significantly affected performance. Whole-body tasks requiring tracking both arm and base goals generally resulted in lower scores compared to fixed-base tasks, as the robot had to manage more complex dynamics and potentially sacrifice arm goal tracking for base goal achievement or collision avoidance.

**D. Success Rate across Algorithms (Q5)**
- **SSS** achieved the highest success rate for whole-body motion tasks, followed by CBF.
- **SMA** attained the highest success rate for fixed-base tasks, with SSS and SSA also performing well.
- **PFM** consistently showed the lowest success rate across all tasks.
- Conditional success rate analysis indicated that optimization-based methods (SSA, SSS, CBF) generally outperformed PFM and SMA. Specifically, SSA often successfully completed trajectories that CBF could, but the reverse was less frequent.

**E. Use Case Demonstrations**
- **Simulated Teleoperation (Use Case 2):** A Unitree G1 robot successfully teleoperated to retrieve objects from a confined cabinet. The safe controller effectively prevented the robot's hands from leaving the cabinet, even when teleoperation targets were outside, demonstrating safety enforcement.
- **Real Robot Autonomy (Use Case 3):** A physical Unitree G1 robot, using an Apple Vision Pro for perception, autonomously avoided a human hand. The robot moved away from the hand when the minimum distance threshold was breached and resumed its nominal static or dynamic trajectory (e.g., circular path tracking) once the environment was clear, prioritizing safety over goal tracking.
- **Real Robot Teleoperation (Use Case 4):** In a scenario where the robot was teleoperated to pick up objects from a table, if a human user reached for the same object, the safe controller activated, causing the robot to prioritize collision avoidance over teleoperation commands. This highlights safe human-robot interaction in unpredictable environments.
- **Sim-to-Real Comparison:** Quantitative data (Table 4) showed a strong correlation (high Pearson r values, low p-values) between simulation and real robot performance for key metrics (goal distance, self-collision distance, environment collision distance) in both teleoperation and autonomy scenarios, suggesting effective transfer from simulation to reality.
- **Computational Costs:** SPARK achieved real-time operation on a laptop CPU, with control loop times averaging around 0.013s (approx. 77 Hz) in simulation and 0.011s (approx. 90 Hz) on the real robot in teleoperation mode. When the safe controller was triggered, times increased slightly (e.g., 0.017s sim, 0.015s real for teleop), indicating feasibility for real-time deployment.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SPARK represents a significant contribution to the field of robotics by addressing the critical challenge of ensuring safety in humanoid robot operations. Its significance and potential impact can be categorized as follows:

**A. Advancing Safe Control Research for Humanoids:**
SPARK fills a notable gap in existing research by providing a dedicated, comprehensive benchmark for safe control of high-dimensional humanoid robots. Previous benchmarks primarily focused on reinforcement learning or simpler robotic systems, or on general humanoid control without an explicit emphasis on safety. By offering a standardized framework, SPARK enables researchers to:
- **Systematically compare and evaluate** a wide array of safe control algorithms, including both model-based and potentially data-driven approaches.
- **Gain insights** into the trade-offs between safety and performance, as well as the impact of various task complexities and constraint types on algorithmic efficacy.
- **Accelerate the development of new safe control methods** by providing a modular and extensible platform for rapid prototyping and testing.

**B. Lowering Barriers to Safe Robot Deployment:**
The modular and composable design of SPARK simplifies the process of synthesizing and deploying safe controllers, making it more accessible to non-experts.
- **Composability** allows users to mix and match predefined or custom modules for task goals, safety requirements, and robot configurations, reducing the need to build systems from scratch.
- **Extensibility** supports the integration of novel control algorithms, sensors (e.g., Apple Vision Pro), and task planners, fostering innovation.
- **Deployability** on real hardware, demonstrated with the Unitree G1 humanoid robot, and compatibility with real-time middleware ensure that research findings can transition from simulation to real-world applications with reduced friction. This is crucial for practical applications where safety is paramount, such as in assistive robotics or industrial settings with human interaction.

**C. Enhancing Human-Robot Interaction Safety:**
The framework directly supports scenarios involving human-robot interaction and teleoperation, which are areas of growing importance for humanoid robots.
- SPARK's ability to integrate real-time human perception (e.g., via Apple Vision Pro) and prioritize safety over nominal task objectives in dynamic environments demonstrates its potential to enable safer collaborative and assistive humanoid operations.
- The comparison between simulation and real-world performance, showing strong correlations in safety and goal tracking metrics, builds confidence in transferring controllers validated in simulation to physical robots.

**D. Facilitating Future Research and Development:**
SPARK also lays a foundation for future advancements by identifying current limitations and outlining future work. This includes:
- Integrating state-of-the-art safe reinforcement learning approaches.
- Extending compatibility with standardized safety assessment pipelines, such as formal verification tools.
- Simplifying task specification through intuitive interfaces and natural language processing.
- Automating the selection and tuning of safety strategies, potentially through meta-control techniques.

In conclusion, SPARK's creation of a robust, modular, and deployable framework for safe humanoid control has the potential to significantly accelerate research, improve the safety and reliability of humanoid robots in diverse real-world applications, and ultimately contribute to the broader acceptance and integration of these complex machines into human environments.
