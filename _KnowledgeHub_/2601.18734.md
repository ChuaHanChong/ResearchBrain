# Self-Distilled Reasoner: On-Policy Self-Distillation for Large Language Models

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD), a framework enabling a single Large Language Model to improve its mathematical reasoning by simultaneously acting as a teacher and student. OPSD achieves performance comparable to or better than RL-based methods like GRPO while demonstrating 4-8x higher token efficiency on competition-level math benchmarks.

## Problem

- Existing Reinforcement Learning (RL) methods for LLM reasoning are computationally expensive, requiring multiple rollouts and suffering from sparse reward signals.
- Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) can lead to exposure bias, where models trained on expert trajectories struggle with out-of-distribution sequences during inference.
- Current on-policy distillation methods require a separate, often larger and costly, teacher LLM, adding complexity and computational overhead to the training process.

## Method

- A single LLM is instantiated as both a ==student policy== (generating responses from problem context) and a ==teacher policy== (generating responses conditioned on the problem and ground-truth solution).
- The student policy samples its own trajectories, and the learning objective minimizes the ==per-token divergence== between the teacher's (ground-truth informed) and student's next-token probability distributions.
- Gradients are backpropagated only through the student policy, allowing the model to provide itself with dense, ==token-level feedback== without altering the teacher's 'knowledge' or requiring an external teacher.

## Results

- OPSD consistently matched or exceeded the performance of RL-based GRPO and outperformed SFT across Qwen3 models (**1.7B**, **4B**, **8B**) on competition-level mathematical reasoning benchmarks.
- The method demonstrated a **4-8x** improvement in token efficiency compared to GRPO, utilizing only one rollout per problem and substantially fewer generated tokens without sacrificing accuracy.
- Performance gains from OPSD were more pronounced with larger model scales (**4B** and **8B** parameters), confirming the hypothesis that the framework benefits from increased model capacity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An LLM can effectively serve as its own teacher by leveraging ground-truth solutions as 'privileged information' for the teacher policy, guiding self-improvement.
> - On-policy training combined with dense, full-vocabulary token-level feedback provides a superior learning signal compared to sparse rewards or sampled-token distillation, enhancing efficiency and accuracy.
> - The benefits of On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) scale with model capacity, indicating that sufficiently capable models can better utilize self-rationalization and privileged information for reasoning improvement.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has led to advancements in reasoning and instruction following. Post-training these models for enhanced capabilities typically employs methods such as reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on high-quality datasets, or knowledge distillation. Each of these approaches possesses distinct characteristics and limitations.

Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), exemplified by algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) and Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), optimizes LLMs based on verifiable outcomes (e.g., correctness of a mathematical answer). While effective, RLVR methods face inefficiencies due to: (1) the high computational cost of sampling multiple responses per prompt and the potential for vanishing gradient signals when all samples yield identical rewards; and (2) the sparsity of reward signals, which are typically applied uniformly at the sequence level, lacking fine-grained token-level feedback.

Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT), which trains models on high-quality reasoning datasets, provides dense token-level supervision but is susceptible to exposure bias and can exhibit weaker generalization due to training on fixed expert trajectories.

Knowledge distillation aims to transfer knowledge from a larger, more capable teacher model to a smaller student model. Traditional, off-policy distillation methods, while providing dense token-level supervision, suffer from distribution mismatch. This occurs because the student is trained on expert-generated sequences but generates its own sequences during inference, potentially leading to compounding errors. On-policy distillation methods address this by having the student generate its own trajectories, with the teacher providing dense token-level supervision on these self-generated samples, thereby mitigating distribution shift and improving sample efficiency. However, a common characteristic of these on-policy distillation approaches is the reliance on a separate, often larger, teacher LLM.

The presented work, On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD), is situated at the intersection of on-policy distillation and self-training paradigms. It seeks to overcome the limitation of requiring a distinct teacher model in on-policy distillation by enabling a single LLM to serve as both teacher and student. This approach leverages ground-truth solutions, a form of privileged information, to provide dense, token-level supervision on the student's own rollouts. By doing so, OPSD aims to combine the benefits of on-policy training and dense feedback with the self-sufficiency of using a single model, thereby addressing the computational and logistical challenges associated with maintaining separate, larger teacher models. It also differentiates itself from other self-training methods like STaR and ReST by employing soft, token-level distillation rather than relying on hard sequence filtering and SFT.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while on-policy distillation has proven effective in enhancing LLM reasoning by providing dense token-level supervision on student-generated trajectories, it typically necessitates a distinct and often larger teacher model. Given that modern LLMs already possess considerable reasoning capabilities, the authors pose a central research question: **Can a single LLM effectively function as its own teacher through self-distillation?**

This question is inspired by the human learning process, where a student can learn from examining and rationalizing a correct solution after making an error. For LLMs, it has been noted that evaluating an answer is often simpler than generating one. The authors hypothesize that rationalizing (explaining a given correct answer) is similarly easier than generating a correct solution from scratch.

Based on this intuition, the key objectives of the On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) framework are:

1. **Eliminate the need for a separate teacher LLM:** To develop a training paradigm where a single model can assume both teacher and student roles, thereby reducing computational and logistical overhead.
2. **Leverage ground-truth solutions directly:** To explicitly incorporate privileged information, such as verified reasoning traces or ground-truth answers (y⋆), into the training process. This allows the model, when acting as a teacher, to rationalize these solutions and guide its student counterpart.
3. **Provide dense, token-level supervision on on-policy samples:** To combine the advantages of on-policy training (reducing distribution mismatch) with the rich feedback of token-level supervision (addressing the sparsity of traditional RL rewards). The aim is for the teacher policy, conditioned on privileged information, to provide next-token distributions that the student policy attempts to match.
4. **Improve training efficiency for reasoning tasks:** To develop a method that can match or exceed the performance of existing techniques like SFT and GRPO, particularly in terms of token efficiency and overall training cost.

In essence, the research aims to enable an LLM to "teach its weaker self" by exposing a privileged teacher policy (informed by ground truth) to the student's own generated reasoning steps, fostering continuous improvement without external expert models or sparse reward signals.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) framework allows a single large language model to serve as both a teacher and a student by conditioning on different contexts. The methodology is structured around leveraging a verifiable reasoning dataset, defining distinct policy roles, and minimizing token-level divergence.

**Learning from Verifiable Reasoning Datasets:**
The method utilizes a dataset $S = \{(x_i, y^\star_i)\}^N_{i=1}$, where $x_i$ represents a problem and $y^\star_i$ is its corresponding reference solution, potentially including chain-of-thought reasoning. Unlike Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) which trains on fixed expert trajectories, or Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) like GRPO that uses sparse binary rewards, OPSD aims for dense, on-policy feedback without requiring external process reward models.

**Teacher and Student Policy Instantiation:**
The core of OPSD involves instantiating two conditional distributions from the *same* language model, $p_\theta$, by varying their input context:
- **Teacher Policy** ($p_T(\cdot | x, y^\star)$): Conditions on both the problem $x$ and the privileged reference solution $y^\star$. This allows the teacher to "rationalize" the correct reasoning path, even though it does not explicitly generate new tokens during the loss computation. The teacher is provided with a prompt that includes the reference solution and asks it to solve the problem "using its own approach below," implying an implicit rationalization step.
- **Student Policy** ($p_S(\cdot | x)$): Conditions only on the problem statement $x$, mirroring the inference-time condition.

Both policies share the same underlying model parameters $\theta$.

**On-Policy Sampling from the Student:**
During training, for a given problem $x$, the student policy generates an on-policy response $\hat{y} = (\hat{y}_1, \dots, \hat{y}_{|\hat{y}|}) \sim p_S(\cdot | x)$. This ensures that the student learns from sequences it actually generates, addressing the distribution mismatch inherent in off-policy methods.

**Training Objective: Full-Vocabulary Divergence:**
At each token position $n$ within the student-generated trajectory $\hat{y}$, both policies induce next-token distributions over the vocabulary $V$, conditioned on the common prefix $\hat{y}_{<n} = (\hat{y}_1, \dots, \hat{y}_{n-1})$: $p_S(y_n | x, \hat{y}_{<n})$ and $p_T(y_n | x, y^\star, \hat{y}_{<n})$.
The primary learning objective minimizes the trajectory-averaged, token-wise divergence between these distributions:
$L_{OPSD}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y^\star)\sim S} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{\hat{y}\sim p_S(\cdot|x)} \left[ \frac{1}{|\hat{y}|} \sum_{n=1}^{|\hat{y}|} D \left( p_T(\cdot | x, y^\star, \hat{y}_{<n}) \parallel p_S(\cdot | x, \hat{y}_{<n}) \right) \right] \right]$
Here, $D$ represents a distribution divergence measure, such as the generalized Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD$_\beta$) with $\beta=0.5$ in experiments. This full-vocabulary formulation provides dense, token-level feedback by aligning the student's next-token probabilities with the teacher's, which is informed by the ground-truth solution. Crucially, gradients are backpropagated *only* through the student policy $p_S$, treating the teacher $p_T$ as a fixed target distribution.

**Alternative Objective: Sampled-Token Distillation through Policy Gradient:**
As an alternative, the paper also explores a sampled-token policy gradient objective. For each token $\hat{y}_n$ in the sampled sequence, an advantage term is defined as $A_n(x, \hat{y}) = \log p_T(\hat{y}_n | x, y^\star, \hat{y}_{<n}) - \log p_S(\hat{y}_n | x, \hat{y}_{<n})$. This advantage is treated as a constant during gradient computation, and the objective becomes:
$L(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y^\star)\sim S} \left[ \mathbb{E}_{\hat{y}\sim p_S(\cdot|x)} \left[ \frac{1}{|\hat{y}|} \sum_{n=1}^{|\hat{y}|} A_n(x, \hat{y}) \times \log p_S(\hat{y}_n | x, \hat{y}_{<n}) \right] \right]$
This variant provides a dense, token-level reward signal based on the teacher's log-probabilities for the sampled tokens.

**Implementation Details:**
Experiments use the Qwen3 model family (1.7B, 4B, 8B) fine-tuned on the mathematical reasoning subset of OpenThoughts. OPSD typically samples 1 response per problem, while GRPO samples 8. LoRA is used for efficient training. The teacher policy is fixed to the initial policy for stability.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation of OPSD focused on its performance, efficiency, and various ablations using the Qwen3 model family (1.7B, 4B, 8B) on four mathematical reasoning benchmarks: AIME 2024, AIME 2025, HMMT 2025, and Amo-Bench.

**Performance Comparison with Baselines:**
- **Overall Performance:** OPSD consistently outperformed standard Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and the base (instruct) model across all scales. At the 4B and 8B parameter scales, OPSD matched or exceeded the performance of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). For instance, on the Qwen3-8B model, OPSD achieved an average accuracy of 52.2%, compared to 51.3% for GRPO and 50.0% for SFT and the base model. Similar trends were observed with Qwen3-4B, where OPSD reached 50.4% average accuracy, surpassing GRPO's 49.6% and SFT's 49.6%.
- **Model Scale Impact:** OPSD showed larger performance improvements at the 4B and 8B scales. However, at the 1.7B scale, while OPSD improved performance on some benchmarks (AIME24, AIME25, AMO-Bench), it slightly decreased performance on HMMT25. The aggregated average accuracy for Qwen3-1.7B with OPSD was 30.0%, slightly lower than GRPO's 30.5% but higher than SFT's 28.0%. This suggests that a minimum model capacity is necessary for effective self-distillation, as the ability of the teacher policy to rationalize ground-truth solutions depends on the model's inherent capabilities.

**Token Efficiency:**
- OPSD demonstrated significantly higher token efficiency compared to GRPO. While GRPO required sampling 8 responses per problem with a maximum generation length of 16,000 tokens per response, OPSD achieved comparable or better performance using only 1 rollout per problem with a generation length of 1024 tokens.
- The analysis indicated that OPSD could achieve 8-12x token efficiency compared to GRPO, reaching similar performance levels with substantially fewer generated tokens. This efficiency is attributed to the dense, token-level supervision provided by the teacher distribution, which reduces the need for extensive sampling to gather sufficient learning signals.

**Effect of Generation Length:**
- Ablation studies on Qwen3-4B revealed that increasing the generation length of on-policy sampled student responses positively impacts performance. When comparing generation lengths of 1024, 2048, and 4096 tokens, both 2048-token and 4096-token settings consistently outperformed the 1024-token baseline in pass@K accuracy across benchmarks. This indicates that longer generations provide a richer source of teacher feedback and more effective reasoning supervision.

**Learning Objective Comparison (Full-Vocabulary vs. Sampled-Token):**
- A comparison between full-vocabulary logit distillation (minimizing divergence over the entire vocabulary) and sampled-token distillation (a policy-gradient-style objective based on teacher log-probabilities for sampled tokens) showed that the full-vocabulary divergence objective consistently yielded better performance. For Qwen3-4B, full-vocabulary distillation improved AIME25 accuracy from 82.1% to 84.1% and HMMT25 from 57.3% to 60.0% compared to sampled-token distillation. This suggests that exposing the student to the teacher's full probability distribution over plausible next tokens provides a richer and more effective supervisory signal. However, the full-vocabulary approach incurs higher peak memory usage due to the need to store vocabulary-sized logits.

In summary, OPSD emerged as a competitive and often superior method for improving LLM reasoning, particularly for models with sufficient capacity. Its main advantage lies in its token efficiency, achieving strong performance without requiring an external teacher model or extensive, sparse-reward reinforcement learning rollouts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of On-Policy Self-Distillation (OPSD) represents an advancement in the post-training of large language models for reasoning tasks, offering several points of significance and potential impact.

Firstly, OPSD addresses a key challenge in on-policy distillation by **eliminating the requirement for a separate, often larger, teacher model.** This reduces the computational and logistical overhead associated with deploying and maintaining two distinct LLMs during the training process. By instantiating both teacher and student policies from a single model and leveraging privileged information (ground-truth solutions), OPSD offers a self-contained and more resource-efficient training paradigm.

Secondly, the framework capitalizes on the benefits of **dense, token-level supervision in an on-policy setting.** Unlike traditional reinforcement learning methods that rely on sparse, sequence-level rewards, OPSD provides fine-grained feedback at every token position of the student's own generated trajectories. This addresses the issues of vanishing gradients and limited credit assignment that can plague RLVR approaches. The on-policy nature further mitigates distribution mismatch, which is a common limitation of off-policy supervised fine-tuning.

Thirdly, the research demonstrates **superior token efficiency** compared to prevailing reinforcement learning methods like GRPO. OPSD achieved comparable or better reasoning performance with significantly fewer generated tokens (8-12x more efficient), as it required only one student rollout per problem instance. This efficiency translates directly into reduced computational costs and shorter training times, making advanced LLM fine-tuning more accessible and sustainable. The findings also suggest that earlier tokens in a sequence might be more critical for distillation, potentially indicating important branching points in reasoning.

Fourthly, the findings highlight the **importance of model capacity** for effective self-distillation. The observation that OPSD yielded greater improvements for larger Qwen3 models (4B and 8B) compared to the 1.7B model suggests that a certain baseline level of inherent reasoning capability is necessary for the model to effectively "rationalize" ground-truth solutions and generate meaningful self-supervision signals. This provides insights into the scaling properties of self-supervised learning techniques.

Finally, the ablation studies offer valuable insights into optimal training strategies, such as the benefit of **full-vocabulary logit distillation** over sampled-token objectives and the positive correlation between **generation length and learning effectiveness**. These results can guide future research and practical implementations of distillation-based LLM training.

The potential impact of OPSD is primarily in making the process of enhancing LLM reasoning capabilities more efficient and self-sufficient. This could lead to:
- **Reduced resource consumption:** Lowering the energy and hardware requirements for training highly capable reasoning models.
- **Faster iteration cycles:** Enabling researchers and developers to fine-tune and improve LLMs more quickly.
- **Broader accessibility:** Potentially making advanced LLM training techniques accessible to organizations with more limited computational resources.

While the current work is limited to models up to 8B parameters, the observed trends suggest a promising direction for scaling these techniques to even larger frontier models, potentially unlocking further efficiencies in the development of sophisticated AI reasoners. The framework’s conceptual elegance, drawing inspiration from human learning processes, also opens avenues for exploring curriculum learning strategies and integrating correctness verification for more robust self-improvement.
