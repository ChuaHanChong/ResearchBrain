# Learning Getting-Up Policies for Real-World Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> The HUMANUP framework from the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign enables humanoid robots to autonomously recover from diverse fall configurations on varied real-world terrains, achieving faster and smoother recovery than hand-designed controllers while demonstrating efficient motor utilization.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots frequently fall in unstructured environments, requiring significant human intervention for recovery, which limits their autonomous deployment.
- Hand-designing robust fall recovery controllers is exceedingly difficult due to the unpredictability of post-fall robot configurations and the variety of terrains.
- Traditional learning-based methods for locomotion are not directly applicable to getting-up tasks due to their non-periodic nature, rich whole-body contacts, and sparse reward signals.

## Method

- The proposed **HUMANUP** framework uses a novel ==two-stage RL curriculum==: a ==Discovery Policy== (Stage I) for efficient trajectory generation and a ==Deployable Policy== (Stage II) for refinement.
- Stage I uses simplified simulation and weak regularization to discover a high-speed, effective trajectory, addressing reward sparsity and non-periodic behavior.
- Stage II refines this motion using the full robot ==URDF== model, extensive randomization of **20,000** initial lying postures and diverse terrains, and strong control regularization for ==Sim2Real== transfer.

## Results

- HUMANUP achieved real-world deployment on a Unitree G1 humanoid, recovering from falls on **6 diverse terrains** including concrete, muddy grass, and snow fields.
- Achieved a **78.3%** success rate for getting up from supine and **98.3%** for rolling over in real-world tests, significantly outperforming the manufacturer's controller which failed on most challenging terrains.
- Recovery time was **~6 seconds**, more than twice as fast as the manufacturer's **11 seconds**, with smoother motions and optimized motor usage producing significantly lower and safer arm motor temperatures.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A two-stage curriculum separating motion discovery from deployable policy refinement is crucial for learning complex, contact-rich tasks for real-world robots; single-stage training failed to converge.
> - Accurate physical modeling (full URDF) and extensive domain randomization (initial postures, terrains, dynamics) are essential for successful simulation-to-real transfer in contact-rich scenarios.
> - Strong control regularization is indispensable for generating smooth, safe, and generalizable policies for real robot deployment, even if it slightly reduces raw simulation success rates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The reliable deployment of humanoid robots in real-world, unstructured environments hinges on their ability to autonomously recover from falls. This paper addresses this critical prerequisite, positioning itself at the intersection of humanoid control, reinforcement learning, and sim-to-real transfer.

Historically, humanoid control has seen significant advancements through model-based techniques like Zero Moment Point (ZMP), optimization, and Model Predictive Control (MPC). While these methods have enabled fundamental locomotion tasks such as walking and running, they often struggle with generalization to novel or highly unpredictable environments, including varied fall configurations.

More recently, learning-based approaches, particularly reinforcement learning (RL) combined with Sim2Real transfer, have revolutionized legged locomotion. Successes have been demonstrated with quadrupeds and humanoids in challenging terrains, agile movements, and even vision-driven control. However, the paper identifies a crucial gap: most of these successes primarily involve structured, periodic contacts, such as those between the robot's feet and the ground during walking. Contact-rich tasks involving the entire body, such as crawling, tumbling, or getting up from arbitrary fall postures, remain largely under-explored.

Fall recovery specifically has been tackled in several ways:
- **Motion Planning:** Early work focused on simpler robots or predefined discrete states. While some progress has been made for toy-sized humanoids, these methods often lack the generalization needed for unpredictable initial and intermediate states encountered on challenging terrains.
- **Hand-Designed Trajectories:** Commercial robots, like the Unitree G1 used in this study, often come with pre-defined fall recovery controllers. The paper highlights the significant limitation of these solutions: they are typically designed for a limited set of fallen states (e.g., supine on a flat surface) and lack the robustness and generalization required for diverse real-world conditions (e.g., slopes, uneven surfaces). For instance, the manufacturer's G1 controller only handles supine poses on flat, smooth surfaces.
- **Learned Policies for Quadruped Robots:** There have been successful applications of RL and Sim2Real for quadruped fall recovery, demonstrating robustness in complex and dynamic scenarios.
- **Learned Policies for Character Animation:** Parallel efforts in character animation have shown impressive learned getting-up behaviors in simulation (e.g., DeepMimic, AMP). However, these often rely on simplified dynamics and higher Degrees of Freedom (DoFs) that lead to physically unrealistic or infeasible motions for real robots, making direct Sim2Real transfer challenging.

This paper directly addresses these limitations by developing a learning framework specifically for *real-world human-sized humanoid robots* that can recover from *diverse fall configurations* on *varied terrains*. It critically distinguishes the getting-up task from typical locomotion due to its non-periodic nature, the richness and unpredictability of whole-body contacts, and the inherent sparsity of rewards, thereby carving out a novel and impactful niche in the broader robotics research landscape.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to develop learned controllers that enable humanoid robots to autonomously recover from various fall configurations on diverse terrains. The motivation stems from a clear and pressing need for enhanced robot autonomy and reliability in real-world deployment.

The paper highlights several key motivations:
- **High Fall Frequency and Human Dependence:** Humanoid robots are inherently susceptible to falls due to their complex dynamics and the unstructured nature of target environments. Data from the DARPA Robotics Challenge (DRC) starkly illustrates this, with 26 out of 46 trials resulting in falls, 25 of which required human intervention. This heavy reliance on human assistance fundamentally limits the practical deployment of humanoids in scenarios where human presence is difficult or undesirable.
- **Difficulty of Hand-Designing Controllers:** The unpredictability of a robot's final configuration after a fall, coupled with the varied and unknown terrains it might land on, makes hand-crafting robust fall recovery controllers exceedingly difficult, if not impossible. A single, pre-programmed trajectory cannot account for the myriad of possible initial states and environmental conditions.
- **Unique Challenges of Getting-Up vs. Locomotion:** While learning-based methods have excelled in humanoid locomotion, the authors quickly recognized that the "getting-up" problem presents distinct challenges that render a naive adaptation of existing Sim2Real paradigms inadequate:
 - **Non-periodic Behavior:** Unlike the cyclic patterns of walking, getting up involves a unique, non-repeating sequence of actions and contacts that must be discovered. This makes traditional phase-coupled control or pattern-based reward structures less effective.
 - **Richness in Contact:** Getting up often necessitates dynamic and extensive whole-body contact with the environment, not just foot contact. This demands accurate collision modeling and fine control over the entire robot body, unlike locomotion tasks where upper body dynamics might be simplified or ignored.
 - **Reward Sparsity:** Defining dense, immediate feedback for progress in getting up is challenging. Early actions might appear counter-intuitive (e.g., tilting the torso down) before leading to eventual uprightness, making the reward signal sparse and learning difficult.

To address these challenges, the paper outlines the following key objectives for its proposed **HUMANUP** framework:
- **Discover Effective Getting-Up Trajectories:** To overcome reward sparsity and non-periodic behavior, the first objective is to efficiently discover a feasible getting-up motion, even if it is initially unrefined or energy-intensive.
- **Refine Trajectories for Real-World Deployability:** The discovered motions must then be refined to be smooth, safe (within torque/speed limits), and robust for real-world deployment. This involves mitigating the issues of high velocities and infeasible actions often seen in character animation methods.
- **Achieve Robustness and Generalization:** The learned policies must generalize to varying initial fall configurations (both supine and prone poses) and diverse terrains (flat, deformable, slippery, slopes). This implies a need for extensive domain randomization during training.
- **Demonstrate Real-World Success:** The ultimate objective is to successfully deploy the learned policies on a human-sized humanoid robot (Unitree G1), showcasing its practical utility and expanding the robot's inherent capabilities beyond manufacturer-provided controllers.
- **Decomposition for Prone Recovery:** Specifically, for getting up from prone poses, the objective is to decompose the task into a more manageable sequence of rolling over to a supine position, followed by standing up.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper introduces **HUMANUP**, a novel two-stage reinforcement learning (RL) training framework designed to tackle the unique challenges of learning contact-rich getting-up policies for real-world humanoid robots. The core of the approach lies in a carefully designed curriculum that progresses from motion discovery to deployable policy refinement.

**Decomposition of the Task:**
To simplify the problem, especially for varying fall configurations, the authors decompose the getting-up task from a prone (face-down) pose into two sequential sub-tasks:
1. **Rolling Over:** From a prone pose to a supine (face-up) posture.
2. **Getting Up:** From a supine posture to a standing position.
Separate policies are learned for these two tasks, which can then be chained for full prone recovery.

**Two-Stage Policy Learning:**

**Stage I: Discovery Policy (f)**
- **Goal:** Efficiently discover a "good" getting-up or rolling-over trajectory without being heavily constrained by real-world deployment requirements like smoothness or torque limits. This stage addresses the "reward sparsity" and "non-periodic behavior" challenges.
- **Training Environment:** Uses a simplified collision mesh for faster simulation and training convergence. Initial poses are canonical (fixed lying postures) to ease the discovery process.
- **Rewards:** Primarily task-oriented with very weak regularization.
 - **For Getting Up:** Rewards include increasing height, uprightness, standing on feet, increasing foot contact forces, and a soft symmetry reward (encouraging, but not enforcing, bilateral symmetry).
 - **For Rolling Over:** Rewards focus on changing body orientation to approximate a supine posture.
- **Output:** A high-speed, potentially jerky, but effective trajectory to achieve the task.

**Stage II: Deployable Policy (π)**
- **Goal:** Refine the motion discovered in Stage I into a policy that is smooth, safe, robust, and generalizable for direct Sim2Real transfer. This stage addresses the "richness in contact" and "Sim2Real transferability" challenges.
- **Training Environment:**
 - **Collision Mesh:** Switches to the full, accurate URDF (Unified Robot Description Format) collision mesh of the Unitree G1, which is crucial for precise contact modeling in the real world.
 - **Posture Randomization:** Policies are trained from a large dataset (20K each for supine and prone) of randomized initial lying postures. This significantly enhances generalization to arbitrary fall configurations.
 - **Terrain Randomization:** Training incorporates diverse terrains (flat, rough, slopes, varying friction/compliance) and dynamics randomization (e.g., CoM offset, control delay) to improve robustness to real-world variations.
- **Rewards:**
 - **Tracking Rewards:** The policy is trained to imitate a *slowed-down* version (8x slower for getting up, 4x for rolling over) of the trajectory discovered in Stage I. This provides dense rewards and ensures a stable, manageable target for real-world execution. Rewards specifically track DoF positions and body positions/orientations relative to the reference trajectory.
 - **Strong Control Regularization:** Extensive regularization terms are introduced to ensure smooth actions, low joint velocities, and adherence to torque/DoF limits, which are vital for hardware safety and longevity.
- **Output:** A final policy ready for real-world deployment.

**Curriculum Design:**
The two-stage approach effectively forms a curriculum:
- **Task Difficulty:** Hard-to-easy (Stage I: discover motion; Stage II: track motion).
- **Regularization and Variability:** Easy-to-hard (Stage I: weak regularization, canonical poses, fixed terrain; Stage II: strong regularization, random poses, varied terrains). This structured increase in complexity is found to be crucial for successful learning and transfer.

**Policy Architecture:**
Both policies (f and π) are implemented as Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs).
- **Observation (o_t):** Includes proprioceptive information (robot's roll, pitch, angular velocity, DoF velocities, DoF positions) and encoded environmental latents (predicted from observation history, used for adaptation to terrain variations). Linear velocity and yaw are excluded due to unreliable real-world estimation.
- **Action (a_t):** 23 DoF joint positions (wrist DoFs are disabled as they are not relevant to getting up).
- **Learning Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to maximize discounted policy return.

**Platform and Simulation:**
- **Robot Platform:** Unitree G1, a medium-sized humanoid robot with 29 actuatable DoFs (23 used). It features waist yaw and roll DoFs, which are found to be useful.
- **Control:** Position control with a PD controller operating at 50 Hz.
- **Simulation:** NVIDIA Isaac Gym, a high-performance GPU-based physics simulator. A high simulation frequency of 1000 Hz is used to accurately model numerous contacts, while the PD controller runs at 50 Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study presents extensive simulation and real-world experiments validating the efficacy of the HUMANUP framework.

**Simulation Results (Table I):**
- **Superior Performance of HUMANUP:** The full HUMANUP method consistently achieves the highest success rates across all three tasks (getting up from supine, rolling over, and getting up from prone) on held-out initial posture sets. Critically, it also demonstrates superior smoothness (lower action/DoF position jitter, lower energy consumption) and safety (higher S_Torque and S_DoF scores), which are essential for real-world deployment.
- **Crucial Role of Stage II Regularization:** Baselines lacking strong regularization (e.g., "Tao et al." and "HUMANUP w/o Stage II") achieved comparable or even higher simulation success rates for some tasks, but their smoothness and safety metrics were dramatically worse (e.g., 18x higher action jitter for Tao et al.), rendering them undeployable. This highlights that simply achieving the task is insufficient; the *manner* in which it's achieved (smoothly, safely) is paramount for real robots.
- **Necessity of the Two-Stage Curriculum:** A single-stage training approach ("HUMANUP w/o Two-Stage Learning"), attempting to learn the task directly with all Sim2Real regularization constraints, *failed to solve the task at all* (Fig. 4). This strongly supports the authors' hypothesis that separating motion discovery from refinement through a curriculum is vital for complex, contact-rich tasks.
- **Importance of Full Collision Mesh for Sim2Real:** While "HUMANUP w/o Full URDF" performed well in simulation, it failed completely in real-world deployment. This underscores that despite good simulation performance, an accurate physical model (full URDF) is indispensable for reliable Sim2Real transfer, especially in contact-rich scenarios where precise interaction geometry matters.
- **Generalization through Posture Randomization:** "HUMANUP w/o Posture Randomization" showed significantly lower success rates and worse task metrics compared to the full HUMANUP method. This confirms that randomizing initial postures during training is critical for developing generalized policies capable of recovering from diverse fall configurations.
- **Benefits of Soft Symmetry:** The soft symmetry reward used in HUMANUP outperformed "HUMANUP w/ Hard Symmetry," particularly for the rolling-over task. This suggests that while symmetry can aid learning efficiency, enforcing strict bilateral symmetry can limit the robot's ability to discover optimal or more generalizable contact sequences for complex, asymmetric maneuvers like rolling.

**Real-World Results (Fig. 3, Fig. 5, Fig. 6):**
- **Robustness Across Diverse Terrains:** HUMANUP policies were successfully deployed on a Unitree G1 robot across six varied real-world terrains, including concrete, brick, stone tiles, muddy grass, grass slope, and snow field. This demonstrates exceptional robustness to differing surface properties (roughness, bumpiness, compliance, slipperiness) and inclines, significantly outperforming the manufacturer-provided controller.
- **Superiority over Manufacturer Controller:**
 - The manufacturer's controller, which uses a hand-crafted, three-phase motion, only reliably worked on flat, firm concrete. It consistently failed on challenging terrains due to issues like arms getting stuck, unstable squatting on slopes, and slipping on compliant/slippery surfaces.
 - HUMANUP achieved a 78.3% success rate for getting up from supine poses and a remarkable 98.3% for rolling over, validating its superior generalization and robustness.
- **Improved Efficiency and Motor Management:** HUMANUP enabled the G1 robot to stand up within approximately 6 seconds, which is more than twice as fast as the manufacturer's controller (11 seconds). Furthermore, HUMANUP's learned motion is continuous and smoother. Crucially, HUMANUP makes greater use of the robot's stronger leg motors, resulting in significantly lower and safer temperatures for the weaker arm motors compared to the manufacturer's controller, which heavily relies on arm-based propping.
- **Demonstrated Generalization to Other Poses and External Disturbances (Appendix):**
 - In simulation, HUMANUP successfully generalized to getting up from sitting on stairs and leaning against walls, indicating broader applicability beyond just lying poses.
 - Real-world tests showed the policy's robustness against external turbulence, such as thumps from a stick and object hurling, showcasing its practical resilience.

**Failure Modes:** While highly robust, HUMANUP still showed some limitations on extreme terrains, sometimes failing due to unstable foot placement on steep slopes or slippage on highly deformable/slippery snow. The manufacturer's controller, in contrast, often failed earlier by getting stuck or being unable to initiate the sequence.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Learning Getting-Up Policies for Real-World Humanoid Robots" makes several significant contributions and holds substantial potential impact on the field of humanoid robotics:

- **Enabling Autonomous Deployment:** The most profound impact is the advancement towards truly autonomous and reliable humanoid robot operation in real-world, unstructured environments. Automatic fall recovery is a fundamental prerequisite for any practical deployment of humanoids beyond controlled laboratory settings. By providing a robust, learned solution to this challenge, the paper removes a major bottleneck that has historically necessitated human intervention and limited robot autonomy.
- **Addressing a Complex Robotics Problem:** Getting up from arbitrary fall configurations on varied terrains is an exceptionally complex problem due to unpredictable whole-body contacts, sparse rewards, and the need for dynamic balance. The paper's successful demonstration on a real human-sized humanoid robot showcases a significant breakthrough in tackling these difficulties.
- **Novel Methodological Contribution (Two-Stage Curriculum):** The proposed two-stage reinforcement learning framework (Discovery Policy followed by Deployable Policy) represents a significant methodological innovation. This curriculum design effectively separates the hard problem of "discovering a solution" from the equally challenging problem of "making it deployable and generalizable." This approach provides a blueprint for tackling other contact-rich, sparse-reward tasks in robotics, where traditional single-stage RL with full regularization might fail to converge.
- **Validated Sim2Real Transfer:** The work provides strong empirical evidence for effective Sim2Real transfer in a highly challenging domain. The meticulous attention to detail in Stage II (full URDF, extensive domain and dynamics randomization, strong control regularization, slowed-down reference trajectories) is critical for bridging the reality gap, offering valuable lessons for future robotic learning projects.
- **Enhanced Robot Capabilities:** The learned policies demonstrably expand the capabilities of a commercially available human-sized humanoid robot (Unitree G1) beyond its manufacturer-provided functionalities. Achieving faster, smoother, and more energy-efficient recovery motions while utilizing stronger motors more effectively leads to safer and more durable robot operation.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper lays crucial groundwork for future advancements in humanoid control. It opens avenues for exploring even more complex and dynamic recovery scenarios (e.g., stairs, extremely uneven surfaces, recovering with objects in hand). It also highlights ongoing challenges in physics simulation fidelity and reward specification, pointing toward critical areas for future research in embodied AI.
- **Practical Relevance:** The project page and real-world videos (not part of the paper text itself, but mentioned) signify a commitment to making the learned policies and insights accessible to the broader research and practitioner community, fostering further development and application.

In conclusion, this research makes a substantial contribution to the field of humanoid robotics by providing a practical, robust, and generalizable solution to the crucial problem of fall recovery. Its innovative two-stage learning framework, coupled with rigorous real-world validation, positions it as a significant step towards the reliable and autonomous deployment of humanoid robots in the diverse and unpredictable environments of the real world.
