# FIRE-VLA: Failure-Informed Self-Evolution for Vision-Language-Action Models in Autonomous Driving

> [!summary] Summary
> FIRE-VLA is a post-training framework for driving vision-language-action models that detects rollout groups where every sampled trajectory fails with near-identical reward, then supervises them by distilling from a frozen same-scale copy of the policy that alone sees the hidden future trajectory. GRPO stays active everywhere; the updated policy becomes the next round's teacher, cutting G=4 mean L2 from 1.848 to 1.500 m.

## Problem

- Relative-reward post-training derives its signal from reward contrast within a rollout group, so groups where every trajectory is poor rank failures without indicating escape behavior.
- Prior failure-aware pipelines obtain corrective information through external refinement or a larger privileged teacher, adding models and verification machinery beyond the policy itself.
- A fixed teacher encodes a static view of failure, so supervision stops tracking where the evolving policy actually breaks as its rollout distribution shifts.

## Method

- ==Failure-informed routing== flags unresolved groups by batch-relative 30th-percentile gates on group reward mean and reward standard deviation with valid fraction at least 0.5.
- ==Privileged self-distillation== supervises routed groups from a frozen round-start copy that additionally reads the hidden future waypoints, matching answer-token distributions along the student's own prefix.
- Compact capped ==Jensen-Shannon loss== over the teacher's top-16 tokens plus residual tail, cap 0.05, weight lambda 0.1 added to ==GRPO==; the updated policy becomes the next round's teacher.

## Results

- On **6,019** held-out nuScenes samples, G=4 mean L2 falls from **1.848** to **1.500 m**, an **18.8%** cut, paired scene-clustered **95%** CI [-0.7711, -0.0319].
- Evaluation-persistent failure prevalence drops from **13.03%** to **11.20%** and samples with any candidate above **10 m** from **1.35%** to **0.83%**; CVaR99 falls **118.58** to **79.21 m**.
- Single-sample planning is statistically comparable, CI [-0.1104, 0.0087]; GRPO remains better at candidate median, P90, and P95, and holds higher scalar reward **0.637** versus **0.616**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Low reward variance within a group signals missing corrective information, not a vanishing gradient, so it works well as a gate for adding dense supervision.
> - A same-size copy given privileged future context is a sufficient teacher; capability gaps can come from information asymmetry rather than parameter scale.
> - Bounded reciprocal rewards saturate on severe errors, so higher average reward can coexist with a heavier physical-error tail; check geometry, not only reward.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models represents a significant direction in autonomous driving research, aiming to integrate visual perception, language-based reasoning, and executable driving behaviors. Traditional VLA systems often begin with supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to establish foundational driving priors and an action format. Subsequent improvement frequently leverages reinforcement learning (RL) by evaluating trajectories sampled from the current policy.

Within the realm of RL for VLA models, Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) has emerged as an approach that derives its learning signal from reward contrasts among multiple responses to a single prompt. Methods such as AutoDrive-R², for instance, have demonstrated the effectiveness of GRPO-style post-training for reasoning-based driving VLAs.

However, a recognized limitation of GRPO-style methods occurs in "unresolved failure groups," where all sampled trajectories for a given prompt are uniformly poor, resulting in similar low reward outcomes. In such scenarios, while GRPO can still rank these failures relatively, it provides limited informative signal for the policy to escape the failed behavioral region. This limitation arises from an insufficiency of specific corrective information relevant to the policy's own failed responses.

Existing research has explored various strategies to address such failures, including failure refinement and privileged distillation. These approaches typically involve identifying recurring failure patterns and constructing feedback-guided corrections, sometimes employing larger external teachers or pre-defined privileged information sources.

FIRE-VLA positions itself within this landscape by seeking to overcome the limitations of GRPO in unresolved failure groups. It proposes a novel framework that enables the VLA model to leverage its own uniformly poor outcomes to self-supervise its successor, thereby generating corrective information without relying on an external, larger teacher. This work contributes to the area of self-improving and failure-aware learning within autonomous driving VLA models, aiming to enhance robustness and reduce severe errors by targeting specific failure modes.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of the FIRE-VLA research is to introduce and evaluate a failure-informed self-evolution framework designed to enhance Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for autonomous driving, particularly in scenarios where existing reinforcement learning methods exhibit limitations.

The core motivations driving this work are as follows:

- **Addressing Limitations of Relative Reward Optimization:** Current reinforcement learning methods, such as Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), are effective when a prompt yields a diverse range of responses, including both successful and poor trajectories, allowing for informative ranking based on reward differences. However, in "unresolved failure groups," where all sampled trajectories for a given prompt are uniformly poor and produce similar low reward outcomes, GRPO's capacity to provide actionable corrective signals is diminished. While it can rank failures, it offers limited guidance on how to generate behavior that escapes the problematic region entirely. The motivation is to provide this missing corrective information.
- **Leveraging Internal Failure Information:** Rather than relying on external, larger teacher models or pre-defined failure refinement strategies, the authors are motivated to explore whether a VLA model can leverage its *own* unresolved failures to supervise its subsequent policy iterations. This approach aims for a more autonomous and self-contained improvement process.
- **Enhancing Robustness and Reducing Severe Failures:** In autonomous driving, the occurrence of severe or persistent failures, even if rare, can have significant safety implications. A key motivation is to specifically target and reduce the prevalence of such critical errors that may not be effectively addressed by standard relative reward methods alone. The research aims to shift the error distribution, mitigating the impact of the long tail of severe rollouts.
- **Maintaining Planning Performance:** While addressing severe failures, the framework also aims to preserve or improve the nominal single-sample planning performance of the VLA model, ensuring that specialized failure handling does not degrade general driving capabilities.
- **Adaptive Policy Evolution:** The motivation extends to creating a dynamic learning system where the distribution of unresolved failures, which serves as the trigger for additional supervision, can change as the policy itself evolves. This allows the model to continuously adapt its learning focus based on its current deficiencies.

In summary, the research is motivated by the need for more targeted and efficient corrective learning mechanisms in autonomous driving VLAs, particularly for high-stakes failure modes where conventional relative reward signals prove insufficient, while seeking to enable self-improvement without external dependencies.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FIRE-VLA (Failure-Informed Self-Evolution for Vision-Language-Action models) is a post-training framework that integrates failure-informed routing with privileged self-distillation (PSD) to augment Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The approach involves a multi-stage, round-wise self-evolution process.

#### 3.1. VLA Backbone and Trajectory Representation

The framework utilizes the Qwen2.5-VL-3B-Instruct model as its base architecture. For each example, the model receives a front-view image ($I_i$) and an ego-history description ($h_i$). The policy is tasked with generating reasoning followed by six ego-frame waypoints, representing a future trajectory ($\tau^*_i$) at 0.5-second intervals, embedded within an `<answer>` span.

#### 3.2. Trajectory Learning with Relative Reward (GRPO)

The foundational learning mechanism is GRPO. For valid generated trajectories, the reward $r^{(g)}_i$ is inversely proportional to the mean squared error between the predicted waypoints and the hidden ground-truth trajectory. Invalid responses receive a zero reward. GRPO samples $G$ responses per prompt and computes group-relative advantages ($\hat{A}^{(g)}_i$) by standardizing individual rewards against the group mean and standard deviation. This clipped policy objective reinforces responses based on these relative advantages, a mechanism effective when trajectories within a group exhibit varied success levels.

#### 3.3. Failure-Informed Routing

The core innovation begins with identifying "unresolved failure groups" that require additional supervision. For each group of on-policy rollouts, FIRE-VLA calculates the reward mean ($\bar{r}_i$), population standard deviation ($\sigma_i$), and valid fraction ($v_i$). A group is classified as an "unresolved failure group" if it satisfies three criteria:
1. Low mean reward: $\bar{r}_i \le Q_{batch}^{0.3}(\bar{r})$ (below the 30th percentile of batch reward means).
2. Low reward diversity: $\sigma_i \le Q_{batch}^{0.3}(\sigma)$ (below the 30th percentile of batch reward standard deviations).
3. Sufficient validity: $v_i \ge 0.5$ (at least half of the responses are valid).
This routing mechanism is online and batch-relative during training, ensuring adaptability to the current policy's performance. GRPO remains active for *all* groups, but unresolved groups additionally receive privileged self-distillation.

#### 3.4. Privileged Future Information and Same-Policy Teaching

For groups routed to privileged self-distillation, a teacher-student pair is constructed. At the start of each evolution round $k$, both the student ($\pi_{\theta_k}$) and teacher ($\pi_{\bar{\theta}_k}$) are initialized with identical parameters from the current policy. The teacher's parameters are then frozen. The key distinction is that the teacher's context ($c^T_{i,g,t}$) includes the hidden ground-truth future trajectory ($\langle priv \rangle \tau^*_i \langle/priv \rangle$), in addition to the input image, ego-history, and student-generated prefix ($y_{i,g,<t}$). The student's context ($c^S_{i,g,t}$) only includes the image, ego-history, and its own generated prefix. This means the teacher has privileged access to future information while sharing the same parameter scale and initial weights as the student, but it produces no gradients for policy updates.

#### 3.5. On-Policy Answer-Token Distillation

Privileged Self-Distillation (PSD) is applied along the student's on-policy generated prefix. This ensures supervision is placed on the distribution that the policy actually visits. The PSD loss aims to align the student's token distribution ($P^S_{i,g,t}$) with the teacher's distribution ($P^T_{i,g,t}$). This alignment is computed using a Jensen–Shannon divergence ($J_{i,g,t}$), which is compact (considering the teacher’s top 16 tokens plus a residual tail bucket) and capped at 0.05 per token divergence to prevent overly strong signals from specific tokens. Supervision is restricted to tokens within an unambiguous `<answer>` span, making it trajectory-specific. The divergences are aggregated as an answer-token-weighted average across active responses within a minibatch (Equation 7).

#### 3.6. Policy Update

The final actor loss ($L_B$) for a minibatch combines the GRPO objective and the selectively applied PSD loss: $L_B = L_{GRPO,B} + \lambda z^{train}_B L_{PSD,B}$, where $\lambda = 0.1$ and $z^{train}_B$ is an indicator that the minibatch contains routed groups. This ensures that GRPO is always active, while PSD provides dense, targeted corrective guidance for unresolved failures.

#### 3.7. Round-Wise Self-Evolution

FIRE-VLA operates in rounds. The updated student policy from round $k$ becomes the initialization for both the student and the frozen teacher in round $k+1$. This means the teacher policy and the on-policy distribution of unresolved failures evolve together, with the failures of $\pi_k$ driving the privileged supervision for $\pi_{k+1}$. The experimental setup involved two evolution rounds (K=2), each consisting of 75 policy updates.

#### 3.8. Experimental Setup

The comparison involved matching the Qwen2.5-VL-3B SFT checkpoint, unique RL prompts (1,200), student rollout count (4,800), and policy update count (150) between FIRE-VLA and standard GRPO. Evaluation was performed on 6,019 examples from 150 held-out nuScenes scenes, using both single-sample low-temperature (n=1, T=0.2) and G=4 stochastic (T=0.8) rollouts. Statistical significance was assessed using scene-clustered paired bootstrap confidence intervals. The evaluation-time persistent failure detector was a fixed criterion derived from a separate SFT calibration split, distinct from the online training-time routing.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of FIRE-VLA involved a direct comparison with a standard GRPO approach, starting from the same Qwen2.5-VL-3B SFT checkpoint, with matched rollout and policy update counts.

#### 4.1. Single-Sample Low-Temperature Planning

In the single-sample, low-temperature evaluation regime (n=1, temperature 0.2), FIRE-VLA demonstrated lower numerical L2 error across all three evaluated horizons (1s, 2s, 3s) compared to standard GRPO. Specifically, FIRE-VLA reduced the average L2 error from 0.6421 m (GRPO) to 0.6023 m. The scalar reward remained nearly unchanged. However, the scene-clustered paired 95% confidence interval for this difference crossed zero, indicating that while FIRE-VLA numerically improved L2, the difference was not statistically conclusive. The nominal planning performance of the two policies was therefore interpreted as comparable.

#### 4.2. G=4 Stochastic Evaluation

A more pronounced difference was observed in the G=4 stochastic evaluation (temperature 0.8), which involves averaging metrics over four rollouts per example.
- **Mean L2 Error:** FIRE-VLA significantly reduced the mean L2 error from 1.8478 m (GRPO) to 1.5001 m, representing an 18.8% reduction. The paired difference of −0.3476 m was statistically significant, with a scene-clustered 95% CI of [−0.7711, −0.0319].
- **Reward Discrepancy:** Standard GRPO maintained a slightly higher scalar reward (0.6370 vs. 0.6156 for FIRE-VLA). This apparent contradiction is explained by the reward function's saturation; very large physical errors lead to similar low reward values, meaning GRPO could have higher average reward while still exhibiting a heavier tail of severe physical errors.
- **Error Distribution:** Distributional analysis indicated that the reduction in mean error under FIRE-VLA was predominantly attributable to the suppression of rare, severe rollouts, rather than a uniform improvement across the entire spectrum of ordinary trajectories. GRPO performed better at the candidate median, P90, and P95, while FIRE-VLA improved performance at the P99 and in Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) metrics.

#### 4.3. Learning from Unresolved Failures

The effectiveness of FIRE-VLA in addressing specific failure modes was also quantified:
- **Evaluation-Persistent Failures:** The prevalence of evaluation-persistent failures, as defined by a frozen, SFT-derived detector, decreased from 13.03% (784/6,019 samples) for GRPO to 11.20% (674/6,019 samples) for FIRE-VLA. This −1.83 percentage-point paired difference was statistically significant (95% CI [−2.55, −1.11]), indicating that FIRE-VLA reduced the number of samples falling under this persistent failure criterion.
- **Severe Stochastic Failures:** A substantial reduction was observed in samples containing at least one candidate trajectory exceeding 10 meters. This rate dropped from 1.35% (81/6,019) for GRPO to 0.83% (50/6,019) for FIRE-VLA. The paired difference of −0.52 percentage points was statistically significant (95% CI [−0.83, −0.22]).
- **Recovery Rate:** When considering a fixed set of 484 SFT reference-persistent failures, GRPO recovered 113 (23.35%) and FIRE-VLA recovered 122 (25.21%). This difference in recovery rate was not statistically conclusive.

In summary, FIRE-VLA demonstrated a statistically significant capacity to reduce severe stochastic trajectory errors and the prevalence of evaluation-persistent failures, primarily by mitigating extreme outcomes, while maintaining comparable nominal planning performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The FIRE-VLA framework presents several significant contributions and potential impacts within the field of autonomous driving VLA models and broader reinforcement learning.

**Addressing a Critical RL Limitation:** The work directly addresses a fundamental limitation of relative reward-based reinforcement learning (like GRPO) when applied to scenarios with uniformly poor outcomes. By providing a mechanism to learn from "unresolved failure groups" where relative signals are insufficient, FIRE-VLA offers a method to generate corrective guidance that helps the policy escape these problematic regions. This has implications for enhancing the robustness and safety of autonomous systems where even rare, severe failures are unacceptable.

**Autonomous Self-Improvement:** FIRE-VLA demonstrates a novel approach to self-evolution where the VLA model leverages its own failures to improve. This self-contained learning process eliminates the need for a larger, external teacher model or extensive manual failure analysis and feedback construction. This could lead to more scalable and continuous improvement cycles for deployed autonomous driving agents.

**Targeted and Efficient Learning:** The framework's failure-informed routing mechanism ensures that privileged supervision is applied selectively to specific failure modes—those exhibiting both low reward and low diversity. This targeted approach may be more computationally efficient and lead to more focused improvements compared to applying auxiliary losses uniformly across all training samples, regardless of their informational content.

**Enhanced Reliability in High-Stakes Domains:** The most pronounced benefit observed in the evaluation is the significant reduction in severe stochastic trajectory errors and evaluation-persistent failures. In autonomous driving, such improvements in the "tail risk" of the error distribution are critically important for system safety and public acceptance. By reducing the likelihood of catastrophic outputs, FIRE-VLA contributes to building more reliable autonomous systems.

**Adaptive Policy Evolution:** The round-wise self-evolution mechanism, where the updated policy defines the teacher and the new distribution of unresolved failures for the subsequent round, allows the learning process to adapt dynamically. As the policy improves, the nature of its failures may change, and the framework can continuously route supervision to the most pertinent current deficiencies.

**Potential for Broader Application:** The core concepts of failure-informed routing, self-distillation from a privileged, same-policy teacher, and round-wise self-evolution could be transferable to other complex reinforcement learning tasks beyond autonomous driving. Any domain where uniform failure modes hinder policy improvement and where privileged information (e.g., ground truth or oracle actions) can be simulated during training might benefit from similar mechanisms.

**Limitations and Future Directions:** While significant, the study notes several limitations: the evaluation is open-loop (not closed-loop control), the comparison does not imply equal computational cost due to additional teacher passes, the contributions of individual components are not isolated, and monotonic improvement beyond two rounds has not been established. Addressing these aspects in future work could further solidify the impact and generalizability of FIRE-VLA.
