# Hierarchical Proportion Models for Motion Generation via Integration of Motion Primitives

> [!summary] Summary
> A hierarchical bilateral-control imitation learning framework where an upper layer does long-horizon planning and multiple lower-layer MLPs each learn a time-segmented motion primitive, combined by weighted average. Three proportion schemes (learned, Monte-Carlo-sampled, playback) set the weights. On real-robot pick-and-place, sampling and playback variants hit 100% on the seen task and 70%/90% on an unseen two-object task, beating the 90%/60% baseline.

## Problem

- Imitation learning still demands large volumes of high-quality demonstrations, and long-horizon or complex tasks force repeated data collection and full retraining before deployment.
- Single LSTM policies have limited memory capacity, so prediction accuracy and efficiency degrade over the long sequences that multi-stage manipulation tasks require.
- Prior primitive-based hierarchies pick one expert per timestep or segment by task semantics, and mixture-of-experts motion work stays largely in simulation without real-robot verification.

## Method

- ==Uniform time-based decomposition== splits each demonstration into ten overlapping segments rather than semantic phases, yielding 50 reusable primitives trained as stateless MLP lower-layer models.
- Learning-based proportion model: an ==LSTM== upper layer jointly predicts long-horizon follower states and softmax-normalized mixing weights over primitive outputs for the next command.
- Sampling-based model injects noise into lower-layer leader trajectories and weights them by a ==Monte-Carlo-MPC== cross-entropy cost on angle, velocity, and torque; the playback variant swaps the learned upper layer for recorded data.

## Results

- Sampling-based and playback-based models reached **100%** success on right-to-left pick-and-place versus 90% for the jointly trained hierarchical baseline over ten trials each.
- On the unseen two-object transfer task, playback reached **90%** and sampling **70%**, against 60% baseline and only 40% for the learning-based proportion variant.
- Both sampling variants ran at roughly **2.2 ms** average update period, real-time capable, though placement deviations of about 10 cm and 3 cm persisted outside the primitive training range.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Determining mixture weights by sampled cost evaluation instead of learning them avoids the capacity ceiling that appears once the primitive library grows large.
> - Time-uniform segmentation, not semantic segmentation, gave primitives general enough to compose motions absent from the training set.
> - Playback weighting is cheapest and most accurate but shows motion-copying behavior, tracking the reference rather than genuinely adapting to environmental change.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotics is increasingly focused on enabling intelligent agents to perform complex tasks in dynamic environments, with machine learning approaches gaining prominence for facilitating adaptive behaviors. Within this context, imitation learning (IL) has emerged as a strategy for endowing robots with human-like motion skills by learning from demonstrations. This approach aims to enhance data efficiency and reduce the time required for training. Recent advancements in IL have integrated force information and bilateral control techniques, which involve synchronized position and force feedback between a leader (human-controlled) and a follower (robot), to improve motion flexibility and stability.

Despite its benefits, IL commonly requires extensive volumes of high-quality demonstration data, a challenge that becomes particularly pronounced for long-horizon or intricate tasks. The necessity for repeated data collection and subsequent retraining for task success or adaptation to new scenarios limits the practical applicability of IL. To address this, previous research has explored decomposing complex tasks into reusable motion primitives. These primitives can be learned individually and then recombined to generate novel tasks. Approaches often involve a higher-level controller selecting optimal primitives at each time step or using frameworks like Mixture of Experts (MoE), which integrate multiple expert outputs through weighted averaging to achieve smooth transitions and adaptability. Monte Carlo Model Predictive Control (MC-MPC) represents another relevant technique, offering sampling-based optimization for robust performance under uncertainty, particularly useful for non-linear or discontinuous problems.

This study builds upon these foundational concepts by proposing an extended hierarchical framework for bilateral control-based imitation learning. It integrates the idea of motion primitives with a proportion-based synthesis mechanism, where learned primitives are combined according to specific weights. The work aims to refine the decomposition and recombination strategies for motion primitives and explores different methods for determining these combination proportions, thereby addressing the challenges of data efficiency and adaptability in complex robotic tasks. By investigating learning-based, sampling-based, and playback-based approaches for proportion determination, the paper contributes to understanding the trade-offs between learning flexibility, computational cost, and adaptability within hierarchical IL systems.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enhance the data efficiency and adaptability of imitation learning (IL) for robotic motion generation, particularly for complex and long-horizon tasks, by reducing the reliance on extensive high-quality demonstration data and frequent retraining.

The motivation behind this objective stems from several limitations observed in current IL paradigms:
1. **Data Volume and Quality:** Traditional IL often necessitates large quantities of high-quality demonstration data, which can be time-consuming and difficult to acquire, especially for complex or extended tasks.
2. **Retraining Requirements:** Adapting robots to new tasks or environmental changes typically requires collecting new data and retraining the entire IL model, limiting its flexibility and scalability in practical applications.
3. **Limitations of Single Models:** For long-term motion generation, conventional single-layer neural networks, such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, may exhibit limited memory capacity, which can diminish efficiency and prediction accuracy over extended sequences.

To address these challenges, the study proposes a hierarchical IL framework that integrates motion primitives with a novel proportion-based motion synthesis approach. The specific objectives and motivations for its design choices are detailed below:

- **Hierarchical Architecture:** The proposed method employs a two-layer architecture. The upper layer is designed to perform long-term planning, processing information over extended time horizons, thus mitigating the memory limitations of single networks. The lower layer consists of a set of models, each responsible for learning an individual motion primitive. This division of labor aims to improve the efficiency and accuracy of motion generation for complex tasks by decoupling long-term strategy from short-term execution details.

- **Motion Primitive Decomposition and Reusability:** Complex tasks are decomposed into simpler, reusable motion primitives. Unlike task-specific segmentation (e.g., separating "grasping" from "moving"), this study advocates for a uniform time-based separation strategy. The motivation here is to enhance generalization; by learning generic, time-segmented primitives, the system is expected to combine them in various ways to generate a broader range of tasks, including those not explicitly present in the primitive training set (e.g., generating a "rotating" motion from grasp, transport, and place primitives). This reusability reduces the need to train entirely new models for every novel task.

- **Proportion-Based Motion Synthesis:** Task motions are generated by computing a weighted average of the outputs from the lower-layer motion primitives. The determination of these "proportions" (weights) is central to recombining primitives effectively. The study investigates three distinct model variants for proportion determination to explore different trade-offs:
 - **Learning-based Proportion Model:** The upper layer is trained to learn both long-term planning and the optimal proportions for combining motion primitives. This approach aims to maximize learning flexibility by allowing the system to discover complex combination rules, but it may require retraining if the primitive set changes.
 - **Sampling-based Proportion Model:** The upper layer focuses solely on long-term planning, while the proportions for combining primitives are dynamically derived. This model integrates concepts from Monte Carlo Model Predictive Control (MC-MPC), generating diverse samples from lower-layer predictions and selecting optimal combinations based on a cost function. The motivation is to achieve more stable and adaptable motion generation, particularly under uncertainty, and simplify updates to the lower-layer models by decoupling proportion learning.
 - **Playback-based Proportion Model:** This variant simplifies the upper layer by replacing its learning component with pre-collected motion data. Proportions are determined by comparing lower-layer outputs to this reference data using a sampling-based evaluation. The primary motivation is to achieve rapid adaptation to new tasks or modifications without requiring upper-layer retraining, thereby significantly reducing overall learning costs.

- **Integration with Bilateral Control:** The entire framework is built upon bilateral control-based imitation learning. The motivation for this is to leverage bilateral control's capability to collect high-quality demonstration data that captures both position and force information, enabling the robot to reproduce fast and stable motions.

In summary, the research aims to develop a robust, flexible, and data-efficient robotic motion generation system by combining hierarchical learning, reusable motion primitives, and proportion-based synthesis, critically evaluating how different strategies for proportion determination impact performance and adaptability in real-world scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology integrates a hierarchical model with bilateral control-based imitation learning, focusing on decomposing complex motions into primitives and synthesizing new motions through proportion-based combination.

**A. Technical Foundations**

The approach is built upon three established technical foundations:

1. **Bilateral Control:** This teleoperation technique uses a leader robot, manipulated by an operator, and a follower robot that reproduces the leader's motion. The leader and follower synchronize joint angles ($\theta$) and angular velocities ($\omega$), while exchanging torque feedback ($\tau$). The control objectives aim to minimize the difference in response position and ensure force equilibrium. This method is used for collecting fast and stable motion demonstration data, providing position and force information.

2. **Hierarchical Model (Baseline Concept):** Previous work on bilateral control-based IL identified the need for a hierarchical structure to improve long-term motion generation. A conventional hierarchical model uses an upper layer to predict follower states ($n$ steps ahead) for long-term planning and a lower layer to predict the next leader state based on the upper layer's output and current follower state. This addresses the memory limitations of single LSTM networks for long sequences by dividing responsibilities between long-term (upper layer) and short-term (lower layer) processing.

3. **Monte Carlo Model Predictive Control (MC-MPC):** MC-MPC is a sampling-based control method that generates multiple randomized input sequences (trajectories), simulates their outcomes over a prediction horizon, and selects an optimal sequence. It does not require smooth cost functions or precise system dynamics, making it suitable for complex, nonlinear problems. The method uses a cost function to evaluate samples and applies a cross-entropy formulation to assign weights to top-performing candidates for computing a weighted average of inputs.

**B. Proposed Hierarchical Proportion Models**

The core of the proposed method involves three distinct models, which differ in how motion primitives are combined and how the upper layer functions.

1. **Motion Decomposition and Combination:**
 - **Primitive Definition:** Instead of decomposing tasks based on predefined actions (e.g., "grasping," "moving"), the proposed method separates motion primitives using *uniform time-based segmentation*. This approach aims to enhance generalization, allowing the combination of these generic primitives to generate a broader range of tasks, including novel ones.
 - **Lower-Layer Role:** Each lower-layer model learns one of these decomposed motion primitives.
 - **Upper-Layer Role:** The upper layer's role varies across models but generally involves combining these primitives to generate the overall task motion.

2. **Three Model Variants:**

 - **Learning-based Proportion Model:**
 - **Architecture:** Multiple lower-layer models (Multi-Layer Perceptrons, MLPs, without internal states) generate specific motion primitives. An upper-layer model (LSTM network) performs long-term planning and learns the combination proportions for these primitives.
 - **Proportion Determination:** The upper layer directly outputs proportions, which are passed through a softmax function to ensure valid weighting.
 - **Motion Generation:** The final execution command is a weighted average of the lower-layer outputs based on these learned proportions.
 - **Training:** Lower-layer models are trained individually on motion primitives. Subsequently, the upper-layer model is trained with the pre-trained lower layers to learn optimal proportions for target tasks.

 - **Sampling-based Proportion Model:**
 - **Architecture:** The upper layer (LSTM) predicts a sequence of long-term follower states ($\hat{F}_{k+1:k+n}$). The lower layers predict leader states ($\hat{L}^m_{k+1:k+n}$). A pre-trained mapping network (L to F, also an MLP) converts leader outputs to follower commands for cost evaluation.
 - **Proportion Determination:** This model integrates MC-MPC. The lower layer generates diverse leader trajectories by adding noise to its predictions, creating multiple samples. Each sample is evaluated against the upper-layer prediction using a cost function that comprises Mean Squared Error (MSE) for joint angle, angular velocity, and torque, weighted by coefficients ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma$). The cross-entropy method (Eq. 3) then assigns weights to samples based on their costs.
 - **Motion Generation:** A weighted average of the top-performing samples is computed, and the first element is applied as the next command.
 - **Training:** The upper layer, lower layers, and the L to F mapping network are trained independently. This modularity allows for parallel training and simplifies the modification or expansion of the primitive set.

 - **Playback-based Proportion Model:**
 - **Architecture:** This is a variation of the sampling-based model where the *upper layer is replaced by pre-collected motion data*.
 - **Proportion Determination:** Similar to the sampling-based model, the lower layers generate multiple samples. These samples are evaluated directly against the pre-collected upper-layer motion data using the same cost function and cross-entropy weighting (Eq. 3).
 - **Motion Generation:** A weighted average of the top samples produces the final output.
 - **Training:** Only the lower-layer models are trained on motion primitives. The "upper layer" is simply a demonstration dataset for the desired motion, eliminating the need for upper-layer training.

**C. Experimental Setup**

- **Task:** A real-robot pick-and-place task was used for validation.
- **Data Collection for Primitives:** Datasets were prepared for 5 different pick-and-place motions (e.g., left-to-right, front-to-back). These were then divided into 10 overlapping segments each, yielding a total of 50 motion primitives for training the lower-layer models. All data was collected using bilateral control.
- **Validation Tasks:**
 - **Right-to-Left:** A task corresponding to one of the learned motion primitives.
 - **Two-Object Transfer:** A complex task not included in the primitive set, designed to test the models' ability to generate novel motions through recombination.
- **Adaptability:** The objects used during execution differed in shape and stiffness from those in the demonstration to assess environmental adaptability.
- **Baseline:** A standard hierarchical model where both upper and lower layers are trained specifically on the target task.
- **Model-Specific Settings:** The sampling-based and playback-based models augmented lower-layer outputs tenfold with noise, resulting in 500 samples for increased robustness. The learning-based model, due to its learning capacity limitations with many primitives, used a reduced set of 30 motion primitives.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The validation of the proposed models was conducted through real-robot pick-and-place experiments, including a standard right-to-left task and a more complex two-object transfer task not explicitly contained within the primitive set. Each validation task was performed 10 times, and success rates were recorded.

1. **Baseline Model Performance:**
 - The standard hierarchical model achieved a 90% success rate in the right-to-left task.
 - In the complex two-object transfer task, its success rate dropped to 60%.
 - Observed failure modes included the inability to properly grasp the second object and premature termination after completing only the first pick.

2. **Learning-based Proportion Model Performance:**
 - This model exhibited lower success rates: 70% for the right-to-left task and 40% for the two-object task.
 - The reduced performance was attributed to challenges in accurately estimating appropriate proportions when combining a large number of primitives. This difficulty arose from the cumulative uncertainty in both the upper-layer's long-term planning and the lower-layer's motion generation.
 - Despite the lower success rates, the model demonstrated an ability to generate the complex two-object transfer task, which was not part of its primitive training, indicating its capacity for novel task generation.

3. **Sampling-based and Playback-based Proportion Model Performance:**
 - Both the sampling-based and playback-based proportion models demonstrated higher motion quality and achieved superior success rates compared to both the baseline and the learning-based model.
 - **Right-to-Left Task:** Both models achieved a 100% success rate.
 - **Two-Object Task:** The sampling-based model achieved 70% success, and the playback-based model achieved 90% success.
 - These models generated more stable motions and were more effective at grasping objects compared to the baseline.
 - Their primary failure mode in the two-object task was premature termination after the first pick, similar to the baseline, but this occurred less frequently.
 - The playback-based model specifically exhibited a characteristic of "motion copying," reproducing actions synchronized with its reference motion data, as opposed to adapting extensively to varying environmental conditions or situational changes.

4. **Computational Performance:**
 - The sampling-based and playback-based models demonstrated comparable and efficient update periods, averaging approximately 2.2 ms. This rate is sufficient for real-time execution on a physical robot.

5. **Observations and Limitations:**
 - The proposed models, particularly their lower layers, were trained using a larger volume of demonstration data than the baseline model, as they learned multiple distinct motion primitives. This factor may have contributed to their improved performance.
 - In the complex two-object task, positional deviations were observed, approximately 10 cm for the second object's pick-up point and 3 cm for the final placement position. These deviations were hypothesized to result from target positions falling outside the trained spatial range of the motion primitives.

In summary, the sampling-based and playback-based proportion models successfully generated complex, novel tasks by reusing motion primitives, outperforming the learning-based and baseline models in stability and success rate. While the playback model excelled in consistency, the sampling-based model offered a balance of adaptability and performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic motion generation, particularly in the context of imitation learning, with several potential impacts:

1. **Addressing Data Efficiency and Adaptability in IL:** The proposed hierarchical framework directly addresses two major challenges in imitation learning: the need for extensive high-quality demonstration data and the requirement for repeated retraining for new or complex tasks. By decomposing complex motions into reusable primitives and facilitating their recombination, the framework allows for the generation of diverse tasks from a limited set of learned fundamental movements, thus reducing data collection and retraining efforts.

2. **Enhanced Generalization Capabilities:** The adoption of uniform time-based segmentation for motion primitives, rather than task-specific action segmentation, enhances the generalization capabilities of the robot. This approach enables the synthesis of novel motions and tasks that were not explicitly included in the primitive training set, demonstrating a pathway towards more versatile and flexible robotic systems. The successful generation of a complex two-object transfer task from simpler primitives supports this claim.

3. **Reduced Learning Costs and Faster Deployment:**
 - The reusability of lower-layer motion primitive models across various tasks significantly reduces the overall learning cost. Once primitives are learned, they can be shared, minimizing the need for new low-level training.
 - The playback-based proportion model, in particular, offers a notable advantage by eliminating the need for upper-layer training. This allows for rapid adaptation to new tasks or modifications using pre-recorded demonstration data, accelerating the deployment process for practical robotic applications.

4. **Improved Motion Stability and Quality:** The experimental results indicate that the sampling-based and playback-based proportion models generated more stable and accurate motions compared to the baseline and the learning-based model, especially for complex tasks. This improvement in motion quality is critical for reliable robot operation in real-world environments, where precise grasping and placement are often essential.

5. **Practical Real-Time Applicability:** The observed real-time update periods (approximately 2.2 ms) for the sampling-based and playback-based models suggest their feasibility for deployment on physical robots, enabling responsive and dynamic interaction with the environment.

6. **Foundation for Future Advanced Robotics:** The framework provides a robust foundation for future research in several directions. The identified limitations, such as positional deviations for targets outside the primitive training range, point towards avenues for further improvement. Integrating advanced models like world models or large-scale models into the upper layer could enhance positional accuracy and overall generalization, potentially leading to robots capable of even more adaptive and flexible behaviors in unpredictable environments.

In conclusion, this work advances the state of imitation learning by offering a practical and efficient hierarchical approach for generating complex robot motions, with a focus on reusability, adaptability, and reduced learning overhead, thus moving closer to more autonomous and capable robotic systems.
