# Score the Steps, Not Just the Goal: VLM-Based Subgoal Evaluation for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces StepEval, a framework that automates the granular evaluation of multi-step robotic manipulation tasks by leveraging Vision-Language Models (VLMs) as judges. This work promotes a shift from single binary task success rates to diagnostic vectors of per-subgoal success rates, aiming to provide detailed insights and accelerate policy development.

## Problem

- Robot learning often relies on a single binary success rate for complex, multi-step manipulation tasks, providing insufficient diagnostic information.
- Manually labeling subgoal successes for detailed evaluation is labor-intensive, subjective, and impractical for large-scale experiments.
- Lack of standardized, granular evaluation hinders effective debugging, iterative policy improvement, and meaningful comparison of robotic policies.

## Method

- Proposes **StepEval**, a modular and ==cost-aware framework== that employs off-the-shelf ==Vision-Language Models (VLMs)== as black-box, automated judges.
- Utilizes VLMs to infer ==subgoal outcomes== directly from recorded visual trajectories (images or videos) in a ==zero-shot or few-shot== manner.
- Outlines a ==prompting strategy== where VLMs receive task subgoals and visual context, generating a ==binary success vector== for all subgoals in a trajectory.

## Results

- Conception and a detailed architectural blueprint for the StepEval framework, designed for extensibility and community contributions.
- Formal definition of a trajectory-level vector of per-subgoal success rates as the primary output for robotic manipulation task evaluation.
- A four-step plug-and-play workflow that enables researchers to integrate automated VLM-based subgoal evaluation into their existing robot learning pipelines with minimal effort.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A "subgoal-first" evaluation, providing a vector of per-subgoal success rates, offers superior diagnostic insights into policy performance compared to a single task-level metric.
> - Existing Vision-Language Models can serve as effective, model-agnostic, and post-hoc judges for robotic subgoal completion without requiring task-specific fine-tuning.
> - The framework benefits from separating primary evaluation metrics (subgoal success rates) from optional diagnostic metrics (cost, latency, judge accuracy) used for framework optimization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical and increasingly recognized challenge in robot learning: the inadequacy of single binary success rates for evaluating complex, multi-step manipulation tasks. For many years, roboticists have relied on a simple pass/fail metric for task completion. While straightforward, this metric provides minimal insight into *why* a policy failed or *where* it excelled, making debugging and iterative improvement a laborious, often speculative process.

The broader research landscape in robot learning has recently seen a shift towards more nuanced evaluation. Leading figures and new benchmarks are advocating for granular metrics, failure mode analysis, and the reporting of subgoal completion rates. Papers such as Kress-Gazit et al., Wang et al. (RoboEval), and Han et al. (RoboCerebra) explicitly call for these best practices. However, the practical implementation of such detailed evaluation has been hampered by the significant manual effort required for labeling subgoal successes in numerous experimental trials.

Concurrently, the rapid advancements in large language models (LLMs) and, more recently, Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have opened new avenues for perception, reasoning, and even control in robotics. VLMs, with their ability to interpret visual information in the context of natural language queries, are being explored for various robotic applications, including:
- **Planning and Control:** Grounding LLMs in robot affordances, embodied multimodal LLMs for long-horizon reasoning (e.g., PaLM-E, RT-2), and using GPT-4V for task planning from demonstrations.
- **Automated Failure Detection:** Domain-tuned VLMs recognizing and explaining manipulation failures (e.g., AHA, RoboFAC, SAFE), or supervising execution within Behavior Trees.

StepEval positions itself distinctly within this landscape. Unlike works that use VLMs for *active control*, *real-time execution supervision*, or *generating novel benchmarks*, StepEval leverages VLMs for *post-hoc, black-box, cost-aware evaluation*. It is not proposing a new benchmark dataset or API; rather, it proposes a *framework and design principles* to operationalize the recommended practice of subgoal-level reporting using existing VLM technology. It complements existing benchmarks (e.g., CALVIN, ARNOLD, The Colosseum) by offering a portable tool that can be attached to any manipulation study, whether in simulation or the real world, to extract the granular data these benchmarks increasingly demand. By aiming for a community-driven, open-source project, StepEval seeks to standardize and democratize this advanced evaluation capability, making it accessible and reproducible across different labs and research groups, thereby fostering a more rigorous and transparent approach to robot learning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for StepEval stems from a critical bottleneck in robot learning research: the widespread reliance on a single, binary success rate (SR) for evaluating robotic policies. While simple, this metric is inherently limited for complex, multi-step manipulation tasks, as it provides no diagnostic information about *where* a policy might be failing or succeeding partially. For instance, a policy might consistently grasp an object but fail at the subsequent placement, leading to a low overall SR that masks its competence in the grasping sub-task. Without granular insights, researchers are left to guess at the root causes of failure, hindering iterative improvement and efficient policy development.

The key objectives of this work are to:

1. **Refactor Evaluation Practice:** Advocate for a "subgoal-first" view of manipulation metrics, where a vector of per-subgoal success rates (SRs) becomes the primary evaluation artifact, rather than a single task-level SR. This provides a rich, diagnostic view of policy performance.
2. **Propose a Scalable Evaluation Framework:** Outline the blueprint for "StepEval," a cost-aware, plug-in framework that automates subgoal evaluation. This framework aims to be easily adoptable across diverse research settings without requiring complex instrumentation of robots or environments.
3. **Leverage Vision-Language Models (VLMs) as Automated Judges:** Utilize the emergent capabilities of off-the-shelf VLMs (both open-source and commercial APIs) to automatically infer subgoal outcomes from recorded visual data (images or videos). This approach aims to overcome the labor-intensive and subjective nature of manual labeling.
4. **Articulate Design Principles for Community-Driven Development:** Establish core principles for StepEval to be model-agnostic, support various input configurations (single/multi-view), and be lightweight. The long-term vision is an open-source project that encourages community contributions for continuous improvement and standardization.
5. **Distinguish Core Evaluation from Optimization Diagnostics:** Clearly separate the primary output (per-subgoal SR vector) from secondary metrics (e.g., computational cost, latency, judge accuracy/confusion matrices). The latter are intended solely for optimizing the framework's efficiency and accuracy when ground-truth labels are available, not for redefining the policy's core performance evaluation.

In essence, the authors aim to make "scoring the steps" a routine, reproducible, and automated practice in robot learning, moving beyond a superficial assessment to provide actionable insights for policy development.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology proposed in this paper outlines a conceptual blueprint for the StepEval framework, emphasizing design principles and a workflow rather than presenting empirical results from a specific implementation. The core idea is to leverage the robust perceptual and linguistic understanding of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to serve as automated, post-hoc judges of robotic manipulation subgoal completion.

#### 3.1. Formal Problem Setup

The authors formalize the problem by defining a task T as an ordered sequence of *n* subgoals (s_1..., s_n). A policy executing task T generates a trajectory τ, which is captured as a sequence of images (I_1..., I_m). For each subgoal *s_k*, a ground-truth outcome *y_k* ∈ {0,1} indicates success or failure. The traditional task success metric is defined as 1 if all subgoals succeed, otherwise 0. StepEval's objective is to predict the full binary success vector ˆy = [ˆy_1..., ˆy_n] for all subgoals from the trajectory τ, without manual labeling.

#### 3.2. VLM as a Black-Box Judge

The central technical approach is to use an off-the-shelf VLM, such as GPT-4o or GLM-4.5V, as the core "judge." These foundation models, pre-trained on vast amounts of vision-language data, are capable of answering questions about images. The VLM is used in a zero-shot or few-shot manner, meaning it is not fine-tuned on task-specific data, but rather queried directly based on its general knowledge.

#### 3.3. Prompting Strategy

A crucial component is the prompting strategy. The paper suggests a simple approach where the VLM receives a single query per trajectory. This prompt includes the list of subgoals and the entire visual context (the sequence of images from the trajectory). The VLM's textual output is then parsed into the binary success vector ˆy. The authors also acknowledge alternative strategies, such as querying the VLM once per subgoal, or providing windowed/key-frame subsets of the trajectory, allowing flexibility for future optimization.

#### 3.4. StepEval Framework Architecture

The proposed architecture for StepEval is modular and designed for community contributions, as illustrated in Figure 1. Key components include:
- **Input Processor:** This module handles the raw visual data. It can fuse multiple camera views (e.g., front, wrist cameras) and select relevant frames or adjust frame rates to optimize the input for the VLM.
- **Prompt Strategy Module:** Manages the generation of prompts for the VLM. It supports templated prompts (zero-shot or few-shot) and can incorporate an optional automated prompt optimizer (e.g., using DSPy) to refine prompts for better accuracy, particularly if a small set of ground-truth labels is available for validation.
- **VLM Judge:** The core VLM, configurable to use either open-source models run locally or commercial APIs.
- **Results & Reporting:** Aggregates and presents the primary evaluation artifact: the per-subgoal success rate vector across all trajectories.

#### 3.5. Framework Optimization Diagnostics

To enhance the framework's utility, especially during development or for comparing different VLM configurations, StepEval incorporates optional diagnostic metrics:
- **Judge Accuracy Diagnostics:** When ground-truth subgoal labels are available, the framework can compute per-subgoal accuracy (A_k) and task evaluation accuracy (A_task) to measure how well the VLM's predictions align with reality. Confusion matrices can also be generated to identify systematic biases.
- **Cost and Latency Diagnostics:** The framework can track the computational or monetary cost per trajectory (based on API token/image usage) and the latency of VLM inferences. These metrics enable users to make informed trade-offs between different VLM models, prompting strategies, and input configurations under budget or time constraints.

#### 3.6. Usage Workflow (4-Step Plug-and-Play Process)

The paper outlines a user-friendly, four-step workflow for adopting StepEval:
1. **Define Subgoals:** Researchers manually specify the ordered list of subgoals for a given task. This can be aided by Multimodal LLMs in the future.
2. **Collect Trajectories:** Run the robot policy and record visual trajectories (videos or image sequences). Optionally, ground-truth subgoal labels can be recorded for diagnostic purposes.
3. **Run StepEval:** The automated pipeline processes trajectories through the Input Processor, applies the chosen Prompt Strategy, and feeds them to the VLM Judge to generate predicted subgoal success vectors (ˆy).
4. **Get Results:** A summary report is generated, including per-subgoal success rates, overall task success rate, and optional diagnostics like confusion matrices, cost, and latency. Results can also be exported for public leaderboards.

This methodological blueprint establishes a clear, flexible, and scalable approach to integrating advanced AI capabilities for rigorous robotic policy evaluation.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

It is crucial to note that this paper presents a *blueprint* and a *design proposal* for a framework, rather than an empirical study with experimental "findings" in the traditional sense of quantitative results. Therefore, the "main findings and results" of this paper are the articulated conceptual contributions and the proposed architecture for StepEval itself, along with the reasoned advantages it offers.

The core "result" of this work is the *conception and detailed outline of StepEval* as a solution to the recognized problem of inadequate robot learning evaluation. The authors "find" or establish the following:

1. **Formalization of Subgoal-First Evaluation:** The paper formally defines the evaluation target as a trajectory-level vector of per-subgoal success rates (y ∈ {0, 1}^n). This explicit shift from a single binary success/failure to a granular success vector is a fundamental conceptual "finding" that underpins the entire framework.
2. **Viability of VLM as a Model-Agnostic Judge:** The authors demonstrate, conceptually, that Vision-Language Models can serve as effective, black-box, and model-agnostic judges for post-hoc subgoal evaluation. This implies that their general knowledge, without task-specific training, is sufficient for inferring outcomes from visual evidence. This is a key insight into how existing AI capabilities can be repurposed for robotic evaluation challenges.
3. **Architectural Blueprint for Scalability and Community Contribution:** The paper presents a modular architecture for StepEval (Figure 1), detailing components like Input Processor, Prompt Strategy, VLM Judge, and Results & Reporting. This blueprint is designed to be extensible and encourages open-source contributions, which is a "result" in terms of providing a concrete pathway for community engagement.
4. **Separation of Primary Metrics from Optimization Diagnostics:** A significant conceptual result is the clear distinction made between the primary evaluation artifact (per-subgoal SR vector) and the optional diagnostic metrics (cost, latency, judge accuracy). This separation clarifies the purpose of each metric, ensuring that the core policy evaluation remains focused on performance, while the diagnostics facilitate the tuning and comparison of the *evaluation framework itself*.
5. **Practical and Cost-Aware Workflow:** The proposed four-step plug-and-play workflow (Figure 2) illustrates how any lab can integrate StepEval with minimal effort. This workflow, combined with the emphasis on cost and latency diagnostics, highlights the practicality and affordability as key "features" of the proposed system, addressing real-world constraints in research.

In summary, the paper's main "findings" are the **design principles and the detailed blueprint for StepEval**, which promises a more insightful, automated, and standardized approach to robot manipulation policy evaluation by leveraging off-the-shelf VLMs. While no empirical data is presented to validate these claims, the theoretical framework and its advantages are thoroughly articulated.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

StepEval has the potential for significant impact on the field of robot learning and manipulation by addressing long-standing challenges in evaluation rigor, reproducibility, and efficiency.

1. **Elevating Evaluation Standards:** The most direct impact will be the shift from coarse, binary success rates to fine-grained, per-subgoal evaluations. This will provide researchers with unprecedented diagnostic clarity, enabling them to pinpoint specific weaknesses and strengths of their policies (e.g., "policy X masters grasping but struggles with precise placement"). This level of detail is crucial for complex tasks and for comparing policies effectively, moving the field towards a more mature and scientific evaluation paradigm.

2. **Accelerating Research and Development:** By automating the granular evaluation process, StepEval dramatically reduces the manual effort and subjectivity associated with labeling subgoal successes. This will free up researcher time, accelerate the iterative development cycle of robotic policies, and allow for larger-scale experiments with hundreds or thousands of trials, which were previously impractical due to annotation costs.

3. **Enhancing Reproducibility and Comparability:** The proposed framework offers a standardized, model-agnostic approach to evaluation. If widely adopted, it can foster greater reproducibility of results across different labs and benchmarks. A common tool for generating subgoal success profiles will enable direct, meaningful comparisons between diverse manipulation policies, promoting healthy competition and clearer understanding of progress in the field. The vision of a public repository of subgoal evaluators and leaderboards reinforces this aim.

4. **Cost-Effective and Accessible Evaluation:** Leveraging off-the-shelf VLMs, particularly open-source ones, makes advanced evaluation capabilities accessible to a broader research community, including smaller labs with limited budgets for specialized instrumentation or human annotators. The explicit inclusion of cost and latency diagnostics allows researchers to make informed decisions about their evaluation setup, optimizing for both accuracy and resources.

5. **Fostering Community Collaboration:** The design as a modular, open-source project is a powerful mechanism for encouraging community contributions. Researchers can improve prompts, integrate new VLMs, or develop specialized input processors, collectively evolving StepEval into a robust and comprehensive tool. This collaborative development model can ensure the framework remains relevant and adaptable to future advances in both VLMs and robot learning tasks.

6. **Paving the Way for Targeted Research:** With detailed subgoal performance profiles, future research can focus on improving specific sub-skills rather than generic task success. This could lead to more specialized architectures, training methodologies, or recovery strategies for particular failure modes (e.g., robust grasping, precise alignment, delicate insertion), ultimately leading to more capable and versatile robotic systems.

While the paper acknowledges limitations such as VLM reliability with ambiguous visual evidence, dependency on environment novelty, and the initial manual definition of subgoals, these are also framed as opportunities for future research and community contributions. The overall significance lies in StepEval's potential to fundamentally change *how* robotic policies are assessed, leading to more insightful, efficient, and collaborative progress in the field.
