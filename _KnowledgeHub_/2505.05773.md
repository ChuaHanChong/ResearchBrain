# Human-Robot Collaboration for the Remote Control of Mobile Humanoid Robots with Torso-Arm Coordination

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Worcester Polytechnic Institute developed and evaluated seven human-robot collaborative (HRC) control approaches for coordinating the torso and arm movements of remotely controlled mobile humanoid robots. The study found that robot-initiated methods like 'Chasing' and 'Scaling' generally led to faster long-range task completion and higher user preference compared to human-initiated or a state-of-the-art inverse kinematics solver.

## Problem

- Coordinating the kinematically redundant torso and arm movements of mobile humanoid robots is complex, especially under remote human control.
- Existing human-robot collaboration (HRC) methods for remote manipulation primarily focus on end-effector movements, neglecting intricate torso-arm coordination.
- Autonomous torso-arm coordination often prioritizes end-effector operation, limiting real-time human intervention for managing contextual trade-offs.

## Method

- The research proposes seven human-robot collaborative (HRC) control approaches for torso-arm coordination, categorized into human-initiated and robot-initiated strategies.
- Human-initiated approaches (e.g., Velocity, ==Preset Heights==) give operators direct control over torso movements.
- Robot-initiated approaches (e.g., Proximity, Scaling, Chasing, Task-based, RelaxedIK) delegate torso control to robot autonomy, varying in reactivity and proactivity.
- A user study with 17 participants evaluated these methods across objective performance, workload, energy consumption, and subjective user preferences using a mobile humanoid robot manipulating objects on shelves.

## Results

- RelaxedIK (RIK) was significantly slower across all task completion metrics and rated lowest in usability and alignment with user intent.
- 'Scaling' mode achieved the fastest long-range task completion, outperforming Velocity, Preset Heights, Proximity, and Chasing.
- Methods with discrete torso movements (Velocity, Preset Heights, Proximity, Task-based) showed lower torso energy consumption compared to continuous movement methods.
- The most frequently preferred modes were 'Chasing,' 'Scaling,' and 'Task-based,' indicating a preference for dynamic and structured autonomous assistance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - There is a clear trade-off between maximizing objective robot performance (e.g., manipulability with RelaxedIK) and optimizing for human-in-the-loop task efficiency and user experience.
> - Operators tend to prefer a 'torso-first, arm-second' strategy in human-initiated control, suggesting a sequential rather than simultaneous coordination mental model.
> - Dynamic, robot-initiated assistance that proactively or continuously adjusts the torso, such as 'Chasing' and 'Scaling,' significantly improves long-range task efficiency and user satisfaction compared to purely manual control or un-tuned autonomous solutions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The deployment of humanoid robots in environments such as hospitals and assisted living facilities, often under remote human control, has highlighted the challenges associated with their kinematic redundancy. While this redundancy offers advantages in reachability and manipulability within complex, cluttered spaces, it also introduces complexity in coordinating the robot's macro-micro structure, specifically the torso and arms.

Current research in human-robot collaboration (HRC) for remote robot manipulation predominantly focuses on shared autonomy for end-effector movements. While some advanced HRC approaches have expanded to assist humans with perception, decision-making, and motion control, these typically have not been extended to the intricate torso-arm coordination of mobile humanoid robots. In the context of motion planning, torso-arm coordination is often addressed through generic kinematic redundancy resolution or by employing separate motion planners for macro- and micro-structures with defined coordination policies. These autonomous approaches often prioritize end-effector operation, treating macro-structure movement as a constraint. However, a limitation of existing methods is the reduced opportunity for real-time human intervention to manage trade-offs involving multiple, contextual considerations that are difficult to encode in predefined cost functions.

This paper addresses this gap by proposing and evaluating various human-robot collaborative control approaches specifically designed for torso-arm coordination in remotely controlled mobile humanoid robots. It seeks to integrate human input with robot autonomy to navigate the trade-offs between robot performance, human factors, and task requirements, moving beyond purely autonomous or purely manual control paradigms for this specific type of redundancy. By introducing a collection of HRC methods, the work aims to provide flexible solutions that can be adapted to different robotic systems, tasks, and operator skill levels, rather than positing a single optimal solution.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to investigate and compare various human-robot collaborative (HRC) control approaches for coordinating the torso and arm movements of remotely controlled mobile humanoid robots. The motivation stems from the increasing deployment of such robots in complex environments like healthcare, where their kinematic redundancy offers benefits in reachability but simultaneously presents significant control challenges in managing the coupled movements of the torso and arms.

Specifically, the paper identifies the following detailed objectives and motivations:

- **Address Control Complexity of Kinematic Redundancy:** Mobile humanoid robots possess a macro-micro structure (torso and arm) that provides kinematic redundancy, enhancing their ability to reach and manipulate objects in confined spaces. However, this redundancy complicates control, particularly when remote human operators are involved. The research aims to develop methods that simplify this coordination.
- **Balance Autonomy and Human Input:** The authors are motivated to create HRC methods that effectively balance the precision and optimization capabilities of robot autonomy with the task knowledge, experience, and adaptive decision-making abilities of human operators. This balance is intended to enhance overall system efficiency and improve the success rate of task execution.
- **Enhance System Efficiency and Task Execution:** A core motivation is to improve the practical utility of mobile humanoid robots in real-world applications. By optimizing torso-arm coordination, the goal is to reduce task completion times, improve the robot's ability to reach target locations (reachability), and maintain dexterity (manipulability) throughout tasks.
- **Consider Human Factors:** The research explicitly acknowledges the importance of human operators' abilities and experiences. This includes objectives such as improving the consistency and predictability of robot autonomy, avoiding visual occlusion for the operator, and reducing the operator's physical and cognitive workload during manipulation tasks.
- **Optimize Robot and Task Performance Metrics:** Beyond human factors, the methods are designed to consider objective metrics relevant to robot and task performance. These include the reachability and manipulability of the end-effector, the robot's operational speed, and energy consumption, particularly of the torso motor.
- **Develop a Range of HRC Approaches:** Recognizing that a single solution may not fit all scenarios, the authors are motivated to propose a diverse set of HRC approaches. These include human-initiated methods, which offer granular control to experienced operators, and robot-initiated methods, which delegate coordination to the robot's autonomy, catering to less experienced users or specific task demands.
- **Empirical Evaluation and User Preference Analysis:** A key motivation is to conduct a user study to empirically evaluate the proposed methods. This involves comparing them based on objective performance metrics (task completion time, manipulability, energy efficiency) and subjective human factors (workload, preferences). The aim is to gain insights into which approaches are most effective, user-friendly, and suitable for different levels of operator experience.
- **Contribute to HRC and Teleoperation Research:** The work seeks to contribute new control strategies for torso-arm coordination within the field of human-robot collaboration, particularly for redundant manipulators in teleoperation scenarios. The findings are intended to inform the design of future control interfaces and autonomous assistance features for such robotic systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research proposes and evaluates seven human-robot collaborative (HRC) control approaches for coordinating the torso and arm of a mobile humanoid robot, categorized into human-initiated and robot-initiated strategies.

**A. Proposed HRC Torso-Arm Coordination Methods**

1. **Human-Initiated Approaches:** These methods place the operator in direct control of torso movements.
 - **Velocity Torso Control (V):** This serves as the human-initiated baseline. Operators use a joystick on a handheld controller to continuously adjust the torso's position, while independently controlling the robotic arm with their hand movements. This mode offers continuous, granular control.
 - **Preset Heights (PH):** This method segments the vertical workspace into predefined regions (e.g., "top," "middle," "bottom"). Operators use joystick inputs (up/down) to select discrete torso heights, while the arm is controlled independently. This reduces choices, potentially speeding up torso pose adjustments.

2. **Robot-Initiated Approaches:** These methods delegate torso control to robot autonomy, allowing operators to focus on micro-structure manipulation.
 - **Reactive Autonomy Methods:** These adjust the macro-structure in response to specific triggers.
 - **Proximity (P):** The torso moves discretely only when the end-effector approaches its vertical motion range limits. This aims to reduce energy consumption by minimizing torso movement. A compensation feature maintains consistent end-effector height by adjusting the arm opposite to the torso's movement.
 - **Scaling (S):** This mode continuously maps the user's arm movement workspace to the combined vertical workspace of the robot's arm and torso. This scales human hand motion to cover the robot's full vertical range, potentially enabling faster manipulation, but consumes more energy due to continuous torso motion.
 - **Chasing (C):** The robot torso continuously follows the arm's vertical motion to maintain optimal manipulability and reachability, providing a 1:1 mapping between human hand and system motion. Like Proximity, it includes vertical compensation.
 - **Proactive Autonomy Methods:** These anticipate end-effector position requirements and adjust the torso in advance.
 - **Task-based (TB):** This method predicts future user movements based on task state and sequence, adjusting the torso preemptively to optimize end-effector reachability and manipulability. It uses discrete torso movements and incorporates vertical compensation. In the current implementation, an expert predefined the torso position sequence based on task requirements.
 - **Robot-Initiated Baseline:**
 - **RelaxedIK (RIK):** This mode adapts the state-of-the-art RelaxedIK solver for inverse kinematics of the integrated torso-arm system. It optimizes for manipulability, reachability, self-collision avoidance, and motion smoothness. The implementation did not include additional weight tuning and did not specifically account for energy consumption or the slow motion caused by frequent torso movements. For robotic arm control in all other modes (V, PH, P, S, C, TB), a RelaxedIK model without the torso's prismatic joint was used, as developed in prior work.

**B. User Study Evaluation**

A user study with N=17 participants (11 males, 6 females; mean age 25.71 years) was conducted to compare these seven HRC approaches.

1. **Experiment Setup:**
 - The setup involved two separate workspaces: a robotic workspace and an operator workspace.
 - The robotic workspace featured an IONA robot with a motorized torso and a 7-degree-of-freedom (DoF) robotic arm, alongside a four-level shelf unit with medicine containers. A workspace camera provided a full view of the manipulation area with augmented reality (AR) cues.
 - The operator workspace included a screen displaying a graphical user interface (GUI) streaming video from the robot workspace. Operators used a MetaQuest 2 handheld controller for robot control. An additional camera tracked the operator's body posture for physical workload estimation.

2. **Task Design:**
 - Participants were required to rearrange medicine containers on the shelves according to a predefined order.
 - The task comprised 12 sub-tasks, balancing short-range operations (minimal torso movement) and long-range operations (significant torso adjustment).
 - AR cues guided operators for pick-and-place actions, with target objects highlighted. Autonomous precise actions were executed by the robot after operator confirmation. An "Out of Reach" prompt signaled the need for torso movement.
 - A secondary task, solving verbal math problems displayed on the GUI, was incorporated to assess cognitive workload.

3. **Experiment Procedure:**
 - **Training Session:** Participants were familiarized with the setup, telepresence interface, and controller. They practiced robot control until comfortable, with task sequence consistency emphasized.
 - **Performance Session:** Two trials of the task using Velocity Control (V) were performed to establish baseline performance (completion time, accuracy, confidence).
 - **Evaluation Session:** Participants completed two trials for each of the seven torso-arm coordination modes. The order of modes was randomized. Each mode's principle was explained and briefly practiced. Post-trial, participants provided oral feedback, and after all modes, they completed NASA-TLX, SUS, and custom questionnaires, and ranked their top-three preferred modes. Each session lasted approximately 2 to 2.5 hours.

4. **Evaluation Metrics:**
 - **Objective Metrics:**
 - Task completion time (overall, short-range, long-range).
 - Robotic arm manipulability index.
 - Torso energy efficiency (torso motion time, fraction of trial duration with torso motion, assuming minimal energy at rest).
 - Operator cognitive workload (number of correct math problems, response rate).
 - Operator physical workload (index estimated from upper-body posture tracking).
 - **Subjective Metrics:**
 - NASA-TLX (National Aeronautics and Space Administration Task Load Index).
 - SUS (System Usability Scale).
 - Custom questionnaire assessing perception of robotic arm reactions, ease of torso control, completeness of torso control, alignment of torso motion with user intent, and efficiency in long/short-range motions (1-5 scale).
 - Top-three preferred modes.

5. **Statistical Analysis:** Due to non-normal distribution of objective data (Shapiro-Wilk test), the Kruskal-Wallis test by ranks was used, followed by Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction for pairwise comparisons.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study evaluated the proposed HRC torso-arm coordination methods across objective performance metrics, workload, energy consumption, and subjective user preferences.

**A. Task Completion Time**
- **Overall Task Completion Time:** RelaxedIK (RIK) mode was significantly slower than all other methods. No significant difference was observed for Velocity (V) control compared to other modes.
- **Short-range Completion Time:** RIK was significantly slower than all other methods.
- **Long-range Completion Time:** Scaling (S) demonstrated superior performance, outperforming Velocity (V), Preset Heights (PH), Proximity (P), and Chasing (C). Chasing (C) also outperformed V and RIK. Task-based (TB) mode was faster than V.

**B. Manipulability Index**
- RIK exhibited the highest manipulability index. Chasing (C) maintained higher manipulability than Scaling (S), which in turn was higher than PH, P, and TB. Velocity (V) showed higher manipulability than PH, P, S, and TB.

**C. Workload**
- **Physical Workload:** No significant differences were detected across any of the methods.
- **Cognitive Workload:** No significant differences were detected across any of the methods. Both hypotheses (H3.1 and H3.2) regarding workload differences were rejected.

**D. Torso Energy Consumption**
- Significant differences were found in both torso motion time and the fraction of time the torso was in motion.
- Methods with discrete torso movements—Velocity (V), Preset Heights (PH), Proximity (P), and Task-based (TB)—demonstrated lower energy consumption (less motion) compared to continuous movement methods like Scaling (S), Chasing (C), and RIK.
- Specifically, TB showed less torso motion than P, and V and PH also showed less torso motion than P.

**E. Subjective Data**
- **NASA-TLX:** Scaling (S) was rated lower in workload compared to RIK (p < 0.05).
- **SUS (System Usability Scale):** Velocity (V), Preset Heights (PH), and Task-based (TB) were rated higher in usability than RIK (p < 0.05). Scaling (S) and Chasing (C) were also rated higher than RIK (p < 0.001).
- **Custom Survey:**
 - **Ease of Torso Control:** Task-based (TB) was rated significantly easier to control than RIK (p < 0.01).
 - **Alignment of Torso Motion with User Intent:** TB showed better alignment than RIK (p < 0.01).
 - **Long-range Motion Efficiency:** Chasing (C) was rated more efficient than RIK (p < 0.05), and Task-based (TB) was rated more efficient than RIK (p < 0.01).
 - No significant differences were observed in robotic arm control perception or short-range motion efficiency.
- **Participant Preferences:** The most frequently selected top-three preferred modes were Chasing, Scaling, and Task-based.

**F. Discussion Points from Analysis**
- **RelaxedIK Performance:** RIK's suboptimal performance was attributed to its tendency to prioritize torso motion without specific fine-tuning, leading to a mismatch between slower torso movement and faster arm operations. This resulted in unexpected system responses and overshooting by operators.
- **Human-Initiated Strategy:** In Velocity and Preset Heights modes, operators consistently adopted a "torso-first, arm-second" strategy, suggesting a preference for gross manipulation followed by precise alignment. No participants reported simultaneous torso-arm adjustments.
- **Workload Clustering (Preliminary):** Although no significant overall workload differences were found, a preliminary K-means clustering based on math-solving rates (cognitive workload) identified three groups:
 - **Fast Cluster (5 participants):** Preferred dynamic modes (Chasing, Scaling).
 - **Moderate Cluster (9 participants):** Preferred structured modes (Task-based).
 - **Slow Cluster (3 participants):** Also leaned towards Task-based, Chasing, and Scaling, but found human-initiated approaches more challenging.
- **Physical Workload Interpretation:** The lack of significant differences in physical workload, despite methods like Scaling theoretically demanding more effort, was hypothesized to be influenced by the Meta Quest Controller's pose clutching feature, which may have allowed participants to maintain more comfortable hand postures.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of human-robot collaboration by proposing and evaluating a suite of control strategies specifically designed for torso-arm coordination in redundant mobile humanoid robots during remote teleoperation. The work addresses a gap in existing shared autonomy literature, which has largely overlooked the complexities of integrating torso movements with arm manipulation.

The findings demonstrate that certain robot-initiated collaborative control modes, such as **Chasing** and **Scaling**, can improve task performance, particularly for long-range operations, and are generally favored by human operators. The **Task-based** mode also emerged as a preferred option, indicating user appreciation for structured assistance. These results suggest that dynamic adaptability and structured autonomy can enhance both the efficiency and perceived control in complex robotic manipulation tasks.

A significant outcome is the identification of limitations in applying a state-of-the-art inverse kinematics solver, **RelaxedIK**, directly to integrated torso-arm systems without specific tuning for teleoperation. Its tendency to prioritize torso motion, which is inherently slower, led to performance degradation and was the least favored by participants, underscoring the importance of human factors and real-time interaction in HRC system design. Conversely, despite its drawbacks in human-in-the-loop performance, RelaxedIK was found to be optimal for objective manipulability, highlighting a potential trade-off between pure robot optimization and human operator experience.

The preliminary clustering analysis of cognitive workload, while based on a limited sample, provides initial insights into how operator characteristics might influence the suitability and preference for different HRC methods. This suggests a potential for personalized HRC system design, where control strategies could be adapted based on an operator's cognitive style or experience level.

The potential impact of this work lies in informing the development of more effective and user-centric teleoperation interfaces for redundant humanoid robots. These robots are increasingly being deployed in sensitive environments such as hospitals and assisted living facilities. By offering a range of HRC strategies that balance human input with robot autonomy, this research can lead to:
- **Improved Efficiency and Safety:** Enhanced coordination can reduce task completion times and minimize errors in delicate manipulation tasks, thereby improving overall operational efficiency and safety in real-world applications.
- **Reduced Operator Workload:** By delegating complex coordination to the robot, certain modes can reduce the physical and cognitive burden on human operators, potentially enabling longer operation times and reducing training requirements.
- **Increased System Adoption:** More intuitive and effective control mechanisms can increase user acceptance and adoption of advanced robotic systems in various service industries.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The identified directions for future work, such as integrating gaze tracking and human intent estimation into Task-based modes, or optimizing Preset Heights based on task constraints, provide a roadmap for advancing predictive and adaptive HRC systems. The exploration of how different user backgrounds and expertise levels affect performance across various HRC methods represents a relevant avenue for further investigation.
