# Principal Steering Subspaces for Online Adaptation of Frozen Generative Robot Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Principal Steering Subspaces (PSS) adapts frozen diffusion or flow-matching robot policies by steering their initial sampling noise along a few high-response directions. A one-time finite-difference probe builds a decoder-response Gram matrix. SAC controls its top-k eigenvectors, and the orthogonal complement is resampled from the Gaussian prior. PSS beats full-latent DSRL on RoboMimic and lifts a frozen 3B humanoid VLA from 2/10 to 6/10 successes.

## Problem

- Online RL fine-tuning of large diffusion or flow-matching policies is computationally costly on real robots and risks degrading useful pretrained behavior priors.
- Latent-noise steering methods like DSRL make SAC explore and fit critics over every initial-noise coordinate, a dimension that grows with action chunk length.
- Initial-noise coordinates have strongly anisotropic effects on decoded actions, so isotropic exploration wastes scarce robot interaction on latent changes that barely alter physical motion.

## Method

- Response probe: ==forward finite differences== of the frozen decoder over 64 probe states estimate a ==noise-to-action Jacobian==, and the eigenvectors of their uncentered ==Gram matrix== rank steering directions by the ==Ky Fan principle==.
- **PSS** action space: the ==SAC== actor and critics operate on only k leading-eigenbasis coordinates, while the ==orthogonal complement== is freshly sampled from the Gaussian prior at each query as environment stochasticity.
- Real-robot learning system: synchronous queries give clean replay transitions, a ==RESET/INFER/EXECUTE/LABEL/UPDATE state machine== runs SAC updates during scene reset, and latency-aware asynchronous deployment uses ==temporal aggregation==.

## Results

- At **k=8** of **28** noise dimensions, diffusion policies retain at least **97.9%** of response energy and flow policies **84.2-98.1%**, against a **28.6%** isotropic reference.
- Final success versus full-latent DSRL: Flow-Can **0.914** vs **0.302**, Flow-Lift **0.999** vs **0.773**, Diffusion-Square **0.878** vs **0.716**, tie on Diffusion-Lift, and fewer post-threshold regressions in every pair.
- On Diffusion-Square, the Top-8 basis reaches **0.838** versus Random-8 **0.346** and Least-8 **0.498**. The k sweep is non-monotonic (**k=16** scores **0.129**). The humanoid VLA improves from **2/10** to **6/10**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Which coordinates the RL agent controls matters as much as the RL algorithm: aligning the action space with decoder sensitivity beats dimensionality reduction alone.
> - Generative policy noise spaces are highly concentrated, so a cheap forward-only Jacobian probe can expose a compact steerable subspace, even for closed-source models.
> - Local response energy is not reward relevance: low-sensitivity directions may matter near contact, and the evidence rests on single seeds and a ten-trial hardware study.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A humanoid robot must put a screwdriver in a plate. Its frozen vision-language-action policy often approaches or grasps the thin shaft instead of the handle. Retraining such a large pretrained generator during online robot learning is computationally demanding, and it can risk degrading useful behavior. The authors keep the generator frozen. They train a smaller controller, Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), to steer the generator's random input. That controller acts only along the noise directions that most strongly affect decoded actions, and the remaining noise is freshly sampled. The method is called PSS.

Top: “Frozen-VLA failure,” an ineffective approach/grasp that does not complete placement. Bottom: “SAC+PSS success,” a successful grasp and placement after adaptation. These are representative trajectories, not the aggregate result.

### 2. Find the directions that move the action

A frozen generator maps initial noise and the current observation to an action chunk. When RL controls all of that noise directly, it must explore and learn over many coordinates. Equal changes in those coordinates can have very unequal effects on physical motion.

Before RL, the authors query the generator at representative states and perturb each noise coordinate slightly. Finite differences estimate how the decoded chunk changes. The method averages these local responses into a response Gram matrix and ranks its eigenvectors. The highest-ranked eigenvectors are the directions with the largest measured local effects. The basis is computed once for each task and frozen checkpoint, then held fixed during adaptation. In PSS, SAC controls only the first $k$ ranked directions, and a fresh Gaussian vector fills the orthogonal complement at every query.

A one-time probe (probe states and noise, finite-difference responses, response Gram matrix, ranked directions) is distinguished from online queries. Online, the SAC actor produces a k-dimensional z, and fresh complement noise ε is resampled per query. Together they reconstruct the full-dimensional w, which the frozen decoder turns into an H-step action chunk.

A strong local decoder response is not a guarantee of reward relevance. A direction with weak measured response may still matter for contact or sparse reward. The learned controller's noise is also bounded and non-Gaussian. Only the independently resampled complement retains the exact Gaussian-prior property, not every learned noise vector.

### 3. Test whether ranking matters

The closest precedent, [[2506.15799|Diffusion Steering via Reinforcement Learning (DSRL)]], already freezes a generative policy and uses RL to steer its initial noise. PSS differs in how it chooses what RL controls. It does not expose the whole latent vector as the action.

In RoboMimic simulation, each evaluation is a success rate over 200 stochastic episodes. The summary averages the final five checkpoint evaluations. On Flow-Can, full-latent SAC controls all 28 noise coordinates. PSS controls the leading eight response-ranked coordinates and resamples the rest, under the shared SAC configuration. The final-five mean success rate is 0.914 with PSS and 0.302 with full-latent control, a reported gain of 61.2 percentage points. These are results from one training seed per condition, not estimates across seeds.

The full-latent comparison leaves a question open: the gain might come only from using fewer coordinates. The Diffusion-Square basis ablation tests this. All three conditions control eight coordinates and resample the same Gaussian complement. The response-ranked basis has a final-five mean of 0.838, versus 0.346 for a random basis and 0.498 for the least-responsive basis. This supports the value of ranking beyond dimension reduction alone. The ablation is limited to one task and one seed, and it does not establish a generally optimal basis or dimension.

Unsmoothed 200-episode checkpoint evaluations from one matched seed. Left: fixed k=8. Final-five means are 0.838 for PSS top-k, 0.346 for Random-k, and 0.498 for Least-k. Right: the leading basis with k = 2, 4, 8, 16, and 28 (final-five means 0.519, 0.714, 0.838, 0.129, 0.445). The k=16 trace reaches competitive success earlier and then declines.

The dimension sweep is non-monotonic. In this checkpoint and configuration, $k=8$ has the strongest final-five mean, which does not make it a universal optimum.

### 4. Control What Matters

PSS leaves the pretrained decoder unchanged. Instead, it changes which latent directions RL can deliberately steer. The equal-dimension ablation is the key evidence that response-based selection matters beyond compression alone.

On hardware, the screwdriver task was an exploratory study with ten trials per condition. Success was observed in 2/10 frozen-policy trials and 6/10 after SAC+PSS. The 95% Wilson intervals are wide and overlap. The study has no matched full-latent baseline, so it supports the system's feasibility rather than a hardware comparison of PSS with full-latent control.
