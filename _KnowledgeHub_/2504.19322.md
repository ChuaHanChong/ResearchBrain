# Learned Perceptive Forward Dynamics Model for Safe and Platform-aware Robotic Navigation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from ETH Zurich, NVIDIA, and the Max Planck Institute developed a learned perceptive Forward Dynamics Model for quadrupedal robots, enabling safe and adaptable navigation in complex, unstructured terrains. The model, trained with a hybrid simulation-real-world data approach, predicts future robot states and failure risks, allowing an integrated Model Predictive Path Integral planner to achieve up to 88.33% navigation success rates in diverse environments with reduced manual tuning.

## Problem

- Traditional robotic navigation methods often struggle with accurately assessing real-time traversability and understanding intricate robot-environment interactions due to reliance on simplified dynamics models and labor-intensive, environment-specific tuning of cost functions.
- Physics-based dynamics models fail to capture complex, nonlinear robot-terrain interactions in contact-rich scenarios, while purely data-driven models face a 'reality gap' and data scarcity, especially for high-risk maneuvers.
- Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) and similar classical motion planners typically require extensive and tedious manual tuning of reward functions for different environments, hindering generalizability and 'zero-shot' adaptation.

## Method

- A novel perceptive ==Forward Dynamics Model (FDM)== is developed to predict the robot's future pose and probability of failure, conditioned on surrounding geometry (height scans) and a history of proprioceptive measurements, implicitly learning terrain traversability.
- A hybrid data collection and training strategy is employed, combining large-scale synthetic data (generated in NVIDIA IsaacLab, including high-risk maneuvers) with targeted real-world interaction data to bridge the simulation-reality gap.
- The learned FDM is integrated into a Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) planning framework, enabling a simplified reward function that uses only a goal-oriented pose reward and a penalty for learned failure risk, thus eliminating the need for complex, handcrafted cost maps and extensive tuning.

## Results

- The proposed FDM achieved an average of **41.28%** lower position error compared to a perceptive FDM baseline and **70.57%** lower error compared to a constant velocity model at the final prediction step, across various environments.
- The FDM demonstrated high accuracy in predicting collisions, with an F1 score of **0.9** in 2D environments and **0.85** in 3D environments, indicating precise and reliable failure estimation.
- When integrated into an MPPI planner, the method achieved navigation success rates of **88.33%** in 2D and **73.75%** in 3D environments, outperforming baselines, and generated the shortest mean path length and mean path time for successful trajectories.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A learned perceptive forward dynamics model can implicitly capture complex robot-terrain interactions, including failure probabilities, and inherently adapts to different robot embodiments and their locomotion policies, demonstrating platform-awareness crucial for safe navigation in unstructured environments.
> - A hybrid training approach, leveraging both vast simulated risky data and real-world subtle dynamics, effectively bridges the simulation-reality gap for robust deployment.
> - Integrating such a dynamics model into a planning framework like MPPI significantly simplifies the planning objective, enabling "zero-shot" adaptation to new environments without extensive manual tuning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Safe and robust robotic navigation in complex, unstructured environments remains a significant challenge in robotics. Traditional approaches often rely on simplified dynamics models and manually tuned cost functions for traversability assessment. These methods are frequently tedious, environment-dependent, and lack generalizability, especially in contact-rich scenarios or when dealing with highly nonlinear robot-terrain interactions.

The broader research landscape in robot dynamics modeling and motion planning can be categorized as follows:

- **Physics-Based Models:** These models, derived from first principles and calibrated through system identification, struggle to accurately capture complex, nonlinear dynamics, especially in contact-rich environments. They are often computationally expensive and prone to errors.
- **Data-Driven Dynamics Learning:** To overcome the limitations of physics-based models, learning-based approaches have gained prominence. These methods approximate complex dynamics from large datasets. While effective, they face the challenge of data scarcity in the real world, leading to a reliance on synthetic data. However, simulators often provide only simplified dynamics, introducing a "reality gap" that must be bridged for real-world deployment. Previous works in this area, particularly for quadrupedal robots, include the pioneering perceptive Forward Dynamics Model (FDM) by Kim et al., which used 2D LiDAR scans but was primarily demonstrated in flat environments with 2D obstacles and had open challenges in sim-to-real transfer and rough terrain.
- **Physics-Informed Learning:** Some approaches integrate physics constraints into learned models to improve generalization and accuracy. While promising, they are often limited to short control timescales.
- **Classical Motion Planning:** Frameworks like Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) or iCEM are popular, using modular components for mapping, traversability assessment, and trajectory optimization. Traversability is typically evaluated using heuristics or learned from experience. A major drawback of MPPI is the extensive manual tuning of cost functions required for different environments and tasks.
- **End-to-End Learning (e.g., Reinforcement Learning):** These methods directly map sensor inputs to motion commands, offering fast inference and avoiding error accumulation between modules. However, they often face significant sim-to-real transfer challenges due to domain discrepancies.

This paper directly addresses several of these limitations. By proposing a "perceptive" FDM, it moves beyond simplified dynamics to implicitly learn robot-terrain interactions, including failure probabilities, from both simulated and real-world data. This hybrid training strategy is crucial for tackling the reality gap, allowing the model to learn safe behaviors from risky simulated maneuvers while capturing unmodeled real-world effects. The integration of this FDM into an MPPI framework significantly simplifies the planning objective by reducing the need for manual cost map design and extensive parameter tuning. This represents a substantial improvement over traditional MPPI, making it more adaptable and "zero-shot" capable for new environments. Essentially, this work pushes the boundaries of learned dynamics models for complex legged robots, enabling safer and more generalizable navigation in previously challenging unstructured terrains.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust, scalable, and safe navigation system for quadrupedal robots operating in complex, unstructured environments. The authors identify several key motivations and specific objectives:

- **Addressing Limitations of Traditional Navigation:** Current robotic navigation methods often suffer from an inability to accurately assess real-time traversability and understand intricate robot-environment interactions. This is largely due to reliance on simplified dynamics models and the labor-intensive, environment-specific tuning of cost functions for path planning. Such methods are not generalizable and hinder autonomous deployment in diverse, unknown terrains.
- **Need for Accurate Dynamics Models:** For safe and effective control, especially in contact-rich scenarios like navigating rough terrain, a precise understanding of the robot's future state given its actions (i.e., a Forward Dynamics Model, FDM) is critical. Traditional physics-based models fail here, motivating the need for data-driven alternatives.
- **Bridging the Simulation-Reality Gap:** While large-scale simulation offers a safe and scalable way to generate training data, it often falls short in capturing the full complexity of real-world physics (e.g., non-rigid body interactions like snow or soft vegetation). Moreover, real-world data collection, though vital, cannot safely include high-risk or catastrophic maneuvers.
- **Simplifying Motion Planning:** Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control is powerful but traditionally requires extensive and tedious tuning of reward functions to ensure safety and desired behavior. The goal is to offload this complexity to a learned model.

Based on these motivations, the authors set forth the following key objectives:

1. **Develop a Perceptive Forward Dynamics Model (FDM):** Create a novel learned FDM capable of predicting the robot's future state, including its pose and probability of failure, conditioned on surrounding geometry (height scans) and a history of proprioceptive measurements. This FDM should implicitly learn terrain traversability.
2. **Implement a Hybrid Training Strategy:** Train the FDM using a combination of vast amounts of synthetic data (from a high-fidelity simulator, including high-risk maneuvers) and real-world interaction data. This strategy aims to leverage the safety and scalability of simulation while incorporating the full, unmodeled dynamics of the real system.
3. **Integrate FDM into a Zero-Shot MPPI Planner:** Use the learned FDM within an MPPI planning framework. The FDM's ability to predict future states and failure probabilities should allow for a simplified reward function, eliminating the need for complex, handcrafted cost maps and extensive tuning for safety. This enables "zero-shot" adaptation to new environments.
4. **Demonstrate Sim-to-Real Transfer and Robust Navigation:** Validate the proposed system on a real quadrupedal robot (ANYmal), showcasing reliable transfer from simulation to reality and robust navigation capabilities in various rough terrain scenarios.
5. **Achieve Platform-Awareness:** Ensure the FDM can inherently adapt to different robot embodiments and their specific locomotion policies, making it broadly applicable across different legged and wheeled-legged systems.

By achieving these objectives, the authors aim to provide a more scalable, safer, and less heuristic-dependent solution for autonomous robotic navigation in challenging, unstructured environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology revolves around a learned perceptive Forward Dynamics Model (FDM) integrated into a Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) planning framework.

**A. Forward Dynamics Model (FDM) Formulation and Architecture:**
The FDM is formulated as an *n*-step prediction model that takes the current observation (`o_t`) and a sequence of future actions (`a`) to predict a sequence of future states (`~s`). The predicted state `~s` comprises the robot's pose (`p` in SE2: x, y, yaw) and a failure risk (`r` between 0 and 1, indicating catastrophic failure). Actions (`a`) are defined as linear and angular velocities.

- **Observations:** The model uses a rich observation space (Table I):
 - **Proprioceptive History:** Past states (`~s_t...,t-n`) and a history of robot measurements (`m_1...,7`), including twist commands, projected gravity, base velocities, joint positions and velocities, and last joint actions. This allows the model to infer terrain properties by observing recent motion.
 - **Exteroceptive Input:** A current height scan (`h_t`) provides detailed geometric information about the surrounding environment for traversability assessment and obstacle detection.
 - **Data Augmentation:** Uniform noise is added to synthetic proprioceptive and exteroceptive observations, and missing patches/occlusions are modeled in height scans to improve robustness to sensor noise and bridge the sim-to-real gap.

- **Model Structure (Fig. 2):**
 - **Encoders:** A GRU (Gated Recurrent Unit) processes the history of past states and proprioceptive measurements. Multiple convolutional layers process the height scan.
 - **Recurrence:** The flattened output of the CNN (height scan encoding) and the last embedding from the proprioceptive GRU are used to initialize the hidden state of a main forward prediction GRU. This GRU sequentially receives encoded action sequences (from an MLP) and generates a latent representation for each future state.
 - **Prediction Heads:** Two separate MLPs (Multi-Layer Perceptrons) operate in parallel on these latent representations. One head predicts residual twist commands (`delta_a`), which are then integrated using a constant-velocity model to yield future poses (`~p`). The other head estimates the failure risk (`~r`).

**B. Data Collection and Sampling Strategy:**
A hybrid data collection strategy is employed to leverage the strengths of both simulation and real-world interaction.

- **Synthetic Data Generation:**
 - Conducted in parallelized simulation (NVIDIA IsaacLab) with thousands of robots, enabling rapid, scalable data generation and exploration of dangerous maneuvers.
 - Simulation parameters are identified from real-world data or modeled with learned networks to minimize the sim-to-real gap. Locomotion policies are identical to those used on the real robot.
 - Terrain randomization increases generalization.
 - Action sequences are initially generated using linear and normal time-correlated sampling. In later stages, the FDM-integrated MPPI planner itself generates actions, aligning the training data distribution with the planner's behavior.
- **Real-World Data Collection:**
 - A human expert guides the ANYmal robot, equipped with the Boxi sensor payload, through diverse real-world environments (e.g., pavement, snow, forest).
 - Proprioceptive inputs and height scans (from onboard depth cameras) are recorded. High-accuracy ground truth pose is obtained by fusing RTK-GNSS, IMU, and Leica Total Station data using the Holistic Fusion framework.
- **Data Sampling:** Data is stored in replay buffers. Training samples are created by decomposing trajectories into sub-trajectories, extracting `n` past states as history and `n` future states as ground truth for prediction. Poses are translated to the robot's base frame for consistency.

**C. FDM Loss Formulation and Training:**
The FDM is trained to minimize a combined loss function (`L`) consisting of three terms:
- **Pose Loss (`L_pose`):** Mean Squared Error (MSE) between predicted and true poses, with heading handled by sine-cosine encoding to avoid discontinuities.
- **Risk Loss (`L_risk`):** Binary Cross-Entropy Loss (BCELoss) for failure risk prediction.
- **Stop Loss (`L_stop`):** An additional MSELoss applied when a failure is predicted (`r > delta_risk`), forcing the model to predict a constant pose for the remainder of the trajectory, emphasizing collision behavior.
The total loss is a weighted sum of these terms (`L = epsilon_pose * L_pose + epsilon_risk * L_risk + epsilon_stop * L_stop`).

Training involves an iterative process:
- Initial phase: Purely synthetic data for robust pre-training (15 rounds, 80k samples per round, 8 episodes per update) using AdamW optimizer.
- Later stages: Real-world data is integrated with synthetic data for fine-tuning with a smaller, constant learning rate to capture the full system dynamics and bridge the reality gap in dynamic, non-rigid scenarios.

**D. Path Planning with MPPI:**
The learned FDM is integrated into a Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) control framework, enabling safe and efficient navigation.
- **Zero-Shot Adaptation:** A key benefit is the ability to adjust planning behavior without retraining the FDM, by simply modifying the reward function.
- **Simplified Reward Function (Eq. 13-15):** The traditional MPPI's complex cost maps are replaced by a straightforward reward function:
 - **Pose Reward (`R_pose`):** A terminal reward that encourages paths minimizing the Euclidean distance to the goal. A `lambda_pull` multiplier is applied when close to the goal to create a stronger pull factor.
 - **Risk Reward (`R_risk`):** A state-dependent penalty for trajectories with high predicted failure risk (`r > delta_risk`). To enhance robustness against false negatives, this term also considers the cumulative risk of a small number of `q` neighboring paths.
- **MPPI Process:** The planner generates a population of action sequences by perturbing a previous solution with Gaussian noise. The FDM then predicts the future states and risk for each sequence. These are evaluated using the simplified reward function, and a weighted update is performed to refine the optimal action sequence. The action sequence with the maximum reward is then executed.

This comprehensive methodology, combining data-driven dynamics learning with a simplified planning objective, allows for robust and adaptable robotic navigation in challenging environments, significantly reducing the manual tuning effort typically associated with such systems.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents compelling quantitative and qualitative results that validate the effectiveness and advantages of the proposed perceptive FDM for robotic navigation.

**A. FDM Perceptiveness:**
Qualitative demonstrations (Fig. 4) highlight the FDM's ability to generate environment- and platform-aware state predictions. Unlike a simple constant velocity model, the FDM accurately captures the robot's actual dynamics, even on flat ground. More importantly, in complex terrains, it correctly detects potential collisions and models intricate movements on stairs and ramps, showcasing its deep understanding of robot-terrain interactions. This perceptiveness is consistently observed in both simulation and real-world scenarios, indicating successful sim-to-real transfer.

**B. Baseline Comparison (Position and Collision Estimation):**
The proposed FDM significantly outperforms competitive baselines in predicting robot states:
- **Position Estimation (Fig. 5, 6, Tab. II):** The FDM achieves an average of **41.28% lower position error** compared to the perceptive FDM by Kim et al. and **70.57% lower error** compared to the constant velocity model at the final prediction step (averaged across all environments). This superior accuracy is maintained even in complex 3D environments, where the 2D LiDAR-based baseline (Kim et al.) struggles due to its limited perception.
- **Collision Estimation (Tab. II):** The FDM demonstrates high accuracy in predicting collisions, with an F1 score of **0.9 in 2D environments** and **0.85 in 3D environments**. While the baseline might have a higher recall (predicting more potential collisions), the proposed FDM boasts higher precision, meaning its collision predictions are more likely to be correct. This is critical for reliable safe navigation, avoiding unnecessary conservative actions while still preventing actual failures.

**C. Platform-aware Predictions:**
The FDM exhibits remarkable platform-awareness (Fig. 7). By training individual FDMs using data from different robot platforms (ANYmal, Barry, ANYmal-On-Wheels) and even varying locomotion policies (e.g., Barry's robust vs. quiet policy), the models successfully capture the unique capabilities and dynamics of each specific embodiment. This demonstrates the model's ability to tailor its predictions to the hardware and control software, a significant step towards generalizability across different robotic systems.

**D. Real-World Fine-Tuning Impact:**
Integrating real-world data into the training process (fine-tuning) proves crucial for performance in dynamic, non-rigid environments (Fig. 8). While the sim-trained FDM already performs better than constant velocity models in the real world, fine-tuning with data from snow, forest, and pavement environments further **reduces the mean position error by 30-34%**. This highlights the necessity of real-world data to capture subtle, unmodeled effects like slipping on snow or entanglement in vegetation, effectively bridging the reality gap for scenarios beyond rigid-body physics.

**E. Planning Performance:**
When integrated into an MPPI planner, the FDM-based approach significantly enhances navigation success (Tab. III):
- **Success Rate:** The proposed method achieves the highest navigation success rates: **88.33% in 2D** and **73.75% in 3D environments**. This is notably superior to baselines, which show a steep performance drop in 3D (e.g., Kim et al. at 48.75% and heuristics at 33.13%), demonstrating the FDM's improved generalization across complex obstacles.
- **Path Efficiency:** The FDM-based planner generates the most effective paths, resulting in the **shortest mean path length (MPL)** and **mean path time (MPT)** for successful trajectories. This indicates that the precise dynamics knowledge from the FDM enables more direct and efficient navigation compared to baselines that may circle or get stuck.

**F. Qualitative Planning Evaluation:**
Qualitative assessments (Fig. 1, 9) confirm that the FDM-integrated MPPI effectively generates safe and efficient trajectories. The planner successfully avoids obstacles and untraversable regions by steering toward the goal, even in the presence of real-world challenges such as sensor noise and terrain inconsistencies. The simplified reward function, combining goal-oriented pose reward and learned failure risk penalization, proves sufficient for robust planning.

**G. Ablation Studies (Appendix D):**
- **Past States:** Essential for accurate failure estimation (up to 0.06 F1 score improvement), but not critical for pose accuracy.
- **Proprioceptive Inputs:** Crucial for both pose accuracy and failure prediction; their removal leads to significant performance degradation.
- **Exteroceptive Height Scan:** Indispensable for perceptive navigation. Removing it causes a **110% average increase in position error** and a **0.13 decrease in F1 score**.
- **Failure Risk Reward:** Its inclusion in the planner improves success rates (2.5% in 2D, 4.6% in 3D) but leads to slightly longer mean path times, indicating a more cautious planning behavior.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that the learned perceptive FDM provides highly accurate state predictions and robust failure estimation, directly translating into significantly improved navigation success rates and efficiency for quadrupedal robots in challenging, unstructured terrains. The hybrid training approach and the simplification of the planning objective are key enablers for this performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a significant advancement in the field of robotic navigation, particularly for legged and wheeled-legged systems operating in complex, unstructured environments. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

- **Pioneering Rough-Terrain FDM:** This work marks the "first application of a rough-terrain Forward Dynamics Model trained in simulation and deployed on a quadrupedal robot." This achievement is crucial for extending the operational capabilities of legged robots beyond structured or mildly uneven terrains into truly challenging environments that are common in real-world applications like disaster response, inspection, or planetary exploration.
- **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer:** The proposed hybrid training strategy, combining vast synthetic data with targeted real-world fine-tuning, offers a powerful paradigm for bridging the notoriously difficult simulation-reality gap. By safely training on high-risk maneuvers in simulation and then refining the model with real-world interactions that capture unmodeled physics (like deformable terrain, slippage, or entanglement), the method ensures robust performance in diverse, dynamic scenarios where rigid-body simulators might fall short. This approach is highly scalable and practical.
- **Simplified and Generalizable Planning:** The integration of the learned FDM into an MPPI framework revolutionizes the planning process by virtually eliminating the need for extensive, environment-specific cost function tuning. By implicitly learning traversability and providing direct risk scores, the FDM enables "zero-shot" adaptation to new environments, requiring only simple adjustments to high-level reward terms. This significantly reduces development time, increases deployment flexibility, and makes complex robot navigation more accessible.
- **Enhanced Safety and Robustness:** The FDM's ability to accurately predict future states and, crucially, the probability of failure (e.g., collisions, untraversable terrain) directly contributes to safer navigation. The system can proactively identify and avoid hazardous paths, leading to higher success rates and fewer risky situations compared to traditional methods. The model's robustness against sensor noise and terrain variability further cements its practical applicability.
- **Platform-Aware Autonomy:** The demonstrated ability of the FDM to capture the unique dynamics and locomotion capabilities of different robot embodiments (ANYmal, Barry, AoW) underscores its generalizability across a variety of legged platforms. This platform-awareness ensures that the navigation system is tailored to the specific robot's hardware and locomotion policy, leading to more efficient and reliable behaviors.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Broader Robot Deployment:** By enabling more robust, safer, and adaptable navigation in challenging terrains, this research paves the way for wider adoption of legged robots in domains like search and rescue, remote inspection of industrial facilities, autonomous construction, and environmental monitoring, where human access is difficult or dangerous.
- **Advancement in Simulation Fidelity:** The success of the learned FDM, particularly its ability to benefit from real-world data for scenarios beyond rigid-body simulation, could inspire further research into improving the fidelity of physics simulators themselves, especially for complex robot-environment interactions.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** This work provides a strong foundation for several future research directions, including:
 - **Adaptive Timesteps:** Exploring more dynamic command execution.
 - **Richer Environmental Representations:** Integrating RGB camera input for semantic understanding, going beyond geometric information.
 - **Uncertainty-Aware Planning:** Utilizing ensemble learning with the FDM to quantify prediction uncertainty, which can then be incorporated into planning for even more robust decision-making.
 - **Human-Robot Collaboration:** The improved safety and predictability could facilitate more intuitive and reliable human-robot interaction in shared workspaces.

In conclusion, this paper presents a significant leap forward in autonomous robot navigation, combining sophisticated data-driven dynamics modeling with practical, adaptable planning. Its contributions simplify deployment, enhance safety, and unlock new possibilities for legged robots in the most demanding real-world scenarios.
