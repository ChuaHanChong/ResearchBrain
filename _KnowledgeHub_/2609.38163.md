# Rethinking Representations for World-Action Modeling

> [!summary] Summary
> ReWAM is a world-action model built on frozen DINOv3 features instead of generative video latents. Feature Calibration and a Temporal Representation Bottleneck compress multi-view frames into compact world states, and only the action loss trains that bottleneck. Without video pre-training it reaches 93.6% success on RoboTwin 2.0, an 8.28% success rate on RoboDojo, and 91.7% on a real bimanual robot.

## Problem

- Recent world-action models predict in Video-VAE latents tuned for pixel reconstruction, which can spend capacity on appearance rather than action-relevant object configuration and contact.
- Pre-trained perceptual features such as DINO are computed frame by frame and have distributions poorly matched to diffusion or flow-matching prediction objectives.
- Independent frame features do not encode temporal change, and prior designs assume reconstruction quality or a pre-trained encoder automatically yields a good control state.

## Method

- **Feature Calibration** averages ==DINOv3== patch features from seven layers, standardizes prediction targets, and shifts the ==flow-matching== noise schedule according to target dimensionality.
- **Temporal Representation Bottleneck** groups ==2x2x2 spatio-temporal blocks== across three cameras, fuses them with ==self-attention==, and projects them to 120 tokens of 128 dimensions.
- **Action-Grounded Representation Shaping** lets only ==action-loss gradients== update the bottleneck, while separate ==Mixture-of-Transformers== world and action branches learn dynamics and actions with flow matching.

## Results

- On RoboTwin 2.0 ReWAM scores **93.6%** in both clean and randomized settings, versus best baselines at **93.4%** and **92.2%**, without generative video pre-training.
- Raw DINO beats Video-VAE latents in success rate (**71.0%** vs **66.1%** clean) despite lower reconstruction SSIM (**0.70** vs **0.91**); calibration adds **12.2 points** on average.
- Action-grounded shaping beats a DINO-reconstruction bottleneck (**90.7%** vs **85.6%**), and letting world-loss gradients reach the bottleneck costs **6.3 points**; on the real robot ReWAM reaches **91.7%** vs **80.0%** for Fast-WAM.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Reconstruction fidelity is a poor proxy for control utility; a lower-SSIM representation can drive substantially better policies than a high-fidelity video latent.
> - Asymmetric gradient routing works well: let the policy decide what the state encodes and the world model only learn how it evolves, avoiding predictive shortcuts.
> - Skipping generative video pre-training saves compute but costs open-ended instruction following and precision, so the choice of representation trades control gains against language generality.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position in the Broader Research Landscape

World-action models (WAMs) are designed to learn robot control policies while also predicting how observations or latent world states change over time. This combines elements of vision-language-action modeling, robot imitation learning, video prediction, and generative dynamics modeling. Recent WAMs commonly use latent spaces produced by video variational autoencoders (Video-VAEs) or other generative video systems. These systems benefit from dynamics priors learned during large-scale video pre-training, but their latent spaces are primarily optimized for visual reconstruction.

The paper addresses a central question in this area: what properties should a representation have when it must serve both as a prediction target for a world model and as an interface for policy learning? The authors distinguish three types of representation. Video-VAE latents are reconstruction-oriented, frozen perceptual features such as DINO features are trained to capture semantic and visual structure, and action-grounded representations are shaped directly by the control objective.

This question is related to a growing body of work on representation-space world models, including methods that predict DINO features, use visual-action tokenizers, align future visual states with policy representations, or predict geometric and motion-aware features. It also connects to recent research on representation autoencoders and feature alignment for diffusion models. The paper’s specific contribution is to treat representation construction as a separate design problem rather than assuming that either pixel reconstruction quality or a pre-trained feature encoder automatically produces a suitable control state.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to develop a world-action model whose latent representation is useful for both future-state prediction and action generation without relying on generative video pre-training.

The authors are motivated by three observations. First, visual reconstruction fidelity does not necessarily correspond to control utility. A representation can preserve detailed appearance information while allocating insufficient capacity to action-relevant information, such as object configuration, contact relationships, or changes associated with manipulation. Second, pre-trained perceptual features are not automatically appropriate for dynamics prediction. DINO features are computed frame by frame and may have distributions that are poorly matched to diffusion or flow-matching objectives. Third, temporal structure must be explicitly organized. Independent frame features do not directly encode how observations change across time.

To address these issues, the paper proposes three design principles:

1. **Calibration of perceptual features.** DINO features should be normalized, aggregated across encoder layers, and paired with a noise schedule that accounts for the dimensionality of the prediction target.
2. **Temporal compression.** Frame-wise features should be converted into compact states that organize spatial and temporal information for world modeling.
3. **Action-grounded shaping.** The policy objective should determine which information the representation retains, while the world-model objective should learn the dynamics of that representation without directly changing its target space.

These principles are implemented in a model called **ReWAM**, or representation-centric world-action model. The authors evaluate whether this design improves control performance in simulated and physical manipulation settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Overall Model Structure

ReWAM receives two historical multi-view observations, the current observation, proprioceptive state, and a language instruction. The observations include a head camera and two wrist cameras. Pairs of consecutive observations are encoded into latent states. The first state, \(z_0\), conditions both the action and world-prediction branches, while future states \(z_{1:H}\) serve as targets for prediction.

The model follows a Mixture-of-Transformers architecture with separate world and action Diffusion Transformer branches. Both branches receive language and proprioceptive conditioning. Future-state tokens and action tokens attend to current-state world features, but attention between the future-state and action token groups is blocked. The world branch predicts future representations causally, while the action branch generates an entire action chunk. Both branches are trained using flow matching. During inference, only the current state is needed to condition action generation; the model does not have to generate future states before executing actions.

#### 3.2. DINO Feature Calibration

The visual encoder is a frozen DINOv3 ViT-L/16 backbone. For every image and camera view, the method extracts patch features from seven intermediate and late layers: blocks 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 22, and 24.

The features are calibrated in three ways:

- **Multi-layer aggregation:** Features from the selected layers are averaged, and the spatial mean of the final layer is added to every patch. This combines information from different encoder depths while retaining the patch grid.
- **Normalization:** Prediction targets are standardized. For ReWAM, running statistics are used because the bottleneck representation changes during training.
- **Representation-aware noise scheduling:** The flow-matching noise level is adjusted according to the number of scalar elements in the prediction target. Larger targets receive a shifted noise distribution that favors noisier inputs, following a strategy developed for representation autoencoders.

The ablation results indicate that each component contributes to performance, with multi-layer aggregation producing the largest individual gain among the tested calibration steps.

#### 3.3. Temporal Representation Bottleneck

The Temporal Representation Bottleneck (TRB) transforms calibrated frame-level DINO features into compact world states. For each camera, non-overlapping spatial-temporal blocks of size \(2 \times 2 \times 2\) are grouped into tokens. Tokens from all camera views are concatenated and processed by multi-head self-attention, followed by a linear projection to a 128-dimensional representation.

The resulting state contains 120 tokens: 80 from the head camera and 20 from each wrist camera. Causal running normalization is applied using statistics accumulated during training, which are fixed at inference time.

The TRB serves two purposes. It explicitly integrates information across time, and it reduces the representation size used by the world model. This differs from a reconstruction codec, which would be trained to preserve pixel or feature information rather than to retain information most useful for action prediction.

#### 3.4. Action-Grounded Representation Shaping

The central training mechanism is Action-Grounded Representation Shaping (AGRS). The method routes gradients asymmetrically:

- The **action loss** is allowed to update the TRB through the current state \(z_0\).
- The **world-prediction loss** is prevented from updating the TRB.
- The world model itself is trained using the future representations produced by the bottleneck.

This creates a division of roles. The action objective determines what information the representation should encode, while the world branch learns how that action-relevant representation changes over time. The authors argue that allowing the world loss to directly modify the representation can encourage predictive shortcuts or collapse the representation toward features that are easy to predict but less useful for control.

Training uses flow matching. A clean future state or action chunk is linearly interpolated with Gaussian noise, and the model learns the corresponding velocity field. The action vectors contain 14 dimensions for joint and gripper commands, and actions are standardized using z-score normalization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. RoboTwin 2.0

RoboTwin 2.0 contains 50 manipulation tasks evaluated in clean and randomized settings. ReWAM is trained using 2,500 clean trajectories and 25,000 randomized trajectories.

It achieves a **93.6% success rate in both settings**. The strongest comparison methods reported success rates of 93.4% in the clean setting and 92.2% in the random setting. ReWAM therefore improves over those references by 0.2 and 1.4 percentage points, respectively.

The representation comparisons provide the main evidence for the paper’s thesis. Without generative video pre-training, raw DINO features achieve 71.0% success in the clean setting and 71.54% in the random setting, compared with 66.10% and 62.32% for the corresponding Fast-WAM configuration using Video-VAE latents. The Video-VAE representation has substantially higher pixel-reconstruction SSIM, 0.91 compared with 0.70 for DINO, but lower control performance.

Calibration raises average success by 12.20 percentage points over raw DINO features. Normalization contributes 3.92 points, the representation-aware noise schedule contributes 1.41 additional points, and multi-layer aggregation contributes 6.87 additional points in the cumulative experiment.

#### 4.2. RoboDojo

RoboDojo evaluates generalization, precision, long-horizon behavior, memory, and open-ended instruction following. Without embodied pre-training, ReWAM achieves an average score of **9.42** and a success rate of **5.82%**, compared with 7.69 and 3.83% for the strongest listed alternative in that setting.

With approximately 600 hours of embodied pre-training, ReWAM reaches an average score of **12.29** and a success rate of **8.28%**. It obtains the highest generalization score and success rate in the comparison and the highest long-horizon success rate. However, its open-instruction performance remains low, with a score of 0.25 and success rate of 0.25%. The authors associate this limitation with the lack of generative video pre-training and the resulting weaker instruction generalization. ReWAM also trails geometry-enhanced methods on precision tasks.

The comparison should be interpreted with attention to data scale. ReWAM and the matched Fast-WAM baseline use the same approximately 600 hours of embodied data, while several other methods report thousands or tens of thousands of hours.

#### 4.3. Ablation and Representation Analysis

A fixed random projection that temporally organizes DINO features improves average RoboTwin success by 2.89 points over the calibrated frame-wise baseline. However, AGRS improves over the fixed projection by an additional 3.94 points in the clean setting and 3.18 points in the random setting.

Reconstruction-based TRB training performs less well. An RGB reconstruction codec produces 71.04% and 70.48% success, while a DINO reconstruction codec produces 85.58% and 86.08%. AGRS reaches 90.70% and 89.12%, showing that reconstruction objectives alone do not provide the same control benefit.

Gradient-routing experiments support excluding world-loss gradients from the TRB. Allowing world-loss gradients through the current representation reduces average success by 6.30 points, and allowing them through both current and future paths produces a further decline. AGRS also remains effective with a 64- or 128-dimensional bottleneck, whereas a fixed projection loses performance when reduced from 512 to 128 dimensions.

A representation analysis using t-SNE and nearest-neighbor direction agreement finds that AGRS representations show clearer grouping by commanded motion direction than Video-VAE representations. Ten-nearest-neighbor direction agreement rises from 43.0% to 47.1% for one task and from 64.4% to 70.5% for another.

#### 4.4. Real-Robot Evaluation

On a bimanual Piper platform, ReWAM is tested on Collect Objects, Fold Clothes, and Unpack Lunchbox. It succeeds in 55 of 60 trials, or **91.7%**, compared with 48 of 60, or **80.0%**, for Fast-WAM. Both systems complete all Collect Objects trials. ReWAM improves success by 15 percentage points on Fold Clothes and by 20 points on Unpack Lunchbox.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper’s main significance is methodological: it reframes representation design in world-action models as an interaction between perception, temporal prediction, and control rather than as a by-product of video reconstruction. Its results suggest that representations optimized for visual fidelity may not be optimal for robot action, and that pre-trained perceptual features can become more useful when calibrated and shaped by the policy objective.

ReWAM also offers a route to reducing dependence on large-scale generative video pre-training. This may lower computational and data requirements for systems focused on manipulation, particularly when control performance matters more than producing visually realistic future frames. The real-robot results indicate that the approach transfers beyond simulation, although the evaluation covers only three tasks and one robot platform.

Several limitations remain. The model is large, at approximately 3.58 billion parameters, and training uses 32 H20 GPUs. Performance on open-ended instruction following is weak, and comparisons involving embodied pre-training involve different data scales across methods. The paper also evaluates a limited set of robot embodiments and manipulation scenarios. Nonetheless, the proposed separation between action-driven representation shaping and world-model dynamics learning provides a testable design principle for future WAMs and related vision-language-action systems.
