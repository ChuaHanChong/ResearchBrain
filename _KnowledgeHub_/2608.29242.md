# AnyWorld: Factorized Egocentric World Models for Cross-Embodiment Generalization

> [!summary] Summary
> AnyWorld is a factorized egocentric video-diffusion world model that expands one unpaired human egocentric interaction into many robot-native rollouts. It separates an interaction into pixel-space skeleton action control, Plücker-ray camera control, and an embodiment condition given by an edited first frame plus text tag, so body, viewpoint, and scene recompose independently; generated data lifts real-robot grasping from 20.0% to 55.0%.

## Problem

- Collecting contact-rich robot demonstrations is expensive and hardware-bound, so datasets rarely repeat the same interaction structure across different bodies, viewpoints, object layouts, and scenes.
- Human egocentric video is abundant but each clip is locked to one body, one camera trajectory, and one environment, capturing only a narrow slice of experience.
- Prior human-to-robot transfer and robot world models predict futures inside a fixed embodiment and expose no unified interface that separately controls action, camera, and embodiment.

## Method

- ==Action-camera-embodiment factorization==: a rendered ==skeleton control video== sets in-image motion, ==Plücker ray embeddings== encode camera geometry, and an edited initial frame plus text tag specifies target body and scene.
- ==Latent diffusion== backbone initialized from ==WAN Fun-Control 14B==, where action latents concatenate channel-wise with noisy latents, a camera adapter adds to patch embeddings, and embodiment text enters through ==cross-attention==.
- Two-stage training: ==EgoDex== human pretraining then unpaired mixed-embodiment fine-tuning, plus an ==action-calibration module== mapping human wrist trajectories into the target robot's relative end-effector action space.

## Results

- Controllability averages **0.778** versus **0.609** for WAN Fun-Control and **0.417** for Cosmos-Predict2.5, with CameraAlign **0.789** and EmbodAcc **0.886**, and VBench quality still highest at **0.971**.
- Recomposed rollouts lift the UniT VLA from **49.8%** to **54.6%** on 18 RoboCasa GR1 pick-and-place tasks and from **20.0%** to **55.0%** over 20 real IRON grasp trials.
- A **2:1** human-to-robot fine-tuning mixture scores best at **0.740** average; an action-only counterfactual intervention fails to yield stable spatial instruction following without visual recomposition.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Pixel-space skeleton control is a more embodiment-agnostic conditioning interface than joint commands, letting one video model share motion structure across human and robot bodies.
> - Action relabeling alone is insufficient: policies need robot-native visual states in which language and action are jointly grounded, not human imagery with robot labels.
> - Cross-embodiment data mixing has a sweet spot, since more human data improves motion diversity but erodes embodiment grounding and viewpoint fidelity.
> - Targeted recomposition can repair a pre-identified closed-loop failure, evidencing a data-expansion mechanism rather than a generic scaling effect.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalizable robot manipulation capabilities is currently constrained by the challenges associated with collecting diverse, contact-rich robot experiences at scale. Modern robot learning systems often rely on large-scale visual-action datasets. However, obtaining such data directly from physical robots is resource-intensive, hardware-specific, and difficult to scale across varied scenes and robot embodiments. Generalizable policies necessitate not only a large quantity of interactions but also repeated exposure to similar task-relevant interaction structures under varying body morphologies, camera viewpoints, object arrangements, and scene contexts.

Human egocentric videos represent a potential source for addressing this data bottleneck. These videos are abundant, naturally capture intricate manipulation activities, and encompass a wide array of daily physical interactions, providing a valuable source of action diversity and auxiliary supervision for robot learning. However, each human video is inherently tied to a specific body, camera trajectory, and environment, presenting a form of heterogeneous embodiment data with distinct visual characteristics. The fundamental challenge lies in transforming each recorded human interaction into a reusable "seed" that can be recomposed into multiple robot-native experiences.

Prior research has explored human-to-robot transfer, but these methods typically do not offer a unified framework that explicitly controls action, camera, and embodiment for generating diverse robot rollouts. Existing robot world models generally focus on predicting future states within a fixed embodiment domain, adapting to a specific target robot, or enhancing action prediction. This paper introduces AnyWorld to fill this gap by proposing a model that can decouple the "what happens" of an interaction (motion pattern, object involvement, temporal progression) from "how it is visually realized" (acting body, viewpoint, visual scene context). This allows for the recomposition of a single human interaction into multiple robot-domain experiences by independently controlling these factors, thus converting human videos from static demonstrations into adaptable sources of robot training data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the recognized bottleneck in robot learning: the difficulty and expense of collecting sufficient quantities of diverse, contact-rich robot experiences. Generalizable robot manipulation policies require exposure to a wide range of interaction structures across various robot bodies, camera viewpoints, and environmental configurations. While large-scale visual-action datasets have advanced robot learning, their collection on physical robots remains a significant practical challenge.

Human egocentric videos offer a promising alternative due to their abundance and natural capture of diverse physical interactions. However, these videos present a narrow, fixed slice of experience, being specific to a single human body, camera perspective, and environmental context. This inherent limitation prevents their direct and versatile application for training robots that operate in different visual and physical domains.

The core objective of this work is to address this limitation by developing a framework that can transform a single human egocentric interaction into a multitude of diverse robot-native experiences. This involves:
- **Decoupling Interaction Factors:** The central aim is to separate the intrinsic dynamics and sequence of an interaction ("what happens") from its specific visual realization ("how it is realized"). "What happens" refers to the abstract motion patterns, object interactions, and temporal progression of a task. "How it is realized" pertains to the specific acting body (e.g., human vs. robot), the camera's trajectory and viewpoint, and the visual properties of the surrounding scene and objects.
- **Enabling Independent Recomposition:** By achieving this factorization, the framework seeks to allow for the independent manipulation and recomposition of the action, camera, and embodiment factors. This capability would enable the model to preserve the underlying task dynamics and object interactions from a human demonstration while generating it under different robot bodies, camera viewpoints, and scene configurations.
- **Zero-Shot Generalization without Paired Data:** A key objective is to achieve this cross-embodiment generalization and experience expansion without requiring paired human-robot demonstrations. This avoids the prohibitive cost and logistical challenges of collecting the same interaction performed by both a human and a robot in precisely matched settings.
- **Generating Robot-Native Rollouts:** The ultimate goal is to produce visual-action rollouts that are explicitly "robot-native" in appearance, suitable for direct use in training or adapting robot manipulation policies. This includes adapting not just the visual observations but also generating morphology-calibrated actions for the target robot.

In summary, the motivation is to overcome data scarcity and heterogeneity in robot learning by leveraging abundant human egocentric data, transforming it into a versatile, recomposable source of robot training experiences through a factorized world modeling approach.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AnyWorld addresses the problem of zero-shot recomposition, aiming to convert an egocentric human interaction video into a target-robot visual experience without requiring paired human-robot demonstrations.

#### 3.1. Problem Formulation

Given a source human video $V_h = \{I_h_0, \dots, I_h_T\}$, the model extracts an action control sequence $A_{1:T}$ and a camera control sequence $C_{1:T}$. For a target embodiment $e$, an initial frame $\tilde{I}_{e0}$ is provided, specifying the desired body and scene context. The objective is to generate $\hat{V}_e_{1:T} = G_{\theta}(\tilde{I}_{e0}, A_{1:T}, C_{1:T}, \tau_e)$, where $G_{\theta}$ is a cross-embodiment world model and $\tau_e$ specifies the target embodiment. This formulation allows for controllable recomposition of interaction trajectory, viewpoint, embodiment, and scene.

#### 3.2. Action-Camera-Embodiment Factorization

The framework factorizes each interaction into three key components: action, camera, and embodiment, to control "what motion is performed," "how the scene is observed," and "which body performs the interaction," respectively.

- **Action Condition ($A_{1:T}$):** This is represented as a rendered skeleton control video, specifying motion in the image plane rather than specific joint commands. This pixel-space control aims to be embodiment-agnostic and is encoded into a latent tensor $Z_a_{1:T}$ to serve as a dense motion condition.
- **Camera Condition ($C_{1:T}$):** Camera intrinsics and extrinsics for each frame are converted into a Plücker ray embedding. This conditioning helps the model separate the actor's motion from the camera's motion. The embedding $Z_c_{1:T}$ provides an additive geometric conditioning signal.
- **Embodiment Condition:** This is specified by the initial frame ($\tilde{I}_{e0}$) and a text tag ($\tau_e$). The initial frame conveys the target body's appearance, scene context, object layout, and initial spatial relationships. The text tag (e.g., "human," "RoboCasa GR1," "IRON") identifies the acting embodiment, encoded together with a caption.

#### 3.3. Cross-Embodiment World Model (Gθ)

AnyWorld implements $G_{\theta}$ as a latent diffusion video model. The model predicts diffusion noise conditioned on the three factors:
- **Action Control Injection:** The encoded action control video ($Z_a_{1:T}$) is concatenated with the noisy video latent ($Z_s$) along the channel dimension, acting as an input-channel condition for the Diffusion Transformer (DiT).
- **Camera Control Injection:** The camera control embedding ($Z_c_{1:T}$) is processed by a lightweight adapter and added to the patch embedding of the DiT, providing an additive geometric signal.
- **Embodiment Tag and Caption Injection:** These are encoded as text embeddings ($E_{\tau}$) and injected into each DiT block via cross-attention, allowing the model to bind the rollout to different embodiment domains.

#### 3.4. Training

The training process comprises two stages:
- **Stage 1: Human Interaction Pretraining:** The model is initially pretrained on a large dataset of egocentric human interaction videos, EgoDex (200K clips for 30K steps). This stage familiarizes the model with diverse, contact-rich human motion patterns and establishes a robust action-camera conditioned video prior.
- **Stage 2: Mixed-Embodiment Fine-tuning:** Following pretraining, the model is fine-tuned using unpaired multi-embodiment data (EgoDex, RoboCasa GR1, and IRON clips for 5K steps). All videos are converted into the same factorized format. No paired human-robot videos are used. The model learns to associate different embodiment appearances with compatible interaction dynamics through this shared action-camera conditioning interface. A 2:1 human-to-robot data ratio is employed during this fine-tuning stage, found to balance human interaction diversity with robot embodiment grounding.

#### 3.5. Zero-Shot Experience Recomposition (Inference)

At inference time, the factorized interface enables independent recomposition. Given a source video, its action ($A_{1:T}$) and camera ($C_{1:T}$) controls are extracted. The initial frame is then edited ($\tilde{I}_{e0}$) to specify the target visual context (e.g., changing the acting body, background scene, or visual style while preserving spatial layout and interaction objects). Along with the target embodiment tag ($\tau_e$), these inputs define the desired target domain for rollout generation. This allows for body, viewpoint, scene, or combined recomposition from a single human interaction.

#### 3.6. Joint Visual–Action Experience Recomposition

For downstream policy training, AnyWorld transfers both the visual observation and the corresponding action. The source video is converted into a target-robot observation by re-embodying the actor and, if necessary, intervening on the task-relevant scene state. Concurrently, an action-calibration module maps the human wrist trajectory to the target robot's relative end-effector action space. Each transferred experience thus consists of the re-embodied rollout ($\hat{V}_e_{1:T}$), task instruction ($\ell$), and morphology-calibrated robot action ($\tilde{A}_e_{1:T}$).

#### 3.7. Supplementary Implementation Details

- **Generalizable Human-to-Robot Embodiment Editing:** A novel "reverse pseudo-pair construction" strategy is used to generate the target initial frames ($\tilde{I}_{e0}$). This involves first editing real robot images into human-like observations using an off-the-shelf image editor (Nano-Banana). These robot-to-human pseudo-pairs then supervise the training of an inverse human-to-robot embodiment editor (Qwen-Image-Edit), which performs the required human-to-robot replacement at inference time while preserving scene and interaction geometry. Synchronous offline augmentation is applied to these pseudo-pairs to increase diversity.
- **World Model Training:** Full fine-tuning of the Wan 2.1 14B backbone and conditioning modules is performed. Structured captions are used to explicitly separate scene, action, and acting subject, which are then encoded and injected via cross-attention.
- **Policy Adaptation:** UniT serves as the VLA policy baseline, pretrained on EgoDex. AnyWorld's generated robot-domain rollouts are mixed with target-domain robot data (1:1 ratio) during an intermediate adaptation stage (1K steps). This is followed by a final robot-only fine-tuning stage (500 steps) to fine-tune on the deployment embodiment.
- **Targeted Visual-Action Pair Construction:** For specific capability transfer experiments, human demonstrations are jointly transferred in observation (re-embodied and re-scened by AnyWorld) and action (human wrist trajectory calibrated into IRON's end-effector space). This allows for interventions targeting specific policy gaps, such as repairing a completion shortcut or grounding spatial instructions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of AnyWorld focused on its capacity for controllable experience recomposition and its utility in improving downstream Vision-Language-Action (VLA) adaptation.

#### 4.1. Qualitative Robot Experience Recomposition

- **Embodiment Recomposition:** The model demonstrated the ability to recompose human interactions under different robot bodies (RoboCasa GR1 and IRON). By keeping action and camera controls fixed and only changing the initial frame and embodiment tag, AnyWorld generated rollouts where the specified robot body performed the interaction, preserving the source motion and scene layout (Figure 3).
- **Viewpoint Recomposition:** AnyWorld showed camera controllability by generating the same physical interaction from different viewpoints. When the action, target body, and scene remained fixed, changing the camera trajectory resulted in a different perspective while maintaining the underlying interaction dynamics (Figure 4).
- **Scene and Body Recomposition:** The model also supported first-frame-based scene and body editing. It could reuse human action-camera controls to generate interactions with a different acting body, a new background scene, or both, illustrating its ability to create diverse robot-domain visual experiences from a single human interaction (Figure 5).

#### 4.2. Controllability and Video Quality

- **World-Model Controllability (Table 1):** AnyWorld achieved superior controllability across action, camera, and embodiment conditions compared to baseline models. It recorded an ActionAlign score of 0.659, a CameraAlign score of 0.789, and an EmbodAcc of 0.886, resulting in an average controllability score of 0.778. This performance surpassed Cosmos-Predict2.5 (Avg. 0.417) and WAN Fun-Control (Avg. 0.609), indicating the effectiveness of its factorized design.
- **Video Quality Evaluation (Table 2):** The model maintained competitive video quality, as measured by VBench metrics. AnyWorld achieved strong subject consistency (0.942), background consistency (0.949), flicker stability (0.995), and motion smoothness (0.996), with an average VBench score of 0.971, which was comparable to or slightly better than the baselines.
- **Physically Consistent Outcome Simulation (Supplementary Figure 2):** AnyWorld demonstrated the ability to simulate both successful and failed interaction outcomes based on variations in root motion. When root motion facilitated interaction (e.g., robot close to target), successful grasps were generated. Conversely, misaligned root motions led to plausible failure cases (e.g., robot missing the target), indicating the model captures the dependency between motion, object reachability, and interaction outcomes.

#### 4.3. Training Ablations

- **Human-to-Robot Ratio (Table 3):** An ablation study on the ratio of human-to-robot data during mixed fine-tuning showed that a 2:1 mixture achieved the best average controllability (0.740). Ratios of 4:1 and 1:1 resulted in lower scores, suggesting that balancing human interaction diversity with robot embodiment grounding is crucial.

#### 4.4. Transferred Experience for VLA Adaptation

- **Downstream VLA Adaptation Gains (Table 4):** AnyWorld's generated robot-domain rollouts significantly improved VLA policy adaptation.
 - In RoboCasa GR1 simulation, adding transferred experience to a UniT baseline improved pick-and-place task success from 49.8% to 54.6% (+4.8%).
 - On a real IRON humanoid robot, the same strategy boosted banana-grasping success rates from 20.0% to 55.0% (+35.0%) over 20 trials. These results indicate that recomposed human experience, converted into robot-native observations, serves as effective training data for target-embodiment policy adaptation.

#### 4.5. Targeted Capability Transfer Through Controlled Recomposition

- **State-Conditional Policy Repair (Table 5, Figure 6a):** AnyWorld was used to address a specific policy gap. A robot-only policy failed to continue a pick-and-place task in a partially completed state (one object placed, another remaining). By re-scening human demonstrations into this missing robot-domain state and pairing them with calibrated IRON actions, the adapted policy resumed task-directed motion and completed the remaining placement. This demonstrates the model's capacity for targeted policy repair by filling specific state coverage gaps.
- **Counterfactual Grounding of Spatial Instructions (Table 5, Figure 6b):** To transfer language-grounded spatial target selection, AnyWorld constructed matched dual-target IRON observations paired with opposite spatial instructions and corresponding left/right actions. An ablation showed that an "action-only" intervention (pairing counterfactual actions with original one-sided IRON observations) failed to produce reliable instruction following. Stable target switching only emerged when the counterfactual actions were grounded in the re-embodied and re-scened human visual pairs, demonstrating the necessity of robot-native visual recomposition in addition to action calibration.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

AnyWorld represents a development in robot learning by addressing the critical bottlenecks of data scarcity and diversity for generalizable manipulation policies. Its primary significance lies in providing a scalable mechanism to transform abundant human egocentric interaction videos into diverse, robot-native training experiences without relying on expensive and logistically challenging paired human-robot demonstrations.

The key aspects of its significance and potential impact include:
- **Data Augmentation and Scalability:** AnyWorld serves as an experience multiplier, allowing a single human interaction to be expanded into numerous robot-domain rollouts under varying embodiments, viewpoints, and scene configurations. This capability can significantly augment existing robot datasets, offering a cost-effective pathway to scale robot training data.
- **Cross-Embodiment Generalization:** By factorizing interaction into action, camera, and embodiment, the model enables a robot to learn from human experiences, adapting the "what happens" of an interaction to its specific "how it is realized." This approach facilitates the transfer of skills and interaction dynamics across different robot morphologies.
- **Improved Policy Adaptation:** The empirical results demonstrate that robot-native rollouts generated by AnyWorld can enhance the performance of downstream Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policies. The observed gains in both simulation (RoboCasa GR1) and real-world robot experiments (IRON humanoid) suggest that this method provides a valuable resource for adapting generalist policies to specific target robots and tasks.
- **Targeted Capability Transfer and Policy Repair:** The ability to recompose experiences to address specific policy gaps is a notable contribution. By generating robot-native visual-action pairs that target known deficiencies (e.g., correcting spurious completion priors or grounding language-based spatial instructions), AnyWorld offers a mechanism for controlled, targeted policy improvement, moving beyond generic data scaling effects. The finding that robot-native visual recomposition is crucial for grounding such capabilities, beyond just action calibration, underscores the depth of transfer achieved.
- **Bridging Human and Robot Domains:** The framework offers a method for effectively leveraging the vast repository of human interaction data for robotics, bridging the visual and morphological gap between human and robot operations. This could accelerate the development of more capable and versatile robot systems for complex, contact-rich manipulation tasks.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The paper acknowledges several limitations, which also inform future research directions:
- **Sensory Modalities:** Current visual rollouts do not fully capture tactile feedback or precise contact forces, which are essential for many intricate contact-rich control tasks. Future work could incorporate tactile supervision or other sensory inputs.
- **Robustness of Extraction:** The method relies on accurate action and camera extraction from source videos, which can be affected by severe occlusion, fast motion, tracking errors, or camera shake. Uncertainty-aware filtering could enhance robustness.
- **Generalization Scope:** The current evaluation covers three embodiments. Expanding generalization to a wider array of robot morphologies, object types, and longer-horizon tasks remains a future area of exploration. Larger and more diverse mixed-embodiment datasets would also be beneficial.

Overall, AnyWorld contributes to the goal of generalist robot learning by providing a systematic, data-driven approach to generate diverse and targeted robot experiences from human demonstrations, potentially alleviating a major bottleneck in robot skill acquisition and deployment.
