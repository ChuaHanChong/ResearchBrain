# Generalizable End-to-End Tool-Use RL with Synthetic CodeGym

> [!summary] Summary
> CodeGym, developed by researchers from Carnegie Mellon University and ByteDance Seed, provides a scalable framework for training large language model agents using reinforcement learning by converting coding problems into diverse, verifiable, multi-turn tool-use environments. Training with CodeGym led to an 8.7 absolute accuracy gain on the out-of-distribution 	-Bench benchmark for the Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct model, demonstrating improved generalization.

## Problem

- Large Language Models (LLMs) are limited by static parametric memory, hindering dynamic interaction with external environments and new information acquisition.
- Current LLM agent training, primarily through supervised fine-tuning (SFT) or narrow reinforcement learning (RL), often results in brittle agents with poor generalization to out-of-distribution tools or workflows.
- There is a lack of diverse, scalable, and verifiable RL environments for LLM agents to develop broad, general-purpose tool-use capabilities.

## Method

- Introduced **CodeGym**, a scalable framework that synthesizes diverse, verifiable ==multi-turn tool-use== environments from competitive programming problems and their solutions.
- The framework extracts atomic functions from code solutions to create callable tools and formalizes interactive environments as Partially Observable Markov Decision Processes (POMDPs).
- Implemented a robust Gym Verification stage with LLM-generated unit tests and quality control filters based on tool-use complexity and difficulty to ensure high-quality training data for RL.

## Results

- CodeGym training consistently improved average reward across all model sizes, with Qwen2.5-**32B**-Instruct achieving an 8.7 absolute accuracy gain on the OOD 	-Bench benchmark and an average 7.3-point improvement across various OOD tasks.
- Models trained on CodeGym demonstrated consistent OOD generalization gains across tool-use (e.g., 	-bench, 	^2-bench), multi-turn interaction (ALFWorld), and reasoning (ZebraLogic, MMLU-Pro) benchmarks.
- Ablation studies confirmed that RL training within CodeGym yielded superior generalization compared to supervised fine-tuning, and explicit environment filtering was crucial for achieving strong performance.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Coding problems, with their inherent rigorous execution logic, provide a structured and verifiable foundation for constructing rich, interactive tool-use environments.
> - Active learning via reinforcement learning within CodeGym environments significantly enhances LLM agent out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization capabilities, outperforming supervised fine-tuning.
> - Larger LLMs benefit more from CodeGym training, exhibiting improved planning capabilities and systematic problem-solving, which translates to better OOD performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated advanced capabilities in reasoning, code generation, and instruction following. However, a limitation of these models is their reliance on static parametric memory, which restricts their ability to interact dynamically with external environments or acquire new information beyond their training data. This limitation is being addressed by a paradigm of "tool-augmented LLM agents," which integrate LLMs with external tools such as databases, search engines, and code executors. These tools enable LLM agents to expand their problem-solving and interaction capacities, allowing them to interface with the real world.

Current training methodologies for LLM agents predominantly involve supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on static trajectories or reinforcement learning (RL) applied to narrow, domain-specific tasks. SFT, while improving performance on specific benchmarks, often produces agents that are brittle and generalize poorly to out-of-distribution (OOD) settings, such as new tools or unforeseen workflows. This is attributed to the hand-crafted nature of training trajectories and the limited diversity of environments and task configurations explored during SFT.

Reinforcement learning offers a potential alternative, as it allows agents to learn through active exploration and interaction, exploiting feedback from dynamic contexts and failures. This approach may foster greater adaptability and generalization compared to static demonstrations. However, existing RL environments for LLM agents are often tailored to narrow tasks (e.g., coding assistants, information search), which limits their utility for developing general-purpose tool-use capabilities.

The presented work introduces CodeGym, a framework designed to bridge the gap by providing a scalable, diverse, and verifiable RL environment for training LLM agents in multi-turn tool-use scenarios. By leveraging coding problems as a structured substrate, the authors aim to create an environment that reflects the complexity and diversity of real-world agent workflows, thereby addressing the limitations of current training practices and the scarcity of broadly applicable general-purpose tool-use environments for RL.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a scalable and generalizable method for training end-to-end tool-use reinforcement learning agents based on large language models. The authors aim to overcome the limitations of existing training paradigms, particularly the poor generalization of supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and the narrow scope of current reinforcement learning (RL) environments for LLM agents.

The motivation for this work stems from several observed deficiencies in the current landscape of LLM agent training:

1. **Brittleness and Poor Generalization**: Current LLM agents, often trained on SFT datasets, exhibit brittleness and struggle to generalize to distribution shifts, such as new tools or unseen workflows. This is because existing agent interaction data in standard pretraining corpora is insufficient in quality and quantity, leading to reliance on hand-crafted patterns and limited environment exploration.
2. **Lack of Diverse and Scalable RL Environments**: While RL shows promise for improving generalization by enabling active exploration and learning from feedback, existing RL environments for LLM agents are typically designed for specific, narrow tasks. This prevents LLM agents from developing broad, transferable tool-use strategies that are essential for real-world applications involving diverse tool sets and complex workflows.
3. **Need for Verifiable and Structured Environments**: Real-world workflows often involve rigorous logic and verifiable outcomes. Coding problems inherently possess these characteristics, offering a natural foundation for constructing rich, structured tool-use environments. The execution semantics of code provide a built-in mechanism for verifying actions and assigning rewards.

To address these motivations, the key objectives of the CodeGym framework are threefold:

- **Introduce CodeGym**: To establish a scalable pipeline that transforms static coding problems into explorable and verifiable multi-turn tool-use environments. This involves extracting atomic functions and logic from coding solutions to create callable tools that agents can invoke interactively.
- **Create a Diverse Task Suite**: To generate a large collection of tasks within CodeGym that feature varied logic and tool sets. This diversity is intended to ensure broad trajectory coverage and provide stable, rigorous feedback, fostering robust agent adaptation.
- **Demonstrate Generalization via RL**: To show empirically that training LLM agents using reinforcement learning on CodeGym environments significantly improves their out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization capabilities. This objective aims to highlight CodeGym's value in developing agents with transferable interaction strategies, preventing overfitting to specific tasks.

By achieving these objectives, the researchers aim to take a step toward developing general-purpose RL environments that align with the complexity and diversity of real-world agent workflows.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology centers on CodeGym, a scalable framework that synthesizes diverse, verifiable, and controllable multi-turn tool-use environments for reinforcement learning (RL) from coding problems.

**Core Insight**: The fundamental insight guiding CodeGym's design is that code inherently embodies rigorous execution logic, which parallels the structured nature of real-world workflows. This property makes coding problems suitable for constructing rich, interactive tool-use environments.

**Resource Collection**: The raw corpus for CodeGym is derived from competitive programming problems within the KodCode dataset, specifically focusing on Coding Assessment Questions. Each problem includes a task description and a corresponding solution code. An LLM (Seed-1.6-Thinking) is used to standardize the format of these coding solutions.

**CodeGym Generation Pipeline**: This pipeline consists of two stages: Gym Synthesis and Gym Verification.

1. **Gym Synthesis**:
 - **Tool Extraction**: Reusable, atomic code logic or functions are extracted from programming solutions and converted into callable tools. These tools can be standalone functions, calculation utilities, or frequently occurring code fragments. An LLM is prompted to synthesize these tools, generating precise documentation (functionality, parameters, examples). The examples are withheld from the agent during training to encourage interactive learning.
 - **Environment Formalization**: Environments are synthesized in the OpenAI Gym format, defined as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP): $E = \langle S, A, T, R, O \rangle$.
 - **State (S)**: Encodes task-specific variables, partially observable to the agent.
 - **Action Space (A)**: Comprises generic functions (`Observe`, `Done`) and domain-specific tools.
 - **Transitions (T)**: Executed by calling corresponding functions.
 - **Rewards (R)**: Sparse and binary (1 for correct answer, 0 otherwise), assigned only upon task termination.
 - **Observation (O)**: Textual descriptions of action results, including error messages.
 - `Observe` is designed to reveal only partial state information to discourage shortcut solutions. `reset` initializes the environment with a unit test input.
 - This design provides a flexible template, using a one-shot example and repeated instructions, to integrate diverse coding tasks while ensuring consistency and encouraging tool use.

2. **Gym Verification**:
 - This stage addresses correctness errors (compilation failures, timeouts, out-of-memory issues) and solvability errors (insufficient actions for task completion).
 - **Unit Test Generation**: A collection of unit test inputs is synthesized using LLMs, covering various difficulty levels and corner cases. The ground truth coding solution generates corresponding unit test outputs.
 - **Solution Function Generation**: An LLM is prompted to generate candidate "solution functions" that call the environment's tools to solve the task. Only tool documentation and example usages are exposed to prevent state leakage.
 - **Pass@K Strategy**: K=10 candidate solution functions are generated. An environment is deemed solvable if at least one candidate passes all unit tests within specified time and memory limits. These verified unit tests then serve as task configurations for RL training.

**Quality Control**: To ensure high-quality data for RL, two filtering mechanisms are applied to the generated task configurations:

1. **Tool-Use Complexity**: Task configurations are required to exhibit non-trivial tool use. Filters remove configurations with fewer than 10 tool calls or more than 256 tool calls (to avoid trivial or excessively repetitive patterns). Environments must also contain at least 4 distinct tools. These metrics are calculated using oracle functions.
2. **Tool-Use Difficulty**: Tasks are filtered to ensure they are not too easy. Only configurations where Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct achieves an accuracy of no more than 25% across 4 evaluations are retained.
 - After filtering, the dataset comprises over 80,000 task configurations across 13,000 environments, with an average of 6.52 tools and 44.07 steps per task.

**Difficulty Augmentation**: For Long-CoT models, task configurations are augmented to increase the difficulty of solving problems via pure reasoning, thereby encouraging tool interaction. This involves scaling parameter values to larger magnitudes or requiring more intricate logic and function dependencies.

**Training Framework (Distributed RL)**:
- A distributed rollout framework with a CPU-bound environment server supports high-throughput RL training (Figure 5).
- The environment server centralizes control, initializing environments from the CodeGym database and launching them with dedicated service ports.
- GPU-bound rollout workers connect to these ports, transmit tool calls, and receive observations, with responses appended to trajectories.
- A maximum of $T_{max}$ tool calls per trajectory prevents blocking due to repetitive calls.
- The server computes rewards upon rollout completion and returns them to a replay buffer. This decoupling enables stable and highly concurrent RL training.

**Trial-then-Overwrite Mechanism**: To ensure robustness against erroneous or unpredictable tool calls, especially early in training, a "trial-then-overwrite" mechanism is implemented. The server serializes the environment state, executes the tool call in a subprocess, and only commits the resulting state if the call completes successfully within a time limit. Otherwise, the original state is preserved, and an error message is returned.

**RL Algorithm**: The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) variant, GRPO, is applied for training. Models of various sizes (Qwen2.5 series: 7B, 14B, 32B, 72B for short-CoT; QwQ-32B for long-CoT) are trained with a batch size of 512 x 8 until training reward saturation.

**Evaluation**: Models are evaluated on an in-domain validation set (500 unseen CodeGym environments) and held-out (OOD) benchmarks categorized by domain (tool use: τ-bench, τ$^2$-bench), pattern (multi-turn interaction: ALFWorld), and skill (reasoning: ZebraLogic, MMLU-pro).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the CodeGym framework and its impact on LLM agent performance.

**Training Dynamics and In-Domain Performance**:
- All models, regardless of size, exhibited consistent improvement in average reward (equivalent to accuracy, due to binary rewards) during training on the CodeGym dataset. Larger models consistently achieved higher performance and faster reward stabilization.
- Similar performance trends were observed on the in-domain validation set, comprising unseen CodeGym environments, indicating limited overfitting to the training data.
- For the QwQ-32B model, trained on the harder version of CodeGym, strong performance gains were also noted on both training and in-domain validation sets. The average trajectory length initially increased but then declined, potentially due to context window limitations encouraging more concise generation.

**Out-of-Distribution (OOD) Generalization**:
- **Short-CoT Models (Qwen2.5 series)**: Models trained on CodeGym demonstrated consistent performance gains across all evaluated OOD benchmarks: tool-use (τ-bench, τ$^2$-bench), multi-turn interaction (ALFWorld), and reasoning (ZebraLogic, MMLU-Pro). The improvements were more pronounced in tool-use and multi-turn interaction scenarios, aligning with the nature of CodeGym tasks. For instance, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct achieved an absolute accuracy gain of 8.7 points on the OOD benchmark τ-Bench.
- **Impact of Model Size**: Larger models generally benefited more from CodeGym training on OOD benchmarks. For example, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct showed an average improvement of 7.3 points, compared to 2.8 points for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. This observation suggests that larger models may leverage CodeGym training for stronger generalizability rather than memorization.
- **Long-CoT Models (QwQ-32B)**: While these models are heavily tuned for reasoning, CodeGym training resulted in substantial gains in tool-use and multi-turn interaction capabilities. A slight reduction in reasoning performance was observed, indicating a trade-off or the need for combined objectives.

**Tool Call Behavior Analysis**:
- The average number of tool calls made by LLM agents steadily increased during training, suggesting that agents learned to execute longer and more structured procedures. This trend also indicated improved identification and adherence to multi-step workflows, narrowing the gap with oracle solutions.
- The average trajectory length also increased, implying that agents allocated more computational steps for reasoning or interaction to solve tasks.
- An observation was made that the smallest trained model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct) produced the most tool calls. Trajectory inspection revealed this was often due to repetitive failure-recovery loops, where the model reinvoked the same tool with identical arguments after unsatisfactory outputs, highlighting potential limitations in error diagnosis for smaller models.

**Qualitative Analysis**:
- A qualitative comparison of agent trajectories, particularly on ALFWorld tasks, indicated that CodeGym-trained agents developed stronger planning capabilities. They demonstrated an ability to anticipate potential scenarios and perform explicit multi-step planning before executing actions, in contrast to base models that tended to commit to actions with minimal anticipation.

**Ablation Studies**:
- **RL vs. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)**: A comparison with SFT methods (Oracle-SFT and Distillation from a stronger LLM) showed that while SFT approaches achieved reasonable in-domain performance, they exhibited either degradation or limited gains on OOD tasks. This indicates that active learning via RL in CodeGym promotes better generalization compared to passive learning from static demonstrations.
- **Environment Filtering**: Training on the unfiltered CodeGym dataset resulted in worse performance compared to training on the quality-controlled, filtered dataset. This finding underscores the importance of data quality and the effectiveness of the proposed filtering mechanisms in improving both in-domain validation and OOD task performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of CodeGym represents an advancement in the training of large language model (LLM) agents, particularly in the domain of tool-use capabilities. The primary significance of this work lies in its contribution of a scalable, synthetic reinforcement learning environment generation pipeline that addresses critical limitations in current LLM agent training.

**Advancing LLM Agent Generalization**: By converting diverse coding tasks into interactive, verifiable environments, CodeGym enables LLM agents to actively explore and adapt to a wide range of workflows. This approach has been shown to foster significant out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization, as evidenced by consistent performance improvements on unseen tool-use, multi-turn interaction, and even general reasoning benchmarks. This indicates that CodeGym facilitates the learning of transferable interaction strategies, moving beyond the brittleness and task-specific overfitting often associated with supervised fine-tuning.

**Scalable and Verifiable Training Data Generation**: The automated pipeline for generating CodeGym environments from existing coding problems offers a scalable solution to the challenge of acquiring high-quality, diverse agent interaction data. The integrated verification mechanisms ensure the correctness and solvability of the generated environments, providing a robust foundation for RL training. This contrasts with labor-intensive manual environment creation or reliance on static datasets.

**Foundational Work for Robust Agents**: CodeGym provides a foundation for developing more robust LLM agents capable of handling the complexity and diversity inherent in real-world tool-augmented workflows. The findings suggest that training in such structured, interactive environments can cultivate stronger planning capabilities and systematic problem-solving approaches in agents.

**Potential Research Directions**: The work also opens avenues for future research, such as exploring methods to combine reasoning objectives with CodeGym training to achieve complementary benefits in both reasoning accuracy and agent abilities. Further investigation into synthesizing environments with theoretical guarantees that prevent LLMs from exploiting shortcuts could enhance the robustness of agent training. The observed differences in how model sizes leverage CodeGym training also invite further study into the mechanisms of generalization in larger models.

Overall, CodeGym offers a novel framework that can contribute to the development of more adaptable, intelligent, and general-purpose LLM agents, enhancing their utility across various real-world applications requiring complex tool interaction.
