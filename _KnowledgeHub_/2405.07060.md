# Memory-Maze: Scenario Driven Visual Language Navigation Benchmark for Guiding Blind People

> [!summary] Summary
> A novel Visual Language Navigation (VLN) benchmark, Memory-Maze, simulates a robot guiding blind people through complex public environments using instructions recalled from human memory. The benchmark evaluates VLN models against these varied and error-prone memory-based instructions, revealing low performance across state-of-the-art models and underscoring the critical challenge of complex language interpretation for realistic assistive navigation.

## Problem

- Existing Visual Language Navigation (VLN) benchmarks use idealized instructions, which do not reflect the inaccurate, memory-based directions sighted people give to blind individuals in unfamiliar public spaces.
- Current assistive navigation systems for blind people often rely on costly pre-built maps or place a high cognitive load on users, limiting their practical deployment in diverse, unmapped environments.
- VLN models struggle with the inherent ambiguities, omissions, conversational elements, and varied phrasing found in human memory-derived instructions, especially when navigating complex, maze-like public environments without prior navigation graphs.

## Method

- Developed '**Memory-Maze**,' a CARLA-based virtual environment meticulously designed to replicate complex, maze-like public spaces (university, museum buildings) with high visual fidelity and detailed objects.
- Collected two distinct instruction datasets: traditional 'think-out-loud' instructions and novel 'onsite memory-based' instructions from participants verbally recalling routes, capturing real-world human memory errors.
- Implemented a baseline VLN model that utilizes a Large Language Model (GPT-4) for single-inference parsing of natural language into step-by-step plans and direct Python code generation for robot control, enabling zero-shot transfer without navigation graphs.

## Results

- Memory-Maze benchmark analysis showed that memory-based instructions were longer, more diverse, and exhibited significantly higher failure rates (25-**40%** for university routes) compared to online 'think-out-loud' instructions (0-**9%**).
- The proposed LLM-based baseline model generally outperformed state-of-the-art NavGPT and NaVid models across most Memory-Maze routes and metrics (SR, OSR, SPD, CLS), such as achieving an SR of 0.30 for University R1 with online instructions compared to 0.04 (NavGPT) and 0.00 (NaVid).
- All evaluated VLN models, including the proposed baseline, demonstrated suboptimal or low performance across the Memory-Maze benchmark, particularly with memory-based instructions, underscoring the benchmark's difficulty and the need for new approaches to handle complex and erroneous human language.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Memory-based instructions present a significantly greater challenge to VLN models due to their increased length, linguistic diversity, higher error rates (e.g., inaccurate turns, missing details, 'hallucinations'), and conversational elements.
> - The tendency for humans to describe alternative routes, even for the same destination, highlights the necessity for VLN models to be robust to diverse path descriptions rather than adhering to a single ground truth path.
> - The primary challenge for current VLN systems in realistic assistive navigation scenarios is complex language interpretation and robust reasoning under uncertainty, rather than just improvements in low-level perception or control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visual Language Navigation (VLN) involves an agent navigating an environment based on human-provided instructions, leveraging visual perception. This field has seen advancements with robots assisting blind individuals, aiming to enhance their independence. Existing assistive navigation systems often rely on prebuilt maps and infrastructure, which incurs significant costs for acquisition and maintenance, limiting their deployment to familiar or specially prepared environments. Some map-less approaches exist, but they typically impose a high cognitive load on users, requiring them to understand and memorize instructions for manual input.

Current VLN research and benchmarks, such as R2R for indoor settings or Touchdown for outdoor street environments, primarily utilize instructions generated by annotators who describe routes while visually observing the environment (referred to as "thinking out loud"). These instructions tend to be precise and comprehensive. However, this approach does not accurately reflect a real-world scenario where a blind person might ask a sighted passerby for directions in an unfamiliar public space. In such situations, the passerby would describe the route from memory. Memory-based instructions are characteristically less structured, often containing inaccuracies, omissions of details, varied phrasing, and conversational elements (e.g., stutters, estimations, mentions of non-existent landmarks).

The Memory-Maze benchmark addresses these limitations by specifically focusing on the challenges posed by memory-based instructions in complex, maze-like public environments. It aims to bridge the gap between idealized VLN tasks and the practical demands of assistive navigation for blind people, where instructions are inherently imperfect and the environment may lack prior mapping or navigation graphs. This work introduces a benchmark that combines the complexity of real-world public spaces with the variability of human memory-derived language, thus pushing the boundaries of VLN research toward more ecologically valid applications.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce "Memory-Maze," a novel benchmark designed to simulate a realistic scenario for Visual Language Navigation (VLN) in the context of guiding blind people. This benchmark specifically targets situations where a robot-assisted blind individual seeks route instructions from a sighted passerby who must recall the route from memory.

The motivation for this work stems from several limitations observed in existing VLN research and its applicability to real-world assistive navigation:

- **Real-world Scenario Gap**: Current VLN benchmarks typically rely on instructions generated by annotators who observe a virtual environment concurrently ("thinking out loud"). This does not reflect the common scenario where blind individuals solicit directions from sighted people in unfamiliar buildings. Such passersby would describe routes based on their recollection, which is fundamentally different from a real-time, observation-driven description.
- **Characteristics of Memory-Based Instructions**: Instructions derived from human memory are prone to specific types of errors and variations. These include inaccurate distance estimations, "hallucinations" of landmarks that do not exist or are misplaced, omissions of crucial turning points, stutters, and more conversational, less precise phrasing. Existing VLN models, trained on more structured instruction sets, are not adequately equipped to handle these complexities.
- **Environment Complexity**: Many existing VLN tasks are set in relatively simple environments like static houses or roadways. However, blind individuals often need to navigate large, complex public spaces such as shopping malls or university hallways. These environments are characterized by numerous physical turning points and intersections, resembling a maze, and may also feature both static and dynamic obstacles. Current benchmarks often lack this specific environmental complexity or rely on pre-built navigation graphs, which may not be available in unseen public spaces.
- **Practical Deployment Requirements**: For assistive navigation robots to be practically useful for blind people, they need to function in previously unseen environments without extensive prior mapping (e.g., navigation graphs). Furthermore, navigation should be responsive and efficient, ideally avoiding frequent stops or prolonged inference times that iterative decision-making processes can introduce.

To address these motivations, the key objectives for Memory-Maze are:
1. **Construct a Realistic Virtual Environment**: Develop maze-like virtual environments based on real-world public spaces using the CARLA simulator to enable high-fidelity sensor data simulation.
2. **Collect Novel Instruction Data**: Gather two distinct sets of natural language instructions: one through a traditional "think-out-loud" online method and another through an "onsite memory-based" method, specifically designed to capture the characteristics of human memory.
3. **Analyze Instruction Characteristics**: Compare and contrast the collected instruction sets to highlight the unique challenges posed by memory-based instructions, such as length variation, word diversity, error rates, and the presence of alternative routes.
4. **Develop a Suitable Baseline Model**: Implement a VLN baseline model that emphasizes modular perception and control, zero-shot transferability to unseen environments, and single-inference navigation to interpret instructions without relying on navigation graphs or iterative decision-making.
5. **Evaluate Current VLN Models**: Assess the performance of state-of-the-art VLN models, including the proposed baseline, against the Memory-Maze benchmark to demonstrate the difficulty presented by memory-based instructions and the need for new approaches.

Ultimately, the motivation is to advance VLN technology to enable robots to autonomously guide blind users in diverse, unfamiliar settings by effectively interpreting the varied and often imperfect instructions provided by sighted passersby from memory.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology employed in this research involved the construction of a novel benchmark environment, the development of a control program for a simulated robot, a detailed process for collecting varied natural language instructions, and the implementation of a baseline VLN model for evaluation.

**Benchmark Environment: Memory-Maze**
The virtual environment for Memory-Maze was built using the CARLA simulator, chosen for its flexibility, compatibility with Unreal Engine, and ability to simulate diverse sensor data (RGB cameras, depth sensors, LiDAR) and obstacles. The environments were meticulously designed to replicate real-world public spaces: one floor of a university building and two floors of a museum building. These locations were selected for their maze-like characteristics, featuring numerous turning points, intersections, and common objects (e.g., doors, chairs, statues) along the routes. The 3D models were created using Fusion 360 and imported into CARLA, ensuring visual fidelity and accurate floor layouts.

**Robot Control Program Implementation**
A control program for the robot in the CARLA simulator was developed using its Python API. This program provided modular control functions for the baseline VLN model, decoupling low-level navigation from language interpretation. Key functions included:
- `move_forward(distance)`: Enables the robot to move straight while maintaining a central path within corridors using PID control, based on pre-defined corridor corner coordinates to avoid collisions.
- `detect_turning_point`: Identifies pre-annotated turning point areas and returns available navigation directions to the robot.
- `turn(direction)`: Executes a turn command once the robot is positioned at a detected turning point.
- `detect_from_RGB_image(object)`: This function integrates an open-vocabulary object detection model, Grounding DINO, which uses the object's name as a query to identify and output bounding boxes from the robot's ego-centric RGB camera feed. It also utilizes CARLA's depth sensors to filter objects beyond four meters, focusing on nearby landmarks, and employs tracking algorithms to prevent recounting the same object. The assumption for these functions is that component algorithm development for perception and control is beyond the study's scope, thus, ideal inputs (e.g., pre-annotated coordinates) are used to focus on language interpretation.

**Instruction Data Collection**
Two distinct studies were conducted to gather natural language instruction data for six routes across the three virtual environments:
- **Study 1: Online Think-Out-Loud Instruction**: 78 participants, unfamiliar with the routes, completed an online questionnaire. They were presented with a scenario involving a blind person and a navigation robot and watched 360° video walkthroughs of two routes. Participants were allowed to re-watch videos and type instructions. A total of 312 instructions were collected, then translated from Japanese to English using GPT-4. This method mimics traditional VLN instruction collection.
- **Study 2: Onsite Memory-Based Instruction**: This in-person study aimed to collect data reflecting real-world memory-based instructions. Participants (40 for university routes, 43 for museum routes), all confirmed to be familiar with the respective environments, were asked by an experimenter (role-playing a blind individual) to verbally describe routes from memory, without video access or prior route experience during the study. For both studies, instructions were collected in two iterations for each route: a first natural description, and a second refined description after providing information about the robot's capabilities and pointing out initial errors. This second iteration aimed to gather more accurate memory-based instructions. A total of 80 university instructions and 172 museum instructions were collected.

**Baseline VLN Model Implementation (Proposed Method)**
The proposed baseline VLN model was designed with two key requirements for practical deployment: zero-shot transfer to unseen environments without navigation graphs, and single-inference operation for faster navigation. It leverages a Large Language Model (LLM), specifically GPT-4 (gpt-4-1106-preview).
- **Parsing Instruction**: The system first uses the LLM to parse raw natural language instructions into a step-by-step, itemized format. The LLM is guided by a prompt with rules (e.g., turn points, objects to detect) and examples. A two-stage prompting method (initial thought, refinement with second thought) is used for accuracy.
- **Navigation Code Generation**: The parsed instructions, along with a detailed API specification (commands, docstrings, usage notes), are fed to the LLM. The LLM then generates Python code that directly calls the robot control functions. For `detect_from_RGB_image(object)`, the LLM dynamically generates the `object` argument (e.g., "red chair"). This generated code is then executed using the API implementation. This single-inference, code-generation approach allows flexible integration of low-level planning algorithms and avoids iterative inference.

**State-of-the-Art Baselines**
The proposed model was compared against two state-of-the-art VLN models:
- **NavGPT**: This model uses an LLM (GPT-4o-mini), a visual foundation model, and an object detector (Grounding DINO) to iteratively select nodes within a pre-constructed navigation graph. Navigation graphs for the Memory-Maze environments were manually created for its evaluation.
- **NaVid**: A VLN model designed for strong generalization to unseen environments, operating without a navigation graph and relying solely on camera input. It employs a visual foundation model (Vicuna-7B) and iteratively predicts low-level actions.

**Metrics**
Performance was evaluated using standard VLN metrics: Success Rate (SR), Oracle Success Rate (OSR), Shortest Path Distance (SPD), and Coverage weighted by Length Score (CLS). For CLS, dense navigation graphs were created by dividing passable corridors into 50 cm grids, allowing for mapping and comparison of predicted and ground truth paths. For routes where participants described alternative paths, the described path was considered the ground truth.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The analysis of the Memory-Maze benchmark and the evaluation of VLN models yielded several findings regarding the nature of memory-based instructions and the challenges they pose for robotic navigation.

**Benchmark Analysis and Statistics**
- **Instruction Length and Variation**: As presented in Table I, onsite memory-based instructions were generally longer and exhibited greater variability in length (higher standard deviation) compared to online "think-out-loud" instructions. This tendency was particularly pronounced in the second iteration of onsite data, where participants provided more detailed, albeit memory-reliant, descriptions. The increased length and variation are attributed to participants' attempts to be highly descriptive, including conversational elements (e.g., "I'm not 100% sure about this"), sensory cues (audio, olfactory), and stutters when recalling from memory.
- **Word Diversity**: Word clouds (Fig. 3) and quantitative analysis revealed that onsite memory-based instructions contained a higher number of unique words compared to online instructions (e.g., 521 vs. 381 for university, 611 vs. 586 for museum). This indicates a richer and more diverse vocabulary used when describing routes from memory, including unique phrases such as "uh," "maybe," and "okay," which are characteristic of spontaneous recall.
- **Failure Rates**: Manual analysis of instructions showed that onsite memory-based instructions had significantly higher failure rates (e.g., 25-40% for University routes) compared to online instructions (0-9%). These failures were due to common human memory errors such as incorrect turning directions, inaccurate turning points, leading to wrong destinations, missing essential turn information, and inaccurate environmental details.
- **Alternative Routes**: A notable observation in the onsite study was the high rate of participants describing alternative routes (Alternative Rate, AR in Table I) different from the anticipated ground truth. For instance, Museum 7F R2 showed an AR of 85.7% and 90.1% in the two iterations. This highlights the natural variability in human route descriptions and the necessity for VLN models to be robust to such deviations.
- **Complexity of Language**: The benchmark demonstrated that even geometrically simple routes were accompanied by complex natural language. Instructions often required a combined understanding of language and building structure, such as "go along this path and turn right in the first intersection," which implicitly skipped an immediate right turn due to the corridor's topology. This variation in topological detail poses a significant challenge.

**Performance of VLN Models**
- **Proposed Method vs. Baselines**: As shown in Table II, the proposed baseline model generally outperformed the state-of-the-art NavGPT and NaVid models across most routes and metrics (SR, OSR, SPD, CLS). For example, for University R1 with online instructions, the proposed method achieved an SR of 0.30, while NavGPT and NaVid were 0.04 and 0.00 respectively.
- **Baselines' Limitations**: NavGPT and NaVid exhibited suboptimal or very low performance. Their iterative decision-making process made them susceptible to errors; a single mistake could lead to unrecoverable deviations or premature termination. NaVid, in particular, struggled with longer sequences of turns and instructions in Memory-Maze, often making frequent, unnecessary turns.
- **Proposed Method's Advantages**: The proposed method's single-inference code generation strategy allowed it to process an entire navigation plan at once, bypassing issues related to iterative decision-making and prolonged navigation times. The instruction parsing module was also observed to generally boost the model's performance by structuring the complex natural language.

**Difficulty of the Benchmark**
- **Overall Low Performance**: Despite the proposed model's relative advantage, all evaluated models, including the baseline, demonstrated suboptimal or low performance across the benchmark. This indicates the inherent difficulty of Memory-Maze.
- **Memory-Based Instruction Challenge**: Performance on onsite memory-based instructions tended to be lower than on online "think-out-loud" instructions (Table II). This reinforces that the errors and ambiguities inherent in human memory make these instructions significantly harder for VLN models to process.
- **Language Interpretation as Primary Challenge**: The modular design of the proposed baseline (idealized perception and control) suggests that the primary challenge in Memory-Maze lies not in low-level perception or control, but in the complex interpretation and reasoning required for varied and inaccurate language instructions. The models struggled with instructions requiring nuanced understanding of spatial relationships and implicit topological details.
- **Human-like Error Correction**: The research also highlighted that humans can often infer intended destinations even from incorrect instructions (e.g., a non-existent corridor), a capability currently lacking in VLN models.

**Effect of Refining Instruction**
- **General Improvement**: Table III indicates that refining instructions (second iteration) generally led to a slight improvement in the performance of the proposed method. This suggests that assisting sighted passersby in recalling and articulating routes more clearly could benefit VLN-equipped robots.
- **Increased Complexity**: However, in some instances (e.g., Museum 5F R1, University R1), performance decreased with refined instructions. This was attributed to participants adding more descriptive, sometimes redundant, information or mentioning objects to ignore, which inadvertently increased the linguistic complexity for the LLM-based model.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Memory-Maze benchmark represents a significant contribution to the field of Visual Language Navigation (VLN) by addressing a previously underexplored, yet critical, real-world scenario: guiding blind people using instructions recalled from human memory in complex public environments.

**Key Significance:**
- **Enhanced Realism for Assistive Robotics**: Memory-Maze moves VLN research closer to practical applications in assistive robotics by simulating the natural interaction between blind individuals and sighted passersby. This shift from idealized, "think-out-loud" instructions to error-prone, memory-based instructions is crucial for developing robust navigation systems for blind people.
- **Identification of New Research Challenges**: The benchmark explicitly highlights the limitations of current state-of-the-art VLN models when confronted with the ambiguities, inaccuracies, and diverse linguistic styles inherent in human memory. The consistently low performance of even advanced models underscores that language interpretation and robust reasoning under uncertainty are major unresolved challenges, rather than merely improving perception or low-level control.
- **Focus on Linguistic and Cognitive Aspects**: By modularizing perception and control components in its baseline, the research directs attention to the cognitive load on VLN models in interpreting complex, often contradictory, human instructions. It emphasizes the need for models capable of understanding implicit spatial reasoning, handling varying levels of topological detail, and even inferring intent from erroneous information, similar to how humans compensate for imperfect directions.
- **Inspiration for Novel VLN Architectures**: The findings suggest that future VLN models may need to move beyond strictly defined navigation graphs or sequential action predictions. Instead, more adaptive map representations, flexible approaches to topological descriptions, and architectures that can perform single-inference, long-horizon planning are indicated.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancing Assistive Technologies**: The successful development of VLN models capable of mastering Memory-Maze could lead to autonomous navigation robots that significantly enhance the independence of blind individuals in unfamiliar public spaces, reducing reliance on costly pre-built infrastructure or high cognitive load on the user.
- **Driving Innovation in Human-Robot Interaction**: The benchmark implicitly calls for more sophisticated human-robot interaction paradigms. Future work could explore interactive dialogues where robots clarify ambiguous instructions or provide feedback to passersby to elicit more precise directions, mimicking human communication strategies.
- **Broader VLN Applications**: The challenges identified, such as interpreting vague language, handling errors, and adapting to diverse phrasing, are not unique to assistive navigation. Solutions developed for Memory-Maze could generalize to other VLN applications where human-provided instructions are common, such as in logistics, search and rescue, or domestic robotics.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: Memory-Maze provides a concrete environment and dataset for researchers to develop and evaluate new algorithms, particularly those leveraging large language models and visual foundation models, towards more robust and generalizable VLN capabilities.

While the current size of the benchmark is acknowledged as a limitation for large-scale training, the framework and collected data serve as a critical foundation for qualitative analysis and for inspiring future data augmentation strategies, potentially through sophisticated LLM-based generation, to scale up the dataset while preserving its realistic characteristics.
