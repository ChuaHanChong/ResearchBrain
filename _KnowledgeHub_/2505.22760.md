# Non-convex entropic mean-field optimization via Best Response flow

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from RIKEN AIP and Heriot-Watt University analyze the convergence properties of the Best Response flow for entropy-regularized optimization and min-max games with non-convex functionals over probability measures. Their work demonstrates that exponential convergence to a unique global minimizer or Mixed Nash Equilibrium in L1-Wasserstein distance can be achieved under specific conditions linking the regularization parameter to the degree of non-convexity and the reference measure's properties.

## Problem

- Many practical machine learning objectives, such as in neural network training or RL policy optimization, are inherently non-convex, rendering traditional theoretical guarantees based on convexity inapplicable.
- Previous mean-field optimization and game theory analyses for entropy-regularized problems largely assumed convexity or convex-concavity of the functional, limiting applicability to broader non-convex scenarios.
- A clear quantitative understanding of how entropic regularization strength interacts with non-convexity and reference measure properties was needed to guarantee convergence in non-convex mean-field settings.

## Method

- The research applies the continuous-time ==Best Response flow== to entropy-regularized optimization problems and two-player min-max games involving non-convex or non-convex-non-concave functionals over probability measures.
- It rigorously proves that the Best Response operator is an L1-Wasserstein contraction, guaranteeing a unique fixed point, under explicit quantitative conditions on the regularization parameter and functional properties.
- The framework is directly applied to policy optimization in single-agent Markov Decision Processes and two-player zero-sum Markov games with softmax-parametrized policies, which introduce non-convexities.

## Results

- For single-agent non-convex optimization, the Best Response operator is proven to be an L1-Wasserstein contraction, leading to a unique global minimizer and exponential convergence of the flow if the regularization parameter exceeds a derived lower bound.
- For two-player non-convex-non-concave min-max games, the joint Best Response operator is shown to be an L1-Wasserstein contraction, ensuring a unique global Mixed Nash Equilibrium and exponential convergence of the coupled flow under specific regularization and learning rate conditions.
- The Best Response flow's stability is demonstrated for both optimization and min-max problems, showing that its trajectory and fixed points are robust to variations in initial conditions and regularization parameters.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The Best Response flow can achieve exponential convergence to unique global solutions in non-convex entropy-regularized problems, provided the entropic regularization is sufficiently strong relative to the functional's non-convexity.
> - Explicit formulas are derived that precisely quantify the required relationship between the regularization parameter, constants characterizing the functional's non-convexity (e.g., Lipschitz continuity of the flat derivative), and the first moment of the reference measure.
> - The Best Response flow offers an alternative to methods like the Wasserstein gradient flow, as it achieves similar exponential convergence rates under weaker regularity assumptions on the functional and the potential of the reference measure.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research contributes to the field of mean-field optimization, specifically focusing on problems formulated over the space of probability measures, where functions are regularized by relative entropy (Kullback-Leibler divergence). This area has seen increasing attention due to its relevance in machine learning applications, including the training of two-layer neural networks in the mean-field regime, policy optimization in Reinforcement Learning (RL), Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), and Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL).

Previous work in this domain primarily focused on scenarios where the functional to be minimized, or the payoff function in a min-max game, was assumed to be convex or convex-concave. Methods commonly employed for these problems include the Best Response/fictitious play flow, Wasserstein gradient flow, and Fisher-Rao gradient flow. For convex functionals, convergence of these flows to a minimizer typically holds for arbitrary positive regularization parameters and reference measures.

This paper extends this established landscape by relaxing the fundamental assumption of convexity for single-agent optimization problems and convexity-concavity for two-player min-max games. It demonstrates that convergence of the Best Response flow can still be achieved in these more general non-convex settings, provided that specific conditions on the regularization parameter, the degree of non-convexity, and the tail behavior of the reference measure are met. This represents a generalization of existing results, such as those by Chen et al. (2023) for convex mean-field optimization and Lascu et al. (2025) for convex-concave mean-field min-max problems, by precisely characterizing the trade-offs involved when moving from convex to non-convex landscapes. It also offers an alternative approach to other methods, such as the Wasserstein gradient flow studied by Leahy et al. (2022) for entropy-regularized MDPs, by proposing the Best Response flow under potentially weaker regularity assumptions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to analyze the convergence properties of the Best Response flow (also known as fictitious play flow) when applied to entropy-regularized optimization problems and min-max games involving non-convex or non-convex-non-concave functionals defined on the space of probability measures.

The motivation for this work stems from several challenges and opportunities in modern machine learning:

1. **Relaxing Convexity Assumptions**: Many theoretical analyses in mean-field optimization and game theory rely on strong assumptions of convexity or convex-concavity. However, many practical machine learning problems, particularly those involving neural network parametrizations or complex policy spaces in RL, inherently lead to non-convex objective functions. For instance, in policy optimization for Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) with softmax parametrized policies, the resulting objective function is non-convex due to the normalization constant in the policy definition. This makes standard theoretical guarantees based on convexity inapplicable. The paper aims to bridge this gap by establishing conditions under which convergence can still be guaranteed in these non-convex settings.

2. **Characterizing Regularization for Non-Convexity**: For convex problems, any positive entropic regularization parameter `σ` generally ensures convergence (though the rate may vary). For non-convex problems, a key motivation is to precisely understand how `σ` needs to be chosen in relation to the degree of non-convexity of the functional and the properties of the reference measure. The paper seeks to derive explicit formulas that quantify this relationship, thereby providing practical guidance for algorithm design. This includes understanding how the tail behavior of the reference measure `ξ` (e.g., its first moment) influences the required strength of regularization.

3. **Application to Reinforcement Learning**: Policy optimization in RL, particularly in the mean-field regime with neural network-based policies, is a significant application area. For both single-agent MDPs and two-player zero-sum Markov games, softmax-parametrized policies often result in non-convex or non-convex-non-concave value functions. This work is motivated by the need for theoretically robust methods that can guarantee convergence to optimal policies or Nash equilibria in such settings. The paper explicitly demonstrates how its theoretical results apply to these RL contexts, offering an alternative to existing methods like the Wasserstein gradient flow.

4. **Extending Best Response Flow**: The Best Response flow has been successfully applied to convex optimization and convex-concave games. A core motivation is to rigorously extend its theoretical analysis to non-convex and non-convex-non-concave scenarios. This involves studying how its convergence properties (existence and uniqueness of fixed points, exponential convergence rates, stability) are affected by the generalized problem structure. The paper also aims to relax other constraints present in prior work, such as uniform bounds on second-order derivatives or identical learning rates/regularization parameters for all players in min-max games.

In summary, the work is motivated by the prevalence of non-convex problems in modern machine learning and the need for theoretical guarantees for algorithms that address them. It focuses on the Best Response flow as a robust candidate and aims to precisely define the conditions under which it maintains desirable convergence properties despite the absence of global convexity.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employs a rigorous mathematical framework from optimal transport, functional analysis, and stochastic calculus to analyze the continuous-time Best Response flow. The methodology can be broken down into several key steps and tools:

1. **Problem Formulation**:
- **Single-agent optimization**: The core problem is minimizing an entropy-regularized functional `F_σ(ν) = F(ν) + σ KL(ν|ξ)` over the space of probability measures `P_1(R^d)`, where `F` is a non-convex functional, `KL(ν|ξ)` is the relative entropy with respect to a fixed reference measure `ξ`, and `σ > 0` is a regularization parameter.
- **Min-max problems**: The framework is extended to two-player zero-sum games, `min_ν max_μ F_σ_ν,σ_μ(ν, μ) = F(ν, μ) + σ_ν KL(ν|ξ) - σ_μ KL(μ|ρ)`, where `F` is non-convex-non-concave, and `σ_ν, σ_μ` are regularization parameters for each player.

2. **Best Response Flow Dynamics**:
- The central algorithmic tool is the continuous-time Best Response flow. For single-agent optimization, this is given by `dν_t = α (Ψ_σ[ν_t] - ν_t) dt`, where `α > 0` is a learning rate and `Ψ_σ` is the Best Response operator.
- For min-max problems, coupled Best Response flows are considered: `dν_t = α_ν (Ψ_σ_ν(ν_t, μ_t) - ν_t) dt` and `dμ_t = α_μ (Φ_σ_μ(ν_t, μ_t) - μ_t) dt`, with distinct learning rates `α_ν, α_μ` and Best Response operators `Ψ_σ_ν`, `Φ_σ_μ`.
- The Best Response operator `Ψ_σ[ν](dx) ∝ exp(-1/σ δF/δν(ν, x)) ξ(dx)` is defined via the flat derivative `δF/δν`, which is a concept of differentiation for functionals on probability spaces (Definition F.1). It represents the unique minimizer of an entropy-regularized linearization of the functional `F` at a given measure `ν`.

3. **Key Assumptions**:
- **Regularity of `F`**: `F` is assumed to be `C^1` (flat differentiable) and its flat derivative `δF/δν` is assumed to be Lipschitz continuous and uniformly bounded (Assumptions 2.1 and 3.1). These assumptions quantify the degree of non-convexity of the functional.
- **Reference Measure `ξ`**: The reference measure `ξ` is assumed to have a density `e^(-U_ξ(x))` with respect to Lebesgue measure, where `U_ξ` is bounded below and has at least linear growth (Assumptions 2.2 and 3.2). This ensures that the first moment of `ξ` is finite, which is crucial for `L_1`-Wasserstein distance analysis.

4. **Analytical Strategy**:
- **Contraction Mapping Principle**: The core of the methodology is to establish that the Best Response operator `Ψ_σ` (or the joint operator `(Ψ_σ_ν, Φ_σ_μ)`) is a contraction mapping with respect to the `L_1`-Wasserstein distance (`W_1`) under certain conditions. This is a generalization of results from convex settings. The `W_1`-Lipschitz constant of the Best Response operator is explicitly derived.
- **Fixed-Point Existence and Uniqueness**: By demonstrating contractivity, Banach's fixed-point theorem is applied. This guarantees the existence of a unique fixed point for the Best Response operator. Crucially, it is shown that any minimizer of the entropy-regularized functional (or MNE of the game) must be a fixed point of the Best Response operator. Thus, the unique fixed point is the unique global minimizer/MNE.
- **Convergence and Stability Analysis**: For the continuous-time flow, existence and uniqueness of solutions are established using Picard iteration (Proposition C.2, C.5). The stability of the flow with respect to initial conditions and regularization parameters is then analyzed using Gronwall's lemma. This leads to deriving exponential convergence rates of the Best Response flow to the unique minimizer/MNE in `L_1`-Wasserstein distance.

5. **Application to Reinforcement Learning**:
- The general framework is applied to policy optimization in MDPs (Section 2.2) and two-player zero-sum Markov games (Section E.2) with softmax parametrized policies.
- It is explicitly shown that the value function `F(ν) := V^π_ν,τ(γ)` in this context satisfies Assumption 2.1, with explicit expressions for constants `C_F` and `L_F` derived (Proposition E.3). This allows the general theoretical results to be directly applied to these practical RL problems.

6. **Numerical Considerations (Brief)**: A Langevin-based particle algorithm (Algorithm 1) is briefly described for numerical simulation in the bandit problem setting, outlining how the continuous-time flow might be approximated in discrete time. However, a rigorous analysis of this discrete scheme is left for future work.

The methodology relies on meticulous analytical derivations to quantify how non-convexity, regularization strength, and properties of the reference measure interact to ensure the desirable contraction property of the Best Response operator, which underpins the convergence guarantees.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several key findings across single-agent optimization and two-player min-max problems, extending the theoretical understanding of Best Response flow in non-convex settings:

#### 4.1. Single-Agent Optimization

1. **L1-Wasserstein Contraction of Best Response Operator (Theorem 2.3)**:
- The Best Response operator `Ψ_σ` is shown to be Lipschitz continuous with respect to the `L_1`-Wasserstein distance `W_1` for any `σ > 0`.
- Crucially, it is proven to be a contraction mapping (Lipschitz constant less than 1) if the regularization parameter `σ` is sufficiently large. The condition for contractivity is precisely given by formula (10): `σ > 2C_F + e(e + 1)L_F ∫_R^d |x|e^(-U_ξ(x)) dx`. This formula quantifies the relationship between the degree of non-convexity (`C_F`, `L_F`), the regularization parameter `σ`, and the first moment of the reference measure `ξ`.
- The constants `C_F` and `L_F` measure the uniform boundedness and Lipschitz continuity of the flat derivative of the non-convex functional `F`.
- This finding indicates that a stronger regularization `σ` is required for more non-convex functionals (larger `C_F`, `L_F`) or for reference measures `ξ` with heavier tails (larger `∫ |x|e^(-U_ξ(x)) dx`).

2. **Existence and Uniqueness of Global Minimizer (Corollary 2.4)**:
- As a direct consequence of the Best Response operator being an `L_1`-Wasserstein contraction, and given that any minimizer of the entropy-regularized functional `F_σ` is a fixed point of `Ψ_σ`, it is established that the problem (1) possesses a unique global minimizer under condition (10). This extends results from convex settings to a class of non-convex problems.

3. **Stability of the Flow (Theorem 2.5)**:
- The Best Response flow (4) is shown to exhibit stability with respect to variations in its initial condition `ν_0` and the regularization parameter `σ`. This means that small changes in initial conditions or `σ` lead to commensurately small changes in the flow's trajectory and its fixed point.

4. **Exponential Convergence of the Best Response Flow (Theorem 2.6)**:
- Under condition (10), the Best Response flow (4) is proven to converge exponentially to the unique global minimizer `ν_σ^*` in `L_1`-Wasserstein distance. The explicit convergence rate is `O(e^(-αt(1-L_Ψ_σ,U_ξ)))`, where `α` is the learning rate and `L_Ψ_σ,U_ξ` is the Lipschitz constant of `Ψ_σ`. This provides a concrete convergence rate, which was not explicitly established in previous work by Chen et al. (2023) in `L_1`-Wasserstein.

5. **Comparison to Wasserstein Gradient Flow (Section 2.3)**:
- The paper compares its results to those obtained using the Wasserstein gradient flow in related MDP problems (Leahy et al., 2022). It is highlighted that the Best Response flow achieves similar exponential convergence rates under significantly weaker regularity assumptions on the functional `F` and the potential `U_ξ` of the reference measure. Specifically, `U_ξ` only requires to be bounded below and have linear growth, rather than the strong convexity and Lipschitz gradient conditions often required for Wasserstein gradient flow.

#### 4.2. Min-Max Problems

1. **L1-Wasserstein Contraction of Joint Best Response Operator (Theorem B.2)**:
- For the two-player min-max problem (6), the joint Best Response operator `(Ψ_σ_ν, Φ_σ_μ)` is shown to be an `L_1`-Wasserstein contraction if `σ_ν` and `σ_μ` are sufficiently large. Conditions (29) and (30) define these lower bounds, which are analogous to the single-agent case but incorporate the min-max structure and parameters for both players.

2. **Existence and Uniqueness of Global Mixed Nash Equilibrium (MNE) (Corollary B.3)**:
- Under conditions (29) and (30), the min-max game (6) is guaranteed to have a unique global MNE. This is a direct consequence of the joint Best Response operator being a contraction mapping.

3. **Stability of the Min-Max Flow (Theorem B.4)**:
- The coupled Best Response flow (8) for min-max problems demonstrates stability with respect to initial conditions and the regularization parameters `σ_ν, σ_μ`. The derived stability bound depends on the regularization parameters, the learning rates `α_ν, α_μ`, and the Lipschitz constants of the Best Response operators.

4. **Exponential Convergence of Min-Max Best Response Flow (Theorem 3.3)**:
- Under specific conditions (41) and (42) which depend on the learning rates `α_ν, α_μ` as well as regularization parameters, the coupled Best Response flow (8) converges exponentially to the unique global MNE in `L_1`-Wasserstein distance.
- This generalizes previous work by Lascu et al. (2025) by removing the convexity-concavity assumption on `F`, lifting requirements for uniformly bounded second-order flat derivatives, and allowing for distinct regularization parameters (`σ_ν, σ_μ`) and learning rates (`α_ν, α_μ`) for each player. It also explicitly characterizes convergence in `L_1`-Wasserstein distance.

In summary, the paper systematically extends the theoretical foundations of Best Response flows to non-convex settings, providing precise quantitative conditions for convergence, existence, and uniqueness of solutions in both optimization and game theory contexts.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries significant theoretical and practical implications, particularly for machine learning and optimization fields:

1. **Advancement in Mean-Field Theory for Non-Convex Problems**: The most significant contribution is the extension of mean-field optimization theory beyond the restrictive assumptions of convexity or convex-concavity. By providing precise conditions under which the Best Response flow converges for non-convex functionals, the work broadens the applicability of these analytical tools to a wider range of realistic problems in machine learning. This represents a foundational step towards understanding and developing provably convergent algorithms in complex, non-convex landscapes.

2. **Robustness of Best Response Flow**: The findings underscore the robustness of the Best Response flow as an optimization and game-solving algorithm. Its demonstrated exponential convergence in `L_1`-Wasserstein distance, even under non-convexity, suggests it could be a valuable alternative to other gradient-based methods, particularly when high-order regularity assumptions are not met.

3. **Guidance for Algorithm Design and Parameter Selection**: The derived quantitative conditions, such as formula (10) and (29)-(30), offer concrete guidance for practitioners. They specify how the regularization parameter `σ` (or `σ_ν`, `σ_μ`) should be chosen in relation to the estimated "degree of non-convexity" of the objective function and the properties (e.g., tail behavior) of the chosen reference measure `ξ`. This can help in designing more stable and effective learning algorithms for mean-field models. For example, by selecting reference measures `ξ` with lighter tails, it may be possible to use smaller regularization parameters `σ` while still guaranteeing convergence.

4. **Applications in Reinforcement Learning**: The direct applicability of the results to policy optimization in both single-agent MDPs and two-player zero-sum Markov games with softmax parametrized policies is a key impact. These settings inherently introduce non-convexities, and the paper provides a theoretical basis for global convergence for such policy optimization approaches, offering an alternative method for achieving optimal policies or Nash equilibria. This can lead to the development of new, theoretically grounded RL algorithms that are less susceptible to local optima.

5. **Benchmarking and Future Research**: The explicit exponential convergence rates established in the `L_1`-Wasserstein distance provide a benchmark for evaluating the performance of numerical implementations and inspire further theoretical work on convergence rates under different metrics or weaker conditions. The brief discussion of the Langevin-based numerical scheme also points to an important avenue for future research: bridging the gap between continuous-time theoretical guarantees and practical discrete-time algorithms, particularly regarding the interplay of learning rates, step sizes, and regularization parameters.

6. **Comparative Advantage**: The comparison with the Wasserstein gradient flow highlights that the Best Response flow may offer an advantage in scenarios where strong convexity of the potential function `U_ξ` or uniform bounds on the Hessian of `F` are difficult to satisfy, making it a viable alternative under weaker assumptions.

Overall, this research deepens the understanding of non-convex optimization and game theory in mean-field settings, offering both theoretical breakthroughs and practical implications for developing robust and efficient machine learning algorithms.
