# Learning to Balance Motor Thermal Safety and Quadrupedal Locomotion Performance with Residual Policy

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Huazhong University of Science and Technology developed a two-stage reinforcement learning framework that uses a thermal-aware residual policy to balance quadrupedal locomotion performance with motor thermal safety. The approach enabled a Unitree A1 robot to traverse a 650m outdoor path with a 3kg payload in 13 minutes, maintaining peak motor temperatures below 50°C, and reducing overheating rates to under 10% in high-temperature scenarios.

## Problem

- Electrically-actuated legged robots face performance limitations and potential hardware damage due to motor overheating during sustained, high-load operations.
- Current reinforcement learning policies for locomotion often neglect motor thermal dynamics, assuming actuators can function reliably across various thermal states.
- Attempting to optimize both locomotion performance and thermal safety within a single policy typically results in conservative behaviors, sacrificing agility and terrain adaptability.

## Method

- A two-stage ==reinforcement learning== framework is proposed, pre-training a nominal policy for baseline locomotion and then training a thermal-aware residual policy.
- A whole-body lumped-parameter thermal model is integrated into the training pipeline to provide real-time motor temperature feedback.
- Adaptive reward functions guide the residual policy to make minimal corrections at low temperatures and progressively increase interventions to prevent overheating.

## Results

- The framework significantly reduced motor overheating rates to less than **10%** in high-temperature simulated terrain traversal, down from **70%** in policies without thermal management.
- A physical Unitree A1 robot successfully completed a 650m outdoor path with a 3kg payload in approximately 13 minutes, maintaining peak motor temperatures below 50°C throughout.
- The residual policy preserved high locomotion performance and terrain adaptability, unlike monolithic thermal policies which exhibited slow responses and failed to traverse complex terrains.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A hierarchical control structure, using a residual policy to modulate a nominal locomotion policy, effectively reconciles the conflicting objectives of high performance and thermal safety.
> - Integrating a real-time, whole-body thermal model into the RL training enables the policy to learn adaptive behaviors that respond to the robot's long-term physical state.
> - The residual policy can learn sophisticated, adaptive strategies, such as "drifting" on complex terrains, to mitigate heat buildup without entirely sacrificing traversal capability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of legged robotics has seen significant advancements, particularly with the widespread adoption of reinforcement learning (RL) for achieving high-performance locomotion. Electrically-actuated legged robots are increasingly deployed in applications involving heavy loads and complex environments. Current RL-based policies, however, often operate under the implicit assumption that actuators can reliably function across various thermal states. This assumption presents a limitation in practical, long-duration tasks, where sustained high current output leads to heat accumulation in motors. Motor overheating can trigger low-level protective mechanisms, affecting subsequent robot locomotion, or, in severe cases, cause permanent hardware damage. Consequently, actuator thermal constraints have become a bottleneck for consistent high-performance robot operation.

Previous research has explored integrating temperature-aware regularization into RL training to encourage thermally safe locomotion, primarily on relatively flat terrains. These approaches, such as those discussed in and, aim to incorporate thermal considerations into the policy learning process. However, a limitation of coupling locomotion performance and thermal safety objectives within a single policy is the potential for conservative locomotion behaviors, even within safe temperature ranges. This conservatism can restrict a robot's adaptability in complex terrains, as a single neural network may struggle to capture the distinct action distributions required for agile and thermally-constrained locomotion styles. Training often involves assigning high weights to temperature-related penalties, which can lead the policy towards overly cautious behaviors and prevent the robot from fully utilizing its performance capabilities when motors are cool.

The concept of residual policies has emerged in robotics as a method to modulate nominal control outputs by adding corrective terms. This hierarchical structure typically separates fundamental capabilities from additional regulation mechanisms, thereby enhancing system adaptability without significantly altering the inherent style of the nominal policy. Residual policies have been applied to address dynamics model errors, improve robustness, facilitate sim-to-real transfer, and enable precise adjustments through high-frequency control. More recently, they have been used in RL-based motion imitation to improve policy generalization and adaptability to action distributions outside the training dataset. For instance, a nominal policy trained for a fixed locomotion style on flat terrain can be compensated by a residual policy to adapt to complex terrains while retaining original characteristics. This capability of reconciling disparate action distributions makes residual policies a promising approach for modulating robot behavior based on long-term physical states, which includes thermal management.

This work contributes to this landscape by specifically addressing the identified gap in thermal management for high-performance legged robot locomotion. It leverages the strengths of residual policy learning to overcome the trade-offs observed in monolithic approaches, aiming to achieve a dynamic balance between aggressive performance and thermal safety across varying operating conditions.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observation that while reinforcement learning has enabled electrically-actuated legged robots to achieve high-performance locomotion, the critical aspect of motor thermal management is often neglected. In real-world applications, particularly those involving long-duration operation under payload or in challenging environments, motors can accumulate significant heat. Exceeding safety temperature thresholds can trigger protective shutdowns, compromise locomotion, or lead to irreparable hardware damage. Existing RL policies generally assume ideal thermal conditions, which is a significant limitation for practical deployment.

The central question addressed by this work is how to effectively balance thermal safety and locomotion performance, specifically enabling high locomotion performance when motors are at low temperatures while simultaneously preventing motor overheating as temperatures rise.

To address this challenge, the key objectives of this research are detailed as follows:

- **Develop a Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning Framework:** The researchers aimed to propose a novel training framework that decouples the learning of fundamental locomotion capabilities from thermal management. This framework consists of an initial pre-training stage for a nominal policy and a subsequent stage for training a residual policy.
- **Integrate a Whole-Body Thermal Model:** A crucial objective was to incorporate a realistic whole-body thermal model of a quadruped robot directly into the reinforcement learning pipeline. This integration would allow for real-time motor temperature updates, providing the necessary thermal feedback for the policy to learn temperature-aware behaviors.
- **Design Adaptive Reward Functions:** The work sought to design specific reward functions for the residual policy. These functions were intended to guide the policy to make minimal corrections and preserve the nominal locomotion performance at low temperatures, while progressively increasing corrective actions as joint temperatures approach or exceed safety thresholds, thereby promoting thermal safety.
- **Achieve a Balance Between Performance and Safety:** The overarching goal was to create a control policy that could adapt its behavior based on the robot's thermal state. This adaptive behavior would ensure that the robot maintains agile, high-performance locomotion when thermal conditions allow, and safely adjusts its operation (e.g., reducing motor load) when overheating risk is present, without completely sacrificing its ability to traverse complex terrains.
- **Validate the Approach in Simulation and Hardware:** The researchers aimed to conduct comprehensive evaluations in both simulated environments and on a physical Unitree A1 quadruped robot. This validation was intended to demonstrate the practical effectiveness and robustness of the proposed framework under various operating conditions, including carrying payloads and traversing diverse terrains.

By achieving these objectives, the authors sought to overcome the inherent trade-offs encountered by monolithic policies that attempt to concurrently optimize for both performance and thermal safety, thereby enhancing the reliability and operational duration of electrically-actuated legged robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology involves a two-stage reinforcement learning framework designed to balance quadrupedal locomotion performance with motor thermal safety. This framework integrates a whole-body thermal model of the robot into the training pipeline.

**A. Framework Overview**
The framework comprises two sequential training stages. First, a nominal policy is pre-trained to establish a foundational locomotion capability with robust terrain adaptability. Second, a thermal-aware residual policy is trained to modulate the actions generated by the nominal policy based on the robot's real-time thermal state.

**B. Nominal Policy Training**
The objective of the nominal policy (π_nom) is to develop a baseline for agile, terrain-adaptive locomotion. An Asymmetric Actor-Critic framework, combined with an encoder, is employed to handle partially observable physical states in complex terrains.

1. **Nominal Policy Network:** This network consists of an encoder and an Actor. The encoder processes historical sequences of proprioceptive observations to extract latent environmental features, with parameters optimized using Hybrid Internal Optimization (HIO). The Actor's input includes the current proprioceptive observation ($o_t$), an estimated body velocity ($\hat{v}_t$), and a latent vector ($\hat{l}_t$) from the encoder. The proprioceptive observation $o_t$ includes commanded velocity, angular velocity, gravity vector, joint positions, joint velocities, and the previous nominal action.
2. **Critic Network:** To enhance state-value estimates, the Critic receives both proprioceptive and privileged observations during training. Privileged observations encompass actual body velocity, external forces on the body, and surrounding ground height information.
3. **Action Space:** The nominal policy outputs joint position offsets ($a_{nom,t}$). These offsets are added to default joint positions ($\theta_0$) to determine target joint positions ($\theta_{target} = \theta_0 + a_{nom,t}$), which are then converted into joint torques via PD controllers.
4. **Reward Function:** The nominal policy's reward function (summarized in Table I of the paper) is designed solely to optimize locomotion performance across diverse terrains and conditions. It includes terms for linear and angular velocity tracking, penalties for Z-axis velocity, XY-plane angular velocity, orientation, joint accelerations, and body height, along with regularization for foot clearance, action rate, and action smoothness. Crucially, it contains no terms related to motor temperature.

**C. Thermal-Aware Residual Policy Training**
After the nominal policy is trained and its parameters are frozen, a residual policy (π_res) is trained to ensure actuator thermal safety while retaining the nominal policy's locomotion performance. The residual policy generates corrective actions ($a_{res,t}$), which are linearly combined with the nominal actions: $\theta_{target} = \theta_0 + a_{nom,t} + a_{res,t}$.

1. **Integration of Quadrupedal Thermal Model:** To provide real-time temperature feedback, a whole-body thermal model of the Unitree A1 robot, based on, is integrated into the training pipeline.
 - **Model Structure:** The model (Fig. 2) is a lumped-parameter thermal network comprising 14 thermal nodes (12 motors, onboard computer, ambient environment) and thermal resistances ($R_{i \sim j}$) for heat transfer.
 - **Heat Generation:** Heat generation at each motor node ($Q_{in,i}$) is primarily attributed to Joule heating from motor current, constant heat from the actuator driver, and mechanical friction heat from joint motion.
 - **Convection:** The model includes a forced convection correction mechanism that dynamically adjusts thermal resistance parameters ($R_{i \sim E}(v_{xy})$) based on the robot's linear velocity.
 - **Dynamics:** The thermal dynamics are represented as a discrete-time, multi-input multi-output (MIMO) state-space form (Eq. 4), where system matrices $A(v_{xy,t})$ and $B$ describe thermal coupling and the effect of motor inputs (root mean square torque) on temperature, respectively.
2. **Residual Policy Network:** The residual policy acts as an independent compensation layer. Its observation space ($o_{res,t}$) includes commanded velocity, angular velocity, gravity vector, joint positions, joint velocities, real-time motor temperatures ($T_t$), the latent vector ($\hat{l}_t$) from the nominal policy's encoder, and the previous residual action. The Critic also receives privileged observations (persistent external forces, ground height).
3. **Reward Design:** To balance thermal safety and locomotion performance, the residual policy's reward function ($R_{res}$) comprises three components:
 - **Thermal Safety Reward ($R_{th}$):** This reward is exponentially weighted and based on the motor temperature change rate ($\dot{T}$). It encourages the policy to avoid further temperature increases in high-temperature motors and to take actions that reduce motor temperatures. At low temperatures, this penalty is near zero, allowing the policy to prioritize locomotion performance. As temperatures approach $T_{max}$ (60°C), the penalty increases exponentially, compelling the policy to prioritize temperature reduction.
 - **Residual Action Regularization Reward ($R_{reg}$):** This term is an $\ell_2$ norm penalty on the residual actions ($a_{res,t}$). Its purpose is to encourage minimal intervention from the residual policy, especially at low temperatures, thereby preserving the original locomotion style of the nominal policy.
 - **Nominal Policy Task Reward ($R_{nom}$):** The full reward function from the nominal policy training is directly inherited. However, during residual policy training, any action-related penalty terms within $R_{nom}$ are applied to the residual actions ($a_{res,t}$) rather than the nominal actions ($a_{nom,t}$).

**D. Training Details**
Training is conducted in the Isaac Gym simulator. 4096 parallel agents are used for nominal policy training, while 16384 agents are used for residual policy training to ensure a wide range of temperature samples. The policies and thermal model run at 50 Hz, with the low-level PD controller operating at 200 Hz. Domain randomization is applied to payloads (0-5 kg), external disturbances (±30 N), body center of mass shifts (±0.05 m), and initial motor temperatures (ranging from $T_{max}-25^\circ$C to $T_{max}+10^\circ$C) and ambient temperatures (0-35°C) to enhance robustness and generate overheating conditions. A terrain curriculum is used, progressively increasing terrain difficulty and expanding commanded velocity ranges as performance thresholds are met.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed two-stage residual policy framework against two comparative policies: the Nominal Locomotion Policy (NLP) and a Monolithic Thermal Policy (MTP). Experiments were conducted in both simulation (Isaac Gym) and on a physical Unitree A1 quadruped robot.

**A. Evaluation with Simulation**

1. **Long-horizon Locomotion on Flat Terrain:**
 - Simulations involved 4096 robots continuously walking for 800 seconds on nearly flat terrain under randomized conditions (payload, external forces, ambient temperature, velocity commands).
 - **NLP:** Demonstrated low velocity tracking errors, indicating high locomotion performance. However, a significant portion of samples exceeded the motor safety temperature threshold, indicating a high risk of overheating.
 - **MTP:** Achieved an overall reduction in motor temperature distribution, effectively mitigating overheating risks. This came at the cost of considerable variability in tracking errors, suggesting compromised locomotion performance.
 - **Proposed Policy (Ours):** Maintained tracking accuracy comparable to NLP. The peak motor temperatures for most robots remained near but below the safety threshold, demonstrating a balance between locomotion performance and thermal safety.

2. **Performance under Step Velocity Commands:**
 - Robots were tested under step velocity commands with initial motor temperatures set at both low (30°C) and high (58°C) conditions.
 - **MTP:** Exhibited slow responses to velocity steps in both x and y directions at both temperature conditions. This indicated a globally conservative walking style that did not fully utilize the robot's capabilities when temperatures were low.
 - **Proposed Policy (Ours):** At low temperatures, the policy's response speed along x, y, and yaw axes was nearly identical to NLP, suggesting minimal intervention from the residual term. At high temperatures, the policy showed reduced step response performance in x and y directions and increased fluctuations along the y-axis and in yaw. This behavior was attributed to the residual policy actively modulating control outputs to regulate actuator temperatures, maintaining stability despite the adjustments.

3. **Terrain Adaptation:**
 - Experiments were conducted on stairs (0.1m height, 0.3m width) and slopes (20° inclination), each 6m long, over 300 trials with a 3 kg payload and a 1 m/s commanded velocity. Initial motor temperatures were 30°C, 50°C, and 58°C.
 - **NLP:** Demonstrated strong terrain traversal capabilities, with over 99% success rates at low and medium temperatures. However, at an initial motor temperature of 58°C, the overheat rate reached approximately 70% due to the absence of thermal management.
 - **MTP:** Consistently resulted in failed or stuck outcomes on stairs and showed a 50% drifting rate on slopes. This indicated that MTP's approach to thermal management significantly compromised its ability to adapt to complex terrains.
 - **Proposed Policy (Ours):** At low temperatures, the policy's terrain traversal performance was nearly identical to NLP. As temperatures increased (medium thermal conditions), the success rate slightly decreased, and traversal times marginally extended, reflecting the gradual intervention of the residual policy. At high temperatures (58°C), the proposed strategy reduced the overheat rate to below 10%, a significant improvement over NLP's 70%, while maintaining average maximum temperatures around 59°C. A notable observation was an increase in "drifting" behavior (70% on stairs, 45% on slopes). This drifting was identified as an adaptive strategy by the residual policy to change heading and reduce continuous motor temperature rise, differentiating it from MTP's unproductive stalling.

**B. Real-World Outdoor Experiments**

- A Unitree A1 robot, carrying a 3 kg payload, traversed a 650m outdoor path comprising slopes, grass, cobblestones, and stairs.
- **NLP:** While capable of stable traversal of individual terrains, the lack of thermal management led to one motor (LF_KFE) exceeding its temperature threshold in approximately 5 minutes, causing task failure before completing half the route.
- **MTP:** The robot adopted a high body posture, which resulted in a slower temperature increase. However, it failed to descend stairs due to a loss of body balance, indicating a failure in complex terrain adaptation.
- **Proposed Policy (Ours):** The robot successfully completed the entire 650m path in approximately 13 minutes. Throughout the traversal, the peak motor temperature remained below 50°C. Analysis of residual actions showed that their root mean square values progressively increased as the highest motor temperature rose, particularly at the HFE and KFE joints of each leg. This demonstrated the adaptive thermal regulation mechanism, where the residual policy intervenes more strongly under higher thermal load conditions, thereby enabling sustained and stable locomotion across diverse terrains while maintaining thermal safety.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that addresses a notable challenge in electrically-actuated legged robotics: the trade-off between achieving high-performance locomotion and ensuring motor thermal safety during extended operations. By proposing a two-stage training approach with a residual policy, the work offers a method to dynamically manage thermal constraints without significantly compromising the robot's agility or terrain adaptability.

The significance of this work can be understood through several points:

- **Addressing a Critical Bottleneck:** The paper directly tackles the issue of motor overheating, which is a key factor limiting the duration and reliability of electrically-actuated legged robots, especially under demanding conditions like carrying payloads or traversing complex environments. By providing a solution for thermal management at the control policy level, it enhances the practical viability of these robots.
- **Novel Framework for Conflicting Objectives:** The proposed two-stage framework, combining a nominal locomotion policy with a thermal-aware residual policy, offers an effective strategy for reconciling conflicting objectives (agile performance vs. thermal safety). This hierarchical structure allows the robot to leverage its full performance capabilities when motors are cool, and adaptively modify its behavior to prevent overheating when temperatures rise, avoiding the conservative behaviors often seen in monolithic policies.
- **Integration of Physical Models into RL:** The integration of a whole-body thermal model directly into the reinforcement learning pipeline represents a step towards more physically-informed and robust robot control policies. This approach enables the policy to learn from and react to real-time physical states that extend beyond instantaneous proprioception, leading to more realistic and sustainable behaviors.
- **Enhanced Robot Reliability and Operational Duration:** The experimental results, particularly the real-world demonstration of a Unitree A1 robot completing a challenging 650m path with a 3 kg payload in 13 minutes while maintaining motor temperatures below safety limits, indicate a substantial improvement in operational duration and reliability. This directly translates to increased utility for real-world applications requiring sustained operation.
- **Potential for Broader Applications of Residual Policies:** The successful application of residual policy learning for thermal management suggests its potential utility in addressing other long-term or global physical constraints in robot control. This could include power consumption management, battery life optimization, or even wear and tear reduction, by allowing a corrective layer to modulate a high-performance baseline.

The potential impact of this research is in enabling the development and deployment of more resilient and capable legged robots. By mitigating the risk of thermal damage and extending operational durations, such robots could be more effectively utilized in diverse fields such as logistics, inspection, disaster response, and exploration, where sustained high performance in varying conditions is crucial. Future extensions of this work, such as integrating thermal management into the planning layer, could lead to even more sophisticated and anticipatory thermal-aware behaviors, further enhancing the autonomy and robustness of legged systems.
