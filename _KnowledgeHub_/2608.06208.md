# ErgoSurf: Ergodic Control for the Coverage of Unknown Surfaces

> [!summary] Summary
> The ErgoSurf framework enables robots to perform task-driven ergodic coverage on unknown surfaces by integrating online Gaussian Process Implicit Surface (GPIS) reconstruction with a heat equation-driven control approach. It achieves effective coverage and accurate surface reconstruction, outperforming uncertainty-driven exploration policies by reducing ergodic cost by up to 21x in simulations, and demonstrates robust performance in real-robot experiments.

## Problem

- Ergodic control methods typically require prior knowledge of the operational domain's geometry, limiting their application in real-world unknown or unstructured environments.
- Active learning strategies for surface reconstruction primarily focus on reducing geometric uncertainty, rather than providing guarantees for systematic or task-specific coverage.
- A circular dependency exists where acquiring surface geometry necessitates exploration, but effective ergodic exploration strategies rely on having geometric knowledge.

## Method

- Develops **ErgoSurf**, an integrated framework that combines online surface reconstruction using a ==Gaussian Process Implicit Surface (GPIS)== with ==Heat Equation Driven Area Coverage (HEDAC)==.
- Introduces a ==dual surface representation==: a global GPIS for probabilistic surface modeling and a lightweight, discrete point cloud (incrementally built via local tangent plane approximations and GPIS projections) for efficient control.
- Employs a ==two-stage potential field computation== where user-defined task goals are first projected onto the evolving surface estimate, then diffused along the surface to generate ergonomic control gradients.

## Results

- In simulation experiments, ErgoSurf achieved significant reductions in Chamfer distance for reconstruction and ergodic cost for coverage, outperforming uncertainty gradient policies with an approximately **5x** to **21x** lower ergodic cost.
- Demonstrated robust and consistent convergence of both ergodic cost and reconstruction error across diverse objects and randomized initial conditions in multi-run simulations.
- Successfully deployed on a **7-DoF** real robot, maintaining stable exploration and achieving converging coverage and reconstruction metrics on physical objects like a wooden chair and a synthetic car seat backpanel within a **4 Hz** control loop.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - It is feasible to concurrently learn surface geometry and perform task-driven ergodic coverage without requiring prior geometric knowledge, resolving the interdependence between exploration and reconstruction.
> - Combining a probabilistic continuous surface model with a discrete, dynamically updated point cloud provides a balance between accurate global understanding and computational efficiency for online control.
> - A heat diffusion analogy effectively translates abstract, workspace-defined task goals onto an unknown, evolving surface to guide ergodic exploration, ensuring task-relevant coverage.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is positioned within the fields of robotic control, machine learning for perception, and autonomous systems. It specifically addresses challenges at the intersection of ergodic control and online surface reconstruction.

Ergodic control is a principled framework for generating trajectories that ensure the time-averaged spatial statistics of an agent's movement match a desired spatial distribution. Existing ergodic control methods, such as Spectral Multiscale Coverage (SMC) or Heat Equation Driven Area Coverage (HEDAC), typically rely on a priori knowledge of the operational domain's geometry. This assumption limits their applicability in real-world scenarios where surfaces are unknown or dynamic, requiring either extensive pre-scanning or offline geometric modeling.

Concurrently, surface modeling and reconstruction from sensor data have been extensively studied, utilizing methods ranging from classical geometric techniques to deep learning and implicit representations like Gaussian Process Implicit Surfaces (GPIS). GPIS is particularly noted for its ability to provide uncertainty quantification and handle noisy, sparse data. Previous research leveraging tactile sensing for GPIS reconstruction often focuses on active learning strategies, where the robot explores to reduce geometric uncertainty. These strategies primarily aim to reconstruct the surface accurately, but they do not inherently provide guarantees for systematic or task-specific coverage.

This paper addresses a notable gap: the absence of an ergodic control framework capable of operating on a domain that is simultaneously being reconstructed online. Prior methods either treat geometric learning and trajectory generation as separate, offline phases, or their exploration strategies are driven by uncertainty reduction rather than task-specific coverage objectives. The proposed work integrates these two functionalities, allowing task-driven ergodic exploration to concurrently facilitate surface learning, thereby removing the requirement for prior geometric knowledge or separate offline reconstruction phases.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical necessity for robots to perform contact-centric tasks (e.g., inspection, cleaning, sanding, polishing) on surfaces whose precise geometry is unknown beforehand. While systematic surface coverage is a fundamental requirement for such tasks, conventional ergodic control methods face a significant limitation: they assume prior knowledge of the surface geometry. This dependency on pre-existing models or pre-scanning hinders their deployment in real-world, unstructured, or dynamic environments.

The authors identify several specific challenges:
- **Circular Dependency:** An ergodic exploration strategy requires knowledge of the surface geometry, yet acquiring this geometry necessitates an exploration strategy. This interdependent relationship cannot be resolved through offline planning alone.
- **Limitations of Vision-Based Approaches:** Although vision systems can provide geometric data, they are susceptible to issues such as occlusions, transparent or specular materials, and variable lighting conditions, which can compromise reconstruction quality. Tactile sensing offers an alternative modality less prone to these issues.
- **Computational Efficiency for Online Control:** Real-time control necessitates that both surface learning and trajectory planning remain computationally tractable at each control step.
- **Robustness to Sensor Data:** Sensor measurements are inherently noisy and incomplete, requiring estimation methods that are robust and can account for uncertainty without overfitting.

To address these challenges, the paper outlines four key objectives and contributions:
1. **Integrated Framework:** To develop a framework that combines online surface reconstruction with ergodic control, enabling concurrent geometric learning and task-specific coverage without the need for offline planning or prior geometric knowledge.
2. **Dual Surface Representation:** To introduce a dual representation strategy comprising a Gaussian Process Implicit Surface (GPIS) for a global, probabilistic model of the surface (including normals), and a lightweight, discrete point cloud derived from local tangent plane samples projected onto the GPIS zero-level set. This dual approach aims to provide both accurate global understanding and computational efficiency for local control.
3. **Two-Stage Potential Field Formulation:** To design a potential field computation that allows users to specify task-relevant regions in workspace coordinates, independent of the unknown surface geometry. This involves a first stage spreading user-defined goals from 3D space onto the current surface estimate to form a surface-supported target distribution, followed by a second stage diffusing this target along the surface to drive ergodic control.
4. **Efficient Online Updates:** To implement a direct point cloud gradient computation via local linear least squares on unstructured neighbors and a selective coverage update mechanism. This ensures that only newly sampled or projected points are recomputed, reducing the computational load for online operation.

These objectives collectively aim to enable robots to autonomously explore and cover unknown surfaces in a task-efficient and geometrically aware manner.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed ErgoSurf framework integrates online surface reconstruction with ergodic control through a dual surface representation and a two-stage heat diffusion process. The overall framework operates in a continuous control loop, adapting robot actions based on an evolving understanding of the surface geometry.

**A. Contact Observation:**
During exploration, the robot maintains stable contact with the surface using a Cartesian impedance controller with a force overlay. The system leverages a momentum-based observer for full dynamical decoupling, isolating the external end-effector wrench from the robot's own dynamics. This process yields an estimated contact force and moment. From these measurements, two critical quantities are extracted at each time step `k`: the contact point `q_k` and the corresponding surface normal `n_k`. These are determined by identifying the wrench axis (line of force action) and intersecting it with the known tool geometry, assuming a rigid body and single-point, frictionless contact.

**B. Surface Representation (Gaussian Process Implicit Surface - GPIS):**
An unknown implicit function `f(x)` defines the surface as its zero-level set. This function is learned using a Gaussian Process (GPIS). Each contact observation `q_k` provides two pieces of information to the GP: a function value `f(q_k)` (which is 0 as the point is on the surface, modeled as `N(0, σ_n^2)`) and a gradient `∇f(q_k)` (which aligns with the measured contact normal `n_k`, modeled as `N(n_k, σ_n^2 I)`). These observations incrementally update the GPIS model, with new observations added only if they are beyond a threshold `d_obs` from existing points to manage the size of the kernel matrix. Cholesky updates are employed for efficient `O(n^2)` matrix inversion. Gradient observations are particularly important as they constrain the local orientation of the surface, enabling robust reconstruction from sparse data.

**C. Point Cloud for GPIS Discretization:**
To enable computationally tractable ergodic control, the continuous GPIS model is approximated by a discrete point cloud `Z_k`. Instead of computationally expensive global voxelization (e.g., marching cubes), the point cloud is built incrementally using local tangent plane approximations. At each contact point `q_k`, the GPIS-inferred normal `ˆn_k` (which is smoother than raw sensor data) defines a tangent plane `Π_k`. `N_s` new points are sampled within this plane, typically drawn from a Gaussian distribution around `q_k`, with a spread `σ_tp`. These sampled points are added to `Z_k` if they meet a minimum distance criterion `d_min` from existing points.

A crucial step is **projection refinement**: each newly sampled or existing point in `Z_k` is iteratively projected onto the current GPIS zero-level set. This is achieved by repeatedly moving the point `z_m` along the estimated normal `ˆn(z_m)` by the inferred signed distance `E[f(z_m)]` until it converges to the surface (`z_m+1 = z_m - E[f(z_m)] ˆn(z_m)`). This ensures that all points in the control domain accurately lie on the learned surface, even in regions of high curvature. Points that diverge (due to high uncertainty beyond the kernel length scale) are excluded.

**D. Ergodic Control on an Evolving Surface Geometry:**
The Heat Equation Driven Area Coverage (HEDAC) method is adopted as the underlying ergodic control formulation due to its adaptability to curved surfaces via the Laplace-Beltrami operator and its ability to generate per-step gradients. The target distribution `p_S_k(x)` and coverage distribution `c(x,t)` are defined and evaluated on the discrete point cloud `Z_k`. `p_S_k(x)` dynamically adapts as the surface geometry `S_k` is refined. The coverage distribution `c(x,t)` is updated efficiently using a selective strategy: only newly sampled or projected points have their coverage recomputed from the control trajectory, while static points are updated incrementally.

**E. From Heat Sources to Potential Field of Temperatures on the Surface:**
A two-stage heat transfer analogy computes the control potential:
1. **Radiative Heat Transfer:** User-specified task goals are defined as heat sources `p_0(x)` in workspace coordinates, independent of the unknown surface. This heat radiates onto the current surface estimate `S_k`, with intensity decaying as `1/r^2`. The convolution `p_S_k(x) = (p_0 * κ)(x)` defines the surface-supported target distribution, continuously adapting as `S_k` evolves.
2. **Conductive Heat Transfer:** A coverage-deficit signal, `s(x,t) = max(p_S_k(x) - c(x,t), 0)`, acts as a heat source on `S_k`. This signal diffuses across the surface according to the quasi-static heat equation `α∆_S_k u - βu + s = 0`, where `∆_S_k` is the Laplace-Beltrami operator (discretized as `−M^(−1)L` using the strong Laplacian on `Z_k`). The solution `u = (αL + βM)^(-1)Ms` provides a smooth potential field of temperatures.

**F. Deriving Ergodic Actions from Temperature Gradients:**
The surface gradient `∇_S_k u(x)` of the potential field `u(x)` is computed for each point `x` in `Z_k` using local linear least squares over its `M_V` neighbors. A first-order Taylor expansion `u(z) ≈ u(x) + ∇u(x)^T (z - x)` is minimized to find `∇u(x)`. This gradient is then projected onto the tangent plane at `x` (using the GPIS-inferred normal `ˆn(x)`) to ensure it lies entirely within the surface: `∇_S_k u(x) = P_n(x)∇u(x)`. The robot's next action is then derived directly from this normalized surface gradient, `ẋ_a = v * (∇_S_k u(x) / ||∇_S_k u(x)||)`, guiding it towards under-explored, high-target regions at a constant speed `v`.

This entire process is iteratively executed in an online control loop, enabling concurrent surface reconstruction and ergodic coverage.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The framework was validated through both simulation and real-robot experiments using a single agent, demonstrating its capacity for simultaneous surface reconstruction and ergodic coverage of unknown surfaces. Performance was assessed using the ergodic cost `ϵ_k` (quantifying coverage quality), Chamfer distance `d_C` (measuring reconstruction error between the reconstructed surface and ground truth), and a target-weighted Chamfer distance `d_TC` (emphasizing reconstruction quality in task-relevant regions).

**A. Bunny Experiment (Simulation):**
The Stanford bunny model, characterized by complex geometry with high curvatures and non-convex regions, served as a simulation benchmark. A single point heat source defined the target distribution.
- **Reconstruction Accuracy:** Both Chamfer distance `d_C` and target Chamfer distance `d_TC` decreased significantly over 10000 time steps, approaching the ground truth. `d_TC` converged faster, indicating improved reconstruction in task-relevant areas.
- **Ergodic Coverage Performance:** The ergodic cost `ϵ_k` consistently decreased, demonstrating effective coverage.
- **Comparison with Uncertainty Gradient Policy:** When compared against an uncertainty gradient-based active learning policy (Driess et al., 2017), the proposed method achieved comparable reconstruction quality. However, its ergodic cost was substantially lower (approximately 5x lower at step 1000 and 21x lower at step 5000). The uncertainty gradient policy's ergodic cost stagnated and even increased after an initial reduction, indicating its inability to achieve global ergodic coverage, as it only optimizes local uncertainty. In contrast, the diffusion-based formulation of ErgoSurf maintained a steady decrease in ergodic cost.
- **Dynamic Adaptation:** The target distribution, initially defined in workspace coordinates, progressively concentrated around the heat source location as the GPIS representation expanded and refined. GPIS prediction uncertainty was observed to reduce in explored areas.

**B. Multi-Run Robustness Analysis:**
A statistical robustness study was conducted across four diverse objects (Stanford bunny, YCB mustard bottle, Spot model, torus). Each object underwent 20 independent runs, with randomized numbers of heat sources (1-10), their positions, and initial contact points.
- **Consistency:** Across all objects, both the ergodic cost and reconstruction error metrics converged consistently, exhibiting narrow standard-deviation bands. This indicates that the framework reliably achieves both ergodic coverage and accurate surface reconstruction under varying initial conditions and task specifications.
- **Adaptation to Initial Conditions:** In specific runs (e.g., Bunny Run 7), where the heat source was initially on the opposite side of the starting point, the framework demonstrated adaptive behavior. A temporary increase in ergodic cost and a sharp drop in target Chamfer distance were observed when the agent first reached and uncovered the target region, after which convergence resumed.

**C. Hardware Platform and Real-Robot Experiments:**
Experiments were conducted on a 7-DoF robot manipulator with a spherical probing tool and multiple force sensors. A momentum-based observer provided dynamically decoupled external end-effector wrench measurements for contact localization. The control loop operated at 4 Hz, with a low-level controller at >1 kHz.

- **Chair Experiment:** On a wooden chair with smooth geometry, the `ϵ_k`, `d_C`, and `d_TC` metrics converged, confirming the framework's successful deployment and performance on a real physical system.
- **Backpanel Experiment:** On a synthetic car seat backpanel with stronger curvatures and intricate features, the framework maintained stable exploration and achieved consistent reconstruction quality for two distinct target regions, with all metrics converging towards ground truth.
- **Computational Performance:** A runtime analysis showed that surface projection and Laplacian assembly were the dominant computational costs per control step. However, the total runtime per step (e.g., 50 ms for bunny, 107 ms for chair at step 1000) remained within the 250 ms budget for the 4 Hz control rate.

Overall, the results demonstrate that the ErgoSurf framework effectively balances surface learning with task-driven ergodic coverage, outperforming uncertainty-driven methods in terms of coverage guarantees and exhibiting robust performance across diverse simulated and real-world scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework for robotic surface interaction that significantly advances capabilities in scenarios where prior geometric knowledge is absent. The key significance and potential impact of "ErgoSurf" can be outlined as follows:

- **Enabling Ergodic Control for Unknown Surfaces:** The most direct impact is the removal of a fundamental assumption in ergodic control: the need for known surface geometry. This opens up a broad range of real-world applications for ergodic coverage, such as autonomous inspection, cleaning, finishing, or maintenance tasks on workpieces or environments lacking CAD models or prior scans.
- **Integrated Planning and Learning:** The framework demonstrates a functional integration of online surface reconstruction and task-driven control. Instead of treating perception and planning as sequential or decoupled stages, the system concurrently learns the environment's geometry as a byproduct of performing a coverage task. This reduces operational overhead and delays associated with separate mapping phases.
- **Task-Specific Coverage Guarantees:** Unlike active learning methods that primarily aim to reduce uncertainty, ErgoSurf's heat diffusion-based approach provides explicit guarantees for systematic coverage according to a user-defined task distribution. This ensures that exploration effort is directed towards relevant areas while still discovering geometry, a critical differentiator for goal-oriented applications.
- **Robustness and Real-World Applicability:** The validation through both extensive simulations and real-robot experiments on diverse physical objects (a chair, a car seat backpanel) demonstrates the robustness of the approach against real-world complexities such as sensor noise, robot dynamics, and varying surface characteristics. The multi-run analysis further substantiates its consistent performance across randomized task configurations.
- **Dual Representation for Efficiency:** The combination of a global GPIS for coherent surface modeling with local tangent plane approximations and a discrete point cloud for efficient control updates is a pragmatic solution to balance model accuracy with computational tractability, a critical aspect for online robot control.
- **Intuitive Task Specification:** The ability for users to specify task goals as heat sources in workspace coordinates, independent of the unknown geometry, simplifies human-robot interaction and makes the system more accessible for operators without specialized geometric modeling skills.

Despite these advancements, the authors acknowledge limitations that also suggest avenues for future research:
- **Surface Discontinuities:** The current approach assumes smooth, continuous surfaces. Extending it to handle sharp edges or discontinuities (e.g., gaps, holes) would require modifications to the GPIS kernel or explicit feature detection mechanisms.
- **Adaptive Hyperparameters:** GPIS kernel hyperparameters are currently fixed. Implementing adaptive, online learning of these parameters based on local surface complexity could enhance reconstruction efficiency and accuracy.
- **Thin Objects and Computational Scaling:** The discrete point cloud representation might face challenges with very thin objects, where the discrete Laplacian could erroneously connect points across distinct sides. Scaling the approach to extremely large surfaces or very high-resolution requirements remains an area for further optimization.

In conclusion, "ErgoSurf" represents a step towards truly autonomous robots capable of performing complex physical interaction tasks in unknown and unstructured environments, paving the way for more flexible and adaptable robotic systems across various industries.
