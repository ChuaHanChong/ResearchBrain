# Continual Learning for 6-DoF Grasp Synthesis via Experience and Demonstrations

> [!summary] Summary
> A continual-learning framework for single-view 6-DoF parallel-jaw grasping in clutter that adapts through memory instead of weight updates. A learned grasp embedding feeds a Beta-posterior nearest-neighbor scorer updated instantly with each grasp outcome, while registered user demonstrations add new candidate grasps. It matches strong baselines before adaptation and exceeds 90% real-world success on five of six categories after 50 attempts.

## Problem

- Most 6-DoF grasp synthesis networks are trained offline and frozen at deployment, so they fail on unfamiliar geometries absent or underrepresented in the training data.
- Prior grasp-adaptation methods often assume top-down grasping or test-time CAD models, and adapt via parametric finetuning that needs many labels and GPU retraining.
- Retrieval and trial-based grasping depend on database coverage, learn only from successes, or keep independent per-grasp success estimates that never generalize to similar grasps.

## Method

- Hybrid grasp proposal: a ==contact-normal geometric sampler== plus top-down grasps, augmented by ==demonstration recall== that registers stored point-cloud patches via ==FPFH features== and ==point-to-plane ICP==.
- Grasp embedding encoder: a ==Basis Point Set== serialized local patch fed to an MLP, trained with ==soft nearest-neighbor== plus reconstruction loss onto a 32-dimensional unit sphere.
- Memory-based ==Beta-posterior scorer==: K nearest neighbors contribute ==RBF-weighted pseudo-counts==, with capped offline evidence, upweighted online outcomes and adaptively weighted demonstrations; adaptation just appends memory entries.

## Results

- In simulation on **443** unseen objects, the base model averages **94.6%** success versus **92.9%** for EdgeGraspNet; category adaptation lifts it to **98.1%**, while finetuned EdgeGraspNet reaches only **93.5%**.
- Sequential continual learning across **ten** categories ends at **97.8%** average success with worst forgetting of **2.4 points**, a **1.35 MB** memory footprint and planning under **1.5 seconds**.
- On the real robot over **1500** grasps, adaptation raises screwdrivers from **85.7%** to **96.0%** and control objects from **80.0%** to **93.2%**; merged memories reach **89.6%** on mixed scenes versus **69.2%** for AnyGrasp.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Non-parametric memory over a learned embedding gives instant, gradient-free adaptation and resists forgetting, outperforming finetuning when only around 100 deployment labels exist.
> - Decouple candidate generation from scoring: recalled demonstrations add missing grasp modes, while accumulated outcome evidence can still veto a well-registered but unreliable transfer.
> - Proposal heuristics silently cap performance, since contact-normal samplers collapse on thin objects; separately collected memories merge cleanly, hinting at fleet-level parallel adaptation.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

Single-view grasping in cluttered scenes is hard: the robot's first plausible grasp may fail. In this work, the robot keeps a record of what worked and what failed. A person can also optionally show it a grasp it did not propose. In a 20-attempt test on a shampoo bottle that had been difficult for the robot, the outcome-only version had five failed grasps. The full version with demonstrations had two. The full method began with two demonstrations and received a new one after each failed grasp. This was an illustrative three-object study, not the main benchmark. Demonstrations reduced the number of failures, although they did not remove all late failures.

Failure traces over 20 attempts for three selected difficult objects. Bars mark failures. Failure counts are 7 versus 3 for the toilet cleaner, 5 versus 2 for the shampoo bottle, and 6 versus 4 for the rubber duck (Ours without demonstrations versus Ours full). The full method starts with two demonstrations and gets another after each failure. The rubber duck still has late failures. These traces are not a comprehensive real-world benchmark.

### 2. Memory changes the next choice

The robot adapts after offline training by adding experience to memory rather than updating network weights. Grasp outcomes revise scores for similar grasps. Demonstrations can add grasp proposals the geometric sampler missed. All of this happens without gradient-based retraining.

This happens in two stages. First, geometric sampling proposes candidate grasps from the observed point cloud, using contact surface normals, plus top-down grasps. Optional recall matches the local point-cloud patch around a user-demonstrated grasp to similar geometry in a new scene, which proposes another candidate. Recalled candidates still undergo feasibility and collision filtering.

Second, an encoder maps each grasp's local geometry to a compact learned embedding. A memory of labeled grasps supplies nearby evidence: successes increase a candidate's estimated success score, and failures decrease it. Each outcome can be added immediately, and the model's weights are not retrained.

A demonstration does two jobs. It enters the scoring memory as positive evidence and the recall memory as a transferable proposal. Recalled grasps are proposals, not automatic choices. The same adaptive scorer ranks both sampled and recalled grasps.

### 3. What the trials establish

**Simulation.** The authors evaluated 443 unseen objects across ten categories. Each category-specific adaptation used 100 grasp attempts and at most five demonstrations. Under these conditions, the full method averaged 98.1% success, versus 94.6% for its base model. Scoring-only reached 97.1%, whereas recall-only reached 85.6%. This supports the authors' interpretation that recall supplements geometric proposals rather than replacing them.

EdgeGraspNet fine-tuning received the same 100 category-specific labels plus a balanced mixture with its original training data. It rose from 92.9% to 93.5%, and the authors report one hour per category on an RTX 4070. This holds for this task, data budget, and implementation. It is not a general finding that fine-tuning cannot work.

**Real world.** The setup used a Franka Panda arm, a parallel-jaw gripper, a depth camera, and 54 objects. The objects formed five challenge sets plus a control set, tested over more than 1,500 attempts. *Grasp success rate* is the fraction of attempts that succeed, labeled from a resulting finger width greater than zero. *Scene-clear rate* is the fraction of scenes fully cleared.

Up to 50 adaptation attempts were run per category, with a demonstration after each failed attempt. Success improved in all six sets and exceeded 90% in five. The exception was pliers, which rose only from 62.5% to 68.3%. The authors attribute this to one plier instance needing a grasp strategy that conflicts with the strategies that work on the others, while geometry alone does not distinguish it reliably.

Real-world evaluation: grasp success, with scene-clear rate in parentheses. Pliers rise only from 62.5% to 68.3% after adaptation, while the other five adapted categories exceed 90%. On toys, AnyGrasp (85.0%) beats the base model (83.6%) but not the adapted model (94.4%).

### 4. Failures become evidence

In this method, a failed grasp's label can lower scores for nearby grasps, and a demonstration can supply a candidate the sampler missed. The study shows these as complementary ways to change future choices. Its boundary is clear: the real scenes had moderate occlusion, and sensor noise and contact dynamics were not systematically tested. Adaptation also cannot recover geometry that is missing or distorted in the input.
