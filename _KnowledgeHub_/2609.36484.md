# The Teacher Is a Direction, Not a Destination: Extrapolating RL-Induced Representation Residuals in On-Policy Distillation

> [!summary] Summary
> RIDE (RL-Induced Direction Extrapolation) is an on-policy distillation method that lets a student surpass its RL-trained teacher. On each student rollout, it computes the layerwise hidden-state residual between the teacher and its pre-RL checkpoint, then regresses student hidden states toward targets placed beyond the teacher along that residual. Across four base/teacher pairs, RIDE matches or exceeds the teacher, while output-space extrapolation does not.

## Problem

- Standard on-policy distillation treats the teacher as the endpoint, so a student that matches teacher next-token distributions cannot exceed the RL-trained teacher it distills from.
- Output-space extrapolation measures the RL change only after the anisotropic LM head attenuates it: the weakest head directions carry most residual hidden energy but little logit energy.
- Extrapolating sampled-token log-probability ratios multiplies sampling noise by the squared coefficient, causing length bias, format collapse and instability, especially when the teacher sits close to its base.

## Method

- **RL-induced representation residual**: the frozen teacher and the pre-RL base run on identical student prefixes, and their layerwise ==hidden-state difference== measures what RL changed at each position.
- **RIDE** target and objective: targets h_T + (λ-1)Δ sit beyond the teacher, and the student regresses toward them with ==masked squared error== over all layers and the final 2,000 response positions.
- Reward-and-penalty interpretation: the regression maximizes a linear directional reward along the residual under a quadratic penalty centered at the teacher; λ=1 recovers ==OPRD==, and each gradient is deterministic given the rollout.

## Results

- On R1-Distill-1.5B, RIDE reaches **56.38** Avg@16 against **55.30** for the teacher, **54.50** for OPRD and **49.87** for ExOPD. It is the only method whose mean exceeds the teacher on all four pairs.
- Output-space ExOPD falls below the untouched student on the three small-gap pairs, by **14.1** points on Qwen3-4B. RIDE beats ExOPD by **9.1** points on average and beats OPRD by **0.97**-4.06.
- Random, reversed, mismatched-origin and trajectory-mismatched direction controls all land within one point of OPRD, while the true RL residual reaches **56.38**. ExOPD conditional variance rises **12.6x** from λ=1 to λ=2.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An RL-trained teacher gives a direction, not just a destination. A student that shares the teacher's initialization can keep moving along that direction and surpass the teacher.
> - Measure model differences before the output head: anisotropic projections hide most of the representation change, so logit-space objectives under-supervise exactly the directions RL updates most.
> - Extrapolate quantities you compute, not ones you sample. Scaling a noisy sampled reward estimate amplifies its variance quadratically, while computed targets only move the destination.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A common use of on-policy distillation (OPD) is to transfer gains from an expensive RL run back into its base model. The student starts from the same base and trains on its own responses, with the teacher supervising the contexts the student actually visits. Matching the teacher treats its current performance as the endpoint. This approach measures how RL moved the model's hidden states and trains the student toward targets beyond the teacher along that direction. The method, RIDE, has a reported mean score above the RL teacher's on all four tested base/teacher pairs, and it beats direct hidden-state matching on each.

Overview of RIDE. A student samples a response. The frozen base and RL teacher are evaluated on the same prefix, and their layerwise hidden states define residuals. The trainable student is regressed toward masked targets across layers. When λ>1, the target lies beyond the teacher. The figure explains target construction; it does not show a measured result.

### 2. The RL change becomes the target

The base is the checkpoint before RL, and the teacher is that model after RL. The student starts from the base. On each student-generated prefix, both frozen models run on the same tokens. At every supervised layer and position, the base hidden state is subtracted from the teacher's. This context-specific difference is the RL-induced residual. Using identical prefixes is intended to separate model change from differences caused by different trajectories.

The target is

$$h^{\star} = h_{T} + (\lambda-1)(h_{T}-h_{B})$$

where $h_{T}$ and $h_{B}$ are the teacher's and base's hidden states for the same layer, position, and prefix. At λ=1 the target is the teacher, and the method is OPRD, a prior method that matches teacher and student hidden states on student-generated contexts. With λ>1, the target lies beyond the teacher in the RL-induced direction. The student minimizes masked squared error to these targets over all layers. In the reported setup, this covers the final 2,000 response positions. Main results use λ=1.25, with the loss coefficient rescaled by λ⁻² to equalize its initial scale. The formula specifies the regression target and does not guarantee exact realization.

For a fixed rollout, the squared-error target is equivalent to rewarding student displacement aligned with the RL residual while penalizing squared distance from the teacher. The closest prior work, ExOPD from [Learning beyond Teacher: Generalized On-Policy Distillation with Reward Extrapolation](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2602.12125), scales the teacher-versus-reference log-probability reward in output space. With λ and the pre-RL reference held the same, RIDE and ExOPD differ in where they measure the displacement: hidden states versus output-space log-ratios.

### 3. Above the teacher, by small margins

Avg@16 averages correctness over 16 sampled responses per problem. That average is taken over problems separately for AIME 2024, AIME 2025, and AIMO (AMC 2022–2023), and the three benchmark scores are then averaged equally. On the R1-Distill-1.5B → JustRL-1.5B pair, the reported means are 56.38% for RIDE, 55.30% for the teacher, 54.50% for OPRD, and 49.87% for ExOPD. Trained-method means are over three seeds. Teacher scores are fixed-checkpoint evaluations. All methods shared the prompts, sampling settings, optimizer schedule, and 500-step budget.

RIDE's mean is above the teacher's on all four pairs and beats OPRD on each. ExOPD is below the teacher on every pair. Three of RIDE's four margins over the teacher are within one across-seed standard deviation. This is evidence of higher reported means, not a statistical test establishing reliable superiority. On the Llama pair, AIME scores are near the evaluation floor, so the evidence there rests mainly on AIMO.

Main results (Avg@16, %). The teacher/RIDE averages are 55.30/56.38, 65.59/66.07, 13.01/13.33, and 17.96/18.30. ExOPD is below the teacher in all four pairs. Trained methods are three-seed means. Teacher and untouched-student rows are fixed-checkpoint evaluations. The margins above the teacher are small, and no significance test is reported.

### 4. Is it the RL direction?

Fixed-magnitude controls tested alternative displacements on R1-Distill-1.5B:

- a random same-norm direction scored 55.04;
- reversed displacement toward the base scored 53.90;
- a residual from an unrelated same-architecture model origin scored 54.75;
- a residual computed on separately generated trajectories scored 55.12.

By comparison, RIDE scored 56.38 and OPRD scored 54.50. None of the controls matches RIDE's reported score. The paper does not report uncertainty for these controls, so the comparison supports the authors' interpretation without proving it.

Direction controls at fixed displacement magnitude. RIDE's 56.38 is higher than the controls' 53.90–55.12 and OPRD's 54.50. The table has no uncertainty intervals.

### 5. A direction, not a destination

Matching the teacher copies its endpoint. RIDE turns the RL change, measured on the student's own contexts, into a target for further learning. The direction controls are consistent with this being more than arbitrary displacement. The method requires the pre-RL checkpoint and a shared representation space. These experiments cover mathematical reasoning and one RL recipe.
