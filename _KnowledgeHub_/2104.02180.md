# AMP: Adversarial Motion Priors for Stylized Physics-Based Character Control

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at UC Berkeley developed Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP), a framework that allows physically simulated characters to perform tasks in specific styles learned from unstructured motion data. This approach automatically composes diverse skills like locomotion and actions, demonstrating high-fidelity motion quality comparable to state-of-the-art tracking methods without explicit phase synchronization.

## Problem

- Designing effective objective functions for physics-based characters to produce natural and graceful behaviors is challenging and often requires extensive manual tuning.
- Existing data-driven animation techniques (e.g., motion tracking) rely on carefully designed, hand-tuned tracking objectives and often require pre-processed or annotated motion data, limiting their scalability and generalizability.
- Prior applications of adversarial imitation learning to physics-based character control struggled with instability and produced lower motion quality compared to tracking-based methods.

## Method

- A dual reward system combines a high-level, manually-designed task reward with a learned, task-agnostic style reward derived from an adversarial motion prior (**AMP**).
- ==The AMP== is an adversarial discriminator trained using a Least-Squares GAN (LSGAN) objective and a gradient penalty, which operates on state transitions to distinguish between generated and reference motions.
- The control policy is trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), utilizing reference state initialization and a replay buffer to enhance exploration and training stability.

## Results

- The AMP framework generates high-quality, diverse stylized motions (e.g., 'Locomotion', 'Zombie', 'Stealthy') and automatically composes disparate skills (e.g., gait transitions, walking to punch) from unstructured motion data.
- AMP achieves motion quality comparable to state-of-the-art motion tracking methods, without requiring manually designed tracking objectives or phase synchronization.
- Ablation studies demonstrate that a gradient penalty is vital for training stability and that explicit velocity features in the discriminator's observations are crucial for accurately imitating dynamic motions like rolling.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adversarial imitation learning can achieve high-fidelity physics-based character control when carefully designed with components like LSGAN objectives, gradient penalties, and robust observation features.
> - Learning a motion prior directly from unstructured data decouples task and style, enabling automatic composition of diverse skills without explicit motion planners.
> - Discriminators operating on state transitions are effective for imitation learning when demonstrator actions are unknown, capturing essential motion characteristics relevant to style.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The synthesis of natural and life-like behaviors for virtual characters is a long-standing and fundamental challenge in computer animation. This paper operates at the intersection of computer graphics (specifically character animation), reinforcement learning (RL), and imitation learning.

Historically, character animation techniques have been broadly categorized into kinematic and physics-based methods. Kinematic methods leverage motion capture data or artistic keyframe animations to generate motions, offering high fidelity but often limited generalizability to novel situations or environments. Physics-based methods, in contrast, generate motions from first principles using physical simulations, ensuring plausibility and responsiveness to new scenarios. However, designing effective objective functions that lead to *natural* and *graceful* physics-based behaviors has proven exceptionally difficult. Heuristics like symmetry, stability, or effort minimization are often insufficient or require extensive manual tuning.

Data-driven physics-based animation emerged to combine the strengths of both approaches: using reference motion data to guide physics-based characters. A prominent technique within this paradigm is *motion tracking*, where a simulated character is encouraged to follow specific reference trajectories from a dataset. While tracking-based methods can yield high-quality motions, they face significant limitations:
- **Manual Objective Design:** They often rely on carefully designed, hand-tuned tracking objectives and pose error metrics, which can be challenging to generalize across diverse skills.
- **Motion Selection:** For large and varied motion datasets, selecting the appropriate motion clip for the character to track at each time step requires complex and often manually-annotated motion planners. This overhead limits their scalability to unstructured data.
- **Limited Generalization:** Characters might struggle to deviate from reference motions, even when necessary to solve a task not explicitly demonstrated.

This paper directly addresses these limitations by proposing **Adversarial Motion Priors (AMP)**, moving beyond tracking-based imitation. It draws inspiration from **Adversarial Imitation Learning (AIL)**, particularly Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL) [Ho and Ermon, 2016], which learns an objective function (a discriminator) to distinguish between expert demonstrations and agent-generated behaviors. While prior AIL applications to character control [Merel et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017] struggled with instability and motion quality compared to tracking-based methods, AMP introduces several crucial design decisions to overcome these issues.

Furthermore, the work differentiates itself from *latent space models* [Heess et al., 2016; Merel et al., 2019], another approach to learning motion priors. Latent space models encode behaviors into a low-dimensional representation, which then constrains the policy's actions. However, they enforce style *indirectly*, meaning the high-level controller might still specify latent encodings that lead to unnatural behaviors. AMP, by contrast, directly enforces similarity on the *actual motions* produced by the character through the reward function, leading to higher fidelity.

In essence, AMP represents a significant advancement in data-driven physics-based character control. It aims to create a unified framework that decouples task specification from style specification, enabling characters to perform complex tasks in a desired style by learning from *unstructured* datasets, without the need for manual reward engineering, explicit motion planners, or pose synchronization. This positions it as a promising step towards more autonomous and versatile physically simulated agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a system that allows users to easily specify the desired behavior of a physically simulated character, combining high-level task objectives with low-level stylistic movements, using readily available unstructured motion data.

The key motivations driving this research stem from the limitations of existing character animation techniques:

- **Simplifying Style Specification:** Current methods for achieving natural and life-like motions often require painstaking manual design of reward functions or explicit objectives. This process is time-consuming, difficult to generalize across different behaviors, and often requires re-tuning for each new task or motion. The authors aim to obviate this by learning the style directly from examples.
- **Leveraging Unstructured Motion Data:** While large datasets of motion capture (mocap) data exist, their effective utilization in physics-based character control remains challenging. Existing tracking-based methods typically require these datasets to be carefully annotated, segmented, and organized, often necessitating a complex "motion planner" to select appropriate clips. The goal is to develop a framework that can learn from "raw," unstructured motion clips without such heavy pre-processing.
- **Decoupling Task and Style:** The authors seek to separate the "what" (the task the character performs, e.g., reaching a target) from the "how" (the style of movement, e.g., walking gracefully or like a zombie). This allows for modularity, where different motion datasets can impart different styles to the same task.
- **Improving Adversarial Imitation Learning:** Prior work in adversarial imitation learning for physics-based characters suffered from instability and produced lower-quality motions compared to tracking methods. A significant motivation is to introduce design choices that stabilize the adversarial training process, leading to consistently high-fidelity and diverse results.
- **Enabling Skill Composition and Generalization:** Traditional methods struggle with combining disparate skills (e.g., walking and punching) or generalizing beyond the exact motions seen in the training data. A key objective is for the system to automatically compose multiple skills and adapt them to novel scenarios, without explicit high-level planners or task-specific annotations.
- **Real-world Applications:** Ultimately, the research is motivated by the demand for more realistic and responsive virtual agents in interactive experiences like films, games, and VR, as well as the potential for generating natural and efficient behaviors for robotic systems.

By addressing these motivations, the authors aim to provide a more convenient, automated, and scalable interface for controlling physics-based characters with rich, data-driven styles.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the AMP framework is a novel combination of goal-conditioned reinforcement learning (RL) with an adversarial motion prior. This approach decouples the specification of *task* (what to do) from *style* (how to do it).

#### 3.1. Dual Reward System

The character's total reward function, $r_t$, is composed of two main terms:
- **Task-Reward ($r_G$):** A relatively simple, manually-designed reward function that defines high-level objectives (e.g., moving towards a target location, maintaining a target speed). This is task-specific.
- **Style-Reward ($r_S$):** A learned, task-agnostic reward that encourages the character's movements to resemble motions from a given dataset. This is the "adversarial motion prior" (AMP).
The total reward is a linear combination of these two, $r_t = w_G r_G + w_S r_S$.

#### 3.2. Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP) as a Discriminator

- **Modeling:** The style-reward $r_S$ is modeled by an adversarial discriminator, $D$. This discriminator is trained to distinguish between "real" motion clips (from the reference dataset M) and "fake" motions generated by the character's policy ($\pi$).
- **Imitation from Observations:** A crucial design choice is that the discriminator operates on *state transitions* ($D(s_t, s_{t+1})$) rather than state-action pairs ($D(s_t, a_t)$). This is vital because, in motion capture datasets, the underlying actions of the demonstrator are typically unknown. The discriminator's objective is to minimize the difference between the distribution of state transitions from the dataset and those from the policy.
- **Least-Squares Discriminator (LSGAN):** Instead of the standard sigmoid cross-entropy loss used in GANs, the authors adopt the objective from Least-Squares GANs (LSGAN) [Mao et al., 2017]. This objective trains the discriminator to output a score of 1 for real samples and -1 for fake samples. This change is explicitly motivated by its demonstrated ability to lead to more stable training and higher quality results in generative tasks by avoiding vanishing gradients. The policy's style reward is then derived from the discriminator's output, transformed to be bounded between.
- **Discriminator Observations ($\Phi(s)$):** To make robust and task-agnostic style judgments, the discriminator receives a carefully selected set of features from the state transition:
 - Linear and angular velocity of the character's root (pelvis), in its local coordinate frame.
 - Local rotation of each joint (using a 6D normal-tangent encoding for spherical joints).
 - Local velocity of each joint.
 - 3D positions of end-effectors (hands, feet) in the character's local frame.
 These features represent the dynamics and posture changes across a single time step, capturing motion characteristics relevant to style.
- **Gradient Penalty:** To further stabilize the adversarial training and prevent "mode collapse" (where the generator only produces a limited subset of the desired behaviors), a gradient penalty [Gulrajani et al., 2017] is applied to the discriminator's objective. This penalizes large gradients on the data manifold, encouraging the discriminator to be smoother and more robust. The authors highlight this as a "crucial" component for stable training and effective performance.

#### 3.3. Policy Training with Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning

- **Policy Architecture:** The control policy $\pi(a_t|s_t, g)$ is a neural network (fully-connected with ReLU activations) that maps the current character state ($s_t$) and a given goal ($g$) to a Gaussian distribution over actions ($a_t$).
- **Actions:** Actions specify target positions for proportional-derivative (PD) controllers at each of the character's joints, which then generate control forces in the physics simulation.
- **RL Algorithm:** The policy and a separate value function are trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) [Schulman et al., 2017], a popular and robust on-policy RL algorithm.
- **Training Enhancements:**
 - **Reference State Initialization:** Characters are initialized to states randomly sampled from the reference motion dataset, helping the policy explore relevant states and learn faster.
 - **Early Termination:** Episodes can terminate early if the character falls (e.g., non-feet body parts contact the ground), or other task-specific criteria are met.
 - **Replay Buffer:** Trajectories generated by the policy are stored in a replay buffer. The discriminator samples batches from both the reference dataset and this replay buffer, which helps prevent the discriminator from overfitting to the most recent policy data and improves training stability.

#### 3.4. Characters and Environment

The system is demonstrated on diverse, complex 3D simulated characters: a 34 DoF humanoid, a 59 DoF T-Rex, and a 64 DoF dog. The simulations are run using the Bullet physics engine at a high frequency (1.2kHz), with policies queried at 30Hz.

This comprehensive methodology, particularly the robust adversarial training setup (LSGAN, gradient penalty, careful observation selection), enables AMP to learn from unstructured motion data and synthesize high-quality, physically-plausible, and stylistically distinct behaviors.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The results section thoroughly demonstrates the effectiveness and advantages of the AMP framework across a diverse suite of tasks and characters.

#### 4.1. High-Quality Stylized Motion and Automatic Skill Composition

- **Diverse Styles:** AMP successfully enables characters to perform tasks in a variety of distinct styles (e.g., "Locomotion," "Zombie," "Stealthy") simply by providing the motion prior with different unstructured datasets.
- **Automatic Gait Transitions:** For tasks like "Target Heading" and "Target Location," characters automatically learn to transition between different locomotion gaits (walking, jogging, running) based on the target speed or distance. This composition of skills emerges naturally from the motion prior without requiring a high-level motion planner or explicit clip selection, a significant improvement over prior tracking-based methods.
- **Novel Skill Composition:** Characters learn to compose entirely disparate skills that are not explicitly present together in the motion data. Examples include:
 - **Strike Task:** The character learns to walk to a target and then punch it, even though the dataset only contains separate walking-only or punching-only clips.
 - **Recovery Behaviors:** By adding "getting up" motions to the dataset, the character learns to recover from falls, even discovering novel strategies like tucking into a roll to quickly transition into a getup.
 - **Obstacle Traversal:** For obstacle courses, the character learns to leap over gaps and roll under overhead obstructions by composing relevant skills from the dataset. It can also traverse stepping stones with styles like cartwheeling or jumping.
- **Robustness Across Characters:** The framework is shown to be effective for complex non-humanoid characters like a 59 DoF T-Rex and a 64 DoF dog, learning distinct gaits like trotting and cantering.

#### 4.2. Comparisons to Alternative Approaches

- **Baseline (No Data):** Policies trained without motion data ("from scratch") produce significantly less life-like behaviors compared to those trained with AMP or latent space models.
- **Latent Space Models:** While latent space models (another approach to learning motion priors) achieve comparable task performance, AMP consistently produces *higher fidelity motions*. This is attributed to AMP's direct enforcement of motion style through the reward function, which better mitigates artifacts that can arise when latent space models specify encodings outside their pre-trained distribution. A key advantage of AMP is that it doesn't require a separate, sample-intensive pre-training phase for a low-level controller.
- **Single-Clip Imitation vs. Motion Tracking:** When evaluated on imitating individual motion clips (without additional task objectives), AMP achieves results of *comparable quality* to the state-of-the-art "Motion Tracking" approach (Peng et al., 2018a). This is a crucial finding because AMP achieves this *without* manually designed tracking objectives or the need for phase synchronization (which motion tracking requires). This demonstrates AMP's ability to produce highly dynamic and acrobatic skills with similar fidelity while being more generalizable. The primary trade-off is slightly slower learning and susceptibility to local optima for some complex motions (e.g., "Front-Flip") due to the lack of phase synchronization.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

The ablation studies highlight the importance of specific design choices for the stability and performance of AMP:
- **Gradient Penalty:** This is identified as the "most vital component." Without it, training is highly unstable, exhibiting large performance fluctuations and leading to noticeable visual artifacts in the final policies. Its inclusion significantly improves stability and learning speed.
- **Velocity Features in Discriminator Observations:** Essential for accurately imitating certain dynamic motions, such as rolling. Without these features, the character might default to simpler, less dynamic behaviors (e.g., holding a fixed pose on the ground instead of rolling). While pose changes implicitly contain velocity information, explicit velocity features are crucial for specific dynamic behaviors.

#### 4.4. Spatial Composition (Exploratory)

An additional experiment explores spatial composition (combining skills simultaneously, like walking and waving). While the policy learned to combine these actions, demonstrating some spatial composition capability, the quality was not always perfect, especially for challenging targets (e.g., high hand wave). This points to an area for future improvement but validates the framework's potential in this domain.

In summary, AMP represents a significant step forward in physics-based character animation, offering a powerful, automated, and flexible framework for generating high-fidelity, stylized, and task-performing behaviors from unstructured motion data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The AMP framework presents a significant advancement with profound implications for computer animation, robotics, and the broader field of reinforcement learning.

#### 5.1. Revolutionizing Character Animation Workflows

- **Reduced Manual Effort:** The most immediate and impactful contribution is the substantial reduction in manual effort required for creating realistic and versatile character animations. By obviating the need for manually designed imitation objectives, complex motion planners, and extensive data annotation, AMP streamlines the animation pipeline. This shifts the burden from meticulous hand-crafting or rule-based systems to a more automated, data-driven learning paradigm.
- **Unlocking Unstructured Data:** The ability to leverage large, unstructured datasets of motion capture and keyframe animations is transformative. Previously, such diverse data was challenging to utilize effectively in physics-based simulation due to the overhead of motion selection and synchronization. AMP makes these rich data sources readily accessible, allowing for a broader range of styles and behaviors to be learned.

#### 5.2. Enhanced Realism and Versatility in Virtual Worlds

- **Life-like and Responsive Characters:** The framework enables the creation of virtual characters that not only appear life-like but also respond dynamically and naturally to their environment and tasks. This is crucial for immersive experiences in video games, virtual reality (VR), and film, where users demand highly believable digital actors.
- **Decoupled Style and Task:** The clear separation of task objectives from stylistic priors offers unprecedented flexibility. Animators and developers can easily experiment with different motion styles for the same task, simply by swapping out the reference motion dataset, fostering creativity and rapid prototyping.

#### 5.3. Advancing Adversarial Imitation Learning

- **Practical Stability and High Fidelity:** The paper demonstrates that adversarial imitation learning, often criticized for its instability and lower quality results in high-dimensional continuous control, can indeed achieve state-of-the-art performance for complex, physics-based tasks. The specific design choices (LSGAN objective, gradient penalty, careful observation features) provide a blueprint for future research in stabilizing and improving GAN-based control.
- **Generalizable Objective Function:** By learning a task-agnostic motion prior, the system creates a reusable objective function for "naturalness" or "style." This is a step towards more modular and composable RL systems, where a learned prior can be plugged into various task-specific training regimes.

#### 5.4. Implications for Robotics

- **Learning Natural Robot Behaviors:** While the focus is on character animation, the underlying principles are highly relevant to robotics. Natural human motions implicitly encode safety, energy efficiency, and adaptability. AMP's ability to learn such characteristics from demonstration data could lead to more robust, energy-efficient, and human-like control strategies for humanoid or animal-inspired robots operating in complex environments.
- **Skill Transfer and Composition:** The automatic composition of disparate skills demonstrated in AMP (e.g., walking and punching) is a critical capability for general-purpose robots that need to perform a wide array of complex tasks.

#### 5.5. Future Research Directions

The paper itself highlights limitations and future avenues:
- **Mode Collapse:** Addressing the susceptibility to mode collapse (where the policy only imitates a subset of behaviors) for even larger and more diverse datasets remains an open challenge.
- **Transferable Motion Priors:** Developing truly general and transferable motion priors that don't need to be re-trained for every new task would further enhance modularity. This might involve training priors on extremely diverse data to reduce task dependencies.
- **Spatial Composition:** Further research into techniques that explicitly facilitate the *simultaneous* spatial composition of multiple skills could lead to even more sophisticated and flexible behaviors.

In conclusion, AMP represents a significant step towards creating more autonomous, intelligent, and lifelike virtual characters, simultaneously pushing the boundaries of reinforcement learning and adversarial methods in physically simulated environments. It provides a robust and practical tool that can bridge the gap between vast motion data archives and dynamic, responsive character control.
