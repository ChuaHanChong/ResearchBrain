# Retargeting Matters: General Motion Retargeting for Humanoid Motion Tracking

> [!summary] Summary
> A study from Stanford University's Department of Computer Science demonstrates that the quality of motion retargeting critically influences the performance and robustness of humanoid motion tracking policies. Their proposed General Motion Retargeting (GMR) method generates physically plausible and perceptually faithful reference motions, leading to improved policy success rates and reduced tracking errors compared to existing open-source approaches.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots struggle to robustly track human motion due to the 'embodiment gap' (differences in morphology, kinematics, and dynamics) between humans and robots.
- Existing motion retargeting methods introduce artifacts like ground penetration, self-intersection, and physically infeasible movements, which are often overlooked or assumed to be corrected by downstream reinforcement learning (RL) policies.
- The unproven assumption that RL policies can easily compensate for poor retargeting without extensive reward engineering leads to inefficient policy development and reduces robustness.

## Method

- Systematically evaluated the impact of various retargeting methods (PHC, ProtoMotions, Unitree) on the performance of humanoid motion tracking policies, specifically isolating the effect by ==suppressing excessive reward tuning==.
- Developed **GMR** (==General Motion Retargeting==), a novel ==two-stage optimization-based pipeline== that includes non-uniform local scaling and robustly handles physical constraints.
- Trained motion imitation policies using an independent framework (**BeyondMimic**) on retargeted data and evaluated their performance across diverse motions and rigorous simulated environments, including conditions mimicking real-world deployment.

## Results

- GMR consistently achieved higher success rates and significantly lower tracking errors compared to PHC and ProtoMotions, often matching or closely approaching the performance of a high-quality closed-source baseline (Unitree).
- Policies trained on GMR-retargeted data demonstrated enhanced robustness, particularly for dynamic and long motion sequences like 'Dance 1' and 'Run (stop & go)', where other methods often resulted in **0% success rates**.
- A user study confirmed that GMR-retargeted motions were perceived as more faithful to the original human movements than those generated by other open-source techniques, indicating high perceptual fidelity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The quality of retargeted motion is a fundamental upstream factor that critically impacts the success rates and robustness of humanoid motion tracking policies, particularly for dynamic and complex movements.
> - Specific physical artifacts in retargeted data, such as ground penetration, self-intersection, and sudden joint value jumps, severely hinder the ability of RL policies to learn and perform effectively.
> - Investing in high-quality motion retargeting can significantly reduce the need for laborious reward engineering and domain randomization, streamlining the development of more robust and generalizable humanoid controllers.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research paper addresses a critical, yet often underestimated, challenge in the rapidly evolving field of humanoid robotics: bridging the "embodiment gap" between humans and robots to enable robust and generalizable motion tracking. The broader landscape involves several interconnected areas:

- **Humanoid Motion Tracking and Control**: A major goal in robotics is to enable humanoid robots to perform complex, agile, and human-like movements. This is crucial for teleoperation, human-robot interaction, and deploying robots in unstructured environments. Recent advancements have heavily relied on imitation learning, particularly reinforcement learning (RL), where policies are trained to mimic reference human motion data.
- **Reinforcement Learning for Robotics**: RL has shown remarkable success in teaching robots complex skills. However, training effective RL policies for high-dimensional, continuous control tasks like whole-body humanoid motion often requires vast amounts of data and careful reward engineering. The current paradigm often involves training policies on motion data "retargeted" from humans to robot embodiments.
- **Motion Retargeting in Computer Graphics and Robotics**: Originating in computer graphics, motion retargeting is the process of transferring motion from a source character (e.g., a human) to a target character (e.g., a robot) with different kinematic structures, proportions, and joint limits. While sophisticated methods exist in graphics, their application to physics-based robot control introduces additional constraints related to physical plausibility, contact, and stability.
- **The Sim-to-Real Gap**: Policies trained in simulation must ultimately transfer to the real world. Artifacts in training data can exacerbate this "sim-to-real" gap, leading to unstable or unrealistic behaviors when deployed on physical robots. Prior work has often compensated for these artifacts through extensive reward shaping, domain randomization, and trial-and-error, treating retargeting as a secondary, somewhat solved problem.

This paper directly challenges the implicit assumption that downstream RL policies can simply "correct" for poor retargeting. It posits that the quality of the retargeted reference motion is a fundamental upstream factor that critically impacts policy performance, robustness, and ultimately, sim-to-real transfer, particularly when excessive reward tuning is suppressed. By systematically evaluating existing retargeting methods and proposing a novel one, GMR, the authors aim to elevate the importance of motion retargeting from a mere preprocessing step to a core component of the humanoid control pipeline. This work contributes to a growing trend in robotics research that emphasizes data quality, robust perception, and principled data processing as foundational elements for achieving truly generalizable and reliable robot behaviors.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation of this research stems from a fundamental challenge in humanoid robotics: the "embodiment gap" between human and robot morphologies. While imitation learning from human motion data has become a popular approach for training humanoid control policies, the direct application of human motion to robots is hindered by significant differences in bone length, joint ranges, kinematic structure, and mass distribution.

The primary solution to this embodiment gap has been **kinematic retargeting**, where human motion is adapted to the target humanoid's physical form. However, the authors identify a critical oversight in current practices: the artifacts introduced during the retargeting process are often either ignored or inadequately addressed. These artifacts include:
- **Foot sliding**: Robot feet not maintaining stable contact with the ground.
- **Ground penetration**: Robot parts sinking into the floor.
- **Self-penetration**: Different parts of the robot's body colliding with each other.
- **Physically infeasible motion**: Movements that violate physical laws or robot capabilities.

The conventional wisdom has been that sophisticated reinforcement learning (RL) policies, coupled with extensive reward engineering and domain randomization, can mitigate or correct these retargeting artifacts. The authors hypothesize that **without such extensive engineering efforts, the quality of retargeting results plays a significant and often underestimated role in policy performance and robustness.**

Based on this, the paper sets out to achieve the following key objectives:

1. **Systematically Evaluate Retargeting Impact**: Conduct rigorous experiments to determine how the quality of retargeted motion affects the performance of humanoid motion tracking policies, specifically when "excessive reward tuning is suppressed." This aims to isolate the true impact of retargeting quality.
2. **Identify Critical Retargeting Artifacts**: Pinpoint which specific types of artifacts in retargeted data most negatively impact an RL policy's ability to learn and perform effectively.
3. **Propose a Novel Retargeting Method (GMR)**: Develop "General Motion Retargeting (GMR)," a new method designed to address the identified shortcomings of existing open-source retargeting techniques and produce high-quality, physically plausible reference motions.
4. **Benchmark GMR Against State-of-the-Art**: Evaluate GMR against two prominent open-source methods (PHC and ProtoMotions) and a high-quality closed-source baseline (Unitree) across a diverse range of motions and rigorous evaluation conditions.
5. **Assess Perceptual Faithfulness**: Quantify the perceptual similarity of retargeted motions to the original human source, ensuring that policies learn the *intended* human-like behaviors rather than distorted variants.

The overarching motivation is to demonstrate that investing in high-quality motion retargeting upstream can significantly streamline the downstream policy learning process, reduce the need for laborious reward engineering, and ultimately lead to more robust, stable, and generalizable humanoid control policies that are better suited for real-world deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors employ a multi-faceted approach to systematically evaluate motion retargeting methods and introduce their novel technique, GMR.

**A. Retargeting Methods Under Study:**
The paper evaluates four distinct retargeting approaches:
1. **PHC (Perpetual Humanoid Control)**: A widely used method that fits a parametric human body model (SMPL) to the robot shape, then optimizes robot root pose and joint angles to minimize position error between the robot's forward kinematics and the target joint positions derived from the SMPL robot model. It uses gradient descent and includes a post-processing step for height adjustment.
2. **ProtoMotions**: An optimization-based method that scales the source motion using global axis-aligned factors and then uses a differential inverse kinematics (IK) solver (Mink) to minimize joint position and orientation errors between scaled human and robot key bodies. It also includes a height post-processing step.
3. **GMR (General Motion Retargeting)**: The proposed method, designed to address artifacts like deviation from source motion, foot sliding, ground penetrations, and self-intersections. Its core differences lie in its scaling procedure and a two-stage optimization.
4. **Unitree (Closed-Source)**: A high-quality proprietary retargeted dataset from Unitree, included as a strong baseline to gauge the performance of open-source methods.

**B. General Motion Retargeting (GMR) Pipeline:**
GMR is a sequential, optimization-based pipeline (see Fig. 2):
1. **Human-Robot Key Body Matching**: Defines a user-specified mapping between human and robot key bodies (torso, head, legs, feet, arms, hands) and allows for weighting of position/orientation tracking errors.
2. **Human-Robot Cartesian Space Rest Pose Alignment**: Offsets human body orientations to match robot orientations in rest pose and can apply local position offsets to mitigate artifacts (e.g., "toed-in").
3. **Human Data Non-Uniform Local Scaling**: This is a critical departure from prior methods. It calculates a general scaling factor based on source human height, then applies *custom local scale factors* for each key body (e.g., different for upper vs. lower body). Crucially, the root translation is scaled uniformly to prevent foot sliding.
4. **Solving Robot IK with Rotation Constraints (Stage 1 Optimization)**: To avoid local minima, a two-stage optimization is used. The first stage minimizes body orientation errors for all key bodies and position errors for end-effectors (hands and feet), subject to joint limits. It's initialized with scaled root position and yaw component of human root orientation. This uses Mink, a differential IK solver, to compute generalized velocities.
5. **Fine Tuning using Rotation & Translation Constraints (Stage 2 Optimization)**: The solution from Stage 1 is used as an initial guess. This stage minimizes both position and orientation errors for *all* key bodies with different weights, further refining the pose.

For motion sequences, GMR applies this process sequentially, using the previous frame's result as an initial guess. A final post-processing step adjusts the global translation to fix height artifacts.

**C. Data Processing:**
- **Dataset**: A diverse subset of 21 motions from the LAFAN1 dataset is used, ranging from simple walks to complex dances and martial arts, excluding motions with complex environment interactions.
- **Robot Target**: All motions are retargeted to a Unitree G1 robot.
- **Format Conversion**: LAFAN1 BVH files are converted to SMPL(-X) format for PHC and ProtoMotions by fitting SMPL(-X) shape parameters to the BVH skeleton and copying joint rotations.

**D. Motion Tracking Evaluation:**
- **Policy Training**: BeyondMimic is used to train individual motion imitation policies for each retargeted motion. BeyondMimic is chosen because it is developed independently of the retargeting methods and does not rely on reward tuning, thus isolating retargeting effects.
- **Evaluation Setups**: Policies are evaluated in three rigorous environments:
 - `sim`: IsaacSim (training simulator) without domain randomization (100 trials).
 - `sim-dr`: IsaacSim with domain randomization (4096 trials) to test robustness to variations.
 - `sim2sim`: MuJoCo/ROS simulator (100 trials), mimicking real-world deployment conditions with state estimation noise and ROS timing variations, without privileged simulator information.
- **Metrics**:
 - **Success Rate**: Ratio of rollouts where the policy completes the reference motion without the robot falling or deviating beyond a strict height/orientation threshold.
 - **Tracking Errors**: Average position error of body parts in global coordinates (E_g-mpbpe, mm), average position error of body parts relative to the root (E_mpbpe, mm), and average angular error of joint rotations (E_mpjpe, 10^-3 rad).

**E. User Study Evaluation:**
To assess perceptual faithfulness, 20 users were shown 5-second clips of original human reference motions (SMPL-X fit) alongside two retargeted clips (GMR vs. PHC/ProtoMotions/Unitree) in a randomized, blind comparison. Users selected which retargeted video was more similar to the reference, or indicated no difference.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The comprehensive evaluation yielded several key findings regarding the impact of retargeting quality on humanoid motion tracking performance:

1. **Retargeting Quality Critically Impacts Policy Performance (Q1)**: The experiments unequivocally demonstrate that the choice of retargeting method significantly affects the success rates and robustness of motion tracking policies. While many motions (14 out of 21) could be tracked with high success rates across all methods, a critical subset of 7 motions showed large variations in performance. This confirms the authors' hypothesis that retargeting quality matters, especially without extensive reward engineering.

2. **Impact on Robustness, Especially for Dynamic/Long Sequences**: Policies trained on retargeted data with artifacts showed reduced robustness, particularly for dynamic or longer sequences. For instance, PHC-retargeted "Dance 1" and "Dance 2" motions resulted in 0% success rates in all evaluation settings, whereas GMR and ProtoMotions achieved near-perfect scores for these long, complex sequences. Even for moderately difficult motions like "Walk 2" and "Turn 1," PHC showed significantly lower success rates compared to GMR and Unitree.

3. **Critical Artifacts Negatively Impact Learning (Q2)**: The study identified specific retargeting artifacts that pose significant challenges for RL policies:
 - **Ground Penetration**: PHC retargets of "Dance 1" and "Dance 2" suffered from severe ground penetration (up to 60 cm), leading to policy failures.
 - **Self-Intersection**: The ProtoMotions retarget of "Run (stop & go)" exhibited robot leg self-intersection, contributing to a substantial drop in success rate (20% vs. GMR's 98%).
 - **Sudden Jumps in Joint Values**: The GMR retarget of "Dance 5" occasionally showed abrupt spikes in waist roll values, which, although rare, reduced its success rate for that specific motion (96% vs. 100% for other methods). This suggests that while GMR generally excels, some edge cases might still require fine-tuning of optimization weights.
 These findings directly answer Q2, emphasizing that physical inconsistencies make it harder, or even impossible, for policies to learn effectively.

4. **GMR Outperforms Open-Source Methods and Approaches Unitree Baseline**:
 - **Success Rates**: GMR consistently achieved higher success rates than PHC and ProtoMotions across challenging motions, often matching or nearly matching the performance of the closed-source Unitree baseline. For example, in "Walk 2", GMR achieved 100% success while PHC had 23%. In "Run (stop & go)", GMR had 98% success compared to PHC's 17% and ProtoMotions' 20%.
 - **Tracking Errors**: GMR produced significantly lower tracking errors (E_g-mpbpe, E_mpbpe, E_mpjpe) compared to PHC and ProtoMotions. Its mean errors were consistently closer to the Unitree baseline, indicating greater faithfulness to the reference motion and more stable tracking by the policies. For instance, mean E_g-mpbpe for GMR was 104.1 mm, much lower than PHC (247.8 mm) and ProtoMotions (139.7 mm), and close to Unitree (77.2 mm).

5. **Perceptual Faithfulness (Q3)**: The user study (N=20) showed that:
 - Users perceived GMR retargets as more faithful to the original human motion than those from PHC and ProtoMotions.
 - Unitree retargets were considered slightly more faithful than GMR, but users had a harder time distinguishing between GMR and Unitree, suggesting GMR achieves a very high level of perceptual fidelity.

6. **Importance of Initial Reference Frame**: Consistent with prior work, the study reaffirmed that the starting frame of the reference motion can critically impact policy success. An unstable initial pose can lead to immediate policy failure, regardless of the retargeting method.

In summary, the results highlight that high-quality retargeting is not a luxury but a necessity for robust humanoid motion tracking, especially for dynamic and complex behaviors. GMR emerges as a strong, open-source contender, producing perceptually faithful and physically consistent motions that significantly improve policy learning outcomes.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research carries substantial significance and potential impact across the fields of robotics, computer graphics, and artificial intelligence, particularly concerning humanoid control and sim-to-real transfer.

1. **Elevating the Role of Motion Retargeting**: The most significant contribution is the emphatic demonstration that "retargeting matters." By systematically evaluating its impact without extensive reward engineering, the paper shifts the focus from solely optimizing downstream RL policies to critically examining the quality of upstream data processing. This encourages researchers to view motion retargeting not as a trivial preprocessing step but as a fundamental component that significantly influences the success, robustness, and stability of learned humanoid behaviors.

2. **Enabling More Robust and Generalizable Humanoid Control**: By identifying specific artifacts (ground penetration, self-intersection, sudden joint value jumps) that hinder policy learning, the paper provides clear guidelines for developing better retargeting methods. GMR, as a novel open-source solution, produces higher-quality, more physically plausible, and perceptually faithful reference motions. This means RL policies trained on GMR data are more likely to learn stable and human-like movements, leading to more capable and versatile humanoid robots.

3. **Reducing the Burden of Reward Engineering and Domain Randomization**: Current practices often rely on laborious reward shaping and extensive domain randomization to compensate for poor retargeting. This paper suggests that improving retargeting quality can significantly reduce this engineering overhead, making the development of humanoid controllers more efficient and accessible. Cleaner reference motions simplify the learning task for RL agents, allowing them to focus on imitating the desired motion rather than correcting fundamental physical inconsistencies.

4. **Improving Sim-to-Real Transfer**: Artifacts in simulated training data can exacerbate the "sim-to-real gap," making it difficult to deploy policies from simulation to real robots. By providing more physically consistent training data, GMR helps create a more faithful simulated environment, potentially leading to smoother and more reliable transfer of learned skills to physical hardware. The strong performance in the `sim2sim` (MuJoCo/ROS) evaluation, which mimics real-world deployment conditions, supports this potential.

5. **Advancing Open-Source Robotics Development**: The release of GMR's code (github.com/YanjieZe/GMR) as an open-source tool is a major impact. It provides the robotics community with a robust, high-performance retargeting solution that can be adopted, extended, and integrated into various humanoid control pipelines. This democratizes access to high-quality data generation, benefiting researchers who may not have access to proprietary methods like Unitree's.

6. **Setting New Benchmarks and Research Directions**: The comprehensive evaluation, including a user study for perceptual fidelity, sets a new standard for benchmarking motion retargeting methods in the context of robot control. Future work can build upon this framework, exploring other data sources (AMASS, video-based motion reconstruction), different humanoid platforms (Unitree H1, others), and more complex interaction-based motions, which were identified as limitations in this study.

In essence, "Retargeting Matters" fundamentally re-emphasizes the importance of quality data generation in robotics. It provides both a critical analysis of existing methods and a powerful new tool, GMR, to move the field closer to building truly agile, robust, and generalizable humanoid robots.
