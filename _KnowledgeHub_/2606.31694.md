# RCT: A Robot-Collected Touch-Vision-Language Dataset for Tactile Generalization

> [!summary] Summary
> The RCT dataset, collected by a robot, introduces a comprehensive touch-vision-language resource designed to rigorously evaluate tactile generalization by preserving full contact sequences and rich metadata. It reveals that common "frame-random" evaluation splits significantly overestimate model performance, with true generalization to unseen materials and sensor instances proving substantially more challenging, achieving only 20.4% tactile-to-text Recall@1 on held-out materials.

## Problem

- Existing tactile datasets and evaluation protocols often overestimate model generalization due to data leakage, where highly correlated frames or even identical materials appear in both training and test sets.
- A lack of structured datasets and evaluation frameworks makes it difficult to quantitatively assess a robot's ability to generalize tactile perceptions to novel materials and contact conditions.
- Robots require the capacity to reason about material properties through touch in unstructured environments, which is hindered by models that learn to identify near-duplicates rather than transferable representations.

## Method

- Introduction of **RCT** (Robotic Contact Tactile), a robot-collected dataset featuring 122 industrial materials, full ==contact sequences== with force readings, studio vision, and human/VLM language annotations.
- Definition of controlled ==held-out evaluation protocols== based on independent units like contact sequences, materials, categories, sensors, and contact positions to prevent data leakage.
- Adoption of a standard ==touch-vision-language (TVL)== training recipe to isolate the impact of the evaluation protocol on generalization metrics like ==Recall@1== and ==Recall@5==.

## Results

- Removing contact-sequence overlap reduced tactile-to-text Recall@1 from **80.01%** (frame-random) to **62.33%**, demonstrating a **17.7 percentage point** overestimation.
- Held-out material generalization (**K=20** unseen materials) achieved only **20.4%** tactile-to-text Recall@1 with RCT-only training, which did not improve with additional external data.
- Holding out a specific DIGIT sensor instance yielded low performance (**21.51%** tactile-to-text Recall@1), indicating sensor instance robustness is a distinct bottleneck.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Frame-random evaluation splits inflate tactile generalization metrics by up to 17.7 percentage points due to contact-sequence and material overlap between training and test sets.
> - Uniformly sampling frames across an entire robot press during training is more effective for contrastive learning than densely sampling near-duplicate frames, improving performance by 5.7 percentage points on held-out sequences.
> - Generalization to novel materials and robustness across different sensor instances remain significant open challenges, with performance plummeting to levels comparable to random chance in these held-out settings.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

Robots operating in unstructured environments necessitate the ability to reason about material properties through touch, which complements visual information by revealing characteristics such as compliance, texture, and deformability. Recent advancements in touch-vision-language (TVL) learning, utilizing sensors like DIGIT, have shown progress in creating multimodal embedding spaces for tactile retrieval and recognition. Existing datasets in this domain prioritize scale, multimodal alignment, or cross-sensor transfer. Examples include human-collected datasets, simulated multisensory objects, and large TVL datasets exceeding 100,000 entries. These efforts have facilitated the development of general-purpose tactile encoders and models for object-property reasoning.

However, a recurring challenge in machine learning, including tactile sensing, involves evaluating model generalization under deployment-relevant distribution shifts, rather than solely on independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) test splits. Prior tactile datasets typically allow for train/test sharing of the same materials and physical interactions, potentially overestimating generalization capabilities. Specifically, the highly correlated nature of sequential frames from a single robot press has been identified as a source of data leakage, where models might leverage near-duplicate observations rather than learning transferable representations. While cross-sensor domain gaps have been addressed with specific adaptation methods, the issue of within-interaction overlap has received less attention in quantitative evaluations. This paper introduces a dataset and evaluation protocols specifically designed to isolate and quantify generalization to previously unseen materials and contact conditions, thereby addressing a fundamental question in robotic tactile perception concerning the transferability of learned representations. It complements existing work by focusing on controlled evaluation of generalization through the preservation of full contact sequences and explicit control over data splits.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to investigate whether tactile representations generalize to materials and contact conditions a robot has not encountered during training. This objective is motivated by the critical need for robots to safely and effectively manipulate diverse objects in open-world environments, where reliance solely on previously observed contacts is insufficient.

The specific motivations and objectives include:
- **Addressing limitations of existing evaluation protocols:** Current tactile datasets and evaluation methods often utilize "frame-random" splits, which can place highly correlated frames from the same physical interaction into both training and test sets. This overlap may inflate performance metrics, suggesting generalization that the model does not possess. The authors contend that such splits do not accurately reflect a robot's ability to generalize to novel materials or contact sequences.
- **Quantifying the effect of data overlap:** A key motivation is to empirically demonstrate the impact of contact-sequence overlap and material overlap on tactile representation learning performance. The researchers aim to show how much performance metrics decline when these forms of overlap are explicitly removed.
- **Introducing a controlled dataset for generalization studies:** The paper's central objective is to present RCT (Robotic Contact Tactile), a dataset specifically designed to facilitate the study of tactile generalization. RCT preserves full contact sequences and includes metadata to enable controlled held-out evaluation across various physically meaningful units, such as materials, categories, sensors, contact positions, and contact sequences.
- **Developing robust evaluation protocols:** The authors aim to establish protocols for evaluating generalization that align with real-world robotic deployment challenges, where robots encounter novel materials and contact scenarios. This involves defining held-out settings that prevent data leakage and provide a more accurate assessment of a model's true generalization capacity.
- **Investigating optimal training strategies:** The research also seeks to provide insights into how tactile data, particularly contact sequences, can be effectively sampled during training to improve generalization. This includes exploring the trade-offs between dense sampling of near-duplicate frames and sparser sampling that covers a broader range of contact states within a sequence.
- **Benchmarking novel-material generalization:** By rigorously controlling evaluation conditions, the paper aims to identify novel-material generalization as a significant open challenge for robotic tactile perception, providing a benchmark for future research in this area.

The specific contributions outlined are:
1. **RCT dataset:** A robot-collected touch–vision–language dataset featuring full contact sequences, material-level vision and language annotations, and metadata for controlled held-out evaluation.
2. **Evaluation protocols:** Defined protocols for evaluating generalization across materials, categories, sensors, contact positions, and robot presses.
3. **Empirical evidence:** Demonstration that frame-random splits overestimate tactile generalization due to mixing contact-sequence and material overlap.
4. **Training insight:** A finding that uniform sampling of a press improves contrastive training.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces the RCT dataset and defines an evaluation framework to assess tactile generalization.

**Dataset Collection Protocol:**
- **Robotic Setup:** Data was collected using a robot arm equipped with a rotating adapter. This adapter held three instances of the DIGIT vision-based tactile sensor, allowing each material to be recorded with all three sensors under standardized conditions.
- **Pressing Procedure:** For each material, the robot executed presses at multiple discrete contact positions. Each press was sampled from initial contact to deeper indentation, typically generating 15–17 tactile frames per contact sequence at fixed 0.10 mm depth steps. Contact force was recorded for each frame.
- **Materials:** RCT comprises 122 industrial reference materials from the Musterkiste set (Modulor GmbH), categorized into 7 top-level groups: Plastic/Rubber (33), Paper/Cardboard (45), Metal (20), Textiles/Leather (10), Wood/Bamboo/Cork (7), Crafts (4), and Small Items (3).
- **Scale:** The dataset contains a total of 29,279 tactile frames.
- **Structural Unit:** The core organizing principle of RCT is the "contact sequence," defined as the ordered tactile frames recorded during a single robot press on one material, with one sensor, at one contact position. This structure is central to enabling controlled held-out evaluation.

**Modalities and Annotations:**
- **Tactile:** The primary data consists of DIGIT sensor frames and associated contact force readings. Each frame is indexed by material, category, sensor, contact position, sequence, and indentation depth.
- **Vision:** Each material is associated with one high-resolution studio photograph (2048 × 1536 pixels). This design means many tactile frames correspond to a single material image.
- **Language:** Material-level perceptual descriptors were collected from two independent human annotators who physically touched all 122 materials and selected terms from a fixed vocabulary. An additional set of descriptors was generated using a vision-language model (Gemma-3-27B-IT) from the material photographs. The default language target for evaluation is the union of the two human annotations. Human-VLM agreement was observed to be lower than human-human agreement.

**Evaluation Protocol:**
- **Central Unit:** The contact sequence serves as the fundamental unit for defining independent test sets, acknowledging that frames within a sequence are highly correlated.
- **Held-out Settings:** The paper defines several held-out evaluation settings to isolate different aspects of generalization:
 - **Held-out material (K=2, 5, 20):** All contact sequences of K selected materials are withheld from training. This is the primary setting for assessing generalization to unseen materials. Test materials are selected to maximize descriptor diversity.
 - **Held-out category:** All materials from a specific top-level category are withheld.
 - **Held-out contact sequence:** Entire contact sequences are withheld, but the materials themselves may appear in the training set through other contact sequences. This isolates the effect of sequence overlap.
 - **Held-out contact position:** One contact position across materials is withheld.
 - **Held-out sensor:** Data from one DIGIT sensor instance is withheld.
 - **Frame-random control:** Random frames are selected for the test set, allowing material and contact-sequence overlap with the training set. This serves as a baseline to quantify the inflation caused by data leakage.
- **Frame Sampling at Test Time:** To control for the effect of adjacent frames, evaluations are conducted using different frame densities: "full" (all frames, ∼16), "uniform5" (five evenly spaced frames), and "deep5" (five deepest frames).
- **Model Architecture:** A standard TVL training recipe is adopted to isolate the evaluation protocol's effect. It employs a ViT-Base as the tactile encoder and OpenCLIP ViT-L/14 for vision and text encoders.
- **Training Parameters:** Models are trained with a batch size of 256 for 200 epochs, including a 10-epoch warmup, a base learning rate of 1.5×10⁻⁴, weight decay of 0.05, and a cosine learning-rate schedule. InfoNCE losses are used across modality pairs.
- **Metrics:** Recall@1 and Recall@5 are reported. For tactile→vision, a material-level multi-positive criterion is used, treating all tactile samples linked to the same material image as valid positives. For tactile→text, TVL's soft-label criterion based on text–text cosine similarity (threshold 0.6356) is applied.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experimental evaluation aimed to assess tactile generalization under various held-out conditions using the RCT dataset.

- **TVL Reproduction Sanity Check:** The implemented model reproduced TVL's reported tactile→vision Recall@1 on the combined SSVTP+HCT dataset, achieving 82.21% compared to TVL's 81.7%, indicating a consistent experimental setup.

- **Contact-Sequence Overlap Inflates Frame-Random Evaluation:**
 - Removing contact-sequence overlap (evaluating on held-out contact sequences using a frame-random-trained encoder) reduced tactile→text Recall@1 from 80.01% (frame-random control) to 62.33%. This represents a 17.7 percentage point (pp) drop, attributed to the removal of near-duplicate physical interactions between train and test sets.
 - An encoder trained directly under the held-out-contact-sequence protocol achieved similar performance (62.44%), confirming that the performance drop was due to the evaluation protocol rather than a weaker representation.
 - Further holding out materials (held-out-material setting) resulted in a sharp decline from 62.33% to 20.35%, indicating that novel materials pose a significant challenge.
 - When adjacent near-duplicate frames were subsampled at test time (Uniform5 and Deep5), the frame-random gain decreased from 17.7 pp to 12.3 pp and 10.4 pp, respectively. This suggests that while immediate neighbors contribute to the effect, models also exploit broader sequence-specific patterns.

- **Audit of Released TVL/HCT Split:**
 - An audit of the publicly available TVL/HCT split revealed that all 279 HCT test contact sequences were also present in the training set.
 - The median nearest same-sequence training frame was found to be one step away from the test frame.
 - A raw-pixel L2 distance analysis showed that in 98.3% of HCT test cases, the nearest same-sequence training frame was closer than any cross-sequence frame, with a median cross/same distance ratio of 2.6×. This indicates that a training-free raw-pixel nearest-neighbor baseline could recover the correct contact sequence with high accuracy (98.3%), exploiting the inherent overlap.

- **Uniformly Sampling a Press Improves Training:**
 - Comparing training-time frame sampling strategies on a held-out-contact-sequence test pool, "Uniform5" (five evenly spaced frames across the press) achieved 68.18% tactile→text Recall@1.
 - This performance was 5.7 pp higher than "full" density training (using all ∼16 frames) and 8.7 pp higher than "Deep5" (using only the five deepest frames), despite Uniform5 using only one-third of the frames of full-density training.
 - This suggests that covering the contact sequence from shallow to deep contact is more beneficial for contrastive learning than densely sampling near-duplicate frames.

- **Held-out-Material Generalization Remains Difficult:**
 - In the held-out-material setting (K=20 unseen materials), RCT-only training achieved 20.4% tactile→text Recall@1 (averaged 25.1 ± 6.1% over three held-out draws).
 - Held-out category performance was similarly challenging.
 - Adding SSVTP+HCT data to the training set did not improve held-out-material generalization on RCT, suggesting that novel-material generalization remains an unresolved challenge.

- **RCT Improves Material Structure in Embedding Space:**
 - The RCT encoder exhibited a higher material-separability margin (0.297 for K=20) compared to the released TVL encoder (0.048), indicating improved clustering of same-material embeddings and separation from other materials.
 - This improved structure translated to stronger downstream category recognition: a linear probe on RCT embeddings achieved 49.8% accuracy on unseen materials, compared to 34.2% for the TVL encoder and 37.8% for a random-initialized encoder.
 - Binary hard/soft prediction remained challenging, staying below the majority baseline for all learned representations and even for raw force features, suggesting that category-level material information transfers better than subtle properties like hardness.

- **Held-out Sensor Performance:**
 - Holding out a contact position (materials seen at other positions) resulted in high performance (65.46% tactile→text R@1), indicating robustness to contact location.
 - However, holding out a specific DIGIT sensor instance (materials seen with other sensors) yielded low performance (21.51% tactile→text R@1), comparable to held-out-material generalization. This suggests that sensor instance robustness is a distinct bottleneck, as models do not readily generalize across calibration differences between sensor instances.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The RCT dataset and its associated evaluation protocols offer several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of robotic tactile perception and multimodal representation learning.

**Refined Evaluation and Benchmarking:**
- **Clarifying Generalization:** The work provides empirical evidence demonstrating that "frame-random" splits in tactile datasets significantly overestimate generalization capabilities by allowing overlap of contact sequences and materials between training and test sets. By introducing "contact sequence" as the fundamental independent unit for evaluation, RCT enables a more accurate and interpretable assessment of whether tactile representations genuinely transfer to new physical interactions.
- **Reproducible Held-Out Evaluation:** RCT makes contact-sequence-aware and held-out-material evaluation reproducible. This is crucial for developing robotic systems that can operate reliably on unfamiliar objects in real-world scenarios, where encountering unseen materials is common. The provided metadata and split generation toolkit facilitate standardized benchmarking.
- **Identification of Core Challenges:** The sharp drop in performance observed under held-out-material and held-out-sensor conditions highlights that novel-material generalization and cross-sensor robustness are central, unresolved challenges for robotic tactile perception. This redirects research efforts towards these difficult yet critical areas.

**Guidance for Dataset Design and Training Practices:**
- **Recommendations for Benchmarking:** The study advocates for future tactile benchmarks to explicitly report the level of independence between training and test samples (e.g., contact sequence, material level) alongside any frame-random scores. This promotes transparency and prevents misinterpretation of retrieval accuracy as true generalization.
- **Optimized Training Strategies:** The finding that uniform sampling of frames across a press improves contrastive training compared to dense sampling of near-duplicates suggests more efficient data utilization. This can lead to the development of better training methodologies that prioritize coverage of the contact sequence over mere data volume, potentially reducing data collection efforts or improving model efficiency.

**Impact on Robotic Manipulation:**
- **Towards Trustworthy Robots:** By exposing the limitations of current generalization capabilities, RCT pushes the field towards developing tactile representations that robots can truly "trust" on unfamiliar materials. This is a prerequisite for advancing safe, robust, and general-purpose manipulation capabilities in unstructured environments.
- **Enabling New Research Directions:** The dataset and protocols enable new avenues for method development. Researchers can explore models that explicitly process full contact sequences, incorporate temporal or indentation order, or learn depth-aware contrastive objectives. The various held-out splits (sensor, position, category) allow for systematic investigation into which invariances tactile encoders learn and which remain brittle.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
- The study acknowledges limitations in its scope, such as using industrial reference materials rather than arbitrary object geometries, and a specific robot-press protocol. It also notes that some physical properties, like binary hard/soft prediction, remain challenging, suggesting a need for richer interaction signals or more targeted labels.
- The audit of the TVL/HCT split is presented as an evaluation-protocol result, not a criticism of multimodal learning frameworks themselves, which RCT itself employs successfully.

In conclusion, RCT significantly advances the methodology for evaluating tactile representation learning, establishing a robust framework for assessing generalization and identifying critical challenges that must be addressed for truly capable robotic tactile perception. Its impact lies in guiding future research towards more generalizable and reliable tactile AI for real-world robotic applications.
