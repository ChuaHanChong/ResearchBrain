# In-Context Multi-Objective Optimization

> [!summary] Summary
> TAMO, a fully amortized transformer-based framework from Aalto University, addresses multi-objective black-box optimization by pre-training a single policy on diverse synthetic tasks. It proposes new designs 50 to 1000 times faster than traditional MOBO, achieving competitive Pareto front approximations and demonstrating generalization across varying problem dimensionalities.

## Problem

- Traditional Multi-objective Bayesian Optimization (MOBO) incurs significant computational overhead and latency due to repeated fitting of statistical surrogates and optimization of acquisition functions for each new task and iteration.
- MOBO methods often lack transferability and generalization, requiring problem-specific modeling choices that limit their application to new, unseen problems and variations in input/output dimensions.
- Many existing MOBO acquisition functions are myopic, optimizing for single-step improvements, which can be suboptimal for discovering complex Pareto fronts that require multi-step planning.

## Method

- A fully ==amortized transformer== architecture, **TAMO**, is developed to be dimension-agnostic for both input and output spaces, using learnable scalar-to-vector embedders and dimension-specific positional tokens.
- The model is pre-trained offline using reinforcement learning (REINFORCE) on a diverse distribution of synthetic multi-objective tasks, framed as a Markov Decision Process maximizing cumulative normalized hypervolume.
- During inference, TAMO proposes new designs with a single forward pass by assigning acquisition utilities to candidate queries via a policy head, eliminating per-task surrogate fitting and acquisition function engineering.

## Results

- TAMO achieved proposal times approximately **50** to **1000** times lower than GP-based MOBO methods, demonstrating a substantial reduction in cumulative inference time on synthetic and real-world tasks.
- The framework maintained competitive or superior Pareto quality (simple regret) compared to strong MOBO baselines across a range of synthetic benchmarks and a real-world oil sorbent problem.
- TAMO successfully generalized to unseen input and output dimensionalities, performing comparably to the strongest GP baselines in these out-of-distribution settings while retaining its significant speed advantage.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Amortizing the entire multi-objective optimization process with a transformer backbone and reinforcement learning enables orders-of-magnitude faster design proposals by eliminating iterative per-task computations.
> - A dimension-agnostic embedding and transformer architecture, utilizing learnable positional tokens, allows effective generalization to tasks with varying input and output dimensionalities without architectural changes.
> - Training the policy with a cumulative reward objective over full optimization trajectories encourages the learning of non-myopic strategies, leading to more effective long-horizon discovery of Pareto fronts.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Multi-objective optimization (MOO), where multiple conflicting objectives must be balanced, is a pervasive challenge across various scientific and engineering disciplines, including drug design, experimental science, and autonomous systems. For expensive black-box problems in these domains, Multi-objective Bayesian Optimization (MOBO) is a standard sample-efficient paradigm. MOBO typically involves fitting probabilistic surrogates, often Gaussian Processes (GPs), and iteratively selecting new designs by maximizing an acquisition function that balances exploration and exploitation to improve a chosen multi-objective utility (e.g., hypervolume).

Despite its effectiveness, MOBO presents several practical limitations. These include the necessity for problem-specific choices of surrogates and acquisition functions, which rarely transfer to new tasks, leading to significant refitting overhead. This overhead can bottleneck decision latency in parallel or time-sensitive applications. Furthermore, most MOBO acquisition functions are myopic, optimizing for one-step gains, which may be suboptimal for discovering complex Pareto fronts that require multi-step planning.

This work addresses these limitations by introducing a fully amortized optimization approach. Amortized optimization seeks to shift computational burden offline by pre-training a model on a distribution of related optimization tasks. Once trained, the model can propose new designs with a single forward pass, reducing inference time significantly. Recent research has explored amortized Bayesian optimization for single-objective problems and, to a lesser extent, for multi-objective settings. Previous efforts, such as BOFormer, have partially amortized MOBO by optimizing the acquisition function while still relying on task-specific GP surrogates and being restricted to a fixed number of objectives, which limits transferability across heterogeneous problems.

TAMO, the method proposed in this paper, aims to provide an end-to-end, dimension-agnostic solution for MOO. It distinguishes itself by being simultaneously multi-objective, fully amortized (eliminating per-task surrogate fitting and acquisition engineering), and capable of cross-dimensional transfer for both input and output spaces. This positions TAMO as a potential foundation-style optimizer, capable of leveraging diverse pretraining data and adapting to new problems without requiring task-specific retraining, thereby addressing a notable gap in the existing amortized black-box optimization literature.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a universal and efficient policy for multi-objective black-box optimization that overcomes the common drawbacks of traditional MOBO methods. This overarching goal is driven by several key motivations:

1. **Reduce Computational Overhead and Latency:** Traditional MOBO requires fitting statistical surrogates (e.g., Gaussian Processes) and optimizing acquisition functions for each new problem, and often at each iteration. This iterative process can be computationally intensive and time-consuming, particularly in scenarios requiring rapid decisions, parallel evaluations, or high-throughput experimentation. The authors aim to minimize this "online" computational cost by developing a fully amortized policy that can propose new designs almost instantaneously after offline pre-training.

2. **Enhance Transferability and Generalization:** MOBO's reliance on problem-specific modeling choices (e.g., kernel functions, likelihoods, acquisition functions) often necessitates expert tuning and limits the transferability of learned strategies to new, unseen problems. The goal is to create a model that is "universal," capable of operating across diverse optimization tasks without requiring architectural modifications or retraining. This includes handling variations in both the dimensionality of the input design space and the number of output objectives. Such a capability would enable practitioners to leverage aggregated heterogeneous datasets for pre-training and apply a single optimizer to a wide range of problems.

3. **Address Myopic Decision-Making:** Many existing acquisition functions in MOBO are designed to optimize a one-step improvement in multi-objective utility. However, discovering an optimal Pareto frontier often requires a sequence of interdependent decisions and a longer planning horizon. The research aims to develop a policy that incorporates multi-step planning by training with reinforcement learning to maximize cumulative rewards over entire trajectories, thereby encouraging long-horizon gains in Pareto quality.

To achieve these objectives, the authors introduce TAMO, which seeks to:
- Develop a transformer-based architecture capable of processing diverse input and objective dimensions.
- Pre-train this architecture using reinforcement learning on a wide range of synthetic multi-objective tasks.
- Enable the pre-trained model to propose the next design with a single forward pass during inference, directly from the observed history.
- Demonstrate that this approach can significantly reduce proposal time while maintaining or improving the quality of Pareto front approximations under constrained evaluation budgets.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TAMO (Task-Agnostic Amortized Multi-Objective Optimization) is presented as a fully amortized framework employing a transformer architecture. The methodology centers on pre-training a single model offline on a diverse set of synthetic multi-objective tasks, enabling it to propose new designs in context with a single forward pass during deployment.

**Pretraining Data Construction:**
TAMO is pre-trained on a distribution of synthetic multi-objective optimization tasks, denoted `p(τ)`. Each task `τ` is defined by a black-box function `f_τ` where both input (`d_τ_x`) and output (`d_τ_y`) dimensions vary. The generative process for `p(τ)` is based on Gaussian Process (GP) priors, incorporating varied kernels, standard deviations, and length scales, along with optional multi-task GP structures to introduce output correlations.
During each training step, two distinct mini-batches are sampled:
- **Policy-learning batches:** Comprise a history set `D_h` (observed input-output pairs) and a query set `D_q` (candidate input designs). The model learns to select the most promising query from `D_q` conditioned on `D_h`.
- **Prediction batches:** Consist of a context set `D_c` (input-output pairs) and a target set `D_p` (input designs). These are used for an auxiliary in-context regression task to facilitate function landscape learning.

**Model Architecture:**
TAMO's architecture uses a shared transformer backbone for both prediction and optimization tasks. It consists of four main components:
1. **Dimension-agnostic embedder:** This component maps individual scalar input features and objective values to a fixed-dimension vector space (`R^d_e`) using learnable scalar-to-vector feedforward neural networks. After concatenating input and output embeddings, learnable dimension-specific positional tokens are applied element-wise. These tokens are sampled from fixed pools for each batch, allowing the model to distinguish between dimensions despite permutation invariance. Finally, the embeddings are mean-pooled across the `d_τ_x + d_τ_y` token axis to yield a single representation `E` of fixed dimension. This design ensures that the cost scales with the number of observations, not the dimensionality.
2. **Transformer encoder–decoder:** The backbone consists of `B` transformer layers, split into two phases. The first `B1` layers allow observed history/context tokens (`D_h`/`D_c`) to self-attend, and query/target tokens (`D_q`/`D_p`) to cross-attend with the history/context. This phase is responsible for information transfer. In the final `B2` layers, the sequence is reduced to only query/target tokens and a small set of task-specific tokens. An attention mask restricts query/target tokens to attend only to these task-specific tokens, ensuring constant overhead irrespective of varying `d_τ_x` and `d_τ_y`.
3. **Task-specific tokens:** These are introduced at the start of the `B2` layers to guide the decoder's computation. For prediction tasks, tokens include a `<PRED>` token and an output-index positional token. For optimization tasks, tokens include an `<OPT>` token, a time-budget token, and an aggregated input-dimension token.
4. **Heads:** Two distinct heads share the transformer backbone:
 - **Prediction head:** For prediction tasks, it outputs the parameters of a K-component univariate Gaussian mixture model for each scalar target `y_p_i,k`, given the target tokens and output-index positional token.
 - **Policy head:** For optimization tasks, it assigns a scalar acquisition utility to each query in `D_q` via an MLP. These utilities are then transformed into a categorical policy over the query set using a softmax function.

**Policy Learning:**
The multi-objective optimization problem is framed as a Markov Decision Process (MDP).
- **State:** At time `t`, the state comprises the current historical observations `D_h`, the current step `t`, and the total budget `T`.
- **Action:** The policy selects an index corresponding to a candidate `x_t` from the query set `D_q`.
- **Reward:** After evaluating `x_t` to obtain `y_t`, the reward `r_t` is defined as the normalized hypervolume, calculated as the ratio of the current Pareto set's hypervolume to the optimal task-specific hypervolume. This provides a scale-invariant measure of Pareto quality.
The policy `π_θ` is trained to maximize the expected discounted return using the REINFORCE algorithm.

**Training and Inference:**
Training involves two phases:
1. **Warm-up:** The model backbone is initially trained on the prediction task by minimizing negative log-likelihood, which encourages accurate in-context regression and the learning of robust representations.
2. **Joint training:** After warm-up, the model is trained to optimize a combined objective: the prediction loss and the policy learning objective (maximizing expected discounted return). The coefficient `λ_p` (set to 1.0) balances these two signals. This joint training, using full trajectories, directly rewards long-horizon improvements.
During inference, TAMO iteratively selects the query `x_t` with the highest probability from the policy's softmax output, evaluates it, updates the history, and repeats until the budget `T` is exhausted. This process involves a single forward pass per proposal.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of TAMO involved comparisons against strong MOBO baselines, including decomposition-based (qNParEGO), indicator-based (qNEHVI, qHVKG), sequence-modeling MOBO (BOFormer), and random search. Performance was assessed using hypervolume-based simple regret and wall-clock proposal time.

**Performance on Synthetic and Real-World Tasks:**
- **Pareto Quality:** TAMO demonstrated competitive or superior simple regret across a range of synthetic multi-objective benchmarks. On in-distribution tasks (e.g., GP-DX2–DY2), TAMO performed comparably to the best GP-based baselines. For out-of-distribution synthetic problems (e.g., Ackley–Rastrigin, Ackley–Rosenbrock), TAMO often achieved the strongest performance. An exception was Branin–Currin, where some conventional MOBO methods performed slightly better, potentially due to objective functions characterized by longer length scales not adequately covered in the pretraining corpus. TAMO also showed competitive performance in single-objective Bayesian optimization settings. On the real-world oil sorbent problem, TAMO was competitive, sometimes exhibiting the best performance.
- **Wall-Clock Time:** A primary finding was the significant reduction in cumulative inference time. TAMO's proposal time was approximately 50 to 1000 times lower than GP-based MOBO methods. This efficiency stems from its single-forward-pass inference mechanism, which bypasses the repeated surrogate refitting and acquisition function optimization overhead of traditional approaches. Even BOFormer, which partially amortizes the acquisition function, remained noticeably slower than TAMO due to its continued reliance on a GP surrogate.

**Generalization Capabilities:**
- **Out-of-Distribution Dimensionalities:** TAMO, pretrained on tasks with limited input and output dimensions (`d_x ∈ {1, 2}`, `d_y ∈ {1, 2, 3}`), exhibited generalization to unseen dimensionalities. On synthetic tasks with `d_x=3` and `d_y ∈ {2, 3}`, TAMO achieved regret comparable to the strongest GP baselines. On the real-world LaserPlasma task (`d_x=4, d_y=3`), TAMO outperformed BOFormer but slightly lagged behind conventional MOBO methods in regret. Crucially, in all these OOD dimensionality tests, TAMO maintained its advantage in cumulative inference time by orders of magnitude.
- **Decoupled Observations:** The model demonstrated flexibility in handling decoupled observation settings, where objectives can be measured independently (e.g., partial feedback). A decoupled variant of TAMO generally tracked the performance of its coupled counterpart on several synthetic tasks, suggesting it can accommodate partial-feedback acquisition without specific retraining. Performance degradation was observed on Ackley–Rosenbrock, attributed to the distinct optimal regions of its objectives, which makes single-objective measurements less informative for joint optimization.

**Ablation Studies:**
- **Batch Size (q):** Smaller batch sizes (q=1) generally led to faster regret reduction. While larger batch sizes (q > 1) resulted in a modest slowdown in regret improvement due to the lack of real feedback within a batch, the degradation was limited. This indicates that for parallel evaluations, using q > 1 could effectively reduce wall-clock time at a minimal accuracy cost.
- **Query Set Size (N_q):** TAMO's regret performance was largely insensitive to variations in query set size (N_q ∈ {256, 512, 1024, 2048}), except for specific problems like Branin–Currin where a small N_q might miss promising regions. Cumulative inference time scaled approximately linearly with N_q, but even at the largest N_q, TAMO remained significantly faster than baselines.
- **Prediction Task and Discount Factor:** The auxiliary prediction warm-up and the inclusion of a prediction term in the policy training loss were found to be influential for performance. The policy's performance was relatively robust to the specific weight of the prediction term (`λ_p`) and the discount factor (`γ`).
- **Pretraining Horizon and Model Size:** Longer pretraining horizons (non-myopic) generally yielded better performance than myopic variants. Larger model sizes consistently achieved lower regret, particularly on more complex tasks.
- **Pretraining Data Composition:** Performance was sensitive to the composition of the synthetic pretraining corpus. For instance, a prior with shorter length scales degraded performance, while a quadratic-bowl prior improved performance on some tasks but hindered others, highlighting the impact of synthetic dataset design on transfer capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents TAMO as a framework that advances the field of black-box optimization by addressing key limitations of traditional multi-objective Bayesian optimization (MOBO). The development of a fully amortized, dimension-agnostic policy with a transformer backbone offers several significant contributions and potential impacts:

1. **Accelerated Scientific Discovery and Engineering Design:** The most prominent impact is the substantial reduction in proposal time (50 to 1000 times faster) compared to conventional MOBO methods. This efficiency can accelerate iteration cycles in domains where objective evaluations are expensive or time-sensitive, such as drug discovery, materials science, and experimental optimization in closed-loop laboratory settings. By providing near-instantaneous design proposals, TAMO can facilitate high-throughput campaigns and rapid decision-making, potentially speeding up research and development pipelines.

2. **Towards Foundation Models for Optimization:** TAMO's ability to operate across varying input and output dimensions and generalize to unseen tasks without retraining positions it as a step towards "foundation-style" or "plug-and-play" optimizers. Such models could be pre-trained on vast and heterogeneous datasets, serving as universal engines for black-box optimization that are readily applicable to new problems without requiring extensive per-task engineering or expert knowledge in surrogate modeling and acquisition function design. This could democratize access to advanced optimization techniques.

3. **Enhanced Transferability and Data Utilization:** The dimension-agnostic architecture enables effective transfer learning by allowing the pooling of diverse legacy datasets for pre-training, even if they originate from problems with different input and output dimensionalities. This capability can lead to improved performance, especially in data-scarce regimes for new tasks, by leveraging collective experience from a broad range of previously solved or simulated problems.

4. **Addressing Myopia in Optimization:** By training with reinforcement learning to maximize cumulative hypervolume improvement over full trajectories, TAMO inherently learns non-myopic strategies. This allows for more effective discovery of Pareto fronts that require multi-step planning, potentially leading to higher-quality solutions compared to methods optimizing one-step gains.

5. **Flexibility and Extensibility:** The modular design of TAMO invites future extensions. It can potentially be adapted to handle black-box constraints, cost-aware optimization, and multi-fidelity settings. Furthermore, its architecture may accommodate alternative data modalities, such as preferential feedback or multi-fidelity observations, with minor modifications. Future work also includes scaling to higher-dimensional design spaces, for example, through factorized policies or generative proposal mechanisms, moving beyond discrete candidate pools.

In summary, TAMO represents an advancement in amortized black-box optimization, offering a path toward more efficient, generalizable, and user-friendly optimization tools that can significantly impact various scientific and engineering workflows.
