# MCP: Learning Composable Hierarchical Control with Multiplicative Compositional Policies

> [!summary] Summary
> Multiplicative Compositional Policies (MCP), developed by UC Berkeley researchers, enable reinforcement learning agents to compose pre-trained skills multiplicatively, significantly outperforming baselines and improving sample efficiency on complex multi-degree-of-freedom control tasks. The approach allows for the simultaneous activation of multiple learned behaviors, enabling more expressive and coordinated actions.

## Problem

- Learning complex behaviors from scratch in reinforcement learning is computationally expensive and sample-inefficient for agents with many degrees of freedom.
- Traditional hierarchical reinforcement learning approaches limit expressivity by selecting only one lower-level skill at a time.
- This limitation prevents the coordinated execution of multiple skills simultaneously, which is often required for complex, multi-degree-of-freedom control problems like a character walking while carrying an object.

## Method

- Proposes **Multiplicative Compositional Policies (MCP)** where an agent's policy is modeled as a ==multiplicative composition== of multiple ==primitive policies==.
- Each primitive is a neural network mapping states to a ==Gaussian distribution== over actions, and the final composite policy is formed by multiplying these distributions, weighted by a task-specific ==gating function==.
- Primitives are pre-trained through ==imitation learning== from motion capture data, and for new tasks, only the gating function is learned, keeping the pre-trained primitives fixed.

## Results

- Multiplicative Compositional Policies (MCP) significantly outperformed baseline methods on complex tasks such as carrying and dribbling, especially with more complex character morphologies (e.g., Humanoid, T-Rex).
- MCP demonstrated improved sample efficiency, learning high-performance policies with fewer environment interactions and converging faster than other approaches.
- The method showed stronger transfer capabilities, effectively leveraging pre-trained skills for novel tasks compared to other transfer learning techniques.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Modeling policy as a multiplicative composition of primitives allows simultaneous activation and coordination of skills, overcoming limitations of traditional discrete skill selection.
> - When primitives are Gaussian, the composite policy is also Gaussian with analytically computable parameters, making the approach computationally efficient.
> - Primitives naturally specialize to distinct aspects of behavior, and a learned gating network coordinates their contributions for complex tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Developing artificial agents that can learn complex behaviors from experience is a central challenge in reinforcement learning (RL). While RL has achieved impressive results across various domains, learning sophisticated skills from scratch remains computationally expensive and sample-inefficient. This is particularly evident in complex control problems involving simulated characters with many degrees of freedom.

Researchers from UC Berkeley have proposed a novel approach called Multiplicative Compositional Policies (MCP) that addresses these challenges by enabling agents to learn reusable skills that can be composed to tackle new tasks. Unlike traditional methods that typically select a single primitive action at each timestep, MCP allows for the simultaneous activation of multiple primitives through multiplicative composition, significantly expanding the range of behaviors an agent can express.

### 2. The Challenge of Skill Composition

Traditional hierarchical reinforcement learning approaches typically decompose complex tasks into simpler subtasks, with higher-level policies selecting which lower-level skill to execute at each timestep. While effective for discrete decision-making, this approach limits expressivity when controlling systems with multiple degrees of freedom that require coordinated actions.

For example, when controlling a humanoid character, separate skills might exist for walking and carrying an object. Executing only one of these skills at a time prevents the character from walking while carrying—a limitation that becomes increasingly problematic as task complexity grows.

### 3. Multiplicative Compositional Policies

The key insight of MCP is to model an agent's policy as a multiplicative composition of primitive policies rather than selecting a single primitive at each timestep. Each primitive represents a Gaussian distribution over actions, and the final composite policy is formed by multiplying these distributions together, weighted by task-specific coefficients.

Mathematically, this is represented as:

$$\pi(a|s,g) = \frac{1}{Z(s,g)} \prod_{i=1}^{k} \pi_i(a|s,g)^{w_i(s,g)}$$

Where:
- $\pi(a|s,g)$ is the composite policy
- $\pi_i(a|s,g)$ represents primitive policies
- $w_i(s,g)$ are the weights determining each primitive's contribution
- $Z(s,g)$ is a normalization factor
- $s$ represents the state and $g$ the goal

A particularly useful property of this formulation is that when the primitives are Gaussian, the composite policy is also Gaussian with analytically computable parameters, making implementation straightforward and computationally efficient.

### 4. Network Architecture

The MCP architecture consists of two main components:

1. **Primitives**: A set of neural networks that map states to Gaussian distributions over actions. Each primitive is parameterized by a mean and covariance matrix.

2. **Gating Function**: A neural network that determines the weight assigned to each primitive given the current state and goal. This function controls how primitives are combined to produce the final behavior.

During pre-training, primitives learn from motion capture data through imitation learning. When transferring to new tasks, these pre-trained primitives remain fixed, while a new gating function is learned to appropriately combine them for the task at hand.

### 5. Experimental Setup

The researchers evaluated MCP on a suite of complex control tasks using various simulated characters with different morphologies:

The experimental setup involved:

1. **Pre-training tasks**: Motion imitation tasks where primitives learn to reproduce reference motions.
2. **Transfer tasks**: Novel tasks including:
 - **Heading**: Navigate to target locations while facing specific directions
 - **Carrying**: Transport an object to a target location
 - **Dribbling**: Control a ball toward a goal

These tasks were tested with characters of increasing complexity: Ant (8 DoF), Biped (12 DoF), Humanoid (30 DoF), and T-Rex (27 DoF).

MCP was compared against several baseline approaches:
- Training from scratch
- Fine-tuning a pre-trained policy
- Hierarchical models with discrete primitive selection
- Option-Critic method
- Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)
- Latent space models

### 6. Results

The experimental results revealed several key findings about MCP's performance:

1. **Superior performance on complex tasks**: While all methods performed similarly on simple tasks, MCP significantly outperformed baselines on more complex tasks like carrying and dribbling, particularly with more complex character morphologies.

2. **Improved sample efficiency**: MCP learned effective policies with fewer environment interactions compared to other methods, converging to high-performance solutions much faster.

3. **Better transfer capabilities**: MCP demonstrated stronger ability to leverage pre-trained skills for new tasks compared to other transfer learning approaches.

4. **Structured exploration**: As shown in the trajectory visualization below, MCP's exploration patterns were more structured and efficient than those of models trained from scratch or with limited composition ability.

### 7. Performance Across Task Complexity

The full suite of experimental results shows a clear pattern: as task complexity increases, the advantage of MCP over other methods becomes more pronounced:

For simple locomotion tasks like heading with the Biped character, most transfer learning methods including MCP achieve similar performance. However, for tasks requiring coordination like dribbling a ball with a T-Rex character, MCP demonstrates a dramatic performance advantage over other approaches.

This suggests that the key benefit of multiplicative composition emerges when tasks require the simultaneous execution of multiple skills, precisely the scenario where traditional hierarchical methods struggle.

### 8. Analysis of Primitive Specialization

An intriguing finding is how primitives naturally specialize to different aspects of behavior. For example, when analyzing a walking humanoid:

Different primitives become active during specific phases of the gait cycle. Some primitives specialize in the stance phase, others in the swing phase, with the gating network learning to coordinate these primitives to produce smooth, coordinated motion.

The right side of the figure shows the t-SNE visualization of actions generated by individual primitives, illustrating their distinct specializations.

### 9. Impact of Primitive Count

The researchers also investigated how the number of primitives affects performance:

Increasing the number of primitives (k) initially improves performance, but with diminishing returns. For the humanoid dribbling task, performance improves substantially when moving from k=4 to k=8, with more modest gains for k=16, and potentially negative effects at k=32.

This suggests that while more primitives offer greater expressivity, there's a point of diminishing returns where additional primitives may interfere with learning or lead to redundancy.

### 10. Conclusion

Multiplicative Compositional Policies (MCP) represent a significant advancement in hierarchical reinforcement learning by enabling the simultaneous activation of multiple skill primitives. The method addresses a fundamental limitation in traditional hierarchical approaches—the inability to express behaviors that require the coordinated execution of multiple skills.

Key advantages of MCP include:

1. **Enhanced expressivity**: By combining primitives multiplicatively rather than selecting a single primitive, MCP can represent a much richer space of behaviors.

2. **Improved sample efficiency**: Pre-trained primitives dramatically reduce the amount of experience needed to learn new tasks.

3. **Better transfer learning**: MCP demonstrates superior ability to repurpose skills for new tasks compared to other transfer learning approaches.

4. **Scalability to complex systems**: The approach scales effectively to high-dimensional control problems with complex morphologies.

These results suggest that MCP could have significant implications for robotics, animation, and other fields requiring complex control of articulated systems. By enabling more efficient skill transfer and composition, MCP brings us closer to artificial agents that can quickly adapt their existing skills to novel situations—a crucial capability for general-purpose AI systems.
