# GRAPE: Generalizing Robot Policy via Preference Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> GRAPE introduces a framework that enhances the generalizability and objective alignment of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) robot policies. It leverages trajectory-wise preference optimization with automatically generated preference data, achieving substantial improvements in success rates and out-of-distribution performance compared to supervised fine-tuning baselines.

## Problem

- Current Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models trained via behavior cloning struggle with generalizability to unseen environments and tasks, often leading to "behavior collapse."
- Supervised fine-tuning datasets are often uncurated, leading to suboptimal trajectories because models implicitly learn from mixed and undefined objectives.
- Traditional reinforcement learning is impractical for VLA model training due to high costs of online data collection and difficulties in defining explicit reward functions for complex tasks.

## Method

- **GRAPE** employs ==Trajectory-wise Preference Optimization== (==TPO==) to fine-tune VLA policies by learning from comparisons of chosen (preferred) and rejected trajectories.
- ==Guided-Cost Preference Generation== (==GCPG==) automatically synthesizes high-quality preference data by decomposing trajectories into stages, generating ==cost functions== with an LLM, and aggregating them with self-evaluated and success-indicating rewards.
- An ==iterative online training loop== continuously refines the VLA policy using these generated preference pairs, progressively enhancing alignment and generalization.

## Results

- Achieved substantial gains in success rates across simulated and real-world tasks, outperforming supervised fine-tuning baselines by an average of **131.72%** in Simpler-Env and **11%** in real-world settings over OpenVLA-DPO.
- Demonstrated robust generalization to various out-of-distribution scenarios, including unseen objects, different sizes, and modified instructions, validating improved adaptability.
- Successfully aligned robot policies with specific objectives, such as GRAPE-Safety reducing collision rates by **37.44%** in real-world scenarios and GRAPE-Efficiency reducing rollout step-length by **11.15%**, while maintaining high success rates.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Learning from both successful and failure trajectories via trajectory-wise preference optimization provides a more comprehensive understanding of task goals and pitfalls, improving robustness.
> - Automating preference data generation through multi-modal models (VLMs, LLMs) for defining stage-specific costs and aggregating rewards reduces reliance on costly human annotation.
> - Policy alignment through preference optimization enables flexible customization of robot behavior to diverse objectives such as safety and efficiency, moving beyond mere task completion.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape: Challenges in Robot Policy Learning

The field of robotic manipulation has seen substantial progress with the advent of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models, which integrate visual observations, language instructions, and robot actions. These models, exemplified by architectures like RT-1, RT-2, Octo, and OpenVLA, have shown impressive capabilities across a variety of robot tasks. However, the paper identifies several critical limitations that hinder their widespread adoption and generalizability:

Firstly, current VLA models primarily rely on **supervised fine-tuning (SFT)** through **behavior cloning (BC)**. This means they are trained by imitating successful demonstrations provided by human experts. While effective for in-domain tasks, this approach suffers from severe **generalizability issues**. Robots trained solely on successful rollouts struggle to adapt to unseen environments, novel objects, or variations in task semantics because they lack a holistic understanding of the task goal and, crucially, do not learn from failure patterns. This can lead to "behavior collapse" where the robot deviates significantly when encountering even minor distribution shifts.

Secondly, the datasets used for SFT are often uncurated collections of expert demonstrations. These demonstrations implicitly embed various, often undefined, objectives such as task completion, safety, and efficiency. Simply imitating these behaviors via SFT can lead to **suboptimal trajectories** because the model gets "confused" by the mixed objectives, failing to align with a specific desired outcome.

While **reinforcement learning (RL)** algorithms, such as Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), offer a promising avenue for enhancing generalizability by allowing robots to learn from trial and error, they face significant practical challenges in the robotics domain. The high cost of collecting online trajectories in real-world environments and the difficulty of explicitly defining reward functions for complex, diverse manipulation tasks make traditional RL impractical for VLA model training.

The paper aims to bridge this gap by introducing a method that harnesses the benefits of RL-like objective alignment without incurring the prohibitive costs and complexities of explicit reward engineering or extensive online data collection typical of traditional RL. It seeks to develop a robust and adaptable framework for VLA models that can generalize better and align with diverse, customizable objectives, moving beyond mere imitation of successful demonstrations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations behind the GRAPE framework are:

1. **Enhance Generalizability:** To overcome the critical limitation of current VLA models, which show poor generalizability to unseen tasks, environments, objects, and semantic contexts due to their reliance on behavior cloning from exclusively successful demonstrations. GRAPE aims to improve the robot's ability to perform well on tasks it has not explicitly been trained on.
2. **Learn from Failure:** To move beyond imitation learning, which only considers successful trajectories. GRAPE seeks to implicitly model rewards from *both successful and failure trials* to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the task and potential pitfalls, thereby boosting robustness and adaptability.
3. **Address Distribution Bias and Suboptimality:** To mitigate the issues arising from uncurated SFT datasets that contain implicitly diverse and potentially conflicting objectives (e.g., speed vs. safety). GRAPE aims to provide a mechanism for explicitly aligning the robot's policy with desired manipulation objectives, preventing "behavior collapse" and generating optimal trajectories.
4. **Enable Customizable Objective Alignment:** To offer flexibility in aligning VLA models with diverse manipulation objectives beyond mere task completion, such as safety, efficiency, and resilience. This is crucial for deploying robots in real-world scenarios where multiple criteria often need to be balanced.
5. **Overcome RL Training Challenges:** To alleviate the high cost and impracticality of training VLAs with traditional RL objectives, which require extensive online data collection and complex reward engineering. GRAPE proposes a preference-based approach that is more data-efficient and scalable for complex VLA tasks.

In essence, GRAPE's core motivation is to make VLA models more intelligent, adaptable, and general-purpose by allowing them to learn human-like preferences and values, rather than just imitating a fixed set of expert behaviors.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GRAPE (Generalizing Robot Policy via Preference Alignment) introduces a novel framework for fine-tuning Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models by leveraging trajectory-wise preference optimization and an automated guided-cost preference generation mechanism.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries: Vision-Language-Action Models

The paper first establishes the context of VLA models, which typically take a task instruction *q* and an environment observation *o_t* (an image) at each timestep *t* to output an action *a_t*. The policy *π_θ(a_t | (o_t, q))* is parameterized by *θ*. Most current VLA models are fine-tuned via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) using a loss *L_SFT* that encourages the model to memorize expert actions. This approach, however, often leads to poor generalizability. GRAPE builds upon this SFT base policy as a reference, aiming to improve it through preference alignment.

#### 3.2. Trajectory-wise Preference Optimization (TPO)

Instead of traditional step-wise reward modeling or imitation, GRAPE adopts **Trajectory-wise Preference Optimization (TPO)**. Inspired by recent advancements in language model alignment (like Direct Preference Optimization, DPO), TPO fine-tunes the VLA policy using an RL objective but without explicitly defining a reward function. The core idea is to learn from comparisons between a "chosen" trajectory (ζ_w) and a "rejected" trajectory (ζ_l) from the same initial state.

The TPO loss (L_TPO) is formulated using the Bradley-Terry model, where the probability of preferring ζ_w over ζ_l depends on the ratio of the policy's likelihood of generating these trajectories relative to a reference policy (the SFT-trained base VLA). This approach is beneficial because:
- It aligns the policy globally on a trajectory level, considering the entire sequence of actions and observations, rather than noisy step-wise signals.
- It stabilizes the policy by backpropagating gradients through all state-action pairs along the trajectory.
- Crucially, it boosts generalizability by learning from both successful and *failed* trajectories, as failures can provide critical information about what not to do.

#### 3.3. Guided-Cost Preference Generation (GCPG)

A major challenge for preference optimization is acquiring high-quality preference data, which typically requires costly human annotation. GRAPE addresses this with **Guided-Cost Preference Generation (GCPG)**, an automated pipeline to synthesize preferences that integrate various alignment objectives.

#### 3.3.1. Multi-Stage Temporal Keypoint Constraints

To simplify the process of defining preferences for complex manipulation tasks, GRAPE decomposes trajectories into temporal stages. This decomposition is achieved using a VLM-based stage decomposer (e.g., Hamster), which partitions a trajectory into a sequence of *S* consecutive stages. For each stage, a vision-language model (e.g., DINOv2 combined with Grounded-SAM) identifies key spatial points. A powerful Large Language Model (LLM) like GPT-4o is then prompted to generate **cost functions** for each stage, tailored to specific alignment objectives (e.g., task completion, safety, efficiency). Lower costs indicate better compliance with the objective.

These stage-specific costs are aggregated to form an **external reward (R_ext)** for the entire trajectory, using an exponential decay to capture causal dependencies between stages (e.g., a failure in an early stage implies poor overall performance). This modular approach allows for flexible customization of objectives by simply adjusting the generated cost functions and their thresholds.

#### 3.3.2. Guided-Cost Preference Generation Reward

To further enhance the stability and optimality of preference synthesis, GRAPE incorporates two additional reward components alongside the external reward, forming the comprehensive **GCPG reward (R_GCPG)**:
- **Self-evaluated reward (R_self):** This is the log-likelihood of the policy generating the entire trajectory, *log(π(ζ,q))*. It acts as a dense approximation of task success, encouraging the model to favor trajectories it is more confident in generating.
- **Success indicator (I_success):** A binary function (1 if task successful, 0 otherwise) that provides a sparse but critical signal for overall task completion.

The final GCPG reward is a weighted sum: *R_GCPG(ζ) = λ_1 R_self(ζ) + λ_2 R_ext(ζ) + λ_3 I_success(ζ)*. These weights (λ values) are tuned to balance the importance of each component. This combined reward holistically evaluates trajectories, accounting for optimality, adherence to customized objectives, and overall task success.

#### 3.4. Iterative Preference Optimization

GRAPE employs an **iterative online training loop**, similar to on-policy RL, to continuously refine the VLA policy. In each iteration *k*:
1. Numerous trajectories are sampled online using the current VLA policy (π_θ).
2. The GCPG reward (R_GCPG) is calculated for each sampled trajectory.
3. Based on these rewards, trajectories are ranked. For each task, the top-ranked (chosen) and bottom-ranked (rejected) trajectories are paired.
4. The VLA policy (π_θ) is then fine-tuned using the TPO loss (Eq. 5) with these chosen-rejected pairs.
This process iterates for a set number of times (*K*), leading to a progressively more aligned and generalized policy.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

GRAPE's effectiveness was rigorously evaluated across a diverse array of tasks in both real-world and simulated environments, demonstrating significant improvements over state-of-the-art baselines.

#### 4.1. Performance in Simulated Environments

Experiments were conducted on Simpler-Env (evaluating in-domain, subject, physical, and semantic generalization) and LIBERO (spatial, object, goal, long-horizon tasks).
- **Superior Success Rates:** GRAPE consistently outperformed Octo-SFT and OpenVLA-SFT (models trained solely with supervised fine-tuning). In Simpler-Env, GRAPE achieved an average success rate improvement of 131.72% over Octo-SFT and 46.10% over OpenVLA-SFT. In LIBERO, these figures were 8.53% and 7.36% respectively.
- **Trajectory-wise Advantage:** GRAPE also significantly surpassed OpenVLA-DPO (a step-wise direct preference optimization baseline) by an average of 33.14%. This highlights the benefit of trajectory-wise learning, which captures global success/failure patterns without being susceptible to low-level step-wise noise.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** Beyond in-domain tasks, GRAPE showed robust performance in various generalization categories (e.g., unseen objects, different sizes, modified instructions), confirming its ability to generalize to out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios.

#### 4.2. Performance in Real-World Robot Environment

A comprehensive real-world evaluation involving 300 experiments across 30 tasks further validated GRAPE's capabilities, focusing on in-distribution and five types of out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization (visual, subject, action, semantic, language grounding).
- **Significant Real-World Gains:** GRAPE achieved a 67.5% success rate on in-domain tasks, a 17.5% improvement over OpenVLA-DPO (50%) and 22.5% over OpenVLA-SFT (45%). Octo-SFT lagged significantly at 20%.
- **Robust OOD Performance:** GRAPE maintained strong performance in challenging OOD scenarios, such as visual generalization (56% success rate) and action generalization, outperforming all baselines.
- **Overall Average:** GRAPE's total average success rate across all real-world tasks was 50.3%, a notable 11% improvement over OpenVLA-DPO's 39.3% and 18% over OpenVLA-SFT's 32.3%.

#### 4.3. Ablation Study of Reward Model

An ablation study analyzed the contribution of each component of the GCPG reward (R_ext, R_self, I_success) and the importance of using the full reward score for preference selection.
- **Full Reward Score is Critical:** Using the full aggregated GCPG reward for preference ranking significantly outperformed random selection based solely on binary success/failure.
- **All Components Contribute:** The study confirmed that all three reward components—external objective-aligned reward (R_ext), self-evaluated reward (R_self), and the binary success indicator (I_success)—individually contribute to the overall model performance, working synergistically to guide effective policy learning.

#### 4.4. Analysis of Iterative Preference Optimization

Tracking performance across iterative optimization rounds revealed that GRAPE progressively enhances model performance.
- **Continuous Improvement:** The model's success rates consistently increased over successive iterations across in-domain and generalization tasks (e.g., 17.5% improvement in in-domain performance after multiple iterations).
- **Convergence:** The rate of improvement diminished over time, indicating that the model approaches convergence and effectively leverages the iteratively generated preference data.

#### 4.5. Analysis of Different Alignment Objectives

A key feature of GRAPE is its flexibility in aligning with diverse objectives beyond just task completion.
- **Customizable Alignment:** By adjusting the multi-stage cost functions (e.g., setting lower thresholds for collision costs for safety, or path costs for efficiency), GRAPE can be aligned with specific goals.
- **Safety and Efficiency Gains:** GRAPE-Safety significantly reduced collision rates (by 37.44% in real-world, 31.05% in simulation compared to OpenVLA-SFT) while maintaining comparable success rates. GRAPE-Efficiency reduced rollout step-length (by 11.15% in real-world, 10.7% in simulation).
- **Case Studies:** Visual case studies confirmed that GRAPE-Safety learned to avoid obstacles effectively, while a GRAPE model aligned purely for task completion might succeed but collide with obstacles. This demonstrates GRAPE's ability to balance or prioritize different objectives as configured.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GRAPE framework represents a significant step forward in the field of robot learning, addressing fundamental limitations that currently impede the widespread and robust deployment of VLA models.

1. **Enhanced Generalizability and Robustness:** By learning from both successful and, crucially, *failed* trajectories through trajectory-wise preference optimization, GRAPE enables VLA models to develop a more profound understanding of task goals and failure modes. This leads to dramatically improved generalization capabilities across diverse, unseen tasks and environments, reducing the brittleness of current robot policies. This is vital for deploying robots in dynamic, unstructured real-world settings where perfectly curated demonstrations are impractical.
2. **Flexible and Customizable Policy Alignment:** One of GRAPE's most impactful contributions is its ability to align robot policies with varying objectives beyond simple task completion, such as safety, efficiency, and resilience. This flexibility, achieved through automatically generated multi-stage cost functions, empowers developers to tailor robot behavior to specific application requirements. For instance, a robot in a factory might prioritize efficiency, while a robot in a healthcare setting might prioritize safety, all within the same underlying framework. This moves robot policy learning closer to human-like value alignment.
3. **Reduced Annotation Burden:** The Guided-Cost Preference Generation (GCPG) pipeline automates the synthesis of preference data, significantly reducing the laborious and costly human annotation typically required for reinforcement learning from human feedback. By leveraging powerful VLMs and LLMs to define task stages and generate cost functions, GRAPE makes preference-based learning more scalable and practical for complex robotic tasks.
4. **Bridging the Gap between Imitation Learning and RL:** GRAPE offers a viable alternative to the limitations of pure behavior cloning and the practical challenges of traditional online RL. It combines the data-efficiency of learning from existing trajectories with the objective alignment benefits of RL, creating a powerful paradigm for post-training VLA models.
5. **Accelerated Robot Deployment:** The ability to train more generalizable, robust, and objective-aligned robot policies with reduced data and annotation costs can significantly accelerate the development and deployment of autonomous robotic systems in various domains, from manufacturing and logistics to domestic assistance and hazardous environment exploration.

In conclusion, GRAPE offers a practical and powerful framework for moving VLA models beyond mere imitation, endowing them with a more nuanced understanding of tasks and the ability to adapt to diverse objectives, paving the way for more intelligent and dependable robots in the real world.
