# F2F-AP: Flow-to-Future Asynchronous Policy for Real-time Dynamic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The F2F-AP framework from Tsinghua University addresses system latency in real-time robotic manipulation by synthesizing future visual observations and integrating them with anticipated future robot states. This approach enables proactive planning and motion, consistently improving task success rates in dynamic environments across both fixed-base and mobile manipulator platforms.

## Problem

- Traditional synchronous robotic execution results in sluggish and discontinuous motion due to computational latency, limiting responsiveness in dynamic tasks.
- Existing asynchronous policies suffer from 'stale' visual observations and outdated initial actions, leading to temporal misalignment and inefficient execution when interacting with a continuously evolving environment.
- Dynamic manipulation tasks, like grasping moving objects, require precise, rapid reactions that are severely compromised by inherent system latency and information lag.

## Method

- **F2F-AP** formulates an ==asynchronous policy== that explicitly conditions actions on anticipated future proprioceptive states and synthesized future visual observations to compensate for total system latency.
- A lightweight ==Flow2Future== (F2F) module uses a ==heatmap-based flow predictor== to efficiently synthesize future visual observations from historical RGB images.
- ==Flow Contrastive Learning== aligns the feature representations of these predicted future observations with ground-truth future observations, minimizing feature discrepancy for robust policy learning.

## Results

- **F2F-AP** consistently achieved higher Task Success Rates (SR) and improved Average Execution Times (ET) across dynamic tasks on a fixed-base UR5e arm, for example, obtaining **15/20** SR for Hammer Handover compared to **7/20** for Asynchronous UMI.
- The framework demonstrated superior performance on a highly stochastic task, grasping a rolling, half-filled water bottle, with an SR of **11/20**, significantly outperforming Asynchronous UMI (**2/20** SR) and other baselines.
- **F2F-AP** proved versatile and robust, showing improved capabilities on both fixed-base arms and quadruped mobile manipulators, and maintaining performance across varying latency magnitudes.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly aligning both visual observations and proprioceptive states to the exact moment of action execution is crucial for enabling proactive and robust dynamic manipulation policies.
> - A specialized, heatmap-based object flow predictor can efficiently generate future visual context, overcoming the computational burden of general video generation models for real-time robotic deployment.
> - Employing contrastive learning effectively bridges the representational gap between synthesized and real future visual features, ensuring the fidelity of the anticipated visual context for the policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robotic manipulation has seen substantial advancements, yet real-world deployment is often hindered by computational latency during policy inference. This latency leads to sluggish and discontinuous robot movements, especially in dynamic environments where objects are actively moving.

**Asynchronous Inference and its Challenges:** To address latency, asynchronous inference frameworks have emerged. These frameworks parallelize model inference with action execution, utilizing "action chunking" where the model predicts a sequence of future actions. However, this approach introduces two primary issues:
1. **Outdated Initial Actions:** Due to inference latency, the initial actions in a predicted chunk are often outdated upon execution and must be discarded.
2. **Persistent Lag:** Compounding latencies cause the policy to consistently lag behind the evolving environment and robot state, severely impacting performance in dynamic tasks where optimal trajectories are continuously shifting.

**Prior Attempts to Mitigate Lag:**
- **Action Chunking and Asynchronous Inference:** Early methods focused on generating action chunks for multiple future timesteps and executing them asynchronously to improve deployment frequencies and smoothness. However, they struggled with trajectory discontinuities.
- **State-Conditional Inputs and Future States:** Some studies, such as VLASH, have attempted to improve smoothness by conditioning policies on anticipated future proprioceptive states. VLASH aims to predict actions aligned with the exact moment of execution, theoretically eliminating the need to discard initial actions. However, VLASH introduces a critical limitation: the temporal misalignment between a stale visual observation (o_t) and an anticipated future proprioceptive state (s_t+H). This discrepancy forces the policy to infer actions based on insufficient and outdated visual information.
- **Affordance-based Manipulation:** This category of research integrates intermediate knowledge like object localization and functionality into policies to enhance scene understanding. Flow-based affordances, in particular, have been used as embodiment-agnostic representations for data transfer or as high-level interfaces for action generation. However, existing flow-based methods primarily focus on how objects *should be moved* within static environments, neglecting the spontaneous motion of the objects themselves in dynamic scenarios.

**F2F-AP's Position:** This work directly addresses the fundamental issue of temporal misalignment between visual observations and action execution, particularly in dynamic manipulation tasks. It builds upon asynchronous inference paradigms by introducing a novel mechanism to synthesize future visual observations, thereby providing the policy with an *anticipated visual context* that is aligned with future proprioceptive states and the moment of execution. By explicitly modeling and compensating for system latency, F2F-AP aims to enable proactive planning and motion, overcoming the limitations of prior asynchronous and future-state-aware policies. It expands the role of object flow by using it to characterize motion trends and encode future observational features for real-time dynamic scenarios.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central motivation for this research stems from a systemic challenge in robotic manipulation: the inherent computational latency of policy inference. While asynchronous inference attempts to mitigate this by parallelizing inference and execution, a critical problem persists. Generated actions inevitably lag behind the real-time environment, a issue exacerbated in dynamic tasks where rapid changes in the environment demand immediate and accurate responses. The policy's ability to interpret and react to evolving surroundings is severely compromised by this temporal misalignment.

The paper identifies specific limitations of existing approaches:
- **Naive Asynchronous Policies:** These policies, using action chunking, suffer from outdated initial actions that must be discarded and a persistent lag behind the environment, leading to non-smooth trajectories.
- **VLASH's Limitation:** Although VLASH improves trajectory smoothness by conditioning on anticipated future proprioceptive states (s_t+H), it still relies on current, and thus stale, visual observations (o_t). This temporal imbalance between future robot state and lagging visual context forces the policy to "guess" future actions rather than accurately "inferring" them from a synchronized context, leading to instability in dynamic tasks.

To overcome these challenges, the key objectives of the proposed Flow-to-Future Asynchronous Policy (F2F-AP) are:
1. **Synthesize Future Observations:** Develop a framework that explicitly leverages predicted object flow to synthesize future visual observations (ˆo_t+H). This aims to enhance the policy's understanding of motion trends and compensate for the information deficit caused by latency in dynamic tasks.
2. **Align Visual Features:** Propose a flow-based contrastive learning objective to align the visual feature representations of predicted future observations with ground-truth future states. This objective aims to minimize feature discrepancies and guarantee the fidelity of the anticipated visual context.
3. **Enable Proactive Planning and Motion:** Empower the asynchronous policy with an anticipated visual context and temporally aligned future proprioceptive states (s_t+H) to facilitate proactive planning and motion. This enables the policy to explicitly compensate for system latency and robustly execute manipulation tasks involving actively moving objects.
4. **Achieve Real-Time Dynamic Manipulation:** Demonstrate that the proposed F2F-AP significantly enhances responsiveness and success rates in complex dynamic manipulation tasks across diverse robotic embodiments.

In summary, the specific contributions outlined by the authors are:
- **F2F Module:** A module to predict object flow as a representation of motion trends, encoding these predictions as future-aware observations.
- **Flow Contrastive Learning:** An objective to align features of flow-based predictions with ground-truth future observations.
- **Real-World System Deployment:** Validation of F2F-AP on a fixed-base robotic arm and a quadruped mobile manipulator for highly dynamic tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The F2F-AP framework is designed to address latency in asynchronous robotic policy inference by explicitly incorporating future observations. The methodology involves a precise problem statement, the development of a Flow2Future (F2F) Module, the integration of Flow Contrastive Learning, and the final F2F Asynchronous Policy architecture.

**A. Problem Statement**
The paper defines total system latency (∆_total) as the sum of:
- **Observation Latency (∆_o):** Delay in sensor acquisition and transmission.
- **Inference Latency (∆_i):** Time for model to generate policy output.
- **Controller Latency (∆_c):** Delay from the low-level controller during action execution.
During this ∆_total, the robot executes actions from the current chunk. The parameter `H` denotes the number of action steps executed during this latency within one cycle, calculated as H = ⌈∆_total / δ_t⌉, where δ_t is the control time step.

The paper contrasts existing asynchronous policy formulations:
- **Naive Asynchronous Policy (Eq. 1):** π(a_t:t+n | s_t, o_t). This policy suffers from non-smooth trajectories and outdated initial actions due to latency.
- **VLASH (Eq. 2):** π(a_t+H:t+H+n | s_t+H, o_t). While anchoring actions to an anticipated future proprioceptive state (s_t+H) improves smoothness, it introduces a critical misalignment between this future state and the lagging visual observation (o_t).
- **F2F-AP (Eq. 3):** π(a_t+H:t+H+n | s_t+H, ˆo_t+H). F2F-AP resolves this by providing temporally aligned future proprioceptive states and *synthesized future visual observations* (ˆo_t+H).

**B. Flow2Future (F2F) Module**
The F2F module is designed to synthesize future observations (ˆo_t+H) without relying on computationally expensive video generation models. It leverages predictive object flow as a lightweight bridge between current and future observations.

- **Heatmap-Based Flow Predictor:**
 - **Challenge:** Standard loss functions for flow prediction (e.g., one-to-one or minimum-distance losses) can fail due to the arbitrary indexing of ground truth flow elements, potentially leading to an averaged, trivial solution.
 - **Solution:** F2F employs a multi-branch decoder centered on heatmap prediction. The object flow `f_t→t+H` is defined as a composition of a refined starting position and a motion displacement vector (Eq. 4).
 - **Process:** Image feature maps (Z_v) are extracted from historical frames (o_t-n→t). A heatmap computation module H(·) generates a probability heatmap. A soft-sampling operation S(·) obtains a coarse flow starting point. Local correlations across temporal features derive Z_h. Finally, a flow decoder outputs fine-grained refinement for the starting point (D_off) and the flow displacement vector (D_d).
 - **Future Observation Synthesis:** The predicted flow vectors `f_t→t+H` are then rendered onto the current observation o_t using a `Paint(·)` operation to generate `ˆo_t+H` (Eq. 5).
- **Data Collection:**
 - The UMI device is used for rapid data acquisition, providing fisheye images for training the flow predictor.
 - Ground truth flow is generated by segmenting interacting objects using SAM, clustering the segmentation masks to extract representative keypoints, and computing pixel-wise displacement of these keypoints over time.
 - To handle object occlusion by the gripper (a common issue), ground truth flow is first obtained from gripper-free video sequences. Training data is then synthesized by superimposing gripper masks onto these videos, following methods like.

**C. Flow Contrastive Learning**
To ensure that the synthesized future observation (ˆo_t+H) provides a representation comparable to a real future observation (o_t+H), F2F incorporates a contrastive learning framework during policy training.
- **Objective:** Minimize the feature discrepancy between `ˆo_t+H` and `o_t+H` (Eq. 6).
- **Mechanism:** Both predicted and ground-truth observations share a common visual encoder (E_θ) but are mapped into a latent space via separate projectors. The goal is to maximize similarity for synchronized pairs while minimizing it for asynchronous pairs.
- **Temporal Masking:** A temporal masking mechanism `M` (Eq. 7) is introduced to filter out features from temporally proximal steps, preventing them from being treated as negative samples due to high visual correlation between adjacent frames.
- **Loss Function:** The directional contrastive loss `ℓ(u, v)` (Eq. 8) is defined using cosine similarity between embeddings. The final symmetric objective `L` (Eq. 9) is the average of bidirectional losses.

**D. F2F Asynchronous Policy**
- **Policy Architecture:** F2F-AP is built upon the Diffusion Policy framework, utilizing a pre-trained ViT as the encoder and a U-Net as the decoder for action generation.
- **Overall Policy Formulation:** `P(ˆo_t+H, s) = D ◦ E(F(o_t−n→t), s)` (Eq. 10), where F is the F2F module, E is the encoder, and D is the decoder.
- **Action Execution:** Unlike previous methods, F2F-AP's actions are explicitly aligned with the real-world timeline due to the future context. This eliminates the need to discard initial `H` action steps; instead, a queue of valid actions with real timestamps is directly maintained.

**Implementation Details:**
- The F2F module is trained separately using Adam, cosine annealing, and mixed-precision, with an auxiliary image reconstruction loss.
- The policy is then trained using AdamW, a cosine schedule, and incorporates a standard denoising loss alongside the auxiliary flow-contrastive loss.
- Inference is conducted on an NVIDIA RTX 3090 GPU at 5 Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates F2F-AP across various dynamic manipulation tasks on two distinct robotic platforms, comparing its performance against baseline methods under both synchronous and asynchronous inference settings.

**A. Experiment Settings**
- **Platforms:**
 - **Fixed-Base Robotic Arm:** UR5e robotic arm.
 - **Quadruped Mobile Manipulator:** Unitree Go2 quadruped robot integrated with a Hexfellow Saber arm, controlled by a Whole-Body Controller (WBC).
- **Baselines:** UMI (for fixed-base arm) and UMI-on-Legs (for quadruped), evaluated in synchronous and asynchronous modes. VLASH integrated into UMI policy, trained with future proprioception states. F2F-AP integrates the F2F module and asynchronous inference into UMI.
- **Metrics:** Task Success Rate (SR) and Average Execution Time (ET, for successful trials).
- **Latency Parameter H:** Measured system latencies (∆o ≈ 125 ms, ∆i ≈ 200 ms, ∆c < 50 ms) and action sampling interval (δt = 0.1s) resulted in H = 4 for fixed-base arm experiments, and similarly for the quadruped platform.

**B. Evaluation in Fixed-Base Arm (Fig. 6)**
Three dynamic tasks were performed:
1. **Human-to-Robot Hammer Handover:** Robot must react swiftly to receive a hammer handed over by a human, predicting interaction points based on object velocity.
 - **Result:** F2F-AP significantly outperforms baselines in both SR and ET, exhibiting superior smoothness and proactive grasping.
2. **Pull-back Car Interception:** Intercepting a fast-moving toy-car (25cm/s) with a narrow grasping region (5cm) and short interception window (<0.4s).
 - **Result:** F2F-AP demonstrates high competence, perceiving car velocity to adaptively select optimal gripper actuation timing.
3. **Pick-up Moving Banana:** Grasping a banana on a continuously moving platform with randomized speeds.
 - **Result:** F2F-AP achieves substantial improvements in SR, demonstrating proactive motion and effective adaptation to varying target speeds.

**Analysis of Fixed-Base Arm Results:**
- **Synchronous UMI:** Slow responsiveness, struggled with time-sensitive tasks.
- **Asynchronous UMI:** Suffered from dual misalignment (proprioceptive states and RGB context), leading to lagging, unsmooth, and jittery trajectories.
- **VLASH:** Achieved smooth trajectory connections with future states but failed to generate actions meeting real-world temporal requirements due to outdated visual context, leading to overshooting or missing interaction timings.
- **F2F-AP:** Directly addresses the training-inference gap caused by latency by aligning visual observations and proprioceptive states to the moment of action execution, ensuring actions strictly meet real-world temporal requirements and enabling robust adaptability to dynamic tasks.

**C. Evaluation in Quadruped Mobile Manipulator (Fig. 7)**
Two dynamic tasks were performed:
1. **Human-to-Robot Carrot Handover:** Similar to hammer handover, requiring swift reaction from the quadruped robot.
 - **Result:** F2F-AP shows measurable improvements in SR and response time, demonstrating proactive motion. Asynchronous UMI had comparable SR but longer ET. VLASH frequently failed due to overshooting.
2. **Collaborative Length Measurement:** Robot grasps a tape measure, presses it down, and holds it stably for human measurement. Demands rapid response to dynamic handover and stability during measurement.
 - **Result:** F2F-AP achieves significantly higher SR. Its ability to infer static poses from zero-flow states provides superior stability compared to baselines, which often failed to maintain a stable hold.

**D. Advanced Dynamic Experiment (Fig. 8)**
- **Task:** Grasping a rolling, half-filled water bottle with highly stochastic and non-linear trajectories due to human throws and internal fluid dynamics.
- **Baselines:** Asynchronous UMI, UMI augmented with RTC (for smoother motions), and MF-UMI (UMI with increased input frames to match F2F-AP's visual input count).
- **Result:** F2F-AP achieved "overwhelmingly superior performance." Asynchronous UMI and UMI+RTC failed to generate appropriate actions due to latency and delayed information. MF-UMI performed even worse than baseline UMI, indicating that simply stacking historical frames does not improve capability and can introduce additional temporal uncertainty.
- **Analysis:** F2F-AP's direct solution to latency by providing correctly timed observation context proved critical. The robustness of F2F-AP was also demonstrated under varying latency conditions, with performance only degrading under excessively large, task-incompatible latencies.

**E. Ablation Studies (Table I)**
- **Key Modules:**
 - Lowering inference frequency undermined asynchronous benefits.
 - Omitting contrastive learning or reducing its weight impeded feature alignment, causing SR drop.
 - Removing the multi-branch heatmap architecture in the flow predictor prevented convergence and significantly degraded performance.
- **Hyperparameters:**
 - Variations in flow line color (pink to green) and number of flow points (four to two) had negligible impact, suggesting robustness.
- **Future Proprioception State:** Excluding `s_t+H` led to misalignment between visual observations and robot status, degrading SR.
- **MF-UMI (vs. F2F module):** MF-UMI showed lower performance than F2F-AP, confirming that the F2F module's explicit future observation synthesis is more effective than simply increasing historical frame input.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in real-time robotic manipulation by directly addressing the persistent challenge of computational latency and temporal misalignment in asynchronous policy inference.

The **Flow-to-Future Asynchronous Policy (F2F-AP)** introduces a novel framework that significantly enhances robot responsiveness and success rates in dynamic tasks. By leveraging predicted object flow to synthesize future observations and integrating a flow-based contrastive learning objective, F2F-AP provides policies with an anticipated visual context that is explicitly aligned with future robot states and the actual moment of action execution. This proactive planning capability allows robots to compensate for system latency, overcoming a fundamental limitation of previous asynchronous and future-state-aware methods.

The **significance** of this work is demonstrated through:
- **Improved Performance:** F2F-AP consistently outperforms baseline methods across a diverse set of complex dynamic manipulation tasks, including human-robot handovers, high-speed object interception, and manipulation of continuously moving or stochastically rolling objects.
- **Enhanced Adaptability:** The framework shows robustness to various object dynamics and operating conditions, making policies more reliable in unpredictable real-world scenarios.
- **Versatile Embodiment:** Validation on both fixed-base robotic arms and quadruped mobile manipulators highlights the transferability and broad applicability of the proposed approach.
- **Robustness to Latency:** The explicit modeling of system latency and the mechanism for providing correctly timed observation context contribute to the policy's stability even in the presence of inherent delays.

The **potential impact** of F2F-AP is substantial for the future of robotic manipulation:
- **More Capable Robots:** It enables robots to operate more effectively in dynamic, unstructured environments, such as factory floors with moving parts, logistics settings, or collaborative tasks with humans.
- **Reduced Development Complexity:** By intrinsically aligning observations and actions, F2F-AP reduces the need for heuristic-based post-hoc action fusion algorithms or complex temporal compensation mechanisms, simplifying policy development and deployment.
- **Foundation for Generalist Models:** The core idea of using predicted flow for future observation synthesis could be extended towards more generalized object flow predictors, moving beyond single-task limitations.
- **Advanced Perception:** The work highlights object flow as a powerful intermediate representation for bridging temporal gaps in perception, which could inspire further research into spatio-temporal reasoning for robotics.
- **Future Extensions:** The identified limitations, such as dynamic latency perception, universal object flow prediction, and 3D input support, offer clear avenues for future research that could further solidify F2F-AP's impact and expand its capabilities into more complex and uncertain real-world applications.

Overall, F2F-AP represents a concrete step towards enabling more intelligent and responsive robots that can interact effectively with dynamic surroundings, bringing robotic manipulation closer to seamless integration into human environments.
