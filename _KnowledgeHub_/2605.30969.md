# Omni-Supervised Motion Editing: Balancing Change and Invariance through Positive-Negative Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> OmniME is a text-driven human motion editing framework combining three supervision mechanisms atop a diffusion transformer: retrospective multi-layer feature supervision, MotionSNR-gated preservation loss, and triplet-based text-motion alignment. It cuts AvgR from 20.88 to 13.06 on MotionFix and 29.05 to 22.77 on STANCE Adjustment.

## Problem

- Diffusion-based editors rely on heuristic similarity cues or coarse global conditioning, causing motion distortion and weak semantic alignment with edit instructions.
- Prior methods lack explicit mechanisms distinguishing editable from non-editable regions, so global conditioning blurs fine-grained semantics while local edits break smoothness.
- Existing supervision considers only positive signals for reconstruction, ignoring negative supervision needed to sharpen text-motion correspondence and reduce ambiguous edits.

## Method

- ==Retrospective Feature Supervision== attaches prediction heads to the 2nd, 4th, and 6th ==DiT== blocks, applying MSE losses against ground truth for coarse-to-fine multi-layer consistency. This is part of **OmniME**.
- ==Motion Preservation Mechanism== computes frame-wise source-target similarity via ==MotionSNR== (top-kappa/bottom-kappa ratio) and applies reconstruction loss only above threshold tau, targeting subtle-change frames.
- ==Triplet-based Semantic Alignment== pools final-block features into motion embeddings and applies a margin ==triplet loss== pulling toward positive text embeddings, pushing from negative ones.

## Results

- On MotionFix test set, OmniME reaches R@1 **32.02** and AvgR **13.06**, versus SimMotionEdit's R@1 **26.88** and AvgR **20.88**, the strongest baseline.
- On STANCE Adjustment test set, OmniME improves AvgR from SimMotionEdit's **29.05** to **22.77** and R@1 from **12.76** to **22.45**.
- Ablation shows all three losses individually improve AvgR (**22.22** to as low as **15.58** on test), and the full combination yields the best AvgR of **13.06**, confirming complementarity.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Balancing change and invariance in generative editing benefits from splitting supervision into explicit positive (reconstruction) and negative (contrastive) branches rather than one unified loss.
> - Gating preservation loss by a signal-to-noise style similarity ratio lets a model automatically focus capacity on subtle edits without hand-labeling editable regions.
> - Multi-layer intermediate supervision in a diffusion transformer improves training stability and downstream alignment beyond supervising only the final output layer.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This work is situated within the field of human motion synthesis and editing, an area with applications in animation, human-robot interaction, and autonomous behavior simulation. Historically, motion generation focused on creating movements from control signals or text prompts. However, the capacity to precisely edit existing motion sequences based on natural language instructions has gained importance for data reuse and fine-grained control.

Existing text-guided motion editing methods, particularly those based on diffusion models, frequently encounter challenges in balancing the modification of target regions with the preservation of unedited parts. Approaches relying on global conditioning can sometimes compromise fine-grained semantic modifications, while local editing methods may introduce temporal incoherence. For instance, MotionFix, a diffusion-based framework, lacks explicit mechanisms to differentiate editable and non-editable regions. SimMotionEdit introduced similarity-based auxiliary supervision for motion preservation but faced limitations in achieving hierarchical alignment and semantic consistency.

This research addresses these limitations by proposing a framework that integrates a comprehensive set of supervisory mechanisms. It extends the concept of supervision in generative models by introducing both positive and negative learning components. This differentiates it from prior works that primarily focus on positive alignment or rely on heuristic cues, aiming for a more robust balance between modifying specific motion attributes as instructed by text and maintaining the overall integrity and naturalness of the unedited motion segments. The paper positions itself as an advancement in achieving more accurate semantic alignment and preserving motion quality compared to previous diffusion-based editing techniques.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a text-based human motion editing framework that effectively balances semantic modification (change) with the preservation of original motion characteristics (invariance). The framework, named OmniME, aims to modify existing motion sequences according to natural language instructions while maintaining the consistency and realism of the original motion.

The motivation for this work arises from several challenges observed in existing diffusion-based motion editing approaches:
- **Motion Distortion and Suboptimal Semantic Alignment:** Current methods often rely on heuristic similarity cues or coarse global conditioning. This can lead to undesirable motion distortions in unedited regions and a lack of precise alignment between the textual instruction and the resulting motion edit.
- **Difficulty in Balancing Change and Invariance:** A fundamental challenge in motion editing is ensuring that target regions are precisely modified according to text semantics, while unedited regions retain temporal coherence and realism. Existing approaches struggle with this trade-off, either blurring fine-grained semantics with global conditioning or destroying motion smoothness with excessively localized edits.
- **Insufficient Supervision Mechanisms:** The authors argue that existing supervision strategies for generative models, including those used in motion editing, are insufficient because they primarily focus on positive supervision. They propose that both positive and negative supervision are necessary to achieve the desired balance and precision in motion editing.

To address these issues, the research proposes a unified omni-supervised positive-negative learning framework. This framework is designed to provide comprehensive guidance at multiple levels (feature, motion, and semantic) to ensure that edits are precise, natural, and consistent with both the instruction and the source motion.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The OmniME framework employs an omni-supervised positive-negative learning approach to balance motion modification and preservation in text-driven human motion editing. The methodology integrates three complementary components within a diffusion-based architecture.

**Motion Representation and Task Formalization:**
A human motion is represented as a temporal sequence of joint configurations `X = [x_0, ..., x_F]`, where each frame `x_i` is a 207-dimensional vector comprising global velocity (`v_i`), global orientation (`o_i`), joint rotations (`r_i`), and local joint positions (`p_i`). The task is to synthesize an edited motion `M = G(X, L)` given a source motion `X` and a text instruction `L`, ensuring `M` accurately executes `L` while preserving unedited regions and maintaining kinematic continuity.

**Overall Framework Overview:**
The framework processes both positive (source motion, target text) and negative (unrelated text) text-motion pairs. Text features are encoded using CLIP. The source motion and positive text features are integrated via a Fusion Transformer. These fused features are then passed through an 8-block Diffusion Transformer (DiT) for denoising and motion prediction. The framework integrates positive and negative supervision through the following mechanisms:

1. **Retrospective Feature Supervision:**
 - **Multi-level Intermediate Supervision:** The DiT architecture, consisting of 8 transformer blocks, is augmented with lightweight prediction heads attached to intermediate blocks (specifically, the 2nd, 4th, and 6th). This allows for multi-level supervision during the denoising process, guiding the model to progressively learn coarse-to-fine correspondences.
 - **Loss Calculation:** For each supervised block `l`, its hidden representation `h^(l)` is mapped to the motion space `x_hat^(l)` by a projection head `f^(l)`. A Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss `L^(l)` is computed between `x_hat^(l)` and the ground-truth motion `x`.
 - **Aggregated Retrospective Loss:** The final retrospective supervision loss `L_retro` aggregates these intermediate losses using weighting coefficients `λ_l`, ensuring that the final layer's output remains the primary supervisory signal while intermediate representations also align with the target distribution, which aims to improve optimization stability.

2. **Motion Preservation Mechanism during Editing:**
 - **Frame-wise Motion Similarity Estimation:** This mechanism identifies regions of the motion that should remain largely unchanged. It computes a similarity score between the source motion `x` and its edited counterpart `m` in three stages:
 - **Raw Similarity:** Frame-level similarity `S_R_i` combines rotation-based (`S_Rr_i`) and location-based (`S_Rl_i`) distances using a sliding window.
 - **Normalized Similarity:** Raw similarity values are normalized to a range to ensure scale-invariant comparison across different motions.
 - **MotionSNR Filtering:** A Motion Signal-to-Noise Ratio (MotionSNR) is calculated as the ratio of similarities from top-κ and bottom-κ frames. Samples with higher MotionSNR are retained, indicating subtle changes between source and target, suggesting regions for preservation.
 - **Preservation Loss:** For motion pairs where the MotionSNR exceeds a predefined threshold `τ`, a preservation loss `L_presv` is applied. This loss is an MSE between the edited motion frames `m_i` and source motion frames `x_i`, encouraging reconstruction consistency. This component focuses the model on learning subtle variations while reinforcing the preservation of the original motion structure in high-SNR cases.

3. **Semantic Alignment via Triplet Learning:**
 - **Motion Embedding Generation:** The hidden representation `h^(L)` from the last DiT block is temporally aggregated via mean pooling to obtain a motion embedding `z_m`.
 - **Triplet Loss Application:** A triplet loss `L_triplet` is applied to `z_m`, a positive text feature `z_p` (corresponding to the editing instruction), and a randomly sampled negative text feature `z_n`. The objective of this loss is to minimize the distance between the motion embedding and its corresponding positive text embedding, while simultaneously maximizing its distance from negative text embeddings, with a margin `α`. This mechanism aims to strengthen the semantic correspondence between the edited motion and the target instruction, regularizing the generation process against semantically irrelevant outputs.

**Overall Objective Function:**
The total loss function combines the primary diffusion loss (`L_diff`), a classification loss (`L_cls`), and the three proposed auxiliary losses:
`L_total = L_diff + λ_clsL_cls + λ_retroL_retro + λ_preserveL_preserve + λ_tripletL_triplet`
where `λ` are hyperparameters controlling the weight of each loss component.

**Implementation Details:**
The diffusion process uses 300 steps with a cosine noise schedule and a guidance scale of 2. Text instructions are encoded using a pre-trained CLIP ViT-L/14. The fusion and diffusion transformers comprise 4 and 8 encoder layers, respectively, with 8 attention heads and a latent size of 512. Training uses the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴. Specific weights for auxiliary losses are `λ_retro = 1`, `λ_triplet = 0.01`, and `λ_preserve` set to 0.2 for MotionFix and 0.1 for STANCE Adjustment. Training is performed for 1,500 epochs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of OmniME was evaluated through extensive quantitative and qualitative experiments on two datasets: MotionFix and STANCE Adjustment.

**Quantitative Analysis:**
The primary evaluation metric was a motion-to-motion retrieval protocol, measuring the consistency between generated motions and target motions using features extracted by a pre-trained TMR model. Metrics included R@1, R@2, R@3 (recall at top-k) for batch sizes of 32 and on the full test set, along with the average rank (AvgR).

- **Performance on MotionFix Dataset (Table 1):** OmniME consistently outperformed all baseline methods. For instance, on the full test set, OmniME achieved an R@1 of 32.02 and an AvgR of 13.06, which is an improvement compared to the best baseline, SimMotionEdit* (R@1 of 26.88, AvgR of 20.88). The improvements were also notable in batch recall metrics.
- **Performance on STANCE Adjustment Dataset (Table 2):** Similar trends were observed on this dataset. OmniME achieved an R@1 of 22.45 and an AvgR of 22.77 on the full test set, surpassing SimMotionEdit* (R@1 of 12.76, AvgR of 29.05).
- **Baseline Comparison:** The results indicated that simple diffusion models like MDM performed poorly, highlighting the necessity of source motion information for editing. Models incorporating source motion (MDM-BP) and text conditioning (TMED, MotionReFit, SimMotionEdit) showed progressive improvements, with SimMotionEdit leveraging similarity-based auxiliary losses. OmniME's further gains were attributed to its comprehensive guidance, including negative supervision via triplet loss, positive supervision via preservation loss, and intermediate-layer supervision via retrospective feature loss.

**Qualitative Results (Figures 3 and 4):**
Visual comparisons against SimMotionEdit provided insights into the practical improvements.
- **MotionFix Examples:** OmniME demonstrated improved semantic consistency. For "bob up and down," OmniME produced accurate vertical bobbing, unlike SimMotionEdit which retained original lateral swinging. For "cut down the motion...", OmniME maintained motion integrity without introducing extraneous movements, accurately following the textual constraint.
- **STANCE Adjustment Examples:** OmniME yielded smoother and anatomically consistent motions, avoiding physically implausible poses generated by SimMotionEdit. For "Both arms are slightly raised," OmniME precisely adjusted the arms to shoulder level, capturing the nuance of "slightly," whereas SimMotionEdit kept arms overhead.
Overall, OmniME produced motions that were more natural, coherent, and faithful to textual instructions, exhibiting better semantic understanding, physical plausibility, and motion fidelity.

**Ablation Study (Table 3):**
An ablation study on the MotionFix dataset quantified the contribution of each proposed component.
- Removing any of the three components (retrospective feature supervision, motion preservation, or triplet-based semantic alignment) consistently led to a drop in performance across all metrics.
- The base model (without these components, similar to SimMotionEdit*) showed the lowest performance.
- Each individual module contributed positively: L_retro improved stability, L_preserve enforced consistency in unchanged frames, and L_triplet strengthened text-motion correspondence.
- The full OmniME model, incorporating all three components, achieved the highest performance, confirming their complementary nature and validating the design of the omni-supervised positive-negative learning framework.

**Perceptual Study (Figure 5):**
A user study involving 30 participants evaluated the perceptual quality of OmniME and SimMotionEdit on 20 motion editing samples. Participants rated methods (anonymously) on a 1-10 scale across four criteria: Semantic Alignment, Motion Preservation, Transition Smoothness, and Overall Naturalness. OmniME consistently received higher average ratings across all metrics and datasets, indicating better perceptual quality, including stronger semantic alignment, motion preservation, smoother transitions, and overall naturalness.

**Out-of-Distribution Robustness (Table 4):**
To assess generalization, models trained on MotionFix were tested directly on the STANCE Adjustment dataset without domain-specific tuning. OmniME consistently outperformed SimMotionEdit on all retrieval metrics in this cross-dataset evaluation (e.g., OmniME's AvgR of 7.44 versus SimMotionEdit's 8.80). This suggests that the framework's balance of change and invariance is not overfitted to specific dataset characteristics and possesses generalization capabilities.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes to the field of human motion editing by proposing a novel omni-supervised positive-negative learning framework, OmniME, which addresses the fundamental challenge of balancing precise semantic changes with the preservation of unedited motion regions.

The significance of this work lies in:
- **Advancing Text-Based Motion Editing:** By introducing a unified and comprehensive supervision paradigm, OmniME pushes the state-of-the-art in text-driven human motion editing. The framework’s ability to achieve more accurate editing alignment, reduce motion distortion, and enhance semantic consistency represents an improvement over existing diffusion-based approaches.
- **Addressing Key Limitations:** The explicit consideration of both positive and negative supervision, along with multi-level feature consistency and motion preservation mechanisms, directly addresses the limitations of previous methods that struggled with maintaining motion realism and semantic fidelity simultaneously.
- **Improved Motion Quality:** Both quantitative metrics and qualitative user studies indicate that OmniME produces edited motions that are not only more aligned with textual instructions but also appear more natural, coherent, and physically plausible. This enhances the practical utility of text-based control for motion generation.
- **Enhanced Generalization:** The demonstrated robustness in out-of-distribution tests suggests that the principles behind OmniME's balancing act are generalizable beyond specific training datasets, indicating its potential for broader application.

The potential impact of OmniME extends across several domains:
- **Animation and Content Creation:** Animators could benefit from more intuitive and precise text-based tools for modifying character movements, significantly streamlining workflows and enabling fine-grained artistic control without extensive manual keyframing. This could lead to faster content generation and easier iteration on motion designs.
- **Human-Robot Interaction:** The ability to generate and edit human-like motions with high fidelity could enhance the naturalness of robot behaviors and interactions, making robots more relatable and intuitive for humans to engage with.
- **Virtual Reality and Gaming:** Developing more realistic and controllable virtual characters through precise motion editing can improve immersion and user experience in virtual environments and video games.
- **Autonomous Behavior Simulation:** In fields requiring simulations of human behavior, such as crowd dynamics or safety training, OmniME could enable the creation of diverse and contextually appropriate motions with higher fidelity.

Future directions, such as extending the framework to multi-person motion editing and interactive motion refinement, suggest a continued trajectory toward more versatile and user-friendly motion synthesis tools, further solidifying the impact of this research on controllable motion generation from natural language.
