# Towards Generalizable Zero-Shot Manipulation via Translating Human Interaction Plans

> [!summary] Summary
> HOPMan factorizes zero-shot manipulation into two stages: a goal-conditioned diffusion model that hallucinates future human hand and object segmentation masks from an initial and goal image, and a transformer translation policy converting those masks into closed-loop robot joint actions. Trained on roughly 150,000 passive web video clips plus about 600 paired human-robot trajectories, it executes 16 skills over 40 objects across 100 real-world tasks with no deployment-time training.

## Problem

- Directly learning policies from robot interaction data does not scale, needing years of collection per task, object, and skill, yet still covering mainly picking, placing, pushing.
- Human-video pretraining approaches mostly yield visual representations or affordances, so they still demand many in-domain robot demonstrations and hours of deployment-time online training.
- Methods using paired human-robot data need tightly aligned in-domain recordings and task-specific policies, so they cannot exploit uncurated passive web video for diverse skills.

## Method

- A human-plan prediction ==diffusion== model: a time-conditioned ==U-Net== denoises seven future hand-object mask frames at 64x64, channel-wise conditioned on the initial RGB frame and goal frame.
- A robot-action ==translation model==: a ==transformer== closed-loop policy taking predicted masks, observation history, and past actions through ==ResNet18== encoders, predicting 10-step chunks of 8-dimensional joint targets. Together these two stages form **HOPMan**.
- A ==hallucinated-pairing== data pipeline that segments the robot from robot-only videos, inpaints it out, then guided-inpaints a plausible human hand at the end-effector to synthesize paired supervision.

## Results

- Evaluated on about **100** tasks spanning **16** skills and **40** object types, HOPMan exceeded goal-conditioned behavior cloning, a MimicPlay adaptation, affordance-conditioned VRB, and passive-video-only baselines under standard and strong generalization.
- Translation-model ablation with ground-truth masks showed combined **400** paired plus **1000** hallucinated trajectories outperformed **400** paired alone, confirming imperfect synthetic hand pairs still improve generalization.
- In-the-wild deployment on a mobile-base Franka in unmodified kitchens and offices retained success after fine-tuning on only about **200** extra paired trajectories, with unseen object categories and skills.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Factorizing policy learning into embodiment-agnostic plan prediction plus a small translation module lets abundant out-of-domain video carry generalization while in-domain data stays tiny.
> - Segmentation masks of hand and object are a useful intermediate: they abstract away background and appearance, so full-trajectory motion transfers better than contact-point affordances.
> - Synthetic pairing via inpainting converts cheap robot-only data into paired supervision, but recovering fine finger articulation from web video still blocks dexterous and long-horizon tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a central challenge in robot learning: developing generalist robots capable of performing diverse, everyday manipulation tasks on unseen objects in varied real-world scenarios, and doing so without deployment-time training (zero-shot).

Current approaches in robot learning often encounter limitations that this work seeks to mitigate. A common strategy involves collecting extensive robot interaction datasets for imitation learning. However, this method is resource-intensive and does not scale effectively for achieving broad generalization across diverse tasks, objects, and skills due to the physical constraints of data collection. Prior efforts have demonstrated that even state-of-the-art systems require substantial on-robot data collection, often limiting their capabilities to fundamental skills such as picking, placing, and pushing.

Other lines of research have explored leveraging out-of-domain data, particularly human videos, to augment robot learning. These efforts have primarily focused on pre-training visual representations or learning robotic affordances. While beneficial, these methods typically necessitate substantial additional in-domain robot demonstrations for policy learning or require significant deployment-time training. Some approaches have focused on learning task-specific action priors from human data, but these usually entail separate policies for each task and often require curated human video datasets or perfectly aligned human-robot data, limiting scalability to diverse, uncurated web videos. Furthermore, methods that leverage passive web data for robot manipulation have generally been restricted to coarse, open-loop motions, lacking the capability for dexterous grasping and diverse skill execution.

This research distinguishes itself by proposing a factorized approach that aims to overcome these limitations. It seeks to bridge the gap between the vast, uncurated passive human video data available on the web and the practical control of robots in diverse, real-world settings. By learning how a human would accomplish a task (a "human plan") separately from "translating" this plan to a robot's embodiment, the work positions itself as a method for enabling generalizable zero-shot manipulation with a single policy across a wide range of skills and objects, without requiring extensive in-domain robot data or on-the-fly training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop robotic systems that can perform a diverse range of manipulation tasks on generic, previously unseen objects in real-world scenarios in a zero-shot manner, meaning without requiring additional training or fine-tuning at deployment time.

This overarching goal is motivated by several challenges and limitations inherent in current robot learning paradigms:

- **Scalability of Data Collection:** Achieving broad generalization in robot manipulation necessitates exposure to an immense variety of tasks, objects, and interaction scenarios. Directly collecting such vast and diverse robot interaction data is prohibitively expensive, time-consuming, and bottlenecked by physical access to robots. Existing methods, even those demonstrating diverse manipulation capabilities, often rely on years of on-robot data collection and remain limited in the scope of skills they can acquire.
- **Leveraging Abundant Human Data:** Humans routinely perform a plethora of manipulation tasks, and a wealth of passive videos documenting these interactions is available on the web. This constitutes a rich, scalable data source. However, directly translating human actions from videos to robot control is challenging due to inherent differences in embodiment (e.g., hand structure vs. gripper, arm kinematics), necessitating a robust method to bridge this domain gap.
- **Generalization to Novel Scenarios:** For practical utility, robots must be able to interact with novel objects and execute skills in unseen environments "out of the box." Many existing robot learning approaches tend to be task-specific or require substantial in-domain data for new objects and skills, hindering zero-shot generalization.

To address these motivations, the research establishes the following specific objectives:

- **Factorize the Learning Problem:** Decompose the complex task of learning a generalizable robot policy into two more manageable components:
 1. **Interaction Plan Prediction:** Learn to predict a high-level "human plan" that captures the essential changes undergone by objects and manipulators during an interaction. This module is designed to leverage large-scale, passive human video data, thereby bypassing the robot data scarcity issue for this stage.
 2. **Plan Translation:** Develop a mechanism to "translate" the predicted human interaction plan into concrete actions executable by a robot. This module is intended to be robust enough to operate with a limited amount of in-domain paired human-robot data and to generalize to tasks and objects not explicitly seen during its own training, by virtue of conditioning on a generalized human plan.
- **Develop a Generalizable Plan Representation:** Define a structured, task-agnostic representation for human interaction plans, specifically using semantic masks of hands and objects, to abstract away irrelevant visual details and facilitate generalization across different objects and skills.
- **Enable Diverse Skill Repertoires:** Allow the robot to acquire and execute a diverse set of core manipulation skills—beyond simple pick-and-place or pushing—such as manipulating articulated objects, scooping, pouring, twisting, stacking, and swiping.
- **Demonstrate Zero-Shot Generalization:** Empirically evaluate the system's ability to perform over 16 distinct manipulation skills on 40 different objects, encompassing approximately 100 real-world tasks, with structured evaluation across multiple axes of generalization (object categories, instances, configurations, and skills) in both table-top and in-the-wild settings.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named HOPMan (Hand Object Plan for robotic Manipulation), adopts a factorized policy model that separates the learning of interaction plans from their execution on a robot. This factorization allows leveraging large-scale passive human videos for plan prediction and a smaller set of robot-specific data for plan translation.

**A. The Human-Plan Prediction Model**

The first stage involves predicting a plausible human interaction plan. Instead of generating future frames in image space, the model focuses on predicting the motion of the human hand and the manipulated object in the form of semantic masks. This approach abstracts away task-irrelevant visual details, concentrating on the functional aspects of the interaction.

- **Representation of Human Plan:** A sequence of K semantic mask frames (M_1:K), where each frame is an image encoded with specific colors for hand pixels (green), object pixels (red), and background (black). For the experiments, K was set to 7, corresponding to uniformly spaced frames within a 2-second video clip.
- **Architecture:** A diffusion model is employed for this prediction task. Diffusion models are generative models capable of producing high-quality samples by gradually denoising a signal.
- **Training Data:** The model is trained on diverse passive human videos from large-scale datasets such as EpicKitchens and Ego4D. Ground-truth hand-object masks are extracted from these videos using existing annotation benchmarks (Visor for EpicKitchens) or off-the-shelf predictors (for Ego4D). Approximately 150,000 video clips were curated for training.
- **Training Process:** The diffusion model's U-Net architecture is trained to predict the noise added during a forward diffusion process. The input to the noise predictor (ϵ_θ) at a given diffusion step (t) is a channel-wise concatenation of the initial RGB frame (X_0), the goal RGB frame (X_g), and the noisy mask frames (M_t_1:K). The output is the predicted noise with the same dimensionality. The model optimizes a squared error loss between the true noise and the predicted noise (Equation 1). This process enables both unconditional prediction (conditioned only on X_0) and goal-conditioned prediction (conditioned on X_0 and X_g). The diffusion model operates at a resolution of 64x64 for the predicted mask frames.
- **Inference:** During deployment, given an initial scene image (X_0) and a desired goal image (X_g), the model generates a sequence of plausible future hand and object masks (M_1:K) by reversing the diffusion process.

**B. The Robot-Action Translation Model**

The second stage of HOPMan is responsible for translating the predicted human-interaction plan (sequence of masks) into a sequence of executable actions for a physical robot.

- **Architecture:** A transformer-based model functions as a closed-loop policy π. This policy is queried at each time step (t) during deployment.
- **Inputs:** The translation model receives the predicted future hand-object masks (M_1:K) from the plan prediction model, along with a history of past RGB observations (X_≤t) and previous robot actions (a_<t). Observations are 224x224 RGB images, processed by ResNet18 backbones. The predicted masks are upsampled to 224x224 and also processed by ResNet18. A history of three steps (past two observations and actions, plus the current observation) is used.
- **Outputs:** The model predicts robot actions for H steps into the future. In the experiments, H was set to 10, for trajectories with a total length of 40 steps. The action space is 8-dimensional: 7 dimensions for target joint positions and 1 dimension for end-effector (gripper) open/close state. Predicting target joint positions directly, rather than delta positions, was found beneficial for smooth motions.
- **Training Data for Translation Model:** The translation model requires paired human-robot data. Two approaches were used to obtain this data:
 1. **Collected Paired Demonstrations:** A human operator tele-operates a robot to perform a task. Subsequently, or in an identical parallel setup, a human performs a similar manipulation with an object. This process yielded approximately 600 trajectories collected over three days.
 2. **Hallucinated Paired Data:** To augment the dataset, robot-only trajectories were leveraged. Robot masks were obtained (e.g., via simulation) and then the robot was inpainted out of the scene. Subsequently, a plausible human hand was inpainted into the scene around the location of the robot's end-effector, using techniques from hand inpainting, effectively converting robot trajectories into human-like trajectories. This approach generated approximately 1000 additional hallucinated trajectories.
- **Training Details:** The transformer encoder has 4 self-attention blocks, and the decoder has 7 cross-attention blocks, with hidden dimensions of 512.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates HOPMan's capability for robot manipulation through experiments in diverse real-world settings, demonstrating generalization across various object categories, instances, configurations, and skills.

**A. Experiment Settings and Generalization Criteria**

The experiments were conducted in two main settings:
- **Table-Top Manipulation:** Involves a fixed robot (Franka Panda arm with a Robotiq gripper) and camera, interacting with 16 different skills (e.g., pouring, pushing, picking/placing, articulated object manipulation, scooping, stacking, unrolling) and 40 object types, totaling around 100 distinct tasks.
- **In-the-Wild Manipulation:** The same robot and camera are mounted on a mobile base, which is moved across natural kitchen and office scenes. For this setting, the translation model was fine-tuned with an additional 200 paired trajectories collected with the mobile robot. This setup presents a greater challenge due to scene variations beyond just objects and skills.

To systematically evaluate generalization, the authors defined a structured criterion:
- **Task Definition:** A tuple of (object category, object instance, skill).
- **Mild Generalization (MG):** Generalization to unseen configurations (position, orientation, minor lighting changes) for previously seen object instances and skills.
- **Standard Generalization (G):**
 - **Instance Generalization (Ga):** Generalization to unseen object instances (variations in shape, color, texture) for seen skills.
 - **Unseen Combinations (Gb):** Generalization to unseen (object category, skill) pairs, where both the object category and the skill have been seen independently during training.
- **Strong Generalization (SG):**
 - **Object Category Completely Unseen (SGa):** Generalization to object categories that were entirely absent from the training data.
 - **Skill Completely Unseen (SGb):** Generalization to manipulation skills that were never observed in any context during training.

Success was defined by manually inspecting whether the object was brought to the desired configuration as depicted in the goal image.

**B. Quantitative Performance**

HOPMan demonstrated notable success rates across the diverse evaluation settings and generalization categories:
- **Table-Top Manipulation:**
 - Mild Generalization (MG): 90%
 - Standard Generalization (G): Instance Generalization (Ga) 82%, Unseen Combinations (Gb) 78%
 - Strong Generalization (SG): Object Category Completely Unseen (SGa) 65%, Skill Completely Unseen (SGb) 61%
- **In-the-Wild Manipulation:** (As expected, slightly lower due to increased complexity)
 - Mild Generalization (MG): 83%
 - Standard Generalization (G): Instance Generalization (Ga) 75%, Unseen Combinations (Gb) 71%
 - Strong Generalization (SG): Object Category Completely Unseen (SGa) 58%, Skill Completely Unseen (SGb) 55%

**C. Baselines and Ablations**

The performance of HOPMan (using combined paired and hallucinated data, P+H) was compared against several baselines and ablations:
- **Baselines:**
 - **Goal-conditioned Behavior Cloning (BC):** A transformer-based policy trained on all available robot data (1600 trajectories) without conditioning on human plans.
 - **MimicPlay (MP) Adaptation:** An adaptation of a prior method, trained on 400 paired human-robot trajectories.
 - **VRB:** An approach utilizing affordance models for affordance-conditioned imitation learning.
 - **H2R:** A baseline designed for coarse manipulation exclusively from passive human videos.
- **Translation Model Ablations:** To isolate the impact of data sources for the translation model, three versions were evaluated using ground-truth human hand-object trajectories:
 - **P+H:** Trained on combined paired (400) and hallucinated (1000) data.
 - **P:** Trained on only paired data (400 trajectories).
 - **H:** Trained on only hallucinated data (1000 trajectories).

**Key Findings from Comparisons and Ablations:**
- **Superior Generalization of HOPMan:** HOPMan (P+H) significantly outperformed all baselines, particularly in Standard (G) and Strong Generalization (SG) categories. For instance, in table-top SGa, HOPMan (P+H) achieved 65% success, while BC, MP, VRB, and H2R scored 28%, 35%, 22%, and 8% respectively. This indicates the effectiveness of the factorized approach in leveraging diverse data for generalization.
- **Effectiveness of Hallucinated Data:** Ablation studies on the translation model demonstrated that training with combined paired and hallucinated data (P+H) yielded the highest success rates (e.g., 78% for SGa). Notably, training with only hallucinated data (H) often performed better than training with only paired data (P) alone (e.g., 70% vs. 60% for SGa), suggesting that imperfect, hallucinated data can still effectively improve the translation model's generalization capabilities, complementing the limited amount of collected paired data.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that offers several contributions and potential impacts on the field of robot learning:

- **Addressing the Robot Data Scarcity Problem:** By factorizing robot learning into plan prediction and plan translation, the work provides a method to leverage readily available, internet-scale passive human videos for a crucial part of the learning process (human interaction plan prediction). This significantly reduces the reliance on expensive and time-consuming robot interaction data collection, which is a major bottleneck in developing generalist robots.
- **Enabling Generalizable Zero-Shot Manipulation:** The ability of HOPMan to perform a diverse set of manipulation tasks on unseen objects and in novel environments without deployment-time training represents a step towards truly generalist robots. This zero-shot capability is critical for practical deployment in dynamic, unstructured real-world settings.
- **Expanding the Repertoire of Robot Skills:** The framework demonstrates success across a broad spectrum of 16 manipulation skills, moving beyond the frequently studied pick-and-place or pushing tasks. This expansion of capabilities is essential for robots to meaningfully interact with and assist in everyday human environments.
- **Novelty in Data Utilization:** The use of semantic hand-object masks as a generalizable representation for human interaction plans is a novel contribution. This representation effectively abstracts away embodiment differences between humans and robots, facilitating the "translation" process and enhancing generalization. Furthermore, the demonstrated effectiveness of hallucinated paired data highlights a scalable method for augmenting training datasets for the translation module.
- **Structured Generalization Evaluation:** The proposal of a structured criterion for evaluating generalization (Mild, Standard, and Strong generalization across object categories, instances, and skills) contributes to a more rigorous and consistent assessment methodology within the robot learning community.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** While demonstrating strong generalization, the authors acknowledge limitations such as handling highly dexterous manipulation and composing multiple skills for long-horizon tasks. This work lays a foundation for future research in these areas, indicating that advancements in recovering precise hand and finger articulations from web videos, and in task planning, could further enhance the capabilities of such factorized systems.

In summary, this research indicates a pathway towards developing more capable and adaptable robotic systems by efficiently transferring knowledge from large-scale human interaction data to robotic platforms, thereby contributing to the development of robust, general-purpose robots for real-world applications.
