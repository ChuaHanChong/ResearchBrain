# MM-Hand: A 21-DOF Multi-modal Modular Dexterous Robotic Hand with Remote Actuation

> [!summary] Summary
> MM-Hand is a 21-DoF multi-modal modular dexterous robotic hand that utilizes remote tendon-driven actuation to reduce end-effector inertia and integrate extensive sensing. It demonstrates a practical fingertip force of 25 N through 1-meter remote sheaths and maintains joint angle control accuracy below 0.1° despite long-distance compliant transmission challenges.

## Problem

- Existing dexterous robotic hands often suffer from high end-effector mass and inertia due to integrated motors, limiting dynamic manipulation and payload capacity.
- Conventional designs occupy valuable internal volume, restricting the number of DoFs and the integration of comprehensive sensing modules.
- Remote tendon-driven actuation, while reducing hand mass, introduces challenges such as increased friction from long tendon-sheath paths and arm-motion-induced tendon length variations, which can affect control accuracy.

## Method

- Developed **MM-Hand**, a ==21-DoF modular dexterous hand== with a ==remote tendon-driven actuation system== that relocates motors to an external hub.
- Integrated a ==multimodal sensing suite==: joint encoders, tactile sensors, and ==in-palm stereo vision==, leveraging the freed internal volume.
- Established an analytical framework to model ==arm-motion-induced coupling disturbances== and ==long-distance friction loss==, informing design and closed-loop control strategies.

## Results

- MM-Hand achieved a practical fingertip force of **~25 N** through a **1-meter remote tendon sheath**, demonstrating viable load capacity despite long-distance transmission.
- The control system demonstrated a **steady-state joint angle error below 0.1°** in step-response tests and successfully tracked sinusoidal inputs.
- Experiments identified the **metal spring tube** as a suitable sheath material due to its low friction, and the integrated in-palm stereo vision system provided dense depth estimation for environmental perception.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Long tendon-sheath friction and the resulting delay have a more substantial impact on control performance than manipulator-induced disturbances on tendon-driven motion.
> - Modular, 3D-printable finger and palm structures with quick tendon connectors significantly enhance manufacturability, maintainability, and simplify internal routing for tendons and sensor wires.
> - Careful selection of sheath materials and an analytical understanding of transmission losses are crucial for achieving practical load capacity and control accuracy in remote tendon-driven systems.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous robotic hands represent a significant area of research in robotics, with the objective of achieving human-like manipulation capabilities for various applications such as humanoid robotics, teleoperation, prosthetics, and learning-based manipulation. The design of such hands is constrained by factors including actuation density, end-effector inertia, sensor integration, thermal management, manufacturability, and long-term maintenance.

Existing dexterous hands are categorized by their transmission principles: rigid and compliant. Rigid transmission systems, which include direct-driven and linkage-driven designs, offer short transmission paths and high control accuracy. However, they typically involve embedding motors and reducers directly within the hand structure, which increases the hand's mass and inertia, occupies internal volume, and can lead to issues with heat dissipation and impact robustness. Examples of such hands include the Kitech-Hand, LEAP Hand, Sharpa Wave, and PsiBot Psi-H1.

Compliant transmission systems utilize non-rigid elements to transmit force. Tendon-driven actuation is noted as a practical approach for high-Degree-of-Freedom (DoF) dexterous hands due to its ability to preserve anthropomorphic morphology and spatially decouple actuators from joints, often with cost-effectiveness. Artificial-muscle, pneumatic, and hydraulic systems also fall under compliant transmission, offering high compliance and force density but often requiring complex sealing and fabrication under high-load conditions.

Within tendon-driven systems, proximal actuation places motors in the palm or forearm, reducing finger inertia compared to fully integrated designs. However, this still imposes constraints on payload capacity for robotic arms, heat dissipation, and actuator size. Remote tendon-driven actuation further separates the actuators by relocating them to a standalone motor hub, transmitting power through long tendon-sheath paths. This architecture is considered advantageous for lightweight humanoid and mobile platforms as it reduces hand mass and frees internal volume for sensing and structural design. Prior examples of remote tendon-driven hands include the Pantheon Hand and ABCDL Hand. This approach, however, introduces challenges related to increased friction from long tendon-sheath transmission and variations in tendon path length dependent on robotic arm motion, which can affect joint control accuracy.

This work addresses the challenges associated with remote tendon-driven actuation by developing a modular, multimodal-sensing dexterous hand that integrates analytical understanding of transmission losses and disturbances with practical design choices, aiming to provide a lightweight, sensor-rich, and maintainable platform for dexterous manipulation research.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind the development of MM-Hand stems from the inherent trade-offs in existing dexterous hand designs, particularly the challenges associated with high-DoF hands regarding compact actuation, rich sensing integration, and reliable thermal management. Conventional designs frequently lead to increased end-effector mass and heat accumulation within the hand, while occupying valuable in-hand space that could otherwise be used for additional DoFs or sensing modules.

Remote tendon-driven actuation is identified as a promising alternative by allowing motors to be relocated to a robot base or an external motor hub. This strategy aims to liberate the fingers and palm, enabling designs with more DoFs, enhanced sensing capabilities, and more maintainable mechanical structures. However, this remote actuation paradigm introduces its own set of challenges, specifically increased friction over long tendon-sheath paths and variations in tendon path length that are dependent on the robotic arm's motion, both of which can impact the precision of finger joint control.

The key objectives of this research, as outlined by the authors, are to:

- **Establish an analytical framework** for remote tendon-driven transmission. This framework is designed to model and understand arm-motion-induced coupling disturbances and long-distance friction loss, providing a basis for informed design decisions regarding routing, motor hub configuration, and closed-loop joint control.
- **Develop a high-DoF, modular, and 3D-printed hand structure** that specifically accommodates the requirements of remote tendon-driven actuation. This involves designing the fingers and palm to reserve internal space for tendon routing, comprehensive sensing integration, and ease of maintenance.
- **Integrate a multimodal sensing and feedback system** into the hand. This system includes joint encoders for precise position feedback, tactile sensors for contact information, and in-palm stereo vision for environmental perception. The aim is to create an open and extensible hardware platform that supports diverse dexterous manipulation research.
- **Validate the performance** of the designed system, particularly in terms of transmission efficiency, output force capacity despite long-distance tendon routing, control accuracy in both static and dynamic arm configurations, and the functionality of its multimodal sensing capabilities.

These objectives collectively aim to mitigate the typical drawbacks of remote actuation while maximizing the benefits of a lightweight, high-DoF, and sensor-rich robotic hand, thereby contributing to the development of more capable and versatile robotic manipulation platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The MM-Hand design and development involved a multi-faceted approach encompassing theoretical analysis, mechanical design, electronic system integration, and control strategy.

**Theoretical Analysis of Remote Actuation:**
The work began with an analytical framework for remote tendon-driven actuation, specifically for the Bowden-cable transmission architecture where tendons are routed through sheaths mounted along the outer surface of a robotic arm. This approach was chosen to reduce arm-motion-induced tendon length variation and improve platform compatibility, despite introducing higher tendon-sheath friction.
The analysis derived an equation for tendon length variation, $l_t = L - \frac{D-d}{2}\phi$, where $L$ is sheath length, $D$ is sheath diameter, $d$ is tendon diameter, and $\phi$ is the accumulated bending angle. This equation indicates that tendon length variation is linearly dependent on the total bending angle, leading to configuration-dependent transmission error.
Friction analysis, consistent with a capstan-type model, derived the relationship for tension loss: $T(L) = T(0) \exp(\mu\phi)$, where $T(0)$ is initial tension, $T(L)$ is tension at length $L$, and $\mu$ is the friction coefficient. This exponential relationship highlighted the importance of minimizing sheath curvature and selecting low-friction materials.

**Mechanical Design:**
MM-Hand is designed with 21 DoFs across 5 fingers.
- **Fingers:** The four long fingers (index, middle, ring, little) share a modular architecture, each providing four DoFs (MCP abduction/adduction, and three flexion joints at MCP, PIP, DIP). These joints employ a single-tendon, spring-return actuation scheme, where tendon pulling causes flexion and passive elastic elements provide extension. The phalanges utilize a vertically split, multi-piece design for improved 3D printability, easier tendon installation, and sensor wire routing. The thumb has five DoFs, reusing the split modular phalange construction. Its CMC1 joint, unlike other joints, uses antagonistic dual-tendon actuation to mitigate parasitic torques from tendon routing.
- **Hand/Palm:** The palm features a two-part enclosure. An in-palm quick tendon connector box is integrated to centralize tendon management from fingers and the remote motor hub. This design allows for rapid replacement of finger or arm-side tendon segments without full re-routing, enhancing modularity and maintainability.
- **Motor Hub:** For spring-return joints, the motor hub is designed to minimize additional resistance, with each tendon leaving the spool tangentially and entering the sheath directly to ensure reliable spring-driven reset. Software-based pretensioning is implemented, where each spring-return joint is initialized with a predefined pretension angle, and the motor reels the tendon back to prevent slack.

**Sensors and Electronics:**
The hand integrates multimodal perception capabilities:
- **Joint Angle Sensing:** AMS AS5047P encoders are mounted on each finger joint.
- **Tactile Sensing:** PaXini Elite sensors are used, with S2015 at the fingertips and S1610 at the phalanges.
- **In-Palm Stereo Vision:** A stereo camera pair is integrated within the palm.
- **Motor-Side Feedback:** Motor encoders provide additional feedback.
To reduce wiring complexity, finger sensors are organized into two shared SPI buses (one for encoders, one for tactile sensors), aggregated on a palm PCB. This PCB collects data and transmits it via CAN to a motor control PCB. The system communicates with a host computer via USB.

**Feedback Control:**
A sensor-based closed-loop control strategy is adopted, utilizing joint-mounted absolute encoders for PID control directly in joint space. This approach compensates for transmission nonlinearity, hysteresis, and disturbances introduced by long tendon transmissions. To prevent tendon slack during rapid release, the motor release speed is limited to match the spring recovery rate. Motor current is monitored for abnormal load detection, triggering a system stop to prevent damage.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of MM-Hand involved several experiments to validate its core properties, including transmission efficiency, output force, control robustness, and sensing functionality. Dyneema tendons with 1mm diameter were used.

**Sheath-Tendon Friction Measurement:**
Four types of sheaths were tested: Shimano SP41, standard PTFE tube, metal spring tube, and Bambu printer feeder tube. The friction test setup involved pulling a tendon loaded with a 2 kg weight through sheaths bent at wrap angles from 0° to 180° and disk diameters from 10–100 mm.
- **Result:** Kinetic friction generally followed the exponential trend predicted by the theoretical model. The standard PTFE tube showed relatively high friction. The Bambu feeder tube, also PTFE-based but with higher extrusion quality, exhibited lower friction. The metal spring tube showed similarly low friction, attributed to its corrugated structure reducing tendon contact points. Shimano SP41 had low kinetic friction but exhibited high static friction. The metal spring tube was selected for subsequent experiments due to its simplicity and balanced performance.

**Fingertip Load Performance:**
A single-finger fingertip force test was conducted by fixing a finger and pressing its fingertip against a force gauge. The base joint was driven by a servo through a metal spring sheath, with lengths of 0.1 m and 1 m tested to compare proximal and remote actuation.
- **Result:** The 0.1 m sheath achieved a peak force of approximately 33 N. The 1 m sheath, representing remote actuation, reached about 25 N. The 8 N difference was attributed primarily to additional friction loss in the longer, slightly curved sheath. In both cases, the force gradually decreased after the peak, likely due to slow sheath deformation under sustained load. This demonstrated a practical load capacity despite long-distance tendon routing.

**Control Accuracy:**
The control system's performance was evaluated using PID-based closed-loop motor position control with joint encoders.
- **Step-Response Test:** Joint 0 was commanded with a step input. The steady-state error was found to be below 0.1°. A delay of approximately 0.2 seconds was observed between the onset of motor actuation and the actual onset of joint motion, attributed to tendon-sheath friction.
- **Dynamic Tracking Test:** Joint 0 tracked a 0.5 Hz sinusoidal reference input over 20 seconds. This was performed under two conditions: (a) with the manipulator stationary and (b) with the manipulator executing a circular trajectory to introduce transmission disturbances.
 - **Result:** A similar delay to the step-response test was observed in sinusoidal tracking. While tracking accuracy degraded under manipulator motion, the experiments indicated that friction-introduced delay had a more substantial impact on controlling performance compared to manipulator-induced disturbances on tendon-driven motion.

**In-Palm Stereo Depth Sensing:**
The in-palm stereo camera pair was used for dense depth sensing. A pretrained RAFT-Stereo network estimated disparity from rectified image pairs, which was then converted to metric depth.
- **Result:** The system successfully provided stereo depth estimation, demonstrating the functionality of the in-palm vision system for perception during manipulation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The MM-Hand represents a contribution to the field of dexterous robotics by addressing several critical design challenges associated with high-DoF robotic hands, particularly through its implementation of remote tendon-driven actuation.

**Lightweight and Sensor-Rich Platform:** By relocating actuators away from the hand, MM-Hand significantly reduces end-effector mass and inertia, which is crucial for dynamic manipulation, minimizing payload constraints on robotic arms, and enhancing safety in human-robot interaction. The freed internal volume allows for the integration of extensive multimodal sensing, including joint angle encoders, tactile sensors, and in-palm stereo vision, providing a rich perceptual input for complex manipulation tasks. This sensor suite can facilitate research in areas such as tactile-based control, object recognition, and precise in-hand reorientation.

**Modularity and Maintainability:** The modular 3D-printed finger and palm structures, combined with quick tendon connectors, improve manufacturability, assembly, and repairability. This modularity allows for easier replacement of damaged components and adaptation to different research needs, thereby reducing the operational cost and downtime associated with high-DoF robotic hands. The split phalanx design also simplifies internal routing of tendons and sensor wires.

**Validated Remote Actuation Performance:** The theoretical analysis and experimental validation of tendon-sheath friction and path length variation provide insights into the challenges of remote actuation. The demonstration of a 25 N fingertip force through a 1-meter remote sheath transmission, alongside a steady-state joint angle error below 0.1°, indicates that the system can achieve practical load capacity and control accuracy despite the inherent complexities of long-distance compliant transmission. The findings also highlight the dominant impact of friction-introduced delay on control performance, informing future improvements.

**Open-Source Hardware and Software:** A notable aspect of this work is the commitment to making all hardware designs and software frameworks fully open-source. This initiative aims to foster community engagement, accelerate research and development in dexterous manipulation, and lower the barrier to entry for other researchers and institutions to build upon or adapt the MM-Hand design. This open-source approach can lead to broader adoption, collaborative improvements, and diverse applications of the technology.

**Future Research and Applications:** The MM-Hand provides a robust platform for advancing research in dexterous manipulation, including learning-based control, teleoperation, and human-robot collaboration. The current limitations, such as tendon friction delays, tendon wear, and challenges with spring-return force for abduction-adduction joints, also define clear directions for future work (e.g., MM-Hand 1.1). Its design principles could inform the development of next-generation humanoid hands and prosthetic devices, where low weight, high DoF, and integrated sensing are critical.
