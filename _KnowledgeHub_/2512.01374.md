# Stabilizing Reinforcement Learning with LLMs: Formulation and Practices

> [!summary] Summary
> The Qwen Team at Alibaba Inc. developed a theoretical formulation that justifies token-level optimization for sequence-level rewards in Large Language Model (LLM) reinforcement learning, identifying training–inference discrepancy and policy staleness as key instability factors. Their work also provides empirically validated strategies, including Routing Replay and clipping, to achieve stable and high-performing RL training for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) LLMs.

## Problem

- Applying reinforcement learning (RL) to Large Language Models (LLMs) often suffers from instability due to the mismatch between sequence-level rewards and token-level optimization objectives.
- The theoretical soundness of optimizing sequence-level rewards via token-level objectives in LLMs was not clearly established, leading to empirical instability and potential training collapse.
- Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures introduce further complexities, as dynamic expert routing can invalidate standard importance sampling ratios and exacerbate RL training instability.

## Method

- A novel theoretical formulation is proposed, demonstrating that a ==token-level objective== can serve as a first-order approximation to the true expected sequence-level reward under conditions where ==training-inference discrepancy== and policy staleness are minimized.
- The =='MiniRL' algorithm== is introduced, incorporating importance sampling (IS) correction to address training-inference discrepancy, group-normalization for variance reduction, and a PPO-inspired clipping mechanism to restrain policy staleness.
- Specific =='Routing Replay' techniques== (Vanilla R2 and Rollout R3) are developed to stabilize RL training for ==Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models== by managing expert routing during gradient updates.

## Results

- On-policy training demonstrated the highest stability and performance with basic MiniRL (including IS correction), while off-policy training critically required Routing Replay and clipping to prevent collapse and sustain performance.
- Rollout Routing Replay (**R3**) proved superior under high off-policiness (larger global batch sizes), whereas Vanilla Routing Replay (**R2**) performed better under low off-policiness conditions for MoE models.
- Stable RL training consistently led to comparable final model performance across various cold-start initializations, indicating that long-term training stability can mitigate the impact of initial model quality.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The stability of token-level RL optimization for sequence-level rewards hinges on minimizing the gap between the target policy and the rollout policy, primarily affected by training–inference discrepancy and policy staleness.
> - Importance sampling correction for the training–inference discrepancy is a fundamental requirement for the first-order approximation to hold, preventing rapid training collapse in LLM-RL.
> - For MoE models, dynamic expert routing amplifies instability, necessitating specific mitigation strategies like Routing Replay to preserve the validity of importance sampling and enable stable training.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has become a cornerstone paradigm for enhancing the capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs), enabling them to perform complex problem-solving tasks, align with human preferences, and generate more helpful and safe responses. Techniques like Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) have popularized the use of RL in LLM fine-tuning. However, the application of RL to LLMs presents unique challenges.

A primary challenge, which this paper directly addresses, is the mismatch between how rewards are typically assigned and how policy updates are performed. In LLMs, rewards are often "sequence-level," meaning a single scalar score is assigned to an entire generated response. In contrast, many mainstream RL algorithms, such as REINFORCE and its variants, operate on a "token-level" optimization objective, updating the policy based on individual token probabilities. The fundamental question this paper tackles is whether optimizing a sequence-level reward via a token-level objective is theoretically sound and empirically stable, and under what conditions. Previous works have either explored direct sequence-level optimization (Zheng et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025a) or have noted instability without a clear explanation.

Furthermore, the advent of Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models has introduced another layer of complexity. MoE models dynamically route different parts of the input to a subset of specialized "experts," making the policy's behavior highly conditional. This dynamic routing mechanism can invalidate standard token-level importance sampling ratios, posing specific challenges for RL training stability in these increasingly popular architectures (Guo et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2025). The paper highlights that current RL practices often implicitly rely on conditions that are not always met, especially in off-policy settings or with MoE models, leading to training instability or collapse.

This work contributes to the broader landscape by providing a principled theoretical justification for common token-level RL objectives for LLMs and systematically investigating practical techniques to maintain training stability. By offering both a novel formulation and extensive empirical validation on large-scale MoE models, it aims to clarify long-standing ambiguities and provide concrete "recipes" for stable RL training, thereby facilitating the scalable and reliable application of RL to future LLM advancements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research paper are twofold:

1. **Theoretically formulate and justify token-level optimization for sequence-level rewards in LLM-RL:** The authors aim to propose a novel formulation that explains *why* and *under what conditions* a token-level optimization objective (common in algorithms like REINFORCE) can effectively optimize a sequence-level reward in LLM reinforcement learning. They seek to establish a principled explanation for the validity of this surrogate objective.

2. **Empirically validate and investigate practical recipes for stable RL training, especially for MoE models:** Building on their theoretical insights, the authors aim to conduct extensive experiments on a large-scale MoE model to:
 - Verify the conditions under which their formulation holds.
 - Demonstrate the efficacy of widely adopted techniques (e.g., importance sampling, clipping) in stabilizing RL training.
 - Specifically investigate the role and effectiveness of MoE-specific techniques like "Routing Replay" in mitigating instability.
 - Provide practical guidelines and "recipes" for achieving stable RL training across different scenarios, including on-policy and off-policy updates.
 - Assess the long-term impact of stable training on final model performance, particularly concerning cold-start initializations.

The motivation for these objectives stems from the observed instability and lack of clear theoretical grounding in current RL practices for LLMs. While RL is critical for enhancing LLMs, scaling it successfully requires a stable training process. The authors identify the mismatch between sequence-level rewards and token-level optimization, coupled with the complexities introduced by MoE architectures, as key sources of instability. By providing both theoretical clarity and practical solutions, they intend to facilitate more robust and efficient development of RL-enhanced LLMs. The substantial computational resources (hundreds of thousands of GPU hours) invested highlight the practical necessity of addressing these stability concerns for large-scale industrial deployment.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The paper adopts a two-pronged methodology, combining theoretical formulation with extensive empirical validation.

#### 3.1. Theoretical Formulation: First-order Approximation of Sequence-level Reward

The core of the theoretical approach is to show that a token-level objective can serve as a first-order approximation to the true expected sequence-level reward.
- **Sequence-level Objective:** The paper starts by defining the true objective, $J_{\text{seq}}(\theta)$, as the expected sequence-level reward, $E_{x \sim D, y \sim \pi_{\theta}(\cdot|x)}[R(x, y)]$. Due to the difficulty of direct optimization, especially with different training and inference engines, it introduces the importance sampling (IS) trick to transform the objective into $E_{x \sim D, y \sim \mu_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}[\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|x)}{\mu_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y|x)} R(x, y)]$, where $\mu_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is the rollout policy from the inference engine.
- **Token-level Objective:** It then proposes a surrogate token-level objective, $J_{\text{token}}(\theta)$, which is essentially the REINFORCE algorithm with token-level IS weights: $E_{x \sim D, y \sim \mu_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|x)}[\sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x, y_{<t})}{\mu_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|x, y_{<t})} R(x, y)]$.
- **Approximation Proof:** The key insight is demonstrated by showing that if the target policy $\pi_{\theta}$ and the rollout policy $\mu_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ are "slightly different," then the sequence-level IS weight can be approximated as $1 + \sum_{t=1}^{|y|} \delta_t$, where $\delta_t$ are small token-level discrepancies. This approximation implies that the gradient of the token-level objective is approximately equal to the gradient of the sequence-level objective.
- **Conditions for Approximation Validity:** For this first-order approximation to hold, the authors derive that the gap between $\pi_{\theta}$ and $\mu_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ must be minimized. This gap is decomposed into two critical factors:
 1. **Training–Inference Discrepancy:** Numerical differences between the training engine (where $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is computed) and the inference engine (where $\mu_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ samples responses). This can arise from different computational kernels, precision settings (e.g., FP8 inference vs. BF16 training), or even batch-invariant kernel behaviors.
 2. **Policy Staleness:** The discrepancy between the rollout policy that sampled responses ($\mu_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$) and the target policy to be optimized ($\pi_{\theta}$). This typically increases with off-policy updates, where a policy is updated multiple times using data generated by an older policy.

#### 3.2. Challenges for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) Models

The formulation is extended to MoE models, where dynamic expert routing complicates the token-level IS weight. The routed experts (e.g., $e_{\pi,t}$ for training, $e_{\mu_{\text{old}},t}$ for inference) become entangled with both training–inference discrepancy and policy staleness, further exacerbating instability.

#### 3.3. Routing Replay for MoE Models

To address MoE-specific challenges, the paper discusses "Routing Replay" techniques:
- **Vanilla Routing Replay (R2):** Fixes routed experts during gradient updates to those determined by the rollout policy in the *training* engine ($e_{\pi_{\text{old}},t}$). This primarily mitigates policy staleness.
- **Rollout Routing Replay (R3):** Fixes routed experts during gradient updates to those determined by the rollout policy in the *inference* engine ($e_{\mu_{\text{old}},t}$). This aims to reduce both training–inference discrepancy and policy staleness.
- **Bias Consideration:** The authors highlight that Routing Replay, while stabilizing, can introduce bias by altering the target policy from its naturally routed experts to a replayed set. The degree of bias depends on the mini-batch and off-policiness.

#### 3.4. MiniRL: A Minimalist Baseline Algorithm

For empirical analysis, the paper introduces "MiniRL," a minimalist REINFORCE-based algorithm that includes:
- **Group-normalization:** Applied to raw rewards for variance reduction and advantage estimation ($\hat{A}(x,y) = R(x,y) - E[R(x,y')] $).
- **Clipping mechanism:** Inspired by PPO, it prevents aggressive policy updates by stopping gradients for tokens where the ratio of current to old policy probabilities ($r_t = \frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_t|x, y_{<t})}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t|x, y_{<t})}$) exceeds predefined thresholds, effectively restraining policy staleness.
- **Truncated Importance Sampling (TIS):** Applied to the token-level IS weight to further stabilize training by bounding extreme ratios.

#### 3.5. Experimental Setup

- **Model:** A 30B MoE model (fine-tuned from Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base).
- **Task:** Mathematical reasoning, with a binary reward (correct/incorrect answer).
- **Data:** 4,096 math problems for training, HMMT25, AIME25, AIME24 benchmarks for evaluation.
- **Hardware/Precision:** FP8 inference and BF16 training, chosen as a "stress test" due to the large training–inference discrepancy.
- **Metrics:** Benchmark accuracy, training reward, token-level entropy (of target policy), and training–inference KL divergence (between inference and training rollout policies) to monitor stability.
- **Framework:** Synchronous RL, where a global batch of responses is sampled and then split into mini-batches for multiple gradient updates. Mini-batch size is fixed at 1,024 responses.
- **Compute:** "Hundreds of thousands of GPU hours" of experimentation, highlighting the scale.

This comprehensive approach allows the authors to theoretically ground their claims and then rigorously test them across various practical RL training conditions, particularly for complex MoE models.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive empirical analysis across different training scenarios yielded several key findings:

#### 4.1. On-policy Training (Global Batch Size = Mini-batch Size)

- **MiniRL (Basic Policy Gradient with IS Correction) is Most Stable:** The fundamental MiniRL algorithm, which includes importance sampling (IS) correction for training–inference discrepancy, demonstrated the best performance and highest training stability. This directly validates the theoretical formulation that IS correction is an inherent component for the first-order approximation to hold.
- **Length Normalization is Suboptimal:** Adding length normalization to the objective, a common practice in other algorithms (e.g., GRPO), led to suboptimal performance. This is attributed to length normalization invalidating the first-order approximation, introducing bias into the token-level objective.
- **Omitting Training–Inference IS Correction Leads to Collapse:** Removing the IS correction for the training–inference discrepancy resulted in rapid training collapse and a sharp drop in policy entropy. This forcefully confirms the crucial role of IS weights in maintaining the validity of the token-level objective.
- **R3 (Rollout Routing Replay) Offers No Gain and Can Introduce Bias:** Under on-policy settings, applying R3 did not yield performance gains, despite reducing the training–inference KL divergence. This suggests that the bias introduced by R3 (altering the original target policy by replaying experts from the inference engine) outweighs any benefits when policy staleness is inherently minimal (on-policy).

#### 4.2. Off-policy Training (Global Batch Size > Mini-batch Size)

- **Routing Replay and Clipping are Essential:** Once off-policy updates were introduced (i.e., a large batch split into multiple mini-batches for gradient updates), both Routing Replay (R2 or R3) and the clipping mechanism became critical for stable training. Omitting either led to premature training collapse and degraded peak performance. This highlights their roles in restraining policy staleness and mitigating the impact of expert routing on MoE models, thereby preserving the first-order approximation's validity.
- **R2 vs. R3 Performance Varies with Off-policiness:**
 - For **small off-policiness** (global batch size = 2 * mini-batch size), R2 (Vanilla Routing Replay) outperformed R3. The authors hypothesize that R2's property of *not* altering the target policy for the first mini-batch makes it more effective when policy staleness is low.
 - For **large off-policiness** (global batch size = 4x or 8x mini-batch size), R3 (Rollout Routing Replay) surpassed R2. Under high off-policiness, R2 struggled to maintain stable training, and its peak performance was lower than R3. This suggests that R3's more aggressive mitigation of both training–inference discrepancy and policy staleness, even with its inherent bias, becomes necessary when policy staleness is substantial.

#### 4.3. Varying Cold-start Initializations

- **Comparable Final Performance with Stable RL:** Experiments with different cold-start initializations (distilled from Qwen3-Max-Thinking-Preview, DeepSeek-R1-0528, and gpt-oss-120b) consistently achieved comparable final performance when trained with a stable RL recipe (MiniRL + R2). This is a significant practical finding, suggesting that with sufficiently prolonged and stable RL training, the specific choice of cold-start initialization becomes less critical for the ultimate performance ceiling.

In summary, the results empirically validate the proposed theoretical framework, demonstrating that techniques directly addressing the training–inference discrepancy and policy staleness (IS correction, clipping, Routing Replay) are crucial for stable and high-performing RL training, especially for MoE LLMs.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This paper makes significant contributions to the field of large language model training, particularly concerning the effective and stable application of reinforcement learning. Its impact can be categorized into theoretical and practical aspects:

**Theoretical Significance:**
- **Principled Justification for Token-Level RL:** The most notable theoretical contribution is providing a clear, first-order approximation-based formulation that justifies the widespread practice of using token-level objectives to optimize sequence-level rewards in LLM-RL. This offers a much-needed theoretical backbone to an empirical technique whose soundness was previously questioned.
- **Identification of Key Instability Factors:** By decomposing the importance sampling weight into "training–inference discrepancy" and "policy staleness," the paper pinpoints the fundamental causes of instability in LLM-RL. This provides a diagnostic framework for understanding why training processes collapse and offers theoretical guidance for designing robust algorithms.
- **Understanding MoE-Specific Challenges:** The extension of the formulation to MoE models, highlighting how dynamic expert routing amplifies discrepancies, is crucial for advancing RL with these complex architectures. It provides a theoretical basis for why MoE-specific stabilization techniques are necessary.

**Practical Impact:**
- **Recipes for Stable RL Training:** The extensive empirical experiments, consuming "hundreds of thousands of GPU hours" on a 30B MoE model, result in concrete, validated recipes for stable RL training. These recipes, involving importance sampling correction, clipping, and especially Routing Replay, provide direct guidance for practitioners developing large-scale LLMs. The nuanced findings regarding R2 vs. R3 based on the degree of off-policiness are particularly valuable.
- **Guidance for MoE Model Development:** The paper's focused investigation into Routing Replay techniques offers practical solutions for stabilizing RL fine-tuning of MoE models, which are becoming increasingly prevalent for their efficiency and performance. This will accelerate the adoption and improvement of MoE architectures in real-world applications.
- **Reduced Dependency on Cold-Start Initialization:** The finding that stable RL training can lead to comparable final performance regardless of cold-start initialization is highly significant. This suggests that resources might be better allocated to refining RL stability and duration rather than obsessing over initial model quality, potentially simplifying the overall LLM development pipeline and making RL more universally applicable.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** By clarifying the conditions for stable RL and providing a robust experimental framework, the paper facilitates future research. It encourages investigations into better advantage estimates, more sophisticated clipping strategies, and new algorithms that explicitly minimize training–inference discrepancy and policy staleness. The shared insights are intended to serve as a bedrock for developing even more advanced and stable RL methods for LLMs.

In essence, this work bridges a critical gap between theoretical understanding and practical implementation in LLM-RL. By stabilizing the training process, it enables LLMs, particularly MoE models, to reliably achieve their full potential through reinforcement learning, thereby contributing significantly to the ongoing advancement of AI capabilities.
