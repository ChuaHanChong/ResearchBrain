# Provably Safe Deep Reinforcement Learning for Robotic Manipulation in Human Environments

> [!summary] Summary
> A deep reinforcement learning framework developed at the Technical University of Munich for robotic manipulators achieves provable human safety in dynamic environments. This system, integrating a high-frequency safety shield, demonstrated a 0% safety-critical collision rate in simulated human-robot interaction tasks while enabling robust learning of complex evasion behaviors.

## Problem

- Deep reinforcement learning (RL) for robotic manipulation lacks formal safety guarantees, hindering its deployment in environments shared with humans.
- The unpredictable and high-degree-of-freedom nature of human movement complicates safety assurance for RL-based manipulators.
- Existing safe RL methods are often limited to simplified or discrete environments, provide only probabilistic safety, or are not directly applicable to continuous, dynamic human-robot interaction.

## Method

- The system integrates a low-frequency ==Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)== reinforcement learning agent with a high-frequency (**250 Hz**) ==safety shield== for ==provably safe trajectory planning==.
- The safety shield continuously plans and verifies both ==intended and failsafe trajectories==, enforcing ==ISO-verified Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM)== by ensuring the robot can come to a complete stop before any potential human collision.
- If an RL-generated action leads to an unsafe intended trajectory, the shield overrides it with a pre-computed ==failsafe trajectory== that guides the robot to a guaranteed safe, stopped state.

## Results

- The safe agent, equipped with the proposed safety shield, achieved a **0%** safety-critical collision rate in both randomized-goal and human-evasion simulated experiments.
- The baseline RL agent, without the safety shield, experienced safety-critical collision rates of approximately **20%** in the randomized-goal experiment and up to **25%** in the human-evasion scenario.
- In the human-evasion experiment, the safe agent successfully learned to maneuver around humans to reach its goals, demonstrating significantly higher success rates compared to the baseline agent's limited learning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Provable human safety in continuous, high-dimensional state spaces with dynamic human motion is achievable by combining an online RL agent with a formally verified, high-frequency shielding mechanism.
> - Implementing a safety shield that prevents episode-ending collisions during training significantly benefits the RL agent's learning process, enabling more effective exploration and the acquisition of complex human-evasion strategies.
> - An inductive safety concept, where intended trajectories are verified against human reachable sets and backed by a guaranteed failsafe trajectory, ensures continuous safety assurances in dynamic environments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep reinforcement learning (RL) has demonstrated capabilities in solving complex manipulation tasks for robotic systems. Applications such as operating door handles, playing table tennis, stacking boxes, and controlling multiple robotic arms have been reported in recent literature. Furthermore, RL-controlled manipulators have exhibited functionality in dynamic environments, including obstacle avoidance and consistent goal reaching. These developments position RL as a potentially transformative approach for robotic control.

However, a significant challenge within the field of RL-based robotics, particularly concerning applications in human environments, is the absence of formal safety guarantees. The unpredictable nature and high degrees of freedom of human movement introduce a complexity that current vanilla RL methods cannot address with formal assurances of safety. This limitation restricts the deployment of RL-based manipulators in real-world settings where human coexistence or collaboration is required, as the risk of collisions is deemed unacceptable.

Existing approaches to enhance safety in RL can be broadly categorized. Simple reward shaping has been identified as insufficient for guaranteeing safety consistently. Constrained policy optimization techniques aim to reduce safety-critical interactions; while these methods can achieve low violation rates, they do not provide formal guarantees against all potential safety breaches. A more rigorous category, often termed shielding, safety layers, sandboxing, or constraining bolts, focuses on ensuring that only safe actions are executed by the agent. Many of these provably safe RL methods have been developed for simplified or discrete environments, such as Atari games, grid worlds, or board games, and often rely on deterministic environment models. Their direct application to complex, continuous, and dynamic real-world scenarios, particularly involving human-robot interaction, is limited.

For manipulator applications, specific attempts at safe RL exist. One method proposed a differentiable safety layer that projects unsafe actions to the closest safe alternatives; however, its formal correctness depends on precise constraint fulfillment and exact optimizer solutions, and defining formal safety constraints for unknown human motions remains challenging. Another approach utilized a reachable set-based safety layer for autonomous driving, but it was designed for discrete action spaces. A reachability-based trajectory safeguard was developed for static environments.

This work addresses the identified gap by proposing a safety measure that provides provable safety for continuous action spaces within high-dimensional state-spaces and unpredictable dynamic human environments. It aims to overcome the limitations of prior methods, which either lacked formal guarantees, were restricted to discrete action spaces, or were not designed for dynamic human interaction. The paper positions itself as presenting the first provably safe robot manipulator control based on deep RL designed for human environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the critical limitation of current deep reinforcement learning (RL) applications in robotics: the absence of formal safety guarantees, particularly when manipulators operate in environments shared with humans. While RL has shown considerable promise in complex robotic manipulation tasks, this deficiency in safety assurance renders the deployment of RL-based systems in real-world human environments impractical and potentially irresponsible. The unpredictable movements and numerous degrees of freedom associated with humans pose a significant challenge to guaranteeing safety.

The central objective of this work is to overcome these safety restrictions by introducing a mechanism that ensures verifiable human safety during both the training and deployment phases of RL algorithms on robotic manipulators. Specifically, the authors aim to:

1. **Guarantee ISO-verified Human Safety**: Implement a safety concept that adheres to established industrial safety standards, such as speed and separation monitoring (SSM) as defined in DIN EN ISO 10218–1 2021, 5.10.3. This standard requires that the robot achieves a complete stop before any potential collision with a human could occur.
2. **Develop a Shielding Mechanism**: Design a safety shield that can prevent collisions with highly dynamic obstacles, like humans, by continuously verifying robot trajectories at a high frequency. This mechanism is intended to integrate with existing RL agents, replacing unsafe actions with provably safe alternatives.
3. **Improve RL Performance**: Demonstrate that the proposed safety shield not only guarantees safety but also positively impacts the RL training process. By preventing episode-ending collisions, which can prematurely terminate learning episodes, the shield is expected to facilitate more effective exploration and faster convergence for the RL agent, especially in scenarios where collision probability is high.
4. **Enable Real-World Application**: Ultimately, the motivation is to bridge the gap between the theoretical capabilities of deep RL for robotic manipulation and its practical application in human-centric industrial or service environments, thereby allowing manipulators to safely operate in proximity to humans.

The work is motivated by the necessity for robust safety frameworks that can enable the advantages of RL in complex, human-shared workspaces without compromising human well-being.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology integrates a deep reinforcement learning agent with a high-frequency safety shield designed to provide provable safety guarantees for robotic manipulators operating in dynamic human environments.

**A. Overall Architecture**
The system consists of an online RL agent that outputs low-frequency actions (intermediate goal joint positions) and a high-frequency safety shield that monitors and, if necessary, overrides the robot's trajectory to ensure safety. The safety shield operates at a significantly higher frequency than the RL agent to allow for rapid reactions to dynamic human movements.

**B. Reinforcement Learning Agent**
- **Algorithm**: The RL agent is based on the Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithm, an off-policy maximum entropy deep reinforcement learning approach. This is extended with Hindsight Experience Replay (HER), which addresses sparse reward problems by generating additional fictional goal transitions from states actually reached during an episode.
- **Action Space**: The agent's actions are defined as intermediate goal joint positions (¯g_i). These are derived from a continuous action vector a_i ∈ [-1, 1]^N (where N is the number of joints), converted to absolute joint positions by adding a scaled difference to the current joint position (q_n,¯g,i+1 = q_n,i + a_n,i * ∆q_max). This approach maintains full control over each joint.
- **Observations**: The agent receives observations including current joint position and velocity, the episode goal, the current Cartesian end-effector position, and the relative Cartesian positions of human wrists and head concerning the end-effector. Static obstacles are assumed to be limited to the table and are not explicitly included in the observation space.
- **Reward Function**: A sparse reward function is employed: r_i = 0 if the episode goal is reached, and r_i = -1 otherwise. Collisions with static environments result in random action generation until a collision-free intermediate goal is found. Collisions with humans terminate the episode.
- **Training Procedure**: During training (Algorithm 1), episodes terminate upon goal achievement, collision, or timeout. Initial exploration utilizes random actions. HER samples are incorporated into the replay buffer, and agent networks are updated periodically.
- **Hyperparameters**: Key RL hyperparameters include three hidden layers with 64 units each, a minibatch size of 128, a replay buffer size of 10^6, a discount factor (γ) of 0.99, an entropy tradeoff (α) of 0.2, and specific values for initial exploration steps, update frequencies, epochs, and episodes per epoch.

**C. Safety Shield (Failsafe Planner)**
The safety shield is based on a provably safe trajectory planner. Its functionality is characterized by long-term and failsafe planning, adaptation for manipulator control, and formal verification.
- **Safety Criterion**: The shield enforces Speed and Separation Monitoring (SSM) according to DIN EN ISO 10218–1 2021, 5.10.3, ensuring the robot comes to a complete stop before any potential collision with a human.
- **Trajectory Planning**:
 - **Intended Trajectories**: For each intermediate goal ¯g_i output by the RL agent, a long-term intended trajectory from the current joint state to ¯g_i is calculated. These are not recomputed at every safety verification step but rather when a new intermediate goal is received until a safe, executable one is identified.
 - **Failsafe Trajectories**: A failsafe trajectory is continuously planned in parallel. This trajectory guarantees to bring the robot to an inherently safe state (a complete stop) even if dynamic obstacles obstruct the intended path. It is path-consistent with the intended trajectory and uses increased acceleration and jerk limits (a_failsafe_max, j_failsafe_max) corresponding to the robot's physical limits for rapid braking.
 - **Execution Logic**: The system operates inductively. An intended trajectory segment (from t_k to t_k+1) is only executed if it is verified as safe AND a collision-free failsafe trajectory exists from t_k+1. If either condition is not met, the robot executes the previously verified failsafe trajectory. This ensures continuous safety, as every failsafe trajectory terminates in a safe, stopped state.
- **Adaption for Manipulator Control**: Due to computational demands of 3D trajectory planning at high frequencies, intended trajectories are only fully recomputed for new intermediate goals from the RL agent. The failsafe planner utilizes velocity scaling. The synchronous Type IV online trajectory planner is employed for both intended and failsafe trajectories, adhering to specific velocity, acceleration, and jerk limits (v_traj_max, a_traj_max, j_traj_max for intended; a_failsafe_max, j_failsafe_max for failsafe).
- **Frequency**: The RL agent provides intermediate goals at a lower frequency (∆T = 200 ms), while the safety shield performs verification steps at a much higher frequency (∆t = 4 ms, equivalent to 250 Hz).

**D. Verification (Collision Checking)**
- **Method**: Trajectory verification relies on collision checking between the reachable sets of all possible human motions and the planned robot trajectory.
- **Human Model**: Human motion is assumed to be constrained by a maximum joint speed of 2 m/s (DIN EN ISO 13855:2010), and human joint positions are assumed to be reliably measurable (e.g., via motion capture).
- **Reachable Occupancy**: The entire space a human can occupy within the time required to bring the robot to a complete stop (Γ([t_k, t_k+b])) is calculated using a task space approach.
- **Geometric Models**: Both human and robot occupancies are modeled using capsules (cylinders with half-spheres) for computational efficiency. Robot occupancy is described by a set of capsules that overapproximate each robot link's movement during a specified time interval.
- **Safety Condition**: A robot motion is deemed safe if no robot capsule intersects with any human capsule at any point before the robot reaches its resting position.

**E. Software Structure**
The system is implemented using Gazebo and ROS Noetic. The robot simulation leverages the Open Dynamics Engine (ODE) solver with a 1 ms step size. The safety shield is developed in C++ for performance, achieving an average verification time of 0.5 ms. The simulation runs approximately five times faster than real-time when the safety shield operates at 250 Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluated the proposed safety shield's performance in two distinct simulated experiments: a randomized-goal experiment and a human-evasion experiment. Both experiments used human motion capture data from CMU, with randomized parameters (x, y position, start time) to prevent overfitting. Training consisted of 200 epochs with 30 episodes each, totaling up to 600,000 RL steps.

**A. Randomized-Goal Experiment**
This experiment involved the robot attempting to reach randomly sampled goal joint positions from a fixed start, with the human potentially blocking the goal.
- **Collision Rate (Figure 5a)**: The baseline RL agent, without the safety shield, exhibited a safety-critical collision rate of approximately 20% across all episodes. In contrast, the safe agent, integrated with the proposed safety shield, demonstrated a 0% rate of safety-critical collisions, successfully preventing all instances where the robot would have collided with the human.
- **Success Rate (Figure 5b)**: Both the baseline and safe agents achieved comparable success rates in reaching the goals, stabilizing at approximately 65% by the end of training. The baseline agent initially learned slightly faster, but the final performance was similar.
- **Episode Ending for Safe Agent (Figure 5c)**: For the 35% of episodes not ending in success, approximately 10% concluded in "safe collisions," where the human animation walked into the already stopped robot. The remaining 25% of episodes ended due to exceeding the maximum allowed RL steps, primarily because the human's presence blocked the path to the goal.

**B. Human-Evasion Experiment**
This experiment was specifically designed with start and goal positions that necessitated the robot to evade the human to reach the goal, as the shortest path would lead to a collision.
- **Collision Rate (Figure 5d)**: The baseline agent showed a high collision rate, colliding in almost every episode during the first half of training. While it learned to reduce collisions in the latter half, it still experienced safety-critical collisions in approximately 25% of episodes. The safe agent, equipped with the safety shield, again maintained a 0% safety-critical collision rate throughout the training.
- **Success Rate (Figure 5e)**: In this scenario, the baseline agent exhibited limited learning in human evasion, resulting in a low success rate for reaching the goal. Conversely, the safe agent successfully learned to maneuver around the human to achieve its objectives, demonstrating a significantly higher success rate.
- **Episode Ending for Safe Agent (Figure 5f)**: Similar to the randomized-goal experiment, a portion of episodes for the safe agent ended in "safe collisions" (human hitting stopped robot) or timeouts where goals were unreachable due to human presence.

**C. General Observations**
The results consistently demonstrated that the safety shield prevented all safety-critical collisions in both dynamic human environments. The baseline agent's performance, even with attempts to improve it through higher operating frequencies or increased negative collision rewards, did not yield successful training improvements. The human-evasion experiment particularly highlighted the benefit of the safety shield in enhancing the RL agent's learning capability in collision-prone scenarios, as it allowed the agent to explore and learn complex evasion strategies without the detrimental effect of frequent episode-ending collisions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes a novel safety shield that provides formal safety guarantees for deep reinforcement learning (RL) in robotic manipulation, specifically for human-robot coexistence scenarios. The primary significance of this work lies in its ability to address a critical limitation of RL—the lack of provable safety assurances in dynamic human environments—which has previously hindered its application in real-world settings.

The proposed safety shield represents an advancement over existing safe RL methods. Unlike approaches that offer probabilistic safety, reduce collision frequency without guarantees, or are confined to discrete action spaces and static environments, this method offers formal safety guarantees for continuous action spaces within high-dimensional state-spaces and unpredictable human motions. By combining a low-frequency RL agent with a high-frequency, formally verified trajectory planner, the system can react quickly to dynamic human movements, ensuring that the robot comes to a complete stop before any collision could occur, in compliance with ISO safety standards.

The potential impact of this work is multi-faceted:
- **Enabling Real-World Deployment**: By providing provable safety, the method facilitates the responsible deployment of RL-controlled manipulators in industrial, service, and domestic environments where humans are present. This could unlock a broader range of applications for autonomous robots.
- **Improved RL Training Efficiency**: The research demonstrates that preventing episode-ending collisions through the safety shield can significantly improve the RL agent's training success, particularly in environments with a high likelihood of collision. This is evidenced by the human-evasion experiment, where the safe agent successfully learned complex evasion strategies, unlike the baseline agent. This suggests that safety mechanisms can not only protect humans but also enhance the learning process itself.
- **Framework for Future Research**: The methodology offers a generalizable framework for integrating provable safety into existing and future online RL agents. Its compatibility with various RL algorithms positions it as a foundational component for developing robust and safe autonomous systems.
- **Advancement in Human-Robot Interaction (HRI)**: By guaranteeing safety, the work contributes to building trust and acceptance for robots in human environments, moving towards more symbiotic and efficient human-robot collaboration.

The authors indicate future work will involve refining the RL agent, training on a more diverse set of real-world human motions, and ultimately testing the safe RL agent on physical robots in human working environments. These next steps will be critical in validating the practical utility and robustness of the proposed safety shield in complex, uncontrolled settings.
