# Whisker-Inspired Tactile Sensing: A Sim2Real Approach for Precise Underwater Contact Tracking

> [!summary] Summary
> Underwater whisker tactile sensors built from pre-curved nitinol wires whose base bending moments are read by Fiber Bragg Gratings on a single optical fiber. A MuJoCo digital twin sweeps simulated whiskers over YCB objects to train WhiskerNet, a transformer decoder mapping torque history to contact position, with Gaussian Process Regression calibrating real wavelengths to simulated torque. Contact tracking reaches under 2 mm error underwater without robot proprioception.

## Problem

- Prior whisker contact tracking relied on Bayesian filtering with accurate robot proprioception of base velocity, unusable on mobile robots subject to drift.
- Base torque readings on a compliant whisker do not map uniquely to a contact location, so model-based force-sensor estimation is ill-posed without extra information.
- Straight whiskers buckle nonlinearly under axial approach, and collecting large real contact-position datasets underwater is laborious and expensive.

## Method

- ==Sensor design==: pre-curved 0.3 mm nitinol wire glued into a 3D-printed undulating base routing one fiber with two FBGs per whisker plus a strain-isolated temperature-compensation grating.
- **WhiskerNet**: ==6-layer causal transformer decoder==, 8 heads, that autoregressively predicts contact position from the history of two-axis base torque signals under MSE loss.
- ==Real2Sim calibration==: a V-groove calibration rig run identically in hardware and MuJoCo, with thin-plate-kernel ==Gaussian Process Regression== mapping FBG wavelength shifts onto simulated base torques.

## Results

- Across **10** seen YCB and **10** unseen underwater objects, real-world RMSE stayed near or under **2 mm**, versus **0.15-1.38 mm** in simulation.
- Sim-to-real ablation showed in-air and in-water errors nearly identical (vase **0.85** vs **0.88 mm**, rock **1.90** vs **1.89 mm**), confirming negligible drag at low speed.
- Speed ablation: error grew from **0.42 mm** at 4 mm/s to **2.25 mm** at 12 mm/s, degrading sharply beyond the 8 mm/s training range, worsened by randomized speed.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Calibrating real sensor signals into the simulator's physical units, rather than matching sim data to reality, lets one trained model deploy without retraining.
> - Feeding signal history to a sequence model resolves the ambiguity of a single base-torque reading, removing the need for proprioceptive velocity input.
> - Generalization tracks geometric features, not object identity: unseen shapes transfer well, while thin edges, concavities, and slip-induced discontinuous contact break the single-contact assumption.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

This work addresses challenges in robotic perception, particularly for underwater applications, by drawing inspiration from biological tactile sensing systems. Animals, such as rats, cats, and pinnipeds, utilize whiskers (vibrissae) to gather information about their environment, navigate obstacles, and detect water movements, especially in low-light or turbid conditions where vision is compromised. This bio-inspiration has led to the development of robotic whiskers for contact sensing, surface discrimination, and flow analysis.

The field of robotic whisker sensing includes approaches with active whisking mechanisms, involving rotational joints and actuators at the whisker base, as well as passive systems designed for contact sensing and tracking. Early work often focused on tip-contact detection, while more advanced applications aim to track continuous contacts along a deflected whisker. Model-based methods using multi-axis force sensors at the whisker base have been explored for estimating contact points, but these solutions can be complex due to the non-unique relationship between base torque measurements and contact locations. Prior research by some of the current authors achieved contact tracking accuracy within 2 mm using Bayesian filtering methods, which incorporated known robot proprioception data (e.g., whisker base velocity) to resolve ambiguities. A key departure in the current work is the aim to remove this dependency on precise robot proprioception, thereby increasing applicability to mobile robots that may experience drift.

For underwater contexts, whisker-like sensors have also been investigated for detecting water currents and vortices, mimicking the hydrodynamic sensing capabilities of pinnipeds. The current paper, however, primarily focuses on contact detection with objects rather than flow sensing, using thin, metallic whiskers at low velocities to minimize the influence of water drag on whisker curvature.

The chosen transduction technology, Fiber Bragg Grating (FBG) optical fibers, is recognized for its robustness in harsh environments, including deep-sea conditions and high-pressure oil and gas wells, making it suitable for underwater robotics. While optical fiber-based whiskers are not unprecedented in robotics, this work specifically tailors the design for continuous whisker/surface contact tracking and operation in deep-ocean environments, which are corrosive and subject to significant pressure variations.

Finally, the increasing reliance on deep learning in robotics necessitates large datasets, which are often expensive and time-consuming to collect in physical environments. This has driven the adoption of simulation environments (e.g., PyBullet, MuJoCo, Drake, Isaac Sim) for data generation. The "sim-to-real" paradigm, which involves training models in simulation and transferring them to real-world robots, has become a standard approach to bridge the "reality gap" through techniques like domain randomization and system identification. This paper employs a similar sim-to-real framework, but distinctively uses a "real-to-sim" calibration process to map real-world FBG wavelength signals to simulated torque signals, allowing the model trained in simulation to operate in physical environments without explicit robot proprioceptive input. This approach builds upon the advancements in both tactile sensing and sim-to-real transfer methodologies, targeting a critical need for enhanced underwater robotic perception.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop and implement a system for precise underwater contact tracking using whisker-inspired tactile sensors. A central aim is to achieve this precision without relying on accurate robot proprioception, which represents a significant advancement over previous methods.

The motivation for this work stems from several factors:

- **Bio-inspiration from Aquatic Mammals:** Aquatic mammals like pinnipeds possess highly sensitive whiskers that are crucial for detecting and discriminating objects, as well as analyzing water movements, particularly in turbid or low-visibility underwater conditions where visual acuity is limited. This natural capability highlights the potential for similar sensory modalities in artificial systems.
- **Enhancing Underwater Robotic Capabilities:** Underwater robots, such as OceanOne, frequently operate in murky waters and can encounter physical obstacles, leading to situations where they become stuck. Current camera-based systems may be insufficient for localizing contact points in such environments. Integrating tactile whiskers can provide crucial contact or proximity sensing capabilities, thereby improving safe operation, navigation, and obstacle avoidance in cluttered underwater scenarios.
- **Addressing Limitations of Existing Robotic Whisker Systems:**
 - **Environmental Robustness:** Many existing robotic whisker designs are not optimized for harsh underwater environments characterized by deep, corrosive saltwater and significant pressure changes. There is a need for robust sensing technology that can withstand these conditions. The use of Fiber Bragg Grating (FBG) optical fibers within 3D-printed structures, encapsulated for protection, is motivated by their proven resilience in such demanding settings.
 - **Complex Contact Tracking:** Traditional approaches often focus on simple tip-contact detection. However, for tasks like surface reconstruction, it is necessary to track the continuous path of a contact location as a curved whisker brushes over a surface. The design incorporates pre-curved whiskers to avoid highly nonlinear buckling behavior observed with straight whiskers, enabling more stable continuous contact tracing.
 - **Dependency on Robot Proprioception:** Previous work has demonstrated accurate contact localization but often relied on precise proprioceptive data from the robot (e.g., known whisker base velocity). This dependency limits the applicability of such systems to mobile robots, which may experience drift or have less precise internal state estimation. A core motivation for this research is to develop a deep learning-based framework that does not explicitly require robot proprioceptive data, making it suitable for a wider range of autonomous underwater vehicles.
- **Feasibility of Data-Driven Approaches:** Deep learning models, while powerful, require extensive datasets for training. Generating such data from real-world robotic experiments is typically time-consuming and expensive. This motivates the development of a sim-to-real learning framework, leveraging physics-based simulators (like MuJoCo) to efficiently generate large-scale training data and then calibrating the models for real-world deployment.

In summary, the key objective is to deliver a robust, non-intrusive underwater tactile sensing solution that can precisely track contact points on various objects, generalize to unseen geometries, and operate independently of accurate robot proprioception, thereby significantly enhancing the perception and manipulation capabilities of underwater robots.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The methodology presented involves the design and fabrication of novel whisker sensors and the implementation of a sim-to-real learning framework for contact tracking.

**Sensor Design and Fabrication:**
The whisker sensors are designed for underwater operation. They consist of thin, pre-curved, super-elastic Nitinol (TiNi) wires, 0.3 mm in diameter and 60 mm in total length with a 20 mm distal arc radius. Nitinol was selected for its resistance to plastic deformation. The base of each wire is embedded in a 3D-printed structure (Stratasys Objet24 with VeroWhitePlus). This structure contains an undulating groove into which a Corning Ultra glass optical fiber is placed and glued, respecting the fiber's minimum bend radius of 8 mm.

The transduction mechanism utilizes Fiber Bragg Gratings (FBGs) embedded along the optical fiber. FBGs are strategically located adjacent to each whisker wire's anchor point. When the whisker deflects, it causes bending strains in the plastic structure, which are then transmitted as tensile or compressive strains to the FBGs. Each whisker anchor has two FBGs to measure bending moments about local x and y axes at its base, along with an additional FBG for temperature compensation. The FBGs operate at different nominal wavelengths (1525-1565 nm) and are sampled simultaneously at 2 kHz by a Micron Optics sm130 optical sensing interrogator. Finite Element Analysis (FEA) was employed to optimize FBG placement for maximum strain sensitivity. For protection, the base structure is encapsulated in a thin layer of Shore 20A silicone rubber, chosen for its softness to minimize interference with FBG strain detection.

**Whisker Sim-to-Real Learning Framework:**
This framework addresses the challenge of collecting large datasets in the real world by leveraging simulation. It comprises three main components: data collection in simulation, a deep learning model (WhiskerNet), and a real-to-sim calibration process.

- **Contact Localization Assumptions:** The approach operates under specific assumptions: (1) Objects in contact are stationary relative to the world frame; (2) Only one contact point exists on a whisker at any given time (capturing the proximal contact for concave surfaces); (3) Frictional forces along the whisker are negligible in their effect on the sensor.
- **Data Collection in Simulation:** A digital twin of the whisker sensor was created in the MuJoCo simulator. Whiskers are modeled as inextensible, elastic 1D objects using MuJoCo's Cable object (a sequence of capsules). To enhance stability and represent realistic behavior, damping parameters for capsule connections were slightly increased (to 0.03), and surface impedance at contacts was reduced to mitigate vibrations and moment spikes. Object models from the YCB dataset were used, decomposed into convex components via CoACD for MuJoCo compatibility. During data generation, YCB objects were subjected to random rotations and transformations, and whiskers were swept across them with random initial speeds (3-7 mm/s) and varying accelerations (±0.4 mm²/s). The system records whisker base torques and corresponding contact positions. Data underwent filtering (for high torque values and contact jumping), downsampling (to 5 Hz), and augmentation (random no-contact signals, truncation).
- **WhiskerNet: Contact Prediction from Torque:** A transformer-decoder architecture was designed for contact prediction, treating it as a sequence prediction problem. The model takes sequences of base torque signals (y and z directions, as axial torque is negligible) as input. It encodes the signal sequence, adds positional embeddings, and uses multiple transformer decoder layers to learn relationships between past signals and the current contact position. A linear head maps the final features to the predicted contact position. Training uses a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function.
- **Real-to-Sim Calibration:** This step bridges the reality gap by mapping real-world FBG wavelength shifts to the simulated torque signals. This is necessary because structural coupling in the real sensor can cause FBG signals for different axes to be interdependent. A calibration rig with a V-shaped groove (to enforce known contact locations) was used on a multi-axis optical calibration stage in both physical and simulated environments. Data was collected by sweeping 9 linear trajectories, yielding 27 pairs of wavelength and torque values. Gaussian Process Regression (GPR) with a Thin-plate (TP) kernel was employed to establish the mapping. Prior to GPR, heuristic methods were applied to filter signal noise and signals below a certain threshold.

**Experiment Setup:**
Experiments were conducted in simulation and in the real world (both in air and immersed in water).
- **Simulation Setup:** The MuJoCo digital twin was used to train WhiskerNet on 78 YCB objects, generating 200 sweeping samples per object. The training-validation split was 80/20. Performance was evaluated using Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) between predicted contact positions and the object surface.
- **Immersed Experiment Setup:** A Flexiv Rizon 4 robot arm, equipped with the whisker sensor array via a 3D-printed connector, was used to sweep whiskers through a water tank. Ten seen YCB objects and ten unseen objects (common in underwater environments) were tested. Objects were mounted using a steel nut and magnetic holder system. Before testing, whiskers were immersed for 30 minutes for signal stabilization. The robot arm swept whiskers at 4 mm/s (with 80-120% random variations), with each object rotated 90 degrees for four trials. RMSE was the primary performance metric.
- **Ablation Studies:**
 - **Speed Ablation Study:** Evaluated model robustness to different movement speeds (4, 6, 8, 10, 12 mm/s), both with and without random speed variations, using a coffee can.
 - **Sim2Real Ablation Study:** Compared performance in simulation, in air, and in water for three diverse representative objects.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research demonstrates the effectiveness of the whisker-inspired tactile sensing system and the sim-to-real learning framework for precise underwater contact tracking.

**Overall Accuracy:**
The approach achieved contact tracking accuracy of less than 2 mm in real-world underwater experiments, with the ability to generalize to unseen objects.

**Simulation vs. Real-World Experiment Results (Table I):**
- **Simulation Performance:** The model exhibited high accuracy in simulation, with RMSE errors consistently below 1.5 mm and often below 0.6 mm for all tested objects.
- **Real-World Performance (Seen YCB Objects):**
 - The model performed effectively on objects with smooth, cylindrical geometries or minimal curvature changes (e.g., coffee can, strawberry, clamp), with RMSE errors ranging from 0.83 mm to 1.37 mm.
 - Objects with relatively flat surfaces also showed good performance, maintaining stable contact points without significant lateral slipping (e.g., sugar box), achieving errors around 1.34 mm.
 - For more complex shapes, such as a power drill or banana, the model maintained good performance, with errors around 1.06 mm to 1.51 mm.
 - Accuracy was slightly reduced in cases involving slipping orthogonal to the whisker's travel direction (e.g., wood block, 1.91 mm RMSE).
- **Real-World Performance (Unseen Objects):**
 - The model demonstrated robust performance on unseen objects with standard or simple geometries (e.g., vase, bottle, chest), with errors between 0.88 mm and 1.64 mm.
 - Even for objects with highly complex surfaces, such as a rock, the accuracy remained within 2 mm (1.89 mm RMSE). This indicates that the model can generalize to objects with geometric characteristics similar to those encountered in training, even if the specific object instance is novel.
- **Identified Failure Cases:**
 - **Thin and Flat Objects:** For objects like a coin, the model showed higher errors (2.71 mm RMSE). This was attributed to the training dataset primarily containing trajectories on continuous surfaces, leading the model to incorrectly predict slight contact movement rather than a static contact at a sharp edge.
 - **Concave Objects with Discontinuous Contact:** When the whisker interacted with concave structures (e.g., the gap in a lamp's handle), multiple contacts occurred, leading to inaccurate predictions (3.09 mm RMSE). The current model assumes a single contact point.
 - **Jumping or Slipping:** In cases where insufficient friction caused the whisker to jump or slip on the surface (e.g., a fish model), contact position was lost, resulting in higher errors (2.01 mm RMSE). These cases generally involved discontinuous whisker contact tracing, which the current approach is not designed to explicitly handle.

**Speed Ablation Study (Table II):**
- The study showed a clear correlation between whisker movement speed and model performance. As speed increased, accuracy gradually declined.
- Performance significantly deteriorated when speeds exceeded 8 mm/s, which was outside the range of the training data.
- Introducing variability in whisker speed (random variations) further exacerbated prediction errors compared to consistent speed scenarios. For example, at 4 mm/s, RMSE was 0.42 mm without random speed, but 0.88 mm with random speed. At 12 mm/s, it rose from 2.25 mm to 2.99 mm.

**Sim2Real Ablation Study (Table III):**
- Performance in air was found to be comparable to performance in water for representative objects (e.g., Vase: 0.85 mm in air, 0.88 mm in water; Rock: 1.90 mm in air, 1.89 mm in water).
- This similarity in performance suggests that at the low operating speeds and with the thin Nitinol whisker design, hydrodynamic drag forces from water have a negligible effect on whisker curvature or contact localization.

**Case Studies of Successes and Failures (Fig. 9):**
- **Successes:** The model successfully tracked contacts on smooth surfaces (coffee can), distinct faces with corners (Lego block, though with a slight outlier at the corner), and uneven surfaces (rock), predicting its general shape.
- **Failures:** Confirmed the limitations identified above for thin, flat objects (coin), concave objects leading to multiple contacts (lamp), and instances of slipping or jumping (fish).

### 5. Significance and potential impact

This research presents a significant contribution to the field of robotic tactile sensing, particularly for underwater applications, with several areas of potential impact:

- **Enhanced Underwater Robotic Perception:** The development of robust, low-force, and non-intrusive whisker sensors directly addresses a critical challenge in underwater robotics: operating safely and effectively in cluttered, low-visibility, or turbid environments. By providing precise contact location information, this technology can significantly improve the perception capabilities of underwater robots, enabling more dexterous manipulation, safer navigation around obstacles, and better interaction with delicate underwater structures or biological samples. This could reduce instances of robots getting stuck or causing damage.

- **Robust Sensor Design for Harsh Environments:** The utilization of Fiber Bragg Grating (FBG) technology within Nitinol whiskers offers a highly robust sensing solution. This design is inherently waterproof, resistant to corrosive saltwater, resilient to large changes in ambient pressure, and the Nitinol material prevents plastic deformation upon accidental impact. The ability to locate the optical interrogator kilometers away further enhances its suitability for deep-ocean deployments, where local electronics might be vulnerable. This robust design broadens the scope of environments where sophisticated tactile sensing can be deployed.

- **Reduced Dependency on Robot Proprioception:** A key innovation is the sim-to-real learning framework that allows accurate contact tracking without explicitly relying on precise robot proprioceptive data. This is a crucial advancement over previous methods, as it makes the system more adaptable and viable for mobile underwater robots, which may experience sensor drift or have less accurate internal state estimation. This independence simplifies deployment and expands the potential applications to a wider range of autonomous platforms.

- **Effective Sim-to-Real Transfer for Tactile Sensing:** The successful implementation of a sim-to-real learning pipeline, including a digital twin for data generation, a deep learning model (WhiskerNet), and a real-to-sim calibration method (GPR), demonstrates a viable strategy for developing data-driven tactile sensing solutions. This approach mitigates the prohibitive costs and time associated with large-scale real-world data collection, making advanced machine learning techniques more accessible for complex sensing problems in robotics.

- **Generalization Capabilities:** The demonstrated ability of the model to generalize contact prediction to unseen objects, including those with complex geometries, is significant. This suggests that the learned mapping between whisker deflections and contact locations is robust and broadly applicable, which is essential for real-world scenarios where robots encounter diverse and unpredictable objects.

- **Foundation for Future Advancements:** The research lays a foundation for future work addressing current limitations. Plans to account for multiple and discontinuous contacts, broaden material property accommodation, increase movement speeds, and refine evaluation metrics indicate a clear path toward even more sophisticated and versatile tactile sensing systems. Furthermore, exploring the sensor's potential for high-rate water flow detection opens new avenues for multi-modal underwater sensing, allowing robots to perceive both contact and hydrodynamic cues, similar to their biological inspirations.

In conclusion, this work significantly advances the state of the art in underwater tactile sensing by providing a robust, precise, and proprioception-independent solution. Its potential impact spans safer and more capable underwater robot operations, improved environmental exploration, and a generalizable methodology for complex robotic perception challenges.
