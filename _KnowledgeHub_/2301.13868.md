# PADL: Language-Directed Physics-Based Character Control

> [!summary] Summary
> NVIDIA and University of Waterloo researchers developed PADL, a framework enabling natural language control of physically simulated characters. The system learns a joint motion-text embedding space and uses adversarial reinforcement learning to train policies, achieving accurate skill reproduction and dynamic multi-task control in complex environments.

## Problem

- Existing character animation interfaces force a trade-off between accessibility (simple but limited control) and versatility (fine-grained but laborious control).
- Translating high-level human intent into low-level motor commands for complex, full-body physically simulated characters is challenging.
- A unified interface was needed to specify both high-level tasks (e.g., navigate) and low-level skills (e.g., walk vs. sprint) for virtual agents.

## Method

- A ==skill embedding== stage aligns natural language captions with motion sequences in a shared latent space, leveraging a fine-tuned ==CLIP text encoder== and a ==motion autoencoder==.
- Control policies are trained using ==goal-conditioned reinforcement learning==, guided by an ==adversarial discriminator== that encourages physically simulated actions to match target skills and task-specific reward functions.
- Multi-task aggregation combines single-task policies, employing a language-based ==multiple-choice question-answering (QA) model== to dynamically select the appropriate task and object from user commands.

## Results

- The system successfully reproduced diverse motor skills, including various locomotion gaits and combat actions, from natural language commands.
- PADL demonstrated robust multi-task control in environments with multiple objects, accurately identifying tasks and target objects from user instructions.
- Evaluations showed PADL achieving better dataset coverage and generating higher quality, more stable motions compared to baselines using direct CLIP text embeddings.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A custom-learned 128D motion-text skill embedding is more effective for conditioning physics-based control policies than raw or PCA-reduced CLIP text embeddings, leading to improved motion quality.
> - Dynamic selection of tasks and objects from natural language commands can be achieved by aggregating single-task policies and using a multiple-choice QA model.
> - The learned skill embedding space enables semantically meaningful interpolations between different motor skills, producing coherent intermediate behaviors.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The field of computer animation has consistently pursued the development of systems capable of synthesizing natural and lifelike motions for simulated characters. A central challenge in this domain has been to create not only high-quality motion, but also an intuitive and versatile interface for users to direct character behaviors.

Prior work has explored various approaches to character control interfaces. Traditional methods often rely on compact control abstractions such as joystick commands or target waypoints. While these interfaces offer accessibility, they typically restrict the variety and granularity of controllable behaviors. Conversely, motion tracking models provide a versatile interface for fine-grained control by specifying target motion trajectories. However, authoring these trajectories is often labor-intensive, requiring specialized equipment like motion capture systems or significant domain expertise. This introduces a trade-off between accessibility and versatility in existing character animation systems.

Concurrently, recent advancements in Natural Language Processing (NLP), particularly with large language models, have enabled the development of language-based interfaces for various applications, including image generation, program synthesis, and even generating functional code from natural language descriptions. These developments demonstrate the potential of natural language as an expressive and accessible medium for specifying user intent across different modalities.

This work, PADL, integrates these advancements in NLP with physics-based character animation. It addresses the long-standing trade-off in control interfaces by proposing natural language as a mechanism for both accessibility and versatility in directing simulated characters. The paper positions itself as one of the initial systems to leverage language commands for controlling full-body physically simulated characters, aiming to enable complex motor skills through a natural language interface, moving beyond kinematic models or speech-driven animations that rely on temporal correspondence between language and motion.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a system that allows users to direct the behaviors of physically simulated characters using natural language commands. This objective is motivated by the recognized limitations of existing character animation interfaces and the potential of natural language to overcome these.

Specifically, the core motivations and objectives are:

- **Bridging the Accessibility-Versatility Gap**: Current character control interfaces often force a compromise between ease of use (accessibility) and the range/granularity of behaviors that can be controlled (versatility). Simple interfaces like joysticks are accessible but limited, while versatile interfaces like motion tracking are laborious. The researchers aim to leverage natural language to provide an interface that is both accessible and sufficiently versatile to control a rich corpus of skills for physics-based characters.
- **Leveraging NLP Innovations**: Recent breakthroughs in large and expressive language models have demonstrated their capability in interpreting and acting upon natural language prompts for various content generation tasks. The authors are motivated to apply these modern NLP techniques to the challenging domain of physics-based character animation, where translating high-level human intent into low-level motor commands for a physically simulated agent is complex.
- **Enabling Both High-level Tasks and Low-level Skills**: The system is designed to allow users to specify two distinct aspects of character behavior via natural language:
 - **High-level tasks**: Broad objectives that the character needs to accomplish, such as navigating to a specific location or interacting with an object.
 - **Low-level skills**: The specific manner or style in which a task should be executed, such as "walking" versus "running" to a target, or "swinging a sword" versus "shield bashing."
- **Developing Robust Control Policies**: A significant motivation is to train control policies that can map language commands to precise, low-level motor controls, enabling the character to perform a diverse array of desired motor skills within a physics simulation, while also grounding these skills in language.
- **Facilitating Multi-Task and Multi-Object Interactions**: The research aims to explore methods for aggregating single-task policies into a more flexible controller that can handle multiple tasks and interact with multiple objects in an environment, all directed through natural language.

In summary, the motivation stems from the desire for an intuitive and powerful user interface for physics-based character animation, utilizing natural language as the medium to achieve granular control over diverse skills and tasks while maintaining accessibility.

### 3. Methodology and approach

The PADL framework operates in three distinct stages: Skill Embedding, Policy Training, and Multi-Task Aggregation.

#### 3.1. Skill Embedding

The initial stage focuses on creating a shared embedding space that aligns motion sequences with their corresponding natural language descriptions. This process largely follows the MotionCLIP approach:
- **Dataset**: A reference motion dataset `M = {(m_i, c_i)}` is utilized, consisting of motion clips `m_i` (sequences of poses) annotated with natural language captions `c_i`.
- **Encoders**: A motion encoder `Enc_m(m_i)` maps each motion clip to a latent embedding `z_i^m`. Similarly, a language encoder `Enc_l(c_i)` maps each caption to a latent embedding `z_i^l`. The embeddings are normalized to a unit sphere.
- **Architecture**: `Enc_m` is modeled as a bidirectional transformer. `Enc_l` uses a pre-trained CLIP text encoder with an added head of two fully-connected layers, which is fine-tuned during this stage.
- **Autoencoder Training**: A motion decoder `Dec(z, U)` (also a bidirectional transformer) is jointly trained with `Enc_m` to reconstruct the original motion `m` from its latent representation `z` and a learned constant query sequence `U`.
- **Loss Function**: The autoencoder is trained using a combined loss:
 - **Reconstruction Loss (`L_recon`)**: Measures the squared error between the reconstructed motion sequence and the original motion.
 - **Alignment Loss (`L_align`)**: Measures the cosine distance between the motion embedding `Enc_m(m)` and the language embedding `Enc_l(c)`, encouraging similarity between the two modalities in the latent space.
- **Data Augmentation**: To mitigate overfitting, random subsequences from motion clips are included in each minibatch, contributing only to the reconstruction loss.

#### 3.2. Policy Training

Once the joint embedding space is established, the learned skill embeddings are used to train control policies for physically simulated characters.
- **Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning**: Policies `π(a_t | s_t, g, z)` are trained using a goal-conditioned reinforcement learning framework. At each timestep `t`, the policy receives the character's state `s_t`, a task-specific goal `g`, and a skill latent `z`. The goal `g` specifies high-level objectives (e.g., target location), while `z` (derived from `Enc_m(m)`) specifies the desired skill.
- **Reward Function**: The reward for the agent at each timestep is `r_t = r_t^skill + λ_task * r_t^task`.
 - **Skill Objective (`r_t^skill`)**: An adversarial discriminator `D(s, s', z)` is trained to distinguish between state transitions `(s, s')` originating from a reference motion clip (corresponding to `z`) and those generated by the policy or other motion clips. The discriminator loss includes terms for maximizing prediction on real transitions, minimizing prediction on policy transitions and other motion transitions, and a gradient penalty for stability. The skill reward for the policy is then derived from the discriminator's output, encouraging the policy to produce transitions that the discriminator classifies as real for the given `z`. This joint conditioning on `(s, s', z)` aims to reduce mode collapse compared to discriminators operating only on marginal distributions.
 - **Task Objective (`r_t^task`)**: Task-specific reward functions are designed for each high-level task.
 - **Facing Task**: Rewards the character for orienting towards a target direction, with a saturated "optimal cone" reward.
 - **Location Task**: Rewards the character for approaching a target location and moving towards it at a minimum speed, saturating when close to the target.
 - **Strike Task**: Rewards the character for knocking over a target object, in addition to position and velocity rewards. The episode terminates when the object is sufficiently tipped.
 - **Adaptive Task Weight (`λ_task`)**: An adaptive proportional controller dynamically adjusts `λ_task` to balance the skill and task rewards based on a target task-reward value, preventing either objective from dominating.
- **Policy Direction**: After training, a policy can be directed using a natural language command `c_skill` by encoding it through the language encoder `z = Enc_l(c_skill)`.

#### 3.3. Multi-Task Aggregation

To enable a single controller to perform multiple tasks and interact with various objects, individual single-task policies are aggregated.
- **Challenge**: Training a single multi-task policy directly was found to lead to poor performance.
- **Approach**: A collection of single-task policies (each trained as described above for a specific task) is maintained. At runtime, a language-based selection model determines which policy to activate.
- **Multiple-Choice Question Answering (QA)**: A pre-trained BERT model, fine-tuned on the SWAG dataset, is used as a multiple-choice QA model.
 - **Task Identification**: Given a user's task command, prompts are formulated as "Sentence A" (the task command) and multiple "Sentence B" candidates, each corresponding to a trained policy and describing its ability. The QA model selects the most likely candidate, identifying the task.
 - **Object Identification**: Similarly, for object identification, candidate sentences describe different objects in the environment. The QA model selects the candidate describing the object referenced in the user's command.
- **Dynamic Control**: The identified task and object dictate which single-task policy becomes active, and the state of the identified object is passed as input to that policy. This allows dynamic control over task and object focus using natural language.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Character**: A 3D humanoid character with 37 degrees-of-freedom, equipped with a sword and shield.
- **Simulator**: Isaac Gym, a massively parallel GPU-based physics simulator, running at 120Hz (policies operate at 30Hz).
- **Training Resources**: 4096 environments simulated in parallel on a single A100 GPU. Policies trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with approximately 7 billion samples (7 years of simulated time), requiring about 2.5 days of real-world training.
- **Data**: 131 motion clips (approx. 9 minutes total) manually labeled with 1-4 captions each, totaling 265 captions.
- **Latent Space**: 128D latent space for skill embedding.
- **Network Architecture**: Policy, value function, and discriminator modeled using separate multi-layer perceptrons with ReLU units and hidden layers of units.

### 4. Main findings and results

The research presents several findings regarding PADL's capabilities in language-directed character control.

#### 4.1. Skill Reproduction

The trained policies demonstrated the ability to follow a variety of language commands and reproduce diverse motor skills from the motion dataset.
- **Diversity of Skills**: The character was observed to perform locomotion skills (walking, running), as well as more complex athletic behaviors like sword swings and shield bashes.
- **Generalization to New Commands**: Due to the foundation on the large CLIP language model, the system exhibited some robustness to commands not explicitly present in the dataset captions. For example, the command "take a leisurely stroll" prompted a casual walking motion, despite neither "leisurely" nor the phrasing "taking a walk" being in the original captions.
- **Limitations in Generalization**: The model also showed instances of incorrect behavior when faced with novel phrasing. For example, while "right slash" resulted in the correct action, "right slash with sword" surprisingly led to a left slash, indicating sensitivity to specific command structures, possibly due to the relatively small number of captions used for fine-tuning the encoder.

#### 4.2. Multi-Task Control

The multi-task aggregation method, which combines single-task policies using a language-based multiple-choice question-answering (QA) model, proved effective for directing the character in environments with multiple objects.
- **Task and Object Identification Accuracy**: The QA framework consistently identified the correct task and target object from user commands in many cases. For instance, "knock over the blue block" correctly identified the "Strike" task and the blue block as the target.
- **Parsing Unconventional Commands**: The system demonstrated an ability to interpret less literal commands, such as "mosey on down to the maroon saloon," correctly identifying the "Location" task and the red block.
- **Limitations in QA Model Robustness**: Instances of incorrect task identification were observed. "Go to the blue target" was misidentified as a "Strike" task instead of a "Location" task, while "go to the blue block" and "go to the blue tower" were correctly parsed as "Location" tasks. The model also showed sensitivity to minor paraphrasing, correctly interpreting "navigate to the lime rectangular prism" but failing to identify the task for "navigate toward the lime rectangular prism" (identifying "facing" instead of "location").

#### 4.3. Dataset Coverage

An evaluation using a thresholded coverage metric compared PADL's performance against baselines in reproducing behaviors from the motion dataset.
- **Superiority of Learned Embedding**: PADL, utilizing its learned 128D skill embedding, demonstrated better dataset coverage and skill reproduction compared to baselines that directly used the 512D raw CLIP text encodings or PCA-reduced 128D CLIP encodings as policy inputs.
- **Improved Motion Quality**: Directly using CLIP embeddings tended to result in lower quality motions and a higher propensity for incorrect behaviors, suggesting that the PADL's learned motion-text aligned embedding is more suitable for conditioning the control policies.

#### 4.4. Skill Interpolation

The learned skill embedding space enabled semantically meaningful interpolations between different skills.
- **Meaningful Intermediate Behaviors**: Interpolating between the latent representations of commands like "walk forward" and "sprint forward while swinging arms" produced locomotion behaviors at varying speeds. Similarly, interpolation between "walk forward" and "crouching walk forward" resulted in gaits with different walking heights.
- **Inconsistent Intuition**: While many interpolations yielded intuitive intermediate behaviors, the authors noted that not all pairs of commands resulted in equally intuitive or predictable intermediate actions.

### 5. Significance and potential impact

The PADL framework represents a step towards developing language-directed controllers for physics-based character animation, offering a novel approach to the long-standing challenge of creating accessible and versatile animation interfaces.

The primary significance of this work lies in demonstrating the feasibility of controlling complex, full-body physically simulated characters using natural language commands. By integrating recent advancements in NLP with adversarial imitation learning, PADL provides a system where users can specify both high-level tasks and the desired low-level skills. The introduction of a joint motion-text embedding space and an adversarial discriminator conditioned on both state transitions and skill latents contributes to more effective imitation learning, as evidenced by improved dataset coverage compared to alternative embedding approaches. The multi-task aggregation method, leveraging a multiple-choice QA model, offers a practical solution for allowing a character to dynamically switch between tasks and objects through language, despite the inherent challenges of multi-task learning in reinforcement learning.

**Potential impacts include:**

- **Enhanced Animation Workflows**: PADL could simplify character animation workflows for artists and developers by allowing them to describe desired motions and tasks in natural language, potentially reducing the need for laborious keyframe animation, motion capture, or complex scripting.
- **More Intuitive Human-Character Interaction**: The natural language interface could make simulated characters more interactive and easier to control for a broader user base, extending beyond domain experts. This could have implications for video games, virtual reality, robotics simulation, and interactive storytelling.
- **Foundation for Future Research**: This work provides a foundation for further exploration into language-guided control for physics-based agents. The skill embedding concept and the multi-task aggregation strategy can be built upon to address more complex scenarios and increase the robustness of language understanding.

**Current Limitations and Future Directions:**

The paper acknowledges several limitations, which also point to future research avenues:
- **Limited Task Variety**: The current models are restricted to a finite, a priori collection of high-level tasks. Future work could focus on developing more scalable approaches that allow users to specify arbitrary environment interactions through natural language, moving beyond pre-defined task sets.
- **Robustness of Language Encoder**: The language encoder's performance, particularly its robustness to paraphrasing and generalization to new commands, is influenced by the size of the caption dataset. Scaling PADL to much larger, labeled motion capture datasets could significantly improve the diversity of modelable skills and the generalization capabilities of the language encoder.
- **Intuition of Skill Interpolation**: While skill interpolation yields meaningful intermediate behaviors in some cases, its consistency across all command pairs could be improved. Further research into shaping the latent space could enhance the semantic coherence of interpolated skills.

Overall, PADL contributes to the convergence of artificial intelligence, natural language processing, and computer graphics, offering a step towards creating highly controllable and versatile physically simulated characters with broadly accessible interfaces.
