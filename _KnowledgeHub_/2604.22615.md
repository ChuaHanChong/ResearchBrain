# GazeVLA: Learning Human Intention for Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The GazeVLA framework introduces a Vision-Language-Intention-Action (VLIA) model that learns human intention, specifically through gaze, to enhance robotic manipulation capabilities. This model achieved a 22% relative improvement over prior methods in out-of-distribution simulated tasks and doubled success rates in real-world fine-grained manipulation by effectively transferring human intention.

## Problem

- Embodied foundation models for robotic manipulation require extensive, costly real-robot demonstration data, leading to scalability and generalization limitations.
- Existing methods for leveraging human egocentric video data often fail to fully extract high-level behavioral structures, focusing on 'what' actions are performed rather than 'why,' and struggle with the 'embodiment gap' between human and robot kinematics.
- A lack of robust intermediate representations hinders effective knowledge transfer from diverse human demonstrations to robotic systems.

## Method

- A ==Vision-Language-Intention-Action (VLIA)== model, **GazeVLA**, explicitly learns human intention via ==gaze== from a large-scale aggregated egocentric human video dataset.
- The model incorporates an ==intention-action reasoning chain==: intention (discretized gaze tokens) is autoregressively predicted first, followed by continuous action sequences conditioned on this inferred intention.
- A ==multi-stage training strategy== involves extensive pre-training on human data with gaze supervision, followed by fine-tuning on a mixture of robot data (without intention annotations) and additional human data.

## Results

- GazeVLA achieved an intention prediction error of **4.8%** of the image diagonal (11 pixels on 224x224 images) during human pre-training, with a mean hand keypoint position error of **4.71 cm**.
- Demonstrated a **+22% relative improvement** over the best baseline in OOD scenarios on the AV-ALOHA simulation benchmark, with consistent gains under distractors and lighting variations.
- In real-world experiments on **ALOHA** and **Unitree G1**, GazeVLA **doubled** the success rate for fine-grained screw tightening tasks and showed improved performance in dexterous and long-horizon tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Human gaze serves as a natural and scalable proxy for intention, providing a robust, embodiment-agnostic intermediate representation that effectively bridges the human-robot 'embodiment gap.'
> - An explicit intention-action reasoning chain (Chain-of-Thought) allows robots to acquire a deeper understanding of human goals and decision-making, enabling more generalizable knowledge transfer than direct action imitation.
> - Pre-training on large-scale, diverse human egocentric video data is crucial for learning strong intention-action priors, which significantly enhance the robustness and generalization of robotic manipulation policies.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of embodied artificial intelligence has seen advancements with embodied foundation models, which are designed to enable general-purpose manipulation capabilities in robots. However, a significant bottleneck in this area is the heavy reliance on extensive real-robot demonstration data. Collecting such data is costly, time-consuming, and challenging to scale, hindering the development of more robust and generalized robotic skills.

To mitigate this data scarcity, a growing body of research has explored the use of human data, particularly egocentric human videos, as a complementary training source. Human data is more readily available, diverse in scenes and tasks, and naturally encodes high-level behavioral structures such as operational intent and task decomposition. Current approaches to integrating human data into robotic learning typically fall into three categories:
1. **Explicit Visual Primitives:** Using 2D cues like keypoints, bounding boxes, or trajectories as intermediate supervision. These are often post-processed and may not fully capture the underlying decision-making process.
2. **Cross-Embodiment Alignment:** Treating humans as an alternative embodiment by co-training on human and robot data or aligning behaviors in a shared latent action space. This still faces challenges due to the inherent physical differences (embodiment gap) between human and robot bodies.
3. **General Representation Pretraining:** Leveraging human data for pretraining general visual representations or predictive world models. While these methods help in understanding *what* actions are executed, they often overlook *why* a particular action is chosen, thereby missing the rich intentional structure in human behavior.

GazeVLA addresses this gap by proposing that human intention can serve as a powerful intermediate representation to bridge the embodiment gap. Drawing inspiration from cognitive and behavioral principles that suggest intention precedes action, this work positions itself as a method that aims to explicitly learn and transfer this underlying human intent. By focusing on intention, GazeVLA seeks to enable deeper, more generalizable knowledge transfer from human demonstrations to robotic manipulation, moving beyond mere imitation of physical actions to understanding the rationale behind them.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework that explicitly learns and transfers human intention to facilitate robotic manipulation, thereby addressing the limitations of existing embodied foundation models. The core motivations driving this objective are detailed as follows:

1. **Overcoming Data Scarcity in Robotic Manipulation:** Embodied foundation models, despite their breakthroughs, heavily depend on large-scale, costly, and difficult-to-scale real-robot demonstrations. This scarcity of robot data constitutes a fundamental bottleneck for achieving general-purpose embodied intelligence. The researchers aim to alleviate this by leveraging human data, which is abundant and easier to collect.

2. **Bridging the Embodiment Gap through Intention:** While human data offers a promising alternative, effectively extracting transferable knowledge remains a challenge due to the inherent differences in physical form and capabilities (the embodiment gap) between humans and robots. The central argument of this work is that the "intention" underlying human actions can serve as a robust, embodiment-agnostic intermediate representation. This intention is proposed as a way to connect the understanding of human behavior to the execution of robotic actions, rather than attempting direct, low-level behavioral alignment.

3. **Modeling Intention via Gaze:** The researchers posit that human gaze is a natural, observable, and reliable proxy for human intention. Gaze typically precedes physical actions and focuses on task-relevant information, providing direct external signals of intent. This signal can be collected at scale using AR/VR devices, offering a more reliable form of supervision than post-processed visual features. Therefore, explicitly modeling intention through gaze is a key objective to capture the causal structure of human manipulation behavior (perception → intention → action).

4. **Exploiting Rich Behavioral Structures in Human Data:** Egocentric human videos contain rich high-level behavioral structures, including operational intent, task decomposition, and object-centric affordances, which are valuable for learning transferable manipulation skills. Existing methods often overlook *why* an action is taken, focusing primarily on *what* action is executed. GazeVLA aims to leverage these deeper cognitive aspects by explicitly modeling intention, thereby enabling more effective and generalizable knowledge transfer.

5. **Enhancing Generalization and Robustness:** By learning the underlying goals and rationale (intention) behind human actions, the model is expected to acquire stronger prior knowledge and a better task-level semantic understanding. This, in turn, is hypothesized to improve robotic manipulation performance, particularly in terms of generalization to novel situations, unseen objects, and varying environmental conditions (e.g., distractors, lighting changes), and for executing long-horizon and fine-grained tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The GazeVLA framework (Vision-Language-Intention-Action) is designed to learn human intention and transfer it to robotic manipulation through a structured training and inference paradigm.

#### 3.1. Data Collection and Processing

- **Large-scale Human Egocentric Dataset:** A foundational dataset was constructed by aggregating 13 existing egocentric human datasets, resulting in over 150 million frames. This dataset includes both hand and gaze annotations, covering diverse scenes and interaction types, intended to provide extensive prior knowledge of human behavior and intention.
- **Post-training Data:** For fine-tuning, additional human demonstrations were collected using Pupil Neon glasses, synchronously recording egocentric RGB images, high-frequency gaze coordinates, and camera poses. Hand trajectories were captured using the HaWoR dual-hand motion protocol, followed by temporal smoothing and noise filtering. A small amount of robot data was also collected for adaptation.
- **Unified Processing Pipeline:** All unstructured video data underwent a standardized processing pipeline. Long videos were segmented into shorter clips corresponding to atomic actions using Qwen. Redundant segments without hand-object interactions were removed, and language annotations were normalized.
- **Coordinate Alignment:** Local coordinate frames of the camera and wrists were aligned, with the camera coordinate system of the first frame in each clip serving as the reference.
- **Action Representation:** Human hand motions were reconstructed using the MANO model to obtain keypoints. The action space `a ∈ R^(2×(5×3+3+6))=48` includes the 3D positions of five fingertip endpoints, as well as the 3D positions and 6-DoF rotations of both wrists. Binary hand masks indicated hand presence.
- **Intention Representation:** Intention `i ∈ R^2` was modeled using gaze signals, represented as a single 2D pixel coordinate in the image plane. Gaze data underwent filtering to remove saccades and low-confidence samples, accompanied by a binary gaze mask.
- **Sampling Rate:** All data were sampled at 30 frames per second (fps).

#### 3.2. VLIA Model Architecture

The VLIA model takes a language task description `l`, an egocentric camera image `o_t` at time `t`, and the current human hand or robot state `s_t` as inputs. It then predicts an intention `i_t` and a sequence of future actions `a_t:t+H` over a horizon `H`.
- **Hierarchical Prediction:** The model's prediction process is formulated as `π_θ(a_t:t+H, i_t | s_t, o_t, l) = π_θ(i_t | o_t, l) · π_θ(a_t:t+H | i_t, s_t, o_t, l)`. This indicates that intention is inferred first, and then actions are generated conditioned on that inferred intention.
- **VLM Backbone:** PaliGemma was employed as the Vision-Language Model (VLM) backbone, integrating a SigLIP vision encoder and a Gemma-2B language model for multimodal information processing.
- **Action Expert:** A dedicated action expert component is responsible for generating high-frequency, continuous actions. This expert utilizes a conditional flow matching approach.

#### 3.3. Intention-Action Reasoning Chain

Central to the VLIA model is its "intention-action reasoning chain," which explicitly decomposes decision-making.
- **Gaze Tokenization:** Gaze coordinates, representing intention, are discretized into tokens using spatial binning to be compatible with the VLM.
- **Inference Process:** During inference, the model first predicts intention tokens using a next-token prediction mechanism within the VLM. The resulting KV (Key-Value) cache, which encodes the inferred intention, then conditions the action expert to generate continuous action sequences. This design imposes a causal dependency, where intention inference precedes action generation.

#### 3.4. Loss Function

The training objective consists of two main components:
- **Intention Prediction Loss (L_intent):** A standard autoregressive next-token prediction objective for the VLM, calculated as the negative log-likelihood of predicting subsequent intention tokens.
- **Action Generation Loss (L_action):** Follows a conditional flow matching formulation, minimizing the squared difference between the model's action prediction and a target derived from noisy actions and a Gaussian noise term.
- **Combined Objective:** The final loss `L` is a weighted sum: `L = λ_action * L_action + λ_intent * L_intent`. The weights were set to `λ_action = 1` and `λ_intent = 0.1`.

#### 3.5. Training Strategy

- **Pre-Training:** The model undergoes pretraining on the large-scale human egocentric dataset. A staged training strategy is used: an initial warm-up phase freezes the vision encoder and VLM, optimizing only the action expert (including its encoder and decoder). Subsequently, all model parameters are unfrozen and jointly optimized. This strategy aims to stabilize training and prevent representation collapse while preserving the foundation model's capabilities. Synchronized data augmentation (for both images and gaze) is applied to mitigate the distribution gap between human gaze patterns (e.g., center bias) and robotic viewpoints.
- **Post-Training (Fine-tuning):** The model is then fine-tuned by jointly training on a small amount of robot data and a larger volume of human data. Crucially, the robot data does not include intention annotations, while the human data provides diverse supervision including gaze. A 1:1 sampling ratio between human and robot data is maintained. Robot actions are zero-padded and aligned with human action lengths. This joint training is designed to transfer the intention knowledge learned from human demonstrations to robot execution, despite the absence of explicit intention supervision in robot data.

#### 3.6. Implementation Details

Pretraining involved 20,000 steps on 8 NVIDIA A800 GPUs, totaling 1,344 GPU hours. A batch size of 2048 and a learning rate of 5x10^-5 were used.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides both qualitative and quantitative evidence for the effectiveness of the GazeVLA framework across simulation and real-world experiments.

#### 4.1. Human Pretrain Evaluation

- **Qualitative Assessment:** The model's ability to interpret tasks and infer intentions based on language instructions and visual observations was evaluated. As depicted in Figure 4, when presented with identical visual inputs but varying task descriptions (e.g., "uncharge airpods" vs. "charge airpods"), the model generated distinct and appropriate gaze predictions and hand trajectories, demonstrating its capacity to perform task-dependent reasoning and capture the underlying intent.
- **Quantitative Performance:** On the human pretraining data, the average intention prediction error was 4.8% of the image diagonal, which corresponds to an 11-pixel error on a 224x224 image. For hand motion reconstruction, the mean keypoint position error was 4.71 cm, and the wrist rotation error was 12.31 degrees. These metrics indicate that the pretraining phase effectively enabled the model to learn human intention and its synergy with action.

#### 4.2. Simulation Experiment

- **Benchmark and Setup:** Evaluation was conducted on the AV-ALOHA benchmark, a simulation environment featuring bimanual manipulation with active visual perception (21-DoF action space). The training involved 100 trajectories per task, with 100 inference trials for evaluation. To assess robustness and generalization, out-of-distribution (OOD) settings were introduced, including distractors and lighting variations.
- **Baselines:** Performance was compared against LFA, DP (diffusion-based policy), H-RDT (human-pretrained model), and π_0.5 (robot-pretrained VLA model).
- **Results (Table 1):**
 - GazeVLA consistently outperformed all baselines in both in-distribution (ID) and OOD settings.
 - The improvement was particularly notable in OOD scenarios, where GazeVLA achieved a 22% relative improvement compared to π_0.5.
 - Baselines like LFA and DP, while performing adequately in ID settings, showed significant degradation in OOD conditions, with some methods failing entirely under lighting changes.
 - Models with large-scale pretraining (GazeVLA and π_0.5) demonstrated stronger generalization, benefiting from enhanced task-level semantic understanding and visual perception.
 - GazeVLA's explicit intention-based Chain-of-Thought reasoning contributed to improved precision in fine-grained tasks such as "hook package" and "needle threading" (Figure 5).

#### 4.3. Real-World Experiment

- **Robot Platforms and Tasks:** Experiments were performed on two real-world robotic platforms: a bimanual ALOHA robot (gripper-based, 14-DoF) and a Unitree G1 (dexterous manipulation with Inspire hands, 26-DoF). Tasks included gripper-based pick-and-place, screw tightening (fine manipulation), dexterous bottle placement, and long-horizon keyboard typing.
- **Data and Evaluation:** For each task, 10 robot and 50 human demonstration trajectories were collected (approximately 10 minutes each). Evaluation involved 20 trials per method.
- **Baselines:** ACT, DP, and π_0.5.
- **Results (Figure 6):**
 - GazeVLA consistently outperformed all baselines across all task categories and robotic platforms.
 - For gripper tasks, GazeVLA achieved an 85% success rate on pick-and-place and a success rate double that of π_0.5 on screw tightening, indicating enhanced precision from intention guidance.
 - In bimanual dexterous manipulation, GazeVLA demonstrated more stable object placement (e.g., bottles) and more accurate execution in long-horizon tasks (e.g., typing "VLIA" on a keyboard), preventing compounding errors common in baselines.
 - Qualitative visualizations (Figure 7) showed the predicted intention (green cross) accurately localized on the manipulated objects, even in the real-world setting.

#### 4.4. Ablation Study

Ablation studies were conducted using a pick-and-place task (fruit onto a plate) with variations in object positions, object categories, and backgrounds to evaluate generalization.
- **Intention Transfer from Human to Robot:**
 - Qualitative results (Figure 4, 7) indicated that learned intention could jointly reason over language and vision, reflecting task-dependent goals rather than merely visual content.
 - On real robots, despite the absence of intention annotations in robot training data, predicted intention remained accurately localized on manipulated objects, confirming that human intention can be successfully transferred and utilized by robots.
- **Intention-Action Reasoning:**
 - Comparing GazeVLA with a variant (`ours w/o CoT`) that disables autoregressive intention prediction during inference (Table 2), results showed that the intention-action reasoning paradigm consistently improved success rates across diverse scenarios. This indicates that intention provides effective guidance for action reasoning and precise manipulation.
- **Generalization Ability (Table 2):**
 - GazeVLA demonstrated superior generalization compared to ACT, DP, and π_0.5, especially under OOD conditions (object positions, novel objects, unseen backgrounds).
 - Smaller models like ACT and DP showed strong overfitting, with success rates dropping to 0% under background changes. π_0.5 showed some generalization but still suffered from grasping errors.
 - GazeVLA's robustness was attributed to stable intention prediction under visual variations and intention-guided action generation.
- **Contribution of Human Data:**
 - **On-task human data during fine-tuning (without sufficient pretraining):** Comparing `ours (robot only)` with `ours (robot + human finetune)` revealed that such data could be detrimental, potentially leading to model collapse due to overfitting without effective joint representation learning.
 - **On-task human data with pretraining:** Comparing `ours (robot + human pretrain)` with `ours` showed that when sufficient pretraining is provided, on-task human data significantly improves generalization, particularly in varying scene conditions. This benefit arises from the diversity of human demonstrations and intention guidance.
 - **In-the-wild human data during pretraining:** Comparing `ours (robot + human finetune)` with `ours` highlighted the critical role of pretraining on large-scale human data. Without it, fine-tuning on small robot/human datasets led to severe overfitting and ineffective intention transfer.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The GazeVLA framework presents several significant contributions to the field of embodied artificial intelligence and robotic manipulation.

**Significance:**
- **Novel Approach to Embodiment Gap:** The work introduces a novel paradigm for bridging the embodiment gap between humans and robots by explicitly modeling human intention, rather than relying solely on low-level action imitation or feature alignment. Gaze serves as an effective, observable proxy for this intention.
- **Effective Knowledge Transfer:** GazeVLA demonstrates that high-level human knowledge, specifically intent, can be successfully transferred to robotic platforms even when robot training data lacks explicit intention annotations. This enhances the utility of readily available human demonstration data.
- **Improved Generalization and Robustness:** By incorporating intention into a Chain-of-Thought reasoning process, the model achieves superior generalization capabilities across various out-of-distribution scenarios, including novel object positions, new object categories, and unseen backgrounds. This robustness is crucial for deploying robots in varied and unpredictable real-world environments.
- **Enhanced Manipulation Performance:** The intention-action reasoning chain leads to consistent performance improvements in robotic manipulation, particularly for fine-grained tasks requiring precision and for long-horizon tasks that benefit from sequential goal-directed guidance.
- **Leveraging Large-scale Human Data:** The research underscores the critical role of large-scale egocentric human video datasets for pretraining, providing foundational knowledge for understanding human behavior and intention that is transferable to robots.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Reduced Reliance on Robot Data:** GazeVLA offers a method to significantly reduce the dependency on costly and difficult-to-scale real-robot data, making robot learning more accessible and scalable. This could accelerate research and development in robotics by leveraging abundant human demonstration resources.
- **More Intelligent and Adaptive Robots:** By enabling robots to understand the "why" behind human actions, not just the "what," GazeVLA could lead to the development of more intelligent, adaptive, and human-aware robotic systems capable of more sophisticated reasoning and problem-solving.
- **Advancement in Embodied AI:** This work contributes to the broader goal of general-purpose embodied intelligence by demonstrating a principled way to incorporate cognitive-level understanding (intention) into robot control policies.
- **New Directions for Human-Robot Interaction:** A robot capable of inferring human intent through gaze could facilitate more intuitive and effective human-robot collaboration, as the robot would better anticipate and respond to human goals.

**Limitations and Future Directions:**
The authors acknowledge that the current framework does not incorporate robot data during the pretraining stage, nor does it explicitly align human and robot data in a unified latent action space. These represent areas for future work. Constructing a shared action space and jointly pretraining on both human and robot data is identified as an important step towards achieving more general-purpose embodied intelligence.
