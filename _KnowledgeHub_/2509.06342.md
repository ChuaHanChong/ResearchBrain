# Towards bridging the gap: Systematic sim-to-real transfer for diverse legged robots

> [!summary] Summary
> The PACE framework systematically aligns simulation physics with reality using a minimal set of physically interpretable parameters, enabling zero-shot policy transfer for diverse legged robots. It trains energy-efficient locomotion policies by integrating a physics-grounded energy model, resulting in a 32% reduction in Cost of Transport for ANYMAL D (CoT 1.27) and a CoT of 0.97 for TYTAN.

## Problem

- The notorious simulation-to-reality gap causes policies trained in simulation to perform poorly or fail on real robotic hardware, especially due to actuator dynamics inaccuracies.
- Many existing RL policies for legged locomotion neglect actuator-specific energy losses, leading to suboptimal endurance and limited operational time for real robots.
- Designing effective RL controllers often relies on complex, high-dimensional, and labor-intensive hand-tuned reward functions that lack clear physical interpretations.

## Method

- The Precise Adaptation through Continuous Evolution (PACE) framework uses a three-stage bottom-up approach, starting with isolated in-air joint data collection.
- Simulator alignment is achieved by optimizing a compact set of physically interpretable parameters (inertia, damping, friction, bias, command delay) via Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) to match real robot joint dynamics.
- Locomotion policies are trained using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) in the precisely aligned simulator, integrating a physics-grounded energy model for Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSMs) into a compact, four-term reward function.

## Results

- PACE-fitted simulators achieved near-overlap with real measurements for in-air trajectories across TYTAN, ANYMAL, and MINIMAL, outperforming URDF-only baselines and matching state-of-the-art actuator networks.
- Policies trained with PACE demonstrated a 32% reduction in Cost of Transport (CoT) for ANYMAL D (CoT of 1.27) compared to previous work, and an even lower CoT of 0.97 for TYTAN, by enabling more efficient gaits.
- The framework allowed for successful zero-shot deployment of policies on diverse robots, enabling dynamic behaviors such as two-legged balancing, high-speed running (near 3 m/s), and continuous stair climbing.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A minimal set of physically interpretable parameters (e.g., 4n+1 for n joints) is sufficient to accurately model joint-space dynamics, bridging the sim-to-real gap with as little as 20 seconds of encoder-only real-world data per robot.
> - Integrating a physics-grounded energy model directly into the RL reward function effectively optimizes for electrical and mechanical efficiency, leading to substantial reductions in the robot's Cost of Transport.
> - Precise simulator alignment via parameter identification eliminates the need for dynamics randomization during RL training, simplifying the learning process and enabling robust zero-shot policy transfer to hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly tackles one of the most significant hurdles in modern robotics: **bridging the sim-to-real gap** for high-performance legged locomotion. The broader landscape of robot learning, especially with reinforcement learning (RL), heavily relies on simulations for training due to the cost, time, and potential damage associated with real-world experimentation. However, discrepancies between simulated and real-world physics often lead to policies trained in simulation performing poorly or failing entirely when transferred to physical robots—a phenomenon known as the reality gap.

Existing approaches to address this gap generally fall into several categories, as detailed in the paper's related work section:
- **Low-data approaches:** Rely on simplified physics models and **domain randomization** (e.g., varying physical parameters in simulation during training) to make policies robust to uncertainty. While effective for some tasks, extensive randomization can make learning harder or require broad parameter ranges.
- **Moderate-data approaches:** Combine physics priors with **learned corrections** (e.g., residual models, actuator networks) to capture unmodeled effects. These often require specialized sensors (like joint torque sensors) or moderate amounts of real-world data for identification.
- **High-data approaches:** Attempt to learn full dynamics models from extensive real-world interaction, minimizing physical priors.
- **Online adaptation:** Mechanisms to adjust policies or models during deployment to cope with remaining discrepancies.

The paper positions its proposed framework, Precise Adaptation through Continuous Evolution (PACE), as a **moderate-data approach** that differentiates itself by using a **minimal, physically interpretable parameter set** and a **physics-grounded energy model**. Unlike many prior works that use extensive domain randomization (e.g., DexTreme) or rely on complex, high-dimensional reward functions (e.g., for agile locomotion on ANYmal or Spot), PACE simplifies the reward structure and eliminates the need for dynamics randomization in RL training.

Furthermore, this work significantly contributes to the growing interest in **energy-efficient locomotion** for autonomous robots. While many RL controllers achieve agility, they often neglect actuator-specific energy losses, using simplified proxies like squared torque penalties, or overly complex numerical models that are difficult to integrate into RL. PACE introduces a tractable, physics-grounded energy model for Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSMs)—the dominant actuator type in modern legged robots—that balances electrical and mechanical dissipation. This contrasts with approaches that either omit mechanical power consumption (e.g., Lee et al., Miki et al.) or use weak correlations to actual energy. By focusing on a compact, physically meaningful objective, PACE directly addresses the challenge of making legged robots more enduring and practical for real-world deployment, setting a new benchmark for energy efficiency in sim-to-real transfer.

The generalizability across diverse platforms (TYTAN, ANYMAL, MINIMAL, and ten others) without relying on specialized sensors like joint torque sensors is a significant advancement compared to previous actuator network approaches (e.g., Hwangbo et al. on ANYmal), broadening its applicability across the robotics community.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations driving this research are centered on enabling **robust and energy-efficient locomotion** for legged robots in real-world environments by systematically addressing the sim-to-real gap.

Specifically, the authors aim to:

1. **Bridge the simulation-to-reality gap systematically:** The core motivation is that controllers trained in simulation frequently fail to transfer reliably to real hardware due to modeling inaccuracies, especially in actuator dynamics. The goal is to develop a framework that precisely aligns simulation physics with reality, allowing for zero-shot transfer of policies.
2. **Enhance energy efficiency:** Many existing RL approaches for legged locomotion neglect actuator-specific energy losses, leading to suboptimal endurance and payload capacity in real robots. The paper is motivated by the need to integrate a physics-grounded energy model into the learning process to explicitly optimize for electrical and mechanical efficiency.
3. **Simplify reward design:** Prior RL controller pipelines often rely on complex, high-dimensional, hand-tuned reward functions (sometimes more than ten terms), making tuning difficult and disconnecting rewards from physical notions like efficiency. The authors seek to demonstrate that a compact, physically meaningful reward function can be sufficient for effective locomotion training, reducing complexity and expert tuning.
4. **Develop a data-efficient and generalizable framework:** Existing sim-to-real methods can demand specialized sensors, exhaustive parameter identification, or extensive real-world data. The motivation is to create a framework that requires a minimal parameter set to capture the simulation-reality gap and can be applied across a diverse range of legged robot platforms, even those without specialized torque sensors.
5. **Enable zero-shot policy transfer without dynamics randomization:** By accurately modeling the system dynamics through parameter identification, the authors aim to eliminate the need for computationally expensive and often suboptimal domain randomization in the RL training phase, thereby improving training efficiency and policy quality.

In summary, the research is motivated by the desire to make legged robots more practical and deployable by improving the fidelity of simulation models, making control policies more energy-aware, simplifying the learning process, and ensuring broad applicability across different robot designs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, named **Precise Adaptation through Continuous Evolution (PACE)**, follows a three-stage, bottom-up approach to bridge the sim-to-real gap and enable energy-efficient locomotion.

1. **Data Collection (Section 2.1):**
 - **Setup:** Data is collected on the real robot with a fixed base (legs in the air), preventing motion cross-coupling and isolating leg/drive dynamics. This avoids confounding identification with unmeasured external forces during ground contact.
 - **Excitation:** Joints are simultaneously excited using chirp signals (sweeping from minimum to maximum frequency) for 20-60 seconds per sequence. The excitation bandwidth is carefully chosen to cover relevant frequencies for the control policy (up to f_policy/2) while avoiding structural constraints.
 - **Control:** Joint impedance (PD) control is used to execute and record trajectories. Small PD gains are used during identification and policy training to ensure characteristic dynamics appear at lower, more identifiable frequencies.
 - **Logging:** Signals and measurements are time-synchronized and logged at high sampling rates (400-10,000 Hz).

2. **Simulator Alignment via Evolutionary Parameter Identification (Section 2.2):**
 - **Parameter Set:** The core of PACE involves fitting a compact set of physically interpretable parameters that dominantly shape joint-space dynamics. This includes per-joint armature/effective inertia ($I_a$), viscous damping ($d$), Coulomb friction ($\tau_f$), and joint bias ($\tilde{q}_b$), plus a global command delay ($T_d$). For $n$ actuated joints, this results in a manageable $4n+1$ parameters.
 - **Optimization:** Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy (CMA-ES) is used to optimize these parameters. CMA-ES is a robust, gradient-free evolutionary search algorithm suitable for moderate dimensionality, especially when run in massively parallel GPU simulations.
 - **Objective Function:** The optimization minimizes the time-averaged mean-squared joint-position error between the real measured trajectories and the simulated trajectories: $\ell_e = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \|q_{real,i} - q_{sim,i,e}\|^2$. This directly quantifies the fidelity of the simulated dynamics.
 - **Single-joint Dynamics Model:** The method implicitly relies on a closed-loop form of single-joint dynamics, accounting for inertia, damping, and various compensations (Equation 6), while assuming PD gains are known and not co-optimized to avoid non-uniqueness.

3. **Policy Learning and Deployment (Section 2.3):**
 - **Reinforcement Learning Setup:** Once the simulator is aligned, blind locomotion controllers are trained in simulation using Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). Crucially, **no dynamics randomization** is used due to the precise parameter identification. Task randomization (pushes, ground friction) and terrain randomization (flat/rough, stairs, slopes) are still employed.
 - **Partial Observability:** The problem is modeled as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP), using an asymmetric actor-critic architecture. The policy receives proprioceptive observations (base velocities, joint states, commands, previous actions), while the critic has access to privileged state information (noise-free proprioception, base wrench, foot contacts, height map).
 - **Actions and Safe Control:** The policy outputs joint position offsets relative to a default posture. These are converted to torques via PD control. A novel **hard-limit safe PD control** strategy is implemented, applying joint-limit saturation to guarantee zero commanded torque towards a hard limit, thus protecting hardware.
 - **Compact Reward Formulation:** Only four reward terms are used:
 - **Velocity Tracking ($r_v$):** Rewards proximity to commanded base linear and angular velocities.
 - **Energy ($r_e$):** A physics-grounded energetic reward based on Permanent Magnet Synchronous Motors (PMSMs). It combines electrical dissipation ($P_{el} \propto \tau^2_j R_j / (r^2_j k^2_{i,j})$), mechanical power ($P_{mech}$), and gravitational potential power ($P_{pot}$). This formulation balances electrical and mechanical losses and is normalized by a velocity-dependent factor.
 - **Foot-Touchdown (FTD) Penalty ($r_{ftd}$):** Penalizes high foot speed at touchdown to discourage harsh impacts.
 - **Collision Penalty ($r_c$):** Penalizes joint-limit and thigh-environment collisions.
 - **Penalty and Entropy Scheduling:** Energy and FTD penalties are scheduled with an exponential decay factor to allow initial gait discovery, and the entropy coefficient is annealed with a smooth tanh schedule to balance exploration and precision.
 - **Zero-Shot Deployment:** Policies trained in the fitted simulation are deployed directly onto hardware without further fine-tuning.

This bottom-up approach systematically addresses various levels of dynamics (single actuator to full robot), ensuring high-fidelity simulation and enabling the training of robust and energy-efficient locomotion policies.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The paper presents a series of meticulously designed experiments to validate the PACE framework, moving from single-drive analysis to full-robot locomotion and energetic assessments.

#### 4.1. In-Air Evaluation and Validation (Controlled, Contact-Free Conditions)

- **Single Drive Analysis (TYTAN hip actuator):**
 - **Current Loop Validation:** Bode plots confirmed high-bandwidth motor torque tracking up to >100 Hz, exceeding the policy Nyquist frequency. The inner current loop behaves as a near-unit-gain motor torque source within the policy's frequency range.
 - **Mechanical Loop Identification:** When firmware compensations were *disabled*, the identified armature inertia ($I_a$) matched analytical expectations within 2% (rotor only), 6% (interface), and 15% (added mass), with low standard deviation across PD settings. This confirms that PACE accurately recovers physical parameters under ideal conditions.
 - With firmware compensations *enabled*, an additional "virtual inertia" ($I_{comp} = 8.1 \times 10^{-3}$ kg m$^2$) was observed, highlighting the importance of consistent firmware modes between identification and deployment.

- **Full Robot Identification and Validation (TYTAN, ANYMAL, MINIMAL):**
 - **Trajectory Fidelity:** PACE-fitted simulators demonstrated near-overlap between real measurements and simulated replays for in-air trajectories across all three primary platforms (TYTAN, ANYMAL, MINIMAL) (e.g., Figure 5d). This high fidelity was maintained even when validating with different PD gains and unseen random joint step trajectories, indicating a consistent physical model rather than an overfit.
 - **Comparison with Baselines (ANYMAL):** PACE significantly outperformed a "no model" (URDF-only) baseline, which showed large divergences. Crucially, PACE achieved better or comparable performance to a state-of-the-art actuator network (an LSTM-augmented model from ANYbotics) in terms of reducing the "delta" phase portrait (difference between real and simulated joint position/velocity), indicating a smaller reality gap (Figure 1c, Figure 10).
 - **Data Efficiency:** PACE achieved this high fidelity using only ~20 seconds of encoder-only in-air data per robot, a notable advantage over actuator networks which typically require minutes of torque-instrumented data and more complex acquisition techniques.
 - **Physicality of Parameters:** The fitted full-robot $I_a$ for TYTAN was approximately four times higher than the single-drive baseline, attributed to underestimation of link inertia in CAD, compensation effects, and rotor inertia. This shows that the fitted parameters encapsulate combined effects, even if not precisely matching individual CAD values. Identified viscous damping ($d$) and Coulomb friction ($\tau_f$) varied but remained consistent with expectations. All platforms identified a global communication delay ($T_d$) of 7.5 ms, except MINIMAL (0.0 ms).

#### 4.2. On-Ground Locomotion Analysis

- **TYTAN Locomotion:** Open-loop replays of commanded base velocities showed close alignment between real and simulated sequences, confirming that identified models enable robust on-ground tracking (Figure 6). Measured vs. commanded joint torques also matched well.
- **ANYMAL Locomotion:** Policies trained with the URDF-only model failed to walk, whereas both the actuator network and PACE policies succeeded in forward, sideways, and yaw-rate tracking (Figure 7, Figure 21). PACE's performance was competitive with the actuator network, achieving similar steady-state velocity tracking.
- **Energetic Running-Track Evaluations (ANYMAL D and TYTAN):**
 - **Significant Energy Reduction:** PACE-trained policies demonstrated substantial improvements in energetic efficiency. ANYMAL D achieved a Cost of Transport (CoT) of 1.27, a 32% reduction compared to ANYMAL C (CoT 1.86 from previous work). TYTAN achieved an impressive CoT of 0.97.
 - **CoT Decomposition:** Less than half of the total energy budget for both ANYMAL and TYTAN was attributed to actual locomotion (CoL). The majority was consumed by electronics (CoE) and inverter switching (CoD). This highlights that improvements in overall CoT require not only efficient locomotion but also more efficient electronics.
 - **Gait Characteristics:** The precise simulator fitting enabled ANYMAL to adopt straighter-knee gaits, contributing to the observed efficiency gains by reducing locomotion losses by half.
- **Dynamic-Limit Demonstrations:** Policies trained with PACE-fitted simulators were successfully deployed for challenging dynamic behaviors, including two-legged balancing and high-speed running on ANYMAL (peaking near 3 m/s, limited by battery current, Figure 11, Figure 12), and continuous stair climbing on MINIMAL (4 kg robot). These results suggest that residual performance constraints are primarily hardware-power and sensing limited, rather than model-limited.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in this paper, "Towards bridging the gap: Systematic sim-to-real transfer for diverse legged robots," makes several significant contributions with substantial potential impact on the field of legged robotics and broader robot learning.

**1. Reliable and Data-Efficient Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
The development of the PACE framework demonstrates that a compact, physically meaningful parameter set, identifiable from minimal (approximately 20 seconds) encoder-only in-air data, is sufficient to achieve highly accurate simulator alignment. This eliminates the need for extensive, often computationally expensive, and complex dynamics randomization during RL training, simplifying the overall learning pipeline. The ability to perform zero-shot transfer of policies to diverse hardware platforms (ANYMAL, TYTAN, MINIMAL, and ten others) without relying on specialized torque sensors (unlike some state-of-the-art actuator networks) significantly broadens the applicability of RL to a wider range of robotic systems. This data and computation efficiency (single GPU, 10-24 hours per robot) makes advanced robot control more accessible.

**2. Enhanced Energy Efficiency through Physics-Grounded Rewards:**
By integrating a physics-grounded energy model for PMSMs directly into the reward function, PACE enables the training of policies that are explicitly energy-efficient. The demonstrated 32% reduction in Cost of Transport (CoT) for ANYMAL D (achieving 1.27) and the even lower CoT of 0.97 for TYTAN are remarkable achievements. This level of efficiency is crucial for extending the operational endurance and payload capacity of autonomous legged robots, making them more practical for real-world applications such as inspection, logistics, and search and rescue in harsh environments. The finding that less than half of the total energy is spent on locomotion itself also provides critical insights for future robot design, emphasizing the need for more efficient electronics, sensors, and computing.

**3. Simplified and Interpretable Reward Design:**
The empirical evidence that only four physically intuitive reward terms (velocity tracking, energy, foot-touchdown penalty, collision penalty) suffice for effective locomotion training is a significant simplification compared to the complex, high-dimensional, and often hand-tuned reward functions typically used in RL for legged robots. This reduces the burden on roboticists, makes the learning process more transparent, and grounds the control objectives directly in physical principles.

**4. Generalizability and Robustness Across Diverse Platforms:**
The successful deployment and validation of PACE on a wide array of robots, from large quadrupeds like ANYMAL and TYTAN to small, 3D-printed MINIMAL, and even other systems like AIBO and GR-1, highlights the framework's robustness and generalizability. This indicates that the chosen parameterization (inertia, damping, friction, bias, delay) captures fundamental aspects of rigid-body dynamics and actuator behavior common across various designs, making it a valuable tool for the broader robotics community.

**5. Enabler for Advanced Dynamic Behaviors:**
The framework's ability to facilitate dynamic-limit demonstrations, such as two-legged balancing, high-speed running (reaching hardware limits), and continuous stair climbing on a small robot, underscores that the identified models are sufficiently accurate to push the boundaries of robotic capabilities. This opens avenues for developing more agile, adaptable, and robust locomotion skills.

**Potential Future Impact:**
The core takeaway—that a small, physically meaningful parameter set is sufficient for robust sim-to-real transfer—provides a clear direction for future research. It encourages developers to invest in systematic model identification rather than relying solely on black-box learning or extensive randomization. Future work, as outlined by the authors, including accurate identification of electrical constraints, explicit modeling of compliance, and exploring higher-order motion terms, promises to unlock even higher-frequency gaits and more sophisticated control strategies that deliberately exploit hardware dynamics. This could lead to a new generation of legged robots that are not only more agile and robust but also fundamentally more energy-efficient and reliable in deployment.
