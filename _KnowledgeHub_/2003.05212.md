# A Mobile Robot Hand-Arm Teleoperation System by Vision and IMU

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Universität Hamburg and collaborators developed a mobile robotic hand-arm teleoperation system integrating a vision-based deep learning network for dexterous hand control and an IMU-based method for arm control. The system, utilizing a wearable camera holder, allows operators to perform complex manipulation tasks in an unrestricted physical space, demonstrating high success rates in tasks like precision grasping and object insertion.

## Problem

- Achieving dexterous manipulation with anthropomorphic robotic hands via teleoperation remains difficult due to complex human-robot kinematic differences.
- Existing vision-based teleoperation systems restrict operator mobility and workspace, relying on fixed camera setups.
- Previous end-to-end deep learning methods for teleoperation were often limited to simpler grasping tasks or stationary robot positions.

## Method

- An auto-encoder based deep learning network, Transteleop, is introduced to translate human hand depth images into robot hand joint angles using an image-to-image translation approach.
- An IMU-based system tracks human arm movements, which are then mapped to the robot arm's joint angles via inverse kinematics for arm control.
- A custom-designed wearable camera holder enables the depth sensor to move with the operator's arm, providing an unrestricted workspace for teleoperation.

## Results

- The Transteleop network demonstrated an average **10.63%** improvement in joint angle prediction accuracy compared to a baseline TeachNet approach.
- The integrated teleoperation system achieved **100%** success rates in pick-and-place tasks and cup insertion, completing these in average times of **18.5** to **37.2** seconds and **25.5** seconds, respectively.
- The system successfully performed more complex tasks, including object pushing with an **80%** success rate and dual-arm handover with a **60%** success rate, indicating robust multi-task manipulation capabilities.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Image-to-image translation, particularly with a keypoint-weighted reconstruction loss, effectively learns shared pose features between human and robot hands, outperforming direct regression or GAN-based approaches for joint angle prediction.
> - Combining a vision-based deep learning network for dexterous hand control with an IMU-based arm control, facilitated by a wearable camera, enables mobile teleoperation in unrestricted workspaces.
> - While the system achieved high performance in mapping human-to-robot poses, a robot-only model operating within the robot's visual domain still demonstrates an upper performance bound.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Teleoperation represents a critical area within robotics, with established applications in fields such as space exploration, rescue operations, medical surgery, and imitation learning. It often maintains an advantage over fully autonomous intelligent programming when rapid decision-making or handling exceptional scenarios are required. However, achieving dexterous manipulation with anthropomorphic robotic hands through teleoperation continues to present significant challenges.

The present work addresses these challenges by integrating advancements across several subfields of robotics and machine learning:

- **Markerless Vision-based Teleoperation:** This approach is gaining prominence due to its advantages of low cost, reduced invasiveness, and promotion of natural human limb motions. Prior research, such as Dexpilot, has demonstrated the capabilities of vision-based methods. However, many existing markerless systems rely on fixed camera setups, restricting the operator's workspace, or involve complex multi-camera systems and multi-phased data collection procedures. Other methods, like those based on hand pose estimation, often depend heavily on the accuracy of such estimations and require extensive post-processing. Earlier end-to-end deep learning approaches, such as TeachNet, while efficient, were limited to simpler grasping tasks and fixed robot positions.
- **Wearable Device-based Teleoperation:** Various wearable devices, including marker-based trackers, inertial measurement units (IMUs), electromyography (EMG) sensors, and haptic devices, have been employed for teleoperation. While motion capture systems offer high accuracy, they can be expensive and present challenges in marker-camera correspondence. Glove-based systems can restrict natural joint movements, and IMU- or EMG-based methods sometimes lack the versatility or dexterity required for fine manipulation. However, contact-based methods, particularly IMUs, are noted for their ease of implementation and effectiveness in controlling multiple degrees of freedom (DoF) robotic arms, as shown by Fang et al..
- **Image-to-image Translation:** This area of deep learning, leveraging generative models like Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) and auto-encoders, focuses on mapping representations between different image domains. In robotics, image-to-image translation has been applied to convert human demonstrations into robot actions or to learn shared features between human and robot states. The current work specifically explores how this concept can be used to bridge the kinematic and appearance disparities between human and robot hands, a key hurdle in vision-based teleoperation.

By proposing a multimodal system that combines a novel vision-based deep learning network for hand control with an IMU-based method for arm control, facilitated by a custom wearable camera holder, this research aims to overcome limitations of previous approaches regarding dexterity, mobility, and the inherent kinematic differences between human and robotic effectors. The work positions itself by seeking to improve the robustness and flexibility of teleoperation systems, enabling more natural and wide-ranging manipulation tasks.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research was to develop a mobile robotic hand-arm teleoperation system that allows the human operator to work in an unrestricted physical space while performing natural hand motions for a diverse set of manipulation tasks.

This objective was driven by several key motivations:

- **Addressing Challenges in Dexterous Teleoperation:** Teleoperating anthropomorphic robotic hands to perform intricate, dexterous manipulations remains difficult. The goal was to enable the robot to replicate the nuanced movements of a human hand effectively.
- **Compensating for Kinematic Disparities:** A fundamental challenge in vision-based teleoperation is the significant difference in appearance, bone length, global pose, and joint range between human hands and robotic hands. The authors were motivated to find a method that could effectively comprehend and bridge these kinematic similarities and differences without requiring manual calibration or complex retargeting rules. They hypothesized that if a robot could translate the visual representation of a human hand into a corresponding visual representation of its own hand, it would inherently perceive valuable, shared pose features.
- **Overcoming Workspace Limitations of Existing Vision Systems:** Many current vision-based teleoperation systems require the human operator's hand to remain within a fixed, limited field of view of a stationary camera system. This restriction severely impedes the execution of manipulation tasks that demand a wide working area or significant arm movement. A key motivation was to free the operator from this constraint and facilitate true mobility.
- **Improving on Prior Vision-based Methods:** The authors aimed to build upon previous work in markerless vision-based teleoperation, particularly their own TeachNet, by enhancing dexterity and allowing for mobile operation beyond fixed robot positions. They sought a method less dependent on the absolute accuracy of hand pose estimation or extensive post-processing, moving towards an end-to-end solution for hand control.
- **Developing a Complete Hand-Arm Teleoperation Solution:** Recognizing that dexterous hand manipulation often requires coordinated arm movements, the motivation extended to developing a comprehensive system capable of controlling both the robot hand and arm simultaneously. This necessitated a robust and easy-to-implement arm tracking system.
- **Leveraging Image-to-image Translation:** Inspired by the success of image-to-image translation methods in learning shared features between different visual domains, the authors were motivated to apply this paradigm to establish a coherent mapping between human and robot hand poses directly from depth images. This approach was intended to infer not only joint angles but also a visual representation of the robot's corresponding pose, thereby enriching the learned features.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed system integrates a novel vision-based deep learning network, termed Transteleop, for anthropomorphic robot hand control with an IMU-based method for robot arm control. Mobility is facilitated by a custom camera holder. The robot system used is a PR2 robot equipped with a 19 DoF Shadow hand on its right arm.

**A. Transteleop: Vision-based Teleoperation by Image-to-Image Translation**

Transteleop is designed to extract shared kinematic features between human and robot hands and predict the robot hand's joint angles from depth images of a human hand.

- **Network Architecture:** Transteleop employs an auto-encoder structure for image-to-image translation, complemented by a joint prediction module (Figure 2).
 - **Preprocessing Module:** A Spatial Transformer Network (STN) is applied to the input human hand depth images (96 × 96 pixels). This module aims to normalize the spatial orientation of the input images, making the system robust to variations in human hand pose.
 - **Encoder-Decoder Module:**
 - **Encoder:** Takes a depth image of the human hand (I_H) as input. It consists of six convolutional layers, including four downsampling layers and two residual blocks. This module extracts a latent feature embedding (Z_pose) of size 6 × 6 × 512, which represents the shared pose information between the human and robot hands.
 - **Embedding Module:** Extracts a useful feature embedding (Z_pose) from the encoder's output, transforming it into an 8192-dimensional representation.
 - **Decoder:** Reconstructs a depth image of the robot hand (Î_R) from a fixed viewpoint, using the latent pose feature Z_pose. It connects to the encoder through fully-connected layers, allowing each decoder unit to reason over the entire image content, compensating for non-matched pixel areas between I_H and I_R. The decoder uses four up-convolutional layers followed by a convolutional layer for image generation.
 - **Joint Module:** This module takes the robot feature vector (Z_R) from the embedding module, which contains richer pose features specific to the robot hand, and predicts the 19-dimensional joint angles (J_hand) of the robot hand. It consists of three fully-connected layers.

- **Loss Functions:**
 - **Keypoint-based Reconstruction Loss (L_recon):** This is an L2 loss designed to minimize pixel-wise error in the reconstructed robot hand image (Î_R) compared to the ground truth (I_R). Crucially, it incorporates a weighting factor (α_i) for each pixel, which is higher for pixels in the vicinity of 15 predefined keypoints (e.g., fingertips, PIP, MCP, DIP joints) on the robot hand. This emphasizes accurate local feature reconstruction around anatomically important points.
 - **Joint Loss (L_joint):** This is a Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss that minimizes the difference between the predicted joint angles (J_hand) and the ground truth robot joint angles (J).
 - **Overall Training Objective (L_hand):** The total loss is a weighted sum of the reconstruction and joint losses: L_hand = λ_recon · L_recon + λ_joint · L_joint. The scaling factors were set to λ_recon = 1 and λ_joint = 10.

- **Paired Dataset:** Transteleop was trained on a dataset from comprising 400,000 pairs of simulated human and robot hand depth images. For the robot hand reconstruction, only images taken from a fixed front viewpoint of the robot were utilized.

- **Optimization and Inference:** The network was optimized using minibatch stochastic gradient descent with the Adam optimizer (learning rate 0.002). Batch normalization and ReLU activation functions were used. During inference, only the encoder and joint modules are activated to regress joint angles efficiently.

**B. Hand-Arm Teleoperation System**

- **Mobile Camera Holder:** To address the limitation of a fixed camera viewpoint, a lightweight (248 g) 3D-printed camera holder was designed to mount an Intel RealSense SR300 depth sensor (108 g) on the human forearm (Figure 4). This design ensures that the camera moves with the operator's arm, maintaining the hand within its field of view during large arm movements.

- **IMU-based Arm Control:**
 - **Device:** A Perception Neuron (PN) device, an IMU-based motion capture system, is used to track the operator's arm movements. Three IMU elements are specifically employed to capture the motion of the palm, upper arm, and forearm.
 - **Pose Estimation:** The global frame of the PN device is aligned parallel to the robot's base frame. Rotation data from the PN and known link lengths of the robot arm are used to calculate the desired wrist pose for the robot.
 - **Inverse Kinematics (IK):** The BioIK solver computes the joint angles for the robot's 5 DoF right arm based on the desired wrist pose.
 - **Velocity Control:** The angular velocity (V_n,t) for each robot arm joint is calculated as a weighted sum of two terms: a feedforward term based on the difference between the current and previous desired IK joint angles (J_ik_n,t - J_ik_n,t-1), and a feedback term based on the difference between the current desired IK joint angle and the current robot joint state (J_ik_n,t - J_robot_n,t). Scaling factors δ_1 = 0.7 and δ_2 = 0.1 were used. The arm's velocity control operates at 20 Hz, while the hand's trajectory control operates at 10 Hz.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presented two main sets of results: a network evaluation of Transteleop against baselines and a series of manipulation experiments demonstrating the full multimodal hand-arm teleoperation system.

**A. Transteleop Network Evaluation**

The regression performance of Transteleop was quantitatively compared against four baselines: TeachNet, GANteleop (Transteleop with an added PatchGAN discriminator and adversarial loss), Robotonly (a model that directly predicts joint angles from robot hand images without an image-to-image translation module, taken from a fixed third-person viewpoint), and Transteleop without the Spatial Transformer Network (STN) module. Evaluation metrics included the fraction of frames with maximum joint angle/distance errors below a threshold and the average angle error over all joints.

- **Performance Comparison:**
 - **Robotonly** demonstrated superior performance across all metrics. This outcome was attributed to the matched domain (robot images for training and inference) and identical viewpoint, serving as an upper bound for performance within the robot's own domain.
 - **Transteleop and GANteleop** both showed an average improvement of 10.63% in accuracy (fraction of frames with maximum joint angle error below a threshold) compared to TeachNet. This indicates that image-to-image translation methods are more effective in capturing informative pose features from the robot hand, likely because they are tasked with generating a full robot hand image.
 - **Transteleop vs. GANteleop:** Transteleop generally performed better than GANteleop. The authors inferred that the discriminator in GANteleop, while aiming for visually realistic reconstructed images, might dilute the supervision signals for joint angle prediction (L_joint), leading to slightly inferior regression performance.
 - **Effect of STN:** The inclusion of the STN module in Transteleop did not yield a statistically significant improvement over Transteleop without STN. This suggests that while STN provides some appearance normalization, its impact on transforming human hand poses to a canonical robot pose was limited in this specific task.
- **Joint Error Analysis:** The absolute average joint regression error for all methods was observed to be below 0.05 radians. The highest errors consistently occurred on thumb joint 5, which the authors attributed to the substantial kinematic discrepancies between the human thumb and the Shadow Hand's thumb. Examples of reconstructed robot images, while somewhat blurry, were reported to closely resemble the ground truth, supporting the claim that the latent pose feature contained sufficient pose information.

**B. Manipulation Experiments**

The full multimodal teleoperation system was evaluated across four types of physical manipulation tasks using a PR2 robot with a Shadow Hand, conducted by three human demonstrators after a warm-up phase. The control frequencies were 20 Hz for the arm and 10 Hz for the hand.

- **Pick and Place:**
 - **Scenario 1 (Pringles can into bowl):** Required power grasp skills. Achieved a 100% success rate with an average completion time of 18.5 seconds.
 - **Scenario 2 (Wooden cube onto brick):** Required precision grasp skills and demonstrated the benefit of a wide workspace. Achieved a 100% success rate with an average completion time of 37.2 seconds. The longer time was attributed to the smaller target and need for higher precision.
- **Cup Insertion:** Involving three concentric cups, this task tested precision grasping and releasing abilities. Achieved a 100% success rate with an average completion time of 25.5 seconds.
- **Object Pushing:** Required pushing a rectangular brick to a designated pose, involving pushing, sliding, and precision grasping. Achieved an 80% success rate with an average completion time of 62.0 seconds. The longer duration was primarily due to the time taken to precisely orient the brick (error below 5 mm).
- **Dual-arm Handover:** The robot's left arm (IMU-controlled) handed a roll of paper to its right hand (Transteleop-controlled), testing system coordination. Achieved a 60% success rate with an average completion time of 36.33 seconds. The relatively lower success rate was mainly attributed to imprecise control of the left gripper (controlled by IMU), sometimes leading to accidental object loss.

**Summary of Manipulation Results:** The high success rates and relatively short completion times for pick and place and cup insertion tasks demonstrated the system's proficiency in both precision and power grasps. The object pushing task highlighted the system's capability for nuanced manipulation, though fine-tuning orientation proved time-consuming. The dual-arm handover task, while demonstrating system coordination, also indicated a limitation in the precision of the IMU-based gripper control compared to the vision-based multi-finger control. Qualitatively, figures illustrated the robot successfully executing these tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The presented research introduces a functional multimodal mobile teleoperation system that combines deep learning for dexterous hand control with IMU-based arm control, marking several contributions to the field of robotics.

**Significance:**

- **Novel Multimodal Teleoperation System:** The primary significance lies in the integration of vision-based hand control (Transteleop) and IMU-based arm control into a cohesive, mobile system. This addresses the need for comprehensive control over both fine hand movements and large-scale arm motions.
- **Bridging Kinematic Disparities via Image-to-Image Translation:** Transteleop's approach of using image-to-image translation to learn and map the complex kinematic relationships between human and robot hands directly from depth images is significant. It offers an effective, data-driven method to overcome the inherent differences in appearance and anatomy, resulting in robust joint angle prediction for the robot hand.
- **Enhanced Mobility and Workspace Freedom:** The self-designed, wearable camera holder represents a practical solution to a common limitation in vision-based teleoperation: the restricted field of view of fixed cameras. By moving the camera with the operator's arm, the system grants the teleoperator an effectively unlimited workspace, enabling more natural and wide-ranging manipulation tasks that were previously difficult or impossible.
- **Demonstrated Dexterity and Reliability:** The successful execution of a variety of complex manipulation tasks, including precision and power grasps, cup insertion, object pushing, and dual-arm handover, validates the feasibility and reliability of the proposed system in real-world scenarios. This demonstrates the system's capability to perform intricate manipulations that go beyond simple pick-and-place operations.
- **Insight into Control Modality Suitability:** The comparative performance in tasks, particularly the lower success rate in the IMU-controlled left gripper during the handover task, provides insights into the strengths and weaknesses of different control modalities. It suggests that vision-based methods are more suitable for multi-finger dexterous control, while IMU-based methods remain effective for broader arm movements.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Expanding Applications of Teleoperation:** By providing a more mobile and dexterous control interface, this system could broaden the applicability of teleoperation in domains requiring human-level manipulation in dynamic or unstructured environments, such as remote assistance, disaster response, maintenance in hazardous settings, or advanced manufacturing.
- **Advancing Human-Robot Collaboration:** The ability to naturally transfer human hand and arm movements to a robot could facilitate more intuitive human-robot collaboration and imitation learning scenarios, reducing the training burden for operators.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The Transteleop network structure, particularly the keypoint-based reconstruction loss, offers a novel approach to learning hand pose mappings, which could inspire further research in visual perception for robotics. The demonstrated mobile hand-arm control system provides a robust baseline for integrating additional sensory feedback (e.g., tactile, force) and autonomy layers.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The authors acknowledge several limitations that indicate directions for future research:

- **Operator Comfort:** The wearable camera holder, while enabling mobility, adds an extra burden to the operator during long-term teleoperation. Future work aims to achieve real-time hand tracking using a camera mounted directly on the robot arm, eliminating the need for a wearable device.
- **Dataset Limitations:** The current dataset lacks specific hand poses frequently used in highly dexterous manipulations (e.g., bottle opening, screw tightening). Future plans include collecting a more comprehensive dataset focusing on subtle thumb, index, and middle finger poses.
- **Advanced Sensory Integration:** The system currently lacks slip detection and force estimation capabilities, which are crucial for reducing the operator's control burden and preventing unintentional collisions during contact-rich tasks. Integration of these features is planned to enhance robustness and safety.

Overall, this research represents a step towards more intuitive, mobile, and dexterous teleoperation of complex robotic systems, with implications for a wide range of real-world applications.
