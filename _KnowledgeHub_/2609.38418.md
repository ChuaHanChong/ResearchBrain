# PneuTac: Tactile Manipulation with Soft Pneumatic Robots via Unified MPM-Gaussian Splatting Simulation

> [!summary] Summary
> PneuTac is a unified simulator for soft pneumatic robots carrying vision-based tactile sensors. It models robot and gel dynamics with the material point method and renders them with coupled 3D Gaussian splatting. After calibration from a few images, learned surrogate networks speed it up, and it turns 10 real demonstrations into 100 tactile-guided simulated ones, raising real-world success on three delicate tasks over real-only training.

## Problem

- Vision-based tactile simulators such as Taxim, Tacchi and DOT-Sim assume rigid robot and object physics, so the sensor cannot be attached to a compliant, deforming robot body.
- Soft robot simulators built on finite elements or multi-physics engines largely ignore contact and tactile sensing, and need heavy, repeated calibration to close the sim-to-real gap.
- Collecting demonstrations on real soft hardware is slow and wears out compliant parts, yet no prior framework jointly models compliant actuation and compliant tactile sensing for policy learning.

## Method

- **MPM-3DGS simulator**: ==material-point== particles sampled from CAD models represent a ==Neo-Hookean== finger and gel, a rigid multi-joint spine drives bending, and each particle is bound to a ==3D Gaussian== for rendering.
- **Vision-based real-to-sim calibration**: ==CMA-ES== fits material and pressure-to-bending parameters using a ==masked appearance loss== plus a ==silhouette-IoU loss==, and a ==photometric contact model== based on slope and depth is fit from one indentation image.
- **Action and perception surrogates with tactile-guided CEM-MPC**: an ==MLP residual-torque network== drives a skeleton model, a ==UNet== maps fast probe-grid indentation to MPM depth, and the planner tracks demonstrated contact-area trajectories.

## Results

- Tactile rendering beats Tacchi, DOT-Sim and Taxim on held-out images (RMSE **10.18**, PSNR **28.11 dB**, NCC **0.982**). A second Digit sensor reaches NCC **0.976** after recalibration, while reusing the original calibration gives RMSE **22.96**.
- Calibrated tip error is **4.2 mm** for the finger (under **4%** of its length) and **4.9 mm** for the arm. The surrogates give **5x** action and **3000x** tactile speedups with **0.077 mm** indentation error.
- Sim+Real policies reach **83.3/90.0/66.7%** on switch, egg carton and card tasks, versus **60.0/26.7/33.3%** for real-only, and beat naive augmentation. Removing touch drops the switch task to **30.0%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Binding Gaussian splats to physics particles turns the renderer into a calibration tool: ordinary camera images replace dedicated system-identification rigs for soft, deformable devices.
> - Calibrate on the expensive high-fidelity model, then distil it into learned surrogates: the fidelity cost is paid once, while rollouts stay fast enough to generate demonstrations at scale.
> - Simulation augmentation helps most where a few real demonstrations undersample the success space, and reward-driven RL suffers more from simulator inaccuracy than cloning anchored on demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

PneuTac addresses a practical gap in training soft robots. Collecting demonstrations for delicate contact tasks on soft robots is slow, costly, and wears the hardware. Existing simulations generally handle either a soft robot or a vision-based tactile sensor, not both as a coupled learning system. A vision-based tactile sensor uses a camera to read contact-induced deformation in a soft gel. PneuTac's central move is to simulate the soft robot and gel together. It then uses simulated demonstrations to supplement a small real dataset. The real-world tests were switch flipping, egg-carton opening, and pulling a card from a deck in a container.

### 2. One model for body and touch

The simulator rests on two techniques. MPM is a particle-and-grid method for simulating deformable bodies. 3D Gaussian splatting (3DGS) renders images from colored, positioned 3D Gaussians. PneuTac samples particles from CAD models and uses MPM for both the soft robot and the tactile gel. It then couples particle motion to Gaussian motion for rendering.

Calibration happens in two parts. Robot parameters are calibrated against images of the finger at six quasi-static pressures: 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 kPa. For tactile appearance, PneuTac trains a rest image model. It then fits a contact-lighting model using a registered rest/indentation image pair. The authors describe the indentation image as a single-image calibration for contact rendering.

Fast stand-ins replace expensive simulation:

- **Robot body.** A learned residual-torque network lets a simplified skeleton stand in for full robot MPM. The skeleton model takes about 10 ms, compared with 50 ms for full MPM (5×).
- **Tactile gel.** A perception network maps indentation from a fast probe grid to MPM-equivalent gel deformation. The probe takes about 50 ms, compared with 150 s for high-fidelity tactile simulation (reported as 3000×).

These figures are simulation timings.

### 3. Simulated touch on real tasks

The data pipeline started small. The authors collected 10 real demonstrations for each task. From these, a GPU-parallel CEM model-predictive planner bootstrapped 100 simulated demonstrations per task. The planner warm-started from the real demonstrations and used both trajectory and tactile contact-area guidance.

The Sim+Real policy was pretrained on the 100 simulated demonstrations. It was then fine-tuned on the same 10 real demonstrations.

Results are reported as success rates. Success is the percentage of successful task trials. Each task had 10 trials per seed across 3 seeds, and results are mean ± SEM across seeds.

On egg-carton opening, Sim+Real achieved 90.0 ± 5.8% success. The Real policy, trained on the same 10 real demonstrations, achieved 26.7 ± 8.8%.

A naive-augmentation condition tested whether the simulator adds value beyond simply augmenting the same demonstrations. It made 100 augmented trajectories without PneuTac's simulator and used the same pretraining/fine-tuning protocol. According to the authors, Sim+Real outperformed both the Real and naive-augmentation conditions across the three reported tasks.

Doubling the real data qualifies this result. On switch flipping, 20 real demonstrations reached 90.0 ± 5.8%, compared with 83.3 ± 3.3% for Sim+Real. Sim+Real did better on egg-carton opening and card pulling. Simulation is therefore not uniformly better than more real data. The paper also has no direct downstream policy comparison against another simulator.

### 4. A bridge to real touch

PneuTac's contribution is a coupled model of the bending finger and its tactile gel. It turns a small set of real demonstrations into additional, touch-guided simulated experience, then returns to the real finger for testing.

The supporting evidence has clear limits. It covers the tested finger and three tasks, with real-data fine-tuning. It does not show that simulation beats a larger real-data budget on every task.
