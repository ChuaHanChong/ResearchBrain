# Semantically Structured Mixture-of-Experts for Compositional Robotic Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Hong Kong and Southern University of Science and Technology introduced Semantically Structured Mixture-of-Experts Diffusion Policy (SMoDP), which integrates language-grounded skill semantics into diffusion policies to guide expert routing for robotic manipulation. The method achieved a 0.97 success rate on LIBERO-10 and 0.95 on LIBERO-90, outperforming existing diffusion and MoE baselines while demonstrating superior data efficiency and enabling compositional transfer with only 3.4% of parameters fine-tuned.

## Problem

- Diffusion policies excel in single-task control but struggle with scalability, generalization, and inference efficiency in diverse multi-task robotic manipulation environments.
- Existing Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) routing in diffusion policies relies on low-level signals, leading to fragmented expert specialization and 'routing confusion' at skill boundaries, hindering interpretability and transferability.
- Developing general-purpose robot policies faces a 'trilemma' of precise control, broad generalization across tasks, and inference efficiency suitable for real-time deployment, often requiring large, computationally expensive models.

## Method

- An offline ==VLM-based skill abstraction module== automatically generates fine-grained, open-vocabulary semantic skill annotations (verb-noun pairs) from unstructured demonstrations, avoiding manual labeling or online VLM calls.
- A skill-conditioned **Diffusion MoE Policy** replaces feed-forward networks with ==MoE blocks==, where expert routing is explicitly guided by a lightweight skill predictor's output and enforced with ==chunk-consistent routing==.
- A ==dual semantic contrastive alignment== strategy (Inter-Modal and Intra-Modal) ensures that the predicted skill embeddings align with language semantics and that semantically similar skills induce consistent expert routing patterns.

## Results

- SMoDP achieved state-of-the-art success rates on both LIBERO-10 (**0.97**) and LIBERO-90 (**0.95**), surpassing diffusion and MoE baselines, including those with large-scale pre-training.
- The method demonstrated superior data efficiency, achieving performance comparable to a fully trained MoDE (Pretrain) using only **50%** of the training data (25 vs. 50 demonstrations per task) on LIBERO-90.
- On real-world bimanual ALOHA tasks, SMoDP significantly outperformed MoDE, achieving an average success rate of **91.25%** compared to MoDE's **47.50%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly grounding expert specialization in language-defined skill semantics provides an effective inductive bias, enabling parameter and data-efficient learning for multi-task robotic manipulation.
> - Automatically derived semantic skill annotations, even without online VLM interaction, can effectively supervise and structure MoE routing, promoting interpretable expert usage and coherent behavior segmentation.
> - A modular policy architecture with skill-aware routing facilitates compositional transfer, allowing policies to adapt to novel tasks and unseen skill combinations by reusing frozen expert weights and fine-tuning minimal parameters.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Recent advancements in robotic manipulation have seen diffusion-based policies emerge as a powerful paradigm, particularly for achieving precise control in single-task scenarios. These policies excel at modeling multimodal action distributions and generating temporally coherent action sequences. However, a significant challenge arises when attempting to scale these models to general-purpose, multi-task environments. This scalability issue presents a "trilemma": the simultaneous desire for precise control, broad generalization across diverse tasks, and inference efficiency suitable for real-time robotic deployment. Current approaches often necessitate compromises, with standard diffusion policies struggling to generalize without extensive parameter scaling, while large "robot foundation models" (e.g., RDT, Pi-series) achieve breadth at the cost of massive computational overhead, rendering them impractical for resource-constrained platforms.

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures have been proposed as a solution to this efficiency-capacity trade-off. By activating only a sparse subset of parameters per inference step, MoEs can increase model capacity without a proportional increase in computational cost. While MoE has been integrated into diffusion policies (e.g., MoDE), existing MoE routing mechanisms typically rely on low-level signals such such as diffusion noise levels or latent statistics. This approach often overlooks the inherent semantic and temporal structure of manipulation tasks, leading to what the authors term "routing confusion" at skill boundaries. Consequently, reusable behaviors (e.g., "grasping") may be fragmented across different experts, which limits both the interpretability and transferability of learned policies.

Previous research has explored structured guidance for MoE routing to improve expert specialization and efficiency, often deriving structure from latent similarity. Other works have utilized language or skill abstractions for planning and orchestration, or discovered reusable skills from latent or action-space structures. However, these methods have not explicitly leveraged language-grounded skill semantics to organize expert routing directly within the policy's internal mechanisms. This paper addresses this gap by introducing a method that explicitly grounds expert specialization in semantic task structure, aiming to enable more consistent reuse of shared behaviors and facilitate compositional transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives and motivations for this research are:

1. **Address the scalability bottleneck of diffusion policies in multi-task robotic manipulation**: While diffusion policies achieve precision, they struggle with broad generalization and inference efficiency in diverse multi-task environments without massive computational resources. The authors aim to overcome this by introducing a more parameter-efficient architecture.
2. **Overcome limitations of existing MoE routing mechanisms**: Current MoE routing often relies on low-level statistical signals (e.g., diffusion noise or latent variance), which do not account for the semantic and temporal compositionality of manipulation tasks. This leads to inefficient expert specialization and fragmentation of reusable behaviors across tasks, hindering interpretability and transferability. The motivation is to introduce a routing mechanism that is semantically aware.
3. **Ground expert specialization in semantic task structure**: The core motivation is to align the policy's modular architecture with the compositional nature of manipulation tasks. By explicitly linking expert activation to human-interpretable, language-defined skill semantics, the authors seek to promote more coherent and interpretable expert specialization.
4. **Enable compositional transfer and parameter-efficient learning**: The research aims to facilitate the transfer of knowledge from existing skills to semantically similar new skills. This includes enabling the policy to tackle novel tasks by recombining previously learned skill modules through parameter-efficient fine-tuning, thereby minimizing the number of parameters and computational cost for adaptation.
5. **Develop an automated method for skill annotation**: Recognizing the impracticality of manual skill labeling for large datasets, a key motivation is to leverage Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to automatically derive fine-grained, open-vocabulary semantic skill annotations from unstructured demonstrations offline. This provides the necessary supervision without manual effort or expensive online VLM calls.
6. **Ensure robust and consistent routing decisions**: The authors aim to develop a learning strategy that not only aligns predicted skill embeddings with language semantics but also enforces consistency in routing behavior for semantically similar skills. This dual alignment is motivated by the need for stable expert activation and effective skill reuse across visually distinct but functionally related scenarios.

In summary, the motivation is to bridge the gap between high-performance but resource-intensive diffusion policies and efficient, generalizable multi-task robot learning by embedding semantic task structure directly into the policy's MoE routing mechanism.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Semantically Structured Mixture-of-Experts Diffusion Policy (SMoDP), integrates skill semantics into multi-task diffusion policies to guide expert selection. SMoDP builds on a standard diffusion policy formulation for action chunk generation and introduces two primary innovations: an offline VLM-based skill abstraction module and a skill-conditioned diffusion MoE policy with dual semantic contrastive alignment.

**A. Offline Semantic Skill Abstraction**
Recognizing the absence of fine-grained skill annotations in existing datasets and the cost of manual labeling, SMoDP leverages Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to automatically generate offline skill annotations. These annotations are used exclusively during training to supervise skill prediction and routing, without involving the VLM during inference. The pipeline comprises three stages for a given trajectory $\tau_n$ and task instruction $g_n$:

1. **Video Downsampling**: High-frequency raw observation sequences are temporally downsampled by a factor $\lambda$ to create a video format $V_n$. This filters out redundant frames and non-essential temporal jitter, reducing VLM token budget and mitigating potential hallucinations, while retaining critical state transitions.
2. **Semantic Skill Extraction**: The downsampled video $V_n$ and task instruction $g_n$ are fed into a VLM (specifically, Qwen3-VL is used). A structured prompting strategy, constraining the output to verb–noun pairs (e.g., $\langle \text{pick up, red cup} \rangle$), is employed to distill behaviors into a sequence of atomic skills $S_n = \{s_{n,1}, \dots, s_{n,K_n}\}$. This open-vocabulary verb-noun format serves as a semantic bottleneck, enhancing interpretability and compositionality.
3. **Temporal Partitioning**: For each identified skill $s_{n,j} \in S_n$, the VLM is prompted to estimate its corresponding temporal boundary $[t^{(j)}_{\text{start}}, t^{(j)}_{\text{end}}]$ within the original trajectory $\tau_n$.
This process results in a semantically labeled dataset $D_{\text{sem}}$, where each timestep is assigned a skill label.

**B. Skill-Conditioned Diffusion MoE Policy**
The core of SMoDP is a diffusion transformer where feed-forward networks are replaced by MoE blocks, with expert selection guided by skill semantics.

1. **Diffusion Policy Over Action Chunks**: The policy learns a conditional denoiser $f_\theta$ that predicts the noise $\epsilon$ from a noisy action chunk $x_s$, current observation $o_{n,t}$, task instruction $g_n$, and diffusion step $s$. The training objective is a standard denoising loss $L_{\text{diff}}$.
2. **Lightweight Skill Predictor**: To avoid expensive online VLM calls, a lightweight skill predictor $P_\phi$ is trained to infer a compact semantic skill embedding at inference time. From a multimodal context token sequence $C$ (visual tokens from observation $e_{\text{obs}}$ and language tokens from instruction $e_{\text{goal}}$), a learnable query $q_{\text{skill}}$ extracts skill-relevant information via cross-attention. This is followed by a feed-forward projection into the frozen text-embedding space, yielding $\hat{z}_{\text{skill}}$. This query-based design is computationally efficient and produces a semantic skill representation.
3. **Skill-Conditioned MoE Denoising**: The denoiser $f_\theta$ is implemented as a Diffusion Transformer. The predicted skill embedding $\hat{z}_{\text{skill}}$ is projected into a conditioning vector $c$ and broadcast to all tokens in the transformer layers, guiding the MoE routing decisions.
4. **Chunk-Consistent Skill-Based Routing**: Within each MoE layer, expert routing incorporates $\hat{z}_{\text{skill}}$ to ensure consistency across all action tokens within an predicted chunk. The router input is formed by replacing the action-token segment with a single skill token $h_{\text{skill}} = W_z \hat{z}_{\text{skill}}$. A multi-layer perceptron (MLP) router then predicts routing logits $\tilde{\Psi}_l$. To enforce chunk-level consistency, the logits of the skill token are broadcast to all $H$ action tokens in the chunk. Expert gates $G_l$ are obtained via temperature-scaled softmax and top-k sparsification, determining which experts are activated for each token. This design aims to reduce expert switching within a chunk and encourage skill-aligned expert specialization.

**C. Dual Semantic Contrastive Alignment**
To ensure the skill predictor and MoE router effectively utilize the VLM-derived semantic structure, SMoDP employs a dual contrastive learning strategy:

1. **Language-Derived Continuous Weights**: Continuous similarity weights $w_{ij}$ are constructed for a minibatch from pairwise cosine similarities between normalized text embeddings ($u_i$) of VLM-generated skill labels $y_i$, extracted using a frozen Sentence-BERT text encoder. This allows for soft, continuous supervision signals. Only top-m most similar samples are retained to reduce noise.
2. **Inter-Modal Contrastive Learning (InterCL)**: This objective aligns the normalized predicted skill embedding $q_i = \text{norm}(\hat{z}_{\text{skill},i})$ with the frozen language-encoder embeddings $u_j$. The $L_{\text{interCL}}$ loss is formulated as a weighted contrastive loss based on inter-modal similarity logits $\ell_{ij} = \langle q_i, u_j \rangle / \tau_c$. This encourages $\hat{z}_{\text{skill}}$ to inherit compositional structure and generalize based on semantic proximity.
3. **Intra-Modal Contrastive Learning (IntraCL)**: This objective encourages semantically similar skills to induce similar routing patterns. It operates directly on the flattened and normalized routing logits $r_{l,i}$ from selected MoE layers. The $L_{\text{intraCL}}$ loss is a weighted contrastive loss based on intra-modal similarity logits $\ell^l_{ij} = \langle r^l_i, r^l_j \rangle / \tau_c$, leveraging the same language-derived weights $w_{ij}$. This acts as a topology-preserving constraint, compelling the router to reflect semantic distances in its expert selection.

**D. Overall Training Objective**
The total loss function combines the diffusion denoising loss ($L_{\text{diff}}$), a standard MoE load-balancing regularizer ($L_{\text{lb}}$), InterCL ($L_{\text{interCL}}$), and IntraCL ($L_{\text{intraCL}}$), weighted by hyperparameters $\lambda_{\text{lb}}$, $\lambda_{\text{inter}}$, and $\lambda_{\text{intra}}$:
$L = L_{\text{diff}} + \lambda_{\text{lb}} L_{\text{lb}} + \lambda_{\text{inter}} L_{\text{interCL}} + \lambda_{\text{intra}} L_{\text{intraCL}}$.
This objective trains the model to perform action denoising while ensuring its skill representations and routing behavior are aligned with meaningful language-derived semantic structure.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of SMoDP was conducted on both simulated (LIBERO benchmark) and real-world (ALOHA bimanual system) robotic manipulation tasks, comparing it against various diffusion-based, MoE-based, and skill-structured imitation learning baselines.

**A. Multi-task Performance on LIBERO-10 and LIBERO-90**
- SMoDP achieved the highest average success rates on both LIBERO-10 and LIBERO-90 benchmarks.
- On LIBERO-10, SMoDP obtained a 0.97 success rate, outperforming DP-T (0.51), DP-CNN (0.66), QUEST (0.73), SDP (0.78), MoDE (0.69), and even MoDE (Pretrain) (0.92), which was pre-trained on a large-scale dataset.
- On LIBERO-90, SMoDP reached a 0.95 success rate, surpassing DP-T (0.71), DP-CNN (0.76), QUEST (0.89), SDP (0.91), MoDE (0.94), and MoDE (Pretrain) (0.95).
- The results indicate that language-grounded skill routing provides an effective inductive bias, enabling strong multi-task performance without reliance on external large-scale pre-training data, and the gains are attributed to structured expert activation rather than merely sparse capacity.

**B. Data Utilization Efficiency**
- When trained on reduced datasets (5, 10, or 25 demonstrations per task on LIBERO-90), SMoDP consistently outperformed MoDE and MoDE (Pretrain), despite using fewer total parameters (SMoDP: 401M vs. MoDE: 487M, MoDE Pretrain: 740M).
- SMoDP trained with 25 demonstrations (50% of the full dataset) achieved performance comparable to MoDE (Pretrain) trained with the full 50 demonstrations, indicating superior data efficiency.
- This suggests that semantic skill supervision and skill-aware routing are particularly beneficial in low-data regimes, where purely data-driven routing might overfit task-specific correlations.

**C. Task Transfer and Compositional Generalization**
- **LIBERO-90 → LIBERO-10 (Long-horizon tasks)**: After pre-training on LIBERO-90, SMoDP was fine-tuned on LIBERO-10 with 1, 5, or 10 demonstrations per task, updating only the skill predictor and router while freezing expert parameters.
 - SMoDP achieved success rates of 0.520 (1 demo), 0.765 (5 demos), and 0.840 (10 demos), significantly outperforming MoDE+LoRA (0.245, 0.425, 0.509) and MoDE-scratch (0.085, 0.245, 0.519).
 - SMoDP updated only 13.7M parameters (3.4% of the full model). This demonstrates that source-suite experts capture reusable behaviors, and new tasks can be learned by adapting skill prediction and routing, without full policy retraining.
- **LIBERO-OBJECT/GOAL → LIBERO-GOAL-OOD (Novel skill compositions)**: On LIBERO-GOAL-OOD, designed to test generalization to unseen verb–noun compositions, SMoDP matched the strongest baseline in the 1-demo setting (0.320) and achieved superior results in the 5-demo (0.590 vs. MoDE+LoRA 0.410) and 10-demo (0.660 vs. MoDE+LoRA 0.635) settings.
 - This indicates that explicitly aligning routing with language-grounded skill semantics facilitates adaptation by allowing the skill predictor and router to recombine reusable behaviors for novel task compositions.

**D. Real-world Results**
- On four bimanual ALOHA tasks, SMoDP consistently outperformed MoDE.
- SMoDP achieved an average success rate of 91.25% across the four tasks, compared to MoDE's 47.50%.
- Individual task success rates for SMoDP were: Hang Up Tape (95%), Transfer Tape (90%), Put Cup Sleeve on Cup (80%), and Handoff Cup (100%). MoDE's performance ranged from 75% on Hang Up Tape to 0% on Handoff Cup.
- These improvements are attributed to SMoDP's semantic skill decomposition and skill-aware routing, which enable knowledge sharing across related phases and stable expert specialization, particularly at high-variance transition points.

**E. Ablation Studies**
- Removing InterCL (aligning predicted skill embeddings with language semantics) reduced the LIBERO-90 success rate from 0.970 to 0.958.
- Removing IntraCL (enforcing routing consistency for similar skills) reduced the success rate to 0.957.
- Removing both InterCL and IntraCL further decreased performance to 0.946. This indicates that both dual contrastive alignment components contribute complementarily to performance.
- The number of experts per MoE layer was optimized: 4 experts per layer yielded the best performance (0.970), outperforming 3 (0.957), 6 (0.958), or 8 (0.950) experts. This suggests a balance in capacity and specialization.

**F. Qualitative Analysis**
- Visualization of expert activation heatmaps showed that different semantic skills induce distinct activation patterns, and semantically related skills exhibit similar expert usage across MoE layers. This suggests skill-dependent expert specialization beyond simple load balancing.
- Semantic similarity analysis during task execution demonstrated smooth transitions between predicted skills, with dominant similarity aligning with the executing primitive, indicating interpretability.
- Routing probability analysis revealed that similar experts were consistently activated during semantically corresponding phases of different tasks, supporting the concept of reusable motor primitives and skill modularity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research introduces Semantically Structured Mixture-of-Experts Diffusion Policy (SMoDP), representing a development in multi-task robotic manipulation by explicitly integrating language-based skill representations into the policy's routing mechanism.

**Key Contributions and Significance:**

- **Enhanced Parameter and Data Efficiency**: SMoDP demonstrates superior performance on complex multi-task benchmarks (LIBERO-10, LIBERO-90) with significantly improved parameter efficiency compared to existing diffusion and MoE-based baselines. It also exhibits better data utilization, especially in low-data regimes, allowing for effective learning with fewer demonstrations per task. This addresses a critical bottleneck for scaling robotic policies.
- **Interpretable and Structured Expert Specialization**: By grounding expert selection in semantic task structure, SMoDP promotes expert specialization that is aligned with human-interpretable manipulation skills (verb-noun pairs). This improves the interpretability of the policy's internal decision-making, which can be valuable for debugging, analysis, and refinement.
- **Compositional Transfer to Novel Tasks**: The framework enables effective compositional transfer, allowing the policy to adapt to novel tasks by reusing frozen expert weights and fine-tuning only a small subset of parameters (the skill predictor and router). This mechanism supports generalization to new skill combinations and long-horizon tasks, signifying a step towards more flexible and adaptable robotic systems.
- **Automated Skill Abstraction**: The development of an offline VLM-aided skill abstraction module is a practical contribution, eliminating the need for expensive manual skill annotations or inference-time VLM calls. This automation reduces the overhead associated with preparing multi-task robotic datasets.
- **Robust Routing through Dual Contrastive Alignment**: The proposed dual contrastive learning strategy (Inter-Modal and Intra-Modal) ensures that the learned skill embeddings are semantically meaningful and that the expert routing consistently reflects these semantics. This robust alignment contributes to the stability and reliability of the policy.
- **Validation in Real-world Settings**: The empirical validation on a real bimanual ALOHA system, where SMoDP demonstrated substantial performance improvements over baselines, indicates its practical applicability and robustness to the complexities of real-world robotic environments.

**Potential Impact:**

The methodology could impact the development of more capable and adaptable robotic systems, especially in scenarios requiring multi-task generalization and continuous learning. By fostering more efficient knowledge transfer and reducing the need for extensive task-specific data or massive models, SMoDP could accelerate the deployment of intelligent robots in diverse applications. The emphasis on interpretable skill-based routing could also lead to more transparent and explainable AI in robotics.

**Discussed Limitations:**

- **Coarse-grained Semantics**: The verb-noun skill abstraction, while structured, may oversimplify fine-grained control dynamics (e.g., variations in speed or force), limiting the model’s ability to adapt execution styles within the same high-level skill.
- **VLM-dependent Annotations**: The quality of offline skill annotations is contingent on the VLM's zero-shot reasoning capabilities. Potential issues like coarse segmentation, over-granularity, motion blur, or occlusion could introduce noisy supervision, affecting expert specialization.
- **Long-tail Skill Distribution**: Robotic datasets often exhibit long-tail distributions, where common primitives are over-represented. This imbalance could bias the router towards frequent skills, potentially reducing expert specialization for rare but critical actions.

These limitations suggest future research directions, such as incorporating more nuanced skill representations, improving VLM robustness for segmentation, and developing techniques to handle imbalanced skill distributions.
