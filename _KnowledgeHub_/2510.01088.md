# Safety Instincts: LLMs Learn to Trust Their Internal Compass for Self-Defense

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper introduces Safety Instincts Reinforcement Learning (SIRL), a self-alignment method that leverages the intrinsic confidence of large language models (LLMs) to enhance their safety. It demonstrates that LLMs achieve over 98% defense success rates against diverse jailbreak attacks across various models, while preserving or improving general capabilities, by using response entropy as an internal reward signal for self-training.

## Problem

- Ensuring LLM safety against "jailbreak" attacks is urgent, but current alignment methods rely heavily on external supervision (human annotations, reward models).
- External supervision presents scalability challenges, potential biases, and difficulties in generalizing against constantly evolving, novel jailbreak techniques.
- The inherent ambiguity of "safe" behavior makes it challenging to obtain consistent and reliable training signals for LLM safety.

## Method

- The work identifies an intrinsic "==entropy gap==" in aligned LLMs, where safe refusals are generated with significantly lower per-token entropy (higher confidence) than harmful outputs.
- **Safety Instincts** Reinforcement Learning (SIRL) is proposed, which uses the negative average per-token entropy of a response as a self-generated internal reward signal.
- The model is optimized using a PPO-like objective with group relative comparison and a KL divergence regularization term to balance safety enhancement with the preservation of general capabilities.

## Results

- SIRL achieved average Defense Success Rates exceeding **98%** across Llama and Qwen models against 20 diverse jailbreak attacks, including strong robustness against adaptive attacks.
- The method improved safety more than six-fold compared to baseline models, while preserving or slightly improving performance on mathematical reasoning, code generation, and conversational benchmarks.
- SIRL accomplished these safety enhancements using only 15,000 unlabeled prompts, demonstrating considerable data efficiency compared to supervised alignment techniques.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Aligned LLMs possess inherent safety beliefs, consistently demonstrating higher confidence (lower entropy) when refusing harmful requests compared to generating harmful content.
> - This intrinsic confidence signal can be effectively leveraged as an autonomous, self-generated reward for reinforcement learning, reducing reliance on external human feedback or reward models.
> - KL divergence regularization is critical for preventing models from becoming overly conservative and refusing benign requests, maintaining utility while bolstering safety.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The widespread deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) has amplified the urgency of ensuring their safety, particularly against "jailbreak" attacks designed to elicit harmful or unethical responses. The broader research landscape for LLM safety is characterized by a two-pronged challenge: the ever-evolving nature of jailbreak techniques and the fundamental difficulty in obtaining reliable and scalable reward signals for safety training.

Existing safety alignment methods broadly fall into several categories:
- **External Safeguards:** Techniques like perplexity filtering and content detection that act as post-hoc filters or monitoring systems.
- **Prompt-level Interventions:** Methods like prompt engineering, self-reminders, or in-context learning (ICL) that modify or augment user inputs to steer models towards safe behavior.
- **Internal Approaches (Fine-tuning and Representation Engineering):** This includes Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT), Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), and Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which aim to directly embed safety principles into the model's parameters. Representation engineering, another internal approach, focuses on manipulating internal model states to influence safety.

However, all these methods, particularly those involving fine-tuning, face a common limitation: they rely heavily on *external* supervision. SFT requires extensive human annotation of safe responses; DPO and RLHF depend on human-generated preference pairs or a separately trained reward model, respectively. This reliance introduces significant scalability challenges, potential biases from human annotators, and a struggle to generalize against novel, adaptive jailbreak methods that constantly evolve. The difficulty of obtaining reliable "safety validators" for effective training signals is a central bottleneck in current LLM alignment efforts.

This paper positions itself within the emerging paradigm of **self-alignment** and **superalignment**, which explore methods for autonomous model improvement without external feedback. While prior work in self-alignment (e.g., SPIN, R-Zero, Constitutional AI) still requires designed principles or model-generated preferences, and others use intrinsic rewards in domains with clear correctness (like math or coding), this work proposes a novel application of *intrinsic confidence signals* for safety. It specifically leverages the discovery that *aligned LLMs already possess intrinsic safety beliefs*, manifesting as a measurable "entropy gap" between safe and unsafe responses.

Therefore, this research represents a significant departure from prevailing extrinsic supervision methods. Instead of imposing external rules or rewards, it harnesses an **internal compass** within the LLM itself, teaching models to trust their own inherent confidence in safe behavior. This moves the field towards more autonomous and scalable AI safety mechanisms, addressing the critical need for robust defenses that do not rely on ever-increasing human oversight as models grow in complexity and capability. By identifying and amplifying an existing, intrinsic safety signal, the work aims to enable models to strengthen their defenses "from within," a crucial step for achieving robust, self-reliant AI safety.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a more autonomous and scalable method for enhancing Large Language Model (LLM) safety against jailbreak attacks, moving away from the limitations of current external supervision-dependent alignment techniques.

The core motivation stems from two critical observations and challenges in the existing LLM safety landscape:
1. **The Absence of Universal Safety Standards and Reliable Content Validators:** Unlike many well-defined tasks with clear metrics, determining what constitutes "safe" behavior for an LLM is inherently ambiguous and context-dependent. This makes it difficult to obtain consistent and reliable training signals for safety. Current methods often require expensive human annotation, carefully crafted reward models, or predefined rules, all of which struggle to scale and generalize to the rapidly evolving landscape of jailbreak attacks.
2. **The Untapped Potential of Internal Model Signals:** Inspired by advances in self-alignment and superalignment, the authors hypothesize that aligned LLMs might possess intrinsic safety beliefs that could be leveraged for self-improvement, reducing the reliance on external feedback.

This motivation led to a key empirical discovery: **aligned models already possess robust internal safety beliefs, which manifest as a distinct "entropy gap."** Specifically, models consistently produce high-confidence (low-entropy) refusals to harmful requests, while exhibiting high-entropy, more uncertain behavior when generating potentially dangerous content. This phenomenon suggests that models intrinsically "know" when they should refuse, even if their outputs might sometimes deviate.

Therefore, the key objectives are:
- To empirically demonstrate and quantify this "entropy gap" as a reliable intrinsic safety signal across diverse LLMs and attack types.
- To propose and validate a novel self-alignment method, **Safety Instincts Reinforcement Learning (SIRL)**, that transforms this internal confidence (response entropy) into a self-generated reward signal.
- To demonstrate that SIRL can significantly enhance LLM safety against a wide array of jailbreak attacks (static and adaptive) without relying on external validators or human annotations.
- To ensure that this safety enhancement preserves, and ideally improves, the model's general capabilities (e.g., reasoning, coding, conversation) rather than degrading them through over-refusal or narrowed utility.
- Ultimately, to pave the way for more autonomous, robust, and scalable AI safety mechanisms that can function effectively without extensive human oversight.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the methodology revolves around identifying and leveraging the intrinsic confidence (quantified by entropy) of aligned LLMs for self-guided safety improvement. The proposed method is **Safety Instincts Reinforcement Learning (SIRL)**.

#### 3.1. Empirical Discovery: Entropy as an Intrinsic Safety Signal

The foundational step was an empirical analysis to discover the "confidence gap."
- **Response Entropy Measurement:** For a given query and its generated response, the average per-token entropy ($\overline{H}(o|q)$) is computed. Lower entropy indicates higher confidence in token generation.
- **Observation of Entropy Gap:** The analysis revealed that safe refusals consistently exhibit significantly lower entropy compared to harmful outputs. This "entropy gap" was quantified across various aligned models (Llama and Qwen families) and jailbreak attack types, demonstrating its robustness. Statistical tests (Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Mann–Whitney U test) confirmed the significance of this difference.
- **Token-Level Analysis:** Further investigation at the token level showed that "Risk Articulation" tokens (e.g., "I cannot," "harmful") had the lowest entropy, "General" tokens had moderate entropy, and "Compliance Signals" (e.g., "Sure," "Here are the steps") had the highest entropy. This indicated that models have internalized confident safety patterns but remain uncertain when complying with harmful requests.
- **Theoretical Intuition:** The authors hypothesize that this entropy-safety connection arises because safety training creates concentrated, low-entropy distributions over refusal patterns for harmful queries, while jailbreaks induce conflict, resulting in high-entropy, unfamiliar harmful outputs.

#### 3.2. Safety Instincts Reinforcement Learning (SIRL)

Based on this discovery, SIRL is designed as a self-alignment framework that transforms intrinsic confidence into an internal reward signal.
- **Entropy-Based Reward Formulation:** The reward for a generated response $o_i$ to a query $q$ is defined as the negative average per-token entropy: $r_i = - \overline{H}(o_i|q)$. This formulation naturally assigns higher rewards to responses generated with higher confidence (lower entropy). Crucially, this reward signal is entirely *internal* to the model, eliminating the need for external human judgments or separate reward models.
- **Policy Optimization with Group Relative Comparison:** To handle the variance in entropy measurements, SIRL employs a group relative policy optimization approach.
 - For each prompt, a group of $G$ responses is sampled from the current policy.
 - Within this group, relative advantages ($\widehat{A}_i$) are computed by normalizing each response's reward against the mean and standard deviation of rewards within that group. This helps in identifying responses that are confidently generated (typically safe refusals) within the context of other generated options.
 - The model then optimizes its policy using a Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)-like objective. This objective incorporates importance sampling to handle off-policy learning, a clipping mechanism to prevent overly aggressive policy updates, and a KL divergence regularization term.
 - The **KL divergence term ($\beta \cdot D_{KL}[\pi_\theta \| \pi_{ref}]$)** is vital. It constrains the updated policy ($\pi_\theta$) from deviating too far from the initial reference policy ($\pi_{ref}$). This regularization is critical for preserving the model's general capabilities while enhancing safety, preventing it from becoming overly conservative or refusing benign requests.
- **Self-Reinforcing Dynamics:** SIRL creates a virtuous cycle: as the model learns to favor its low-entropy (confident) responses, and since these are predominantly safe refusals, these safety patterns become even more ingrained and confident, providing progressively stronger internal training signals. This amplifies the model's existing safety knowledge without external intervention.

#### 3.3. Experimental Setup and Evaluation

- **Models:** Evaluated on instruction-tuned Llama (3.1-8B, 3.2-3B) and Qwen (2.5-3B, 2.5-7B) models.
- **Training Data:** Used 15,000 unlabeled prompts from PKU-SafeRLHF, highlighting data efficiency and independence from annotations.
- **Safety Evaluation:** Assessed using Defense Success Rate (DSR) against 20 diverse jailbreak attacks from JailbreakBench (covering template, optimization, and semantic manipulations), including robustness against adaptive attacks (GCG, PAIR, RandomSearch). Evaluation combined rule-based detection and LLM-as-Judge (GPT-4o, Llama-3.3-70B, Qwen2.5-72B).
- **Capability Evaluation:** Assessed preservation of general capabilities on benchmarks including mathematics (MATH-500, AMC), coding (HumanEval, LiveCodeBench), and general knowledge/conversation (BBH, AlpacaEval).
- **Ablation Studies:** Explored alternative reward formulations (maximizing entropy, random rewards, minimizing perplexity) and the impact of the KL divergence coefficient ($\beta$) to understand the importance of confidence-based signals and regularization.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several significant findings demonstrating the effectiveness and advantages of the SIRL approach.

#### 4.1. Empirical Discovery of the Entropy-Safety Signal

- **Consistent Entropy Gap:** A fundamental discovery was that aligned LLMs consistently exhibit a significant entropy gap between safe and harmful responses. Across four diverse models, safe refusals had average per-token entropy 0.365 to 0.684 nats lower than harmful outputs (Table 1). This indicates that models are more confident when refusing harmful requests.
- **Token-Level Confidence:** Token-level analysis further refined this, showing that "Risk Articulation" tokens (e.g., "I cannot") had the lowest entropy, while "Compliance Signals" (indicating willingness to help with harmful requests) had the highest entropy (Figure 2). This confirmed that safety knowledge is deeply encoded in the model's confidence structure.

#### 4.2. Superior Safety Enhancement with Capability Preservation

- **High Defense Success Rates (DSRs):** SIRL achieved average DSRs consistently exceeding 98% across all evaluated models against 20 diverse jailbreak techniques (Table 2). This represents a more than 6-fold reduction in vulnerability compared to baseline models. For instance, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct saw its DSR improve from 84.3% to 99.1%.
- **Data Efficiency:** SIRL achieved these results using only 15,000 unlabeled prompts, significantly outperforming resource-intensive supervised methods like SFT, DPO, and RLHF, which require extensive human annotations or reward models.
- **Preservation/Improvement of General Capabilities:** Critically, SIRL not only enhanced safety but also preserved or even improved performance across various capability benchmarks. Mathematical reasoning (MATH-500, AMC), code generation (HumanEval, LiveCodeBench), general knowledge (BBH), and conversational abilities (AlpacaEval) all maintained or saw slight improvements (Table 2). In contrast, SFT often led to capability degradation, and DPO showed inconsistent effects.

#### 4.3. Robustness Against Diverse and Adaptive Attacks

- **Broad Attack Coverage:** SIRL demonstrated remarkable consistency in defense across all 20 tested jailbreak methods, ranging from static template-based attacks (e.g., AIM, BETTER_DAN) to sophisticated optimization-based techniques (e.g., GCG, AutoDAN) and semantic manipulations (e.g., PastTense, DeepInception). SIRL achieved $\ge 99\%$ DSR on 19 out of 20 attack types for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (Figure 3).
- **Resilience to Adaptive Attacks:** Against highly challenging adaptive attacks like GCG, PAIR, and RandomSearch, SIRL maintained consistently high DSRs. It achieved perfect defense (100%) against GCG on Llama models and 94-97% on Qwen models. For PAIR's semantic attacks, SIRL achieved 89-100% DSR. Even against RandomSearch, where baseline methods showed extreme variability (16-100% DSR), SIRL provided more reliable defense (71-100% DSR) (Figure 4).

#### 4.4. Ablation Studies and Training Dynamics

- **Importance of Confidence-Based Rewards:** Ablation studies confirmed that minimizing entropy (SIRL) is crucial. Maximizing entropy (neg-SIRL) degraded both safety and capabilities, while random rewards had negligible impact. Minimizing perplexity also showed strong safety improvements, highlighting the efficacy of various confidence-based signals, though entropy was chosen for its interpretability (Table 3).
- **KL Regularization:** The KL divergence coefficient ($\beta$) was found to be critical for balancing safety and capability. Small $\beta$ led to high safety but degraded capabilities (overfitting to refusals), while large $\beta$ limited safety improvements. An optimal range for $\beta$ was identified, allowing for robust safety without significant capability trade-offs (Figure 5).
- **Training Dynamics:** SIRL exhibited rapid, monotonic entropy reduction strongly correlated with DSR improvements, validating entropy as a reliable intrinsic safety signal. In contrast, RLHF showed slower, more variable entropy reduction, optimizing for external rewards. While SIRL could become conservative and over-refuse benign questions at later stages of training, early stopping or appropriate KL regularization effectively managed this trade-off (Figure 6, Figure 11).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work on "Safety Instincts" represents a significant advancement in the field of LLM safety and has several profound implications and potential impacts:

#### 5.1. Paradigm Shift in AI Safety Alignment

The most significant impact is the demonstration of a **paradigm shift** from extrinsic (external supervision, human feedback, reward models) to intrinsic (self-generated, confidence-based) safety alignment. By revealing that aligned LLMs possess inherent "safety instincts" quantifiable through response entropy, the paper offers a fundamentally new perspective: rather than *imposing* safety, we can *amplify* existing internal safety knowledge. This moves AI safety closer to the concept of self-reliant and autonomous defense.

#### 5.2. Scalability and Resource Efficiency

One of the most pressing challenges in LLM alignment is its dependence on vast, expensive human annotations or complex reward model training. SIRL effectively **eliminates this dependence** by leveraging readily available unlabeled prompts and internal model signals. This makes safety alignment significantly more scalable, cost-effective, and practical for future, even larger, LLMs where human oversight at the necessary scale might become intractable. This efficiency is a critical step towards deploying safer AI systems in real-world scenarios without proportional increases in human resources.

#### 5.3. Enhanced Robustness Against Evolving Threats

Traditional alignment methods often struggle to generalize against novel or adaptive jailbreak attacks, as they might overfit to specific attack patterns seen during training. By teaching models to trust their underlying confidence in safe refusals, SIRL fosters a more **fundamental and generalized safety reasoning**. Its effectiveness against diverse and adaptive jailbreak techniques, which were not explicitly encountered during training, suggests that it reinforces core safety principles rather than surface-level defenses, leading to more robust and future-proof AI systems.

#### 5.4. Preservation and Potential Enhancement of General Capabilities

A common concern with safety enhancements is the potential degradation of the model's utility or general capabilities (e.g., over-refusal of benign requests). SIRL's ability to achieve high safety while **preserving or even improving performance** on various reasoning, coding, and conversational benchmarks is a crucial practical advantage. This makes SIRL a more attractive solution for real-world deployment where both safety and utility are paramount. The findings suggest that confident, safe responses are often also more coherent and high-quality, creating a synergistic effect.

#### 5.5. Towards Autonomous and Self-Reliant AI Systems

This research lays groundwork for truly autonomous AI safety mechanisms. As models become more capable and complex, direct human supervision will become increasingly difficult. By demonstrating that models can learn to strengthen their defenses from within, guided by their own "internal compass," SIRL offers a pathway toward **self-defending AI systems**. This aligns with the long-term goals of superalignment, where AI systems can maintain their alignment and safety without constant external intervention.

#### 5.6. Future Research Directions

The paper opens several avenues for future work, including:
- Exploring multi-objective formulations to explicitly balance safety and utility, potentially integrating other intrinsic signals.
- Investigating the application of entropy-based intrinsic rewards beyond jailbreak defense to other aspects of AI alignment.
- Developing more comprehensive theoretical frameworks to deepen the understanding of confidence-safety correlations in LLMs.

In conclusion, "Safety Instincts" offers a transformative approach to LLM safety by tapping into the models' inherent confidence. Its implications for scalability, robustness, and the development of truly autonomous and self-reliant AI systems are profound, marking a significant step forward in the quest for safe and aligned artificial intelligence.
