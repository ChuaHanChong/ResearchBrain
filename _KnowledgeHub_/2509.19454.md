# ROPA: Synthetic Robot Pose Generation for RGB-D Bimanual Data Augmentation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the University of Southern California developed ROPA, an offline data augmentation method that generates synthetic robot poses and corresponding action labels for RGB-D bimanual imitation learning. This approach consistently improved policy success rates and robustness across various simulated and real-world tasks, outperforming baseline methods by enabling the synthesis of diverse training data.

## Problem

- Acquiring diverse, action-labeled real-world demonstration data for bimanual robotic manipulation is time-consuming and costly.
- Existing data augmentation methods often lack the ability to generate both novel robot poses and corresponding accurate action labels, especially for bimanual systems.
- Challenges include maintaining visual consistency across two robot arms, ensuring physically plausible action labels, and integrating depth information for bimanual RGB-D setups.

## Method

- A three-stage method, **ROPA**, utilizes a ==conditional diffusion model (Stable Diffusion with ControlNet)== adapted for ==robot pose-guided image synthesis==.
- A dedicated ==skeleton pose generator== renders explicit skeletal representations with visual cues for joint orientations, enabling precise conditioning for the diffusion model and deriving target joint positions via ==inverse kinematics==.
- An ==action labeling framework== generates physically consistent joint-space actions for novel poses, using ==constrained optimization== for contact-rich scenarios to ensure feasibility and prevent collisions.

## Results

- **ROPA** consistently outperformed baseline methods in simulation, improving success rates across **five** bimanual tasks in both RGB and RGB-D, with an **80%** success rate on Coordinated Lift Ball using a four-camera setup.
- In real-world experiments, the method enhanced robustness and success rates on tasks such as Lift Ball, Push Block, and Lift Drawer, showing benefits from RGB-D data.
- Ablation studies confirmed that detailed visual skeleton pose representations significantly improve the performance of the diffusion model compared to raw joint positions or less informative visual cues.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Adapting pose-guided image synthesis with diffusion models can effectively generate diverse robot poses and corresponding action labels for bimanual manipulation.
> - Visually rich skeleton representations, rather than raw numerical joint data, serve as more effective conditioning inputs for diffusion models in robot image synthesis.
> - Incorporating constrained optimization during action label generation is crucial for ensuring the physical feasibility and consistency of augmented bimanual robot poses, especially in contact scenarios.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Bimanual manipulation is a field within robotics that addresses tasks requiring the coordinated use of two robotic arms. These tasks are prevalent in daily activities, such as lifting and handling objects, and demand complex coordination, spatial awareness, and fine-grained precision. Imitation learning (IL) has emerged as a strategy for training bimanual manipulation policies, particularly with the development of large demonstration datasets and algorithms like Action Chunking with Transformers (ACT). However, a significant challenge in scaling these approaches is the cost and time involved in collecting sufficiently diverse, precise, and action-labeled real-world demonstration data that covers a broad spectrum of robot poses, contacts, and scene contexts.

Current data augmentation techniques in robotics have primarily focused on single-arm manipulation, eye-in-hand (wrist camera) setups with RGB inputs, or generating novel images without the corresponding action labels required for imitation learning. Augmentation for eye-to-hand (third-person) RGB-D training, especially when generating new action labels, remains an area with limited exploration. Extending existing methods to bimanual scenarios introduces complexities such as maintaining visual consistency across two robot arms, ensuring the correctness of augmented action labels, and managing the higher degrees of freedom inherent in bimanual systems.

Relevant prior work in data augmentation for imitation learning includes simple visual transformations (e.g., crops, jitters), structured scene augmentations (e.g., changing backgrounds or objects while keeping actions fixed), and methods leveraging 3D Gaussian Splatting, which often require high-fidelity scene reconstruction. Other approaches focus on state-based inputs or wrist-camera observations. Diffusion-based generative models, such as VISTA and RoVi-Aug, have been used for novel-view synthesis and cross-embodiment transfer. However, these methods typically do not generate new action labels alongside augmented visual observations, nor have they been extensively evaluated on bimanual systems. RoVi-Aug, for instance, generates multiple views of the same robot pose, while the capability to synthesize *novel robot poses* with corresponding actions for bimanual tasks using diffusion models is a less explored area. Work on depth augmentation for robotics is also limited, often restricted to standard image processing techniques without generating new robot poses or actions.

The current research positions itself by addressing these specific gaps. It proposes an offline data augmentation method designed for third-person RGB-D bimanual imitation learning. The core contribution lies in adapting pose-guided image synthesis techniques, originally developed for human pose generation, to synthesize novel robot poses and their corresponding action labels. This approach aims to enhance the diversity and robustness of training data for bimanual manipulation policies, thereby contributing to the scalability and generalization capabilities of learning-based robotic systems without requiring additional physical data collection.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the inherent difficulties and resource intensiveness associated with training robust bimanual manipulation policies through imitation learning. Bimanual tasks, while crucial for many real-world applications, demand comprehensive demonstration datasets that broadly cover a variety of robot poses, contact interactions, and environmental contexts. The process of collecting such diverse and precise real-world demonstrations is costly, time-consuming, and presents a significant bottleneck to the scalability of imitation learning for bimanual systems.

Existing data augmentation techniques have not adequately addressed the specific challenges of bimanual manipulation, particularly in third-person camera setups that provide an "eye-to-hand" view of both arms and the surrounding scene. Challenges include:
- **Visual Consistency**: Ensuring that synthesized images maintain visual coherence across two robot arms and the entire scene.
- **Action Label Correctness**: Guaranteeing that augmented visual observations are paired with physically plausible and executable action labels.
- **High Degrees-of-Freedom (DOF)**: Effectively managing the increased complexity arising from the larger number of joints and coordinated movements in bimanual systems.
- **Depth Information**: Integrating and augmenting depth data consistently with RGB images, which is critical for accurate 3D spatial understanding in manipulation tasks.

Against this background, the key objectives of the ROPA research are:
1. **Develop a novel offline data augmentation method**: To create a system capable of synthetically expanding demonstration datasets for vision-based imitation learning in bimanual manipulation. This method, termed ROPA (Synthetic Robot Pose Generation for RGB-D Bimanual Data Augmentation), focuses on third-person RGB and RGB-D observations.
2. **Synthesize novel robot poses**: To generate realistic and consistent third-person RGB and RGB-D images that depict robots in diverse and new joint configurations, thereby broadening the coverage of the training data.
3. **Generate corresponding joint-space action labels**: To simultaneously produce accurate and physically consistent action labels (target joint positions) for each synthesized image. This ensures that policies trained on augmented data can be executed on real hardware.
4. **Enforce physical consistency**: To integrate constrained optimization techniques that ensure the feasibility of generated poses and actions, particularly in bimanual scenarios where gripper-to-object contact constraints are critical to prevent collisions or physically impossible configurations.
5. **Leverage pose-guided image synthesis**: To adapt and fine-tune diffusion models, specifically Stable Diffusion with ControlNet, by conditioning them on robot joint configurations (represented as skeleton poses). This aims to harness the generative capabilities of these models for creating visually plausible robot images.
6. **Evaluate performance across modalities and environments**: To rigorously test the effectiveness of ROPA on a range of bimanual manipulation tasks in both simulated and real-world settings, assessing its ability to improve the success rates and robustness of imitation learning policies trained with augmented data, including RGB-only and RGB-D modalities, and multi-camera setups.

By achieving these objectives, ROPA aims to mitigate the data scarcity problem in bimanual robotics, enhance policy generalization, and provide a pathway for more scalable and robust robot learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

ROPA employs a three-stage process to generate augmented data for bimanual imitation learning, leveraging a conditional diffusion model for image synthesis and incorporating mechanisms for action labeling and physical consistency.

**A. Pose Guided Robot Image Synthesis (PGRIS)**
Inspired by Pose Guided Person Image Synthesis, ROPA frames the problem as Pose Guided Robot Image Synthesis (PGRIS). Given a source camera image ($I_{s_t}$), a target skeleton pose image ($I_{p_t}$), and a language goal ($g$), PGRIS aims to learn a mapping to produce a target camera image ($I_{d_t}$).
The core of PGRIS is a Stable Diffusion 2.1 model fine-tuned with ControlNet. This architecture utilizes:
- **Frozen Stable Diffusion U-Net**: Processes noisy latent representations and text embeddings.
- **Trainable ControlNet Encoder Stream**: A copy of the Stable Diffusion U-Net that processes conditioning inputs.
The source image ($I_{s_t}$) and target pose image ($I_{p_t}$) are encoded into latent representations. The language goal ($g$) is embedded via a text encoder. ControlNet processes these conditioning inputs and injects pose-aware feature maps into the frozen U-Net denoiser using zero-initialized convolution layers. These zero-convolutions are designed to guide the denoising process towards the desired robot configuration while preventing detrimental noise during early training. The model is trained to predict the noise term by minimizing an L2 loss. During inference, the model iteratively denoises a random Gaussian latent, guided by ControlNet, to generate the final target image. This trained model is then used with sampled camera perturbations to synthesize novel views from the original dataset.

**B. Skeleton Pose Generator**
To provide the necessary conditioning for the diffusion model, a scalable method for rendering robot skeleton poses is developed. This generator creates a skeletal representation of each robot arm's kinematic chain using cylinders for bone segments and spheres for joint connectors. This explicit geometric structure is intended to help the diffusion model understand spatial relationships and maintain kinematic consistency. Striped textures, adopted from GENIMA, are applied to the spheres to provide visual cues for learning joint orientations and rotational states, without requiring explicit 3D information.
The process involves:
1. **Target Joint Position Derivation**: An end-effector transformation ($\Delta\rho$) is applied to the source end-effector poses. Inverse kinematics (IK) are then solved to obtain the target joint positions.
2. **Rendering**: PyRender, a Python-based 3D rendering library, is used to configure virtual arms to these target joint positions. A virtual camera in PyRender is set to match the intrinsic and extrinsic parameters of the original source camera, ensuring geometric consistency between the rendered skeleton and the actual camera view. URDF models of both robot arms are loaded and positioned to match the source setup. The skeleton pose image ($I_{p_t}$) is then captured.

**C. Action Labeling and Dataset Construction**
Beyond image synthesis, ROPA focuses on generating action labels that correspond to the novel robot poses while maintaining physical feasibility. This is particularly crucial in bimanual tasks to prevent collisions or uncoordinated movements.
1. **Contact Detection**: A force-based approach, relying on external torque residuals, is used to detect contact. This method models external torque as the difference between motor torque and a predicted model torque, detecting contact when actual torque exceeds dynamic thresholds. This approach is preferred over vision-based methods due to its robustness to occlusions in third-person views and lower computational overhead.
2. **Pose Perturbation Strategy**:
 - **Contactless States**: For states without detected contact, uniform sampling is used to generate end-effector transformations ($\Delta\rho$).
 - **Contact-Rich States**: For states with detected contact, a constrained optimization framework (based on Dual Annealing) is employed. This ensures kinematically feasible poses and preserves contact relationships. The optimization variable consists of translation coordinates, and the objective function incorporates penalties for undesirable conditions such as overly small perturbations, end-effectors too close to the table, end-effectors too close to each other, and inverse kinematics failures. The Levenberg-Marquardt inverse kinematics solver validates kinematic feasibility.
3. **Action Label Generation**: The computed perturbations ($\Delta\rho$) are used to derive perturbed joint-space actions ($\tilde{a}_t$) from the original actions ($a_t$). These actions correspond to the joint positions required to achieve the transformed end-effector poses.
4. **Dataset Construction**: The original dataset is duplicated. Augmented out-of-distribution states ($I_{d_t}$) and their corresponding perturbed action labels ($\tilde{a}_t$) replace original states every `k` timesteps (set to k=8). This intermittent replacement strategy aims to mitigate compounding errors often seen in behavior cloning policies. The original and augmented datasets are then combined for policy training.

**D. Multi-Modal and Multi-View Extensions**
- **RGB-D Data Augmentation**: For depth image augmentation, the PGRIS architecture is adapted. Instead of an RGB source image, a depth colormap is input as the source. To ensure geometric consistency, ControlNet is conditioned on *both* the corresponding RGB target image and the target pose image. This dual conditioning, achieved by concatenating the two RGB images into a 6-channel input, helps ensure generated depth images maintain geometric correspondence with their RGB counterparts.
- **Multi-View Support**: ROPA is extended to support multi-camera setups (up to four cameras). This is achieved by tiling observations from multiple cameras into a single composite image, which aims to improve spatial robustness and mitigate occlusions.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates ROPA's performance through extensive experiments in both simulated and real-world environments, comparing it against established baselines and conducting ablation studies.

**Simulation Experiments:**
Five bimanual manipulation tasks from the PerAct2 benchmark were used: Coordinated Lift Ball (CLB), Coordinated Lift Tray (CLT), Coordinated Push Box (CPB), Bimanual Straighten Rope Easy (BSR), and Coordinated Put Item in Drawer (CPID). Baselines included ACT (without augmentation), ACT with additional expert data (100 extra demonstrations), and Fine-tuned VISTA (a diffusion-based novel view synthesis method).
- **Overall Performance (Table I)**: ROPA consistently outperformed all baselines across all five tasks in both RGB and RGB-D modalities.
 - For **RGB training**, ROPA achieved success rates such as 68.0% for CLB, 30.7% for CLT, and 62.7% for CPB, demonstrating significant improvements over ACT (w/o augmentation) (e.g., 41.3% for CLB, 10.7% for CLT) and Fine-tuned VISTA. VISTA, in particular, struggled with precision-demanding tasks (e.g., CLT at 1.6%).
 - For **RGB-D training**, ROPA continued to show strong performance, with success rates of 72.0% for CLB, 35.0% for CLT, and 61.3% for CPB. Incorporating depth information resulted in slight but measurable performance boosts across tasks for ROPA and the ACT baseline compared to RGB-only training.
- **Data Scaling vs. Augmentation**: The "ACT (more data)" baseline, which used 100 additional expert demonstrations, did not consistently surpass ACT without augmentation, and in some cases, performed worse (e.g., CPID 17.3% without augmentation vs. 10.3% with more data). This suggests that simply increasing data volume without diversifying its coverage of out-of-distribution states may not yield performance improvements, thus highlighting the utility of ROPA's augmentation approach.
- **Multi-Camera Results (Table II)**: On the CLB task, ROPA with a four-camera setup achieved an 80.0% success rate, outperforming ACT with four cameras (72.0%). Both methods benefited from multi-camera inputs compared to single-camera setups.
- **Generalization Experiments (Table III)**: ROPA's diffusion model, when trained on different PerAct2 tasks and tested on the unseen CLB task, showed generalization capabilities. Direct fine-tuning on 100 CLB demonstrations yielded the best performance (68.0%), but zero-shot (50.7%) and few-shot (10 demos, 58.3%) approaches also achieved reasonable results when target task data was limited.
- **Ablation Studies (Table IV)**:
 - **Skeleton Pose vs. Raw Joint Positions**: Using the rendered Skeleton Pose as input to ControlNet (68.0%) significantly outperformed using raw 14-dimensional joint position vectors (46.7%). This indicates that the visual, spatially rich skeleton representation provides more effective conditioning for the diffusion model than numerical joint data.
 - **Pose Variation Impact**: ROPA's custom skeleton pose with striped textures (68.0%) yielded the best performance. An OpenPose-inspired skeleton also performed well (62.6%), while an all-white skeleton led to substantial performance degradation (32.0%) due to insufficient visual cues for the diffusion model. This confirmed the importance of detailed visual information in the skeleton representation for guiding image generation.

**Real-World Experiments:**
Experiments were conducted using two UR5 robots with Robotiq 2F-85 grippers and an Intel RealSense D415 RGB-D camera on three bimanual tasks: Lift Ball, Push Block, and Lift Drawer. 30 real demonstrations were collected per task.
- **Overall Performance (Table V)**: ROPA consistently demonstrated improved robustness across all real-world tasks compared to baselines.
 - For **RGB training**, ROPA achieved 17/20 successful trials for Lift Ball, 10/20 for Lift Drawer, and 20/20 for Push Block. These figures generally surpassed ACT (w/o augmentation) (13/20, 4/20, 14/20) and Fine-tuned VISTA (11/20, 1/20, 18/20).
 - For **RGB-D training**, ROPA showed further improvements, achieving 19/20 for Lift Ball, 13/20 for Lift Drawer, and 20/20 for Push Block, reinforcing the benefits of depth information in real-world scenarios.
- **Qualitative Observations**: ROPA enabled more reliable and stable grasping (Lift Ball), improved precision and fine motor control for tasks like grasping drawers (Lift Drawer), and demonstrated better robustness in handling objects at greater distances or challenging orientations (Push Block), reducing instances where baselines froze or failed to initiate contact.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

ROPA addresses a fundamental challenge in robot learning: the prohibitive cost and time associated with collecting diverse and precise real-world demonstration data for complex bimanual manipulation tasks. By proposing an offline data augmentation method that synthesizes novel robot poses alongside corresponding, physically consistent action labels for third-person RGB-D bimanual scenarios, ROPA offers a strategy to mitigate this data scarcity bottleneck.

The primary significance of this work lies in its potential to enhance the scalability and robustness of imitation learning for bimanual robots. The ability to synthetically expand training datasets without requiring additional physical data collection can accelerate the development and deployment of more general and capable robotic policies. The experimental results, demonstrating consistent performance improvements over baselines in both simulation (across 2625 trials) and real-world settings (across 300 trials), suggest that ROPA can contribute to policies that are more resilient to out-of-distribution states and possess improved error recovery capabilities.

ROPA's technical contributions include adapting pose-guided image synthesis techniques from human pose generation to robots, specifically for generating action-consistent image-action pairs. The method's extensions to include depth image synthesis, ensuring geometric consistency with RGB images and augmented joint positions, are also notable for tasks where 3D spatial understanding is critical. Furthermore, the integration of constrained optimization for action labeling in contact-rich bimanual scenarios is designed to ensure the physical feasibility and safety of the generated robot poses and actions.

The demonstrated generalization capabilities of the underlying diffusion model to unseen tasks, even with limited target data, suggest its potential for transfer learning in various robotic applications. The ablation studies underscore the importance of informative skeletal representations for guiding the diffusion model, providing insights for future research in visual conditioning.

While the paper acknowledges limitations, such as reliance on robot observability and potential for generated image artifacts, the consistent improvements across a range of tasks indicate a step towards more practical and scalable robot learning paradigms. The project is positioned to inspire further research into advanced data augmentation techniques, improved policy learning architectures, and the broader application of pre-trained diffusion models in the field of robotics. This could lead to bimanual robotic systems capable of performing a wider array of tasks with increased autonomy and reliability.
