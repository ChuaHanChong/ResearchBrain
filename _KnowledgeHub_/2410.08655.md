# FRASA: An End-to-End Reinforcement Learning Agent for Fall Recovery and Stand Up of Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> FRASA, developed by the Rhoban Team, introduces an end-to-end Deep Reinforcement Learning agent for humanoid robots that unifies fall recovery and stand-up tasks. This method dramatically reduces training time to minutes and consistently outperforms a world-champion Key Frame Based method, recovering from various fall postures up to 50% faster.

## Problem

- Humanoid robots are highly unstable, prone to falls, and susceptible to damage, requiring robust recovery mechanisms.
- Existing fall prevention (e.g., MPC, DRL push recovery) methods do not address recovery *after* a fall, and traditional stand-up routines (KFB) lack versatility and adaptability.
- Current DRL approaches for stand-up often suffer from prohibitively long training times or reliance on expert-defined key frames.

## Method

- A DRL agent, FRASA, trained with the CrossQ algorithm and Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), maps raw sensor inputs directly to motor commands for unified fall recovery and stand-up.
- The approach leverages the robot's bilateral symmetry by controlling only 5 symmetric degrees of freedom, significantly accelerating the learning process.
- Extensive domain randomization (e.g., friction, mass, actuator parameters) and modeling of real-robot sensor delays ensure robust sim-to-real transfer.

## Results

- FRASA agents achieved full training in an average of 13 to 37 minutes, accumulating 235,000 to 575,000 timesteps, a significant speedup compared to existing DRL methods.
- In stand-up tasks, FRASA recovered from supine positions in 2.678 seconds (53% of KFB time) and prone positions in 2.135 seconds (68% of KFB time).
- For strong disturbances (7.3J), FRASA recovered frontwards falls in 2.44 seconds (42% of KFB time) and backwards falls in 2.26 seconds (51% of KFB time), consistently outperforming the KFB method of the RoboCup 2023 world champion Rhoban Team.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An end-to-end DRL framework can effectively unify fall recovery (creating new contact points during a fall) and stand-up, offering a more adaptive and comprehensive solution.
> - Strategic DRL algorithm choices (CrossQ+SAC) combined with exploiting robot symmetry enable complex humanoid behaviors to be learned in minutes, a substantial reduction from previous training times.
> - Thorough domain randomization, including real-world communication delays and mechanical variations, is critical for achieving successful and robust policy transfer from simulation to a physical robot.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robust and autonomous humanoid robots capable of stable locomotion and recovery from disturbances is a long-standing and critical challenge in robotics. Humanoid robots, with their inherently unstable standing poses due to limited ground contact points and a high Center of Mass (CoM), are highly susceptible to falls, which can lead to significant damage.

Historically, various approaches have been employed to address stability and recovery:

- **Fall Prevention (Push Recovery):**
 - **Model Predictive Control (MPC):** This is a widely adopted method that plans CoM trajectories to maintain stability, adjusting based on sensor feedback. While effective, MPC is constrained by strong linearity assumptions, arbitrary adjustment choices, and significant computational complexity. Solutions often involve modifying the center of pressure, generating angular momentum, or taking steps, guided by concepts like the capture point.
 - **Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) for Push Recovery:** More recently, DRL agents have emerged to address MPC's drawbacks for push recovery. These methods offer greater adaptability but typically suffer from extensive training times and do not provide solutions for recovery *after* a fall has occurred.

- **Fall Recovery and Stand Up (After a Fall):**
 - **Key Frame Based (KFB) Routines:** These are common in robotics competitions like RoboCup. They involve pre-defined, expert-designed sequences of motor positions (key frames) to bring the robot back to a standing position. While simple to implement, KFB methods lack versatility, require extensive fine-tuning for different fall postures or robot wear, and are open-loop, meaning they cannot adapt to real-time disturbances during the stand-up process.
 - **Genetic Algorithms for Stand Up:** Some approaches optimize stand-up splines using genetic algorithms, offering better generalization across robot architectures but still resulting in open-loop animations without real-time adaptability.
 - **RL-based Stand Up:** Existing DRL methods for stand-up have been explored. For instance, Q-learning approaches incorporate environment feedback but involve complex problem discretization and do not address fall recovery. Other DRL methods learn stand-up motions but often rely on expert key frames and demand substantial training times (e.g., "approximately one day of training").

This paper identifies a critical gap: the lack of a **unified, adaptable, real-time solution** that integrates *both* fall recovery (actively preventing damage during a fall by creating new contact points) and stand-up (from various prone or supine positions) into a single framework. Existing DRL solutions either focus on push recovery or stand-up, rarely combining them efficiently, and often struggle with long training times and dependence on expert knowledge.

FRASA directly addresses this gap by proposing an end-to-end DRL agent that unifies these tasks. It differentiates itself by focusing on "fall recovery, which involves creating new points of contact with the ground as a strategy to regain stability in minimal time," rather than just push recovery. By leveraging DRL and advanced algorithms, FRASA aims to overcome the limitations of both traditional methods (lack of adaptability, fine-tuning burden) and existing DRL approaches (long training times, limited scope). Its direct comparison and superior performance against a world-champion KFB method signify a substantial advancement in the field, pushing the boundaries of autonomous and robust humanoid robot behavior.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching goal of this research is to enhance the robustness and autonomy of humanoid robots by enabling them to effectively recover from falls and stand up in dynamic, unpredictable environments. The authors are motivated by the inherent instability of humanoid robots, the risk of damage during falls, and the limitations of current fall recovery and stand-up strategies.

The key objectives of FRASA are:

1. **Unified Task Handling:** To develop a Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) agent capable of efficiently addressing *both* fall recovery (creating new contact points during a fall to minimize damage and regain stability) and stand-up tasks (recovering from prone or supine positions) within a single, integrated framework. The aim is for the robot to resume walking as quickly and smoothly as possible after any disturbance.
2. **Reduced Training Time:** To achieve effective and rapid training for the DRL agent. The paper specifically aims to significantly reduce the training duration compared to existing RL approaches for similar tasks, making the development cycle more efficient.
3. **Versatile and Adaptive Recovery Strategy:** To enable the robot to adapt to unpredictable real-world disturbances and recover from various fall postures without the need for extensive expert tuning or pre-defined key frames. This objective seeks to overcome the rigidity and lack of adaptability inherent in traditional methods.
4. **Demonstrate Superior Performance:** To empirically validate FRASA's effectiveness by demonstrating its superior performance in fall recovery and stand-up tasks when compared against a high-performance, state-of-the-art Key Frame Based (KFB) method, specifically the one deployed by the RoboCup 2023 world champion Rhoban Team.

These objectives are motivated by the identified shortcomings in the current state of humanoid robotics: traditional MPC methods are primarily for prevention and have limitations in adaptability and computational cost; KFB routines for stand-up are simple but lack versatility and require constant fine-tuning; and existing DRL approaches for push recovery or stand-up often demand prohibitive training times and may not cover the full spectrum of recovery needs. By achieving these objectives, FRASA aims to contribute significantly to making humanoid robots more resilient and practical for real-world applications and competitive environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FRASA employs an end-to-end Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) approach, where raw sensor inputs are directly mapped to motor commands, for autonomous stand-up and fall recovery of humanoid robots.

**A. Core Algorithm and Training Environment:**
The agent is trained using the **CrossQ algorithm** integrated with the **Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)** DRL algorithm, implemented in Stable Baselines X. CrossQ is chosen for its improved sampling efficiency and reduced training time by removing target networks, normalizing batches, and using wider critic layers. Training is conducted in a **MuJoCo physics simulator**, which incorporates a detailed CAD model of the Sigmaban humanoid robot, including an approximation of its parts using basic shapes (51 boxes, 14 cylinders) for effective contact management. The robot's degrees of freedom (DoFs) are position-controlled to mimic servomotor behavior.

**B. Robot Platform and Symmetry Exploitation:**
Experiments are conducted on the **Sigmaban humanoid robot**, a 70 cm tall, 7.5 kg platform with 20 DoFs actuated by Dynamixel servos. To simplify the learning process and exploit the robot's inherent bilateral symmetry, only **5 symmetric DoFs** (shoulder pitch, elbow, hip pitch, knee, ankle pitch) are observed and controlled. These are primarily involved in the x-z plane of movement. The robot's trunk pitch (θ) is also included as a critical state variable. The target posture for recovery is a neutral standing pose, defined by specific joint angles and trunk pitch.

**C. RL Environment Design:**
- **Environment Initialization:** At the start of each episode, the robot's joint angles and trunk pitch are randomized within physical limits, and the robot is released above the ground. This simulates various fallen states, promoting broad policy exploration. A certain probability (`p_s`) is used to occasionally initialize the robot close to the neutral pose, providing early large rewards and accelerating learning.
- **Action Space:** The action space is continuous, representing the desired joint velocity (`˙q_desired`) for the 5 controlled DoFs. These velocities incrementally update the desired joint angles `q_desired` at each simulation timestep.
- **State Space:** The state vector `s_t` comprises:
 - Current joint angles (`q_t`) and velocities (`˙q_t`) of the 5 controlled DoFs.
 - Desired joint angles (`q_desired_t`).
 - Trunk pitch (`θ_t`) and its rate of change (`˙θ_t`).
 - A history of past action updates (`a_t-1`..., `a_t-k`).

**D. Reward Function:**
The reward function is designed to guide the agent towards achieving and maintaining the target upright posture:
`R = R_state + R_variation + R_collision`
- `R_state`: A Gaussian function rewarding proximity to the target state (`ψ_target = [q_target, θ_target]`), with higher rewards as the robot approaches and maintains the desired posture.
- `R_variation`: A small penalty for high control velocities and large variations in actions, promoting smoother and less strenuous movements.
- `R_collision`: A small penalty to discourage self-collisions within the robot's body.

**E. Episode Termination and Truncation:**
Episodes terminate if the robot enters an unsafe or irreversible state (e.g., trunk pitch exceeding a threshold, indicating an upside-down position; or trunk pitch velocity exceeding a critical threshold for violent displacements). Episodes are truncated if they exceed a maximum duration to ensure diversity of encountered states.

**F. Domain Randomization and Sim-to-Real Transfer:**
To enhance robustness and ensure successful transfer from simulation to the real robot, extensive **domain randomization** is employed. Randomized parameters include floor friction, trunk mass and CoM position, battery voltage, actuator proportional gain, absolute force bound, damping, friction loss, and actuator position offsets. Crucially, sensor communication delays (IMU, actuators) measured on the real robot are also modeled in the simulation.
During initial real-robot transfers, significant tremors were observed due to discrepancies between simulated and real actuator friction models. This was mitigated by reducing motor gains in both simulation and real robot, and increasing the agent's inference frequency (from 20Hz training to 100Hz on the robot).

**G. Experiments and Metrics:**
Two main experiments were conducted:
1. **Stand Up Experiment:** Compares FRASA with the Rhoban Team's KFB method for standing up from prone and supine positions. Metric: time from movement initiation to the start of walking (defined by state vector error below 5° for 0.5 seconds).
2. **Fall Recovery Experiment:** Compares FRASA and KFB in rejecting disturbances from a static standing pose using a repeatable pendulum mechanism with varying impact energies (4J to 7.3J). Metric: duration of instability (time from state vector deviation >5° to return to stability <5° for 0.5 seconds).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted on the Sigmaban humanoid robots demonstrate FRASA's significant capabilities in both rapid training and superior performance compared to traditional methods.

**A. Rapid and Efficient Training:**
- FRASA agents, trained using CrossQ + SAC on a PC with an Intel Core i9-12900 CPU and NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU, achieved full training within an remarkably short timeframe of **13 to 37 minutes**, accumulating 235,000 to 575,000 timesteps. This is a substantial reduction in training time compared to other DRL approaches for similar complex humanoid tasks (e.g., "one day of training" mentioned for stand-up motions in related work).
- The reward progression across 40 distinct FRASA agents showed consistent convergence towards high rewards, with the fastest agents learning to stand up in 13 minutes and all networks converging after approximately 30 minutes, indicating robust and efficient learning.
- The mean on-board inference time for generating actuator commands was 40μs, leveraging OpenVINO™ Runtime for computational efficiency.

**B. Qualitative Behavior and Agent Selection:**
- The rapid training allowed for the generation of multiple candidate FRASA agents, which sometimes exhibited diverse behaviors (e.g., squatting vs. extended legs for forward stand-up). This variability enabled the selection of an optimal candidate based on criteria such as recovery speed, movement robustness, mechanical safety, and versatility.
- The DRL approach allowed for the emergence of highly dynamic solutions, some of which were functional but deemed undesirable due to potential mechanical stress or wear on the robot. This highlights the importance of careful reward shaping and agent selection in DRL for real-world robotics.

**C. Quantitative Results: Stand Up Experiment:**
FRASA demonstrably outperforms the KFB method for standing up from both prone and supine positions:
- **Supine Position:** FRASA completed stand-up in **2.678 ± 0.178 seconds**, which is only **53%** of the time taken by the KFB method (5.06 ± 0.008 seconds). This represents an average gain of 2.38 seconds.
- **Prone Position:** FRASA completed stand-up in **2.135 ± 0.042 seconds**, which is **68%** of the time required by the KFB method (3.154 ± 0.005 seconds). This is an average gain of 1.02 seconds.

**D. Quantitative Results: Fall Recovery Experiment (Disturbance Rejection):**
FRASA also showed superior or comparable performance in rejecting significant disturbances:
- **Strongest Impacts (7.3J, d = 0.89m):**
 - **Frontwards Disturbance:** FRASA recovered in **2.44 ± 0.03 seconds**, taking only **42%** of the time required by the KFB method (5.74 ± 0.05 seconds).
 - **Backwards Disturbance:** FRASA recovered in **2.26 ± 0.11 seconds**, taking **51%** of the time compared to the KFB method (4.47 ± 0.23 seconds).
- **Moderate Frontwards Disturbance (5.5J, d = 0.75m):** FRASA achieved stability in **2.41 ± 0.04 seconds**, which is just **40%** of the KFB method's time (5.96 ± 0.11 seconds).
- **Moderate Backwards Disturbance (5.5J, d = 0.75m):** The KFB method recorded a slightly lower mean instability time (0.49 ± 0.10s) compared to FRASA (0.62 ± 0.10s). However, the overlapping standard deviations indicate this difference is not statistically significant. For the weakest backwards disturbance, neither method caused imbalance in the robot.

In summary, FRASA consistently demonstrated its superiority in efficiency, speed, and robustness across various fall and disturbance scenarios, significantly outperforming the expert-tuned KFB method used by a world-champion RoboCup team.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of FRASA represents a significant advancement in humanoid robotics, particularly in addressing the critical challenges of stability, fall recovery, and autonomous operation in dynamic environments. Its impact can be assessed across several key dimensions:

1. **Paradigm Shift in Humanoid Recovery Strategies:** FRASA introduces an end-to-end Deep Reinforcement Learning approach that effectively unifies two distinct, yet interconnected, problems: fall recovery (acting during a fall to minimize damage and regain stability) and stand-up (recovering from various fallen postures). This unified framework offers a more comprehensive and robust solution compared to traditional methods that typically handle these tasks separately or lack real-time adaptability. The focus on actively creating new contact points during a fall is a crucial distinction from mere push recovery, moving towards more biologically inspired, protective behaviors.

2. **Breakthrough in DRL Training Efficiency:** One of the most significant contributions of FRASA is the dramatic reduction in training time. By leveraging the CrossQ algorithm and exploiting the robot's inherent symmetry, the authors achieved fully trained agents in mere minutes (13-37 minutes), a feat previously challenging for complex humanoid tasks that often required days of training. This breakthrough makes DRL a far more practical and viable tool for rapid iteration and deployment in robotics research and development, accelerating the pace of innovation.

3. **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** Unlike rigid, pre-programmed Key Frame Based (KFB) routines, FRASA's DRL agent learns adaptive behaviors that can respond to unpredictable real-world feedback and recover from a wide range of fall postures without manual expert tuning. The extensive domain randomization applied during training further bolsters this robustness, ensuring that the learned policy generalizes well from simulation to the physical robot, even amidst variations in mechanical parameters, environmental conditions, and sensor delays. This adaptability is paramount for humanoids operating in unstructured, real-world environments.

4. **Practical Superiority over State-of-the-Art:** The empirical validation demonstrating FRASA's superior performance over the KFB method used by the RoboCup 2023 world champion Rhoban Team is a powerful testament to its practical efficacy. Outperforming a highly optimized, expert-tuned traditional method in competitive scenarios highlights the DRL approach's potential to set new benchmarks for humanoid robot performance. Faster recovery times translate directly into greater uptime, resilience, and operational efficiency for robotic systems.

5. **Accelerating Humanoid Robot Development and Deployment:** The combination of rapid training, adaptability, and proven performance makes FRASA a valuable contribution to the broader field of humanoid robotics. It paves the way for faster development cycles for more capable and reliable robots, reducing the human effort involved in fine-tuning complex behaviors. This has profound implications for various applications, including:
 - **Robotics Competitions:** Providing a significant competitive advantage.
 - **Disaster Response:** Enabling robots to operate more safely and effectively in hazardous, unpredictable terrains where falls are inevitable.
 - **Domestic and Industrial Assistance:** Making robots safer and more reliable for interacting with humans and performing tasks in dynamic human environments.
 - **Exploration:** Allowing robots to traverse challenging terrains with greater confidence, knowing they can recover from unexpected events.

**Future Directions and Limitations:**
While groundbreaking, the paper also acknowledges areas for further improvement. The exploitation of symmetry, while accelerating training, currently limits the versatility of behaviors, particularly in handling lateral disturbances. Future work could explore extending the framework to full 20 DoF control for complete omnidirectional recovery. Additionally, enhancing actuator modeling in simulation and exploring online (on-robot) training could further bridge the sim-to-real gap, leading to even more robust and mechanically efficient behaviors. Despite these, FRASA represents a major leap forward in developing resilient and autonomous humanoid robots.
