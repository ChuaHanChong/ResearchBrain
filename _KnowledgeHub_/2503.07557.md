# AutoSpatial: Visual-Language Reasoning for Social Robot Navigation through Efficient Spatial Reasoning Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> AutoSpatial enhances Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for social robot navigation by introducing a structured spatial grounding framework and an efficient hybrid data labeling strategy. This approach enables VLMs to interpret spatial relationships with greater precision and achieve up to 20.50% higher scores in action generation and improved social compliance compared to baseline models.

## Problem

- Traditional navigation methods often lack explicit spatial reasoning and an understanding of nuanced social norms, leading to limitations in dynamic social scenarios.
- Vision-Language Models (VLMs) suffer from poor spatial grounding, lacking explicit metric representations of spatial relationships critical for precise navigation.
- There is a scarcity of domain-specific, large-scale annotated datasets tailored for complex social navigation scenarios, especially from a robot's perspective.

## Method

- A structured spatial grounding framework standardizes spatial descriptions, decomposing information into angular position, distance, and movement direction relative to the robot.
- An efficient data labeling strategy combines rule-based auto-generated VQA (==Visual Question-Answering==) pairs from the CODA dataset with targeted manual annotations for challenging social scenarios.
- A two-round VQA structure facilitates hierarchical learning, progressing from fine-grained individual perceptions (auto-labeled) to broader scene-level comprehension and social interaction analysis (manual annotations).

## Results

- AutoSpatial (AS-A72D1) achieved a basic spatial reasoning score of **0.710** on the CODA benchmark, outperforming LLaVA-M (**0.404**) and Vanilla-LLaVA (**0.376**).
- It demonstrated up to **10.71%** improvement in perception & prediction, **16.26%** in reasoning, **20.50%** in action, and **18.73%** in explanation scores compared to baseline models trained only on manual data.
- Qualitative analysis showed AutoSpatial providing superior spatial awareness with standardized terminology and generating specific, socially appropriate instructions like 'Stop and wait for the person in the orange shirt to clear the path,' unlike generic baseline responses.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The combination of auto-labeled and manual data creates a synergistic effect, proving more effective for comprehensive social navigation reasoning than either data source alone.
> - Structured spatial grounding significantly reduces ambiguity in spatial interpretations, providing VLMs with a consistent and precise understanding of object locations and movements.
> - A hierarchical two-round VQA structure enables the model to learn foundational spatial cues before integrating complex social dynamics and generating coherent explanations and actions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robotic navigation in human-populated environments presents a complex challenge due to the dynamic and often unpredictable nature of human behavior. Traditional navigation methods, including hand-crafted rules like the Social Force Model and learning-based approaches, have advanced obstacle avoidance and path planning. However, these methods frequently exhibit limitations in dynamic social scenarios because they often lack explicit spatial reasoning and an understanding of nuanced social norms. Robots operating in such environments are required not only to avoid physical collisions but also to interpret and respond to human intentions, social groupings, and implicit interaction rules.

Vision-Language Models (VLMs) have emerged as a promising technology for various robotics tasks due leveraging their capabilities in combining visual perception with natural language understanding. VLMs can facilitate high-level reasoning, task planning, and human-robot interaction. However, their application in social robot navigation is significantly constrained by two primary limitations. First, VLMs typically suffer from poor spatial grounding, meaning they lack explicit metric representations of spatial relationships, such as precise relative positions, distances, and movement trajectories. This deficiency can lead to ambiguous interpretations of spatial configurations. Second, there is a scarcity of domain-specific, large-scale annotated datasets tailored for social navigation scenarios, with most existing datasets focusing on automotive driving contexts rather than intricate pedestrian interactions.

This work, AutoSpatial, positions itself within this landscape by addressing these two limitations. It aims to enhance the spatial reasoning capabilities of VLMs specifically for social navigation. By introducing a structured spatial grounding framework and an efficient data labeling strategy, AutoSpatial seeks to provide VLMs with the explicit spatial understanding necessary for robust social interaction. The approach distinguishes itself by systematically augmenting VLMs with a structured question-answer paradigm, generating ground truth text labels for visual observations, and training models to understand perception, prediction, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning, final actions, and explanations. This method aims to bridge the gap between general VLM capabilities and the specific demands of socially intelligent robot navigation, thereby contributing to the development of more capable and adaptable autonomous systems in human environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of AutoSpatial is to systematically enhance the spatial reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) for social robot navigation. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations and aims to address key deficiencies identified in current VLM applications for robotics:

1. **Improve Spatial Grounding in VLMs:** A fundamental motivation is to overcome the inherent limitation of existing VLMs regarding explicit spatial understanding. Current VLMs often struggle to interpret metric spatial relationships, such as precise relative positions, distances, and movement trajectories of pedestrians in a scene. This leads to ambiguous spatial interpretations, which are problematic for safety-critical tasks like social navigation. AutoSpatial aims to provide VLMs with a structured spatial grounding mechanism that defines and standardizes positional and directional descriptions, thereby enabling more precise and unambiguous spatial perception.

2. **Address Domain-Specific Data Scarcity:** Social navigation requires understanding complex and often implicit human behaviors that are not adequately captured in generic vision-language datasets. Most available datasets are either too general or focused on other domains (e.g., autonomous driving), lacking the specific annotations for nuanced pedestrian interactions and social dynamics from a robot's perspective. AutoSpatial is motivated by the need for an efficient method to generate relevant, large-scale, and high-quality domain-specific training data without relying heavily on expensive manual annotation. This is achieved through a novel automatic data labeling strategy.

3. **Enable Comprehensive Social Navigation Reasoning:** The research seeks to equip VLMs with the ability to perform comprehensive reasoning for social navigation, which extends beyond simple collision avoidance. This encompasses five essential components:
 - **Perception:** Accurately identifying and describing the current state of pedestrians (e.g., their precise location and movement).
 - **Prediction:** Anticipating the future movements and intentions of pedestrians.
 - **Chain of Thought (CoT) Reasoning:** Articulating the logical steps and considerations that lead to a navigation decision, often involving social norms.
 - **Final Action:** Generating specific and socially appropriate navigation commands for the robot.
 - **Explanation:** Providing justifications for the chosen actions, enhancing transparency and trust.
 The goal is to integrate these components synergistically within the VLM framework.

4. **Reduce Annotation Costs while Maintaining Data Quality:** Manual annotation of complex social interaction data is labor-intensive and expensive. A key motivation is to develop an efficient data labeling strategy that minimizes the reliance on manual supervision. AutoSpatial aims to achieve this by combining minimal manual annotations (for challenging scenarios and higher-level reasoning) with a large-scale, rule-based auto-labeling process (for fundamental spatial understanding), thereby reducing annotation costs while preserving the quality and relevance of the training data.

5. **Facilitate Hierarchical Understanding of Scenarios:** The work is motivated by the observation that effective social navigation requires understanding at multiple levels, from individual pedestrian movements to complex group dynamics and overall scene interpretation. AutoSpatial introduces a two-round VQA structure to foster hierarchical learning, moving from fine-grained individual perceptions to broader scene-level comprehension and social interaction analysis.

By addressing these objectives, AutoSpatial endeavors to advance the capabilities of robots to navigate human environments safely, efficiently, and in a socially compliant manner, thereby moving closer to the deployment of truly intelligent social robots.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AutoSpatial is designed to enhance the spatial reasoning capabilities of VLMs for social navigation through a structured, multi-component approach. The methodology integrates structured spatial grounding, efficient data labeling combining auto-generated and manual annotations, and a two-round VQA (Visual Question-Answering) structure for hierarchical learning.

#### 3.1. A. Structured Spatial Grounding

A core component of AutoSpatial is its structured spatial grounding framework, which aims to standardize and disambiguate spatial relationships. This framework decomposes spatial information into two distinct categories:

1. **Positional Description:** This component describes the angular position and distance of individual humans relative to the robot.
 - **Angular Position:** The robot's field of view is discretized into five specific zones: ‘on the left’, ‘slightly to the left’, ‘directly in front’, ‘slightly to the right’, and ‘on the right’. Each pedestrian is assigned a zone based on the horizontal pixel location of their center point in the image, providing a clear and consistent relative angular description.
 - **Distance Estimation:** Distance is categorized into five distinct levels: ‘very close’ (< 3m), ‘close’ (3–6m), ‘moderate’ (6–9m), ‘far’ (9–12m), and ‘very far’ (> 12m). This classification allows for precise yet generalized distance reporting.
 By combining these, the approach can generate descriptions such as “the pedestrian is slightly to the left of the robot at a moderate distance.”

2. **Movement Direction Description:** A unified coordinate system is adopted, where the robot's forward direction is designated as north. Pedestrian movements are then described using eight cardinal and intercardinal directions (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW), in addition to a stationary state. This standardization provides a consistent and unambiguous reference frame for directional perception and prediction, overcoming the ambiguities of relative directional terms.

This structured spatial grounding serves as a consistent terminology foundation for both auto-labeling and manual annotation, ensuring uniformity in spatial descriptions across the training data.

#### 3.2. B. Data Labeling

The training data for AutoSpatial is a combination of auto-labeled VQA pairs and targeted manual annotations, designed to achieve both broad spatial understanding and detailed scene comprehension.

1. **Auto-labeled VQA Pairs:** A systematic, rule-based approach is used to generate VQA pairs from the CODA dataset, which provides real-world pedestrian interaction data from a robot’s perspective, including trajectory information and bounding box annotations. This process relies on heuristics rather than learning models. For each frame, it covers three key aspects:
 - **Spatial Perception:** Precise positional and movement direction descriptions are generated for each pedestrian instance based on the structured spatial grounding.
 - **Movement Prediction:** Short-term trajectories are analyzed using a multi-frame sliding window. Velocity vectors are smoothed, and movement patterns are categorized into five types: started motion, continued motion, left turns, right turns, or transitions to stationary states. Predictions are made based on changes in velocity and direction exceeding predefined thresholds.
 - **Interaction Description:** The approach identifies, classifies, and describes interaction patterns, including trajectory conflicts (analyzing relative positions and velocities), path crossings (west-to-east or east-to-west), pass-by scenarios (based on relative trajectories), and other scenarios without collision risks.

2. **Manual Annotations:** A select set of 72 challenging scenarios from the CODA dataset were manually annotated. These scenarios were chosen for their complexity, involving high pedestrian density, intricate social grouping, and situations requiring abstracting from individual to group-level understanding, where auto-labeling might be insufficient or ambiguous. The annotations adhere to the SNEI protocol, covering five tasks: Perception, Prediction, CoT Reasoning, Final Action, and Explanation. Crucially, annotators were guided by the structured spatial grounding guidelines (Sec. III-A) to ensure consistency and reduce subjective variations in descriptions, providing clearer supervision.

#### 3.3. C. Two-Round VQA Structure

To facilitate hierarchical learning, AutoSpatial employs a two-round VQA structure for scenes that include manual annotations.

- **Round 1:** This round consists of auto-labeled VQAs, primarily focusing on individual pedestrians' perception, prediction, and CoT reasoning. It establishes a precise spatial-temporal understanding at the individual level. The format is `<image, round 1 question, round 1 ground-truth answer>`.
- **Round 2:** For manually annotated scenes, this round follows Round 1. It integrates the human annotations to refine understanding of group dynamics, social relationships, collective movement patterns, and higher-level scene interpretation. This round specifically includes questions and answers for Final Action and Explanation, which are less amenable to auto-labeling. The format for these scenes is `<image, round 1 question, round 1 ground-truth answer>` followed by `<image, round 2 question, round 2 ground-truth answer>`.

This progressive structure allows the model to learn from fundamental individual spatial cues before progressing to complex scene-level reasoning and socially appropriate actions. For scenes with only auto-labeled data, a single-round format is used. The structured spatial grounding terminology is incorporated as part of the model input, serving as a system prompt to guide the VLM’s responses.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of AutoSpatial involved extensive experiments comparing its performance against baseline models across various metrics and scenarios.

#### 4.1. A. Experimental Setup

The base model for AutoSpatial was LLaVA-1.6 OneVision-7B. Training utilized four A100 80GB GPUs, following LLaVA's default instruction tuning hyperparameters (AdamW optimizer, learning rate 2e-5, batch size 8 with gradient accumulation steps 4). Input to the model consisted of an image and corresponding VQAs adhering to the structured spatial grounding guidelines.

Evaluation adopted a two-fold framework:
1. **Basic Spatial Reasoning:** Assessed using an automated metric on auto-labeled VQA pairs from the CODA dataset. The dataset was balanced for directional bias and split 90-10 for training/testing (6,205 training frames/16,530 pedestrian instances; 573 test frames/1,542 pedestrian instances). The evaluation focused on movement direction prediction and relative position estimation, employing a keyword-based metric (1.0 for exact, 0.5 for approximate matches).
2. **Comprehensive Scene Understanding:** Evaluated on the SNEI benchmark using both expert systems (GPT-4o, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) and 7 human evaluators (graduate computer science students). A randomly sampled subset of 49 SNEI scenarios was used. Expert systems assigned scores from 1-10 across four aspects: Perception & Prediction (P&P), Reasoning (R), Action (A), and Explanation (E). Human evaluators provided relative rankings for these aspects, comparing AutoSpatial's best variant against two baseline models (LLaVA-M and Vanilla-LLaVA) to mitigate subjective bias.

Model configurations included:
- **Vanilla-LLaVA:** Original LLaVA-1.6 OneVision-7B.
- **LLaVA-M:** LLaVA fine-tuned on only 72 manual annotations.
- **AutoSpatial (AS) variants:** Models trained with 72 manual annotations and the full CODA VQA data (18,615 pairs). Variants denoted `AS-AxxDy` where `Axx` is manual annotations (e.g., A72) and `Dy` is downsampling ratio of auto-labeled data (D1 for no downsampling, D2 for 1/2, etc.). `AS-A36D1` used 36 manual annotations, and `AS-A0D1` used no manual annotations.

#### 4.2. B. Quantitative Results

**Overall Performance:** AutoSpatial (AS-A72D1) demonstrated consistent superior performance across all evaluation metrics. On the CODA benchmark for basic spatial reasoning, AS-A72D1 achieved a score of 0.710, outperforming LLaVA-M (0.404) and Vanilla-LLaVA (0.376) (Table I). Expert system evaluations (Gemini 2.0 Flash, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet) also showed AS-A72D1 consistently outperforming baseline models across P&P, R, A, and E aspects. Compared to baseline models trained only on manually annotated data (LLaVA-M), AutoSpatial showed significant improvements in averaged cross-validation scores: up to 10.71% in perception & prediction, 16.26% in reasoning, 20.50% in action, and 18.73% in explanation. Human evaluations (Table III) corroborated these findings, with AS-A72D1 receiving better relative rankings than LLaVA-M and Vanilla LLaVA for P&P, R, A, and E.

**Synergistic Effect of Data Combination:** A key finding was the synergistic effect of combining auto-labeled and manual data. While LLaVA-M (manual annotations only) improved over Vanilla-LLaVA, the performance gap remained substantial compared to AutoSpatial, indicating that manual data alone is insufficient. Conversely, training solely on auto-labeled VQA data (AS-A0D1) yielded only marginal improvements over Vanilla-LLaVA and sometimes underperformed, suggesting that human supervision is crucial for grounding social interactions. The combination proved to be more effective than either data source in isolation.

**Robustness to Data Composition:** The study showed that AutoSpatial is relatively robust to variations in data composition. Different downsampling ratios for auto-labeled data (AS-A72D1 through AS-A72D5) resulted in minor performance differences, indicating that the method is not overly sensitive to the exact proportion of auto-labeled data (Table I). Furthermore, reducing manual annotations by half (AS-A36D1) led to minimal performance degradation, suggesting that strong results can be achieved with limited manual supervision.

#### 4.3. C. Ablation Studies

Ablation studies (Table II) investigated the individual contributions of different VQA types:
- **Perception & Prediction VQAs only (AS-A72D1-P&P-VQA):** Showed strong performance in the P&P metric but limited improvement in Reasoning, Action, and Explanation, highlighting its specific contribution to foundational spatial understanding.
- **Reasoning VQAs only, without P&P ground truth (AS-A72D1-R-VQA-1):** Improved reasoning, action, and explanation scores, but struggled with basic spatial understanding, indicating the importance of explicit perception and prediction data.
- **Reasoning VQAs with P&P ground truth provided (AS-A72D1-R-VQA-2):** Showed improved reasoning, action, and explanation scores, but still had limited improvement in P&P compared to the full model, suggesting that direct training on P&P VQAs is more effective than inferring them.

These results indicate that combining different VQA types with manual annotations creates a synergistic effect where each component addresses specific learning needs, leading to optimal performance across all metrics.

#### 4.4. D. Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative analysis (e.g., Fig. 3) revealed concrete differences between AutoSpatial and baseline models in a crowded sidewalk scenario.
- **Improved Spatial Awareness and Terminology:** AutoSpatial demonstrated superior spatial awareness by accurately identifying key actors and their relative positions using standardized spatial terminology (e.g., “moving towards west”) instead of ambiguous camera-relative descriptions. In contrast, LLaVA-M provided generic descriptions without clear spatial grounding.
- **Socially Appropriate Actions:** While baseline models generated general advice like "continue to navigate the street while maintaining a safe distance," AutoSpatial provided specific, socially-aware instructions, such as recommending the robot to "Stop and wait for the person in the orange shirt to clear the path," aligning better with social norms.

**Identified Limitations:** The analysis also highlighted areas for improvement. The model occasionally misinterprets subtle human cues like gaze direction and body orientation, which are critical for inferring pedestrian intent. Additionally, it sometimes reverted to ambiguous camera-relative descriptions, rather than consistently applying the structured spatial grounding, potentially due to data limitations during training. These observations provide directions for future research.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

AutoSpatial presents an efficient methodology for enhancing the spatial reasoning capabilities of Vision-Language Models (VLMs) in the context of social robot navigation. The significance of this work stems from its ability to address key limitations that hinder the deployment of intelligent robots in human-populated environments.

One primary impact is the **improvement in VLM spatial grounding**. By introducing a structured spatial grounding framework that standardizes descriptions of angular position, distance, and movement direction, AutoSpatial enables VLMs to interpret spatial relationships with greater precision and reduced ambiguity. This is crucial for safety and efficiency in social navigation, where misinterpreting a pedestrian's location or trajectory could lead to unsafe or socially inappropriate robot behavior. The quantitative results demonstrate that this structured approach leads to significant improvements in perception and prediction accuracy.

Another significant contribution is the **efficient data labeling strategy**. The approach mitigates the challenge of domain-specific data scarcity by combining rule-based auto-labeled VQA pairs with a minimal set of carefully selected manual annotations. This hybrid strategy substantially reduces the manual annotation burden and associated costs, making the development of social navigation systems more scalable and practical. The robustness of AutoSpatial to varying downsampling ratios and even reduced manual annotations suggests that the method can achieve strong performance with limited human effort. This efficiency in data acquisition has broad implications for robotics research, enabling faster iteration and development cycles for VLM-based systems.

The **hierarchical two-round VQA structure** further enhances the model's capabilities by fostering a deeper, multi-layered understanding of social scenarios. By first establishing fine-grained spatial-temporal understanding of individual agents through auto-labeling, and then refining this with human-annotated scene-level group dynamics and social interactions, AutoSpatial demonstrates a more comprehensive reasoning process. This hierarchical approach leads to more coherent Chain-of-Thought reasoning, more appropriate final actions, and more descriptive explanations, as validated by expert system and human evaluations.

The **potential impact** of AutoSpatial is substantial for the field of social robotics. Robots equipped with these enhanced spatial reasoning capabilities could exhibit more natural, predictable, and trustworthy interactions with humans. This could lead to:
- **Safer Navigation:** By accurately perceiving and predicting human movements, robots can avoid collisions more effectively and navigate in a socially compliant manner.
- **Improved Human-Robot Interaction:** Robots that can explain their decisions and adapt their actions based on social norms are more likely to be accepted and integrated into human environments.
- **Broader Deployability:** The reduced reliance on extensive manual data annotation makes VLM-based social navigation solutions more accessible for various applications, from service robots in public spaces to autonomous vehicles in pedestrian-heavy areas.

While the qualitative analysis identified areas for further refinement, such as interpreting subtle human cues, the foundational improvements in spatial reasoning provided by AutoSpatial lay a groundwork for developing more sophisticated and socially intelligent autonomous systems. This work represents a step towards creating robots that not only perceive their environment but also understand its social complexities, leading to more robust and ethical robot integration into human society.
