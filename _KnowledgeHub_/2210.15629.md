# Language Control Diffusion: Efficiently Scaling through Space, Time, and Tasks

> [!summary] Summary
> The Language Control Diffusion (LCD) framework enables efficient and scalable language-conditioned robotic control from high-dimensional pixel inputs, by performing high-level planning in a low-dimensional latent space and using temporal abstraction. This approach achieves superior multi-task success rates on challenging benchmarks while demonstrating 3.3x to 15x faster inference speeds compared to prior diffusion-based methods.

## Problem

- Developing generalist agents capable of executing natural language instructions for complex tasks, often limited by existing language-conditioned control methods.
- Current language-conditioned control often relies on high-level discrete actions, requiring a lower-level skill oracle, which restricts generalization to novel environments or tasks.
- Direct application of diffusion models to high-dimensional pixel inputs is computationally intensive, struggles with task relevance, and may fail to account for control precision.

## Method

- Introduces **Language Control Diffusion (LCD)**, a hierarchical policy framework with a ==high-level diffusion model (HLP)== for planning and a ==low-level policy (LLP)== for execution.
- The LLP is a pre-trained, goal-conditioned policy whose frozen visual encoder maps high-dimensional states to a low-dimensional, task-optimized latent space (32 dimensions).
- The HLP performs ==diffusion-based planning== over ==temporally abstracted== latent goals, conditioned by a frozen ==T5-XXL== language model for natural language instructions.

## Results

- LCD achieved an **88.7%** success rate on CALVIN One-Horizon tasks, surpassing the strongest prior model, SPIL, by **4.1%** and Diffuser-1D by **41.4%**.
- The framework demonstrated strong zero-shot generalization on held-out tasks, achieving an average success rate of **61.00%**, significantly outperforming Diffuser-1D's **12.67%**.
- Inference time for LCD was **0.336 seconds**, making it **3.3x** faster than Diffuser-1D and **15x** faster than Diffuser-2D, with its HLP training in **13.3 hours**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Performing high-level planning in a compact, task-relevant latent space, derived from a goal-conditioned deep reinforcement learning model's encoder, is critical for efficiency and precision in high-dimensional control.
> - Temporal abstraction through state subsampling combined with DDIM inference provides substantial computational speedups (3.3x to 15x faster inference) while maintaining strong performance.
> - Diffusion models are superior to simpler MLP or Transformer architectures for modeling the goal space and generating coherent long-range plans in hierarchical control.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of generalist agents, particularly household robots capable of executing natural language instructions for complex tasks like cleaning or organizing, represents a longstanding objective in artificial intelligence. Such agents require the ability to plan over extended time horizons, interpret human feedback, and generalize to novel tasks. Language conditioning offers an intuitive mechanism for task specification and supports generalization capabilities. Recent advancements have leveraged large language models (LLMs) to enhance robotics and decision-making by providing powerful reasoning capabilities.

However, existing language-conditioned control methods often present limitations. Many approaches that utilize LLMs for control rely on high-level discrete action spaces, requiring a lower-level skill oracle to provide predefined actions. This dependency on a fixed set of skills can restrict generalization to unfamiliar environments or tasks, leaving the challenge of direct long-horizon planning in the original action space largely unresolved.

Denoising diffusion models have emerged as a promising candidate for direct long-horizon planning in reinforcement learning (RL) and offline RL settings, demonstrating superior performance over some Transformer or MLP-based models in low-dimensional state spaces. Specifically, models like Diffuser have shown potential due to their flexibility in task specification, temporal compositionality, and scalability to long horizons.

Despite these strengths, directly applying diffusion models to high-dimensional pixel inputs presents significant challenges. In such scenarios, a diffusion model effectively operates as a text-to-video generator, a task where even internet-scale models sometimes exhibit limited understanding of physics and temporal coherence. A key issue is that the generative training objective does not intrinsically account for task relevance, leading models to allocate equal importance to all scene details, including those irrelevant to the control problem. This can compromise precision and lead to control failures. Furthermore, training such models on high-dimensional data is computationally intensive and resource-prohibitive for many researchers.

This research aims to address these identified gaps by developing a hierarchical diffusion policy that scales efficiently across spatial, temporal, and task dimensions, leveraging language for robust control in complex, long-horizon environments, and thereby advancing the practical realization of generalist agents.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this work is to advance the development of practical generalist agents by overcoming several computational and representational challenges inherent in current language-conditioned robotic control. The authors identify three critical axes along which current methods face difficulties: high-dimensional inputs (space), long execution horizons (time), and generalization to novel tasks.

The motivation for proposing the Language Control Diffusion (LCD) framework stems from the following detailed points:

1. **Addressing Limitations in Direct Long-Horizon Planning:** Current language-conditioned control approaches often rely on decomposing high-level language instructions into sequences of predefined, low-level skills executed by a policy or oracle. While LLMs excel at this decomposition, a fixed skill set can severely limit a robot's ability to generalize to new environments or tasks where these skills may not be applicable or sufficient. The research is motivated by the need for a system that can perform direct long-horizon planning in the robot's fundamental action space, without the need for manual skill specification. Diffusion models offer a promising avenue for flexible, long-horizon generation, but their direct application faces other challenges.

2. **Overcoming Non-Task-Specific Representations in High-Dimensional Spaces:** When applied directly to high-dimensional pixel data, existing diffusion models (e.g., Diffuser) act as generative models for entire visual trajectories. Their training objective focuses on accurately reconstructing the visual input, meaning they must model all aspects of a scene indiscriminately. This includes details that are irrelevant to the specific control task at hand. Such an undifferentiated approach is computationally inefficient and can lead to inaccuracies in control, as fine details crucial for task execution might be overshadowed or misinterpreted amidst a plethora of non-essential information. The authors are motivated to create a task-specific representation that filters out irrelevant visual noise, focusing the planning process on salient features for control.

3. **Improving Computational Efficiency:** Training and inference with diffusion models on high-dimensional image spaces are computationally demanding, often requiring significant time and specialized hardware resources. This computational cost poses a barrier to broader research and practical deployment. The paper aims to develop a diffusion-based planning system that is both effective and computationally efficient, making it more accessible and scalable for complex robotics problems.

To address these motivations, the LCD framework proposes a hierarchical diffusion policy. This approach leverages the generative capabilities of diffusion models for flexible, long-horizon planning while mitigating their weaknesses in generating precise low-level details in high-dimensional spaces. By utilizing a frozen, low-dimensional, task-optimized latent representation derived from a goal-conditioned low-level policy (LLP), LCD aims to:
- Scale efficiently along the **Spatial dimension** by performing diffusion in a compact, control-relevant latent space.
- Scale along the **Time dimension** through temporal abstraction, where the LLP can be conditioned on goals several timesteps away, allowing the high-level policy to plan over extended horizons with coarser temporal granularity.
- Scale along the **Task dimension** by directly conditioning the diffusion model on natural language instructions, enabling generalization to novel tasks by interpreting human language commands effectively.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to develop an efficient, generalizable, and scalable system for language-conditioned robotic control that navigates the complexities of high-dimensional inputs and long horizons, thereby contributing a crucial step towards the realization of practical generalist agents.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Language Control Diffusion (LCD) framework proposes a hierarchical policy structure designed to facilitate efficient and scalable language-conditioned control across varying spatial, temporal, and task dimensions. The approach separates the control problem into a high-level policy (HLP) and a low-level policy (LLP).

#### 3.1. Hierarchical Diffusion Policies Framework

The core of LCD lies in a hierarchical setup where a high-level diffusion model acts as a planner, generating a sequence of latent goals, and a low-level policy executes the actions required to achieve these goals.

- **Problem Formulation:** The problem is framed as imitation learning, aiming to minimize the KL divergence between an expert trajectory distribution P_beta(τ | R) and the learned policy's distribution P(τ | R). The atomic objects for planning are state trajectories τ = (s_0, s_1..., s_T), rather than individual actions. This formulation is then converted into a standard denoising diffusion training objective:
 min_θ E_[τ_0, ϵ] [||ϵ_t - ϵ_θ(sqrt(ᾱ_t)τ_0 + sqrt(1 - ᾱ_t)ϵ_t, t)||²],
 where t is a uniformly sampled diffusion timestep, ϵ_t is Gaussian noise, τ_0 is the original trajectory, α_t is a diffusion variance schedule, and ᾱ_t is the cumulative product of α_s. This objective learns to predict the noise added to a noisy trajectory at timestep t, effectively learning to reverse the diffusion process.

- **Near Optimality Guarantees:** The paper provides a theoretical proposition (Proposition 3.1) stating that a near-optimal policy can be recovered using the LLP encoder latent space if two conditions are met: the LLP has closely imitated the expert dataset, and the transition dynamics are Lipschitz smooth. By factoring the LLP into π_lo(s_t, g_t) := ϕ(E(s_t), g_t), where E is an encoder mapping state s to a latent representation z, the suboptimality of the induced abstract state and action space is bounded by an error ϵ, which is proportional to the difference between the LLP's actions and optimal actions. This implies that minimizing the LLP's action reconstruction error directly optimizes for the conditions that ensure near-optimality in the abstracted space.

#### 3.2. Practical Instantiation

The theoretical framework is implemented through specific architectural choices and training procedures for both the HLP and LLP.

- **Low-Level Policy (LLP):**
 - **Architecture:** The HULC architecture (Mees et al., 2022a) is adopted for the LLP. HULC is a hierarchical Multi-Context Imitation Learning (MCIL) model designed for text-to-control policies, incorporating architectural enhancements for encoding sequences, aligning language and visual representations, and optimization techniques.
 - **Training:** The LLP is trained through hindsight relabeling on an offline dataset D. Its objective is to minimize action reconstruction error, specifically using HULC's L_act loss function, which models actions as a mixture of logistic distributions.
 - **State Encoder:** The visual encoder component within the HULC policy is designated as the state encoder E. This encoder maps high-dimensional image observations into a low-dimensional latent space (32 latent dimensions). After the LLP is trained, this state encoder E is *frozen* and its output latent space becomes the domain for the high-level diffusion policy's planning.

- **High-Level Policy (HLP):**
 - **Dataset Preparation:**
 1. After the LLP is trained, its frozen encoder E is used to compress the entire offline dataset of states into their low-dimensional latent representations. This preprocessing step significantly reduces memory usage (100-200x less) and accelerates training by allowing more gradient updates and larger batch sizes.
 2. **Temporal Abstraction:** Trajectories from the preprocessed dataset are subsampled by taking every c-th state, where 'c' is the temporal stride. This creates "latent plans" (τ_c) with reduced sequence length, further enhancing efficiency and allowing flexible distribution of computational load between HLP and LLP.
 - **Architecture:**
 1. **Textual Encoder:** A frozen T5-XXL model (Raffel et al., 2020) with 11B parameters is used to encode natural language instructions into 4096-dimensional embeddings.
 2. **Diffusion Model:** A temporal U-Net (Janner et al., 2022) is employed. Unlike 2D convolutions typical in text-to-image models, it uses 1D convolutions across the time dimension of the latent plan to preserve equivariance along the temporal axis. This architecture is modified to incorporate language conditioning via cross-attention, akin to latent diffusion models (Rombach et al., 2022).
 - **Training:** The HLP is trained on the prepared dataset of latent plans and language embeddings using the diffusion objective (Algorithm 1). It learns to conditionally denoise latent plans.

- **Inference Procedure:**
 1. The HLP generates a sequence of latent goals (g) conditioned on natural language.
 2. The LLP takes the first goal state from the generated latent plan and the current environment state to produce low-level actions.
 3. This process is repeated every 'c' timesteps: the HLP resamples a new latent plan, and the LLP continues execution, until the task is completed or a fixed timeout is reached (360 timesteps).
 4. **Efficiency:** DDIM (Song et al., 2020a) is used during inference for strided sampling, providing an order of magnitude speedup in planning compared to standard DDPM.

This hierarchical and computationally optimized approach aims to enable robust, long-horizon, language-conditioned control from high-dimensional pixel inputs.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated the Language Control Diffusion (LCD) framework against several state-of-the-art baselines on challenging language-conditioned robotics benchmarks, focusing on multi-task success rates, generalization, and computational efficiency.

#### 4.1. Performance on CALVIN Benchmark (Multi-Task Long-Horizon Control)

On the CALVIN multi-task long-horizon control (MT-LHC) benchmark, LCD significantly outperformed prior methods across various horizon lengths (Table 1).
- **One-Horizon Tasks:** LCD achieved an 88.7% success rate (SR), surpassing the strongest prior model, SPIL, by 4.1% (84.6% SR). It also substantially outperformed Diffuser-1D (47.3% SR) by 41.4% and Diffuser-2D (37.4% SR) by 51.3%.
- **Longer Horizons:** As task horizon length increased (Two, Three, Four, Five), LCD maintained its lead, demonstrating robust performance for complex, multi-step tasks. For example, on Five-horizon tasks, LCD achieved 32.2% SR compared to SPIL's 28.6% SR and Diffuser-1D's 0.5% SR.
- **Average Horizon Length:** LCD achieved an average horizon length of 2.88, indicating its ability to complete more tasks on average compared to SPIL (2.67) and other baselines.

#### 4.2. Performance on CLEVR-Robot Benchmark

On the CLEVR-Robot environment, which tests task compositionality and long-horizon execution with language goal specification, LCD demonstrated superior success rates (Table 2).
- **Seen Tasks:** LCD achieved 53.7% SR, outperforming GCBC Transformer (45.7% SR) and Hierarchical Transformer (38.0% SR).
- **Unseen Tasks:** For novel language instructions, LCD maintained its lead with 54.0% SR, indicating strong generalization capabilities.
- **Noisy Conditions:** Under perturbed language input, LCD showed resilience, achieving 48.0% SR, higher than GCBC Transformer (42.7% SR) and Hierarchical Transformer (33.3% SR).

#### 4.3. Task Generalization to Held-Out Tasks

LCD was tested on five held-out tasks requiring zero-shot generalization (Table 3).
- LCD achieved an average success rate of 61.00%, significantly outperforming Diffuser-1D's 12.67%.
- This demonstrates LCD's ability to compose disparate concepts (e.g., object colors, actions like "lift" and "push", and spatial relationships) from its training data to address novel instructions.

#### 4.4. Inference Time and Training Efficiency

Computational efficiency was a key objective, and LCD demonstrated significant improvements (Table 4).
- **Inference Speed:** LCD's inference time was 0.336 seconds, making it 3.3x faster than Diffuser-1D (1.11s) and 15x faster than Diffuser-2D (5.02s).
- **Training Speed:** LCD's high-level policy (HLP only) trained in 13.3 hours, faster than Diffuser-1D (20.8 hrs) and Diffuser-2D (49.2 hrs), and its full pipeline (including LLP pretraining) required 95.3 hours, still comparable or faster than some baselines like HULC (82 hrs) and SPIL (86 hrs).
- **Gradient Updates/Second:** LCD achieved 6.25 updates/sec, higher than Diffuser-1D (4) and Diffuser-2D (2.1), highlighting its computational efficiency.
- These gains are attributed to DDIM sampling, temporal abstraction (subsampling), and performing diffusion in a low-dimensional latent space (32 dimensions), rather than high-dimensional image or larger latent spaces.

#### 4.5. Necessity of Diffusion Model for High-Level Planning (Ablation Study)

An ablation study (Table 5) investigated whether diffusion is truly necessary by replacing the HLP with a simpler MLP or Transformer that predicts the next goal state directly.
- Both MLP and Transformer HLP variants significantly underperformed LCD across all horizon lengths, even when provided with ground truth validation language embeddings (removing the need for language generalization).
- This suggests that the performance gains observed in LCD stem from the diffusion model's superior ability to model the goal space and generate coherent long-range plans, rather than solely from the hierarchical structure or language conditioning.

#### 4.6. Robustness to Hyperparameters

The method exhibited robustness to variations in hyperparameters, including frame offset (temporal spacing of goal states) and model hidden dimensions. Increasing model size did not lead to overfitting.

#### 4.7. Critical Findings from Representation Analysis

- **Failure of Direct Latent Diffusion:** Direct application of latent diffusion models (Diffuser-1D and Diffuser-2D) for planning in original high-dimensional state spaces failed. Diffuser-1D, using a VAE trained from scratch, suffered from hallucinations and physically infeasible trajectories (Figure 2, Appendix K.1), hypothesized to be due to interpolation between low-density regions in the VAE's latent space. Diffuser-2D, using Stable Diffusion's VAE, exhibited loss of fine details and insufficient precision for control (Figure 2, Appendix K.2). This underscores the challenge of learning effective representations for high-dimensional control tasks.
- **Effectiveness of Goal-Conditioned RL Model Encoders:** The results indicate that using the latent space derived from the encoder of a deep goal-conditioned RL model (like HULC's visual encoder) provides a more effective and task-aware representation for hierarchical reinforcement learning. This shared policy encoder between the high and low-level policies proved crucial for the success and efficiency of generative modeling in hierarchical RL within the LCD framework.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Language Control Diffusion (LCD) framework represents a significant contribution to the field of robotic control and the development of generalist artificial agents. Its primary significance lies in demonstrating an effective and efficient method for scaling diffusion policies to address three fundamental challenges: high-dimensional state spaces (space), long execution horizons (time), and generalization to novel tasks.

The work addresses a critical limitation of previous diffusion-based planning approaches, such as Diffuser, which struggled with high-dimensional pixel inputs and lacked task-specific representation. By introducing a hierarchical structure, LCD strategically leverages the strengths of diffusion models—their capability for flexible, long-range temporal planning and compositionality—while mitigating their weaknesses in generating precise low-level details from raw high-dimensional data. This is achieved by offloading low-level control to a pre-trained, goal-conditioned policy and performing high-level planning within a low-dimensional, task-optimized latent space derived from that policy's encoder.

The demonstrated improvements in computational efficiency are also highly impactful. Through techniques like DDIM for accelerated inference, temporal abstraction via state subsampling, and the use of a compact latent representation, LCD achieves substantial reductions in both training and inference times (3.3x to 15x faster inference compared to other diffusion baselines). This efficiency makes advanced diffusion-based planning more accessible and practical for researchers and facilitates the deployment of more complex robot control systems.

Furthermore, LCD's empirical performance on challenging benchmarks like CALVIN and CLEVR-Robot, where it consistently outperforms prior state-of-the-art methods in multi-task success rates and average horizon length, highlights its robust generalization capabilities. Its ability to perform zero-shot generalization to novel tasks based on natural language instructions is a crucial step toward creating truly versatile robots that can understand and adapt to human commands in varied environments. The ablation studies confirm that the performance gains are attributable to the diffusion model's superior goal-space modeling, not just the hierarchical structure alone.

The theoretical justification, bounding suboptimality under mild assumptions, provides a principled basis for the hierarchical approach and the choice of latent representation.

In a broader context, LCD contributes directly to the longstanding goal of creating generalist agents capable of understanding and executing natural language commands in the physical world. Its findings emphasize the critical role of appropriate representation learning for generative models in control tasks. Potential future work could involve extending LCD to incorporate additional levels of hierarchy or scaling to even longer, more complex horizons, further pushing the boundaries of autonomous robot capabilities.
