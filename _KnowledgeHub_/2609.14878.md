# Real-World Reinforcement Learning with MPC Scaffolding for Dexterous Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> The paper uses sampling-based model predictive control as temporary scaffolding for real-world reinforcement learning on a 16-DoF Allegro hand. Twenty MPC trajectories seed an offline replay buffer and pretrain a Soft Actor-Critic actor and critic, while MPC intermittently guides online data collection before handing over. The learned policy reaches 100% success in 7 minutes and completes 1000 consecutive in-hand rotations without a drop.

## Problem

- Dexterous policies from human demonstrations need specialized multi-fingered teleoperation interfaces and heavy operator effort, impractical for high-DoF hands where suitable teleoperation often does not exist.
- Simulation-trained policies deploy without adaptation and suffer contact, friction and hardware mismatch; earlier model-guided RL only shapes rewards or reset states and still trains in simulation.
- Off-policy methods reusing prior data improve sample efficiency, but early real-world exploration still yields uninformative motion and frequent object drops requiring physical resets.

## Method

- Offline stage collects 20 sampling-based ==CEM MPC== trajectories on hardware, retained as a fixed replay buffer and relabeled with the online RL reward rather than the MPC objective.
- Pretraining uses ==behavior cloning== with constant base standard-deviation initialization, return-weighted episode sampling and epsilon-clipped targets for the actor, plus ==Soft-SARSA== over a 10-critic ensemble for the critic.
- Online ==Soft Actor-Critic== draws 30% of each minibatch from the MPC buffer, and an MPC-policy schedule raises policy-execution probability linearly to one over 6000 steps, switching in 40-step chunks.

## Results

- First 5/5 policy-only evaluation arrives after **7.4 minutes** of online RL with roughly **three** object drops; plain RL fails within **45 minutes** and incurs **181** drops.
- The learned policy rotates at **55.3 deg/s** versus **9.9** for direct MPC and **31.5** for sim-to-real PPO, and runs **1000** consecutive rotations drop-free over **110 minutes**.
- Ablations: removing pretraining raises time to success from **7** to **16 minutes** and drops from **3** to **17**; geometry adaptation lifts speed **45-84%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A reliable but slow model-based controller can bootstrap real-world RL, and the resulting policy can exceed the planner that guided it rather than inherit its ceiling.
> - Prior data plays separable roles: replay mainly cuts physical failures, pretraining accelerates early learning, and intermittent guidance mostly removes run-to-run variance.
> - With suboptimal demonstrations, keep the prior-data fraction low and switch controllers in temporally coherent chunks; step-level alternation or blending breaks multi-finger coordination.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Position Within the Broader Research Landscape

Dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered robotic hands is a difficult control problem because it involves high-dimensional actions, intermittent and nonlinear contacts, uncertain friction, object motion, and frequent failure modes such as dropping the object. Existing research has generally followed three routes.

The first route uses human demonstrations or teleoperation. Demonstrations provide task-relevant behavior and can substantially reduce the exploration burden, but dexterous teleoperation often requires specialized interfaces, skilled operators, and considerable data-collection effort. Human interventions during learning can also be difficult to scale.

The second route trains policies in simulation and transfers them to hardware. Simulation enables large amounts of inexpensive experience, but contact-rich manipulation is particularly sensitive to discrepancies in friction, mass, geometry, actuator behavior, sensing, and hand-object dynamics. Even with domain randomization, a policy that performs reliably in simulation can fail during physical deployment.

The third route uses real-world reinforcement learning, allowing the policy to adapt to the actual robot and object dynamics. Recent offline-to-online methods, replay-buffer methods such as RLPD and SERL, and reinforcement-learning fine-tuning have improved hardware sample efficiency. However, an initially untrained policy may spend much of its early interaction producing uncoordinated movements and dropping objects. These failures consume time and may require manual resets.

The paper places itself at the intersection of model-based control and real-world reinforcement learning. Its central distinction is that model predictive control is not used as the final controller, nor only as a source of reward shaping or reset states. Instead, sampling-based MPC is used temporarily as “scaffolding” in three ways: to generate a small prior dataset, to initialize the actor and critic, and to guide physical data collection during early online learning. The learned policy is then expected to replace the planner and improve beyond its performance.

This approach differs from methods in which MPC remains the execution-time controller. In the proposed framework, MPC supplies structured behavior while reinforcement learning ultimately learns a standalone policy adapted to hardware.

### 2. Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective is to determine whether sampling-based MPC can make real-world reinforcement learning practical for contact-rich dexterous manipulation without relying on human demonstrations, corrective interventions, or extensive simulation training.

The authors are motivated by three related problems. First, the initial policy in high-dimensional dexterous RL has a very low probability of discovering coordinated finger motions that both maintain contact and make task progress. Second, physical failures are more costly than simulation failures because an object drop may require manual resetting and interrupts training. Third, a useful planner may itself be too slow or suboptimal to serve as the final controller. The work therefore investigates whether a planner can provide reliable but imperfect behavior that accelerates learning without imposing a performance ceiling.

The main task is continuous in-hand rotation of an object using a 16-degree-of-freedom Allegro hand. The policy must repeatedly rotate a hexagonal object around its principal axis while retaining the object. The authors also examine whether a learned policy can adapt to new object geometries and whether the same framework applies to goal-conditioned reorientation, in which the object must be moved to a specified heading rather than rotated continuously.

The paper evaluates not only task success but also the cost of learning. It measures the time required to achieve reliable policy-only behavior, rotation speed, and the number of object drops during training. This reflects the practical concern that an RL method should reduce both data requirements and physical reset burden.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

#### 3.1. Overall Training Pipeline

Training proceeds in three stages.

First, a sampling-based MPC controller collects a small set of successful trajectories on the physical robot. The controller maintains a MuJoCo digital twin synchronized with measured robot and object states. It uses the cross-entropy method to sample short-horizon control sequences, evaluates them with a task-specific objective, and executes the first action of the selected sequence in receding-horizon fashion. Low-dimensional motion primitives are combined with joint-level residual sampling to adapt candidate motions to the current hand-object configuration.

Second, the MPC trajectories are used to pretrain an SAC actor-critic system. The actor is initialized by behavior cloning, while the critic is initialized using a Soft-SARSA objective.

Third, the agent performs online SAC training using physical interaction. The initial MPC data remain in a fixed replay buffer, and new transitions are stored in an online buffer. During early interaction, either the learned policy or MPC controls the robot. The probability of selecting the learned policy increases over time until MPC is eliminated from data collection.

Both controllers produce joint-level commands through the same interface, so transitions generated by either controller can be used by the same off-policy learner.

#### 3.2. Actor and Critic Pretraining

For actor pretraining, the authors do not jointly learn the stochastic policy’s mean and standard-deviation outputs. They set the final standard-deviation layer to a constant initial value, allowing the mean network and shared trunk to learn the demonstrated behavior while preserving controlled exploration.

Because the MPC demonstrations are not necessarily optimal, trajectories are sampled with probabilities determined by a softmax over episode returns. Higher-return demonstrations are therefore sampled more frequently. Demonstration actions are clipped slightly inside the valid action range before being transformed through the inverse hyperbolic tangent. The actor’s pre-tanh mean is trained using mean squared error against these transformed actions.

The critic is pretrained with a Soft-SARSA target. The target uses the reward from the demonstration, the value of the next demonstrated action, and an entropy term based on the behavior-cloned policy’s log probability. To reduce overestimation, the method maintains an ensemble of ten target critics and uses the minimum prediction from two randomly selected critics. The same ensemble strategy is retained during online SAC training.

#### 3.3. Mixed Replay and MPC Scheduling

During online learning, each mini-batch contains 30% of its samples from the fixed MPC dataset and 70% from newly collected online experience. This preserves task-relevant prior behavior while allowing new physical data to drive improvement beyond the planner.

MPC guidance is reduced according to a linear schedule. The initial probability of executing the learned policy is 0.5, and it increases to 1 over 6,000 environment steps, corresponding to approximately ten minutes. Controller selection occurs every 40 environment steps rather than at every step. The selected controller controls the entire chunk, which preserves temporally coherent finger motions and avoids rapid switching between unrelated action sequences. MPC and policy actions are not blended.

#### 3.4. Hardware and Task Definition

Experiments use a 16-DoF Allegro hand mounted on a Franka FR3 arm. The hand receives joint-position targets at 10 Hz. Object pose is measured using OptiTrack motion capture and is provided both to the policy and the synchronized MuJoCo model.

For continuous rotation, the observation contains hand joint positions and velocities, object position relative to the end effector, object orientation, rotation progress at the current and previous time steps, relative object motion, and the previous action. Actions are normalized 16-dimensional joint commands.

The reward combines incremental rotation progress, a time penalty, a success bonus for completing a full rotation, and a terminal penalty for dropping the object. Twenty successful MPC trajectories are collected for the initial hexagonal object, which has a 45 mm across-flats dimension. This collection requires approximately 12 minutes of physical interaction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

#### 4.1. Ablation Results

The complete method reaches its first evaluation with five successful policy-only rotations after approximately 7.4 minutes of online learning. It approaches its saturated rotation speed after approximately 15 minutes and averages about three object drops across three training runs, with individual runs reporting 1, 3, and 6 drops.

Actor-critic pretraining provides a substantial early advantage. The pretrained policy alone makes partial progress, reaching an average of 140 degrees over ten trials, but it does not complete a full rotation. With replay and online guidance, removing pretraining increases the time to the first 5/5 evaluation from approximately 7 to 16 minutes and increases average drops from about 3 to 17. The no-pretraining condition eventually reaches a similar rotation speed of approximately 60 degrees per second, indicating that pretraining primarily improves early learning efficiency rather than determining the final performance level.

Online MPC guidance also has an important effect. A configuration using guidance without pretraining reaches the first 5/5 evaluation after 18.0 ± 2.4 minutes and accumulates 35.0 ± 10.2 drops. In contrast, RL without any MPC components fails to achieve a successful rotation within 45 minutes and accumulates 181 drops.

Retaining MPC trajectories in replay reduces failures. Compared with guidance alone, adding the fixed MPC replay buffer reduces drops from approximately 35 to 17.3 ± 3.8, although it does not substantially reduce the time to reliable learning. Similarly, replay without pretraining or guidance reduces drops relative to RL-only training, from 181 to 98, but does not solve the task within the 45-minute window.

The no-guidance condition, which retains replay and pretraining, reaches reliable performance only after 25.8 ± 9.4 minutes and averages 18.0 ± 8.3 drops. These comparisons indicate that replay, pretraining, and online guidance serve different functions. Replay preserves useful task-directed data, pretraining establishes a favorable initial policy and value estimate, and online guidance improves the quality and consistency of new interaction.

#### 4.2. Comparison with MPC and Simulation-Trained RL

After 20 minutes of online learning, the resulting policy is compared with direct MPC execution and a PPO policy trained entirely in MuJoCo. The simulation policy uses 192 parallel environments, 15 million transitions, and domain randomization over mass, geometry, friction, initial pose, hand gains, observation noise, and action noise.

Direct MPC completes all 20 evaluated rotations at 9.9 ± 2.8 degrees per second. The learned hardware-trained policy completes all 20 at 55.3 ± 2.4 degrees per second, more than five times the MPC speed. The simulation-trained PPO policy achieves 100/100 success in simulation but only 20/28 successful hardware trials, with a speed of 31.5 ± 7.8 degrees per second. This result illustrates the transfer gap that the real-world learning procedure avoids for the tested task.

In a long-duration test, the learned policy completes 1,000 consecutive rotations without dropping the object. The run lasts approximately 110 minutes at a mean speed of 55.2 degrees per second.

#### 4.3. Adaptation to New Geometries

A policy trained on the nominal 45 mm hexagonal object is transferred to a 35 mm hexagonal object and a 55 mm circular object. The authors update the geometry in the MPC model and continue online learning, without collecting new offline MPC trajectories or reusing the original replay buffer.

The transferred policy achieves 80% success on its first evaluation with the 35 mm object and subsequently reaches 100%. It achieves 100% on the first evaluation with the circular object. Adaptation requires three drops for the smaller hexagon and eight for the circular object over the respective training runs.

Rotation speed increases through adaptation. For the circular object, speed rises from 45.5 ± 4.0 to 83.6 ± 11.9 degrees per second. For the smaller hexagon, it increases from 51.0 ± 13.7 to 74.1 ± 11.0 degrees per second. Both adapted policies complete 100 consecutive rotations without a drop in extended testing.

#### 4.4. Goal-Conditioned Reorientation

The framework is also applied to reorienting an object toward a commanded heading within a ±20-degree range. Approximately seven minutes of MPC interaction are used for initialization. During 45 minutes of online learning, the mean final error decreases from approximately 17 degrees to only a few degrees, while the robot accumulates 20 drops.

The final policy completes 100 successful trials in 103 attempts, corresponding to a 97.1% success rate. Across successful trials, the mean reaching speed is 20.50 ± 8.20 degrees per second. No object drops occur during this evaluation.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The paper demonstrates a way to combine model-based control and real-world RL without requiring the model-based controller to remain in charge at deployment. Its main contribution is procedural as well as algorithmic: a small amount of reliable MPC behavior is reused for replay, network initialization, and guided exploration, while the policy is allowed to improve through direct physical experience.

The results suggest that this design can reduce the practical cost of learning dexterous skills. The complete method reaches reliable rotation in minutes rather than tens of minutes, and the reduction in object drops is particularly relevant for systems requiring manual resets. The learned policy also exceeds the speed of the MPC controller that generated its initial experience, supporting the claim that MPC functions as temporary scaffolding rather than a performance limit.

The adaptation experiments indicate that a previously learned policy can provide a useful basis for new object geometries, with online MPC guidance supplying task-directed exploration. This may be relevant to manipulation systems operating across object variations. The goal-conditioned experiment further suggests that the method is not restricted to cyclic rotation.

Several limitations remain. The approach depends on a synchronized MuJoCo model, an object-state measurement system, and an MPC controller capable of producing sufficiently successful trajectories. Its performance may be more difficult to reproduce when object pose is not externally observable or when model mismatch is substantially larger. The experiments focus on a hand mounted to a stationary arm and on a limited set of reorientation tasks. The paper identifies coordinated arm-hand control and longer-horizon manipulation as future directions. Further evaluation across objects, hardware platforms, sensing conditions, and randomized task configurations would be needed to establish broader generality.
