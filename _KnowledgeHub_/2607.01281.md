# WaveLander: A Generalizable Hierarchical Control Framework for UAV Landing on Wave-Disturbed Platforms via Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> The WaveLander framework introduces a hierarchical control approach for autonomous UAV landing on wave-disturbed marine platforms, utilizing reinforcement learning for high-level vertical decision-making. It improves touchdown success rates by learning to adaptively time descents based on platform motion, achieving up to 76% success in simulations under 30-degree platform tilt.

## Problem

- Autonomous UAV landing on moving marine platforms is challenging due to stochastic, wave-induced platform motion and time-varying attitude.
- Existing model-based control methods are sensitive to model accuracy and online motion prediction, degrading under dynamic maritime conditions.
- Prior end-to-end learning methods for this task suffer from high training complexity, coarse control resolution, and integration difficulties with low-level flight controllers.

## Method

- A ==hierarchical control framework==, **WaveLander**, decouples high-level vertical landing decisions (via an ==RL policy==) from low-level flight stabilization and lateral tracking (via conventional controllers).
- An ==RL policy== trained with a compact observation space (relative height, vertical velocity, platform tilt, and tilt rate) directly outputs a scalar vertical velocity command.
- A novel, continuous ==attitude-aware reward function== encourages appropriate vertical behaviors (descent, hold, retreat) based on proximity and platform attitude, without explicit switching rules.

## Results

- **WaveLander** improved strict touchdown success rates in MuJoCo simulations from **8-33%** (constant-descent baseline) to **30-76%** across various platform motion severities (**30-60 degrees** max tilt).
- The learned policy demonstrated adaptive vertical command generation, initiating descent during favorable windows and holding/retreating when the platform was highly tilted.
- The framework successfully transferred from MuJoCo to a higher-fidelity Isaac Sim (SITL) without retraining and demonstrated real-world deployment feasibility with a physical UAV under moderate winds and waves.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Decoupling high-level vertical decision-making from low-level control simplifies the learning problem for robust dynamic landing, facilitating integration with existing flight controllers.
> - A compact, platform-relative observation and scalar vertical velocity action interface enables effective learning and direct transferability across simulation environments and to physical systems.
> - Continuously modulating vertical commands based on proximity and platform attitude through reward shaping allows the RL policy to learn adaptive, timing-sensitive landing strategies without explicit motion prediction.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The integration of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) and unmanned surface vehicles (USVs) in marine operations is expanding, enabling tasks such as search and rescue, offshore inspection, and environmental monitoring. A critical component for sustaining these operations is the ability of UAVs to autonomously land on moving marine platforms for recovery, recharging, and redeployment. This capability is challenging due to the stochastic nature of wave-induced platform motion, which introduces significant uncertainty in the relative pose and velocity between the UAV and the landing platform, particularly near touchdown.

Existing research into autonomous UAV landing on marine platforms generally falls into two categories: model-based control methods and learning-based methods.
- **Model-based control methods** typically formulate the problem as trajectory tracking or optimal control. These methods often rely on explicit models of UAV dynamics and require online prediction of platform motion to schedule landings during favorable windows, such as periods of minimal tilt. Examples include MPC-based frameworks that predict vessel motion or vision-guided strategies combining motion prediction with PID control. While offering interpretability and safety guarantees, these methods can be sensitive to the accuracy of models and predictions, which may degrade under realistic, highly dynamic maritime conditions.
- **Learning-based methods**, particularly those utilizing reinforcement learning (RL), offer an alternative by directly learning a feedback policy through interaction, thereby potentially avoiding the need for explicit future motion prediction and repeated online optimization. Prior RL approaches have demonstrated end-to-end control or continuous policy learning for landing on moving platforms. However, many of these approaches involve learning full end-to-end control policies from high-dimensional visual inputs, which can result in high training complexity, coarse control resolution, and challenges in integrating with established low-level flight controllers.

WaveLander addresses the limitations of both categories by proposing a hierarchical RL framework. It aims to reduce the complexity inherent in end-to-end learning while improving adaptability beyond strict model-based predictions. By decoupling the high-level vertical landing decision-making from low-level flight stabilization, the framework seeks to provide a more practical and robust solution for dynamic platform landing in marine environments, building upon the strengths of RL for timing-sensitive decision-making without the full complexity of end-to-end control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a robust and generalizable hierarchical control framework, named WaveLander, for autonomous UAV landing on wave-disturbed marine platforms. This framework aims to overcome the significant challenges posed by the marine environment, specifically:
- **Stochastic platform motion:** Waves induce unpredictable and varied heave, roll, and pitch motions in marine platforms.
- **Time-varying platform attitude:** The continuous changes in platform orientation create dynamic target conditions for landing.
- **Uncertain touchdown conditions:** The combination of relative motion and environmental disturbances leads to difficulties in achieving precise and safe contact.

The motivation for WaveLander stems from the limitations observed in existing approaches:
- **Model-based methods:** These methods, while offering precision, often demand accurate online motion prediction and complex online optimization. Their performance can be compromised by the inherent stochasticity of wave disturbances and sensor noise, leading to unreliable predictions over time.
- **End-to-end learning approaches:** While capable of adapting to uncertainty, these methods typically involve high-dimensional observation spaces (e.g., visual inputs) and broad action spaces, which results in high training complexity. Furthermore, they may offer limited interpretability and can be difficult to integrate with existing, reliable low-level flight control systems. This can restrict their applicability in safety-critical scenarios.

To address these limitations, WaveLander proposes a hierarchical architecture that capitalizes on the strengths of reinforcement learning for decision-making while leveraging conventional control for stabilization. The key objectives are realized through specific contributions:
- **Decoupling high-level decisions from low-level control:** The framework separates the vertical landing decision-making (handled by an RL policy) from low-level flight stabilization, attitude control, and lateral tracking (handled by a conventional flight controller). This aims to simplify the learning problem.
- **Compact policy interface:** The RL policy utilizes a compact, platform-relative observation (relative height, vertical velocity, platform tilt, and tilt rate) to output a scalar vertical velocity reference. This design is intended to reduce learning complexity and facilitate deployment.
- **Attitude-aware reward shaping:** A novel reward function is introduced to encourage desired behaviors (descent, hold, retreat) based on continuous assessments of proximity and platform attitude, rather than relying on explicit threshold-based switching rules. This promotes smooth and adaptive landing behavior.
- **Evaluation across diverse settings:** The framework is evaluated in randomized simulations, through software-in-the-loop (SITL) transfer, and in a representative real-world deployment test to demonstrate its robust performance and generalization capabilities under dynamic conditions.

By achieving these objectives, WaveLander seeks to provide a practical, interpretable, and robust solution for autonomous UAV landing on challenging wave-disturbed marine platforms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

WaveLander employs a hierarchical control framework that integrates reinforcement learning with conventional flight control principles.

#### 3.1. A. System Overview

The framework partitions the control task into two levels: a high-level policy learned via RL and a low-level flight controller. The RL policy determines a scalar vertical velocity reference ($v_{\text{ref},z,t}$), while the low-level controller is responsible for attitude stabilization, velocity tracking, and lateral alignment. This separation allows the RL component to focus on dynamic vertical decision-making without directly managing the intricacies of flight dynamics.

#### 3.2. B. Problem Formulation

The landing task is framed as an episodic, discounted Markov Decision Process (MDP), $M = (S, A, P, R, \gamma)$.
- **State Space (S):** Represents the underlying landing-related conditions.
- **Action Space (A):** Consists of a scalar high-level action ($a_t = \pi_{\psi}(o_t)$) that is directly mapped to the vertical velocity reference.
- **Transition Dynamics (P):** Governed by the combined dynamics of the UAV, the low-level controller, and the wave-disturbed platform.
- **Reward Function (R):** Designed to shape the desired landing behavior.
- **Discount Factor ($\gamma$):** Balances immediate and future rewards.

The objective is to learn policy parameters ($\psi$) that maximize the expected discounted return, with the policy conditioned on a compact platform-relative observation ($o_t$).

#### 3.3. C. Wave-Disturbed Platform Model

For training and evaluation, the landing platform's motion is simulated as a kinematically driven rigid body experiencing heave, roll, and pitch disturbances. A randomized wave generator models these disturbances as a superposition of $K$ sinusoidal components. Each component has an amplitude ($A_i$), angular frequency ($\omega_i$), and phase ($\phi_i$). Frequencies are shared, but amplitudes and phases are sampled independently for each axis to produce varying tilt directions.
To enhance generalization, wave parameters (amplitudes, frequencies, phases) are resampled at the start of each episode, with larger amplitudes assigned to lower frequencies. Temporally correlated stochastic variations are added using an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, and the resulting signal is smoothed to prevent abrupt motions before being applied to heave, roll, and pitch. Yaw is fixed in the model.

#### 3.4. D. Platform-Relative Perception and Control

The UAV's perception of the platform-relative state relies on an AprilTag mounted on the landing platform.
- **Relative Height Calculation:** The AprilTag detector provides a relative pose in the camera frame, which is transformed into the UAV's body frame. By accounting for the landing gear's position and transforming to the gravity-aligned world frame, the relative vertical height ($h$) from the landing gear to the pad surface is computed.
- **Lateral Motion Control:** A proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller manages lateral motion, tracking the platform's horizontal position and generating a desired lateral velocity ($v_{\text{ref},xy}$).
- **Vertical Motion Control:** The RL policy directly provides the desired vertical velocity ($v_{\text{ref},z}$), which is then combined with the lateral velocity commands to form the overall desired velocity vector ($v_{\text{ref}}$).
- **Low-Level Flight Control:** A low-level PID controller with gravity compensation calculates the desired acceleration, which is then normalized to determine the desired thrust direction ($z_{\text{des}}$). This is fed to an SE(3) attitude controller to compute the required body torques and collective thrust.

#### 3.5. E. Observation and Action Design

The RL policy's observation vector ($o$) is compact, consisting of four platform-relative features:
- Relative height ($h$)
- Relative vertical velocity ($\dot{h}$)
- Platform tilt magnitude ($\theta_{\text{tilt}}$), derived from the platform's roll ($\phi$) and pitch ($\theta$) angles
- Rate of change of platform tilt ($\dot{\theta}_{\text{tilt}}$)
The action ($a$) output by the policy is a scalar, which is clipped to a range of [-0.45, 0.45] m/s to serve as the vertical velocity reference ($v_{\text{ref},z}$).

#### 3.6. F. Reward Design

The reward function ($R_t$) is structured to encourage timing-aware vertical decision-making without explicit switching rules:
$R_t = R_{\text{prog},t} + R_{\text{td},t} + R_{\text{rate},t} + R_{\text{time},t}$
1. **Proximity and Attitude Assessment:** Two continuous gate functions, $g_h$ (height gate) and $g_{\theta}$ (attitude gate), are used. $g_h$ increases as the UAV nears the platform, and $g_{\theta}$ decreases when platform tilt exceeds a soft safe threshold ($\theta_{\text{safe}}$).
2. **Descent–Retreat Modulation ($R_{\text{prog},t}$):** These gates combine into an adaptive coefficient $\alpha_t = 1 - 2g_h(1 - g_{\theta})$. This coefficient dynamically modulates the reward for vertical motion ($\dot{h}_t$).
 - When far from the platform or close to a level platform, $\alpha_t \approx 1$, encouraging descent ($\dot{h}_t < 0$).
 - When close to a highly tilted platform, $\alpha_t \approx -1$, penalizing further descent and rewarding upward retreat ($\dot{h}_t > 0$).
3. **Touchdown Quality Evaluation ($R_{\text{td},t}$):** This term rewards contact based on both the platform's attitude and the vertical impact speed at touchdown. Highest rewards are given for near-level, low-speed touchdowns, with graded rewards for less ideal contacts.
4. **Command Smoothness and Delay Suppression ($R_{\text{rate},t}, R_{\text{time},t}$):** An action-rate penalty ($R_{\text{rate},t}$) discourages abrupt changes in vertical commands, and a small time penalty ($R_{\text{time},t}$) discourages excessive hovering before touchdown.

#### 3.7. G. Training Setup

- **Simulator:** NVIDIA Isaac Lab and MuJoCo environments are used for training and evaluation.
- **Algorithm:** Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) with the RSL-RL implementation.
- **Network Architecture:** GRU-based recurrent networks with ELU activations for both actor and critic, allowing for short-term memory of observations.
- **Normalization:** Running mean-standard deviation normalization is applied to policy inputs.
- **Domain Randomization:** Critical for generalization, this includes randomized low-level velocity controller damping gains, random action delays (0-3 steps), $\pm 30\%$ mass perturbations, and resampled wave parameters (number of sinusoidal components, frequency range, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck noise intensity) in each episode.
- **Curriculum Learning:** A lightweight curriculum progressively exposes the policy to larger platform tilt magnitudes to improve training stability.
- **Observation Noise:** Gaussian noise is added to actor inputs (relative position, vertical velocity, pad attitude) to enhance robustness against state-estimation errors.
- **PPO Parameters:** 30,000 parallel environments, 64 steps per iteration, 1,000 iterations, adaptive KL schedule for learning rate (initial $1 \times 10^{-3}$), $\gamma = 0.995$, $\lambda = 0.97$, PPO clip range of 0.2.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of WaveLander was conducted across three environments: MuJoCo simulation for quantitative comparison, Isaac Sim for Software-in-the-Loop (SITL) transfer, and a real-world deployment test.

#### 4.1. A. Policy Behavior Analysis

Analysis of the learned policy's behavior in simulation under various wave disturbances demonstrated adaptive vertical command generation.
- **Case 1 (Mild motion):** The policy commanded a smooth descent, leading to touchdown when the platform remained within safe tilt bounds.
- **Case 2 (Unfavorable initial attitude):** When the UAV started near a highly tilted platform, the policy initially held its position or retreated upward before initiating descent during a favorable, safer window.
- **Case 3 (Strong motion):** Under aggressive platform motion, which produced brief safe windows, the policy primarily maintained descent but commanded retreat-like motion when the UAV was close to an unfavorably tilted platform, landing when a safe window emerged.

These observations indicate that the policy learns to modulate its vertical command based on relative height, vertical velocity, platform attitude, and tilt rate, effectively learning timing-sensitive behaviors implicitly from the reward design rather than explicit predictive models.

#### 4.2. B. Experimental Setup

The framework was evaluated in:
- **MuJoCo simulation:** For quantitative baseline comparison against a constant-descent method under randomized wave-disturbed platform motions.
- **Isaac Sim SITL:** To assess transferability to a high-fidelity simulation stack (Pegasus, ArduPilot-based flight controller) using ROS 2 for integration, without additional training.
- **Real-world deployment:** A representative test on a physical UAV system to demonstrate practical implementation feasibility under dynamic platform motion and moderate wind conditions.

All methods shared the same hierarchical structure, with the low-level flight controller handling attitude stabilization and lateral tracking, isolating the impact of the vertical landing strategy.

#### 4.3. C. MuJoCo Evaluation and Baseline Comparison

Quantitative evaluation in MuJoCo involved comparing WaveLander with a constant-descent baseline (fixed vertical velocity of -0.3 m/s) using two UAV configurations (CF2 and X2). A successful touchdown was defined by a touchdown attitude mismatch ($\theta_{\text{td}}$) of $\leq 0.1$ radians. Three platform motion cases were tested, with maximum per-axis roll/pitch ranges of $30^\circ$, $40^\circ$, and $60^\circ$.

The results in Table II demonstrate WaveLander's improved strict touchdown success rate:
- **CF2 Configuration:** Success rates increased from 33%, 14%, and 8% (constant descent) to 66%, 60%, and 39% (WaveLander) for the $30^\circ$, $40^\circ$, and $60^\circ$ motion cases, respectively.
- **X2 Configuration:** Success rates improved from 27%, 21%, and 5% (constant descent) to 76%, 54%, and 30% (WaveLander) for the corresponding motion cases.

Cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) in Fig. 5 further illustrate that WaveLander consistently shifts the distribution of touchdown attitude mismatch towards smaller values, especially for $30^\circ$ and $40^\circ$ motion cases. This indicates a higher frequency of favorable platform attitudes at contact. While the performance gap narrowed under the aggressive $60^\circ$ stress-test, WaveLander still maintained a higher success rate. These findings suggest that the learned vertical policy significantly improves touchdown timing by being more selective about landing conditions compared to a constant descent rate.

#### 4.4. D. Transfer to Isaac Sim with SITL

The policy, trained in MuJoCo, was directly transferred to Isaac Sim (integrated with Pegasus and ArduPilot via ROS 2) without any retraining or parameter tuning. The use of the same compact four-dimensional observation and scalar vertical velocity command validated that the learned high-level command is executable within a different, higher-fidelity simulation environment integrated with an autopilot stack.

#### 4.5. E. Real-World Deployment

A representative real-world test was performed with a physical UAV under moderate easterly winds (10-18 kt gusts) and waves (0.8-0.9 m height, 5 s period). The experiment demonstrated lateral alignment, vertical descent, and touchdown, showcasing the feasibility of implementing the compact platform-relative observation and vertical velocity command interface on a physical system. This test served as a deployment-oriented validation of the interface and control stack, with larger-scale field validation noted as future work.

#### 4.6. F. Discussion

The experimental results consistently indicate that WaveLander's primary improvement over a constant-descent strategy lies in its ability to learn adaptive touchdown timing. The hierarchical design, which restricts the learned component to a scalar vertical velocity reference, facilitates compatibility with conventional low-level flight controllers and mitigates the sim-to-real gap. The policy's recurrent architecture allows it to implicitly exploit short-term observation history to inform timing decisions without an explicit predictive model of future platform motion.

Current limitations include the use of a lightweight wave-platform model, which primarily generates repeatable heave-tilt disturbances rather than comprehensive marine hydrodynamics. Detailed near-surface aerodynamic effects, sensing noise, and latency were also not modeled extensively. While the hierarchical design and low-speed commands help reduce sensitivity to these factors, future work is planned to address perception uncertainty, richer temporal modeling, and higher-fidelity platform and aerodynamic effects.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The WaveLander framework represents an advancement in autonomous UAV landing capabilities, particularly for dynamic marine environments. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key aspects:

- **Enhanced Robustness and Adaptability:** By learning an adaptive vertical control policy through reinforcement learning, WaveLander demonstrates improved robustness in landing on platforms experiencing stochastic, wave-induced motions. The policy's ability to selectively descend, hold, or retreat based on current platform attitude and proximity allows for safer and more consistent touchdowns compared to non-adaptive strategies. This is crucial for operations where platform motion is unpredictable.

- **Reduced Learning Complexity and Improved Interpretability:** The hierarchical design effectively reduces the complex, high-dimensional problem of end-to-end UAV control to a more manageable, low-dimensional vertical decision-making task for the RL agent. This simplifies the learning process, reduces training complexity, and potentially improves the interpretability of the learned policy's behavior, making it more amenable for deployment in safety-critical applications.

- **Compatibility with Existing Flight Control Systems:** By outputting a scalar vertical velocity reference, WaveLander can be readily integrated as a high-level module with conventional, well-established low-level flight controllers (e.g., ArduPilot). This architectural compatibility minimizes the need for extensive redesign of existing UAV control stacks and eases the transition from simulation to real-world deployment.

- **Generalizability to Unforeseen Disturbances:** The training methodology, which incorporates extensive domain randomization of wave parameters, mass perturbations, and action delays, contributes to the policy's ability to generalize to unseen disturbance conditions. This generalizability is vital for real-world marine environments where specific wave patterns and environmental factors are highly variable.

- **Foundational for Future Marine Robotic Systems:** The demonstrated feasibility of deploying a learned hierarchical control policy on a physical UAV in a dynamic marine setting provides a foundation for more advanced heterogeneous robotic systems. Reliable autonomous landing is a prerequisite for long-duration cooperative missions involving UAVs and USVs, such as extended surveillance, search and rescue, or environmental monitoring where human intervention is limited.

Future work outlined by the authors, including addressing perception uncertainty, richer temporal modeling, and higher-fidelity aerodynamic effects, indicates a path toward making such systems even more robust and capable for widespread adoption in challenging maritime operations.
