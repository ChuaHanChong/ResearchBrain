# Beyond the Best Guess: Improving LLM Solution Coverage with Evolution Strategies

> [!summary] Summary
> Compares Evolution Strategies (ES) against RL (GRPO) for LLM post-training on math reasoning. RL sharpens pass@1 but collapses output distribution, shrinking pass@k coverage; ES, optimizing in weight space via random perturbations, raises both pass@1 and pass@k across 1.5B-32B models, giving broader solution coverage for test-time scaling.

## Problem

- RLVR post-training concentrates probability mass on high-reward outputs, narrowing output-distribution support and pruning low-probability but correct solutions.
- For sufficiently large k, base models can outperform RL-fine-tuned models at pass@k, showing RL actively harms test-time-scaling coverage despite raising pass@1.
- Existing collapse mitigations (KL penalties, reference resets, wider clipping thresholds, multi-objective reward reformulations) add hyperparameters and don't remove the core tension between policy-gradient sharpening and distributional diversity.

## Method

- Large-scale empirical comparison of ==Evolution Strategies (ES)== (via the ES-at-Scale library, population-based ==Gaussian weight perturbations==) against ==GRPO==-style RL (via VERL) across Qwen2.5-Instruct, Qwen3, and Qwen2.5-Math models from 1.5B to 32B parameters.
- ==Pass@k== evaluation protocol using the unbiased Chen et al. estimator on GSM8K, MATH500, Olympiad Bench, and Minerva to directly measure solution coverage versus RL and SOTA checkpoints (OatZero, SimpleRL-Zoo).
- Mechanistic diagnostic suite: per-prompt accuracy-bin distributions, progression/regression counts (knowledge gained vs. lost), and ==Shannon-entropy== analysis of answer distributions to explain why ES preserves coverage.

## Results

- ES beats RL pass@k on GSM8K across all **six** Qwen2.5/Qwen3 model scales, with the crossover typically at **k=2** and RL eventually falling below the base model at large k.
- On Qwen2.5-Math-7B, ES has only **3** regressions vs. **17** for RL on MATH500 (**15** vs. **49** on Olympiad Bench, **6** vs. **40** on Minerva), while also producing more progressions (**6** vs. **3**, **56** vs. **33**, **22** vs. **16** respectively).
- Self-consistency voting mirrors pass@k trends: ES accuracy overtakes RL as k grows on MATH500, Olympiad Bench, and Minerva, and RL's top-voted answer is sometimes worse than the base model's.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Distribution collapse is a general property of policy-gradient RL post-training, not an artifact of one algorithm, since it recurs across model families and scales from 1.5B to 32B.
> - Optimizing in weight space (ES) rather than action space (RL) yields a fundamentally different training pressure toward flat, robust parameter regions instead of a single sharpened output mode.
> - When ES fails it preserves higher answer entropy (uncertainty) versus RL's confident-but-wrong failures, making ES failures more correctable by additional test-time compute.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly being applied to discovery domains, such as mathematics and science, where their reasoning capabilities are leveraged to solve complex, open-ended problems. Traditional evaluation often focuses on a model's "best guess" (pass@1 accuracy). However, advancements in test-time compute (TTS) have shifted the paradigm, enabling models to explore broader solution spaces by generating multiple candidate solutions. This approach is quantified by the pass@k metric, which measures the probability that at least one correct solution exists within `k` samples.

The current standard for post-training LLMs involves Reinforcement Learning (RL), particularly Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). While RLVR has been effective in improving pass@1 performance across various tasks like math and coding, it has a notable drawback: distribution collapse. RL updates tend to concentrate the model's output distribution around high-reward outputs, narrowing its support and often pruning valid but lower-probability solutions. This phenomenon can lead to a decrease in overall solution coverage, making test-time scaling less effective. In some cases, RL-fine-tuned models have even been observed to perform worse than their base models at higher `k` values, highlighting a fundamental limitation for discovery tasks requiring diverse exploration.

Evolution Strategies (ES) has emerged as an alternative post-training technique. Unlike RL, which operates in the action space using policy gradients to directly increase the log-probability of high-reward sequences, ES operates in the parameter space. It optimizes model parameters through random perturbations in weight space, aiming to maximize expected reward over a distribution of weight-space perturbations. This approach is hypothesized to foster a more robust solution distribution, preserving broader support and thereby maintaining greater solution coverage. Previous work has shown ES achieving competitive performance with RL in reasoning tasks, even with relatively small population sizes, suggesting its potential as a scalable and effective alternative. This paper investigates ES's suitability for contexts where solution diversity, as measured by pass@k, is critical, particularly contrasting it with the established RL post-training paradigm.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the observed limitations of Reinforcement Learning (RL) in post-training Large Language Models (LLMs) for discovery problems, specifically its tendency to cause "distribution collapse." While RL-trained models often achieve higher pass@1 accuracy (the probability of the single best guess being correct), this comes at the cost of narrowing the model's output distribution. This narrowing reduces solution coverage, which is detrimental in domains where problems are complex, and a single "best guess" may not suffice. Instead, these domains benefit from the ability to explore a diverse set of candidate solutions through increased test-time compute, a process quantified by the pass@k metric. The paper explicitly notes that standard RL can lead to a situation where a base model might even outperform an RL-trained model at sufficiently large `k`, indicating active harm to solution coverage.

In response, the key objective is to empirically investigate whether Evolution Strategies (ES), a gradient-free, population-based post-training method, can overcome these limitations by preserving or enhancing solution coverage. The authors hypothesize that ES's optimization in parameter space, rather than action space, leads to a broader and more robust output distribution, making it better suited for settings where pass@k performance is crucial.

The specific contributions outlined to achieve this objective are:
1. **Comprehensive Evaluation:** To conduct a detailed evaluation of ES as an LLM post-training technique across a wide range of model scales (from 1.5 billion to 32 billion parameters) and multiple model families (Qwen2.5-Instruct, Qwen3, Qwen2.5-Math). This aims to demonstrate the generality of ES's benefits.
2. **Establish Superiority for Solution Diversity:** To provide empirical evidence that ES consistently improves pass@k performance over RL-trained models across these diverse models and scales, thereby establishing ES as a superior strategy for settings requiring solution diversity.
3. **Mechanistic Insights:** To analyze the underlying reasons for the observed improvements in solution coverage. This involves a systematic examination of the output distributions of ES-trained models compared to base and RL-trained models, including analyses of accuracy distributions, progressions and regressions (knowledge gained vs. lost), and answer entropy.
4. **Demonstrate Downstream Value:** To illustrate the practical value of the higher-quality output distributions produced by ES in downstream applications, specifically through test-time scaling experiments in standard math benchmarks using self-consistency (voting).

Overall, the motivation is to provide a robust alternative to RL for post-training LLMs in discovery problems and other domains where the ability to generate a diverse set of valid solutions is critical for achieving high performance.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research employed a comparative experimental design to assess the effectiveness of Evolution Strategies (ES) against Reinforcement Learning (RL) for LLM post-training, focusing on solution coverage quantified by the pass@k metric. The methodology covered model selection, training procedures, evaluation benchmarks, and analytical techniques.

**Post-training Methods Compared:**
- **Evolution Strategies (ES):** Implemented using the ES-at-Scale library. ES optimizes model parameters by sampling perturbations in weight space, evaluating the performance of perturbed models, and aggregating normalized rewards to update parameters. The population size was set to 32.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Primarily implemented using the VERL library, utilizing policy gradient methods like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) in a Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) setting. Publicly available state-of-the-art RL checkpoints from SimpleRL-Zoo and OatZero were also used for larger models.
- **Base Models:** Untrained or pre-trained versions of the LLMs served as baselines for comparison.

**Key Metric: Pass@k:**
The primary performance metric was pass@k, defined as the probability that at least one correct solution exists within `k` samples drawn from the model's output distribution. An unbiased low-variance estimator for pass@k (Chen et al., 2021) was utilized: `pass@k = E_x~D [1 - (n-c choose k) / (n choose k)]`, where `n` is the total samples per problem, and `c` is the correct responses for problem `x`. This metric captures solution coverage, with `k=1` corresponding to standard accuracy.

**Mathematical Reasoning Domain and Benchmarks:**
The experiments focused on mathematical reasoning problems, varying in difficulty.
- **Training Datasets:** GSM8K (math word problems) and MATH (levels 3-5, a more challenging dataset).
- **Evaluation Datasets:**
 - For models trained on GSM8K: GSM8K test set.
 - For models trained on MATH: MATH500, Olympiad Bench, and Minerva (all subsets or distinct benchmarks related to mathematical reasoning).
- **Inference Parameters:** At test time, responses were limited to 16,384 tokens, sampled with a temperature of 0.6 and top-p of 0.95, consistent with prior work.

**Models Evaluated:**
A range of Qwen model families and sizes were used to ensure generality of findings:
- **GSM8K Evaluation:** Qwen2.5-Instruct (1.5B, 3B, 7B parameters) and Qwen3 (1.7B, 4B, 8B parameters).
- **MATH Evaluation:** Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Qwen2.5-14B, and Qwen2.5-32B parameters.

**Training Details and Infrastructure:**
- Hyperparameters for RL (VERL) and ES (ES-at-Scale) were specified, including optimizer (AdamW), learning rate (1e-6 for RL, `α/2` for ES), batch sizes, and maximum response lengths. For ES, a `σ` of 0.001 was used.
- Training involved 500 total steps for both methods.
- GPU Infrastructure: NVIDIA H200 GPUs for GSM8K experiments and NVIDIA B200 GPUs for MATH experiments.
- Chat Templates: Specific Qwen chat templates were used for system prompts, incorporating distinct answer formats (e.g., "####" for GSM8K, "\boxed{}" for MATH).

**Analytical Techniques for Solution Coverage:**
Beyond pass@k, several analyses were conducted to understand the mechanistic origins of coverage differences:
- **Accuracy Distribution Analysis:** Histograms showing the frequency of prompts falling into different accuracy intervals (e.g., bin 0.0 for all incorrect samples, bin 1.0 for all correct samples, etc.) over `k` responses.
- **Regressions vs. Progressions:** Quantifying the number of problems the base model solved correctly but the fine-tuned model failed (regressions) versus problems the base model failed but the fine-tuned model solved (progressions).
- **Answer Entropy Analysis:** Calculating Shannon entropy over the unique final answers generated for each problem, particularly for regressions, to assess the diversity of incorrect answers.
- **Self-Consistency (Voting):** To evaluate downstream utility in non-verifiable settings, models were assessed on their ability to identify a correct answer among `k` samples using plurality voting. This measures how well the high-quality output distributions translate to improved performance in practical TTS applications.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several consistent findings across diverse models, scales, and benchmarks, demonstrating the comparative advantages of Evolution Strategies (ES) over Reinforcement Learning (RL) in maintaining and improving solution coverage.

**Pass@k Performance:**
- **Consistent ES Superiority:** ES consistently improved pass@k performance compared to RL-trained models across all tested model families (Qwen2.5-Instruct, Qwen3, Qwen2.5-Math), scales (1.5B to 32B parameters), and benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH500, Olympiad Bench, Minerva).
- **RL Distribution Collapse Manifested:** RL pass@k curves exhibited earlier saturation compared to ES. For Qwen2.5-Instruct models on GSM8K, the base model even surpassed the RL-trained model at sufficiently large `k`, empirically confirming prior observations of RL's distribution collapse. This indicates that RL, while improving pass@1, narrows the solution space, making further sampling less effective.
- **ES Preserves Coverage:** In contrast, ES models maintained continuous improvement in pass@k with increasing `k` and were never outperformed by their respective base models. This suggests that ES post-training improves pass@1 accuracy without sacrificing the broad output distribution support of the base model. The advantage of ES over RL generally grew with `k`, indicating its increasing benefit with larger test-time compute budgets.

**Solution Coverage Analysis:**
- **Accuracy Distribution Shifts:**
 - Both ES and RL increased the frequency of prompts where all `k` responses were correct (bin 1.0) relative to the base model. However, ES's improvements were more distributed across the (0.9, 1.0] range.
 - Crucially, RL training led to an increase in the frequency of prompts where none of the `k` responses were correct (bin 0.0) relative to the base model. This phenomenon signifies that RL renders some previously solvable problems entirely unsolvable, imposing a hard ceiling on achievable pass@k.
 - In contrast, ES consistently reduced the frequency of prompts in bin 0.0, indicating improved solution coverage by minimizing unsolvable problems.
- **Progressions and Regressions:**
 - **Increased Progressions for ES:** ES significantly increased the number of "progressions" (problems the base model got wrong but the fine-tuned model solved) compared to RL across all benchmarks. This demonstrates that ES effectively adds new problem-solving capabilities.
 - **Reduced Regressions for ES:** ES resulted in significantly fewer "regressions" (problems the base model got right but the fine-tuned model got wrong) compared to RL. This suggests that ES is less destructive to the existing knowledge embedded in the base model, mitigating catastrophic forgetting. This dual advantage (more progressions, fewer regressions) indicates ES finds parameter space regions that are both more capable and more knowledge-preserving.
- **Answer Entropy in Failure Modes:**
 - When experiencing regressions (failures), ES models maintained higher entropy over their answer distributions compared to RL models. This indicates that ES failures are characterized by preserved uncertainty, meaning it generates a more diverse set of incorrect answers rather than confidently converging on a single wrong answer.
 - A notable fraction of RL regressions (over 25%) exhibited entropy close to zero, meaning the model collapsed to a small set of confidently incorrect responses.
 - This asymmetry suggests that ES's failure modes are more amenable to correction through increased test-time compute, as it offers a broader range of potentially useful, though incorrect, solutions.

**Test-Time Scaling Beyond Pass@k: Voting (Self-Consistency):**
- **Improved Voting Accuracy:** The higher-quality output distributions produced by ES translated into improved performance in non-verifiable test settings. When employing self-consistency (plurality voting among `k` samples), ES-trained models achieved higher voting accuracy than both RL and base models as `k` increased.
- **RL Degradation in Voting:** On some benchmarks, the maximum probability answers from RL were observed to be worse than those from the base model, further supporting the observation of distribution degradation. This suggests that ES is generally more beneficial for practical test-time scaling methods beyond basic pass@k.

In summary, the results consistently demonstrate that ES effectively addresses the distribution collapse issue observed with RL, leading to consistently higher pass@k, improved solution coverage, greater knowledge preservation, and more robust failure characteristics, which translate into better performance in practical test-time scaling scenarios.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research highlights a significant advancement in the post-training of Large Language Models (LLMs), particularly for applications in discovery domains such as science, mathematics, and coding. The findings establish Evolution Strategies (ES) as a robust and superior alternative to Reinforcement Learning (RL) in scenarios where diverse solution coverage and effective test-time scaling are critical.

The primary significance lies in addressing the "distribution collapse" phenomenon observed in RL-trained LLMs. While RL effectively sharpens a model's ability to produce a single correct answer (pass@1), this work demonstrates that it often does so at the expense of pruning the broader solution space. This limitation makes RL-trained models less effective in discovery problems where exploring a wide array of potential solutions is essential. ES, by contrast, has been shown to simultaneously improve pass@1 accuracy *and* preserve or enhance the overall solution coverage, ensuring that models can effectively leverage increased test-time compute.

The potential impacts are multifaceted:

1. **Enhanced Discovery and Problem Solving:** In scientific and mathematical research, LLMs can accelerate discovery by generating novel hypotheses, proofs, or code solutions. By maintaining broad solution coverage, ES-trained models are more likely to generate a correct or innovative solution within a given number of samples, thereby expanding the frontier of automated discovery.
2. **More Robust LLM Deployment:** The findings suggest that ES leads to more knowledge-preserving models, exhibiting fewer "regressions" (losing knowledge the base model possessed) and more "progressions" (gaining new knowledge) than RL. This characteristic makes ES-trained models potentially more robust and reliable when deployed in dynamic or open-ended environments, as they are less prone to catastrophic forgetting.
3. **Improved Test-Time Scaling (TTS) Effectiveness:** The research validates that ES's higher-quality output distributions directly translate to better performance in practical TTS methods like self-consistency (voting). This implies that ES can unlock greater value from increased inference-time compute budgets, making LLMs more powerful in real-world applications where direct verifiers might not always be available.
4. **Foundation for Future LLM Optimization:** The gradient-free nature of ES opens new avenues for directly optimizing complex, non-differentiable objectives that are relevant to TTS. For example, future work could involve directly optimizing pass@k, self-consistency voting accuracy, or multi-step agentic success rates during training, further aligning the training objective with the desired downstream behavior.
5. **Addressing Core LLM Limitations:** By demonstrating a method that mitigates distribution collapse and preserves uncertainty in failure modes, ES contributes to a deeper understanding of how LLMs learn and fail. The observation that ES fails with preserved uncertainty, rather than confidently converging on incorrect answers, makes its outputs more amenable to human intervention or further computational exploration.

In conclusion, this work positions Evolution Strategies as a state-of-the-art post-training approach for LLMs, especially in contexts demanding solution diversity. Its ability to simultaneously enhance accuracy and maintain broad solution coverage offers a more effective foundation for developing LLMs capable of tackling complex, open-ended problems that benefit from extensive exploration.
