# Take A Step Back: Rethinking the Two Stages in Visual Reasoning

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Shanghai Jiao Tong University propose a two-stage framework for visual reasoning, disentangling task-specific symbolization from a shared, generalizable reasoner. This approach allows a simple MLP reasoner to achieve performance comparable to state-of-the-art ad-hoc models across diverse visual reasoning benchmarks, demonstrating improved generalization on out-of-domain tasks when trained multi-domain.

## Problem

- Current end-to-end visual reasoning models entangle recognition and reasoning, leading to poor generalization and susceptibility to data biases.
- Existing methods struggle with efficiency and often remain confined to specific datasets, failing to achieve broad cross-domain generalization.
- Multi-Modal Large Language Models (MLLMs) perform well on text but still struggle with complex visual reasoning tasks requiring precise logical operations.

## Method

- A two-stage framework is proposed, explicitly separating ==task-specific symbolization== (via dedicated encoders) from a ==shared logical reasoning component==.
- The framework uses separate encoders for different data modalities and tasks, feeding their symbolic outputs into a single, unified ==MLP reasoner==.
- Comprehensive experiments systematically evaluate optimal symbolization depth, suitable reasoner architectures, and the generalization capabilities of the shared reasoner via ==multi-domain training==.

## Results

- The proposed "Shared-Reasoner-Only" architecture consistently outperformed other design paradigms, validating the core hypothesis of separating symbolization and sharing reasoning.
- A simple Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) reasoner, when combined with task-specific symbol encoders, achieved performance comparable to or surpassing "lite" ad-hoc state-of-the-art models across various visual reasoning benchmarks.
- Training the shared reasoner on an increasing number of diverse datasets consistently improved its generalization performance on unseen, out-of-domain tasks, confirming the "approximation principle."
- While powerful, MiniGPT-4 struggled with precise logical reasoning in complex visual puzzles compared to the MLP reasoner, highlighting limitations of current MLLMs in this specific area.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Disentangling symbolization (perception) from logical reasoning leads to superior generalization capabilities in visual reasoning systems.
> - The optimal depth and design for the symbolization stage vary significantly across different visual tasks, necessitating task-specific encoders.
> - Surprisingly, a simple Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) can serve as an effective and efficient shared reasoner when provided with well-formed symbolic inputs.
> - Training a shared reasoner on diverse, cross-domain datasets significantly enhances its ability to generalize to unseen, out-of-domain tasks.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Visual reasoning is a cornerstone of artificial intelligence, aiming to enable machines to understand and draw conclusions from visual information in a manner akin to human cognition. Historically, significant research has been dedicated to this field, with a proliferation of deep learning models and datasets designed to tackle various visual reasoning tasks, such as Visual Question Answering (VQA), physical prediction, and abstract puzzles.

However, the authors identify a critical limitation pervading much of the existing visual reasoning landscape: the tendency for current models to "entangle the recognition and reasoning phrases via end-to-end deep learning models." This entanglement leads to several issues:
- **Lack of Generalization:** Models become heavily task-specific and tend to "data bias fitting" rather than achieving cross-domain reasoning. This is exacerbated by the fact that rigorous visual reasoning datasets, especially those requiring complex rule or relation annotations, are often small.
- **Inefficiency:** Pursuing a single, monolithic model for both symbol recognition and logical reasoning simultaneously is challenging and often inefficient. The authors point out that even recent Multi-Modal Large Language Models (MLLMs), despite their vast parameters, "still struggle with visual reasoning" compared to their success in text-based reasoning, where they leverage pre-defined abstract symbols (language).
- **Confinement of Neuro-Symbolic Methods:** While neuro-symbolic approaches attempt to bridge neural networks and symbolic reasoning by transforming raw inputs into explicit symbols, the authors note that these methods often "remain confined to a single dataset," thus failing to achieve broad generalization.

Against this backdrop, the paper proposes a fundamental "rethinking" of visual reasoning, advocating for a **disentangled two-stage perspective**: (1) **symbolization** (grounding symbols from visual signals) and (2) **logical reasoning** (operating on these symbols or their representations). This work directly challenges the prevailing end-to-end paradigm by arguing that reasoning, unlike symbolization, inherently possesses greater generalization capability. This aligns with human cognitive processes, where distinct neural pathways handle perception (symbolization) before information is sent to regions for logical processing (reasoning). By asserting that "it is more efficient to implement symbolization via separated encoders for different data domains while using a shared reasoner," the authors position their work as a principled step towards more generalizable and efficient visual reasoning systems, distinct from and addressing the limitations of prior end-to-end and many neuro-symbolic approaches.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to fundamentally revisit the paradigm of visual reasoning by proposing and validating a two-stage approach: separating the process of **symbolization** from **logical reasoning**. The authors are motivated by the observed shortcomings of current end-to-end visual reasoning models, particularly their struggle with generalization across diverse tasks and their susceptibility to data biases.

The core hypothesis driving this work is that human intelligence, and by extension, a robust artificial intelligence system, tackles visual reasoning by first abstracting visual information into symbols (perception/symbolization) and then applying logical rules to these symbols (reasoning). The authors contend that the reasoning component, unlike the perception component, should ideally be task-agnostic and highly generalizable. They draw parallels to Large Language Models (LLMs), which excel at text-based reasoning precisely because they operate on abstract linguistic symbols, implying that their success stems from the disentangled nature of their input (abstract language) and their reasoning capabilities.

To validate their hypothesis and establish design principles for generalizable visual reasoning frameworks, the authors set out to answer two key questions:

1. **Optimal Symbolization Boundary:** "Within a trained DNN, where does the symbolization phrase conclude?" This objective seeks to identify the ideal "level of abstraction" or "depth" within a neural network at which raw visual data is sufficiently transformed into meaningful symbols suitable for the subsequent reasoning stage. The motivation here is to prevent the reasoner from being burdened with low-level perceptual tasks, ensuring it focuses purely on logical operations.
2. **Generalizable Reasoner Design:** "Given the abstracted symbols, what types of models and training strategies are most suitable for reasoning and endowing the generalization capability?" This objective aims to determine the most effective architecture for the shared logical reasoner and, crucially, to explore how training it on a diverse range of tasks and data domains ("approximation principle") can foster true generalization, enabling it to perform well on unseen or out-of-domain problems.

In essence, the overarching motivation is to move beyond ad-hoc, task-specific solutions towards a more principled, human-cognition-inspired framework that can achieve robust, efficient, and broadly generalizable visual reasoning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors propose a **two-stage framework** for visual reasoning, explicitly separating symbolization from logical reasoning, and systematically evaluate this design choice through a series of comprehensive experiments.

#### 3.1. Proposed Two-Stage Framework

The framework consists of three main components:
- **Symbolization Stage (Separated Encoders):** For each task `i`, a dedicated, task-oriented encoder `E_i` processes the raw multi-modal input `x_i` (e.g., image, video, text) to extract a symbolic representation `f_i`. The key insight here is that symbolization is domain-specific and thus requires separate encoders tailored to different data modalities and tasks.
- **Reasoning Stage (Shared Reasoner):** The symbolic representations `f_i` from various tasks are then fed into a *single, shared reasoner* `R`. This reasoner is designed to perform unified logical processing across all tasks, aiming to capture generalizable patterns and relationships.
- **Task-specific Heads:** Following the shared reasoner, task-specific heads `H_i` receive the reasoning results `c_i` and generate the final output `s_i` for each specific task (e.g., classification, regression).

#### 3.2. Experimental Validation of Design Principles

The authors conduct a series of experiments to validate their core assumptions and derive optimal design principles:

- **Entanglement vs. Disentanglement:** To justify the separated-encoder, shared-reasoner design, they compare four architectural paradigms:
 1. **Both-Separated (Ad-hoc):** Separate encoder and reasoner for each task.
 2. **Both-Shared:** Single shared encoder and shared reasoner for all tasks.
 3. **Shared-Encoder-Only:** Shared encoder, but separate reasoners for each task.
 4. **Shared-Reasoner-Only (Proposed):** Separate encoders for each task, but a shared reasoner.
 These designs are evaluated using ResNet-18 as the encoder and an MLP as the reasoner across five 2D puzzle datasets: RAVEN, CVR, SVRT, Bongard-HOI, and Bongard-LOGO.

- **Optimal Symbolization Depth:** To determine where the symbolization stage "concludes," they investigate the optimal depth for the symbol encoders. Using ResNet-18 encoders connected to a shared MLP reasoner, they systematically vary the depth of the encoder and observe the accuracy changes. They hypothesize that an "inflection point" in the accuracy curve indicates sufficient symbolization.

- **Reasoner Architecture Selection:** They evaluate various architectures for the *shared* reasoner to identify which is most suitable for logical reasoning. The candidates include:
 - Multilayer Perceptron (MLP)
 - Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)
 - Transformer
 - Graph Convolutional Networks (GCN)
 - Hybrid Neuro-Symbolic models
 - MiniGPT-4 (a Multi-Modal Large Language Model)
 This experiment uses task-specific encoders and heads (e.g., ResNet-18 for puzzles, GCN for CoPhy, VGG-19 + LSTM for VQA) but a shared reasoner across a broad range of 2D puzzle datasets, 3D intuitive physics tasks (Filtered-CoPhy: BlockTower, Balls, Collision), and 2D VQA (VQAv2).

- **Generalization of Reasoner ("Approximation Principle"):** To verify that a shared reasoner gains generalizability by being trained on diverse tasks, they perform a transfer learning experiment. A reasoner is trained incrementally on an increasing number of diverse datasets (SVRT, Bongard-HOI, CoPhy-Balls, VQAv2) encompassing 2D puzzles, 3D video, and VQA. This trained reasoner (with a lightweight MLP adapter) is then transferred and tested on an *out-of-domain* task (Filtered-CoPhy Collision) with a separately trained encoder.

#### 3.3. Datasets and Implementation

The study utilizes a diverse and challenging suite of visual reasoning benchmarks:
- **2D Puzzles:** RAVEN, CVR, SVRT, Bongard-HOI, Bongard-LOGO (covering geometric attributes, relational reasoning, and few-shot concept learning).
- **2D Visual Question Answering (VQA):** VQAv2 (bias-free, balancing questions and answers).
- **3D Intuitive Physics:** Filtered-CoPhy (BlockTower, Balls, Collision tasks, focusing on physical prediction).

All models are trained using the Adam optimizer, with hyperparameters finetuned using Optuna. The experiments leverage multiple GPUs (Titan XP and V100 for LLM-based models).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The extensive experimental evaluation yielded several significant findings that support the authors' two-stage perspective and proposed design principles:

- **Disentanglement is Superior:** The "Shared-Reasoner-Only" architecture, where symbol encoders are separated for different tasks but the reasoner is shared, consistently outperformed all other designs (Both-Shared, Shared-Encoder-Only, and in many cases, even the ad-hoc Both-Separated models). Specifically, it performed comparably to or better than ad-hoc models on RAVEN and SVRT datasets and showed clear superiority over shared-encoder or fully shared designs across all five 2D puzzle benchmarks. This strongly validates the core hypothesis that reasoning is more generalizable and should be shared, while symbolization is task-specific.

- **Optimal Symbolization Depth Varies by Task:** The experiments on symbolization depth revealed that performance first increases with encoder depth, then plateaus or decreases, showing clear "inflection points." Critically, these optimal depths vary across different tasks, reflecting their distinct symbolization complexities. For instance, Bongard-HOI, a more complex task, required a deeper symbolization network than RAVEN. This finding underscores the necessity of task-specific symbol encoders with appropriate depths, reinforcing the design choice of *separated* symbolization. Using these empirically derived optimal depths slightly improved overall performance compared to a universal full-depth encoder.

- **Simpler Reasoners Can Excel:** Among the various reasoner architectures tested, a simple Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) surprisingly emerged as the best performer across most datasets, outperforming or matching more complex architectures like CNNs, Transformers, and GCNs in many cases. This suggests that once high-quality symbolic representations are provided, even a relatively "lite" reasoner can achieve high performance, particularly when trained on multi-domain tasks.

- **Lite One-for-All Model Competes with SOTA:** The proposed "One-for-All" framework (separated optimal-depth encoders + shared MLP reasoner) achieved impressive performance, often comparable to or even surpassing "lite" ad-hoc state-of-the-art models across a majority of visual reasoning tasks. Furthermore, the One-for-All model demonstrated higher reasoning consistency (measured by F1 score on questioning methods) compared to individually trained ad-hoc models, indicating a genuine capacity for reasoning rather than mere pattern matching.

- **LLMs' Limitations in Visual Reasoning Context:** When MiniGPT-4 was used as the shared reasoner, its performance, while notable in some areas, generally struggled on complex visual reasoning tasks like RAVEN and Bongard-HOI compared to the MLP reasoner. Analysis of failure cases revealed that LLMs might excel at "symbolization" (identifying objects or actions) and "conceptual understanding" but falter at precise "logical reasoning" or "answer generation" within the context of visual puzzles, sometimes exhibiting "hallucination." This provides compelling evidence that simply scaling up parameters in MLLMs does not automatically confer robust visual reasoning abilities, highlighting a specific deficiency in their logical reasoning capabilities with visual inputs.

- **Approximation Principle Validated:** The experiments confirming the "approximation principle" showed a clear trend: as the shared reasoner was trained on an increasing number of diverse, cross-domain datasets, its generalization performance on an *out-of-domain* target task (Filtered-CoPhy Collision) consistently improved. This key finding confirms that training a reasoner on varied data from different domains effectively teaches it "pure" task-agnostic reasoning, enabling it to generalize to novel tasks.

- **CLIP as General Encoder is Inferior:** An ablation study showed that using CLIP, a powerful general foundation model, as a universal symbol encoder yielded inferior results compared to the proposed framework's use of task-specific separated encoders. This further validates the necessity of domain-specific symbolization, even when leveraging large pre-trained models.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research offers a profound re-evaluation of how artificial intelligence systems approach visual reasoning, proposing a paradigm shift that could significantly influence future AI development. Its significance and potential impact stem from several key contributions:

- **A Principled Framework for Generalizable AI:** By clearly articulating and empirically validating the two-stage perspective (separated symbolization and shared reasoning), the paper provides a more principled and human-cognition-inspired framework for visual reasoning. This addresses one of the most persistent challenges in AI: achieving generalization beyond specific training data. The demonstrated ability of a shared reasoner, trained on diverse domains, to perform well on out-of-domain tasks is a critical step towards truly generalizable AI systems.

- **Efficiency and Scalability:** The proposed framework offers an efficient design by allowing for simpler, "lite" reasoners (like MLPs) to achieve impressive performance when provided with well-symbolized inputs from task-specific encoders. This contrasts with the increasingly parameter-heavy end-to-end models, suggesting a more efficient pathway to scaling visual reasoning capabilities without necessarily relying on ever-larger monolithic networks.

- **Guiding Design Principles:** The study derives concrete design principles for future visual reasoning architectures:
 - **Disentanglement:** Explicitly separate the perception/symbolization stage from the logical reasoning stage.
 - **Task-Specific Symbolization:** Utilize distinct, specialized encoders for different data domains to ensure optimal symbolic representation.
 - **Shared Reasoning:** Employ a single, universal reasoner capable of operating on diverse symbolic inputs.
 - **Optimal Depth Tuning:** Calibrate the depth of symbolization networks according to the complexity of the task.
 - **Multi-Domain Training:** Train the shared reasoner on a wide array of cross-domain tasks to foster robust generalization (the "approximation principle").

- **Insights for Multi-Modal Large Language Models (MLLMs):** The analysis of LLM-based reasoners (MiniGPT-4) provides crucial insights into their current limitations in visual reasoning. It suggests that while MLLMs excel at concept grounding and language understanding, their "logical reasoning" and "answer generation" capabilities, when faced with complex visual patterns, still lag behind. This indicates that integrating MLLMs effectively into visual reasoning might require addressing these specific deficiencies, potentially by leveraging them within a disentangled framework that provides explicit symbolic representations.

- **Paving the Way for Advanced Visual Cognition:** By emphasizing the fundamental nature of symbolic representation and logical manipulation, this work contributes to bridging the gap between connectionist deep learning and symbolic AI. It offers a clear roadmap for developing visual reasoning systems that can form concepts, understand complex relationships, and interact with the world in a more intelligent and adaptable manner, moving closer to human-like cognitive abilities. This research could inspire future work on training larger, more sophisticated LLMs or specialized symbolic modules within such disentangled architectures to unlock unprecedented reasoning capabilities in AI.
