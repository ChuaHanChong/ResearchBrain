# Unleashing VLA Potentials in Autonomous Driving via Explicit Learning from Failures

> [!summary] Summary
> An approach named ELF-VLA enhances Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for autonomous driving by explicitly learning from failures, employing an external Vision-Language Model (VLM) teacher to provide structured diagnostic feedback for trajectory refinement. This method enables VLA models to overcome performance plateaus in challenging scenarios, achieving state-of-the-art results on the NAVSIM benchmark.

## Problem

- Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models fine-tuned with Reinforcement Learning (RL) often reach a performance plateau, particularly in rare, safety-critical 'long-tail' driving scenarios.
- Conventional RL exploration struggles in these difficult scenarios due to limited diversity from Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and the information-sparse nature of scalar rewards, leading to 'persistent failures'.
- Scalar rewards (e.g., PDMS) indicate a failure but do not provide specific diagnostic insights into its root cause, hindering effective policy improvement.

## Method

- The **ELF-VLA** framework augments traditional Reinforcement Learning (RL) with structured ==diagnostic feedback== generated by an external Vision-Language Model (VLM) teacher.
- A two-stage ==Supervised Fine-Tuning== (SFT) process first pre-trains the VLA model on general driving knowledge, then fine-tunes it for trajectory prediction and refinement using both base and feedback inputs.
- RL with Failure Feedback incorporates a 'difficult-sample curation' strategy to focus training on challenging scenarios, utilizes a multi-component reward function, and refines trajectories during GRPO optimization using teacher-generated diagnostic reports, enhanced by Policy Shaping.

## Results

- ELF-VLA achieved new State-of-the-Art (SOTA) performance on NAVSIM benchmarks, with 91.0 PDMS on NAVSIMv1 (**0.7** improvement) and 87.1 EPDMS on NAVSIMv2 (1.0 improvement over previous best).
- The framework significantly reduced the total-failure rate (where all rolled-out trajectories fail simultaneously) for PDMS from **2.73%** (conventional GRPO) to **1.08%**, indicating enhanced robustness in challenging scenarios.
- Ablation studies confirmed the effectiveness of the difficult-sample curation strategy (24k curated dataset yielded 91.0 PDMS vs. 89.1 PDMS for the 85k full dataset) and Policy Shaping, which contributed a **1.7%** PDMS improvement.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicit, structured diagnostic feedback from an external teacher VLM can effectively transform information-sparse failures into actionable learning signals for autonomous driving policies.
> - Focusing RL training on carefully curated 'difficult-samples' significantly improves both the efficiency and performance of VLA models in complex scenarios compared to using full or randomly sampled datasets.
> - Policy Shaping is crucial for enabling the VLA model to effectively learn from low-probability, high-advantage refinement trajectories generated by the teacher, preventing training instability.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of autonomous driving is experiencing a significant paradigm shift from traditional modular architectures, which rely on separate components for perception, planning, and control, towards more integrated, end-to-end frameworks. Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models are at the forefront of this transition, leveraging the power of large Vision-Language Models (VLMs) to directly map raw sensor inputs (like camera images) to coherent vehicle motion commands. This integrated approach aims to eliminate manually engineered interfaces, support large-scale data-driven policy learning, and offer enhanced interpretability through intermediate reasoning steps (e.g., "think" modules).

Despite the promise of VLA models, their fine-tuning through Reinforcement Learning (RL) often encounters a "performance plateau." This critical limitation stems from the constraints imposed by initial Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) datasets, which are typically dominated by common scenarios and lack sufficient representation of rare, safety-critical "long-tail" events. In these challenging scenarios (e.g., complex unprotected left turns or emergency evasions), conventional RL exploration often fails to discover effective actions, leading to "persistent failures" where all explored actions yield a zero-value driving score. The problem is exacerbated by the "information-sparse" nature of scalar rewards (like PDMS), which indicate a failure but provide no diagnostic insight into its root cause (e.g., incorrect planning, flawed reasoning, or poor execution).

This paper, "ELF-VLA," directly addresses this critical bottleneck. It bridges the gap between failure diagnosis and policy correction, drawing inspiration from successful applications of non-numerical feedback in Large Language Model (LLM) literature. While existing VLA-RL approaches use algorithms like Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) with scalar rewards, ELF-VLA proposes a novel mechanism to overcome the sparse reward problem and improve exploration in difficult scenarios. It positions itself as a significant advancement towards achieving more robust, explainable, and trustworthy autonomous driving systems by enabling VLA models to learn explicitly from their mistakes, thereby "unleashing their latent capabilities."

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to **overcome the performance plateau observed during Reinforcement Learning (RL) optimization of Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models for autonomous driving**. This stagnation, termed "persistent failures," occurs in challenging, long-tail scenarios where conventional RL methods struggle due to limited exploration capabilities inherited from Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and the inherent sparsity of scalar rewards.

The key motivations are:
- **Address Information-Sparse Rewards:** Current VLA-RL approaches rely on vague scalar rewards (e.g., PDMS) that, while indicating a failure, fail to pinpoint its specific cause. This lack of diagnostic information hinders effective policy improvement.
- **Enable Learning from Persistent Failures:** In safety-critical scenarios, VLA models often consistently fail, yielding zero-value scores. The goal is to provide a mechanism that allows the model to explicitly learn from these failures rather than being trapped in a local optimum.
- **Improve Policy Exploration and Refinement:** By providing structured, interpretable feedback on failure modes, the research aims to guide the VLA policy to generate "Feedback-Guided Refinements," effectively injecting high-reward, corrected samples into the RL training process. This provides a targeted gradient signal previously absent, enabling the policy to solve critical scenarios.
- **Unlock Latent VLA Capabilities:** Ultimately, the researchers seek to leverage explicit learning from failures to fully realize the potential of VLA models, making them more robust and capable in diverse and challenging autonomous driving environments.
- **Enhance Explainability and Trustworthiness:** By mimicking human problem-solving strategies through a "Think-then-Act" architecture augmented with structured diagnostic feedback, the work aims to move towards more explainable and trustworthy autonomous driving systems.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework, **VLA with Explicit Learning from Failures (ELF-VLA)**, augments traditional RL with structured diagnostic feedback. It comprises two main components: a two-stage Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) process and a Reinforcement Learning (RL) framework enhanced with failure feedback.

#### 3.1. VLA Inputs Formulation

The VLA model is designed to accept two types of inputs:
- **Base Inputs (q_base):** These are standard inputs including front-view camera images (I_cam), high-level navigation commands (q_com), ego vehicle state (q_ego, e.g., velocity, acceleration), and historical trajectory data (q_his).
- **Feedback Inputs (q_fb):** These are generated based on the VLA's initial response (o) and its driving score (PDMS).
 - If the initial response is "correct" (score ≥ s), the feedback inputs consist of q_base, the correct response o_c, and a rule-based positive feedback (f_rule).
 - If the initial response is "wrong" (score < s), an external VLM teacher model is employed. This teacher model (Qwen3-VL-32B) takes q_base, the erroneous trajectory o_w, and the ground-truth trajectory o_gt to generate a **structured diagnostic report (f_teacher)**. This report includes:
 1. Meta Action Analysis
 2. Think Process Analysis
 3. Safety Failure Analysis
 4. Efficiency Failure Analysis
 5. Actionable Correction (with lateral and longitudinal components).
 The feedback inputs then combine q_base, the original wrong response o_w, and f_teacher.

#### 3.2. Two-Stage SFT for Cognition and Refinement

This phase aims to instill both general driving knowledge and the ability to predict and refine trajectories.
- **Stage 1: General Driving Knowledge Pre-training:** The VLA model is pre-trained on a large dataset of driving-related Question & Answer (Q&A) pairs. This dataset is a compilation of open-source datasets (e.g., DriveLM, LingoQA) and a custom multi-turn Q&A reasoning dataset for NAVSIM following a Chain-of-Thought (CoT) paradigm. This stage focuses on tasks like road boundary estimation, critical object identification, ego action prediction, and traffic semantics.
- **Stage 2: Trajectory Prediction and Refinement Fine-tuning:** The model is then fine-tuned on a mixed dataset comprising both base input queries (q_base) and feedback input queries (q_fb), supervised by ground-truth trajectories (o_gt). This equips the model with the dual capability of generating initial trajectories and refining them based on provided feedback.

#### 3.3. RL with Failure Feedback

This is the core innovation, applied during the rollout phase of the GRPO algorithm.
- **Efficient Difficult-Sample Curation:** To improve training efficiency, the method filters out simple, already-mastered scenarios. The SFT model samples N rollouts for each query, estimates mean reward and reward variance, and discards samples with high mean reward and low variance (consistent success). This focuses training on difficult (low mean, low variance) and ambiguous (high variance) scenarios, reducing an initial 85k entries to a core 24k high-value scenarios.
- **Reward Modeling:** A comprehensive reward function is designed with three components:
 1. **PDMS Reward (r_traj):** A continuous value (0-1) based on the Predictive Driver Model Score, evaluating trajectory quality in the Navsim simulator.
 2. **Format Reward (r_fmt):** A binary reward (1 or 0) enforcing strict adherence to the required output format (e.g., `<think>...</think>` and `<answer>...</answer>` sections, correct trajectory point syntax).
 3. **Goal Reward (r_goal):** A tiered reward based on the L1 distance to the ground truth endpoint, incentivizing endpoint accuracy.
 The overall reward is r = r_traj + r_fmt + r_goal.
- **GRPO with Feedback:** This mechanism refines trajectories and increases rewards, enabling the VLA model to overcome performance plateaus.
 1. The policy model generates an initial batch of responses {o_i} from base inputs {q_base_i}. Their rewards {r_i} are computed.
 2. Responses are classified as "correct" or "wrong" based on a PDMS threshold `s`.
 3. Feedback inputs {q_fb_i} are constructed: rule-based feedback for correct responses, and teacher-generated structured feedback for wrong responses.
 4. The VLA model (student) then generates a new batch of refined responses {o_fb_i} using these feedback inputs.
 5. `k` refined responses are selected from {o_fb_i} that have a trajectory reward (r_fb_traj) exceeding the original batch's maximum trajectory reward (max(r_traj)). If fewer than `k` such "better" responses exist, the remaining slots are filled by duplicating the original maximum-reward response.
 6. These `n+k` (original + refined) rollout samples are used for GRPO optimization.
 7. **Policy Shaping:** To address the low probability of teacher-generated outputs under the base policy (due to conditioning mismatch), Policy Shaping `f(x) = x / (x + γ)` (where `γ` is a hyperparameter) is applied to the token-level probability ratios of feedback-generated outputs. This technique assigns higher weights to low-probability, high-advantage tokens, encouraging the model to learn from valuable but rare correct trajectories.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, primarily conducted on the NAVSIM benchmark, demonstrate that ELF-VLA significantly enhances VLA model performance in autonomous driving.

#### 4.1. Performance Comparison on NAVSIM Benchmarks

- **NAVSIMv1 (PDMS):** ELF-VLA achieves a new State-of-the-Art (SOTA) PDMS of 91.0 in the vision-only setting. This is a substantial improvement of 0.7 PDMS over the previous best vision-only method (DriveVLA) and significantly outperforms SFT-only (87.4 PDMS) and traditional RL (89.0 PDMS) baselines.
- **NAVSIMv2 (EPDMS):** ELF-VLA also establishes a new SOTA with 87.1 EPDMS, surpassing the previous best from DriveVLA-W0 by 1.0 EPDMS. This indicates robustness and generalization beyond a single metric.

#### 4.2. Quantitative Evaluation with Ablation Models

- **Comparison to Baselines:**
 - ELF-VLA (91.0 PDMS) outperforms conventional GRPO (89.0 PDMS) by 2.0 PDMS, demonstrating the effectiveness of explicit feedback in resolving persistent failure issues.
 - It also surpasses GT-GRPO (89.2 PDMS) by 1.8 PDMS, highlighting that directly injecting ground truth trajectories, which exhibit distributional shifts, is less effective than generating refined, in-distribution trajectories.
 - ELF-VLA outperforms Rule-GRPO (89.6 PDMS) by 1.4 PDMS, indicating that simplistic, rule-based feedback lacks the granular guidance of the teacher model for effective trajectory correction.
- **Total-Failure Ratio Analysis:** ELF-VLA significantly reduces the proportion of samples where all rolled-out trajectories fail simultaneously on key metrics (PDMS, DAC, NC). The total-failure PDMS rate is reduced from 2.73% (GRPO) to 1.08%, with similar reductions for NC and DAC, validating its ability to learn from mistakes and enhance safety/robustness.
- **High-Level Planning Evaluation:** ELF-VLA achieves the best overall Planning Accuracy of 80.3%, 1.0% higher than conventional GRPO. It outperforms significantly larger open-source models (e.g., Qwen2.5-VL-72B by 51.6%), showing its capacity to refine high-level planning through teacher-provided meta-actions.

#### 4.3. Ablation Studies

- **Training Data for RL:** Using a carefully **curated 24k dataset** (focused on difficult/ambiguous scenarios, as per Section 3.3) achieved the best performance (91.0 PDMS), outperforming the full 85k dataset (89.1 PDMS) or a randomly sampled 24k subset (88.9 PDMS). This validates the efficiency and effectiveness of the difficult-sample curation strategy.
- **Number of Refinement Responses (k) and Policy Shaping (PS):** Optimal performance (91.0 PDMS) was achieved with `k=1` (one refinement response), suggesting that a single, targeted refinement is most effective. Increasing `k` degraded performance. The absence of Policy Shaping (PS) led to a significant 1.7% drop in PDMS, confirming its critical role in preventing training collapse and ensuring the model learns from high-advantage, low-probability refinement trajectories.
- **Training Pipeline:** Each stage of the training pipeline (Pre-training, SFT, and RL with feedback) incrementally improved performance, demonstrating the cumulative benefits of each component. Pre-training on driving QA data boosted SFT results by 2.1 PDMS, and the subsequent Feedback-GRPO added another 3.6 PDMS.
- **Feedback Threshold (s):** An optimal threshold of `s=0.8` was identified for triggering teacher feedback. Lower thresholds (e.g., 0 or 0.5) neglected suboptimal but not completely failed samples, while a higher threshold (e.g., 0.9) introduced noise by attempting to refine already high-quality responses.

#### 4.4. Visualization of Refinement Process

Qualitative examples, such as a complex left-turn scenario, illustrate ELF-VLA's ability to correct faulty trajectories. The teacher model provides structured feedback, precisely diagnosing "Think Process" errors (e.g., misestimation of obstacle location) and offering actionable corrections (e.g., adjustments to target lateral position and longitudinal speed). This feedback guides the VLA model to generate a refined trajectory that safely avoids obstacles.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The ELF-VLA framework represents a significant advancement in the training of Vision-Language-Action models for autonomous driving, offering several key contributions and potential impacts:

- **Breaking the Performance Plateau:** By introducing explicit learning from failures, ELF-VLA directly tackles the critical problem of RL performance plateaus in VLA models. This enables models to move beyond common scenarios and effectively learn from rare, safety-critical long-tail situations that frequently cause traditional RL methods to fail.
- **Enhanced Robustness and Safety:** The ability to explicitly diagnose and correct specific failure modes (planning, reasoning, execution) leads to a more robust autonomous driving policy. By reducing "total-failure" rates in challenging scenarios, ELF-VLA contributes directly to the overall safety of autonomous systems.
- **Improved Interpretability and Trustworthiness:** The use of structured, human-like diagnostic reports and a "Think-then-Act" architecture moves VLA models closer to explainable AI. This increased transparency can foster greater trust in autonomous driving systems by allowing engineers to understand *why* a failure occurred and *how* the system learned to correct it.
- **Efficient Learning in Sparse Reward Environments:** The framework effectively overcomes the challenge of information-sparse rewards in complex environments. By generating targeted, high-reward refinement samples, it provides a stronger gradient signal for policy optimization, leading to more efficient and effective learning.
- **Data Efficiency through Curation:** The difficult-sample curation strategy demonstrates how focusing training on high-value, informative scenarios can significantly improve model performance and training efficiency, reducing the reliance on vast, undifferentiated datasets.
- **Leveraging Teacher Model Intelligence:** The effective integration of a powerful VLM teacher model (Qwen3-VL-32B) for diagnostic feedback highlights a promising direction for knowledge transfer and skill acquisition in autonomous systems.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors candidly acknowledge certain limitations:
- **Dependence on External Teacher Model:** The performance of ELF-VLA is inherently bounded by the analytical capabilities of the external teacher model. Future work could explore different teacher models or methods for self-diagnosis.
- **Non-reactive Simulation Environment:** All experiments were conducted on the NAVSIM benchmark, a non-reactive simulation environment. This means the simulator's environment does not dynamically respond to the ego vehicle's actions, which might simplify certain aspects of real-world driving. Future work will involve closed-loop evaluations on more diverse and reactive datasets to validate the framework's effectiveness in more realistic dynamic scenarios.

Despite these limitations, ELF-VLA provides a practical and impactful path forward for developing more intelligent, reliable, and safer autonomous driving systems by transforming failures into explicit learning opportunities.
