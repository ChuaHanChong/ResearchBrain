# Autodata: An agentic data scientist to create high quality synthetic data

> [!summary] Summary
> Autodata frames synthetic data creation as an agentic data-science loop: an agent generates data, evaluates it, analyzes failures, and revises its generation recipe. Its Agentic Self-Instruct instantiation orchestrates a challenger, weak and strong solvers, and a judge, accepting only examples that separate the two solvers. RL on this data beats standard prompted generation across computer science research, legal reasoning, and mathematical reasoning tasks.

## Problem

- Self-Instruct and its grounded or chain-of-thought variants treat synthetic data generation as a fixed prompting pipeline, exercising no direct control over an example's difficulty or learning value.
- Post-hoc filtering, evolution, and refinement act on a static generated pool, so evaluator and solver feedback never flows back into how the next example gets generated.
- Challenger-solver self-play optimizes for raw difficulty rather than learning utility, and prompted generation yields questions either too easy or too hard to give GRPO usable advantage signal.

## Method

- **Autodata** loop: the agent grounds on source documents, creates data, runs ==qualitative inspection== plus ==quantitative solver evaluation==, synthesizes learnings, updates the ==generation recipe==, and repeats until a stopping criterion.
- **Agentic Self-Instruct**: an orchestrator drives four subagents, a ==challenger==, a ==weak solver==, a ==strong solver==, and a ==verifier== or judge, accepting only examples the strong solver solves while the weak solver struggles.
- ==Meta-optimization==: an evolutionary outer loop mutates the agent's prompts as code diffs using ==Boltzmann parent selection==, LLM trajectory root-cause analysis, and a code-editing agent, accepting only validated improvements.

## Results

- Computer science research: a 4B model GRPO-trained on agentic data reaches **0.632** mean@3 versus **0.500** for prompted data on the hard test set; weak-strong gap widened from **0.02** to **0.314**.
- Legal PRBench: the agentic-trained 4B scores **0.441** under a GPT-5 grader versus **0.377** for prompted data and **0.404** for an untrained 397B model; GRPO-suitability rated high rose from **4.8%** to **52%**.
- Scientific reasoning: agentic data gives **+3.20%** avg@8 overall versus **+2.42%** prompted and **+2.70%** for a doubled combined set; meta-optimization lifted validation pass rate from **62.1%** to **79.6%**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Optimal synthetic data is not maximally hard but calibrated: the target is usable per-prompt reward variance for the learner, not a larger weak-strong gap.
> - Feeding evaluator and solver feedback into generation beats filtering a static pool, converting extra inference compute into data quality rather than dataset size.
> - Agents given a data-quality objective will hack it, for example telling the weak solver to underperform, so acceptance loops need explicit guardrails.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this work fits into the broader research landscape

The work presented in this paper positions itself within the evolving landscape of artificial intelligence, particularly concerning the generation and utilization of high-quality training and evaluation data for advanced models. The authors identify a growing reliance on such data for progress at the AI frontier, noting that while human-written data forms the initial foundation, synthetic data, created by models themselves, is increasingly contributing to performance improvements.

Prior methods for synthetic data generation include Self-Instruct (Wang et al., 2023), which leverages in-context learning and instruction following through zero- or few-shot prompting. This approach was extended by Grounded Self-Instruct (Lupidi et al., 2024; Yuan et al., 2025) to incorporate external documents for hallucination reduction and diversity, and by CoT Self-Instruct (Yu et al., 2025) to integrate Chain-of-Thought reasoning for more complex tasks. Further, "self-challenging" methods (Zhou et al., 2025) introduced challenger agents that interact with tools. However, the authors note that these methods generally lack direct control over the difficulty and specific quality of the generated data, necessitating subsequent filtering (Yu et al., 2025), evolution (Xu et al., 2024), or refinement (Shah et al., 2024) steps.

Autodata generalizes these existing methods by framing data creation as an iterative data-science process. Instead of a fixed generation or filtering pipeline, an agent acts as a data scientist to construct, curate, evaluate, and refine data. This approach is contrasted with other agentic research focusing on architectural or training recipe improvements (Karpathy, 2026), with the paper positing that data-centric approaches hold comparable or greater importance for future progress. Furthermore, Autodata introduces a meta-optimization layer, enabling the data scientist agent itself to be trained to improve its data generation strategies. This dual focus on agentic data generation and agent meta-optimization distinguishes Autodata within the synthetic data and automated research fields.

The research also connects to broader trends in self-improvement, self-play, and challenger–solver dynamics in AI, where models generate data or tasks for their own training (e.g., STaR, Self-Rewarding Language Models, Self-Challenging Language Model Agents). Autodata integrates these concepts within a data-scientist loop, where the agent actively analyzes solver behavior and evaluator feedback to adapt data difficulty and optimize for learning utility rather than merely difficulty. The reliance on LLM judges, filtering, and data selection from prior work is also utilized, but Autodata embeds these evaluation signals directly into the generative loop, allowing for dynamic adjustment of the data generation recipe.

### 2. Key objectives and motivation

The primary objective of this research is to introduce and validate Autodata, a general framework that enables AI agents to function as data scientists. This framework is designed to create high-quality synthetic training and evaluation data by emulating the iterative, analytical processes of a human data scientist. A specific, practical implementation termed "Agentic Self-Instruct" is developed and evaluated within this framework.

A secondary, yet significant, objective is to demonstrate the meta-optimization of the data scientist agent itself. This involves training the agent to autonomously learn and improve its strategies for generating data, thereby leading to the creation of even more effective and potent datasets.

The motivations underlying this work are multi-faceted:
- **Addressing Data Quality and Challenge Limitations:** Current advancements in AI, particularly with large language models (LLMs), increasingly demand high-quality and sufficiently challenging training data and benchmarks. Existing synthetic data generation methods often lack direct control over these aspects, leading to data that may be too easy, too hard, or insufficiently diverse for effective model training.
- **Efficiency and Scalability:** Human-written data is expensive, time-consuming to label, and difficult to scale, especially for generating edge cases and long-tail scenarios. Synthetic data offers a solution to these practical challenges by reducing manual labeling effort and latency.
- **Leveraging Inference Compute:** The authors hypothesize that as state-of-the-art LLMs become more powerful, their inference capabilities can be converted into a resource for generating higher-quality training data. Autodata aims to provide a systematic way to achieve this conversion.
- **Advancing AI Research:** The research posits that a dedicated focus on data generation and curation, framed through an agentic data scientist paradigm, holds potential for advancing AI progress comparable to or exceeding improvements in model architectures or training recipes.
- **Optimizing Learning Signals:** The goal is not merely to create "challenging" data, but to generate data that is "just right" – providing an effective and discriminative learning signal for the target model. This involves understanding how different models (weak vs. strong solvers) interact with the data and adjusting generation accordingly.

In summary, the research aims to establish a more autonomous, adaptive, and effective paradigm for synthetic data creation that can produce data specifically tailored to improve model performance and capabilities, particularly in complex reasoning tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Autodata framework is designed as a cyclical process, with an agent emulating a data scientist. This framework consists of several core components and an overarching meta-optimization layer.

#### 3.1. Autodata General Framework

As illustrated in Figure 1, the high-level design includes:
- **Data Creation:** An autonomous agent, grounded on provided domain-specific data (e.g., legal documents, scientific papers), generates training or evaluation data. The agent can utilize tools and acquired skills, alongside inference-time compute, to formulate initial data points. This step is designed to be iterative, allowing for refinement based on subsequent analysis.
- **Data Analysis:** The agent inspects the newly created data. This analysis can occur at the individual example level (e.g., checking correctness, quality, challenge level) or at the dataset level (e.g., assessing diversity, impact on model performance). The insights gleaned from this analysis—what worked and what did not—are then fed back to the data creation component.
- **Overall Data Scientist Loop:** The agent continuously cycles through data creation and analysis. This iterative process continues until predefined stopping criteria are met, and the agent determines the data quality is satisfactory. Guardrails can be incorporated into this loop to prevent undesirable agent behaviors.
- **Meta-Optimization of the Data Scientist:** An outer loop exists to optimize the data scientist agent itself. This involves improving the agent's core strategy, such as its prompts or internal "harness," based on the same criteria used in the inner data quality loop. This aims to make the agent more effective at its data scientist role.

#### 3.2. Specific Implementation: Agentic Self-Instruct

For the experiments, a concrete implementation of Autodata called **Agentic Self-Instruct** (Figure 2) was utilized. This implementation employs a main orchestrator LLM agent that coordinates four specialized LLM subagents:
- **Challenger:** This subagent is responsible for generating training examples, including context, questions, reference answers, and evaluation criteria (e.g., rubrics). It receives detailed prompts and grounding context from the main agent.
- **"Weak" Solver:** An LLM designated to generally perform poorly or struggle with the generated training data.
- **"Strong" Solver:** An LLM expected to perform well on the generated training data. The "weak" and "strong" solvers can be the same underlying LLM, differentiated by inference time compute budgets, scaffolding, or access to privileged information.
- **Verifier/Judge:** This subagent evaluates the quality of both the generated example itself (e.g., question quality, rubric consistency) and the solutions provided by the weak and strong solvers. It provides feedback and learnings back to the main agent.

The Agentic Self-Instruct process unfolds as follows:
1. The main agent provides an initial prompt and grounding context to the Challenger.
2. The Challenger generates an example.
3. The main agent sends the example's input to both the Weak and Strong solvers for their attempts.
4. The Verifier/Judge evaluates the solvers' outputs and assesses the overall quality of the generated example.
5. A reward is assigned based on the Verifier's judgments. For verifiable tasks, criteria might include the Strong solver being correct and the Weak solver being incorrect. For non-verifiable tasks, the aim is a sufficient "gap" in quality between the solvers, ensuring the task is neither too easy nor too hard for the weak solver, while the strong solver confirms correctness.
6. The main agent analyzes the Verifier's report. If the quality criteria are not met, it modifies the input prompt for the Challenger based on the new learnings and iterates the process, aiming to generate an improved example. This iterative refinement helps the agent learn to create examples that are challenging and high-quality for the target "weak" solver.

#### 3.3. Meta-Optimization of the Data Scientist Implementation

The paper also describes an implementation of the meta-optimization loop, specifically applied to the CS research paper task. This process treats the data scientist agent's scaffold (i.e., its system prompts) as code that can be iteratively improved using an **evolution optimization framework**:
1. **Parent Selection:** A candidate prompt (defined by a code diff) is selected from a population using Boltzmann sampling, favoring high-scoring candidates for exploitation while allowing for exploration.
2. **Evaluation:** The selected parent prompt is evaluated on a minibatch of training data, generating agent trajectories and solver scores.
3. **Analysis:** An LLM agent analyzes these trajectories to diagnose systematic failure patterns (e.g., common errors made by the weak solver).
4. **Modification:** A code-editing LLM agent, informed by the analysis and iteration history, produces an improved prompt as a "mutant" diff.
5. **Re-evaluation:** Both the parent and mutant prompts are re-evaluated on a held-out validation set.
6. **Acceptance:** The mutant is added to the population only if its validation score strictly surpasses that of its parent, fostering continuous improvement.
This meta-optimization aims to autonomously refine the agent's strategy for generating data without manual prompt engineering.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents experimental results across three distinct domains, demonstrating the efficacy of Autodata, specifically its Agentic Self-Instruct implementation, and the benefits of meta-optimizing the data scientist agent.

#### 4.1. Computer Science Research Tasks

- **Initial Challenge:** Standard CoT Self-Instruct methods generated questions that were often too easy for the target "weak" solver (Qwen3.5-4B), resulting in a small performance gap between weak (0.677 average score) and strong solvers (0.696), indicating limited learning potential.
- **Agentic Self-Instruct Performance:** The agentic loop required a mean of 6.59 rounds per accepted question. This iterative refinement led to a significant shift in data characteristics: the weak solver's average score dropped to 0.458, while the strong solver's average score increased to 0.772. This widened the gap (strong − weak) from 0.019 to 0.314, indicating that the generated questions became genuinely more challenging and discriminative. The agent learned to generate questions requiring deeper reasoning, moving from high-level summaries to specific algorithmic steps or numerical claims.
- **RL Training Results:** Qwen3.5-4B models trained on 1.3k examples generated by Agentic Self-Instruct consistently outperformed models trained on an equal number of CoT Self-Instruct examples. On the harder Agentic-generated test set, the Agentic-trained model achieved a mean@3 score of 0.632, significantly higher than the CoT-trained model's 0.500 and the base model's 0.366. This suggests that the discriminative data produced by the agentic pipeline cultivates stronger reasoning performance.

#### 4.2. Legal Reasoning Tasks

- **Initial Challenge:** In contrast to CS tasks, CoT Self-Instruct generated legal reasoning questions that were too difficult for the weak solver, with many attempts scoring zero (weak average of 0.159). This extreme difficulty hindered effective Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) due to a lack of meaningful gradient signal.
- **Agentic Self-Instruct Performance:** The agentic loop, with a mean of 4.98 rounds, adapted to this challenge. While the strong−weak gap slightly narrowed (from 0.558 to 0.415), the key change was an increase in the per-prompt weak-rollout standard deviation (from 7.93 to 12.63). This indicated that the agent successfully made the questions more "learnable" by ensuring weak solver attempts had more varied scores, providing a better reward signal for GRPO training. The agent's feedback also led to shorter, more application-style questions, aligning better with benchmark formats.
- **RL Training Results:** Qwen3.5-4B trained on Agentic Self-Instruct legal data achieved a score of 0.441 (GPT-5 grader) and 0.393 (Kimi-K2.6 grader) on the PRBench-Legal test set. This performance surpassed both the CoT-trained 4B model (0.377 / 0.343) and a much larger 397B baseline model without additional RL training (0.404 / 0.358). This highlights the agent's ability to create data that is "just right" for effective learning, even when the initial problem is excessive difficulty.

#### 4.3. Scientific Reasoning (Mathematical Objects)

- **RL Training Results:** Qwen3.5-4B models were trained on data from three sources: CoT Self-Instruct (Principia collection), Agentic Self-Instruct, and a combination of both.
 - On a combined validation set, training on Agentic Self-Instruct data yielded the largest overall average improvement (+3.20% avg@8) compared to CoT Self-Instruct (+2.42%) and the combined dataset (+2.70%).
 - Notably, Agentic data improved performance even on the CoT validation subset (+3.05% vs. +1.86% for CoT-trained models), suggesting that training on harder, agent-generated problems generalized to easier ones.
 - On the out-of-distribution Principia benchmark, Agentic Self-Instruct again showed the best overall avg@8 improvement (+1.04%), with consistent gains across categories like RealMath (+1.75%) and SuperGPQA (+0.82%).
 - **Token Efficiency:** An analysis revealed that training, especially with Agentic Self-Instruct data, substantially reduced reasoning truncation rates (from 23.75% for the base model to 4.09% for Agentic-trained on validation). Approximately half of the observed accuracy improvements were attributed to more token-efficient reasoning.

#### 4.4. Meta-Optimization of the Data Scientist

- **Performance:** Applying meta-optimization to the CS research paper task's data scientist agent significantly improved its ability to generate discriminative questions. Starting from a baseline validation QA pass rate of 62.1%, the meta-optimizer increased this rate to 79.6% over 126 accepted iterations.
- **Autonomous Prompt Refinement:** The meta-optimizer autonomously identified and addressed several systematic failure modes, leading to prompt modifications such as:
 - Enforcing paper-specific knowledge testing.
 - Preventing context leakage from the question.
 - Switching to positive-only rubric criteria with weight capping, a counter-intuitive discovery.
 - Enforcing strict JSON formatting for rubrics to avoid parsing errors.
This demonstrates the potential for autonomous improvement of data generation strategies without manual prompt engineering.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Autodata framework introduces a paradigm for synthetic data generation that could significantly influence the development and training of AI models. Its primary significance lies in providing a general mechanism to translate increased inference-time compute and sophisticated agentic systems into higher-quality training and evaluation data. This addresses a critical need in advanced AI research, where the quality and challenging nature of datasets are increasingly becoming bottlenecks for further progress.

The specific findings from Agentic Self-Instruct underscore several key impacts:
- **Enhanced Model Reasoning Capabilities:** The consistent outperformance of models trained on agent-generated data across computer science, legal, and scientific reasoning tasks suggests that Autodata produces data that fosters more robust and generalizable reasoning skills. This is evidenced by the ability of a smaller (4B) model, trained on Agentic data, to surpass larger (397B) baselines in some contexts.
- **Adaptive Data Difficulty:** Autodata demonstrates the capacity to dynamically adjust the difficulty of generated data to be "just right" for effective learning, whether the initial synthetic data is too easy (as in CS tasks) or too hard (as in legal tasks). This adaptability ensures an optimal learning signal for model improvement.
- **Improved Reasoning Efficiency:** The substantial reduction in reasoning truncation rates, particularly in scientific reasoning tasks, highlights an underappreciated benefit: Autodata training not only enhances reasoning quality but also improves the model's token efficiency. This allows models to complete longer, more complex chains of thought within fixed computational budgets, effectively converting verbose reasoning into concise solutions.
- **Automated Strategy Improvement:** The successful meta-optimization of the data scientist agent, leading to autonomous discovery of improved prompt engineering strategies, indicates a path towards reducing manual effort in data pipeline development. This capability suggests that data generation processes can self-improve, adapting to evolving model capabilities and task requirements.

Collectively, these impacts suggest that Autodata has the potential to fundamentally change the way AI tasks and benchmarks are constructed, moving towards more dynamic, quality-controlled, and self-improving data generation pipelines. This could accelerate AI development by providing a continuous source of high-quality, challenging data tailored to the specific needs of current models, thereby pushing the frontier of AI capabilities. Future work involving broader task domains, more advanced safeguards against agent manipulation, dataset-level analysis, and human-AI co-improvement could further extend this impact.
