# Iterative Reinforcement Learning Based Design of Dynamic Locomotion Skills for Cassie

> [!summary] Summary
> An iterative deep reinforcement learning framework enables flexible policy refinement and consolidation for human-scale bipedal robots. This approach allowed the Cassie robot to acquire diverse and robust locomotion skills, including variable-speed walking and high-stepping gaits, with direct simulation-to-real transfer without dynamics randomization.

## Problem

- Iterative design of DRL policies is challenging, as modifying reward functions or network architectures can lead to unpredictable outcomes or "catastrophic forgetting" of previously learned skills.
- Deploying dynamic locomotion policies on physical human-scale bipedal robots from simulation often requires extensive tuning or reliance on dynamics randomization.
- Traditional behavior cloning for policy compression or distillation can suffer from covariate shift, where the student policy encounters states not covered by the expert data, leading to a lack of robustness.

## Method

- A DRL design methodology that integrates policy gradient ==reinforcement learning== with supervised learning, allowing reward functions to be fully redefined in each iteration while preserving prior skills.
- A novel Deterministic Action Stochastic State (DASS) tuple collection method that compactly represents policies by sampling states from stochastic execution and recording deterministic mean actions, suitable for robust supervised learning.
- An objective function that combines the standard RL objective with a supervised learning objective derived from DASS tuples, enabling new policies to be learned while maintaining desired behaviors from expert policies.

## Results

- Successfully compressed large expert policies into significantly smaller networks (e.g., (256, 256) to (16, 16) hidden layers) using as few as 600 DASS samples, maintaining robustness against perturbations.
- Distilled multiple specialized expert policies (e.g., walking forward, backward, stepping in place) into a single, unified policy capable of general locomotion at speeds from -**0.4** m/s to **1.2** m/s.
- Developed robust locomotion skills on the physical Cassie robot, including stable variable-speed walking, gaits with reduced joint accelerations for softer contact, and a high-stepping gait (up to 20 cm foot lift) demonstrating robustness to unmodeled obstacles.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - DASS tuple collection, by sampling states from the stochastic execution of an expert policy and recording deterministic mean actions, provides robust training data that mitigates covariate shift in imitation learning.
> - The combined RL and DASS-based supervised learning approach enables flexible and iterative policy refinement, allowing for targeted behavioral changes without causing catastrophic forgetting of previously acquired skills.
> - Successful direct sim-to-real transfer on a human-scale bipedal robot (Cassie) without dynamics randomization is achievable, suggesting that factors like learning stochastic policies at a lower control rate contribute to inherent robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has demonstrated potential in developing locomotion policies for legged robots. Previous work has shown success on relatively stable systems such as planar bipeds, quadrupeds, and 6-legged robots. However, applying DRL to dynamic locomotion on human-scale bipedal robots, particularly on physical hardware, presents challenges due to issues such as state estimation, modeling discrepancies between simulation and reality, and the potential for motions causing hardware wear. While DRL methods have shown promise for 3D bipedal locomotion in simulation, successful deployment on physical human-scale bipeds without extensive tuning or dynamics randomization remains an active area of research.

Existing approaches to bipedal locomotion include methods based on the Zero Moment Point (ZMP), low-dimensional models like the linear inverted pendulum (LIP) and spring-loaded inverted pendulum (SLIP), and offline trajectory optimization techniques such as direct collocation coupled with tracking controllers. DRL has also been applied to bipedal locomotion, with hardware demonstrations primarily on 2D bipeds or bipeds with large feet. More recent DRL applications to 3D bipedal locomotion have largely been confined to simulation.

This work addresses the practical challenges of DRL policy design by focusing on an iterative process, which is often inevitable in robotics. The paper proposes a methodology that supports the refinement of policies through successive design iterations, allowing for flexible modifications to reward functions and policy architectures. It builds upon concepts from supervised learning for trajectory optimization, imitation learning (behavior cloning, DAGGER), and policy distillation, aiming to overcome issues like covariate shift in imitation learning and catastrophic forgetting in incremental DRL. A key contribution lies in achieving simulation-to-real transfer for a human-scale 3D bipedal robot, Cassie, without explicit dynamics randomization, which distinguishes it from much of the contemporary DRL sim-to-real literature that often relies on such techniques. The research thus positions itself at the intersection of DRL, imitation learning, and practical robot control, specifically targeting the development and deployment of robust, dynamic locomotion skills on complex hardware.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical difficulties encountered during the iterative design process of deep reinforcement learning policies for legged robots. In real-world applications, it is often necessary to refine and adapt policies based on observed behaviors, but the current DRL methodology poorly supports this iterative nature. Specifically, predicting the outcomes of changes to reward functions, policy network architectures, or the set of tasks being trained on can be challenging. Traditional "fine-tuning" approaches, where small changes are made to an existing reward function, can lead to cumbersome reward functions and unintended behavioral shifts or "catastrophic forgetting" of previously learned skills.

Against this background, the key objectives of this work are:

1. **Develop a practical iterative design methodology:** To propose a DRL design process that allows for the reward function to be fully redefined on each successive design iteration while limiting deviation from the previously learned policy. This aims to provide a more flexible and robust framework for policy refinement.
2. **Introduce a novel data collection technique:** To create a sample-efficient method, termed Deterministic Action Stochastic State (DASS) tuple collection, for reconstructing, compressing, and combining trained policies. This technique is designed to represent policies using a small number of samples, enabling efficient supervised learning.
3. **Integrate reinforcement learning with supervised learning:** To combine conventional DRL policy gradient updates with gradient updates derived from DASS tuples. This integrated approach intends to allow new policies to be learned under new reward functions while preserving desired behaviors from the "expert" (previous iteration's) policy.
4. **Demonstrate robust policy transfer to physical hardware:** To validate the proposed iterative design approach by successfully transferring policies learned in simulation to a physical human-scale bipedal robot, Cassie, without requiring dynamics randomization. This objective aims to address a significant challenge in DRL robotics, as many sim-to-real transfers rely on such randomization.
5. **Achieve diverse and stable locomotion skills:** To demonstrate the effectiveness of the methodology in developing stable walking policies for Cassie with various gait styles and at different speeds, ranging from stepping in place to variable-speed forward and backward locomotion, including policies optimized for specific characteristics like stable pelvis movement, reduced joint accelerations, or higher foot lift.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core of the proposed methodology is an iterative DRL design process that integrates policy gradient reinforcement learning with supervised learning, leveraging a specialized data collection technique called Deterministic Action Stochastic State (DASS). This approach supports the refinement of locomotion policies on the Cassie bipedal robot.

**A. Data Collection (Deterministic Action Stochastic State - DASS)**
The method introduces DASS tuples (state, deterministic mean action) as a compact representation of a policy. When an "expert" policy (e.g., a policy from a previous design iteration) is executed, states are sampled from its stochastic execution, and the corresponding deterministic mean action from the policy is recorded.
- **Rationale:** Standard behavior cloning, which records deterministic actions from a deterministic policy, often leads to covariate shift because the student policy may drift to states not seen in the expert data. By simulating the *stochastic* expert policy, the DASS method ensures that the collected states include those where the robot deviates from a precise limit cycle. The recorded *deterministic mean actions* for these off-cycle states provide feedback on how to return to the nominal trajectory, effectively teaching the student policy robust feedback control.
- **Process:** Algorithm 1 outlines this: initialize an empty dataset D; reset the robot to an initial state; for N steps, add (current state, expert policy's mean action) to D; then execute a *stochastic* action from the expert policy (`a_i ~ π_e(. | s_i)`) to determine the next state (`s_{i+1} ~ P(. | s_i, a_i)`). If a termination condition is met, the state is reset.

**B. Combining Reinforcement Learning and Imitation Learning**
The DASS dataset facilitates the iterative refinement of policies by allowing new objectives to be pursued while maintaining previously learned skills.
- **Objective Function:** The training objective is formulated as a combination of the standard RL objective (`J_rl`) and a supervised learning objective (`J_sp`) derived from the DASS dataset: `J_total = J_rl - w * J_sp`. Here, `J_sp` aims to minimize the squared difference between the new policy's mean actions and the expert's mean actions for states in the DASS dataset, with `w` being a weighting factor.
- **Policy Update:** The policy parameters (`θ`) are updated using a gradient descent rule: `θ_{t+1} = θ_t + α(∇_{θt} J_rl - w∇_{θt} J_sp)`. This blends standard policy gradient updates (from on-policy samples) with updates guided by the expert's DASS samples.
- **Flexibility:** This formulation allows for:
 - Learning new skills (by defining new `r` unrelated to the expert).
 - Fine-tuning suboptimal expert policies (by using the original expert reward for `r`).
 - Refining specific behaviors (e.g., smoother movement) while preserving general locomotion capabilities.

**C. Policy Compression and Distillation**
The DASS tuples are also used for policy management:
- **Compression:** A large "expert" policy, potentially trained with a large neural network for better learning efficiency, can be compressed into a smaller network by training a new student policy via supervised learning on a DASS dataset collected from the expert. This aims to reduce model size for deployment without significantly degrading performance.
- **Distillation:** Multiple specialized expert policies (e.g., walking forward, backward, stepping in place) can be combined into a single, unified student policy by training on a DASS dataset formed by the union of samples from all experts. This allows a single network to master multiple skills.

**D. Experimental Setup (Cassie Robot)**
- **Hardware:** The Cassie bipedal robot (1 meter tall, 31 kg, compliant leaf springs on legs).
- **Simulation:** Policies are trained in the MuJoCo simulator, which includes detailed rigid-body dynamics, reflected motor inertia, and empirically measured sensor/actuator noise and delay. The simulator uses the same network-based interface and state estimation code as the physical robot to facilitate sim-to-real transfer.
- **Policy Architecture:** A neural network parameterized Gaussian Policy. The network takes an 85-dimensional state vector (height, orientation, velocities, angular velocities, accelerations of pelvis, joint angles, joint velocities) as input. It outputs the mean of the policy, with fixed Gaussian noise injected during execution. The output is summed with a reference motion to produce target joint angles for a low-level PD controller.
- **Training Algorithm:** An actor-critic algorithm with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) is used to estimate the policy gradient.
- **Training Details:** Reference state initialization and early termination techniques (from Peng et al.) are applied. Symmetry in policies is enforced via input/output transformations every half walking cycle.
- **Iterative Design Process:** (As depicted in Fig. 4)
 1. Initial training of four tracking-based policies (stepping in place, walking forward (0-0.8m/s), walking backward (-0.4-0m/s), fast walking forward (0.8-1.2m/s)) using specified reference motions.
 2. DASS samples are collected from these initial policies.
 3. New policies are trained using the combined RL and DASS-based supervised learning approach, with new reward functions designed to encourage specific behaviors (e.g., stable pelvis, reduced joint acceleration, higher foot lift).
 4. Policies are then compressed or distilled using DASS samples.
 5. Selected policies are deployed and tested on the physical Cassie robot.

**E. Hardware Deployment**
- **State Estimation:** An Extended Kalman Filter combines IMU and joint encoder measurements to estimate robot state at 2 kHz on an embedded computer.
- **Policy Execution:** The estimated state is sent to a secondary onboard computer (Ubuntu, PyTorch) via Ethernet. The learned policy outputs new PD targets every 30 ms, which are sent back to the embedded computer for a 2 kHz joint-level PD control loop.
- **Sim-to-Real Considerations:** The system uses identical filtering and estimation code in simulation and hardware. Minor latency (1-2 ms) and slight mass discrepancies exist between sim and real.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding policy compression, distillation, iterative design with changing rewards, and successful sim-to-real transfer on the Cassie robot.

**A. Policy Compression and Distillation**
- **Policy Compression:** The study found that while larger neural networks (e.g., (256, 256) hidden layers) are beneficial for efficient and robust DRL training (Fig. 5), the resulting "expert" policies can be significantly compressed. A stepping-in-place policy was successfully compressed into a much smaller (16, 16) hidden layer network using as few as 600 DASS samples.
 - Table I demonstrates that policies trained with supervised learning using DASS samples maintained robustness comparable to the original expert policy, even with limited samples (600) and small network sizes (16,16). This robustness was evaluated against policy noise, increased pelvis mass (20%), and 50N pushes.
 - Figure 6 illustrates that DASS sample collection covers a broader range of states than simple behavior cloning, including states off the limit cycle, which is hypothesized to contribute to robustness.
- **Policy Distillation:** The DASS method enabled the distillation of multiple specialized policies into a single, unified policy. Three expert policies (walking forward, stepping in place, walking backward) were combined into one (64, 64) hidden layer policy using 600 DASS samples from each expert.

**B. Iterative Design with Changing Rewards**
The proposed methodology, combining DRL with DASS-based supervised learning, was used to iteratively refine policies with new reward functions.
- **Stable Pelvis Movement:** Initial tracking-based policies exhibited undesirable pelvis shaking. When only fine-tuning with a new reward penalizing angular velocity, the robot "forgot" how to step in place and learned to stand still. By contrast, combining the new reward (`r = 0.5 exp (− ‖ω_pelvis‖^2) + 0.5r_rp`) with DASS samples (3000 DASS samples + 3000 on-policy samples per iteration) resulted in a stepping-in-place policy with significantly smoother pelvis movement (Fig. 7). This refined policy (Policy B) was successfully transferred to the physical Cassie robot, demonstrating reduced shaking.
 - This approach was extended to variable-speed walking policies, from 0m/s to 0.8m/s. By collecting DASS samples and applying the stable pelvis reward at 10 different speeds, a policy capable of stable variable-speed locomotion was developed. This policy, along with other tracking-based policies for backward and fast forward walking, was further distilled into a unified policy capable of walking at speeds from -0.4m/s to 1.2m/s with stable pelvis movement, achieving 1.14m/s on a treadmill.
 - This unified policy was then compressed into a (64, 64) network (Policy C and D) using DASS samples (600 per speed increment), maintaining effective performance on the physical robot, achieving 0.8m/s.
- **Other Stylistic Rewards:**
 - **Reduced Joint Accelerations:** A policy was optimized to reduce joint accelerations, resulting in significantly softer and quieter ground contact on the physical robot compared to previous policies (Fig. 8, Policy E).
 - **Higher Foot Lift:** A policy was trained to lift the robot's feet higher (up to 20 cm, compared to 10 cm in previous policies) by penalizing lower foot lifts. This high-stepping policy (Policy F) was also successfully transferred to hardware and demonstrated robustness by coping with unmodeled obstacles (boards) placed in its path (Fig. 9), recovering after several steps.

**C. Sim-to-Real Transfer without Dynamics Randomization**
A notable finding is the successful direct transfer of policies learned in simulation to the physical Cassie robot without the use of dynamics randomization, which is often a critical component in DRL sim-to-real transfer for other robots. The paper suggests that the robustness observed stems from learning stochastic policies that operate at a relatively low control rate (30 ms update), allowing adaptation to unmodeled disturbances and real-world dynamics discrepancies (e.g., perturbed pelvis mass in simulation tests, and inherent hardware variations). The achieved gaits were noted as comparable or faster than other reported gaits for Cassie.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings of this research carry several implications for the design and deployment of DRL-based controllers for complex robotic systems, particularly human-scale bipeds.

**A. Practical and Iterative DRL Design:** The proposed DASS-based iterative design framework offers a practical methodology for developing and refining DRL locomotion policies. It addresses the challenge of managing policy complexity and the impact of successive reward function modifications. The ability to redefine reward functions entirely in each iteration, rather than accumulating complex composite rewards, simplifies the design process and allows for targeted improvements without catastrophic forgetting of previous skills. This can accelerate the development cycle for robot behaviors.

**B. Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer:** A significant contribution is the successful transfer of policies from simulation to the physical Cassie robot without employing dynamics randomization. This challenges the common assumption in DRL that extensive randomization is a prerequisite for robust sim-to-real transfer. The research suggests that factors like learning stochastic policies and operating at lower control rates may contribute to intrinsic robustness. Understanding these mechanisms could lead to more efficient and principled approaches for real-world deployment, reducing the computational burden and complexity associated with designing diverse randomization strategies.

**C. Efficient Policy Management:** The DASS technique enables efficient policy compression and distillation. This is impactful for resource-constrained robotic platforms, as it allows computationally intensive training with large networks to be followed by deployment of smaller, more efficient networks that retain performance. Furthermore, the ability to distill multiple specialized policies into a single, generalist policy simplifies control architectures for robots expected to perform a diverse set of tasks.

**D. Flexible Skill Acquisition and Refinement:** The methodology provides a means to systematically refine locomotion skills, enabling designers to optimize for specific stylistic or performance objectives (e.g., stable body, reduced joint accelerations, higher foot lift) while preserving baseline locomotion capabilities. This granular control over policy characteristics is valuable for tailoring robot behavior to specific application requirements or aesthetic preferences.

**E. Foundation for Future Research:** The work opens several avenues for future investigation. Exploring the underlying factors that contribute to sim-to-real robustness without randomization is crucial. Further research into learning abstractions for locomotion, integrating rich perceptual inputs (like images) into policies, and balancing the use of reference motions with purely state-based feedback could lead to more intelligent and autonomous bipedal robots capable of navigating complex, unstructured environments. The development of methods that allow DRL to learn compositional skills from a collection of previous designs could further enhance the applicability of DRL in complex robotics.
