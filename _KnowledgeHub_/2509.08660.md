# Replicable Reinforcement Learning with Linear Function Approximation

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from the University of Pennsylvania and Johns Hopkins University developed the first provably replicable reinforcement learning algorithms for linear function approximation, addressing the instability of RL by integrating novel replicable statistical tools into value iteration and UCB-based methods. The framework provides theoretical guarantees for policy replicability and sub-optimality in linear MDPs, with empirical results showing that rounding and regularization can improve consistency in deep RL without sacrificing performance.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms often exhibit instability, leading to different policies on repeated runs even with similar average rewards, which hinders scientific rigor and practical deployment.
- Existing theoretical guarantees for replicability in RL are limited to tabular settings and do not extend to practical scenarios involving function approximation for large or continuous state-action spaces.
- The need for reliable and auditable RL systems in safety-critical applications is currently unmet due to the lack of consistent and predictable outcomes from learning agents.

## Method

- Developed two core replicable statistical primitives: ==R-Ridge-Regression== for efficient random design regression and R-UC-Cov-Estimation for uncentered covariance estimation in linear spaces.
- Integrated these primitives into established RL algorithms: R-LSVI for generative models with a core set and R-LSVI-UCB for episodic exploration in linear MDPs.
- Employed a randomized rounding technique (R-Hypergrid-Rounding) to ensure that repeated executions produce identical outputs with high probability when internal randomness is fixed and inputs are sufficiently close.

## Results

- Provided the first provably efficient replicable random design regression algorithm (R-Ridge-Regression) and replicable uncentered covariance estimation procedure (R-UC-Cov-Estimation), both with theoretical guarantees on replicability and error bounds.
- Developed the first provably replicable RL algorithms for linear MDPs, R-LSVI (generative model) and R-LSVI-UCB (episodic setting), guaranteeing ρ-replicability and ε-optimal policies, though with notably higher theoretical sample complexities than non-replicable counterparts.
- Empirically demonstrated that quantizing (rounding) neural Q-values in Atari environments, especially when combined with regularization, increased pairwise action agreement across runs while maintaining high mean episodic returns, indicating inspiration for more consistent deep RL policies.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Provable replicability can be achieved in linear function approximation settings by applying randomized rounding to fundamental statistical building blocks like regression coefficients and covariance matrix entries.
> - The strong convexity of ridge regression and careful perturbation analysis are essential to bound the error introduced by rounding and to maintain performance guarantees of RL algorithms.
> - Replicability in sequential decision-making can be established inductively, where the replicability of each step's statistical estimation leads to the replicability of the overall policy.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The issue of experimental result replication is a challenge across various scientific disciplines, including machine learning. In reinforcement learning (RL), this issue is particularly pronounced, with two executions of the same algorithm on independently sampled environmental interactions often leading to distinct policies. This instability can arise from factors such as statistical noise, environmental perturbations, local minima in non-convex optimization, varied exploration paths, or non-stationary data distributions. Such inconsistency complicates the verification and development of new research, particularly in high-stakes or safety-critical applications where reliability is paramount.

A recent line of theoretical work has formalized replicability as the requirement that a randomized algorithm produces identical outcomes with high probability when executed multiple times on different samples from the same distribution, provided its internal randomness is fixed. Initial studies on replicability focused on basic statistical primitives and later extended to tabular RL settings, where the state-action space can be easily enumerated. However, extending these provable replicability guarantees to settings involving function approximation, which are essential for practical applications with large or continuous state-action spaces, has remained an open problem. This paper addresses this gap by developing replicable methods for linear function approximation in RL, building upon and extending the formal notion of replicability to more complex, practical scenarios beyond tabular settings.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop provably replicable algorithms for reinforcement learning in settings that utilize linear function approximation. This addresses a significant limitation in the current understanding and practice of reliable RL, where instability, particularly in the context of function approximation, poses a substantial challenge for scientific rigor and practical deployment.

The motivation for this work stems from several key observations:
- **Prevalence of Instability in RL:** Practical RL algorithms, especially those incorporating function approximation techniques like neural networks, are known to exhibit instability. This means that repeated runs of the same algorithm can yield dramatically different policies, even if average rewards are similar. This lack of consistency undermines the scientific process of replicating and building upon research findings.
- **Gap in Replicability Theory:** While a formal definition of replicability has been established for basic statistical tasks and recently extended to tabular RL, these guarantees do not cover the more practical scenarios where function approximation is necessary due to large or continuous state-action spaces. The existing replicable RL methods are limited to settings where state-action spaces can be exhaustively enumerated.
- **Need for Reliable RL Systems:** In applications where reliability is critical, such as autonomous systems or medical robotics, inconsistent RL outcomes are unacceptable. Developing provably replicable algorithms is a step towards building more predictable and auditable RL systems suitable for such domains.
- **Foundation for Function Approximation:** Linear MDPs provide a theoretical framework where provable learning guarantees are known with function approximation. Developing replicable methods in this context offers a foundational step before tackling more complex, non-linear function approximation settings. The authors aim to recover existing learning guarantees while adding new guarantees for replicability.

To achieve this, the authors identified a need for new replicable statistical tools for linear spaces, specifically for regression and second-order moment estimation, which are fundamental building blocks for many RL algorithms.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research introduces a framework for achieving replicability in linear Markov Decision Processes (MDPs) by developing two core replicable statistical primitives and integrating them into established RL algorithms. The overarching strategy relies on randomized rounding, a technique adapted from prior work on replicability.

**Core Replicability Technique:**
The approach is founded on Algorithm 1, "R-Hypergrid-Rounding," which rounds numerical inputs (e.g., weights or matrix entries) to midpoints of randomly shifted grid intervals. This process is deterministic given a shared random string, ensuring that two executions with identical internal randomness will produce the same rounded output if their inputs are sufficiently close. Lemma 2.1 formalizes this, bounding both the error introduced by rounding and the probability of non-replication based on the Frobenius norm difference between the unrounded inputs.

**Replicable Tools for Linear Spaces:**

1. **R-Ridge-Regression (Algorithm 2):**
 - **Motivation:** Standard least-squares estimators for regression in RL often face challenges with random design and unspanned input spaces. A replicable linear regression procedure is crucial.
 - **Approach:** This algorithm builds on classical ridge regression. It computes the standard ridge regression solution (`b_w`) and then applies R-Hypergrid-Rounding to `b_w` to produce the replicable output (`w`).
 - **Theoretical Foundation:** The strong convexity of the ridge regression objective is key. This property allows the authors to relate small differences in the objective function to small differences in the estimated parameters. A generalized uniform convergence analysis for gradients (Theorem A.2), extended to multiple independent but not identically distributed data blocks (Lemma A.1), is used to show that the empirical gradient is close to the population gradient. This, combined with strong convexity (Lemma A.2), bounds the distance between the empirical and true minimizers.
 - **Guarantees (Theorem 3.1 & 3.2):** Provides ρ-replicability and an L2-norm bound on the difference between the rounded output and the expected minimizer. When a core set (Definition 3.1) is available, a direct bound on the prediction error is also provided, albeit with a higher sample complexity than non-replicable counterparts.

2. **R-UC-Cov-Estimation (Algorithm 3):**
 - **Motivation:** Estimating the second-order moment matrix replicably is necessary to identify visited state-action regions and construct exploration bonuses in RL.
 - **Approach:** The algorithm first computes the upper-triangular part of the empirical uncentered covariance matrix. This part is then subjected to R-Hypergrid-Rounding. To ensure the output matrix (`Σ`) remains symmetric and positive semidefinite (PSD), the rounded upper-triangular part is symmetrized, and the entire matrix is then projected onto the PSD cone by clipping its eigenvalues.
 - **Guarantees (Theorem 3.3):** Establishes ρ-replicability and a bound on the Frobenius norm difference between the rounded output and the true expected uncentered covariance, given sufficient samples.

**Replicable RL Algorithms for Linear MDPs:**

1. **R-LSVI with Core Set (Algorithm 4 - Generative Model Setting):**
 - **Motivation:** This is a "warmup" scenario where a generative model allows sampling next states from any (s,a) pair, and a core set of state-action pairs is assumed to cover the feature space.
 - **Approach:** This algorithm uses a backward induction (value iteration) scheme. In each step `h`, R-Ridge-Regression is used to estimate the Q-function weights (`w_h`) from rewards and estimated future values (`V_{h+1}`). The input data for regression is sampled from the generative model according to the core set distribution.
 - **Guarantees (Theorem 4.1):** Provides ρ-replicability and an ε-optimal policy guarantee, with a sample complexity bound that depends polynomially on `d` (feature dimension), `k` (core set size), and `H` (horizon). Replicability is achieved inductively from `h=H` down to `h=1`, as each step's label distribution depends on the replicable output of the next step.

2. **R-LSVI-UCB (Algorithm 5 - Episodic Exploration Setting):**
 - **Motivation:** This addresses the more challenging online RL setting where the agent must explore the environment and data distributions evolve.
 - **Approach:** This is a batched variant of LSVI-UCB, operating in `T` rounds, with `M` trajectories collected per round under the current policy.
 - **Q-function Estimation:** R-Ridge-Regression is used to estimate `w_h` for the Q-function.
 - **Exploration Bonus:** R-UC-Cov-Estimation is used to obtain a replicable estimate of the Gram matrix (`G_h`), which is then used to compute the Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) bonus term for exploration.
 - **Policy Update:** The policy is updated greedily based on the optimistic Q-values.
 - **Challenges and Solutions:**
 - **Batched Learning:** The replicable tools require fixed-size batches from fixed distributions. This is achieved by holding the policy fixed within a round and collecting a fresh batch of trajectories.
 - **Gram Matrix Perturbation:** The rounding of the covariance matrix perturbs the Gram matrix used for the UCB bonus. A novel perturbation analysis (Lemmas D.2 and D.3) ensures that the rounded covariance still yields an optimistic Q-function and maintains the regret bound.
 - **Inductive Replicability:** Replicability is proven by strong induction over rounds. If policies are identical up to round `t`, the data collected in round `t+1` is from identical distributions, enabling the replicable tools to produce identical outputs for `w_{t+1}` and `G_{t+1}` (Theorem 4.2 Replicability Proof).
 - **Guarantees (Theorem 4.2):** Provides ρ-replicability and a sub-optimality bound (average regret) for the sequence of policies, with a sample complexity that is significantly higher than its non-replicable counterpart.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yields both theoretical guarantees and empirical observations regarding replicability in reinforcement learning with linear function approximation.

**Theoretical Findings:**

1. **Replicable Statistical Tools:**
 - **R-Ridge-Regression:** The authors provide the first provably efficient replicable random design regression algorithm (Theorem 3.1). It guarantees ρ-replicability and an L2-norm error bound, requiring `N ∈ Ω((B+Y)^2 d^3 / (λ^2 ε^2 (ρ-2δ)^2) log(1/δ))` samples. For fixed-design scenarios with a core set, it guarantees uniform prediction error `max_x |x^T (w - θ^*)| ≤ ε` with `N ∈ Ω((B+Y)^2 d^3 k^2 ||θ^*||^4 / (ε^6 (ρ-2δ)^2) log(1/δ))` samples (Theorem 3.2).
 - **R-UC-Cov-Estimation:** The authors present the first provably efficient replicable uncentered covariance estimation procedure (Algorithm 3). It ensures ρ-replicability and a Frobenius norm error bound `||Π_PSD(Σ) - E[xx^T]||_F ≤ ε`, requiring `N ∈ Ω(d^8 t^2 / (ε^2 (ρ-δ)^2) log(d^2/δ))` samples (Theorem 3.3).

2. **Replicable RL Algorithms for Linear MDPs:**
 - **R-LSVI with Core Set (Generative Model):** Algorithm 4 is the first provably replicable RL algorithm for linear MDPs in the generative model setting. It guarantees ρ-replicability and an ε-optimal policy, with a sample complexity of `N ∈ Ω(d^6 k^3 H^23 / (ε^8 (ρ-2δ)^2) log(H/δ))` trajectories (Theorem 4.1).
 - **R-LSVI-UCB (Episodic Setting):** Algorithm 5 is the first provably replicable RL algorithm for linear MDPs in the more challenging episodic exploration setting. It guarantees ρ-replicability and outputs a list of policies with average sub-optimality `E[V^π - V^π_t] ≤ ε`, requiring a total of `M T ∈ Ω(d^56 H^62 log^5(1/δ) / (ε^44 ρ^2))` trajectories (Theorem 4.2).

**Empirical Observations:**

1. **CartPole Environment (Fitted Q-iteration with R-Ridge-Regression):**
 - Replicability was observed with a fraction of the available offline data, suggesting that the theoretical worst-case sample complexities may be less restrictive in practice.
 - Achieving replicability was correlated with higher mean episode returns, implying that when the algorithm effectively fits value functions, policy replication is more likely.
 - While regularization (λ) was included, its explicit effect on replicability was minimal in this setting, possibly due to a few weights dominating the learning process. Data quantity appeared to be the primary driver.

2. **Atari Environments (MsPacman, Breakout) with Quantized Neural Q-values:**
 - To explore the applicability of replicability ideas in deep RL, the authors experimented with quantizing neural network Q-values as a proxy for rounding weights.
 - Quantization alone led to increased pairwise action agreement across different runs (seeds). However, for Breakout, this was sometimes associated with lower returns, suggesting agreement on suboptimal actions, potentially due to low action gaps.
 - Combining quantization with regularization (decoupled weight decay) achieved both increased action agreement and maintained high mean episodic returns, comparable to the non-quantized baseline. This indicates that the principles of rounding (quantization) and regularization from the linear theory can inspire more consistent deep RL policies without sacrificing performance.
 - Adjusting the exploration rate in PQN was necessary with quantization, as quantization itself reduced "policy churn," which can act as an implicit exploration mechanism in non-linear settings.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This work provides a foundational step towards building more reliable and predictable reinforcement learning systems, particularly in practical settings that rely on function approximation.

**Key Significance:**

- **Extending Replicability to Function Approximation:** The primary contribution is extending the formal notion of replicability beyond tabular RL to linear function approximation settings. This bridges a significant gap between theoretical replicability guarantees and the needs of real-world RL applications.
- **Novel Replicable Tools:** The development of R-Ridge-Regression and R-UC-Cov-Estimation offers general-purpose statistical primitives that may find use beyond RL, in any domain requiring replicable linear estimation in the presence of random designs or for second-order moment estimation.
- **Towards Auditable RL:** By providing provably replicable algorithms, this research contributes to the development of more auditable and transparent RL systems. This is critical for safety-critical domains where understanding and verifying agent behavior are paramount.
- **Inspiration for Deep RL:** The empirical evaluation demonstrates that the core ideas of the theoretical framework (e.g., rounding/quantization and regularization) can inspire more consistent neural network policies, even if direct theoretical guarantees for deep RL are still elusive. This suggests promising avenues for future research in making deep RL more stable.

**Potential Impact:**

- **Enhanced Scientific Rigor in ML/RL:** The availability of provably replicable algorithms can help address the reproducibility crisis in machine learning, allowing researchers to more reliably verify and build upon existing results.
- **Safer Deployment of RL Systems:** In safety-critical applications (e.g., autonomous vehicles, medical robotics), consistent and predictable behavior is essential. This work lays the groundwork for developing RL agents whose learned policies are less sensitive to random variations in data, thereby increasing trust and facilitating safer deployment.
- **New Research Directions:** This work opens up several avenues for future research, including:
 - **Feature Learning:** Extending replicability guarantees to the feature learning problem in low-rank MDPs.
 - **Scaling to Complex Environments:** Adapting and scaling the rounding and regularization concepts to larger, continuous, and non-linear deep RL environments.
 - **Improved Sample Complexity:** Investigating whether replicability in the linear setting can be achieved with significantly lower sample complexity overheads, similar to recent advancements in tabular settings.

While the theoretical sample complexities are notably higher than their non-replicable counterparts, the empirical results suggest that practical benefits can be observed with realistic dataset sizes, making this a relevant contribution to the field.
