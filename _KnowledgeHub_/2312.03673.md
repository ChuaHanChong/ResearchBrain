# On the Role of the Action Space in Robot Manipulation Learning and Sim-to-Real Transfer

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at the Volkswagen Group, Machine Learning Research Lab, empirically investigated how robot action space design affects reinforcement learning and sim-to-real transfer for manipulation tasks. Their study of 13 action space variants revealed that Joint Velocity (JV) control consistently offers the most robust transfer performance, characterized by low offline trajectory error, high real-world accuracy, and minimal constraint violations.

## Problem

- Deploying reinforcement learning for robot manipulation in the real world is challenging due to safety concerns and high exploration costs.
- The 'sim-to-real gap' remains incompletely understood, with existing research primarily focusing on state/observation spaces rather than the action space's influence.
- A systematic analysis of how various action space designs impact learning efficiency and real-world transferability was lacking.

## Method

- The authors conducted a large-scale empirical study, training over 250 Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) agents across two manipulation tasks (reaching and pushing) on a 7-DOF Franka Emika Panda robot.
- Thirteen distinct action space variants were systematically evaluated, covering joint vs. Cartesian control, position vs. velocity vs. torque commands, and one-step vs. multi-step delta formulations.
- Performance was assessed using metrics for training efficiency, policy usability (expected constraint violations), sim-to-real gap (offline trajectory error), and real-world task success rates.

## Results

- Joint Velocity (JV) action spaces, including their delta variants, demonstrated superior sim-to-real transfer capabilities, exhibiting low offline trajectory error (OTE), high real-world task accuracy (ACC), and low expected constraint violations (ECV).
- Cartesian action spaces showed an advantage in sample efficiency and reward for the contact-rich pushing task, while velocity-based controls generally outperformed position-based ones in simulation.
- Joint Torque (JT) control proved unsafe and unreliable for real-world contact tasks, and multi-step integrator (MI∆) delta action spaces generally performed less effectively in terms of the sim-to-real gap compared to base or one-step integrator (OI∆) variants.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The choice of action space significantly influences both the efficiency of learning in simulation and the safety and smoothness of policies when transferred to a real robot.
> - Action spaces that control higher-order derivatives (e.g., velocity-based) and inherently limit normalized tracking error (NTE) generally facilitate more effective sim-to-real transfer.
> - Simulation success rates are not direct indicators of real-world performance, highlighting the need for action space designs that specifically address transfer challenges.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Robot reinforcement learning (RL) offers a method for acquiring complex manipulation skills without the need for explicit programming or detailed models of task objects. This capability is significant for expanding the applicability of robot manipulation across various tasks and domains. However, applying RL directly to real-world manipulation tasks presents substantial challenges due to safety concerns and the high cost associated with the extensive, often random, exploration phases required for policy search.

An alternative approach that has gained prominence is learning skills in simulation and subsequently transferring these learned policies to real-world robots, a process known as sim-to-real transfer. While numerous methods have been proposed to mitigate the "sim-to-real gap" – the discrepancy between simulated and real environments – a complete understanding of this gap remains elusive. Much of the existing research in robot policy learning and sim-to-real transfer has historically focused on aspects related to state and observation spaces, as well as perception.

This paper distinguishes itself by focusing on a less explored, yet critical, aspect: the action space and its control implications. Recent trends in robot learning have shifted from end-to-end policies that output low-level commands like joint torques to policies that output higher-level control commands (e.g., desired joint velocities), which are then processed by hand-engineered low-level controllers. This integration of established control principles into the action space, such as proportional-derivative-controlled joint positions or impedance control in end-effector space, simplifies the learning task for the policy by abstracting complex kinematics and dynamics. However, such engineered action spaces, which often involve multiple control loops and abstract physical phenomena, can alter the fundamental nature of the problem, potentially violating assumptions inherent in RL algorithm design, such as the Markov property.

Previous studies investigating action spaces in robotics have been limited in scope, often focusing on a narrow set of action spaces, specific tasks, or conducting evaluations solely in simulation. For instance, some works compared only a few action spaces for manipulation in simulation, while others studied sim-to-real transfer with a very limited number of control spaces. This work aims to address these limitations by conducting a large-scale, systematic study across 13 different action spaces, encompassing various common design characteristics, and evaluating their performance not only in simulation but also through transfer to a real-world robotic system for manipulation tasks. By doing so, the paper contributes to a more comprehensive understanding of the role of action space design in both learning efficiency and the success of sim-to-real transfer.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the challenges associated with deploying reinforcement learning (RL) in real-world robot manipulation. While RL offers advantages in acquiring complex skills without explicit programming, the exploration phase can be dangerous and costly on physical robots. Sim-to-real transfer, where policies are learned in simulation and then deployed in reality, has emerged as a safer and more efficient alternative. However, a comprehensive understanding of the factors contributing to the sim-to-real gap is still lacking, particularly concerning the choice of action space. Previous research has predominantly concentrated on state and observation spaces and perceptual aspects, leaving the influence of control and action space design largely underexplored.

The increasing trend of embedding low-level, hand-engineered controllers into the action space, in contrast to direct torque control, simplifies policy learning but may introduce complexities such as potentially violating the Markov property or abstracting physical dynamics differently in simulation versus reality. Given the multitude of options for action spaces encapsulating various robot control algorithms, a systematic investigation into their properties and impact on learning and transfer is warranted.

Against this background, the authors establish the following detailed objectives:

- **(i) To understand the exploration properties of different action spaces:** This objective aims to evaluate how various action space designs influence an RL agent's ability to explore the environment during the training phase in simulation. This includes assessing their sample efficiency and the rate at which they converge to effective policies.
- **(ii) To examine the emerging properties of policies trained with different low-level controllers:** This involves analyzing the characteristics of the learned behaviors, such as their smoothness, adherence to robot physical constraints (e.g., joint acceleration and jerk limits), and overall usability when transferred to a real robot. The paper highlights that simulated environments often lack mechanisms to enforce such constraints, leading policies to potentially exploit violations for exploration.
- **(iii) To quantify the gap created due to the choice of action space:** This objective seeks to measure how different action space designs contribute to the sim-to-real gap, specifically by analyzing discrepancies in trajectory execution between simulation and the real world. This assesses whether the chosen control interfaces inherently introduce or exacerbate differences in robot behavior across environments.
- **(iv) To evaluate the transferability of policies trained in different control spaces to the real world:** The core of this objective is to determine which action space characteristics are most conducive to successful sim-to-real transfer. This includes assessing the real-world success rates of policies trained in simulation with various action spaces and identifying factors that facilitate or hinder effective deployment.

By addressing these objectives through a large-scale, empirical study involving a diverse set of action spaces and multiple evaluation metrics, the research aims to provide insights and recommendations for the design of action spaces in future robot manipulation RL applications.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors conducted a large-scale empirical study to investigate the impact of action space choice on reinforcement learning performance and sim-to-real transfer in robot manipulation. The study involved training over 250 reinforcement learning (RL) agents across two distinct tasks and 13 different action space variants.

**Reinforcement Learning Algorithm:**
The Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm was selected for training the manipulation policies, a choice justified by its prevalence in recent sim-to-real transfer literature. Tasks were formulated as Markov Decision Processes (MDPs), with the policy aiming to maximize expected discounted reward.

**Action Space Design:**
The study focused on low-level control policies, fixing the policy output frequency for all action spaces at 60 Hz and using robot-specific limits for control variables. The action spaces were categorized into base types and delta variants:

- **Joint Torque (JT):** This is the lowest-level action space where the policy directly outputs joint torques. It grants the policy full control over the robot's dynamics and kinematics, but requires the policy to implicitly learn these complex relationships to achieve task-space objectives.
- **Configuration Action Spaces:** These spaces utilize a Joint Impedance Controller (JIC) to regulate joint behavior, allowing for specified stiffness and damping.
 - **Joint Velocities (JV):** The policy outputs desired joint velocities (˙q_d), which are then integrated to compute desired joint positions (q_d) for the JIC.
 - **Joint Position (JP):** The policy outputs desired joint positions (q_d), and desired velocities (˙q_d) are computed via differentiation for the JIC.
- **Task Action Spaces:** These spaces operate in the robot's Cartesian (task) space. To handle the non-smooth nature of RL actions, Cartesian actions are transformed into joint velocities via inverse kinematics (IK) and then fed to a JIC.
 - **Cartesian Velocities (CV):** The policy outputs desired Cartesian velocities (˙x_d), which are transformed into desired joint velocities (˙q_d) using pseudoinverse IK (with a null-space controller for joint default positions), and then used with the JIC. Euler representation is used for orientation actions.
 - **Cartesian Position (CP):** The policy outputs desired Cartesian positions (x_d). A proportional control obtains smooth desired Cartesian velocities (˙x_d) from x_d, which are then processed as in the CV action space. A 6D representation is used for orientation actions.

- **Delta Action Spaces:** These variants define control targets relative to either the current system feedback or the previous policy step's control targets.
 - **One-step Integrator (OI∆):** `v_d ← v + c ⋅ a ⋅ dt`, where `v` is the current feedback. This is common in literature.
 - **Multi-step Integrator (MI∆):** `v_d ← v_d + c ⋅ a ⋅ dt`, where `v_d` is the control target from the previous step.
 Each of the four base action spaces (JP, JV, CP, CV) has an OI∆ and an MI∆ variant, resulting in 12 delta action spaces, plus the base JT space, totaling 13 distinct action spaces.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Robot:** 7-degree-of-freedom Franka Emika Panda robot.
- **Tasks:**
 - **Goal Reaching:** End-effector moves to a randomly sampled Cartesian goal. This task primarily evaluates motion control without strong force interactions.
 - **Object Pushing:** Robot pushes a wooden box to a randomly sampled goal on a table. This task involves physical interaction and requires reactive capabilities. Domain randomization was applied to the box's friction and mass parameters in simulation.
- **Simulator:** NVIDIA Isaac-sim with the PhysX engine, using a scanned model of the real-world woodbox. Self-collisions were not enabled during training.
- **Observations:** Joint positions, joint velocities, end-effector Cartesian position, and goal position (plus object position/orientation for pushing).
- **Training & Evaluation Procedure:**
 - 5 policies were trained per action space variant per task using different random seeds. The best 3 policies were selected for real-world evaluation.
 - Extensive hyperparameter optimization preceded the main experiments (over 20,000 sim agents, 1500 real-world tests) to ensure fair comparison and consistency.
 - Simulation was run at 120 Hz with actions repeated from the 60 Hz policy output. The real robot utilized an additional 1 kHz low-level safety controller repeating actions.
 - Real robot deployment for all action spaces except JT included a 5 Hz low-pass filter and a rate limiter for motion limits. These safety mechanisms were *not* implemented in simulation during policy learning due to observed deterioration in learning, attributed to potential violation of the Markov property.
 - Policy architectures: 4-layer feed-forward NN for reaching, 5-layer for pushing, with additional layers for torque policies.
 - Reward functions for both tasks were designed with components for distance to goal, exact goal achievement, velocity penalties, smoothness, joint limit avoidance, and collision penalties (for pushing).

**Metrics:**
To assess training performance, emerging properties, and sim-to-real gap, the following metrics were defined:
- **Episodic Rewards (ER):** Measures training performance and sample efficiency in simulation.
- **Expected Constraints Violations (ECV):** Quantifies policy usability by measuring violations of robot constraints (e.g., joint acceleration, jerk limits) during execution.
- **Normalized Tracking Error (NTE):** Assesses the feasibility of policy actions by measuring the normalized difference between desired and actual control variable states. A high NTE indicates difficulty in achieving commanded actions.
- **Task Accuracy (ACC):** Measured as the Euclidean distance to the goal, providing a detailed view of policy performance.
- **Offline Trajectory Error (OTE):** A proxy for the sim-to-real gap, measuring the joint trajectory error when replaying real-world policy actions in an open-loop simulation.
- **Success Rate (SR):** The percentage of successful task completions in both simulation and the real world.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the influence of action space characteristics on learning efficiency, emerging policy properties, and sim-to-real transfer.

**Exploration Behavior During Training in Simulation:**
- **Action Space Type (Cartesian vs. Joint):** In the reaching task, Cartesian and joint action spaces exhibited comparable learning curves. However, for the pushing task, Cartesian action spaces demonstrated an advantage in both sample efficiency and maximum reward achieved. This suggests that the spatial nature of the pushing task naturally benefits Cartesian control.
- **Order of Derivative (Position vs. Velocity vs. Torque):** Velocity-based action spaces (JV, CV, and their delta derivatives) generally outperformed position-based counterparts (JP, CP) in both tasks concerning sample efficiency and final rewards. The Joint Position (JP) space, while achieving high rewards in reaching, struggled significantly with the pushing task. Joint Torque (JT) converged fastest in reaching but proved very expensive and unreliable for solving the pushing task within the allocated training budget.
- **Delta Action Spaces:** Multi-step integrator (MI∆) delta action spaces consistently performed less effectively than base or one-step integrator (OI∆) variants. OI∆ methods showed a slight advantage in some cases. However, this trend was less pronounced in velocity spaces, with MI∆JV performing comparably well in simulation. Overall, no single delta action space characteristic (non-delta, OI∆, MI∆) was conclusively superior in simulation. Cartesian Velocity (CV) was identified as the best-performing action space in simulation, while Multi-step Integrator Joint Position (MI∆JP) was the least effective.

**Emerging Properties and Safety:**
- **Constraint Violations (ECV):** Policies trained with Joint Torque (JT) control were deemed unsafe for real-world deployment in the pushing task due to aggressive and jerky movements. Across the other action spaces, significant differences in Expected Constraint Violations (ECV) were observed. Joint Position (JP) control resulted in the highest ECV, indicating frequent violations of robot limits. Conversely, Joint Velocity (JV) and its delta variants (OI∆JV, MI∆JV) generally exhibited the lowest ECV scores in both tasks. MI∆JP also showed low ECV. This indicates that the choice of action space significantly influences the smoothness and safety of the learned behaviors.

**Sim-to-Real Gap:**
- **Offline Trajectory Error (OTE):** The Offline Trajectory Error (OTE) varied considerably among action spaces, confirming that action space design is a factor in the sim-to-real gap. Joint Torque (JT) demonstrated the highest OTE, which is attributed to its direct reliance on the robot's dynamics, which differ between simulation and reality, and its lack of external feedback loops. Counterintuitively, Cartesian action spaces, despite having more feedback loops than joint spaces, generally showed higher OTE. This suggests that the effectiveness of feedback loops in mitigating dynamics discrepancies can vary between simulated and real environments, and additional loops do not universally reduce the gap. Multi-step Integrator (MI∆) action spaces consistently exhibited smaller OTE values than their corresponding base or OI∆ variants. This effect is potentially due to the integral term ensuring that the same final goal is provided to the lower-level feedback loops, which is a unique property of these spaces.

**Sim-to-Real Transfer Capabilities:**
- **Simulation vs. Real-world Success:** Simulation success rates did not directly predict real-world performance, indicating a critical sim-to-real gap.
- **Favorable Characteristics for Transfer:**
 - **Order of Derivative:** Action spaces that control a higher-order derivative (e.g., velocity-based) generally transferred better to the real world than those controlling lower-order derivatives (e.g., position-based). Joint Velocity (JV) spaces maintained high success rates, while Joint Position (JP) spaces experienced significant performance degradation upon transfer. The Joint Torque (JT) space was an exception to this trend, suffering from a high sim-to-real gap due to its direct dependence on mismatched dynamics and lack of internal feedback loops.
 - **Emerging Tracking Error (NTE):** Action spaces that naturally resulted in or limited a low Normalized Tracking Error (NTE) tended to transfer more effectively. OI∆ action spaces, by integrating actions based on current system feedback, led to lower tracking errors and demonstrated greater robustness to hyperparameter variations (e.g., velocity limits) compared to MI∆ variants. This property was particularly impactful for Joint Position (JP) spaces, where lower NTE correlated with improved transfer.
- **Specific Action Space Performance:** Joint Velocity (JV) action spaces demonstrated superior transfer compared to Cartesian Velocity (CV) spaces. Conversely, for position-based control, Cartesian Position (CP) showed better transfer than Joint Position (JP).
- **Consistently Best-Performing:** Overall, Joint Velocity (JV) action spaces, including their delta variants, were identified as having the most favorable properties for sim-to-real transfer. They exhibited low OTE, high task accuracy (ACC), low ECV, and required minimal tuning, making them suitable choices for manipulation learning among the studied options. Their benefits are attributed to effective configuration-space control, which provides a high-quality feedback loop, and the ability to generate forces for interaction due to controlling a higher-order derivative.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research systematically investigates the role of action space selection in robot manipulation learning and sim-to-real transfer, a domain where previous studies often lacked comprehensive scope. The findings address a gap in understanding by providing empirical evidence for how different action space designs influence exploration efficiency, the characteristics of learned policies, the magnitude of the sim-to-real gap, and ultimately, transferability to physical robots.

The study's primary significance lies in identifying concrete characteristics of action spaces that are advantageous for effective sim-to-real transfer. Specifically, the findings highlight that action spaces controlling higher-order derivatives (such as velocities) and those that inherently yield or limit the normalized tracking error tend to facilitate better transfer. The identification of Joint Velocity (JV)-based action spaces as generally the most effective, requiring minimal tuning and demonstrating favorable properties across multiple metrics (low offline trajectory error, high accuracy, low constraint violations), provides a practical recommendation for practitioners and researchers in robot learning.

The detailed analysis of emerging properties, such as the unexpected constraint violations in some action spaces (e.g., Joint Position) or the safety concerns with direct Joint Torque control in contact-rich tasks, has direct implications for ensuring the safe and reliable deployment of learned policies on real robots. The observation that simulation success rates are not direct indicators of real-world performance underscores the necessity of careful action space design specifically tailored for transfer.

The potential impact of this work is multifaceted:
- **Informed Action Space Design:** The recommendations regarding velocity-based control and the importance of minimizing tracking error can guide the design of future action spaces for robot manipulation, potentially leading to more robust and transferable policies.
- **Enhanced Sim-to-Real Transfer:** By elucidating the mechanisms through which action spaces contribute to or mitigate the sim-to-real gap, the research can inform the development of more effective sim-to-real transfer methodologies, reducing the need for extensive real-world experimentation and tuning.
- **Improved Safety and Efficiency:** Understanding which action spaces naturally lead to safer, constraint-adhering behaviors can contribute to the development of safer robotic systems. The insights into sample efficiency also help optimize the training process, reducing computational resources and time.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** The study's acknowledgement of the need for further validation across different tasks, observation spaces, robot platforms, and hardware setups indicates avenues for future research to generalize these findings and build upon the established framework.

In conclusion, this research provides a comprehensive empirical foundation for understanding the critical role of action space in robot manipulation learning and transfer, offering actionable insights for the development of more capable and reliable robotic systems.
