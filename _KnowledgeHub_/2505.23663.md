# AMBER: Adaptive Mesh Generation by Iterative Mesh Resolution Prediction

> [!summary] Summary
> The AMBER framework, developed by researchers at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology, introduces an iterative, data-driven approach for adaptive mesh generation in finite element method simulations, utilizing graph neural networks to predict sizing fields from expert examples. It produces higher quality meshes, is faster than traditional heuristic methods, and generalizes effectively to diverse 2D and 3D geometries, maintaining or surpassing expert mesh quality for downstream simulations.

## Problem

- Generating high-quality adaptive meshes for Finite Element Method (FEM) simulations is crucial but often requires significant manual effort and domain expertise, leading to bottlenecks.
- Traditional adaptive meshing methods (AMR, AMG) frequently rely on task-specific heuristics, manual crafting of sizing fields, or are computationally intensive, hindering efficiency and reliability.
- Existing learning-based adaptive mesh generation (AMG) approaches struggle with dynamic sampling resolution and do not effectively handle distribution shifts when iteratively refining meshes across multiple scales.

## Method

- AMBER (Adaptive Meshing By Expert Reconstruction) is a supervised learning framework using a ==Message Passing Network== (GNN) to predict spatially-varying sizing fields from expert mesh examples.
- It iteratively refines meshes: starting with a coarse mesh, the GNN predicts a sizing field, a mesh generator produces a new mesh, and this process repeats, using the new mesh's vertices for the next prediction.
- A replay buffer stores intermediate meshes generated during training, addressing distribution shift by exposing the model to progressively refined, model-generated topologies and resolutions.

## Results

- AMBER consistently generated meshes of higher quality, assessed by metrics like Density-Aware Chamfer Distance, compared to existing supervised and reinforcement learning baselines across six novel datasets.
- The framework achieved significantly faster mesh generation, being over an order of magnitude quicker than traditional iterative error estimation for finer meshes, while maintaining or exceeding expert-level downstream simulation quality.
- A single AMBER model demonstrated robust generalization to unseen geometries and cross-dataset performance comparable to individually trained models, suggesting potential for general-purpose mesh generation.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Iterative prediction of sizing fields, where the current mesh's vertices serve as dynamic sampling points for the next step, enables effective adaptation and generation of complex, non-uniform meshes.
> - Graph Neural Networks provide a suitable architecture to learn sizing fields directly on discrete mesh structures, accommodating non-uniform geometries and varying resolutions across refinement steps.
> - A replay buffer mechanism, which samples and relabels model-generated intermediate meshes, effectively addresses distribution shift and implicitly augments training data for iterative learning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Physical simulations, often employing the Finite Element Method (FEM), are fundamental across science and engineering. The computational cost and accuracy of these simulations are directly linked to the resolution of the underlying computational mesh. Adaptive meshing techniques address this by strategically refining mesh resolution in critical regions of the geometry, thereby enhancing computational efficiency and accuracy.

Traditional approaches to adaptive meshing fall into two main categories: Adaptive Mesh Refinement (AMR) and Adaptive Mesh Generation (AMG). AMR methods typically refine existing meshes iteratively based on predefined heuristics or error estimates. While effective, these methods can be inaccurate, unreliable, or computationally intensive. AMG methods generate new meshes from scratch, guided by geometric or solution-derived features, such as curvature or Hessian information, to specify local element sizes. However, both AMR and AMG frequently necessitate task-specific metrics or extensive manual crafting of target sizing fields by human experts, leading to repetitive, time-consuming processes and bottlenecks in design and optimization workflows.

In response to the limitations of traditional methods, a growing body of research explores learning-based approaches for mesh generation and refinement. Early learning-based AMG efforts used Multilayer Perceptrons (MLPs) or Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to predict sizing fields from simplified domain representations like parameterized inputs or fixed-resolution images. More recent methods have employed Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), such as Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs), to operate on coarse mesh vertices, enabling generalization to arbitrary polygonal domains. Concurrently, learning-based AMR has seen the application of recurrent networks and reinforcement learning (RL) to learn refinement strategies, often optimizing complex reward functions tied to simulation error or specific heuristic criteria.

The present work, AMBER, is situated within this context of learning-based adaptive meshing. It differentiates itself by proposing an iterative, data-driven AMG approach that learns to predict sizing fields from expert examples. Unlike prior learned AMG methods that operate on fixed domain representations or single coarse meshes, AMBER iteratively refines meshes, using the vertices of each intermediate mesh as dynamic sampling points for the next sizing field prediction. This iterative scheme, combined with a hierarchical GNN architecture and an innovative replay buffer mechanism for data augmentation and distribution shift mitigation, aims to address the challenge of generating highly non-uniform meshes that adapt across multiple scales, without being constrained by fixed discretizations or requiring complex reward engineering. The work seeks to improve upon existing learning-based methods in terms of mesh quality, scalability, and generalization to diverse and complex 2D and 3D geometries.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the practical challenges associated with generating high-quality adaptive meshes for Finite Element Method (FEM) simulations. While adaptive meshing is crucial for balancing computational cost and simulation accuracy, current practices often entail significant manual effort and domain expertise. Engineers typically spend considerable time hand-tuning local mesh resolutions for each new geometry or problem, a process that becomes a bottleneck in iterative design cycles and optimization tasks.

The key objectives of the AMBER research are:

1. **Automate Adaptive Mesh Generation:** To develop a data-driven method that can automatically generate adaptive meshes, thereby reducing the reliance on manual input and task-specific heuristics.
2. **Achieve Iterative Refinement:** To enable an iterative mesh generation process where the model progressively refines the mesh resolution, allowing for dynamic adaptation to complex geometries and spatially varying target sizes. This contrasts with single-step prediction methods that operate on a fixed input representation.
3. **Leverage Graph Neural Networks:** To utilize a Message Passing Network (MPN), a type of Graph Neural Network (GNN), to operate directly on the discrete mesh structure. This approach is intended to facilitate adaptation to non-uniform geometries and varying local sampling resolutions across refinement steps.
4. **Address Distribution Shift in Iterative Learning:** To develop a robust training mechanism that accounts for the distribution shift between the initial coarse training data and the progressively refined, model-generated intermediate meshes encountered during inference. This objective is addressed through a replay buffer strategy that automatically augments training data.
5. **Generalize to Diverse Geometries and Problems:** To demonstrate the method's ability to generalize effectively to unseen geometries and a wide range of 2D and 3D physics problems, mechanical components, and industrial designs, including those with human-generated expert meshes.
6. **Improve Mesh Quality and Efficiency:** To produce meshes that exhibit higher quality (in terms of local resolution accuracy and downstream simulation performance) compared to existing supervised and reinforcement learning baselines, while also demonstrating improved computational efficiency over traditional iterative error estimation methods.

In essence, the motivation is to streamline the simulation pipeline by automating an often-laborious and expert-dependent step, making advanced FEM simulations more accessible and efficient for engineers and researchers.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

AMBER (Adaptive Meshing By Expert Reconstruction) is a supervised learning approach designed for iterative adaptive mesh generation. It learns to predict a sizing field, which defines the desired local element size across a geometry, and then uses an off-the-shelf mesh generator to produce an adapted mesh.

The core methodology can be broken down into several components:

1. **Problem Formulation:**
 - The goal is to learn a function that takes a geometry ($\Omega$) and optional process conditions ($P$) and generates a mesh ($M$) that closely matches a corresponding expert mesh ($M^*$).
 - Mesh generation is factorized into two steps: a learnable function that outputs a spatially-varying, scalar-valued sizing field ($\Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_{>0}$), and a non-parametric mesh generator ($g_{msh}$) that creates a mesh conforming to this sizing field.
 - Isotropic meshes are considered, where local element size is related to element volume.

2. **Sizing Field Prediction with Message Passing Network (MPN):**
 - AMBER employs an MPN, a type of Graph Neural Network (GNN), as its backbone.
 - The MPN operates on a graph representation of the current mesh, where vertices are nodes ($V$) and neighborhood relations are edges ($E$).
 - Node features ($h_v$) include information such as average sizing field of adjacent elements and vertex degree. Edge features ($h_e$) include Euclidean distance and approximate curvature. For specific datasets (Poisson, Laplace, Mold), process conditions or FEM solutions are also incorporated as features.
 - The MPN iteratively updates latent node and edge features over $L$ message-passing steps, using learned MLPs for update functions and a permutation-invariant aggregation operator.
 - The final node features are fed into a decoder MLP to predict a discrete sizing field ($\hat{f}(v_j)$) for each mesh vertex $v_j$.
 - A softplus transformation is applied to the network's raw output to ensure positive sizing field values.
 - The discrete sizing field at vertices is converted into a continuous, piecewise-linear sizing field across the entire domain using an interpolant ($I_M(\hat{f})$) based on nodal basis functions.

3. **Iterative Mesh Generation:**
 - AMBER starts with a coarse, uniform initial mesh ($M_0$).
 - In each step $t$, the MPN predicts a discrete sizing field based on the current mesh $M_t$.
 - This predicted sizing field is passed to an out-of-the-box mesh generator (GMSH is used), which then produces a new, more refined mesh $M_{t+1}$.
 - This process is repeated for $T$ steps, with the vertices of each newly generated mesh serving as the sampling points for the subsequent prediction.
 - This iterative scheme allows AMBER to dynamically adapt its sampling resolution, enabling the generation of complex and highly non-uniform meshes.

4. **Training Procedure:**
 - **Targets:** For a given intermediate mesh $M_t$, the target sizing field ($y_j$) for each vertex $v_j$ is determined by projecting the element sizes from the expert mesh ($M^*$) onto $p(v_j)$. This means $y_j = f_e(M^*_i)$ where $M^*_i$ is the expert element containing $p(v_j)$.
 - **Loss Function:** A Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss is applied in the inverse softplus-transformed space (Equation 2). This formulation prioritizes smaller elements in the loss, which are often critical regions in simulations.
 - **Replay Buffer:** To mitigate distribution shift between training data and model-generated inference data, a replay buffer stores intermediate meshes generated by AMBER itself during training.
 - It is initialized with coarse uniform meshes.
 - After each training epoch, $k$ meshes are sampled from the buffer, their sizing fields predicted, new meshes generated, and labeled with expert data, then added back to the buffer.
 - This mechanism implicitly performs data augmentation by exposing the model to meshes at various resolutions and topologies it generates.

5. **Empirical Improvements:**
 - **Uniform Refinement Depth:** During replay buffer sampling, a uniform distribution over mesh depths ensures diversity in refinement stages.
 - **Adaptive Batch Size:** Batches are filled greedily based on the sum of nodes and edges to optimize GPU memory utilization.
 - **Hierarchical Architecture:** A hierarchical graph ($G_{hier}$) is constructed by combining the initial coarse mesh graph ($G_0$) with the current intermediate mesh graph ($G_t$), adding cross-edges between $V_t$ and its closest vertices in $V_0$. This maintains a consistent receptive field across different refinement scales.
 - **Input/Output Normalization:** All input features and target labels are normalized using running statistics for zero mean and unit variance.
 - **Residual Prediction:** The network predicts the residual between the target sizing field and the current discrete sizing field (computed from the current mesh elements), which enhances training stability.
 - **Scaling Sizing Fields:** A refinement constant $c_t$ can be introduced to scale the predicted sizing field. This allows for coarsening intermediate meshes ($c_t > 1$) to reduce computational cost during training and enables zero-shot adaptation to different element budgets during inference ($c_{T-1} < 1$ for finer meshes than the expert).

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of AMBER across six novel datasets, spanning 2D and 3D geometries, physics problems, mechanical components, and industrial designs, yielded several findings regarding its performance and capabilities.

**Quantitative Performance:**

- **Superior Mesh Quality:** AMBER consistently produced meshes of higher quality compared to multiple baselines, including supervised learning approaches (Image, GraphMesh, and their variants) and a reinforcement learning (RL) approach (ASMR). This was assessed using the Density-Aware Chamfer Distance (DCD) and a symmetric relative projected L2 error metric, both indicating closer resemblance to expert meshes.
- **Benefits of Iterative Generation:** The full iterative AMBER model generally outperformed its single-step variant (AMBER (1-Step)), particularly as task complexity increased (e.g., Poisson (medium/hard)). This suggests that the dynamic adaptation of sampling resolution through iterative refinement contributes to improved mesh quality in complex regions.
- **Downstream Simulation Quality:** On tasks with underlying equations (Poisson, Laplace), AMBER's meshes achieved error indicator norms comparable to or even surpassing those of the expert meshes, for a given element budget. AMBER also demonstrated generalization beyond the resolution of the training data, producing meshes with over 100,000 elements from experts with up to ~30,000 elements, while maintaining the expected error-element trend. This "zero-shot" generalization was achieved by adjusting a single scalar parameter.
- **Robustness to Complexity:** While all methods performed adequately on simpler problems (e.g., Poisson (easy)), AMBER maintained its performance advantage as geometry and solution complexity increased, where baselines struggled more significantly.

**Runtime and Efficiency:**

- **Faster than Heuristics:** AMBER exhibited significantly faster mesh generation times compared to the classical iterative error estimation heuristic used to generate the Poisson expert data. For finer meshes (e.g., >30,000 elements), AMBER was found to be more than an order of magnitude faster.
- **Dominance of Mesh Generation:** A breakdown of AMBER's runtime indicated that the final mesh generation step, which is common to all mesh generation methods, constituted the majority of the total computational cost (over 75% for complex tasks). The MPN's forward pass and graph operations scaled more favorably.

**Generalization Capabilities:**

- **Unseen Geometries:** AMBER successfully generalized to unseen geometries within each dataset.
- **Cross-Dataset Generalization:** A single AMBER model trained on a mixture of different datasets (Poisson (hard), Laplace, and Airfoil) achieved performance nearly identical to models trained individually on each dataset. This finding suggests a potential for multi-task and general-purpose learned mesh generation.
- **Data Efficiency:** AMBER demonstrated a capacity for accurate mesh generation even with limited training data, performing well with as few as five training meshes per task and showing modest improvements with up to 100 samples. This efficiency is attributed to the local, per-node loss and the symmetry-preserving nature of the MPN architecture.

**Qualitative Observations:**

- **High-Fidelity Meshes:** Visual inspections confirmed that AMBER produced adaptive meshes that closely resembled expert designs, accurately capturing fine details around features like holes, sharp bends, and regions of high stress or flow gradients.
- **Progressive Refinement:** Rollout visualizations of the iterative process showed that AMBER progressively refined intermediate meshes, with each step increasing geometric detail and improving alignment with the target sizing field. Early steps, influenced by the scaling constant, generated coarser intermediate meshes for efficiency.
- **Baseline Artifacts:** In contrast, baseline methods often exhibited limitations such as overly uniform meshes (ASMR due to fixed refinement depth), or inaccuracies in capturing fine local details (Image and GraphMesh baselines).

**Ablation Study Insights:**

- **Critical Components:** The ablation studies revealed that the hierarchical architecture, the proposed inverse softplus MSE loss, and input/output normalization were critical components for AMBER's performance. The hierarchical architecture was particularly important for tasks requiring long-range dependencies (e.g., Beam).
- **Sizing Field Parameterization:** Piecewise-linear sizing fields (vertex-based predictions) were generally more effective than piecewise-constant (element-based) sizing fields, especially on complex geometries, due to their increased expressiveness.
- **Number of Iterations:** AMBER's performance improved with more mesh generation steps, typically converging around three iterations, demonstrating the advantage of the iterative scheme over a single-step prediction.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The development of AMBER represents a significant contribution to the field of computational mechanics and machine learning for scientific computing, with broad potential impact.

**Scientific Significance:**

- **Novel Iterative AMG Framework:** AMBER introduces a novel framework for Adaptive Mesh Generation by iteratively predicting sizing fields. This approach, leveraging graph neural networks, addresses the challenge of dynamic sampling resolution, which is a key limitation in previous learning-based mesh generation methods that rely on fixed discretizations.
- **Robust Learning for Iterative Processes:** The integration of a replay buffer and automatic expert label projection mechanism provides a method for mitigating distribution shift in auto-regressive model generation. This mechanism effectively bootstraps training data, enhancing the model's robustness and generalization without requiring additional human supervision during intermediate steps.
- **New Benchmarking Datasets:** The introduction of six novel datasets (Poisson, Laplace, Airfoil, Beam, Console, and Mold) provides a valuable resource for the research community. These datasets span diverse 2D and 3D geometries, applications (physics, structural mechanics, industrial design), and include both heuristically and human-generated expert meshes, fostering more comprehensive evaluations of future adaptive meshing techniques.

**Potential Practical Impact:**

- **Automation of Complex Meshing Tasks:** AMBER significantly reduces the manual effort and domain expertise traditionally required for adaptive mesh generation. This automation can accelerate the pre-processing phase of FEM simulations, which is often a time-consuming bottleneck in engineering design and analysis workflows.
- **Improved Efficiency and Accuracy in Simulations:** By generating high-quality adaptive meshes that accurately reflect expert strategies and downstream simulation requirements, AMBER can lead to more efficient simulations (fewer elements for a given accuracy) and more accurate results (better resolution in critical regions).
- **Faster Design Iteration and Optimization:** The acceleration of meshing enables quicker turnaround times for design iterations and simulation-driven optimization processes, fostering more agile product development cycles in industries like automotive, aerospace, and manufacturing.
- **General-Purpose Mesh Generation:** The demonstrated ability of AMBER to generalize across different geometries and even across diverse datasets with a single mixed model suggests a pathway toward more general-purpose, learned mesh generation algorithms. Such models could be highly adaptable to new, unseen problems without extensive retraining.
- **Accessibility of Advanced Simulation:** By simplifying and speeding up the meshing process, AMBER could make advanced simulation capabilities more accessible to engineers who may not possess deep meshing expertise, potentially lowering barriers to entry for complex computational analyses.

**Limitations and Future Work:**

The paper acknowledges certain limitations and outlines directions for future research:

- **Isotropic Sizing Fields:** AMBER currently predicts scalar-valued, piecewise-linear sizing fields, which inherently assume isotropic mesh refinement. This limits its expressiveness in scenarios requiring anisotropic refinement or extreme, non-uniform variations in local mesh density. Future work could explore predicting tensor-valued sizing fields or utilizing higher-order polynomials to capture more complex refinement strategies.
- **General-Purpose Models:** While cross-dataset generalization was demonstrated, further research is needed to develop truly general-purpose models that can handle a vast array of engineering problems without task-specific tuning.
- **Direct Simulation Error Metrics:** The current evaluation relies on metrics that compare to expert meshes or approximate error indicators. Directly assessing AMBER's performance through end-to-end simulation error metrics in real-world scenarios would be an important avenue for future validation.
