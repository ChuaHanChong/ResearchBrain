# Internalizing Self-Consistency in Language Models: Multi-Agent Consensus Alignment

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Meta AI and collaborating institutions developed Multi-Agent Consensus Alignment (MACA), a post-training framework that internalizes self-consistency in large language models by leveraging self-generated debate signals. This approach significantly boosts reasoning accuracy and generalization across various benchmarks, with preference learning methods achieving up to +23.7% on MATH.

## Problem

- Large language models often generate inconsistent or contradictory responses, especially under stochastic sampling, due to a lack of intrinsic reasoning stability.
- Existing inference-time methods for consistency, such as self-consistency prompting, only aggregate or refine outputs externally without modifying the model's internal reasoning policy.
- The absence of a framework to formally establish and optimize self-consistency as an intrinsic property hinders the development of fundamentally reliable and trustworthy LLMs.

## Method

- Introduces ==Multi-Agent Consensus Alignment (MACA)==, a reinforcement learning framework that simulates ==multi-agent debates== to generate rich, self-supervised training signals.
- Utilizes multi-round debates among cloned LLM agents where peers observe and update their reasoning, producing ==consensus-supporting and dissenting trajectories==.
- Applies four post-training objectives (MV-SFT, MV-GRPO, MV-DPO, MV-KTO) to learn from these self-generated preference signals, guiding the model to inherently favor consistent reasoning pathways.

## Results

- MACA improved single-agent zero-shot accuracy by up to **+23.7%** on MATH (Llama-8B) and **+22.71%** on GSM8K (Qwen-2B) compared to baselines.
- Unanimous multi-agent debate agreement (3/3 agents) on GSM8K tripled for Qwen-2B, increasing from **13.4%** to **43.4%**, while non-parseable responses significantly decreased.
- Demonstrated strong generalization, boosting performance on unseen benchmarks such as GPQA (**+16.3%** for Qwen2B) and CommonsenseQA (**+11.6%** for Llama3B) after mathematical reasoning training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-generated consensus signals from multi-agent debates provide effective supervision for LLM alignment, often performing comparably to or surpassing oracle ground-truth supervision.
> - Internalizing self-consistency through post-training fundamentally enhances reasoning capabilities, leading to robust generalization across diverse, unseen tasks beyond the training domain.
> - Preference learning methods like DPO and KTO are particularly effective at leveraging debate-derived signals to modify model policy, fostering increased decisiveness, conciseness, and peer insight utilization.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The concept of self-improvement in language models (LMs), where models enhance their performance using self-generated signals without external supervision, represents a persistent challenge in AI research. A core difficulty lies in generating a training signal that surpasses the model's current capabilities. Prior approaches to addressing inconsistencies in LM reasoning have predominantly focused on inference-time techniques. These include methods like self-consistency prompting (Wang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024), which samples multiple reasoning paths and selects a majority-voted answer, and multi-agent debate frameworks (Du et al., 2023; Irving et al., 2018), which leverage consensus across models to improve the reliability of final responses. While these techniques can enhance the quality of the final output, they typically necessitate additional inference compute and do not address the underlying issue of internal reasoning instability or poor self-consistency within the model itself.

Self-consistency, defined as the ability to generate consistent outputs across diverse reasoning paths, is identified as a characteristic of well-aligned reasoning systems. In contrast to biological systems that employ control mechanisms to resolve cognitive conflicts, LMs often lack inherent mechanisms to align diverse reasoning trajectories towards a coherent solution.

Existing training-time alternatives for improving self-consistency and single-rollout performance have demonstrated limitations. Methods utilizing relative log-probability rankings as proxies for confidence may not correlate strongly with accuracy. Approaches that employ large language models as judges (LLM-as-a-Judge) can be susceptible to preference leakage and bias, particularly in ambiguous contexts. While majority-vote reinforcement learning methods like TTRL (Zuo et al., 2025) and ScPO (Prasad et al., 2025) use single-round majority votes from independently sampled responses, they do not explicitly address the deliberative process of consensus formation. This paper positions itself by introducing a post-training framework that specifically optimizes for consensus signals derived from multi-agent, multi-round debate, including the deliberation traces themselves as conditioning contexts during reinforcement learning. This approach aims to enable models to internalize the deliberative process and improve their fundamental reasoning consistency.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to enable self-improvement in language models by enhancing their internal self-consistency and collaborative reasoning abilities through post-training. The motivation stems from several identified challenges in current LM capabilities:

1. **Inconsistent Reasoning:** Language models often produce contradictory solutions to the same problem under exploratory sampling (e.g., high temperature sampling). This instability hinders their reliability, particularly in complex reasoning tasks.
2. **Lack of Internal Alignment Mechanisms:** Unlike human cognitive systems that resolve conflicts to achieve coherent solutions, LMs typically lack mechanisms to align their diverse reasoning paths toward a stable consensus. Current inference-time aggregation methods provide external fixes rather than internal improvements.
3. **Limitations of Simple Aggregation:** While majority voting offers a signal for self-improvement, the inherent poor self-consistency of models, especially smaller ones, can lead to counterproductive aggregation where noisy arguments compound rather than resolve inconsistencies.
4. **Sourcing a Richer Training Signal:** The core difficulty in self-improvement is obtaining a training signal stronger than what the model can currently generate. The authors hypothesize that multi-agent debate, involving collaborative exchange of reasoning over multiple rounds, can provide a richer and more robust signal than single-round majority voting.
5. **Effective Utilization of Debate Signals:** A significant challenge is how to translate the complex dynamics of multi-agent debate into an effective training signal. The paper aims to explore different reinforcement learning objectives to leverage this signal, with a particular focus on preference learning over full reasoning traces.

To address these motivations, the research introduces Multi-Agent Consensus Alignment (MACA), a reinforcement learning framework designed to post-train models. The key contributions outlined are:
- The development of MACA, a reinforcement learning framework for post-training models on consensus signals from multi-agent debate.
- The insight that multi-agent debate consensus signals, grounded in peer interaction, offer richer and more robust training signals for self-improvement than single-round majority voting.
- Empirical validation demonstrating that MACA improves multi-agent collaborative performance, individual reasoning accuracy, self-consistency, and generalization to unseen benchmarks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The methodology involves formalizing self-consistency and then developing and applying the MACA framework with various post-training objectives.

**Formalizing Self-Consistency:**
The paper defines self-consistency in two ways, both designed to quantify the reliability with which a model converges on a particular answer:
1. **Single-agent sampling consistency ($s_{\theta,\tau}^t(x)$):** This metric estimates the internal consensus of a model by sampling $t$ independent reasoning trajectories for a given prompt $x$ and measuring the fraction of these trajectories whose extracted answers match the majority answer among the $t$ samples. As $t$ approaches infinity, this provides a consistent estimate of the true probability mass concentrated on the most likely answer.
2. **Multi-agent debate agreement ($d_{\theta,\tau}^M(x)$):** In a multi-agent setting where $M$ agents deliberate and produce answers, this metric measures the fraction of agents that converge on the majority answer after the debate. Higher agreement indicates a stronger consensus within the deliberative process.

**MACA Framework (Multi-Agent Consensus Alignment):**
MACA is a reinforcement learning framework for post-training. The process is iterative and self-supervised:
1. **Multi-Agent Debate Generation:** For each prompt, $M$ copies of the same model (agents) engage in an iterative discussion over $R$ rounds. Each agent generates an initial response. In subsequent rounds ($R-1$), agents observe each other's reasoning (chains-of-thought) from the previous round and update their answers.
2. **Dataset Creation:** After the debate, final responses from all agents are collected. The majority consensus answer ($\hat{a}(x)$) is determined. This partitions the generated reasoning trajectories into two sets: consensus-supporting ($G^+(x)$) trajectories (those matching $\hat{a}(x)$) and dissenting ($G^-(x)$) trajectories (those not matching $\hat{a}(x)$). This process generates a self-supervised post-training dataset ($D_{post}$) without requiring external ground-truth labels.
3. **Post-Training Objectives:** Four distinct reinforcement learning objectives are adapted to leverage this self-generated preference data:
 - **Majority-Vote SFT (MV-SFT):** This method trains the model via supervised fine-tuning to maximize the log-probability of generating the consensus-supporting trajectories ($G^+$).
 - **Majority-Vote GRPO (MV-GRPO):** This online RL method samples multiple trajectories from the current policy and assigns a scalar reward of 1 if a trajectory's answer matches the pre-computed consensus ($\hat{a}(x)$) and 0 otherwise. It uses group-normalized advantage for optimization.
 - **Majority-Vote DPO (MV-DPO):** This preference learning objective uses the standard DPO formulation. It constructs preference pairs by contrasting consensus-supporting ($y^+$) and dissenting ($y^-$) trajectories, optimizing the model to assign higher log-probability to $y^+$ relative to $y^-$. This captures differences across entire reasoning chains.
 - **Majority-Vote KTO (MV-KTO):** This method applies KTO's unpaired preference learning, using debate-derived labels. It optimizes the model to increase the log-probability of $G^+$ trajectories and decrease the log-probability of $G^-$ trajectories, with class-balancing weights to handle potential imbalances in consensus vs. dissent.

**Experimental Setup:**
- **Models:** Four instruction-tuned small LMs were used: Qwen-2B, Llama-3B, Phi-4B, and Llama-8B.
- **Datasets:** Training was conducted on MATH, GSM8K, and MathQA. Evaluation included these and generalization benchmarks like SVAMP, GPQA, and CommonsenseQA, and AMC.
- **Quantization and LoRA:** Models were trained using 4-bit quantization with QLoRA for memory efficiency, enabling multi-agent RL on single-node clusters.
- **Debate Parameters:** Multi-agent debates involved $M=3$ clones and $R=2$ rounds, with a sampling temperature of $\tau=1.0$ to encourage exploration.
- **Evaluation Protocol:** Models were trained and evaluated on 1500/500 train/test splits, reporting mean agent accuracy with standard deviation across three seeds. Evaluation metrics included final-round majority-vote accuracy in multi-agent settings, individual agent accuracy (zero-shot and debate-conditioned), and self-consistency measures (Pass@t, MV@t).
- **Ablation Studies:** Investigations were performed to compare debate-derived supervision against ground-truth supervision, assess the impact of including peer context during training, and contrast multi-round debate signals with single-round majority votes. The efficiency of reasoning and impact of token limits were also analyzed.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research yielded several findings regarding MACA's effects on language model performance and self-consistency:

#### 4.1. Post-Training on Multi-Agent Debate Improves Debate Performance

- **Ensemble Accuracy:** Post-training on debate-derived signals consistently improved ensemble accuracy across all tested models and datasets (Table 1). Preference learning variants (MV-DPO and MV-KTO) showed the largest gains, up to +42.73 percentage points (Llama-3B on MathQA), by optimizing the log-probability gap between consensus-supporting and dissenting trajectories. MV-KTO was noted to perform best on smaller models (≤ 3B), while MV-DPO performed better on larger models (4-8B).
- **Debate Dynamics:** Post-training produced the most substantial gains in scenarios where agents leveraged peer feedback, as the process directly trains for effective peer context utilization. It also mitigated performance degradation sometimes observed in debates by enabling models to learn from consensus patterns even amidst flawed reasoning (Figure 2).
- **Single Agent Zero-Shot Performance:** The improvements extended to individual agents in a zero-shot setting. MV-RL methods consistently outperformed base and MV-SFT baselines (Table 2), with self-guided preference learning (MV-DPO and MV-KTO) generally surpassing scalar rewards (MV-GRPO). Llama-8B on MathQA, for instance, saw an increase of +21.51 percentage points in single-agent accuracy.

#### 4.2. Post-Training on Multi-Agent Debate Improves Self-Consistency and Accuracy

- **Sampling Consistency:** Post-training substantially improved sampling consistency ($s_{\theta,\tau}^t(x)$), with curves stabilizing up to 27.6 percentage points above baseline (Figure 4, Qwen-2B on GSM8K). This indicates that models more consistently sample trajectories that converge to a consistent answer despite high-temperature exploration. The proportion of non-parseable responses significantly decreased (from 13.8% to 0.6% for Qwen-2B on GSM8K), and unanimous agreement tripled (from 13.4% to 43.4%), reflecting enhanced reasoning quality and collaborative grounding (Appendix O, Figure 12).
- **Inference-Time Sampling:** Gains in self-consistency translated to improved inference-time performance across various sampling regimes (Figure 3). Post-training enhanced greedy (τ=0) accuracy, increased MV@t (majority vote over t samples), and raised Pass@t (oracle upper bound), indicating higher achievable accuracy with any given sampling budget. These improvements were found to be complementary to inference-time sampling techniques.
- **Generalization to Unseen Benchmarks:** Improving internal self-consistency on mathematical datasets enhanced performance across diverse reasoning tasks, including previously unseen domains (Table 3). Training on any single mathematical dataset (MATH, GSM8K, or MathQA) improved performance on SVAMP (math), GPQA (science), and CommonsenseQA. Joint training on combined datasets led to further improvements across nearly all benchmarks (e.g., +16.3% on GPQA and +11.6% on CommonsenseQA for Qwen-2B).

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

- **RL with Debate vs. Ground-Truth Supervision:** Self-generated consensus signals from multi-agent debate (Debate-MV) were found to be consistently comparable to, and often superior to, oracle ground-truth (GT) supervision in both single-agent and multi-agent settings (Tables 4, 11, 12, 17, 18). This finding suggests that debate-derived signals provide a robust, scalable alternative to human-labeled supervision.
- **RL with Debate vs. Single-Round Majority Vote:** Training on multi-round debate signals, particularly with peer context (MV-DPO from MACA), yielded greater single-agent performance improvements compared to reinforcing initial-round majority votes (akin to TTRL or ScPO) (Table 5, 23).
- **Effect of Peer Context:** Including peer context (peer chains-of-thought) during preference learning in multi-agent debate significantly improved multi-agent debate performance, especially final-round individual accuracy, indicating better utilization of peer arguments during deliberation (Table 6, 13, 14).
- **Consensus Quality:** The debate process itself enhanced the quality of consensus signals. Post-training increased unanimous agreement significantly compared to base models, providing more reliable training signals than independent sampling.
- **Iterative Training:** Iterative alternation between debate generation and post-training showed substantial initial gains (Iteration 0 to 1) followed by diminishing but continued modest improvements in subsequent iterations (Table 15).
- **DPO Pair Selection:** Analysis confirmed that partitioning responses into majority/minority groups for DPO pair selection provided a more reliable and discriminative signal than confidence-based selection using model log-probabilities (Appendix H).
- **Implicit Format Reward:** Preference learning through consensus signals implicitly acted as an effective format reward, encouraging more efficient and concise chain-of-thought generation without explicit formatting supervision. However, analysis indicated that 69-100% of performance gains were attributable to fundamental reasoning improvements, rather than solely format optimization (Appendix L).
- **Consistency-Accuracy Correlation:** Self-consistency gains strongly correlated with accuracy improvements (r > 0.86) across all tested inference conditions and quantization settings (Table 16), confirming self-consistency as a robust proxy for performance.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The introduction of Multi-Agent Consensus Alignment (MACA) represents a step in the area of self-improvement for language models. By teaching models to internalize and reinforce consensus signals derived from multi-agent debate, the framework demonstrates an approach for improving reasoning capabilities without reliance on external human supervision.

The findings suggest several potential impacts:
- **Enhanced Autonomy in AI Systems:** MACA offers a mechanism for LMs to self-align and improve, potentially reducing the need for extensive human annotation and fine-tuning datasets. This could enable more autonomous development cycles for AI.
- **Improved Reliability and Consistency:** The framework addresses the issue of reasoning inconsistency in LMs, leading to models that more reliably produce coherent and accurate responses across diverse reasoning paths. This is particularly relevant for critical applications requiring consistent performance.
- **Generalization of Reasoning Capabilities:** The demonstrated generalization of improved self-consistency from mathematical tasks to unseen domains (science, commonsense reasoning) suggests that MACA can unlock and transfer latent reasoning capabilities within LMs, making them more versatile problem-solvers.
- **More Robust Multi-Agent Systems:** By enhancing both individual agent reasoning and their ability to leverage peer context, MACA contributes to more effective and robust multi-agent collaborative systems, where deliberation leads to better collective outcomes.
- **Efficient Reasoning:** The observation that preference learning implicitly encourages more concise and efficient reasoning patterns implies that models can learn to be more economical with their computational resources while maintaining or improving accuracy.

While MACA demonstrates benefits, the paper identifies limitations that could be subjects for future research. These include the requirement for sufficient baseline model competence to generate meaningful consensus signals, the potential for existing biases in model outputs to be amplified through self-reinforcement, and the lack of direct supervision for intermediate reasoning correctness. Future work could explore alternative consensus methods, confidence-weighted voting, the use of heterogeneous agents, and strategies for leveraging minority traces more effectively. The gains observed on difficult unseen tasks suggest that consensus alignment may facilitate capabilities beyond mere consistency.
