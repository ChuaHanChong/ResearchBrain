# Self-Harness: Harnesses That Improve Themselves

> [!summary] Summary
> Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory researchers developed Self-Harness, a paradigm allowing a fixed Large Language Model (LLM) agent to autonomously improve its own operating harness by iteratively diagnosing weaknesses, proposing minimal modifications, and validating them. This method achieved significant performance improvements on the Terminal-Bench-2.0 benchmark across various LLMs, demonstrating the generation of targeted, model-specific harness configurations.

## Problem

- Manually engineering and refining agent harnesses for LLMs is not scalable due to the rapid proliferation and diverse behavioral patterns of modern models.
- Current automated harness improvement approaches often rely on stronger, external LLM agents or extensive computational search, which can be costly or inaccessible for proprietary models.
- LLM-based agents typically operate with static harnesses, lacking mechanisms to autonomously adapt; a universal harness is suboptimal since different LLMs exhibit unique sensitivities and failure modes needing model-specific, hard-to-design-manually adaptations.

## Method

- Introduces "**Self-Harness**," an iterative, ==internal improvement loop== where a fixed LLM acts as both the agent and the proposer to update its own harness.
- The loop consists of Weakness Mining (clustering failed execution traces by 'verifier-grounded failure signatures'), Harness Proposal (the LLM generates diverse, minimal edits based on evidence bundles), and Proposal Validation (rigorous regression testing on held-in and held-out data).
- Candidate harness edits are only accepted if they improve performance on at least one split without degrading the other, ensuring robust and non-regressive improvements.

## Results

- Self-Harness consistently improved agent performance across all three tested LLMs (MiniMax M2.5, Qwen3.5-**35B**-A3B, GLM-5) on the Terminal-Bench-2.0 benchmark.
- Held-out pass rates saw absolute gains of 21.4 percentage points for MiniMax M2.5, 14.3 for Qwen3.5-**35B**-A3B, and 14.2 for GLM-5, demonstrating robust generalization.
- Qualitative analysis confirmed that the evolved harnesses contained targeted, model-specific changes addressing unique failure modes, such as improvements in artifact reliability and refined tool-use strategies tailored to each base model.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - An LLM agent can autonomously diagnose its own operational failures from clustered execution traces and propose targeted, actionable improvements to its foundational harness.
> - The use of 'verifier-grounded failure signatures' allows for the identification of common, reusable agent-level mechanisms that contribute to failures, rather than just superficial symptoms.
> - Iterative, evidence-driven modification combined with conservative regression testing enables the self-improvement process to yield stable and broadly applicable performance gains without overfitting to observed failures.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The performance of Large Language Model (LLM)-based agents is influenced by both the foundational LLM and the "harness" that mediates its interaction with an environment. This harness can encompass various components such as system prompts, tools, memory mechanisms, verification rules, and orchestration logic. Historically, the design and refinement of these harnesses have largely been the domain of human experts, a process referred to as human harness engineering. Examples include early frameworks like ReAct and commercial systems such as Claude Code and OpenHands.

However, the rapid diversification and evolution of modern LLMs present a scalability challenge for this human-centered paradigm. Different models exhibit distinct behavioral patterns, tool-use tendencies, error modes, and sensitivities to prompting, implying that a single harness is unlikely to be optimal across various models. This necessitates model-specific harness design, which becomes prohibitively costly and time-consuming when performed manually.

Existing research has explored alternative approaches to harness improvement. One direction involves using stronger, external LLM agents (referred to as Meta-Harness approaches) to guide the improvement of harnesses for weaker target agents. Another area of related work focuses on self-improving agents that adapt their inputs, memories, contexts, or workflows over time (e.g., Reflexion, agentic context engineering, STOP for code generation). While these systems demonstrate the benefit of accumulated feedback, the adapted object is typically a response strategy or generated program, rather than the explicit, declared agent harness state. Furthermore, automated agent design methods often involve external search or optimization processes over agent designs.

This paper introduces "Self-Harness," a paradigm that distinguishes itself by internalizing the harness improvement loop within the target agent itself. Unlike human engineering or meta-harness approaches, Self-Harness enables a fixed LLM-based agent to improve its own operating harness without reliance on human experts or stronger external agents. This internal self-improvement aims to address the scalability limitations of human-driven design and reduce dependence on external guidance, which may be costly, unavailable for frontier models, or potentially mismatched to the target model's specific failure modes. The work positions itself as a technical analogue of self-creation, where a system actively reshapes its own operational parameters.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of this research are to:
1. **Propose a novel paradigm for agent harness improvement:** Introduce "Self-Harness," where an LLM-based agent autonomously improves its own operating harness.
2. **Operationalize Self-Harness:** Develop an iterative, evidence-driven loop with specific stages for identifying weaknesses, proposing modifications, and validating improvements.
3. **Demonstrate the effectiveness of Self-Harness:** Evaluate the system's ability to enhance agent performance across diverse LLM backends using a minimal initial harness on a challenging benchmark.
4. **Analyze the nature of learned harness modifications:** Investigate whether Self-Harness produces generic or model-specific, targeted interventions, and understand how these changes influence agent behavior.

The motivation for these objectives stems from several challenges in the field of LLM-based agents:
- **Scalability of Human Engineering:** The manual design and tuning of agent harnesses by human experts is inefficient and does not scale with the increasing diversity and rapid evolution of modern LLMs. Each new model often requires a distinct, tailored harness due to varying behavioral patterns, tool-use habits, and sensitivities.
- **Dependence on External Resources:** Current automated approaches often rely on stronger external agents or extensive computational search, which can be expensive, inaccessible for proprietary frontier models, or less aligned with the specific operational failures of a weaker target agent.
- **Lack of Agent Autonomy:** Existing agent systems are typically static regarding their operating environment, being shaped by their harnesses but not participating in their design or refinement. Enabling agents to improve their own harnesses aligns with the broader goal of creating more autonomous and self-evolving AI systems.
- **Need for Model-Specific Adaptations:** A universal harness is suboptimal. There is a need for mechanisms that can generate harness configurations specifically suited to the unique strengths and weaknesses of individual base models.

By addressing these motivations, Self-Harness aims to provide a path toward more adaptable, efficient, and autonomous LLM-based agents that can continuously optimize their performance in dynamic environments.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

Self-Harness is operationalized as an iterative improvement loop designed to systematically update an agent's harness based on its observed behavior. The loop consists of three main stages: Weakness Mining, Harness Proposal, and Proposal Validation.

#### 3.1. Weakness Mining: Identifying Failure Patterns from Clustered Execution Traces

In this initial stage, the fixed language model (M) operates under the current harness (h_t) on a held-in dataset (D_in). This execution generates traces (τ) and outputs (y) for each task instance. An evaluator (E), held fixed throughout the process, assigns a pass/fail outcome (z) to each run, forming a trace record (r).
The core of Weakness Mining involves analyzing failed records (F_t). Instead of treating failures as isolated incidents, the system clusters them based on "verifier-grounded failure signatures." Each failure signature, denoted as ϕ(r_i) = (c_i, q_i, m_i), consists of:
- **c_i (terminal verifier-level cause):** The ultimate reason for failure identified by the verifier (e.g., timeout, missing artifact).
- **q_i (causal status of agent behavior):** How the agent's actions contributed to the failure.
- **m_i (abstract agent mechanism):** The underlying reusable behavioral pattern involved.
Failures are grouped only if their signatures match exactly, ensuring that clustering reflects common, actionable mechanisms rather than superficial symptoms. For each cluster, a structured "evidence bundle" (B_t) is created, summarizing its size, representative tasks, trace symptoms, verifier evidence, and the inferred agent mechanism. This bundle serves as structured feedback for the next stage, separating high-level failure outcomes from the actionable agent-level mechanisms.

#### 3.2. Harness Proposal: Exploring Diverse yet Minimal Candidate Modifications

Given the evidence bundle (B_t) from Weakness Mining, the *same fixed model M* (under its current harness h_t) is invoked in a "proposer" role. This proposer is provided with a constrained context, including the editable surfaces of the current harness, the verifier-grounded failure patterns, records of successful behaviors to preserve, and summaries of past edit attempts.
The proposer generates K parallel and distinct "proposal bundles," P_t = {(∆_j, a_j)}^(K)_j=1. Each bundle contains a candidate harness edit (∆_j) and an audit record (a_j) detailing its target, expected effect, and risks. The proposed edits are subject to specific constraints:
- **Groundedness:** Each proposal must address a primary failure mechanism identified in B_t and map to a concrete editable harness surface.
- **Addressability:** Only failure patterns plausibly mitigated by harness modifications are targeted, excluding task-specific difficulties or model capability limits.
- **Diversity:** Parallel proposals are encouraged to explore different failure mechanisms, harness surfaces, or improvement hypotheses.
- **Minimality:** Each individual edit is constrained to modify only the necessary surface, preserve unrelated behaviors, and avoid broad architectural rewrites.

#### 3.3. Proposal Validation: Ensuring Robust Improvement through Regression Testing

Proposed harness edits are not immediately adopted. Instead, each candidate harness (h^(j)_t = ∆_j(h_t)) is rigorously evaluated against both the held-in split (D_in) and a held-out split (D_ho). The held-in split assesses whether the proposal addresses the diagnosed issues, while the held-out split acts as a regression test to prevent degradation of unseen behaviors.
Performance is measured by the number of passed tasks, P_in(h) and P_ho(h), on the respective splits. A candidate is accepted only if it improves performance on at least one split *without degrading the other*. Formally, this acceptance rule is: ∆^(j)_in ≥ 0, ∆^(j)_ho ≥ 0, and max(∆^(j)_in, ∆^(j)_ho) > 0. This conservative criterion prevents trade-offs where improvements in one area come at the cost of another. If multiple compatible candidates are accepted in a round, their edits are merged to form the next harness (h_t+1). Rejected candidates are logged but do not alter the active harness. The entire process, including changed surfaces, outcomes, and decisions, is auditable.

#### 3.4. Experimental Setup

- **Benchmark:** Terminal-Bench-2.0, a multi-turn agentic benchmark involving terminal interaction in containerized environments. A fixed 64-case subset, excluding tasks with unstable external resources or multimodal inputs, was used to reduce evaluation noise.
- **Models:** Three distinct LLMs were used: MiniMax M2.5, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, and GLM-5. The model is fixed throughout its respective Self-Harness run and acts as both the agent and the proposer.
- **Harness:** The initial harness was a minimal DeepAgent-based system, comprising a short system prompt and default filesystem/shell tools (Figure 3). Self-Harness could only modify declared configuration points within this harness definition.
- **Splits:** The task set was partitioned into a held-in split (for evidence generation) and a held-out split (for regression testing, unseen by the proposer). Split assignments were fixed across harness variants.
- **Metrics:** The primary metric was Pass (%), the percentage of tasks successfully completed according to the benchmark verifier, computed over two repeated attempts for each candidate.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments demonstrated that Self-Harness consistently improved agent performance across all three tested language models on the Terminal-Bench-2.0 benchmark.

#### 4.1. Quantitative Performance Improvements

- **MiniMax M2.5:**
 - Held-in pass rate increased from 43.0% to 50.0% (an absolute gain of 7.0 percentage points, or 16% relative improvement).
 - Held-out pass rate increased from 40.5% to 61.9% (an absolute gain of 21.4 percentage points, or 53% relative improvement).
- **Qwen3.5-35B-A3B:**
 - Held-in pass rate increased from 15.1% to 36.0% (an absolute gain of 20.9 percentage points, or 138% relative improvement).
 - Held-out pass rate increased from 23.8% to 38.1% (an absolute gain of 14.3 percentage points, or 60% relative improvement).
- **GLM-5:**
 - Held-in pass rate increased from 47.7% to 57.0% (an absolute gain of 9.3 percentage points, or 20% relative improvement).
 - Held-out pass rate increased from 42.9% to 57.1% (an absolute gain of 14.2 percentage points, or 33% relative improvement).

These results indicate that Self-Harness successfully evolved an initial minimal harness into model-specific configurations that yielded measurable performance gains. Importantly, improvements were observed on both held-in and held-out tasks, suggesting that the system generated broadly useful modifications rather than merely overfitting to observed failures. No promoted harness exhibited degradation on either split, validating the effectiveness of the conservative acceptance rule.

#### 4.2. Qualitative Analysis of Harness Evolution and Retained Edits

Qualitative analysis of the evolved harnesses and execution traces revealed that Self-Harness introduced targeted, model-specific changes rather than generic instructions.
- **MiniMax M2.5:** The accepted edits addressed issues like missing required artifacts, schema-invalid tool content, and stalled tool-use loops. The final harness encouraged earlier creation of required outputs, more careful handling of structured tool content, and redirection of execution after prolonged unproductive tool interaction. For example, in a `count-dataset-tokens` task, the edited harness guided the agent from prolonged dataset exploration to a concrete workflow of identifying metadata, computing counts, and writing/verifying the answer file, which was a significant improvement over the initial harness where the agent timed out without creating the artifact.
- **Qwen3.5-35B-A3B:** The promoted harness added constraints focusing on artifact checking, recovery from missing artifacts, discipline regarding command retries, and tool-error-triggered middleware. These changes improved the agent's ability to recover from file-editing or tool failures and ensure required artifacts were in place. In an `extract-elf` task, the edited harness, after a tool error, used a triggered system prompt to redirect the agent to recreate a missing extractor script, fix parsing logic, write the output file, and perform targeted validation, preventing a failure due to a missing artifact that occurred with the initial harness.
- **GLM-5:** The accepted edits targeted late artifact production, external computation, session-scoped tools, and transitioning from exploration to implementation. The changes aimed to make environment modifications (e.g., `PATH` changes) persistent across shell sessions and to encourage a shift from prolonged exploration to active implementation and testing. In a `build-pov-ray` task, the edited harness led to more bounded, staged operations, early validation of alternative sources, and repair of failed sanity checks, contrasting with the initial harness's monolithic downloads and rationalization of failures.

A common theme across all models was the improvement of artifact reliability. However, the specific interventions varied, indicating that Self-Harness successfully diagnosed and addressed distinct execution pathologies unique to each base model. For instance, M2.5's harness focused on content tag formation, Qwen3.5's on dependency prechecks and retry discipline, and GLM-5's on persistent environment changes. This model-specific adaptation suggests that Self-Harness can effectively translate observed model weaknesses into concrete, executable harness-level interventions.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Self-Harness paradigm represents a notable advancement in the field of LLM-based agent development, proposing a method for agents to autonomously improve their operational scaffolding.

#### 5.1. Significance

- **Scalability for Diverse LLMs:** Self-Harness offers a potential solution to the problem of manually engineering model-specific harnesses, which is increasingly unsustainable given the proliferation and rapid evolution of LLMs. By enabling agents to adapt their own harnesses, the approach scales better with model diversity.
- **Reduced External Dependence:** The paradigm lessens the reliance on human experts or computationally intensive external meta-optimizers. This can be particularly beneficial for proprietary or frontier models where external access or detailed architectural knowledge is limited.
- **Empirical, Testable Harness Evolution:** By framing harness improvement as an empirical state transition, Self-Harness ensures that modifications are grounded in behavioral evidence and validated through rigorous regression testing. This auditability and testability contribute to more robust and reliable agent systems.
- **Model-Specific Adaptations:** The qualitative findings demonstrate that Self-Harness does not merely generate generic improvements but produces targeted, model-specific changes that address unique failure modes of different LLMs. This capacity for fine-grained adaptation enhances overall agent performance.
- **Foundational for Self-Creating Systems:** Conceptually, Self-Harness points towards a future where AI systems are not only influenced by their environments but also actively participate in shaping and refining their own operational parameters, echoing the idea of self-creation.

#### 5.2. Potential Impact

- **More Robust and Adaptable Agents:** Agents capable of self-improving their harnesses could exhibit greater resilience to novel tasks and environmental variations, continuously optimizing their strategies and tools.
- **Accelerated Agent Development:** Automated harness refinement could significantly speed up the development and deployment cycles of LLM-based agents across various applications, as the tuning phase becomes automated.
- **Emergence of Novel Behaviors:** By iteratively refining its harness, an agent might discover or operationalize previously unarticulated strategies or tool-use patterns that human engineers might not anticipate.
- **New Directions in Agent Engineering:** The work suggests a shift in agent engineering from purely human-centric design to a collaborative model where agents contribute to their own architectural evolution.

#### 5.3. Limitations

The paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Bounded Edits and Fixed Benchmarks:** The current work focuses on bounded harness edits within fixed benchmark environments, rather than open-ended self-improvement in arbitrary scenarios.
- **Reliance on Verifier Quality:** The effectiveness of Self-Harness is dependent on the quality and fidelity of verifier outcomes and trace records. Suboptimal verifiers could lead to misdiagnosed weaknesses or ineffective proposals.
- **Conservative Acceptance Rule:** While beneficial for robustness, the conservative acceptance rule (non-regression on both splits) might slow down the improvement process, especially for higher-stakes changes where more aggressive optimization might be desired.
- **Not Open-Ended:** The system is not designed for open-ended, continuous self-improvement in an unconstrained environment but rather for targeted optimization within a defined operational scope.

Despite these limitations, Self-Harness lays a foundation for future research into agent autonomy and self-evolution, providing a structured and empirically grounded approach to enabling LLM-based agents to improve their own operating mechanisms.
