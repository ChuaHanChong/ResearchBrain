# Failure-Aware RL: Reliable Offline-to-Online Reinforcement Learning with Self-Recovery for Real-World Manipulation

> [!summary] Summary
> Failure-Aware RL (FARL) introduces a framework for reliable offline-to-online reinforcement learning, mitigating human-intervention-requiring failures during real-world robotic manipulation by integrating a world-model-based safety critic and a pre-trained recovery policy. The system reduced failure episodes by an average of 43.6% in simulation and achieved dramatic reductions on a real Franka Emika Panda robot, while maintaining or improving task performance.

## Problem

- Online fine-tuning of pre-trained policies in real-world reinforcement learning often leads to "Intervention-requiring Failures" (IR Failures) due to inherent exploration randomness.
- IR Failures are costly, demanding human intervention, causing damage, and significantly hindering the practical deployment and scalability of robotic systems.
- Existing safe RL methods often prematurely constrain exploration or are incompatible with the offline-to-online paradigm, leading to suboptimal performance when fine-tuning pre-trained policies.

## Method

- **FARL** pre-trains a task policy, a ==recovery policy== from specific recovery demonstrations, and a ==latent world model== augmented with a ==constraint prediction head== to forecast future failures.
- During online fine-tuning, the world model predicts the safety of an action sampled from the task policy by rolling out future steps.
- If an action is predicted as risky, it is discarded and replaced with a safer alternative sampled from the pre-trained recovery policy, guiding exploration away from failures.

## Results

- FARL achieved an average **43.6%** reduction in failure episodes across four FailureBench simulation tasks and demonstrated a dramatic reduction in real-world IR Failures on a Franka Emika Panda robot (e.g., from 10 to 2 failures in Franka Disturbed Push).
- The framework maintained or improved average task returns compared to baseline methods like Uni-O4 in simulation and showed enhanced performance with lower standard deviations in real-world experiments.
- FARL significantly outperformed state-of-the-art online safe RL algorithms (PPO-Lagrangian, P3O, CPO) by over **800%** in average returns when applied to fine-tuning pre-trained policies, highlighting the incompatibility of traditional safe RL with this paradigm.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly predicting future constraint violations with a world-model-based safety critic is crucial for effective risk assessment in dynamic online fine-tuning, outperforming simpler safety metrics.
> - Leveraging a pre-trained recovery policy, derived from expert recovery demonstrations, provides a more robust and effective safety mechanism than implicit planning from a world model alone.
> - A "failure-aware" approach can achieve a balance between drastically reducing intervention-requiring failures and maintaining or improving overall task performance and robustness.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research directly addresses a critical and persistent challenge in the broader field of robotic reinforcement learning (RL): safely and reliably deploying learned policies in real-world environments. It operates at the intersection of several key areas: deep reinforcement learning, offline RL, online fine-tuning, safe RL, and robotic manipulation.

Traditionally, RL has shown immense promise for learning complex behaviors, but its data inefficiency and the necessity for extensive exploration have limited its direct application to physical robots. Pure imitation learning (IL), while data-efficient, often struggles with generalization beyond the training distribution. Offline RL emerged to address data efficiency by learning from static datasets, but it faces challenges with out-of-distribution (OOD) actions during online deployment.

The field has thus moved towards **offline-to-online RL**, where policies are pre-trained on offline datasets (often from demonstrations) and then fine-tuned online. This approach combines the benefits of robust initial learning from demonstrations with the adaptive capabilities of online RL. However, as the authors highlight, this paradigm is still plagued by "Intervention-requiring Failures (IR Failures)" during the online exploration phase. These failures are not merely suboptimal actions but critical events (e.g., breaking fragile objects, robot damage, objects falling out of reach) that necessitate human intervention, making real-world deployment costly, time-consuming, and unsafe.

**Safe RL** has been an active area of research, primarily focusing on constrained Markov Decision Processes (CMDPs) and methods like Lagrangian relaxation, Lyapunov functions, and control barrier functions. However, the authors argue that many existing safe RL methods primarily focus on learning from scratch and can prematurely constrain exploration, potentially limiting overall performance. Furthermore, their ablation studies empirically show that these traditional safe RL methods significantly underperform when directly applied to fine-tuning pre-trained policies, often due to distributional shifts and incompatibility with leveraging prior knowledge.

"Failure-Aware RL" (FARL) distinguishes itself by focusing specifically on **minimizing IR Failures in the offline-to-online context for real-world manipulation**. Instead of aiming for general safety guarantees from scratch, FARL recognizes that robust pre-trained policies exist, and the challenge lies in safely *refining* them. It builds upon modern offline-to-online algorithms like Uni-O4 but explicitly integrates mechanisms for failure prediction and recovery. This targeted approach fills a crucial gap, offering a practical pathway to more reliable robot learning. The introduction of **FailureBench** further contributes to the landscape by providing a standardized benchmark for evaluating failure-aware algorithms, which was previously lacking for this specific problem formulation. By addressing IR Failures, FARL pushes the boundaries of autonomous robot learning towards more practical and deployable solutions in dynamic and sensitive environments.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The overarching objective of this research is to bridge the gap between powerful deep reinforcement learning algorithms and their practical, safe deployment on real-world robotic systems, particularly for manipulation tasks.

The **primary motivation** stems from the inherent challenge of "Intervention-requiring Failures (IR Failures)" during the online exploration phase of reinforcement learning. While offline-to-online RL provides a promising pathway by leveraging pre-training on demonstrations, the continuous fine-tuning in real environments necessitates exploration. This exploration, by its very nature, introduces randomness and can lead to actions that cause severe consequences, such as:
- **Irreversible damage:** Breaking fragile objects (e.g., glass, vases).
- **Robot damage:** Colliding the robot arm with objects or environment boundaries.
- **Loss of task progress:** Pushing objects out of the workspace, making them unreachable.

These IR Failures are not minor setbacks; they demand costly human intervention, either to reset the environment, repair damaged items, or even fix the robot itself. Such interruptions significantly hinder the efficiency and scalability of real-world RL development, making it impractical for continuous learning and deployment. The authors explicitly state that these failures "hinder the practical deployment of such a paradigm."

To address this critical bottleneck, the **key objectives** of FARL are twofold:
1. **Minimize IR Failures during Real-World Online Reinforcement Learning:** The core aim is to drastically reduce the number of instances where human intervention is required, making the online fine-tuning process safer and more autonomous.
2. **Simultaneously Improve Task Performance and Generalization:** While prioritizing safety, the framework must not sacrifice the ability of RL to learn optimal policies and adapt to dynamic real-world conditions. The goal is to balance effective learning with robust safety.

The authors propose the concept of **Failure-Aware Offline-to-Online Reinforcement Learning (FARL)** as a new paradigm to achieve these objectives. They are motivated by the observation that existing online and offline-to-online RL algorithms, despite their strengths, frequently encounter IR Failures due to their inherent exploration-exploitation trade-off. By focusing on a "failure-aware" approach, FARL seeks to proactively predict and prevent these failures, allowing for more reliable and efficient robotic learning in the real world.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

FARL (Failure-Aware Offline-to-Online Reinforcement Learning) introduces a novel framework that integrates a world-model-based safety critic and a recovery policy to minimize IR Failures during online fine-tuning. The methodology is structured into two main phases: an offline pre-training phase and an online fine-tuning phase with safe exploration.

**A. Offline Pre-training Phase:**
The initial phase focuses on preparing three key components that will guide safe online exploration:
1. **Task Policy Pre-training:** The main policy, designed to achieve the task objective, is pre-trained using "task demonstrations" (trajectories showing successful task completion). This process involves an initial behavior cloning phase, followed by fine-tuning with a policy gradient objective, similar to Uni-O4, which unifies offline and online learning. This establishes a strong initial policy without needing extensive online exploration.
2. **Recovery Policy Pre-training:** A dedicated "recovery policy" is trained using "recovery demonstrations." These demonstrations are crucial as they illustrate how to avoid or escape from states that are near-failure or already in a failure trajectory. The training mirrors the task policy's pre-training (behavior cloning followed by Uni-O4 offline fine-tuning). Critically, this recovery policy remains fixed during the online phase because failure data is typically sparse and expensive to collect, preventing effective online updates. This fixed policy provides a robust, pre-learned safety net.
3. **World Model Pre-training:** A latent world model is pre-trained using both "task demonstrations" and "failure demonstrations." The "failure demonstrations" are particularly important, consisting of state-action sequences that lead to IR Failures. The world model's objective is not just to predict future states or rewards, but significantly, it is augmented with a **constraint prediction head**. This head is trained to predict future constraint violations (i.e., failures) over a limited number of near-future steps (H-steps), rather than an entire episode. The model consists of seven components (representation encoder, latent dynamics, reward, value, policy, decoder, and constraint predictor) and uses a multi-objective loss function that balances reward prediction, value consistency, latent state consistency, state decoding, and crucially, constraint prediction (Equation 4-9). The world model, like the recovery policy, is fixed during the online phase to maintain a stable safety evaluation mechanism.

**B. Online Fine-tuning with Recovery Phase:**
In this phase, the pre-trained task policy is fine-tuned in the real (or simulated) environment, but its exploration is guarded by the pre-trained world model and recovery policy.
1. **Safe Exploration Mechanism:** Before executing an action `a` sampled from the current task policy `π_task` for a given state `s`, the pre-trained world model is used to predict the near-future safety `C_π_task_H` (Equation 11). This involves rolling out predictions for a few steps into the future based on the world model's dynamics and constraint prediction head.
 - **If safe (C_π_task_H ≤ ε_safe):** The action `a` from `π_task` is executed. The resulting transition `T_π_task` (s, a, s', r) is used to update the task policy.
 - **If risky (C_π_task_H > ε_safe):** The risky action `a` is *discarded*. Instead, an action `a_rec` is sampled from the pre-trained `recovery policy π_rec`. This recovery action is then executed, and the resulting transition `T_π_rec` (s, a_rec, s', r) is used for policy updates.
2. **Task Policy Update:** The task policy is fine-tuned using the standard PPO objective (Equation 3) with GAE advantage estimation, but critically, it uses the "safe transitions" (either `T_π_task` or `T_π_rec`) gathered during the guarded online exploration. This means the policy learns from experiences that have been filtered to minimize failures.

**C. Theoretical Justification ("Action Correction Benefit"):**
The authors provide a theoretical analysis (Theorem 1) demonstrating that FARL's action correction mechanism offers a "policy improvement" benefit beyond a baseline RL approach. Under reasonable assumptions (non-trivial risk distribution, probabilistic safe recovery, and safe actions providing better expected advantage), FARL's corrected transitions yield a greater improvement in the objective function. This is because FARL effectively replaces risky, low-advantage actions with safer, higher-advantage recovery actions, leading to both improved safety and enhanced performance.

**D. FailureBench Benchmark:**
To systematically evaluate FARL, the authors introduce FailureBench, a benchmark suite based on modified MetaWorld environments. These modifications incorporate realistic IR Failure scenarios (e.g., Bounded Push, Bounded Soccer, Fragile Push Wall, Obstructed Push) that necessitate human intervention, allowing for quantifiable assessment of failure reduction and task performance.

This comprehensive methodology, combining specialized offline pre-training with an intelligent online safety filter and recovery mechanism, aims to provide a robust solution for deploying RL in sensitive real-world robotic applications.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments, conducted in both simulation (FailureBench) and on a real Franka Emika Panda robot, demonstrate the significant effectiveness of FARL in reducing IR Failures while maintaining or improving task performance.

**A. Simulation Experiments on FailureBench:**
1. **Dramatic Reduction in IR Failures:** FARL consistently and significantly reduced the number of failure episodes compared to the baseline Uni-O4 algorithm. Across all four tasks in FailureBench, FARL achieved an average reduction of 43.6% in failure episodes, with reductions as high as 65.8% in dynamic and complex constraint scenarios like Bounded Soccer and Obstructed Push (Figure 4). This confirms FARL's primary objective of enhancing safety during online exploration.
2. **Competitive or Superior Task Performance:** Despite the additional safety constraints, FARL maintained or even improved average returns (task rewards) compared to Uni-O4 (Figure 5). This finding is crucial as it demonstrates that FARL effectively balances exploration and safety without sacrificing the learning efficacy, allowing for effective policy refinement.
3. **Superiority over Traditional Safe RL Methods:** When compared against state-of-the-art online safe RL algorithms (PPO-Lagrangian, P3O, CPO) used for fine-tuning pre-trained policies, FARL exhibited vastly superior performance, achieving over 800% higher average returns across tasks (Table I). This highlights a fundamental incompatibility between these traditional safe RL methods and the offline-to-online paradigm, likely due to their optimization objectives creating detrimental distributional shifts that prevent effective utilization of pre-trained knowledge.
4. **Ablation Studies Validate Design Choices:**
 - **World Model vs. Simple Safety Critic:** Replacing FARL's world-model-based safety critic (which predicts future failures) with a simpler Q-function-based critic (from Recovery-RL) led to a substantial increase in failure episodes (e.g., 92% more failures in Bounded Soccer). This confirms the necessity and superior predictive power of FARL's explicit world model with future rollout prediction for accurate risk assessment.
 - **Recovery Policy vs. MPPI Planning:** Substituting FARL's pre-trained recovery policy with a Model Predictive Path Integral (MPPI) planning method also resulted in higher failure rates. This indicates that a learned recovery policy, leveraging expert demonstrations of recovery behaviors, is more effective, especially in dynamic environments where implicit planning from a world model alone may struggle to find optimal recovery actions.
 - **Combined Benefits:** The ablation studies collectively demonstrate that the synergy of a specialized world model for future failure prediction and a pre-trained recovery policy is critical for FARL's robust performance in both failure reduction and task reward.

**B. Real-World Experiments on Franka Emika Panda Robot:**
1. **Dramatic Real-World IR Failure Reduction:** Deploying FARL on a Franka Emika Panda robot for three challenging manipulation tasks (Franka Fragile Push Wall, Franka Disturbed Push, Franka Bounded Soccer) yielded a dramatic reduction in human-intervention-requiring failures over 50 training episodes (Figure 7). For instance, in the "Franka Disturbed Push" task, Uni-O4 resulted in 10 failures, while FARL reduced this to just 2. This is a direct validation of FARL's practical utility in real-world scenarios, making the online fine-tuning process significantly safer and more efficient.
2. **Enhanced Real-World Performance and Robustness:** FARL-finetuned policies achieved noticeably higher average returns and significantly lower standard deviations compared to initial policies or the Uni-O4 baseline (Table III). This indicates that FARL not only reduces failures but also enables the robot to learn more robust and effective task behaviors in complex, dynamic real-world settings.
3. **Low Data Requirement for Demonstrations:** The authors highlight that collecting the three types of demonstrations (task, recovery, failure) for real-world tasks required only 30-60 minutes of human teleoperation per task, suggesting a relatively data-efficient approach for practical deployment.

In summary, the findings robustly support FARL's ability to provide reliable offline-to-online reinforcement learning by effectively mitigating IR Failures, without compromising task performance, and outperforming alternative approaches in both simulated and real-world robotic manipulation tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The research presented in "Failure-Aware RL" holds significant implications for the advancement and practical deployment of robotic systems, particularly those that rely on deep reinforcement learning.

**1. Enabling Reliable Real-World RL Deployment:** The most profound impact of FARL is its direct addressal of a critical bottleneck in real-world reinforcement learning: Intervention-requiring Failures (IR Failures). By drastically reducing the need for human intervention during online policy fine-tuning, FARL makes the entire robot learning pipeline more autonomous, scalable, and cost-effective. This can accelerate the development and deployment of robots in various domains, from manufacturing and logistics to service robotics, where mistakes can be costly or dangerous.

**2. A New Paradigm for Safe RL:** FARL introduces a novel "failure-aware" approach within the offline-to-online RL paradigm. Unlike traditional safe RL methods that often prioritize general safety constraints from scratch, FARL focuses on explicitly predicting and recovering from specific, high-consequence failures when fine-tuning pre-trained policies. This shift in focus is more practical for real-world robotics, where policies often start from expert demonstrations and need to adapt reliably. It suggests that a more targeted approach to safety, rather than a generalized one, can be more effective for complex robotic tasks.

**3. Dual Benefits of Safety and Performance:** A key finding is that FARL not only improves safety but also maintains or enhances task performance. This disproves the common assumption that increased safety necessarily comes at the cost of performance or exploration. By intelligently guiding exploration away from risky actions towards safer, recovery alternatives (as theoretically justified by the "action correction" mechanism), FARL enables more effective learning, leading to robust and high-performing policies. This dual benefit makes FARL a compelling solution for practical applications.

**4. Contribution of FailureBench:** The introduction of FailureBench as a dedicated simulation benchmark for evaluating failure-aware RL algorithms is a valuable contribution to the research community. It provides a standardized and realistic set of environments that incorporate common IR Failure scenarios, facilitating systematic comparison and development of future algorithms in this critical area.

**5. Reduced Human Effort and Cost:** By minimizing IR Failures, FARL directly translates to reduced human oversight, fewer physical resets, less damage to robots or environments, and ultimately, lower operational costs. This efficiency gain is crucial for industrial applications where robot downtime and maintenance are significant concerns. For researchers, it allows for more ambitious and longer-duration real-world experiments with greater confidence.

**6. Foundation for Future Robotic Capabilities:** FARL's robust framework lays a foundation for future advancements. The authors themselves point to promising future directions:
 - **Multimodality Integration:** Incorporating additional sensory inputs like 2D/3D vision and tactile sensing could further enrich the system's perceptual capabilities, enabling more nuanced failure prediction and recovery.
 - **Advanced Robotic Platforms:** Extending FARL to mobile manipulation and dual-arm robotic platforms would enable it to tackle even more complex tasks.
 - **Large-Scale Failure World Models:** Pre-training comprehensive failure world models across diverse tasks and robot embodiments could lead to better generalization to unseen failure modes, moving towards more intelligent and adaptable robotic systems.

In essence, FARL represents a significant step towards autonomous robotic agents that can learn and operate reliably in unstructured and sensitive real-world environments, accelerating the transition of advanced AI research from laboratories to practical applications.
