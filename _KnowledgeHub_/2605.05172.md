# When Life Gives You BC, Make Q-functions: Extracting Q-values from Behavior Cloning for On-Robot Reinforcement Learning

> [!summary] Summary
> Q2RL introduces an algorithm that boosts Behavior Cloning (BC) policies by extracting implicit Q-values to guide online Reinforcement Learning (RL), enabling self-improvement for robotic tasks. The method facilitates rapid and safe adaptation on physical robots, achieving performance improvements up to 3.75x within 1-2 hours of interaction on contact-rich manipulation tasks, even without access to original BC training data.

## Problem

- Behavior Cloning (BC) policies cannot self-improve online, leading to performance degradation in novel situations or with insufficient data.
- Pure online Reinforcement Learning (RL) typically requires extensive and potentially unsafe exploration, which is costly and risky for real-world robot deployment.
- Existing offline-to-online methods often face the "unlearning" problem, discarding effective behaviors, or require large, diverse datasets not always available in robotics.

## Method

- **Q2RL** derives an estimated ==Q-function (Q̂_BC)== from a pre-trained BC policy by assuming its action distribution follows a ==Boltzmann distribution==, utilizing log-probabilities and entropy.
- ==Q-Gating==: initializes a learning RL Q-function (Q_RL) from Q̂_BC and employs a gating mechanism that executes the BC policy's action or the RL policy's action based on which has a higher Q-value.
- Integrates an ==auxiliary BC loss== into the RL actor's loss function to maintain safer behaviors and prevent the RL policy from deviating excessively.

## Results

- Achieved up to **3.75x performance improvement** on real robots (e.g., Peg Insertion, Pipe Assembly, Modified Kitting) within **1–2 hours** of online interaction, without requiring the original BC training data.
- Consistently outperformed or matched strong offline-to-online RL baselines like **IBRL, CalQL, and WSRL** in simulation, especially in scenarios with limited or no access to BC training data.
- Demonstrated enhanced **safety** in real-world experiments by avoiding costly exploratory actions and robot faults, a common issue with unguided online RL.
- Qualitatively showed intelligent action switching, leveraging BC for robust base movements and RL for fine, contact-rich manipulation and adaptation to task shifts.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Implicit value information within a pre-trained Behavior Cloning policy can be systematically extracted to serve as a strong initialization and guide for online Reinforcement Learning.
> - A Q-gating mechanism enables an RL policy to incrementally improve upon BC, preserving high-value expert actions while allowing exploration of potentially superior alternatives.
> - This combined approach provides a sample-efficient and safer pathway for on-robot learning, reducing exploration costs and mitigating risks in real-world deployments.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Behavior Cloning (BC) has demonstrated efficacy in robot learning by directly modeling expert demonstrations through supervised learning. However, a significant limitation of BC is its lack of a self-guided mechanism for online improvement. When environmental conditions change or the initial training data is insufficient, BC policies can exhibit performance degradation due to covariate shift, leading to compounding errors.

To address these limitations, several research directions have emerged:
- **Interactive imitation learning:** Requires continuous expert supervision for online finetuning.
- **Offline Reinforcement Learning (RL):** Aims to learn value functions from pre-collected datasets without further online interaction. While effective with large, diverse datasets, many offline RL methods struggle with smaller, successful-only robotics datasets and can introduce pessimistic biases or generate unsafe behaviors due to out-of-distribution actions.
- **Offline-to-online learning:** Combines initial offline pre-training (often via BC or offline RL) with subsequent online RL finetuning. A common challenge in these methods is "unlearning" previously good actions from the offline policy, leading to performance collapse. Some approaches introduce residual terms to BC actions, requiring careful tuning to prevent large deviations. Other methods, like Imitation Bootstrapped RL (IBRL), use a randomly initialized critic to combine BC and RL actions, often requiring demonstration data to seed the replay buffer for stable training.
- **On-robot Reinforcement Learning:** This domain specifically demands sample-efficient and safe learning techniques, as random exploration on physical hardware can be costly, lead to wear and tear, or be outright unsafe. Existing on-robot RL systems leverage asynchronous architectures and may incorporate human intervention or reward shaping to enhance efficiency.

This work, Q2RL, aims to bridge the gap between the strong initial performance of BC and the adaptive improvement capabilities of online RL. It specifically targets the challenges of preserving good BC behaviors, enabling efficient online improvement without forgetting, ensuring safety during exploration, and reducing reliance on large or continuously available offline datasets, which is particularly relevant for sample-efficient on-robot learning. The method positions itself as an advancement in offline-to-online learning by extracting explicit Q-values from a black-box BC policy to guide a new RL policy, rather than relying on uncalibrated critics or residual action formulations.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop an efficient offline-to-online learning algorithm that combines the strengths of pre-trained Behavior Cloning (BC) policies with the iterative improvement capabilities of online Reinforcement Learning (RL). Specifically, the authors aim to:

1. **Preserve Effective BC Behaviors:** Prevent the online RL process from "unlearning" good actions initially provided by the BC policy, a common issue in existing offline-to-online methods due to distribution mismatch.
2. **Enable Self-Guided Online Improvement:** Allow the policy to adapt and find better actions when the pre-trained BC policy performs sub-optimally (e.g., due to insufficient training data, limited epochs, or environmental distribution shifts).
3. **Ensure Safe and Stable Transition to Online RL:** Develop a mechanism that allows for online exploration and learning without generating unsafe behaviors, which is critical for real-world robotic deployments where random actions can cause damage to the robot or environment.
4. **Achieve Sample Efficiency:** Minimize the amount of online interaction steps required for the RL policy to surpass BC performance, making it practical for on-robot learning settings where data collection is expensive and time-consuming.
5. **Reduce Data Dependence:** Develop a method that does not require continuous access to the original, potentially large, offline demonstration dataset during the online finetuning phase.

The motivation stems from the observed limitations of current approaches:
- **BC's Static Nature:** While simple and effective for initial learning, BC policies are prone to performance collapse when faced with novel situations or subtle environmental changes because they lack an inherent mechanism for self-correction.
- **Challenges in Offline RL and Offline-to-Online Methods:** Many offline RL approaches require vast and diverse datasets, which are not always available for robotic tasks. Offline-to-online methods often struggle with stably transitioning from offline pre-training to online learning, frequently leading to policies replacing effective learned actions with poorer ones. Some current methods also struggle with safety, as they might involve training an RL policy from scratch or using randomly initialized critics, which can lead to unpredictable or dangerous exploration.
- **On-Robot Constraints:** Real-world robotic applications necessitate algorithms that are highly sample-efficient and prioritize safety during online interaction, as purely exploratory or unsafe actions can be detrimental.

By addressing these points, the authors intend to provide a robust framework that leverages the initial knowledge from BC while enabling continuous, safe, and efficient improvement in dynamic and real-world robotic scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed algorithm, Q2RL (Q-Estimation and Q-Gating from BC for Reinforcement Learning), comprises two main phases: Q-Estimation and Q-Gating.

**Phase 1: Q-Estimation from a Behavior Cloning Policy**
The first phase involves estimating a Q-function, $\hat{Q}_{BC}(s, a)$, for a pre-trained behavior cloning policy, $\pi_{BC}$. The core assumption is that the BC policy's action distribution can be approximated as a Boltzmann distribution with respect to its Q-function. From this assumption, the authors derive an analytic formula for the Q-function:

$\hat{Q}_{BC} = V_{BC}(s) + \alpha \log \pi_{BC}(a | s) + \alpha H[\pi_{BC}(\cdot | s)]$

where:
- $V_{BC}(s)$ is the value function of the BC policy in state $s$.
- $\log \pi_{BC}(a | s)$ is the log probability of taking action $a$ in state $s$ under the BC policy.
- $H[\pi_{BC}(\cdot | s)]$ is the entropy of the BC policy's action distribution in state $s$.
- $\alpha$ is a temperature parameter, assumed to be 1 in this work.

To compute the terms:
- **Value Function Estimation ($V_{BC}(s)$):** The authors collect a few initial online interaction episodes using the BC policy. From these rollouts, Monte Carlo returns are calculated using the online reward function. These returns are then used to train a separate value estimator that approximates $V_{BC}(s)$.
- **Log Probabilities ($\log \pi_{BC}(a | s)$) and Entropy ($H[\pi_{BC}(\cdot | s)]$)**: These terms are directly obtained from the pre-trained BC policy. The methodology is designed to be agnostic to the specific BC policy class, provided it can supply action likelihoods and entropy. Examples are given for Gaussian policies (where these terms have closed-form expressions) and Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs).

This Q-Estimation phase allows for bootstrapping online reinforcement learning without needing access to the original BC training data, relying only on the BC policy's online rollouts and its ability to provide action likelihoods and entropy.

**Phase 2: Q-Gating for Online Reinforcement Learning**
The second phase uses the estimated $\hat{Q}_{BC}$ to guide online reinforcement learning. This phase involves:

1. **Q-Function Initialization:** Two separate Q-functions are initialized from $\hat{Q}_{BC}$:
 - $\hat{Q}_{BC}$: This function is kept frozen and serves as a stable reference representing the value of the BC policy.
 - $Q_{RL}$: This Q-function is trainable and serves as the critic for the RL policy. It is initially trained through a supervised learning period to match $\hat{Q}_{BC}$ using the same BC rollouts used for Q-Estimation.

2. **Action Selection (Q-Gating):** During online training, both the BC policy ($\pi_{BC}$) and the RL policy ($\pi_{RL}$) propose actions, $a_{BC}$ and $a_{RL}$ respectively, for the current state $s$. These actions are evaluated using their respective Q-functions: $\hat{Q}_{BC}(s, a_{BC})$ for the BC action and $Q_{RL}(s, a_{RL})$ for the RL action. The action with the higher estimated Q-value is then executed in the environment:
 $a = \begin{cases} a_{BC} \sim \pi_{BC}(s), & \text{if } \hat{Q}_{BC}(s, a_{BC}) > Q_{RL}(s, a_{RL}) \\ a_{RL} \sim \pi_{RL}(s_t), & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$
 This mechanism ensures that good BC actions are preserved while allowing the RL policy to explore and find potentially better actions. The collected samples from this interaction are stored in a replay buffer to update $Q_{RL}$ and $\pi_{RL}$ using an off-policy RL algorithm.

**Implementation Details:**
- The temperature parameter $\alpha$ for the Boltzmann parameterization is set to 1.
- The underlying off-policy RL algorithm used is Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), optimized for real-world learning.
- An auxiliary Behavior Cloning (BC) loss is added to the RL actor loss. This regularization term aims to keep the RL policy's behavior close to the BC policy, contributing to safer and smoother actions, particularly important for real robot deployments.

The overall approach facilitates a stable transition from offline pre-training to online improvement, leveraging the initial competence of BC while providing a principled mechanism for iterative enhancement and safe exploration.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents extensive experiments in both simulated and real-world on-robot settings to evaluate Q2RL.

**A. Simulation Environments (D4RL and Robomimic Benchmarks)**

Q2RL was evaluated on D4RL tasks (Kitchen-Complete, Adroit-Pen, Adroit-Door) and robomimic tasks (Lift, Can, Square) using both state-based and image-based observations. The performance was compared against several baselines: Conservative Q-Learning (CQL), Calibrated Q-Learning (CalQL), Warm-Start RL (WSRL), RL with Prior Demonstrations (RLPD), and Imitation Bootstrapped RL (IBRL).

1. **D4RL Results:**
 - **Kitchen Task:** Offline RL methods performed poorly as the dataset contained only successful demonstrations, which is challenging for their conservative value estimation. Q2RL demonstrated better initial performance and significant online improvement, outperforming baselines.
 - **Pen Task:** Q2RL achieved near-optimal performance quickly and maintained it. WSRL, CalQL, and IBRL also reached high performance, while CQL struggled.
 - **Door Task:** WSRL achieved the highest success rate, but qualitative analysis of rollouts indicated that its policy learned simulator-exploiting behaviors that would be unrealistic in the real world. Q2RL, by remaining closer to the BC policy, learned more realistic and feasible actions.

2. **Robomimic Results (State and Image-based):**
 - **With Access to BC Training Data:** Q2RL consistently achieved high initial performance and converged rapidly to higher success rates than baselines. IBRL was competitive but started with limited or no performance, requiring substantial interaction to improve. For challenging tasks like Can-Image, IBRL struggled to reach base policy performance, while Q2RL outperformed it. RLPD only succeeded on simpler tasks.
 - **Without Access to BC Training Data:** This setting tested scenarios where only the pre-trained BC policy is available. Q2RL maintained strong performance and continued to improve, demonstrating its robustness without needing expert demonstrations or diverse offline datasets for seeding. In contrast, offline RL and offline-to-online methods (including IBRL) largely failed to learn meaningful performance, particularly for harder tasks. IBRL's reliance on seeded replay buffers for stable Q-value initialization proved critical in this scenario.

**B. Analysis of BC Actions Used During Online Training:**
The study investigated the proportion of BC vs. RL actions selected by Q2RL compared to IBRL. Q2RL rapidly recovered the original BC performance within a few online steps, often while utilizing BC actions less than 50% of the time, suggesting efficient learning of effective RL actions. As training progressed, Q2RL's Q-Gating mechanism intelligently selected BC actions for simple, known motions and RL actions for difficult, contact-rich segments. IBRL, despite similar BC action usage, required significantly more interaction to reach comparable performance, indicating less efficient action selection.

**C. Ablation Studies:**
- **Q-Initialization and Q-Gating:** Q-Gating was identified as a critical component for achieving high performance. While initializing $Q_{RL}$ with $\hat{Q}_{BC}$ did not always lead to superior final performance compared to random initialization in simulation, it provided a safer starting distribution, which is preferable for on-robot RL.
- **Robustness to Sub-Optimality:** Q2RL showed robustness to non-soft-optimal BC policies (e.g., deterministic policies with added noise), recovering from initial performance drops during online RL.
- **Auxiliary BC Loss:** The auxiliary BC loss in Q2RL enhanced safety and smoothness. While adding it to IBRL improved its performance relative to its no-BC loss variant, it did not enable IBRL to match Q2RL's performance without data access.
- **Number of Rollouts for Q-Estimation:** Q2RL demonstrated competitive performance with as few as 25 rollouts for Q-Estimation, indicating its sample efficiency for this initial phase.
- **Initial BC Performance:** Q2RL consistently improved BC policies across a wide range of initial success rates (from 10% to 75%).

**D. Real-World On-Robot RL Experiments:**
Experiments were conducted on a 7-DOF Franka Panda robot for contact-rich and high-precision manipulation tasks: Peg Insertion, Plumbing Pipe Assembly, and Kitting with Task Distribution Shift. GMM-RNN BC policies were trained initially.

1. **Quantitative Results:** Q2RL achieved significant performance improvements within 1-2 hours of online interaction (approximately 60k-165k learner steps).
 - **Peg Insertion:** Q2RL achieved 100% success (1.4x improvement over BC). IBRL performed similarly with data seeding but failed without it.
 - **Pipe Assembly:** Q2RL achieved 75% success (3.75x improvement over BC). IBRL achieved 0% success, even with data seeding, on this long-horizon, high-action-space task.
 - **Modified Kitting (Distribution Shift):** Q2RL achieved 70% success (2x improvement over BC). IBRL achieved 0% success, even with data seeding. Q2RL demonstrated adaptability to the task distribution shift where BC policies struggled.

2. **Safety:** Q2RL exhibited no safety violations. Qualitatively, it produced smoother and safer actions early in training, attributed to the auxiliary BC loss, Q-Gating, and $Q_{RL}$ initialization with $\hat{Q}_{BC}$. IBRL, in contrast, caused two safety violations during Peg Insertion training due to excessive force.

3. **Qualitative Results:** Q2RL policies intelligently switched between BC and RL actions. For Pipe Assembly, BC actions were used for initial grasping and alignment, while RL actions handled the high-precision, contact-rich insertion. For Modified Kitting, BC actions were used for common behaviors like moving between bins, and RL actions adapted to new object locations (distribution shift). This strategic switching often corresponded to recovery behaviors as well.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The Q2RL algorithm represents a significant contribution to the field of robot learning, particularly in bridging the gap between imitation learning and reinforcement learning for real-world applications. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

1. **Efficient Offline-to-Online Learning:** Q2RL provides a method to efficiently transition from a pre-trained behavior cloning policy to an improved policy via online reinforcement learning. By extracting a Q-function from the BC policy and using it to guide subsequent RL, the algorithm bypasses common issues like "unlearning" good behaviors and the need for extensive offline data pre-training characteristic of other offline RL approaches.
2. **Sample-Efficient On-Robot Learning:** The ability of Q2RL to achieve substantial performance improvements (up to 3.75x improvement over BC, and up to 100% success rates) within 1-2 hours of online interaction on physical robots is highly impactful. This sample efficiency addresses a critical bottleneck in real-world robotics, where online data collection is costly and time-consuming.
3. **Enhanced Safety during Exploration:** Q2RL's Q-Gating mechanism and auxiliary BC loss contribute to safer online learning. By explicitly comparing the estimated values of BC and RL actions and prioritizing the better one, coupled with regularization towards the BC policy, the method reduces the risk of unsafe or unpredictable exploratory actions, which is paramount for deploying robots in practical environments. The qualitative observation of smoother, safer actions and the absence of safety violations compared to baselines underscore this impact.
4. **Robustness to Task Distribution Shifts:** The successful adaptation of Q2RL to the Modified Kitting task, where the environment underwent a distribution shift unseen by the original BC policy, demonstrates its ability to generalize and learn in altered conditions. This adaptability is crucial for real-world scenarios where environments are dynamic and rarely perfectly match training conditions.
5. **Reduced Data Dependency:** Q2RL does not require access to the original offline training data for the BC policy during its online improvement phase. It only needs the BC policy's ability to provide action likelihoods and entropy, and a few online rollouts for Q-Estimation. This makes the method broadly applicable, especially in settings where demonstration data is proprietary, limited, or cannot be stored indefinitely.
6. **Broader Applicability:** The method's design, which is agnostic to the specific BC policy class (as long as it provides action likelihoods and entropy), suggests potential for integration with various imitation learning architectures. Future work extending it to policy classes like diffusion models would further broaden its impact.
7. **Foundational for Future Research:** Q2RL's novel approach of "extracting" Q-values from BC provides a new perspective on leveraging imitation learning for RL, potentially inspiring further research into principled ways of combining model-based and model-free techniques, or integrating expert knowledge into RL value functions more directly.

In summary, Q2RL provides a practical, efficient, and safe solution for continuously improving robot policies learned through imitation, significantly advancing the feasibility of robust robotic learning in complex and dynamic real-world settings.
