# ActiveScale: Scaling Active Perception for Robots across Model, Data, and Hardware

> [!summary] Summary
> ActiveScale is a model-data-hardware framework for active-perception manipulation. A π0.5 VLA gains four-frame front-camera history plus learnable per-frame camera tokens supervised to predict pose and field of view, and outputs camera motion alongside arm actions. It mid-trains on over 1000 hours of egocentric human and robot data and collects demonstrations on a single-operator triple-arm teleoperation platform. Mean success across five real-world tasks rises from π0.5's 30% to 70%.

## Problem

- Existing VLAs cannot infer their own camera pose or predict subsequent camera motion, yet active-perception tasks require both, with viewpoint planning contingent on knowing the current pose.
- Egocentric human video naturally contains task-driven head-camera motion, but prior active-perception methods fail to exploit this signal, and prior efforts used only about 200 hours.
- Current approaches such as SaPaVe and ActiveMimic study active perception in static environments, leaving real-world mobile manipulation in dynamic cluttered scenes and unified model-data-hardware infrastructure unaddressed.

## Method

- **Pose-grounded camera tokens**: a learnable token follows each of four front-camera history frames under ==block-causal attention==; a ==VGGT-style head== regresses translation, quaternion, and field of view, removed at inference.
- Human-robot mid-training: 1:1 mixed batches of egocentric EgoLive/EgoVerse clips and AgiBot World, RoboCOIN, AMP robot data, over 1000 hours, supervising head-camera poses, wrist trajectories, subtasks, ==FAST tokens==, and ==flow-matching actions==.
- **AMP platform**: a modified AgileX Cobot-Magic adding a third 6-DoF arm carrying the active camera, teleoperated by one operator via Meta Quest 2 headset (camera), controllers (arms), and buttons (base).

## Results

- Versus directly post-trained π0.5 across five real tasks (20 rollouts each), mean success rises **30.0%** to **70.0%** and task progress **41.6%** to **78.4%**; Table jumps **10%** to **85%**.
- Without human-robot mid-training, mean success drops **70%** to **62%** and progress **78.4%** to **67.7%**; mid-training leaves Pot success at **50%** but lifts its progress from **61%** to **76%**.
- Without mid-training, adding history alone lifts mean success only **30%** to **37%** and cuts Bag from **40%** to **20%**; adding pose-supervised camera tokens reaches **62%**, beating history-only on all five tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Giving a policy history frames without geometric grounding can hurt; supervising each frame's camera pose is what lets temporal context help under viewpoint change.
> - Egocentric head-camera motion is usable active-view supervision, yet here explicit pose grounding contributed far more than 1000 hours of mid-training.
> - Binary success can hide real gains: mid-training left Pot success unchanged while improving lid alignment and progress, so stage-credited progress metrics belong alongside success rate.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Overview

A robot opens a backpack to retrieve a bottle. Opening the bag is not enough if the robot cannot see inside, so it must inspect the interior before it can act. ActiveScale gives the policy two things: recent views, and cues about where the camera was when each view was taken. With both, the policy can connect what it sees as its viewpoint changes and coordinate looking with manipulation. The authors frame this as a coordinated advance in model, data, and hardware. Across five real-world task families, the full system completed substantially more of each task than a directly post-trained baseline.

The complete two-stage ActiveScale pipeline versus direct π0.5 post-training on the same downstream demonstrations. This is not an isolated architecture comparison. Top: Success Rate (%). Bottom: Task Progress (%). No error bars are shown.

### 2. Give the robot a visual trail

A vision-language-action model (VLA) combines visual input and language instructions to produce robot actions. ActiveScale builds on the π0.5 VLA.

**Visual history.** The model receives the current front-camera image plus three historical images sampled 16 frames apart.

**Camera tokens.** Each frame gets a learnable camera token. During training, a lightweight auxiliary head uses that token's hidden state to predict camera translation, rotation, and field of view. The camera-pose head is removed at action inference. This is pose supervision. It is not a claim that the system reconstructs a complete 3D scene.

**Action generation.** The resulting representation conditions the action expert, which jointly predicts manipulation and active-camera motion.

**Training data.** Training starts from π0.5. The model is first mid-trained on over 1,000 hours of human egocentric and robot data, sampled at a 1:1 human-to-robot ratio. It is then post-trained on teleoperation demonstrations for the target tasks, collected on AMP, the authors' platform. Human data provide head-camera motion and wrist trajectories. Robot demonstrations help retain robot-aligned action learning.

### 3. What the comparisons test

The five task families fall into two groups:

- **Bag, Drawer, and Table** test occlusion or targets outside the default view.
- **Pot and Box** test active search and localization across the workspace.

Each family uses 150 post-training demonstrations. Each method is evaluated on 20 real-world rollouts per family. The two metrics differ:

- **Strict success rate (SR)** counts only full task completion: every required stage plus the final-state criterion.
- **Task progress (TP)** gives weighted partial credit for completed stages, so partial progress can register even when a rollout fails the strict criterion.

Against π0.5 directly post-trained on the same task-specific demonstrations, ActiveScale raised mean SR from 30.0% to 70.0% and mean TP from 41.6% to 78.4%. Both measures improved across all five families. On Table, which requires finding an object initially outside the frontal view, SR went from 10% for π0.5 to 85% for ActiveScale. This headline compares the complete pipeline, including mid-training, so it does not isolate the camera-pose mechanism.

This supports the authors' interpretation that pose grounding helps make temporal context usable, while leaving other explanations open.

### 4. Views need context

These are real-world evaluations on five task families using AMP. They do not establish general performance across robots or settings. Direct comparisons with recent active-perception VLAs were not possible because those models' checkpoints and complete training recipes were not publicly available for evaluation.

Looking into an occluded space matters only if the robot can connect the new view to what it has already seen and use it in action. The history-plus-pose ablation is the paper's clearest evidence for that connection.
