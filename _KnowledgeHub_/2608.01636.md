# A Forward-Inverse Dynamic Game Framework for Enhanced Multi-Agent Trajectory Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> This paper presents a forward-inverse dynamic game framework for multi-agent trajectory planning, incorporating adaptive state-dependent regularization and a scene-aware neural network to infer unknown agent objectives. The framework demonstrated successful collision-free navigation in complex multi-agent simulations and real-time performance in hardware experiments, achieving zero collisions in a 5-agent ramp merging scenario.

## Problem

- Traditional multi-agent planning often simplifies interactions or assumes known agent objectives, leading to conservative or unsafe behaviors.
- Existing game-theoretic methods use fixed regularization parameters, failing to adapt to state-dependent inter-agent coupling and varying risk levels.
- Inferring unknown agent objectives from observed behaviors is challenging, especially in complex interactive scenarios, and often lacks consistency with underlying physical dynamics.

## Method

- A **forward-inverse dynamic game framework** with ==KL-regularization== is introduced, featuring an ==adaptive, state-dependent weighting function== that adjusts adherence to reference policies based on real-time interaction density.
- An inverse game module, leveraging a ==scene-aware neural network== and ==maximum-entropy inverse reinforcement learning==, infers unknown agent cost parameters from expert demonstrations.
- Physics-informed regularization is integrated into the inverse game learning process to ensure learned cost functions lead to physically feasible and safe policies.

## Results

- The framework achieved **zero collisions** in 5-agent ramp merging simulations and significantly reduced collisions (from **7 to 4**) in 20-agent collision avoidance scenarios compared to a fixed-regularization baseline.
- The adaptive regularization mechanism demonstrated its intended behavior, with converged `λ_i` values being higher (**~4.85**) in high-risk merge zones and lower (**~1.02**) in low-risk regions.
- Hardware experiments with two TurtleBot4 robots validated real-time planning performance (**~35ms** per step, **~28 Hz**) and collision-free navigation in interactive tasks.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Dynamically adjusting the balance between optimality and adherence to learned priors based on interaction risk (adaptive regularization) is crucial for both safety and efficiency in dense multi-agent environments.
> - Jointly learning agent objectives from demonstrations and enforcing physical constraints through a scene-aware neural network and rule-based regularization improves the accuracy and realism of inferred behaviors.
> - Theoretical guarantees of per-iteration well-posedness and Lipschitz continuity for the adaptive regularization scheme contribute to the solver's stability and convergence.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work addresses a fundamental challenge in multi-agent systems: planning trajectories in safety-critical environments characterized by strong and coupled interactions. Traditional planning paradigms, such as "prediction-then-planning," often treat agents in isolation or with simplified interaction models, which can lead to conservative or unsafe behaviors.

Dynamic game theory offers a more principled approach by explicitly modeling these coupled interactions. It has been applied to problems requiring safety constraints and long-horizon planning, and has advanced equilibrium computation and closed-loop stabilization in areas like networked control systems. However, existing game-theoretic methods typically present several limitations within the context of physical multi-agent environments:
- **Fixed Interaction Coupling:** In networked control systems, coupling is often defined by a fixed communication graph. In physical environments, inter-agent coupling is state-dependent, arising from proximity, and varies continuously over time. Existing methods often employ fixed regularization parameters, which do not adapt to the spatially varying intensity of interactions or the bounded rationality of agents in different risk regimes. For instance, prior KL-regularized dynamic game approaches utilize a fixed trade-off coefficient, limiting their ability to modulate adherence to behavioral priors based on real-time contextual risk.
- **Assumption of Known Objectives:** Many game-theoretic planning methods presuppose prior knowledge of other agents' intentions or cost functions. In practical scenarios, these objectives are frequently unknown due to sensor noise, incomplete observations, or agent unpredictability. Inverse game theory has emerged to infer these latent objectives from observed behaviors. However, existing inverse game methods often focus on recovering latent rewards or cost weights without comprehensively modeling coupled multi-agent interactions or leveraging structured environmental context, thus limiting their applicability to complex interactive planning.

This paper positions itself as an advancement in this landscape by developing a framework that integrates an adaptive, state-dependent regularization mechanism within the forward game and a context-aware inverse game module for inferring unknown objectives, addressing the aforementioned limitations in a unified manner.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a forward–inverse dynamic game framework for multi-agent trajectory planning in nonlinear dynamical systems. This framework is specifically designed to handle situations where agents' objectives are unknown and inter-agent coupling is state-dependent.

The motivations driving this research are rooted in the identified limitations of current multi-agent planning approaches:

- **Addressing the Rigidity of Fixed Regularization:** Existing KL-regularized dynamic game formulations, while incorporating data-driven priors, typically use a fixed regularization coefficient (λ). This fixed coefficient cannot adapt to varying levels of interaction intensity or risk across different operational regimes. For example, in a high-risk merging scenario, agents might need to adhere strictly to safety rules and learned expert behaviors, whereas in open spaces, more flexibility for optimality might be acceptable. The motivation is to introduce a state-dependent mechanism that adaptively balances optimality and behavioral priors. This adaptive weighting mechanism aims to strengthen adherence to reference policies in high-risk regions (e.g., near potential collision points) and relax this adherence in lower-risk areas, thereby enhancing both safety and efficiency.

- **Overcoming the Need for Prior Knowledge of Agent Objectives:** A significant challenge in dynamic game theory is that it often requires full knowledge of all agents' cost functions or intentions. In real-world multi-agent systems, such information is typically unavailable. The motivation here is to enable the inference of these unknown cost parameters directly from demonstrated behaviors. This involves developing an inverse game module capable of learning latent goals, moving beyond methods that solely focus on rewards or cost weights.

- **Ensuring Structural and Physical Consistency:** When inferring agent objectives from demonstrations, there is a risk that the learned policies might violate fundamental physical constraints or lack consistency with the underlying forward game formulation. The motivation is to integrate physics-informed regularization within the inverse game learning process. This ensures that the inferred cost parameters lead to behaviorally realistic and physically feasible trajectories, maintaining structural consistency between the inverse learning process and the forward game solver.

- **Handling Nonlinear Dynamics and Feedback Strategies:** Real-world multi-agent systems often involve complex nonlinear dynamics. The framework aims to compute Feedback Nash Equilibria (FBNE), which allow agents to adjust their strategies dynamically based on real-time state changes, making them more robust than open-loop solutions. The motivation is to develop an iterative approach that can solve for FBNE in nonlinear systems while incorporating the adaptive regularization and learned objectives.

In summary, the key objectives are to:
1. Propose a KL-regularized dynamic game with an adaptive, state-dependent weighting scheme to modulate the balance between optimality and adherence to reference policies.
2. Develop a context-aware inverse game module that infers unknown cost parameters from expert demonstrations using maximum-entropy inverse reinforcement learning (IRL) with physics-informed regularization.
3. Establish theoretical properties, specifically the per-iteration well-posedness of the regularized local game and the Lipschitz continuity of the adaptive weighting function.
4. Demonstrate the framework's effectiveness through numerical simulations and multi-robot hardware experiments in interactive scenarios such as cooperative navigation and ramp merging.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed framework integrates a forward game solver for trajectory planning with an inverse game module for inferring agent objectives. This hybrid approach aims to generate robust and behaviorally consistent multi-agent trajectories.

**Overall Framework (Fig. 2):**
The framework operates in an iterative loop involving both forward and inverse game components. The inverse game module infers unknown cost weights from expert demonstrations using a scene-aware neural network. These inferred weights are then used by the forward game solver, which plans trajectories using an adaptive KL-regularized dynamic game. The forward game generates updated nominal trajectories, which can be used to refine the reference policy for the next iteration of the forward game or, implicitly, for training the inverse model.

**A. Forward Game Solver (Algorithm 1):**
The forward game is formulated for N agents over a discrete time horizon T, where each agent `i` minimizes a cost function `J_i`. This cost function comprises an original stage cost `c_i(x_t, u_t, ω_i)` and a KL-divergence term `λ_i D_KL(π_i(·|x_t) || ˜π_i(·|x_t))`. `λ_i` is a scaling factor, and `˜π_i` is a reference policy derived from expert demonstrations. The objective is to find a Feedback Nash Equilibrium (FBNE).

1. **Iterative Linearization and Quadratic Approximation:**
 - To handle nonlinear system dynamics `x_{t+1} = f(x_t, u_t)` and cost functions, the system is iteratively approximated. At each outer iteration `k`, the dynamics and costs are linearized and quadratized around a nominal trajectory `η^k = {ˆx^k_{0:T}, ˆu^k_{0:T}}`.
 - The system dynamics are approximated as `δx_{t+1} ≈ A_t δx_t + Σ_j B^j_t δu^j_t` (Eq. 4).
 - The cost function `c_i` is approximated quadratically in `δx_t` and `δu^j_t` (Eq. 5), where `Q^i_t` and `R^{ij}_t` are Hessian matrices, and `l^i_t` and `r^{ij}_t` are first-order derivatives. Assumptions include `Q^i_t ⪰ 0`, `R^{ij}_t ⪰ 0`, and `R^{ii}_t ≻ 0`.

2. **Reference Policy Construction:**
 - The reference policy `˜π_i` (representing expert behavior) is constructed using a game-theoretic Boltzmann distribution (Eq. 6a, 6b), where the soft Q-function is approximated by the cumulative cost along the nominal trajectory.
 - For tractability, a Laplace approximation (Eq. 7) is applied to `˜π_i` at each outer iteration, yielding a local Gaussian approximation `N(˜μ^i_t(η^k_t), ˜Σ^i_t(η^k_t))`. The mean `˜μ^i_t` is set to the nominal control `ˆu^i,k_t`, and `˜Σ^i_t` is derived from the Hessian of the log-probability.

3. **KLGame+ Solver (Theorem 1):**
 - This component solves the linear-quadratic dynamic game with KL regularization to find the FBNE. The solution is a set of time-varying Gaussian policies `π^i*t ~ N(μ^i*t, Σ^i*t)`.
 - The mean `μ^i*t` and covariance `Σ^i*t` are determined by solving coupled linear systems for feedback gains (`P^i_t`) and offsets (`α^i_t`) (Eqs. 12a, 12b), and backward Riccati recursions for value function parameters (`Z^i_t`, `z^i_t`) (Eqs. 13a, 13b).

4. **Adaptive Regularization Mechanism:**
 - A key innovation is the state-dependent regularization weight `λ_i(x_t)` (Eq. 14). This function varies based on the agent's observed distance to obstacles or other agents (`d^i_obs(x_t)` in Eq. 15).
 - `λ_i(x_t)` is defined as `λ^i_min + (λ^i_max - λ^i_min)ρ_i(x_t)`, where `ρ_i(x_t)` is an exponential function of `d^i_obs(x_t)`. This ensures that `λ_i` is higher (stronger adherence to priors) in high-risk regions (small `d^i_obs`) and lower (more optimization freedom) in low-risk regions.
 - For each inner Riccati recursion, `λ_i(x_t)` is fixed to `λ^i,k_t := λ_i(ˆx^k_t)`, meaning its state dependence is only evaluated across outer iterations.

5. **Theoretical Guarantees:**
 - **Proposition 1:** Establishes per-iteration well-posedness of the frozen local game. It shows that the value function Hessian `Z^i_t` is positive semi-definite, the matrix `M^i,k_t` (related to the control cost) is uniformly positive definite, and the covariance `Σ^i*t` is positive definite, ensuring a unique Gaussian FBNE.
 - **Proposition 2:** Proves that the state-dependent coefficient `λ_i(x_t)` is Lipschitz continuous, which implies that the frozen coefficients `λ^i,k_t` converge if the nominal trajectory converges.

6. **Nominal Trajectory Update:**
 - The nominal trajectory `η^k` is updated using a linear search strategy (`stepToward`) with a step size `ε ∈ (0, 1]` to ensure stability and prevent divergence. This process repeats until the deviation between successive nominal trajectories falls below a predefined threshold.

**B. Inverse Game Solver (Algorithm 2):**
The inverse game module infers the unknown cost weights `ω_i` for each agent `i` from a set of expert trajectories `D`.

1. **Cost Function Parameterization:**
 - The agent's cost function `c_i` is represented as a linear combination of predefined cost primitive functions `f^i_t` (e.g., goal distance, collision avoidance, lane boundary, control effort) weighted by `ω_i` (Eq. 21).
 - The weights `ω_i` are inferred by a scene-conditioned neural network `ϕ_θ_i(ζ, c)` (Eq. 19), which takes expert trajectory `ζ` and optional scene information `c` (e.g., map context) as input.

2. **Scene-Aware Neural Network (Fig. 3):**
 - **Encoder:** Uses a GRU network to encode agent trajectories and an MLP to embed vectorized map features.
 - **Fusion Module:** Employs a cross-attention mechanism to integrate ego-agent, neighboring-agent, and map representations, generating interaction-aware feature vectors.
 - **Decoder:** A fully connected layer maps these fused representations to the cost weight vector `ω_i`. The output uses a Softplus activation to ensure `ω_i ≻ 0`.

3. **Maximum Entropy Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL):**
 - The inverse game problem is formulated as maximizing the likelihood `P(ζ|ω)` of observing expert trajectories `ζ` under the equilibrium strategy induced by `ω`.
 - This likelihood is modeled using a Boltzmann distribution (Eq. 20), derived from the maximum-entropy principle, which matches the expert's expected feature statistics.

4. **Loss Function (Eq. 22):**
 - The total training objective `L^i_total` combines two terms:
 - **Maximum Entropy IRL Loss (`L^i_MaxEnt`, Eq. 23):** Encourages the learned policy to imitate expert behavior by maximizing the probability of expert trajectories relative to sampled rollout trajectories (`˜ζ^j`).
 - **Rule-based Regularization (`L^i_rule`, Eq. 24):** This term adds a penalty for violating predefined physical rules (e.g., collision avoidance, lane boundaries) using specific cost primitives (`f^i,rule_t`). This ensures that learned cost functions lead to physically feasible and safe policies, mitigating potential issues from purely data-driven learning. `ν_rule` balances these two terms.

5. **Training:**
 - The network `ϕ_θ_i` is trained using the Adam optimizer. Expert trajectories `ζ^k` and rollout trajectories `˜ζ^j_k` (generated with the same initial state as `ζ^k`) are used to compute the feature expectations and subsequently the loss. Network parameters `θ` are iteratively updated.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates the proposed framework through extensive numerical simulations and hardware experiments, comparing it against several baselines and conducting ablation studies.

**A. Simulation Results:**
The framework was tested in two multi-agent interactive scenarios: Collision Avoidance Multi-agent Planning (CAMP), involving coordinated position exchange, and Ramp Merging (RM), a high-speed merging task.

1. **Effectiveness of the Proposed Method:**
 - **Collision Avoidance and Feasibility:** In the CAMP scenario, across 500 trials with learned strategies, all agents successfully reached their goals without collisions. For the RM scenario, the method generated feasible trajectories that successfully accomplished the ramp merging task while jointly considering collision avoidance and lane constraints (Figs. 4 & 5).
 - **Role of KL Regularization:** Comparison with a version where the KL term was removed indicated that KL regularization improves strategy learning from expert data, leading to faster convergence and smaller trajectory errors (Fig. 6b, 6d).
 - **Role of Scene-aware Network:** An ablation study showed that the proposed scene-aware network outperformed gradient-based cost estimation methods. The network effectively captured scene-aware map information and implicit relationships among cost terms, leading to better parameter estimation accuracy and reduced trajectory errors (Table I).

2. **Ablation Study and Comparison with Baselines (Table I):**
 The proposed method (Ours) was compared against Ours (fixed λ), Ours (no net), KKT-Based, MPC-Based, and KLGame.
 - **CAMP (2 Agents):** Ours and Ours(fixed λ) performed comparably, indicating that adaptive regularization provided negligible additional benefit in simple interaction scenarios. Ours(no net) resulted in collisions. Baselines also performed well, but KKT-based methods had slightly higher parameter error.
 - **CAMP (4 Agents):** As complexity increased, the benefits of the proposed components became more evident. Ours achieved lower parameter error (1.18 vs. 1.49 for Ours(fixed λ) and 3.13 for Ours(no net)) and trajectory error (3.21 vs. 3.86 for Ours(fixed λ) and 5.32 for Ours(no net)). Both the scene-aware network and adaptive `λ_i(x_t)` contributed to performance improvements.
 - **CAMP (20 Agents):** The benefit of adaptive regularization became more pronounced in high-density interactions. Ours significantly reduced collisions (4) compared to Ours(fixed λ) (6) and KLGame (7), and achieved the lowest parameter error (3.82) and trajectory error (18.63). This suggests that state-dependent regularization is critical for safety and accuracy as interaction density increases. Baselines like KKT-Based and MPC showed substantially higher collision counts (15 and 12, respectively) and errors in this complex scenario.
 - **RM (5 Agents):** Ours achieved zero collisions, while Ours(fixed λ) had 1, and KLGame had 2. This confirms that tightening reference-policy adherence in high-risk zones via adaptive `λ_i(x_t)` is instrumental for safety. The scene-aware network also contributed by further reducing parameter and trajectory errors compared to Ours(fixed λ).

**B. Sensitivity Analysis of `ν_rule` (Table II):**
An ablation study on the `ν_rule` hyperparameter in the RM scenario demonstrated the method's stability over a range of `ν_rule ∈ [0.01, 1.0]`.
- Setting `ν_rule = 0` (removing the rule loss) increased collisions, indicating the direct contribution of physical constraint terms to safety.
- An excessively large `ν_rule = 10.0` over-constrained the learned cost, leading to degraded trajectory accuracy. This confirms `ν_rule` acts as a meaningful trade-off between data-driven imitation and rule enforcement.

**C. Convergence of State-Dependent `λ_i` (Fig. 7):**
The analysis of `λ_i(ˆx^k_t)` in the RM scenario showed that:
- `λ_i` values converged across outer iterations.
- Converged `λ_i` were higher (approximately 4.85) in regions near the merge zone (high interaction risk) and lower (approximately 1.02) near the goal (low risk), validating the adaptive mechanism's intended behavior.
- The per-iteration perturbation of `|λ^{k+1} - λ^k|` remained bounded and decayed rapidly, which is consistent with the theoretical guarantee provided by Proposition 2.

**D. Hardware Experiments:**
Experiments conducted with two TurtleBot4 robots and a human participant in position exchange and trajectory crossing tasks validated the framework in real-world scenarios.
- Learned strategies enabled safe navigation for both robots and human, without collisions (Fig. 8).
- The system achieved real-time planning performance, with an average per-step planning time of 35 ms (~28 Hz) on an Intel NUC, satisfying the 10 Hz real-time requirement.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework that advances multi-agent trajectory planning in complex, interactive, and safety-critical environments. Its significance and potential impact stem from addressing several limitations of existing approaches:

- **Enhanced Safety and Adaptability:** The introduction of a state-dependent regularization weight (`λ_i(x_t)`) represents a significant improvement. By adaptively balancing optimality with adherence to expert policies based on real-time interaction density and risk, the framework can generate safer and more contextually appropriate trajectories. This is particularly crucial in safety-critical domains like autonomous driving (e.g., ramp merging), where adherence to safety protocols is paramount in high-risk zones, while allowing for greater flexibility in low-risk scenarios. This adaptive mechanism contributes to more robust and realistic multi-agent behaviors.

- **Improved Intent Inference and Contextual Awareness:** The scene-aware neural network, integrated with maximum-entropy inverse reinforcement learning and physics-informed regularization, enables more accurate and robust inference of unknown agent objectives. By jointly encoding trajectory histories and map context, the model better captures the complex interplay of agents and their environment. This ability to learn from demonstrations while ensuring structural consistency with physical rules leads to more reliable predictions of agent intentions, which is fundamental for effective interactive planning.

- **Theoretical Foundations for Adaptive Control:** The establishment of per-iteration well-posedness and Lipschitz continuity for the adaptive regularization scheme provides theoretical guarantees for the stability and convergence of the proposed iterative solver. This theoretical underpinning increases confidence in the framework's reliability in practical applications.

- **Broad Applicability in Multi-Agent Systems:** The validation across various simulation scenarios (collision avoidance, ramp merging, varying agent numbers) and in hardware experiments (multi-robot navigation) suggests broad applicability. This framework has the potential to impact fields such as autonomous driving, air traffic control, robotic swarm coordination, and human-robot interaction, where intelligent and adaptive multi-agent decision-making is required.

- **Foundation for Future Research:** The paper identifies limitations, such as the reliance on forward simulation for expert demonstrations and the need for direct inference from raw sensory inputs. These points highlight clear directions for future research, pushing towards more naturalistic data utilization and fully end-to-end multi-agent interaction frameworks. The hybrid forward-inverse learning paradigm established here can serve as a foundation for further advancements in learning complex interaction strategies in dynamic environments.
