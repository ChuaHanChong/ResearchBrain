# Learn to Teach: Sample-Efficient Privileged Learning for Humanoid Locomotion over Diverse Terrains

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Georgia Institute of Technology developed L2T-RL, a one-stage teacher-student framework that concurrently trains policies for humanoid locomotion, addressing sample inefficiency and the sim-to-real gap. The approach enabled the Digit robot to perform robust zero-shot sim-to-real transfer across diverse, challenging real-world terrains, achieving high success rates in simulation and reducing training sample requirements by 50%.

## Problem

- Reinforcement learning policies for robotics face a 'sim-to-real' gap, where policies trained in simulation often fail on physical hardware due to environmental and dynamic discrepancies.
- Existing domain randomization techniques, used to bridge the sim-to-real gap, typically require high sample complexity and extensive simulation time.
- Traditional two-stage teacher-student learning paradigms suffer from sample inefficiency (discarding teacher samples) and an 'imitation gap' where student policies struggle to mimic teacher actions based on privileged information.

## Method

- The '**Learn to Teach**' (L2T) framework unifies teacher and student policy learning into a single, interactive training stage, leveraging a shared replay buffer to reuse collected simulator samples.
- A sample mixing mechanism probabilistically blends actions from the teacher (with privileged information) and the student (with restricted observations) during environment interaction, fostering a bidirectional feedback loop.
- The teacher policy uses an MLP with full state observations, while the student policy uses an LSTM with noisy proprioceptive inputs, enabling it to infer latent states from sequential, partial observations.

## Results

- L2T-RL achieved a **94%** student success rate across 4096 rough terrain environments in simulation, outperforming asymmetric learning baselines like CTS (**74%**) and ROA (**73%**).
- The framework demonstrated a **50%** reduction in sample requirements compared to conventional two-stage teacher-student learning, saving approximately 12 hours of GPU training time.
- Policies trained with L2T-RL enabled zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on the physical Digit robot, achieving robust locomotion over diverse real-world uneven terrains (e.g., gravel, sand, wet grass, slopes) and demonstrating resilience to external perturbations.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Integrating teacher and student learning into a unified, interactive stage with a shared replay buffer significantly improves sample efficiency by recycling data and synchronizing learning trajectories.
> - The sample mixing mechanism effectively mitigates the imitation gap by exposing the teacher to student-generated 'out-of-distribution' data and allowing the student to continuously track the evolving teacher's optimal behavior.
> - A student policy relying solely on hardware-realistic proprioceptive inputs can achieve robust and generalizable locomotion performance comparable to a privileged teacher, without needing complex depth estimation modules.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has significantly advanced robotic control, enabling robots to perform complex tasks such as dynamic locomotion and manipulation. However, a persistent challenge in deploying RL-trained policies to physical robots is the simulation-to-reality (sim-to-real) gap. Policies trained in simulators often fail in real-world environments due to discrepancies between simulated and physical dynamics, sensor noise, and environmental variability.

Existing approaches to mitigate the sim-to-real gap include domain randomization, which exposes the agent to a wide range of simulated variations during training. While effective, domain randomization typically incurs high sample complexity, requiring extensive simulation time.

Teacher-student learning methods have emerged as a promising alternative, utilizing an "expert" teacher with privileged information to guide a "student" policy with restricted, hardware-realistic observations. Conventional teacher-student frameworks often involve a two-stage training process: first, the teacher is trained to expert levels, and then the student imitates the teacher's behavior. This decoupled approach can lead to several limitations: it discards valuable interaction samples collected by the teacher, and it can suffer from an "imitation gap" or "observation mismatch" where the teacher's optimal actions, based on privileged information, are not easily interpretable or achievable by a student with limited observations. Some concurrent works have explored co-training but often involve shared policy architectures or independent data processing, which can lead to training disruptions or persistent imitation gaps.

This work addresses the limitations of conventional teacher-student learning by proposing a unified, single-stage training framework. It aims to improve sample efficiency, reduce training time, and mitigate the imitation gap, thereby facilitating more robust and agile humanoid locomotion over diverse real-world terrains without requiring depth estimation modules. The research contributes to the field by proposing a method that makes RL-based control for complex humanoid robots more practical and efficient for real-world deployment.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a sample-efficient and robust learning framework for training humanoid locomotion policies that can perform zero-shot sim-to-real transfer to real-world uneven terrain. This overarching goal is driven by several specific motivations:

- **Addressing the Sim-to-Real Gap and Sample Inefficiency:** Reinforcement Learning policies often struggle with the transition from simulation to reality. Domain randomization helps but demands substantial computational resources and long training times due to the large number of samples required to explore diverse environmental variations. The authors are motivated to reduce this sample complexity.
- **Improving Teacher-Student Learning Paradigms:** Traditional teacher-student learning, while effective, suffers from a two-stage training process where the teacher's collected samples are often discarded after its training. This leads to inefficiency and a lack of synchronized learning. There is also a known "imitation gap" where the teacher, having access to privileged information (e.g., terrain height maps), might execute actions that are difficult for the student, with limited observations, to accurately imitate or understand. The motivation is to overcome these inefficiencies and mismatches.
- **Enabling Robust Humanoid Locomotion over Diverse Real-World Terrains:** Humanoid robots are envisioned for complex industrial and service applications, which necessitate robust locomotion capabilities over varied and often challenging terrains (e.g., gravel, sand, slopes, slippery surfaces). Current methods often require extensive environment design or fine-tuning for specific conditions. The research aims to develop a policy that can generalize to a wide range of unstructured real-world environments without requiring additional real-world data or fine-tuning.
- **Developing a Unified and Efficient Training Framework:** The authors aim to integrate the teacher and student policy learning into a single, interactive stage. This unified approach is intended to recycle simulator samples and synchronize the learning trajectories through shared dynamics, thereby enhancing training efficiency and the overall quality of the student policy.
- **Achieving Performance with Restricted Observation Spaces:** The goal is for the student agent to achieve high-level performance and robustness comparable to a privileged teacher, even when relying solely on proprioceptive inputs (e.g., joint positions, velocities, base velocities, projected gravity) and without requiring complex external sensing modalities like depth estimation modules.

In summary, the key motivation is to make the training of high-performance, real-world-deployable humanoid locomotion policies more practical, efficient, and robust by fundamentally rethinking the teacher-student learning architecture.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed methodology introduces "Learn to Teach" (L2T), a one-stage training framework that concurrently optimizes both a teacher policy and a student policy. This approach is implemented as L2T-RL, an RL variant, and validated on a humanoid locomotion task using the Digit robot in the Isaac Lab simulator.

#### 3.1. L2T Framework Overview

L2T unifies teacher and student policy learning within a single, interactive stage, allowing for the reuse of simulator samples and synchronization of learning trajectories. The framework involves two distinct neural networks: an MLP for the teacher policy and an LSTM for the student policy. The teacher has access to privileged information, making its observations fully descriptive and Markovian, while the student operates on a restricted observation space, mimicking hardware sensor configurations.

#### 3.2. Sample Collection and Replay Buffer

During training, the teacher interacts with the environment and generates samples (s, a, r, s'), where 's' represents the full state, 'a' the action, 'r' the reward, and 's'' the next full state. Critically, these samples also include noisy observations (o, o') generated by domain randomization, which constitute the student's observation space. All collected data (s, o, a, r, s', o') is stored in a shared replay buffer. This contrasts with traditional teacher-student frameworks where the teacher's samples are often discarded after its policy is trained.

#### 3.3. Policy Architecture

- **Teacher Policy (π_t):** Implemented using a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) with three fully connected layers. The MLP architecture is chosen because the teacher, with privileged access to the full state (e.g., depth scans, root pose, terrain profile), does not require temporal dependency modeling via recurrence.
- **Student Policy (π_s):** Implemented using an LSTM network with a hidden layer, followed by fully connected layers. The LSTM allows the student to infer latent state representations from sequential, partial observations, addressing the Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) nature of its task.

#### 3.4. Sample Mixing Mechanism

To mitigate the imitation gap between the teacher and student, L2T introduces a sample mixing mechanism. At each time step, the action taken in the environment is determined by a probabilistic mixture of the teacher's policy and the student's policy. This is controlled by a mixture coefficient α_mix:
`a = sample π_s(· | o)` with probability `α_mix`
`a = sample π_t(· | s)` with probability `1 − α_mix`

The α_mix coefficient is linearly scheduled from 0 to a predefined constant (0.2 in this implementation) over the course of training. Initially, the teacher's guidance dominates, gradually allowing the student's policy to influence action selection as training progresses. Student-generated samples are incorporated into the replay buffer, effectively injecting out-of-distribution (OOD) data from the student into the teacher's learning process.

#### 3.5. Policy Update Rules (L2T-RL)

- **Teacher Policy Update:** The teacher policy (π_t) is updated using policy gradient methods. In L2T-RL, a PPO-style update is employed. The critic network for the teacher utilizes privileged, full-state information during training (asymmetric actor-critic), which is known to improve sample efficiency and policy robustness. Samples from both the teacher and the student (via sample mixing) are pooled to update the teacher's policy.
- **Student Policy Update:** The student policy (π_s) is updated by sampling mini-batches from the replay buffer, relying solely on the collected noisy observation data. The research explores two choices for the student's loss function:
 - **Imitation Loss (L_IL):** Minimizes the squared difference between the student's action distribution (conditioned on its observation 'o') and the teacher's action distribution (conditioned on the full state 's').
 - **KL Divergence Loss (L_KL):** Minimizes the KL divergence between the student's and teacher's action distributions.
 - **Asymmetric Learning Loss (L_Asym):** Leverages the teacher's critic by maximizing the expected action-value (Q_π_t) for actions taken by the student policy, regularized by KL divergence to the previous student policy.
 In the Digit robot experiments, the L_IL imitation loss yielded the best performance, with L_Asym providing no significant additional benefit.

#### 3.6. Environment Design (Digit Robot in Isaac Lab)

- **Robot Model:** The Digit robot, a bipedal robot with 30 degrees of freedom (20 actuated joints, 4 per arm, 6 per leg), is used. Its complex leg design with three closed kinematic chains per leg presents a high-dimensional action space and challenging dynamics. A faithful Universal Scene Description (USD) model of the robot was reconstructed in IsaacLab.
- **Observation Space (Table I):**
 - **Shared Observations (Student & Teacher):** Clock input, base linear velocity, base angular velocity, projected gravity, velocity command, joint positions, joint velocities, and last action.
 - **Student-Specific Noise:** Measurement noise (Gaussian or uniform) is added to the student's observations to mimic hardware sensors.
 - **Teacher-Specific Privileged Information:** Full root state, full base linear and angular velocities, base position and quaternion, environment parameters (e.g., randomized base mass), and height scan (local depth map).
- **Action Space:** Target full-body joint positions (q_target). A Proportional-Derivative (PD) controller tracks these targets at a higher frequency (1 kHz) than the policy prediction frequency (50 Hz).
- **Reward Functions (Table II):** A combination of standard RL reward terms (termination penalty, action rate, joint deviation, etc.) and Digit-specific rewards:
 - **Track Foot Height:** Rewards tracking a precomputed quintic polynomial trajectory for foot height, adjusted based on the CoM position to adapt to uneven terrains. This is crucial for robust uneven terrain locomotion.
 - **Foot Contact Matching:** Rewards agents when foot contact aligns with a desired gait cycle (e.g., ground contact when expected, no contact otherwise).
- **Domain Randomization and Curriculum (Table III):** To enhance sim-to-real transfer and robustness, various parameters are randomized: friction coefficients, gravity, base mass, external forces, base location, and robot joints. A curriculum learning setup with seven different terrains (flat, slopes, stepping stones, pyramid stairs) is used to progressively increase task difficulty.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents both simulation results, primarily benchmarking the L2T-RL framework against various baselines, and hardware experiments demonstrating zero-shot sim-to-real transfer on the physical Digit robot.

#### 4.1. Computational Results (Simulation)

**A. Rough Terrain Difficulty Progression and Sample Efficiency:**
- **Performance Comparison:** L2T-RL demonstrated superior performance compared to asymmetric learning baselines: Concurrent Teacher Student (CTS), Recurrent Observation-Agnostic Learning (ROA), and Denoising World Model Learning (DWL). The student success rate across 4096 training environments with random velocity commands was significantly higher for L2T (94%) compared to CTS (74%), ROA (73%), and DWL (27%).
- **Sample Efficiency:** L2T achieved comparable performance to the conventional two-stage Teacher-Student Learning (TSL) paradigm while requiring approximately 50% fewer samples. The time spent training the teacher in TSL was sufficient to jointly train both teacher and student in L2T, resulting in a reduction of roughly 12 hours of GPU training time. The curriculum progression curves showed that both L2T Teacher and Student policies learned to traverse increasingly difficult terrains faster and more effectively than the baselines. A vanilla recurrent PPO trained directly in the student's environment performed poorly.
- **Imitation Gap in Baselines:** Baselines like CTS were observed to suffer from a substantial imitation gap because they processed teacher and student samples independently, relying on reconstruction loss to align encoders rather than direct interaction.

**B. Mitigation of Imitation Gap:**
- The sample mixing mechanism in L2T, combined with imitation learning, created a bidirectional feedback loop. The teacher benefited from student-generated samples, and the student continuously tracked the evolving teacher.
- Quantitatively, the L_IL (imitation loss) between teacher and student policies was computed. L2T exhibited a lower imitation gap (average L_IL of 5.1) compared to TSL (5.5) and L2T without sample mixing (L2T w/ No Mix, 5.9), as shown in Fig. 4a.
- Qualitatively, the walking gait of the L2T student was observed to faithfully mimic the teacher's movements, including subtle details like the toe pad's slight upward tilt. In contrast, a DAgger-trained student from a conventional TSL setup showed a flatter, less agile walking gait (Fig. 5).

**C. Ablation Studies:**
- **Mixture Coefficient (α_mix):** An ablation study on the α_mix parameter demonstrated its importance. While a very large α_mix (e.g., 0.3) led to unstable training due to excessive injection of suboptimal student samples, an appropriately chosen coefficient (e.g., 0.2) enhanced the training process by promoting exploration within the action space.
- **Loss Function for Student:** Comparing the L_IL imitation loss with a combination of L_IL + L_Asym (asymmetric learning loss), the L_IL alone slightly outperformed the combined loss in the specific training setup for the Digit robot (Fig. 4b). The authors conjectured that L_IL provided slightly higher exploration capability.

#### 4.2. Hardware Experiments

The policies trained in simulation with L2T-RL were deployed on the physical Digit robot, demonstrating zero-shot sim-to-real transfer without any offline fine-tuning.

**A. Locomotion over Real-World Uneven Terrain:**
- **Generalization:** The student policy demonstrated robust generalization across a wide range of real-world environments, including concrete walkways, wooden bridges, grass hills (wet and deforming), gravel paths, and sandy beach volleyball courts (Fig. 1).
- **Unseen Scenarios:** The robot successfully navigated scenarios not explicitly included in the training data, such as wet grass and soil that deform upon impact, and soft sandy terrains.
- **Obstacles and Slippery Surfaces:** The policy allowed the robot to perform stable stepping-in-place actions within a crate of gravel and maintained stability on a whiteboard covered with poppy seeds, a slippery surface where the manufacturer's controller reportedly failed (Fig. 7b, Fig. 1). The robot also traversed high stairs (7cm + 9cm) and a gravel hill (Fig. 7c, 7d).

**B. Perturbation Experiments:**
- **Push Recovery:** The policy exhibited robustness to external dynamic perturbations. The robot maintained a stable walking gait when pushed from the front or back at its center of mass using a stick.
- **Stronger Perturbations:** When subjected to more substantial impulsive forces, such as being pulled by a harness (Fig. 7a), the robot dynamically adapted and made agile adjustments to compensate for the pulling force, maintaining balance and locomotion.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a framework, L2T-RL, that addresses several challenges in the application of reinforcement learning to complex robotic systems, particularly humanoid locomotion. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

- **Improved Sample Efficiency and Reduced Training Time:** By unifying teacher and student policy learning into a single, interactive stage, L2T-RL significantly reduces the sample complexity and training time required compared to conventional two-stage teacher-student approaches. This makes the development cycle for RL-based robotic systems more efficient and accessible, particularly for hardware-intensive applications where simulation time is a critical resource.
- **Effective Mitigation of the Imitation Gap:** The proposed sample mixing strategy provides a mechanism to alleviate the discrepancy between teacher policies (trained with privileged information) and student policies (trained with restricted observations). By allowing the teacher to learn from student-generated "out-of-distribution" data, and enabling the student to accurately track the evolving teacher, the framework fosters a more aligned and robust learning process. This leads to student policies that more faithfully reproduce desired behaviors.
- **Robust Zero-Shot Sim-to-Real Transfer for Humanoids:** The successful deployment of L2T-RL trained policies on the physical Digit robot across a diverse range of challenging real-world terrains (e.g., gravel, sand, wet grass, slopes, slippery surfaces) without any additional real-world training or fine-tuning represents a notable advancement. This demonstrates the framework's capacity to generate highly generalized and robust locomotion policies, crucial for practical applications of humanoid robots in unstructured environments.
- **Performance with Restricted Observations:** The student policy, relying solely on proprioceptive inputs without requiring complex depth estimation modules, achieves high performance and robustness. This simplifies the sensor requirements for deployment, potentially reducing hardware complexity and cost, and enhancing the robot's ability to operate in conditions where external sensing might be challenging or unreliable (e.g., poor lighting, occlusions).
- **Foundation for Future Robotic Systems:** The L2T framework is designed to be general, compatible with various policy improvement schemes (e.g., PPO, SAC) and potentially extensible to other learning paradigms like imitation learning or Inverse Reinforcement Learning. This versatility suggests its potential applicability beyond locomotion tasks, opening avenues for developing efficient learning solutions for manipulation, navigation, and other complex behaviors in humanoid and bipedal robots.

In conclusion, L2T-RL offers a practical and efficient paradigm for developing high-performance, real-world-ready control policies for humanoid robots. Its contributions to sample efficiency, imitation gap mitigation, and robust sim-to-real transfer have the potential to accelerate the deployment of advanced RL-based robotic systems into diverse industrial and service applications.
