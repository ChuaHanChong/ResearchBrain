# Set-Supervised Diffusion Policy: Learning Action-Chunking Diffusion through Corrections

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from Delft University of Technology and University of Stuttgart introduced the Set-Supervised Diffusion Policy (SDP), a framework that integrates set-valued supervision with diffusion policies for robotic action-chunking. SDP effectively utilizes both desired and undesired actions from human-in-the-loop corrections, leading to consistently higher success rates in manipulation tasks and improved robustness to noisy feedback compared to standard diffusion policies and other correction learning methods.

## Problem

- Imitation Learning (IL) methods, particularly Diffusion Policies (DP), are prone to distributional shift, leading to failures when robots encounter states unseen during training.
- Current Behavior Cloning (BC) approaches, including DPs, underutilize valuable contrastive data generated from human-in-the-loop corrections (robot's undesired actions vs. human's desired actions), often discarding the negative signal.
- Existing correction learning methods like Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) struggle with high-dimensional action-chunk spaces, while Energy-Based Model-based approaches (e.g., CLIC) face training instability and lack natural integration with action-chunking.

## Method

- A Set-Supervised Diffusion Policy (**SDP**) framework was developed, specifically designed to leverage contrastive action-chunk data from human corrections.
- The concept of ==desired action sets== was extended from single-step actions to high-dimensional action-chunks, defining a set of acceptable actions based on both the robot's undesired actions and the human teacher's corrective actions.
- A compatible training pipeline was proposed for diffusion models, which approximates the ==CLIC loss== by employing a ==constrained diffusion sampling== procedure with a reflection operator to generate action-chunks that conform to the defined desired sets.

## Results

- SDP consistently achieved higher success rates across various manipulation tasks and demonstrated superior robustness to noisy intervention feedback compared to Diffusion Policy (DP), DP-DPO, Ambient DP, and CLIC.
- Policies trained on data collected by SDP agents during online interaction consistently outperformed those trained on DP-collected data, indicating that SDP's online data aggregation generated datasets with broader state coverage and more favorable properties for offline learning.
- In real-world Insert-T and Round-table Assembly tasks, SDP showed significantly larger performance improvements compared to DP, particularly for challenging or failure-prone stages, by effectively utilizing negative actions from intervention data.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly leveraging both positive (teacher's) and negative (robot's undesired) action-chunks through set-valued supervision provides a richer and more robust learning signal for diffusion policies.
> - Defining a "desired action set" for training, rather than a single target action, enhances policy robustness to noisy human feedback and imperfect corrections by allowing for a region of acceptable behaviors.
> - Integrating set-valued supervision with diffusion models using a constrained sampling process effectively extends this learning paradigm to high-dimensional, multi-modal action-chunk spaces.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Broader Research Landscape

Imitation Learning (IL) is a established paradigm in robotic manipulation, enabling robots to acquire skills from expert demonstrations. Within IL, Diffusion Policies (DP) have emerged as a method for policy representation, leveraging generative diffusion models to operate in high-dimensional continuous action-chunk spaces. Diffusion policies are capable of generating expressive, multi-modal actions and have been extended with features such as 3D scene representations, SE(3)-equivariant architectures, hierarchical formulations, and flow-matching objectives for efficiency. Generalist vision-language-action (VLA) policies also utilize diffusion or flow-based action heads. However, like other Behavior Cloning (BC) methods, DPs are susceptible to distributional shift, where the policy's state distribution deviates from the training data.

A common strategy to mitigate distributional shift is human-in-the-loop intervention, where a human teacher corrects robot errors during deployment. These interactions generate data comprising both the robot's undesired actions and the teacher's corrective actions. Existing BC approaches, however, often do not fully utilize this contrastive information. Standard BC objectives primarily maximize the likelihood of the teacher's actions, neglecting the negative signal from the robot's undesired actions. This can lead to overfitting to the teacher's distribution and an increased reliance on high-quality expert data.

Prior work has explored learning from preferences or corrections. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), for instance, can struggle with high-dimensional action-chunk spaces and typically requires a pre-trained policy. Another approach, CLIC (Correction Learning via Implicit Classification), proposes set-valued supervision for corrective feedback, where a positive-negative correction pair defines a set of acceptable actions rather than a single target. This relaxation helps to reduce overfitting to imperfect corrections. However, CLIC uses Energy-Based Models (EBMs) as its policy representation, which can be challenging to train stably and integrate with action-chunking, a key mechanism contributing to the performance of DPs.

The current work aims to integrate the advantages of set-valued supervision with the capabilities of diffusion policies, specifically addressing the limitations of applying set-valued supervision to high-dimensional action-chunks within a diffusion model framework.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from the limitations of current Imitation Learning (IL) methods, particularly Diffusion Policies (DP), when dealing with human-in-the-loop corrections for robotic manipulation tasks.

**Motivation:**
1. **Vulnerability to Distributional Shift:** Diffusion Policies, like other Behavior Cloning (BC) methods, are prone to distributional shift. This phenomenon occurs when the robot policy, trained on expert data, drifts into states not observed during training, leading to failures. Human interventions are then required to correct these failures.
2. **Underutilization of Corrective Feedback:** Human interventions naturally generate paired supervision: the robot's actions that led to a failure (undesired/negative actions) and the human teacher's corrective actions (desired/positive actions). Current BC approaches, however, typically only learn from the positive actions, effectively discarding the valuable negative signal. This selective use of data leads to:
 - **Overfitting:** Policies can overfit to the exact actions provided by the teacher, failing to generalize to variations or noisy demonstrations.
 - **Increased Reliance on Costly Expert Data:** By ignoring negative examples, the learning process becomes more dependent on a larger quantity of high-quality expert demonstrations or interventions, which are expensive to acquire.
3. **Limitations of Existing Preference/Correction Learning Methods:**
 - **Direct Preference Optimization (DPO):** While capable of leveraging comparative feedback, DPO methods often struggle with the high-dimensional continuous action spaces characteristic of robotic manipulation, especially when dealing with action-chunks (sequences of actions). They may also require a strong pre-trained policy, limiting their applicability from scratch or with minimal data.
 - **CLIC (Correction Learning via Implicit Classification):** CLIC introduced the concept of set-valued supervision, where a correction defines a set of acceptable actions rather than a single optimal action. This approach improves robustness to imperfect corrections. However, CLIC relies on Energy-Based Models (EBMs), which are known for their training instability and do not naturally integrate with action-chunking, a crucial aspect of modern Diffusion Policies for managing multimodal behaviors and reducing covariate shift.

**Key Objectives:**
1. **Develop a Novel Learning Framework (SDP):** To introduce the Set-Supervised Diffusion Policy (SDP) framework, specifically designed to utilize contrastive action-chunk data derived from human corrections.
2. **Construct Desired Action Sets:** To define and construct a set of "desired action-chunks" from paired positive (teacher's) and negative (robot's undesired) action-chunks, thereby providing richer, set-valued action targets for policy learning.
3. **Design a Compatible Training Pipeline:** To propose a training methodology that integrates these set-valued action targets with diffusion policies, enabling the policy to generate action-chunks that fall within the defined desired sets. This involves adapting the CLIC loss for diffusion models, which lack explicit probability estimation.
4. **Enhance Policy Performance and Robustness:** To demonstrate that SDP consistently improves policy performance, particularly in terms of robustness to noisy data, by enabling the policy to learn from a broader set of acceptable actions rather than strictly imitating potentially noisy or suboptimal single actions.
5. **Improve Data Aggregation Quality:** To show that SDP induces higher-quality aggregated datasets during online data collection, leading to more efficient and reliable policy learning from human-in-the-loop corrections.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Set-Supervised Diffusion Policy (SDP) framework integrates set-valued supervision with diffusion policies to leverage contrastive action-chunk data derived from human corrections.

#### 3.1. Preliminaries

- **Diffusion Policy (DP) and Action Chunks:** DPs adapt Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) to imitation learning. They model distributions over action chunks `A_t = [a_t, ..., a_{t+T-1}]`, which are sequences of single-step actions. The policy `πθ(A_t|O_t)` generates an action-chunk conditioned on observations `O_t`. During inference, DPs iteratively denoise a Gaussian prior `A_K_t` to obtain a clean action `A^0_t` using a learned noise prediction network `ϵθ`. Training involves minimizing a denoising loss (Eq. 4) that encourages accurate reconstruction from corrupted action data.
- **Set-valued Supervision from Interactive Corrections (CLIC):** CLIC introduced the concept of a "desired action set" `Âss(a-, a+)` from a single-step positive (`a+`) and negative (`a-`) action pair. This set includes actions consistent with the correction, often defined as a ball around `a+` whose radius is scaled by the distance to `a-` (Eq. 5). This approach relaxes the pointwise imitation of BC, mitigating overfitting to imperfect corrections. Policy shaping aims to maximize the probability of selecting actions within this desired set (Eq. 6) by minimizing the KL divergence to a target policy `πtarget` that reweights the current policy based on set membership (Eq. 7).

#### 3.2. SDP Method: Problem Formulation

SDP addresses policy learning from corrections, where the robot learns from human interventions. The input is a correction dataset `D+− = {[O, A−, A+]}` containing paired negative (`A−`) and positive (`A+`) action-chunks. The goal is to define and leverage a set of desired action-chunks to improve a parameterized policy `πθ(A|O)`. SDP considers two settings: online interactive imitation learning (IIL) and offline learning.

#### 3.3. Extending Desired Action Set to Action-Chunks

SDP generalizes CLIC's single-step desired action set to action-chunks. For an observation `O_t`, a positive action-chunk `A+t = [a+t, ..., a+t+T-1]` and a negative action-chunk `A−t = [a−t, ..., a−t+T-1]` are given. Each timestep `i` within the chunk `[0, T-1]` yields a contrastive single-step action pair `(a−t+i, a+t+i)`. Each such pair defines a single-step desired action set `Âss t+i`. The overall desired action set for the action-chunk pair `(A+t, A−t)` is defined as the sequence of these single-step sets: `Ât = [Âss t, ..., Âss t+T-1]` (Eq. 9). An action-chunk `A_t` belongs to `Ât` if all its constituent single-step actions belong to their respective `Âss t+i`.

#### 3.4. Policy Learning via Desired Action Sets

- **Set-valued Supervision for Action-Chunks:** The objective is to train a policy `πθ` that generates action-chunks belonging to `Ât`, maximizing `πθ(A ∈ Ât|O_t)` (Eq. 10). Similar to CLIC, a target distribution `πtarget` (Eq. 11) is defined by reweighting the current policy `πθ` with a likelihood model indicating membership in `Ât`. This objective can be optimized by minimizing the KL divergence between `πtarget` and `πθ`.
- **Approximating CLIC Loss for Diffusion Models:** Directly evaluating the KL loss requires the likelihood of an action-chunk under the diffusion policy, which is not readily available. SDP approximates this by maximizing the likelihood of action-chunks sampled from `πtarget`. This translates into a denoising loss (Eq. 13) structurally similar to the standard DP BC loss (Eq. 4), but crucially, the training targets (`A^0` in Eq. 13) are samples from `πtarget` rather than just the positive actions `A+t`.
- **Sampling Desired Action-Chunks:** To generate samples from `πtarget`, SDP employs a constrained diffusion sampling procedure. The standard denoising update (Eq. 2) is modified with a boundary constraint term, `L_Ât` (Eq. 14). This "reflection operator" `L_Ât` ensures that intermediate samples `A^(k-1)_t` remain within the desired set `Ât` during denoising. `L_Ât` decomposes across individual timesteps, with each `L_Âss t+i` projecting any action `a^k_t+i` that falls outside `Âss t+i` directly to the corresponding positive action `a+t+i`. This simplified projection strategy is chosen over traditional reflections to avoid oscillations and high sampling costs for potentially small desired sets.

#### 3.5. Algorithm

Algorithm 1 outlines the complete SDP procedure:

- **SDP Policy Update (Lines 2-14):** For each contrastive action-chunk pair `[O, A−, A+]` in a batch, a desired action set `Â` is constructed. `N` desired action-chunks are sampled by running a truncated denoising process (from `K_A` to 0 steps) with the `L_Â` reflection operator applied after each denoising step. The diffusion model is then trained using these sampled `A^0` chunks with the standard denoising loss.
- **Online Interactive Imitation Learning (IIL) Loop (Lines 15-30):** The robot executes actions from action-chunks sampled by `πθ`. If undesired behavior occurs, a human intervenes: the human's action `a^h_t` is executed, and the robot's (unexecuted) action `a^r_t` from `πθ` is queried. `[o_t, a^r_t, a^h_t]` is saved. Once `T` consecutive human actions are available, a contrastive action-chunk `[O, A−, A+]` is formed and added to `D+−`. The policy `πθ` is updated periodically using `PolicyShaping`. This process implicitly refines the supervision region as more corrections accumulate.
- **Offline Learning Loop (Lines 31-37):** For offline demonstration datasets (without explicit `A−` actions), an auxiliary negative action `a^r_t` is synthesized for each `a^h_t` such that the desired set `Âss` has a predefined radius `r`. The correction dataset `D+−` is then extracted and used to train the policy via `PolicyShaping`.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The effectiveness of SDP was evaluated through simulation and real-world experiments, comparing it against Diffusion Policy (DP), DP-DPO, Ambient DP (ADP), CLIC, and Implicit Behavior Cloning (IBC).

#### 4.1. Results of Online Interactive Learning

- **Better Utilization of Contrastive Data:** SDP consistently outperformed DP-DPO and CLIC. DP-DPO, which uses a pairwise comparison loss, performed worse than standard DP in most manipulation tasks, suggesting that its weak supervision signal is insufficient for high-dimensional action-chunk spaces. CLIC, while also using set-valued supervision, was outperformed by SDP, indicating an advantage of diffusion models over EBMs for action-chunk data.
- **Outperformance and Robustness to Noise:** SDP consistently achieved higher success rates than DP across tasks under both accurate and noisy intervention feedback (Table I). DP, relying on direct BC, showed significant performance degradation with noisy feedback. In contrast, SDP's use of desired action sets, rather than exact labels, allowed it to learn more robustly under noise, resulting in substantially smaller performance drops. Even Ambient DP (ADP), which uses privileged information to recover clean distributions, was outperformed by SDP, demonstrating SDP's inherent robustness.

#### 4.2. Results of Offline Learning from Different Datasets

- **Higher-Quality Aggregated Datasets:** Policies trained on data collected by the SDP agent during online interaction consistently outperformed those trained on data collected by the DP agent. This indicates that SDP's online data aggregation process generates datasets with more favorable properties for offline learning. Visualization revealed that SDP-collected datasets exhibited broader state coverage compared to DP's, which tended to follow the teacher's trajectory more strictly. This broader coverage is attributed to SDP's set-valued supervision, which encourages exploration around teacher-induced trajectories.
- **Superior Performance Across Datasets:** SDP consistently outperformed DP when trained on various offline datasets, including those collected by SDP, by DP, and the Robomimic dataset (Fig. 4). The performance gap was particularly significant with small action-chunks (T=1) and velocity control, where policies are less resilient to distributional shift. SDP's use of desired action sets is hypothesized to induce smoother action labels, reducing overfitting and improving generalization. SDP's efficacy with the Robomimic dataset demonstrated its applicability to standard offline demonstration datasets, even when negative actions are not explicitly provided (by synthesizing auxiliary negative actions).

#### 4.3. Ablation Study

- **Sampling Strategy for Desired Action-Chunks:** An ablation study on different sampling strategies for desired action-chunks showed that SDP's default "Reflection to a+" method achieved strong and consistent performance, comparable to "Random reflection" (Table II). This suggests that enforcing set membership via reflection is more critical than the exact projection strategy. In contrast, "Classifier Guidance" methods performed substantially worse, indicating their failure to reliably produce in-set desired action-chunks.

#### 4.4. Results of Real-World Experiments

- **Insert-T Task:** In a multi-modal Insert-T task, SDP consistently outperformed DP in terms of success rate, both after pretraining and when trained on a combined dataset of demonstrations and corrections (Table III). SDP showed significantly larger improvements on the "hard" task, suggesting a greater effectiveness in leveraging negative actions from intervention data compared to DP, which only imitates positive actions.
- **Round-table Assembly Task:** For a long-horizon roundtable assembly task, SDP consistently outperformed DP across all datasets (initial demonstrations, combined demonstrations and corrections, and a larger demonstration-only dataset) (Fig. 8). The performance gain with the "Demo30 + Corrections" dataset was the largest, attributed to SDP's ability to effectively utilize the concentrated correction data at failure-prone bottleneck stages, unlike DP which discards negative information. The results also indicated that strategically collected correction data (Demo30 + Corrections) yielded better performance than simply increasing the quantity of demonstrations (Demo60), as corrections naturally target challenging stages.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

**Significance:**
This research introduces the Set-Supervised Diffusion Policy (SDP), a framework that advances robotic imitation learning by effectively integrating set-valued supervision with diffusion policies for action-chunking. The work addresses a limitation of traditional Behavior Cloning (BC) methods, including Diffusion Policies, which often discard valuable negative feedback generated during human-in-the-loop interventions. By constructing "desired action sets" from both desired and undesired action-chunks, SDP provides a richer and more robust learning signal than single-point imitation.

The key contributions include:
1. **Effective Utilization of Contrastive Feedback:** SDP demonstrates a mechanism to explicitly leverage both positive and negative actions from human corrections, leading to more robust policy learning compared to methods that only use positive examples (like standard DP) or struggle with high-dimensional action spaces (like DP-DPO).
2. **Increased Robustness to Noisy Data:** The use of set-valued targets allows SDP policies to learn from a region of acceptable actions rather than strictly adhering to a single, potentially noisy, expert action. This provides a buffer against imperfections in human feedback, a common challenge in real-world interactive learning.
3. **Improved Data Aggregation Quality:** SDP's online learning process encourages broader exploration within the state space by allowing the policy to sample actions from the desired set. This generates aggregated datasets with better coverage, enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of subsequent offline learning.
4. **Compatibility with Action-Chunking Diffusion Models:** The framework successfully extends set-valued supervision, previously applied to single-step EBMs, to high-dimensional action-chunk spaces compatible with modern diffusion policies. This integrates two powerful concepts in robotic learning.
5. **Applicability to Offline Datasets:** The method demonstrates an ability to be applied to standard offline demonstration datasets by synthesizing auxiliary negative actions, broadening its utility beyond purely online correction scenarios.

**Potential Impact:**
The development of SDP has several potential impacts on robotic manipulation and interactive learning:
1. **More Efficient Robot Skill Acquisition:** Robots can learn complex tasks more efficiently with human assistance, as the system makes better use of corrective feedback. This could reduce the number of interventions required and the overall data collection effort.
2. **Enhanced Reliability in Real-World Deployment:** By improving policy robustness to noisy or imperfect human interventions, SDP can lead to more reliable robot behavior in dynamic and unpredictable real-world environments.
3. **Broader Adoption of Advanced Policy Architectures:** By making diffusion policies more robust and amenable to human corrections, SDP could facilitate the deployment of these powerful, high-dimensional action-space models in a wider range of practical applications.
4. **Reduced Dependency on Perfectly Clean Data:** The method’s ability to learn from imperfect and noisy data lessens the stringent requirements for perfectly curated expert demonstrations, making robot learning more accessible and scalable.
5. **Foundation for Future Interactive Learning Systems:** The set-supervised approach provides a foundation for developing more sophisticated interactive learning systems that can interpret and learn from diverse forms of human feedback beyond simple positive actions.

**Limitations and Future Work:**
The authors acknowledge certain limitations. SDP currently assumes "absolute corrections" (i.e., direct demonstrations of correct actions) and cannot be directly applied to "relative corrections" (e.g., preference judgments between two actions). Additionally, the process of sampling desired action-chunks during training introduces an additional computational cost (approximately 38% of total training time in experiments). Future research could focus on extending SDP to handle alternative feedback modalities and improving the computational efficiency of the sampling process.
