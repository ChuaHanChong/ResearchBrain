# SPIDER: Scalable Physics-Informed Dexterous Retargeting

> [!summary] Summary
> SPIDER introduces a scalable physics-informed framework that transforms kinematic human demonstrations into dynamically feasible robot trajectories, achieving an order-of-magnitude faster generation speed than previous methods while ensuring robust execution across diverse robot embodiments and tasks. The framework successfully generated 2.4 million frames of robot data for dexterous manipulation and humanoid control, demonstrating deployment on physical hardware.

## Problem

- Robot control for dexterous manipulation and whole-body tasks faces data scarcity due to the high cost and complexity of collecting robot-specific demonstration data.
- Transferring abundant human motion data to robots is challenging due to the 'embodiment gap' (morphology, dynamics, actuation differences) and the lack of crucial dynamic information in human data.
- Existing retargeting methods often lack physical realism, scalability, robustness to out-of-distribution motions, or computational efficiency for large datasets.

## Method

- Formulates retargeting as a constrained optimization problem, minimizing the distance to human motion references and control effort, subject to physics simulator constraints.
- Employs a scalable sampling-based optimizer with an annealed sampling kernel and parallelized simulation for efficient exploration and refinement of control sequences.
- Introduces 'virtual contact guidance' with curriculum-style relaxation to steer the optimizer toward human-intended contact modes in complex, contact-rich tasks.

## Results

- Generated 2.4 million frames (800 hours) of dynamically feasible robot data for 5 dexterous hands and 4 humanoids across 6 human motion datasets.
- Achieved task success rates up to **100%** in challenging manipulation, with virtual contact guidance improving performance by approximately **18%** over sampling alone.
- Demonstrated an order-of-magnitude faster trajectory generation (2.5-**1.5** FPS) compared to state-of-the-art RL-based methods (0.1-**0.05** FPS) while maintaining competitive task success and validating on physical hardware.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Physics-informed sampling, combined with an annealed kernel, effectively navigates the non-convex landscape of contact-rich robot control, achieving high success rates.
> - Curriculum-style virtual contact guidance resolves ambiguity in contact-rich tasks, enabling convergence to desired contact modes even with noisy human references.
> - Optimizing for worst-case cost across perturbed dynamics parameters (pessimistic objective) enhances trajectory robustness, allowing direct deployment on physical hardware.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of robot control, especially for complex tasks like dexterous manipulation and whole-body humanoid control, faces a significant data scarcity problem. Training robust and agile policies typically requires large-scale, robot-specific demonstration data, which is prohibitively expensive and time-consuming to collect due to hardware constraints and task complexity. In stark contrast, human motion data—from motion capture, internet videos, and virtual reality platforms—is abundant and increasingly easy to reconstruct in 3D. However, directly transferring these human demonstrations to robots is hampered by the "embodiment gap": fundamental differences in morphology, dynamics, and actuation between humans and robots. Furthermore, human motion data often lacks critical dynamic information like forces and torques, rendering direct kinematic transfer dynamically infeasible.

This paper positions itself directly at the nexus of these challenges, aiming to bridge the embodiment gap and leverage readily available human data for robot learning. It builds upon and differentiates itself from several existing approaches:

- **Kinematic Retargeting:** Traditional methods, often based on inverse kinematics (IK), efficiently map human poses to robot configurations. While computationally fast, they largely ignore physics and dynamics, leading to motions that are often dynamically infeasible, unstable, or involve undesirable penetrations, especially in contact-rich tasks. SPIDER addresses this by explicitly incorporating physics.
- **Learning-Based Retargeting Networks:** Neural network approaches learn mappings from human to robot motions, offering fast inference after extensive pre-training. However, they can struggle with out-of-distribution motions, novel embodiments, and may not inherently guarantee physical realism or contact consistency. SPIDER, in contrast, uses a physics-informed optimization framework.
- **Optimization-Based Retargeting:** These methods explicitly incorporate physics and contact constraints to generate plausible motions. While high-quality, they often depend on detailed contact data, specific pipelines, or strong priors, and can be computationally expensive due to the non-convex nature of the problem. SPIDER advances this by introducing scalable sampling and curriculum-style contact guidance.
- **Reinforcement Learning (RL) Based Retargeting:** RL has shown promise in learning dynamically feasible robot motions from human demonstrations. However, it typically requires expensive trajectory-specific training, tedious reward engineering, and significant computational resources, limiting its scalability to large datasets. SPIDER offers an order-of-magnitude speed improvement over RL baselines for trajectory generation.

SPIDER distinguishes itself as a "universal physics-based retargeting method" by combining the scalability of human data with physics-based dynamic feasibility, and the generality of handling complex dynamics with the efficiency of optimization-based approaches. It directly tackles the core problem of transforming kinematic-only human demonstrations into dynamically feasible robot trajectories *at scale*, thereby generating high-quality, robot-executable datasets that can dramatically accelerate policy learning for a wide range of robot embodiments and tasks. This work is a crucial step towards unlocking the potential of vast human motion data for practical, generalizable robot control.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind SPIDER stems from the significant bottleneck in robot learning: the prohibitive cost and complexity of acquiring large-scale, embodiment-specific robot demonstration data. While humanoid and dexterous hand control holds immense promise for real-world applications, generating the necessary data for agile and generalizable policies remains a major challenge due to hardware limitations, intricate task dynamics, and the sheer volume of data required.

In contrast, the researchers observe an abundance of readily available human motion data—ranging from motion-capture (MoCap) datasets to internet-scale video collections and virtual reality (VR) interactions. Recent advancements in computer vision have further facilitated the reconstruction of 3D human and hand motion, as well as object meshes and trajectories, directly from these diverse sources. This creates a unique opportunity to leverage human data as a scalable and affordable source of demonstrations for robot learning.

However, a fundamental challenge known as the "embodiment gap" prevents direct utilization of human demonstrations on robots. This gap arises from mismatches in morphology, dynamics, and actuation between humans and robots. Additionally, human motion data often lacks crucial dynamic information, such as forces and torques, which are essential for ensuring physical feasibility on a robot. These issues render direct kinematic transfer from human to robot motion dynamically infeasible and prone to errors.

**The central research question that SPIDER aims to answer is:** "How can we efficiently and reliably transform human motion into feasible robot trajectories that respect dynamics and contact?"

To address this, the **key objectives** of the SPIDER framework are threefold:
1. **Dynamical Feasibility:** To generate robot motions that are physically plausible and executable, overcoming the embodiment gap and the inherent noise or infeasibility of raw human demonstrations.
2. **Scalability and Efficiency:** To develop an approach that can process and transform large-scale human datasets quickly, making it computationally efficient enough for internet-scale data.
3. **Robustness and Contact Preservation:** To ensure that the retargeted robot trajectories not only respect dynamics but also preserve the intended human-preferred contact sequences and manipulation objectives, even when the original human data lacks explicit force or contact information.

The core insight driving SPIDER is that human demonstrations should provide the "global task structure and objective," while a physics-based approach, involving "large-scale physics-based sampling with curriculum-style virtual contact guidance," should refine these trajectories to guarantee dynamical feasibility and correct contact sequences. Ultimately, SPIDER seeks to serve as a universal, flexible, and efficient physics-based retargeting method that can work with diverse data qualities and generate high-quality, dynamically feasible robot data to enable more efficient policy learning with methods like reinforcement learning.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

SPIDER (Scalable Physics-Informed DExterous Retargeting) is a physics-based retargeting framework designed to transform kinematic-only human demonstrations into dynamically feasible robot trajectories at scale. Its methodology integrates several innovative components:

#### 3.1. Physics-based Retargeting Problem Formulation

The problem is formulated as a constrained optimization task. Given a kinematic reference state sequence $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ref}}_{0:T}$ (composed of robot and object positions and velocities derived from human demonstrations), SPIDER optimizes a robot control sequence $\mathbf{u}_{0:T-1}$. The objective (Eq. 1a) is to minimize the distance between the optimized feasible state $\mathbf{x}_{T}$ and the reference $\mathbf{x}_{\text{ref}}_{T}$, as well as the total control effort, over the trajectory horizon. This is subject to physical constraints: the state transition function $\mathbf{x}_{t+1} = f(\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{u}_t, t)$ (Eq. 1b) governed by a physics simulator, and bounds on the state and control input spaces (Eq. 1c).

#### 3.2. Sampling for Physics-based Retargeting

Given the highly non-convex and often non-continuous nature of contact-rich physics-based optimization, SPIDER employs a sampling-based optimizer. Unlike reinforcement learning (RL) which updates a policy, SPIDER directly optimizes the control sequence $\mathbf{U}$.
- **Annealed Sampling Kernel:** A key component is the use of an annealed sampling kernel (Eq. 2, 3, Algorithm 1) to manage the exploration-exploitation trade-off. It involves iteratively updating the control sequence using a weighted average of sampled Gaussian noise perturbations. The covariance of the Gaussian noise, $\Sigma_h^i$, is scheduled to decrease over outer iterations (controlled by $\beta_1$) and along the prediction horizon (controlled by $\beta_2$). This allows for broad exploration in early stages to find feasible contact modes, gradually narrowing down to fine refinement for stable contact.
- **Parallelized Simulation:** To achieve scalability, the method leverages parallel physics simulators, rolling out multiple candidate control sequences simultaneously to compute their costs efficiently.
- **Early Stopping:** Optimization halts when improvements from further sampling become negligible, enhancing efficiency.

#### 3.3. Virtual Contact Guidance

A critical challenge in retargeting contact-rich tasks is "solution ambiguity," where multiple dynamically feasible contact modes exist, but only one aligns with the human's intended behavior.
- **Mechanism:** SPIDER introduces virtual contact guidance to steer the sampling-based optimizer towards the desired contact mode. This is achieved by imposing a virtual constraint (Eq. 4) between intended contact points on the object and robot fingers. This constraint effectively "sticks" the object to the target configuration early in the optimization process.
- **Curriculum-style Relaxation:** The strength of this virtual constraint, controlled by a penalty parameter $\eta_i$, is gradually relaxed in a curriculum-like fashion as the optimization progresses. This "enlarges the basin of attraction" for the desired contact mode, making it easier for the sampler to converge to it.
- **Robustness against Imperfect Reference Contact:** To prevent noisy or unstable contacts in the human demonstration from biasing the optimization, a contact filter is applied. Virtual constraints are disabled for contacts shorter than a minimum duration ($t_{c,min}$) or if the contact point drifts excessively ($d_{c,max}$), ensuring only reliable contacts contribute to guidance.

#### 3.4. Trajectory Robustification

To minimize the "sim-to-real" gap and make trajectories directly deployable on physical hardware, SPIDER robustifies generated trajectories against unknown or misspecified dynamics (e.g., friction, contact compliance) and noise in reconstructed data.
- **Pessimistic Objective:** It optimizes the control sequence using a pessimistic (min-max) objective (Eq. 5). This evaluates each candidate sequence based on its worst-case cost across a bounded set of perturbed dynamics parameters $\mathcal{D}$ (e.g., variations in contact margin, friction coefficients, object mass).
- **Domain Randomization Integration:** This approach integrates seamlessly with the sampling-based update rule and leverages GPU parallelization for batched rollouts, similar to domain randomization techniques.

#### 3.5. Physics-based Data Augmentation

Beyond generating feasible robot actions, SPIDER can systematically augment a single human demonstration into a diverse set of physically feasible robot behaviors.
- **Geometric Variations:** This includes perturbing initial object poses and scales, replacing object meshes entirely (e.g., changing a "coke" to a "cat toy" for grasping), or modifying environmental elements like terrain (e.g., converting flat-ground running to stair climbing). The robot's trajectory automatically adapts to these changes, demonstrating physics-aware versatility.
- **Physics Variations:** SPIDER can generate new behaviors by applying external forces to the robot (e.g., pulling a robot carrying a heavy object). The retargeted trajectory dynamically adapts to resist these forces, such as by leaning forward or slowing down, showcasing its force-aware nature.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy and broad applicability of SPIDER are thoroughly evaluated across a wide range of robot embodiments, tasks, and data sources, demonstrating its significant contributions to scalable and robust robot control.

#### 4.1. Scale and Generality

SPIDER is presented as the first universal retargeting method capable of handling both dexterous manipulation and humanoid whole-body control tasks at a large scale. It was evaluated across:
- **Embodiments:** 5 dexterous robot hands (Allegro, XHand, Inspire, Ability, Schunk) and 4 humanoid robots (Unitree G1, Unitree H1-2, Fourier N1, Booster T1), which exhibit significant variations in degrees of freedom and morphology.
- **Datasets:** 6 diverse human motion datasets (GigaHands, Oakink, ARCTIC for manipulation; LAFAN1, AMASS, OMOMO for humanoid locomotion/interaction).
- **Data Generation:** The pipeline enabled the generation of a massive 2.4 million frames (equivalent to 800 hours) of dynamically feasible robot data, covering 262 episodes, 5 distinct robotic hands, and 103 different objects, derived from human data.

#### 4.2. Ablation Study

An ablation study on challenging manipulation tasks from Oakink and GigaHands datasets (Table 1) revealed the critical contributions of SPIDER's components:
- **Kinematic retargeting** (fingertip IK) showed very low success rates (0-13%), confirming its dynamic infeasibility.
- **Vanilla sampling** (MPPI-based) offered a modest improvement (23-64%).
- **Sampling with annealed kernel** significantly boosted success rates (43-100%) by effectively managing exploration-exploitation.
- **SPIDER (Sampling + annealed kernel + virtual contact guidance)** consistently achieved the highest success rates, outperforming the annealed-kernel-only version by approximately 18%. While slightly slower than the annealing-only version (2.5 Hz vs 3.0 Hz), the improved quality justified the marginal speed reduction.

#### 4.3. Dexterous Manipulation Retargeting

- **Retargeted Dataset Quality (Table 2, Fig 6):** SPIDER produced high-quality, dynamically feasible trajectories across diverse dexterous hands and datasets. GigaHands, focused on pick-and-place, showed higher success rates than Oakink, which involved more contact-sensitive pre-grasped objects. Hands with higher DoF (e.g., Inspire, Allegro) generally facilitated easier retargeting due to increased flexibility.
- **Comparison with Baselines (Table 3):** SPIDER demonstrated competitive task success rates compared to state-of-the-art RL-based policy learning systems like ManipTrans and DexMachina. Crucially, SPIDER was *an order of magnitude faster* in trajectory generation (2.5 FPS vs 0.1 FPS for ManipTrans; 1.5 FPS vs 0.05 FPS for DexMachina). This speed advantage is vital for large-scale dataset generation.
- **Deployment Results (Fig 7):** Open-loop deployment of SPIDER-generated trajectories on a physical Franka Emika Panda with an Allegro hand successfully executed complex dexterous tasks (e.g., rotating a light bulb, playing a guitar, unplugging a charger). This validated the dynamical feasibility and precision of the retargeted motions, requiring no additional adaptation beyond the robustification strategies.

#### 4.4. Humanoid Whole-Body Control Retargeting

SPIDER proved equally versatile for humanoid robots (Fig 8), using virtual contact guidance between feet and the floor.
- **Artifact Correction:** It effectively corrected common kinematic artifacts like foot penetration and slipping.
- **Performance vs. GMR (Table 4):** Compared to a popular kinematics-based method (GMR), SPIDER achieved significantly lower joint tracking error, pelvis position error, pelvis orientation error, and object pose errors across LAFAN1, AMASS, and OMOMO datasets, while maintaining competitive generation speeds.

#### 4.5. Applications

- **Retargeting from Single RGB Camera (Fig 9):** SPIDER demonstrated robustness to real-world data quality issues by successfully converting noisy 3D reconstructions from single RGB camera videos into executable robot trajectories. It corrected artifacts and penetrations, producing physically plausible motions for tasks like pouring and installing a bolt.
- **Retargeting for RL Policy Training (Fig 10):** SPIDER's output provides a superior foundation for RL. Policies trained with SPIDER-generated, physics-grounded reference motions converged faster and achieved better object tracking performance than those trained with original, infeasible kinematic references. This highlights how SPIDER simplifies the RL problem by providing an excellent feedforward nominal control and a feasible trajectory, allowing the RL policy to focus on learning only a residual feedback term.

In summary, SPIDER delivers a highly scalable, efficient, and robust solution for transforming human kinematic data into dynamically feasible robot trajectories across diverse embodiments and tasks, significantly outperforming previous methods in speed and quality while enabling large-scale data generation and improving downstream robot learning.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

SPIDER represents a significant advancement in the field of robot learning and control, addressing a critical bottleneck in the quest for generalizable and agile robotic capabilities. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

**1. Enabling Scalable Data Generation for Robot Learning:** The most profound impact of SPIDER is its ability to convert vast quantities of readily available, yet kinematically noisy, human motion data into high-quality, *dynamically feasible* robot demonstration data. This directly tackles the data scarcity problem that has traditionally hindered the development of advanced robot skills. By generating millions of frames of robot-executable trajectories, SPIDER unlocks a new paradigm for data-driven robot learning, akin to how large datasets have revolutionized other AI fields.

**2. Bridging the Human-Robot Embodiment Gap Effectively:** SPIDER offers a robust and general solution to the long-standing embodiment gap. It moves beyond simple kinematic mappings by explicitly incorporating physics constraints and sophisticated contact guidance. This ensures that the retargeted motions are not only geometrically similar but also physically plausible and stable for execution on diverse robot morphologies, from multi-fingered dexterous hands to complex humanoid platforms.

**3. Enhancing Efficiency and Practicality:** The framework achieves an order-of-magnitude faster trajectory generation speed compared to state-of-the-art reinforcement learning (RL) based retargeting methods. This efficiency is crucial for processing large datasets and opens doors for potential real-time or online applications, making physics-based retargeting a practical tool for researchers and developers.

**4. Universality Across Embodiments and Tasks:** SPIDER's design is embodiment-agnostic and task-general, successfully demonstrated across nine different robot morphologies (dexterous hands and humanoids) and six diverse datasets. This versatility makes it a powerful, "drop-in replacement" solution for a wide array of robotics applications, reducing the need for specialized, robot-specific retargeting techniques.

**5. Improving Reinforcement Learning Performance:** By providing high-quality, physics-grounded reference trajectories and nominal feedforward controls, SPIDER significantly simplifies the task for downstream RL policies. As shown in the results, RL agents trained with SPIDER-generated data converge faster and achieve superior performance, as they only need to learn a residual feedback term to correct for deviations, rather than learning complex dynamics from scratch. This can accelerate the development of more robust and agile robot controllers.

**6. Robustness to Real-World Data Imperfections:** The successful retargeting from noisy 3D reconstructions derived from a single RGB camera video demonstrates SPIDER's practical utility in real-world scenarios. Its ability to correct for reconstruction artifacts, penetrations, and unstable contacts through physics grounding makes it suitable for leveraging everyday human videos as a source of demonstrations.

**7. Advancing Robot Autonomy and Human-Robot Interaction:** SPIDER's capabilities can lead to more autonomous robots that can learn complex tasks by observing human behavior, fostering more intuitive and natural human-robot collaboration and interaction. The physics-based data augmentation also allows for generating diverse scenarios and stress tests for robots, leading to more resilient and adaptable skills.

**Potential Future Work:**
The paper acknowledges that SPIDER's performance still depends on the quality of reconstructed 3D human-object interaction data, implying future work could focus on making the framework even more robust to extremely noisy or sparse upstream data. A promising application highlighted is the integration of SPIDER into behavior cloning pipelines to unlock more generalizable dexterous manipulation policies. Further exploration could also involve dynamic adaptation of the virtual contact guidance strength based on real-time feedback, or extending the robustification strategies to a wider range of physical parameters and environmental uncertainties. Ultimately, SPIDER lays a strong foundation for building robots that can intuitively understand and replicate human-demonstrated skills in the physical world.
