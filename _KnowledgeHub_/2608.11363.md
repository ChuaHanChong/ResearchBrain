# Adaptation of Generalist Robot Policies with Minimal Data

> [!summary] Summary
> MiDAS adapts a pre-trained vision-language-action policy to a new task from as little as one demonstration. Stage I runs LoRA behavior cloning on the demo to anchor coarse task behavior; Stage II freezes that policy and trains a lightweight residual actor-critic online with sparse-reward value-based RL, using offline warmup, success balancing, and policy-agnostic action-gradient extraction. Average success reaches 91.2% on LIBERO-Long and 89.3% on RoboCasa.

## Problem

- Generalist VLA policies are unreliable zero-shot in new environments, scoring 0% on target tasks, so sparse-reward online RL never reaches reward and cannot bootstrap.
- Prior autonomous-improvement methods assume either an already-competent initial policy or tens to hundreds of demonstrations, and collapse when a weak policy collects mostly failures.
- Few-shot and in-context imitation policies suffer causal confusion and shortcut features, and provide no mechanism for improving autonomously from the robot's own interaction experience.

## Method

- **Stage I**: ==LoRA== behavior cloning of a pre-trained VLA backbone plus full ==flow-matching== action head on K demonstrations, yielding a task-anchored but unreliable base policy.
- **Stage II**: frozen-backbone ==residual actor-critic==, a ==tanh-Gaussian residual== conditioned on state and the base action chunk that predicts the executed action directly instead of a bounded additive offset.
- Three stabilizers: ==offline warmup== regressing the residual onto base proposals while calibrating the critic, ==success balancing== that oversamples a success buffer, and ==Best-of-N action-gradient policy distillation==.

## Results

- From **one** demonstration, MiDAS averages **91.2%** on LIBERO-Long against **33.5%** behavior cloning, **33.5%** action-noise steering, **39.0%** filtered BC, and **89.3%** on RoboCasa versus **34.0%** best baseline.
- On a bimanual YAM robot, one-demo success rises from **40%** to **67%** and from **27%** to **80%** after **5-6** hours of autonomous interaction, averaging **33.5%** to **73.5%**.
- Ablations: removing Stage I yields **0%** throughout training; MiDAS actions sit **4.54** from the base action cloud versus **0.024** for steering and **0.112** for filtered BC.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Action-aligned robot pretraining, not generic visual pretraining, is what makes online value learning sample-efficient; self-supervised vision features work but need far more interaction.
> - Sharpening or steering inside a base policy's action support cannot supply missing fine-grained control; adaptation must be free to move outside that support.
> - Autonomous interaction robustifies behaviors already reachable, but new affordances or grasps stay out of reach; a widening reset curriculum only partly compensates.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of robotic systems capable of autonomous improvement through self-experience represents a significant objective in the field of robot learning. Historically, autonomous robot adaptation has largely relied on specialist, single-task policies, frequently requiring substantial task-specific engineering. The recent advent of generalist robot policies, which are pre-trained on diverse and large datasets, offers an opportunity to re-evaluate the problem of efficient online adaptation. These pre-trained models, often vision-language-action (VLA) models, provide broad action priors and reusable representations derived from extensive data.

Despite these advancements, current generalist policies often exhibit limitations in zero-shot deployment in novel environments. They may struggle with tasks characterized by sparse rewards, insufficient exploration to reach reward-bearing states, or a propensity to visit only a narrow subset of useful states. This can impede the bootstrapping of effective online learning. Concurrently, prior work in autonomous policy improvement typically operates under more favorable conditions, assuming either a substantial initial task competence or the availability of numerous demonstrations (tens to hundreds). While few-shot imitation learning methods explore learning from very limited demonstrations, they often lack mechanisms for continuous autonomous improvement from self-generated experience.

This research addresses a specific gap in this landscape by proposing the "minimal-data adaptation (MDA)" setting. This setting investigates whether pre-trained generalist policies can efficiently adapt to new tasks using a minimal amount of task-specific supervision, such as one or a few demonstrations, combined with subsequent autonomous online interaction. This positions the work in the intermediate regime between unreliable zero-shot deployment and more data-intensive fine-tuning or adaptation methods, exploring how minimal human guidance can render autonomous learning tractable. The paper also aims to elucidate the distinct roles of pre-training, limited demonstrations, and online interaction in facilitating such adaptation.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to enable robots to autonomously refine their behavior in new environments, allowing them to reliably perform tasks after initial exposure. This overarching goal involves addressing several algorithmic challenges related to rapid adaptation with minimal task-specific supervision.

The motivation for this work stems from the observed limitations of current generalist robot policies, despite their large-scale pre-training. While these policies benefit from high-dimensional observation representations useful for policy and critic learning, and they possess behavioral priors from diverse manipulation data, they often fail to operate reliably in new environments without task-specific adjustments. Specifically, in zero-shot scenarios, these policies may not accurately interpret task instructions, consistently miss sparse rewards, or restrict their exploration to a limited set of states, thereby providing insufficient signal to initiate effective online learning.

To address this challenge, the authors propose the **Minimal-Data Adaptation (MDA)** setting. In this context, a pre-trained generalist policy is tasked with improving its performance autonomously using only:
1. A pre-trained Vision-Language-Action (VLA) policy, denoted as $\pi_{base}$.
2. A small number, $K$, of successful demonstrations (e.g., $K=1$) collected in the target environment ($\mathcal{M}_{target}$).
3. The ability to perform autonomous online interaction within $\mathcal{M}_{target}$.

The primary motivations for studying this MDA setting are:
- **Proximity to Full Autonomy:** The setting is designed as a tractable approximation of fully autonomous robotic improvement, examining whether minimal human input can bootstrap the learning process.
- **Feasibility of Minimal Guidance:** It investigates the algorithmic components necessary to make autonomous learning feasible under conditions of very limited human supervision.
- **Addressing Limitations of Prior Approaches:** Existing autonomous adaptation methods often depend on either substantial initial policy competence or the availability of tens to hundreds of demonstrations. MDA seeks to overcome these prerequisites. Similarly, few-shot imitation learning, while effective at inferring tasks from few examples, typically lacks mechanisms for continuous autonomous improvement based on self-generated experience.
- **Understanding Component Roles:** The MDA setting aims to isolate and analyze the distinct contributions of pre-training, demonstrations, and autonomous online interaction to the overall adaptation process. The hypothesis is that pre-training provides general features and priors, demonstrations anchor the policy to the target task making relevant behavior reachable, and online interaction provides the fine-grained corrections needed for reliable control.

By focusing on this minimal-data regime, the research seeks to determine if contemporary generalist robot policies have reached a stage where a single demonstration can be sufficient to specify a task, allowing online interaction to subsequently develop the necessary robustness and broad coverage.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The authors introduce **MiDAS (Minimal-Data Adaptation Strategy)**, a two-stage offline-to-online reinforcement learning framework designed for adapting pre-trained generalist policies with minimal data.

**Stage I: Behavior Cloning on Minimal Demonstrations (Offline)**
The initial phase involves fine-tuning the pre-trained generalist policy, $\pi_{base}$, using $K$ successful demonstrations from the target task. This process aims to anchor the policy to the specific task, as zero-shot deployment of $\pi_{base}$ is typically unreliable.
- **Fine-tuning Strategy:** The Vision-Language-Model (VLM) backbone of $\pi_{base}$ is adapted using Low-Rank Adapters (LoRA). The entire action head is fine-tuned using a standard flow matching objective. This selective fine-tuning strategy maintains most of the VLM backbone frozen while allowing the action head and vision encoder to specialize.
- **Outcome:** This stage yields a policy, $\pi^K_{base}$, which is capable of attempting the task but often lacks reliable execution and fine-grained control.

**Stage II: Online Improvement via Value-Based Residual RL (Online)**
Following the initial behavior cloning, this stage refines $\pi^K_{base}$ through autonomous online interaction using value-based reinforcement learning. The core idea is to learn small, corrective adjustments rather than retraining the entire large pre-trained policy.
- **Frozen Backbone, Residual Policy:** $\pi^K_{base}$ is kept frozen. A lightweight residual actor-critic module is trained on top of its representations. This approach reduces the number of trainable parameters, memory footprint, and computational requirements for online adaptation.
- **Residual Policy Parameterization:** The residual policy, $\pi^{res}_\theta$, is parameterized to directly predict the executed action chunk, $a_{exec_t}$, conditioned on the current state $s_t$ and the action proposed by the base policy, $a_{base_t}$. This direct prediction, $\tanh(\mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(s_t, a_{base}), \sigma^2_\theta(s_t, a_{base})))$, allows the policy to deviate beyond a fixed additive offset around $a_{base}$, thereby exploring actions potentially far from the base policy's immediate neighborhood.
- **Value-Based RL:** An ensemble of $Q$-networks, $\{Q_{\psi_j}(s_t, a_{exec_t})\}^N_{j=1}$, is trained using Temporal Difference (TD) learning (Eq. 3.1) to estimate the expected discounted return of state-action pairs. The policy is updated to select actions with higher estimated values.

**Addressing Challenges with Minimal Demonstrations:**
The minimal-data setting introduces specific challenges, which MiDAS addresses through several design choices:
1. **Offline Warmup:** To stabilize the learning of a randomly initialized residual actor and to calibrate the critic, a "warmup" buffer ($\mathcal{B}_{warm}$) is constructed. This buffer combines the $K$ demonstrations with a small number of initial autonomous rollouts from $\pi^K_{base}$. The residual actor is then trained via an MSE loss (Eq. 4.2) to reproduce the actions proposed by $\pi^K_{base}$, initializing it to approximately preserve the base policy's behavior. The critic is also trained on $\mathcal{B}_{warm}$ to ensure its initial calibration.
2. **Success Balancing:** Given that $\pi^K_{base}$ might achieve very low success rates (e.g., 2-5%), the reward signal can be sparse. To counteract this, MiDAS maintains two replay buffers: a main buffer ($\mathcal{B}$) for all collected trajectories and a separate success buffer ($\mathcal{B}_{succ}$) for only successful trajectories. Both are initialized with the $K$ demonstrations. During critic updates, minibatches are sampled from a mixture of these buffers, oversampling from $\mathcal{B}_{succ}$ (with a ratio $\rho_{succ}=0.2$). This amplifies the signal from successful experiences, guiding value learning towards reward-bearing regions.
3. **Residual Policy Training with Policy-Agnostic RL (PA-RL):** To enable the residual policy to generate actions beyond the effective support of $\pi^K_{base}$, MiDAS employs PA-RL. It samples a single action from $\pi^K_{base}$ ($a_{base_t}$) and $N$ candidate actions from $\pi^{res}_\theta$ conditioned on $s_t$ and $a_{base_t}$. The top $M$ candidates, ranked by their $Q$-values, are selected as "elites" and further refined through gradient ascent on the $Q$-function. The residual actor is then updated to distill these refined actions. This allows for diverse exploration anchored by the base policy but free to move to higher-value regions.

**Experimental Setup:**
Experiments are conducted using $\pi_{0.5}$ as the pre-trained base policy, primarily with $K=1$ demonstration. The evaluation platforms include:
- **LIBERO-Long:** A benchmark featuring 10 long-horizon, language-conditioned manipulation tasks.
- **RoboCasa-365:** A benchmark involving long-horizon household manipulation in diverse kitchen scenes.
The action space consists of 7-dimensional end-effector commands (3D position, 3D rotation, 1D gripper) chunked into sequences of 10 primitive actions. Rewards are sparse and binary. Baselines include Filtered BC, Diffusion-Steering RL (DSRL), and DICE-RL.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research presents several findings regarding the effectiveness of MiDAS and the underlying mechanisms enabling minimal-data adaptation.

**Overall Performance of MiDAS:**
MiDAS consistently achieved high task performance from a single demonstration ($K=1$) across the LIBERO-Long and RoboCasa benchmarks.
- On LIBERO-Long, MiDAS achieved an average success rate of 91.2%, significantly outperforming the zero-shot baseline (0%) and other baselines like BC (33.5%), DSRL (33.5%), and Filtered BC (39.0%). It performed comparably to DICE-RL (89.3%).
- On RoboCasa, MiDAS attained an average success rate of 89.3%, similarly surpassing BC (22.2%), DSRL (19.1%), and Filtered BC (34.0%).
These results indicate that MiDAS successfully recovers strong policies from minimal initial supervision.

**Necessity of Stage I (Behavior Cloning Warm-up):**
A crucial finding is that Stage I, the behavior cloning on minimal demonstrations, is indispensable. When the online learning procedure of MiDAS was attempted directly from the zero-shot base policy without this initial fine-tuning, it failed to learn, achieving 0% success throughout training. This suggests that Stage I is essential for bringing the policy into a task-relevant behavioral region, from which sparse-reward online learning can subsequently bootstrap.

**Enablers of Effective Learning in MiDAS:**
The study identified three core elements contributing to MiDAS's effectiveness:

1. **Pretraining and Demonstrations for Coarse Task-Specific Behaviors:**
 - One-demonstration BC fine-tuning ($\pi^1_{base}$) instills coarse task-following behavior. Qualitative analysis revealed that $\pi^1_{base}$ often approaches correct objects, attempts the appropriate subtask sequence, and generally resembles the demonstration. However, its failures primarily manifest in unreliable execution aspects such as contact, alignment, and grasping.
 - Comparisons with policies trained on generic visual features (ResNet) or privileged state inputs, both of which showed poor performance, indicated that both task grounding from the demonstration and behavior-aware representations from pretraining are necessary for MDA.

2. **Pre-trained Representations for Improved Sample Efficiency:**
 - The choice of representation for the residual learner significantly impacts online adaptation efficiency. Experiments showed that:
 - Training a ResNet backbone from scratch during online RL failed to converge.
 - Using frozen DINO features (generic visual pre-training) improved learning but required considerably more interaction steps.
 - Utilizing representations from the pre-trained VLA backbone of $\pi_{0.5}$ enabled rapid improvement and high sample efficiency throughout training. This suggests that action-aligned pre-training provides superior priors for downstream online adaptation.

3. **Value-Based RL for Fine-Grained Control Beyond Base Policy's Prior:**
 - Analysis of action distributions demonstrated that MiDAS, through its value-based Policy-Agnostic RL (PA-RL) updates, can select actions in regions of the action space that are distinct from and often outside the effective support of $\pi^K_{base}$. In critical states, MiDAS succeeded by choosing actions in a disjoint, higher-value region, whereas baselines like Filtered BC and DSRL, which primarily sharpen or steer within the base policy's distribution, failed. This capability to "break out" of the base policy's action support is critical for learning the fine-grained control necessary to overcome brittle execution.

**Real-World Transfer:**
MiDAS was evaluated on a bimanual YAM platform for two pick-and-place tasks.
- For a block placement task, $\pi^1_{base}$ achieved 40% success, which MiDAS improved to 67% after autonomous interaction, demonstrating qualitative improvements in corrective behaviors.
- For a more challenging knife-and-donut placement task, $\pi^1_{base}$ started at 27% success, which MiDAS elevated to 80% after 5-6 hours of autonomous interaction.
These results indicate the practical applicability of MiDAS on real hardware, even when the initial policy from a single demonstration is not highly reliable.

**Generalization and Robustness:**

- **Observation-Level Robustness:** MiDAS policies (both $\pi^K_{base}$ and $\pi^K_{RL}$) retained most of their performance under observation shifts (e.g., changes in object color/texture or language paraphrases). This robustness is largely inherited from the frozen pre-trained VLM backbone, indicating that online RL improves control without compromising perceptual generalization.
- **State-Level Robustness:**
 - Generalization to state shifts that required no fundamentally new behavior, such as certain shape changes where grasp affordances transferred, was observed.
 - However, performance degraded sharply for object swaps and object changes, suggesting that shifts requiring distinct manipulation strategies or significantly different object affordances fall outside the current policy's behavioral support and are difficult to learn from minimal demonstrations and local online interaction.
 - For position perturbations, $\pi^K_{RL}$ improved over $\pi^K_{base}$ but underperformed $\pi^{50}_{base}$ (behavior-cloned on 50 demonstrations) at larger displacement magnitudes. This gap was attributed to the broader state coverage provided by more numerous human demonstrations, rather than limitations in policy expressiveness.
- **Curriculum for Generalization:** Implementing a curriculum that progressively widens the reset distribution during online RL enabled $\pi^K_{RL}$ to recover much of the position-generalization gap observed with $\pi^{50}_{base}$, without needing additional demonstrations. This indicates that autonomous adaptation can expand its coverage to larger state shifts if the required behaviors are extensions rather than fundamentally new ones.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents a notable advancement in the field of robot learning by demonstrating the feasibility of adapting generalist robot policies with minimal task-specific data. The MiDAS framework addresses a critical gap between the limited reliability of zero-shot generalist policies and the high data demands of conventional fine-tuning or adaptation methods.

The primary significance of this work lies in its empirically validated demonstration that a single successful task demonstration can effectively bootstrap autonomous learning for pre-trained robot policies. This capability, presented as the first demonstration of reliable robot policy adaptation from a single task demonstration, has several potential impacts:

- **Reduced Data Requirements for Robot Deployment:** By enabling adaptation from one or a few demonstrations, MiDAS can substantially lower the data collection burden typically associated with deploying robots in new tasks. This could accelerate the practical deployment of robotic systems in diverse, real-world environments, reducing the human effort and time investment needed for task specification.
- **Enhanced Autonomy and Efficiency:** The framework facilitates autonomous online improvement, allowing robots to refine their skills through self-experience rather than relying solely on human-provided data. The use of a lightweight residual policy, built upon a frozen pre-trained backbone, contributes to computational efficiency, making online adaptation more practical by minimizing the number of trainable parameters and associated memory/compute costs.
- **Fundamental Insights into Robot Learning:** The study provides a detailed understanding of how different sources of supervision—large-scale pre-training, minimal demonstrations, and autonomous online interaction—interact and contribute to successful policy adaptation. It clarifies that pre-trained VLA representations offer robust priors for perception and broad action capabilities, demonstrations anchor these priors to specific tasks, and value-guided online reinforcement learning provides the necessary fine-grained corrections and allows for exploration beyond initial behavioral distributions.
- **Foundation for Future Research:** MiDAS opens several avenues for subsequent research. Future work can investigate more effective exploration strategies to learn qualitatively new behaviors or distinct behavioral modes, rather than just refining existing ones. It also motivates deeper inquiry into characterizing the optimal quantity and type of demonstrations required for different tasks and desired generalization capabilities. Furthermore, extending the MDA regime to a broader range of tasks, robot embodiments, dexterous manipulation, and longer-horizon scenarios will be crucial for assessing its generalizability and revealing its boundaries. Finally, understanding the specific forms of generalization conferred by pre-training versus task-specific adaptation can lead to more balanced and robust policy learning approaches.

In summary, MiDAS contributes a robust methodology for robot policy adaptation that balances minimal human supervision with efficient autonomous learning, paving the way for more adaptable and deployable robotic systems.
