# SPECTRA: Context-Conditioned Spectral Movement Primitives for Robot Skill Generalization

> [!summary] Summary
> The SPECTRA framework introduces a multi-agent traffic simulator that combines agent-type aware flow matching with entropy-regularized distillation. This approach achieves high closed-loop realism and diverse traffic behaviors, securing the top rank in realism on the WOSAC benchmark and demonstrating superior diversity compared to existing methods.

## Problem

- Existing robot imitation learning pipelines often decouple skill generation and execution-time regulation, leading to distortions of original task-critical paths when dynamic constraints are applied post-processing.
- Traditional time-domain movement primitives (MPs) like DMPs or ProMPs entangle task geometry, temporal variation, and dynamic characteristics, making it difficult to modulate one aspect independently.
- Context-aware skill generalization methods typically predict time-domain trajectories, with dynamic admissibility handled separately, often without preserving the learned geometric path.

## Method

- The **SPECTRA** framework represents robot skills using ==finite-horizon Fourier coefficients== (**Spectral Movement Primitives, SMPs**) to explicitly separate motion shape (spectral coefficients) from execution timing (==phase law==).
- It employs frame-aware context-conditioned ==Gaussian Mixture Model/Regression (GMM/GMR)== on ==canonicalized task-band spectral coefficients== for robust skill generalization across varying task contexts (position, orientation, scale).
- A ==shape-preserving dynamic regulation== mechanism is integrated by modifying only the phase law in joint space, ensuring joint velocity and acceleration limits are respected without distorting the end-effector path.

## Results

- SPECTRA achieved the **lowest Procrustes-aligned MSE** and **mean trajectory jerk** across various trajectory families under composite demonstration corruption compared to ProMP, FMP, and MSTOMP.
- Frame-aware canonicalization provided the most significant improvement in cross-board extrapolation, enabling accurate alignment of skills to unseen board planes (e.g., vertical boards), outperforming world-frame prediction variants.
- The phase-coupled dynamic regulator successfully converted an inadmissible motion (exceeding joint velocity by **1.452x** and acceleration by **2.108x**) into a dynamically admissible one (**0.980x** velocity, **0.960x** acceleration) while preserving the end-effector path.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Separating motion geometry from execution timing in the frequency domain allows for a unified approach to context generalization and dynamically admissible execution.
> - Canonicalizing demonstrations into a task-frame and phase-aligned representation is crucial for robust out-of-distribution generalization across diverse task contexts.
> - Dynamically admissible motions can be achieved by solely adjusting the phase progression, thereby preserving the geometric integrity of the learned motion path in both joint and task space.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The work presented in SPECTRA addresses challenges within the field of robot imitation learning, particularly for manipulation tasks requiring both generalization across contexts and dynamic admissibility during execution.

Existing imitation learning pipelines often decouple skill generation and execution-time regulation. Trajectories are learned in the time domain or through latent representations, and dynamic constraints (such as joint velocity and acceleration limits) are applied as a post-processing step via filtering, smoothing, clipping, or time scaling. This sequential approach can lead to distortions of the original task-critical end-effector paths. While path-preserving timing methods exist, they typically assume a pre-specified geometric path, not addressing how that path is learned and generalized from demonstrations.

Movement primitives (MPs), including Dynamical Movement Primitives (DMPs), Probabilistic Movement Primitives (ProMPs), and Kernelized Movement Primitives (KMPs), have been established representations for structured, low-dimensional motion encoding. However, most MPs are time-domain representations, which can entangle task geometry, temporal variation, and dynamic characteristics.

Context-aware and task-parameterized skill generalization methods have improved the ability of robots to adapt demonstrated skills to varying task conditions (e.g., changes in object pose, target position, or scale). These methods typically use context to predict time-domain trajectory distributions, with execution regulation handled separately.

Frequency-domain motion modeling has been explored for rhythmic and periodic robot skills, with approaches like Fourier Movement Primitives (FMPs) demonstrating benefits for compact encoding and handling phase variability. More recent works in visuomotor policies have also used frequency-domain action modeling, recognizing that low-frequency components often capture global motion structure. However, these methods typically do not jointly address context generalization and execution-level dynamic admissibility within a learned movement primitive framework.

SPECTRA aims to integrate these aspects by proposing a frequency-domain imitation learning framework that couples task-space skill generation with joint-space execution regulation. It seeks to fill the gap where existing methods either learn motions without inherent dynamic awareness or enforce dynamics without preserving the learned geometric path, particularly for periodic and quasi-periodic manipulation skills. The paper argues for a common spectral formulation where motion shape (spectral coefficients) is separated from execution timing (phase law) to address these coupled requirements.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of the SPECTRA framework are to:
1. **Develop a robot skill representation that compactly captures dominant motion geometry:** This representation should make the harmonic structure of motions explicit, allowing for differentiation between components defining motion shape and those contributing to derivative growth.
2. **Enable robust generalization of skills across varying task contexts:** The framework should adapt learned motions to changes in task-frame position, orientation, surface pose, and in-plane scale in a structured and frame-aware manner.
3. **Ensure dynamic admissibility of generated robot motions during execution:** The system should produce joint motions that respect prescribed velocity and acceleration limits without distorting the intended end-effector path.
4. **Integrate skill generation and dynamic regulation within a unified framework:** The aim is to overcome the structural gap between learning what motion to perform and how to execute it admissibly, which is common in pipelines that separate these stages.

The motivation for these objectives stems from several limitations observed in current robot imitation learning practices:
- **Decoupled processing:** Many methods generate nominal trajectories first and then apply dynamic constraints through post-processing. This can lead to sample-wise corrections that distort the learned task geometry, particularly for tasks where geometric and rhythmic structure are critical.
- **Entanglement of motion characteristics:** Time-domain movement primitives often combine task geometry, temporal variation, and dynamic characteristics within a single representation, making it difficult to modulate one aspect without affecting others.
- **Relevance for periodic/quasi-periodic tasks:** For skills like wiping or stirring, success depends on preserving both geometry and rhythm. A frequency-domain representation is considered natural for these tasks, as low-frequency harmonics capture dominant geometry, while higher frequencies contribute to rapid temporal variations and derivative growth. This suggests that contextual generation and execution-level admissibility can be addressed within a common spectral formulation.
- **Need for shape-preserving regulation:** When dynamic limits are exceeded, adjusting the timing or phase of a motion is often preferable to altering its geometric path. The framework seeks to implement this path-preserving principle within a learned primitive.

By addressing these motivations, SPECTRA aims to provide a robust and integrated solution for robot skill generalization and dynamically admissible execution, particularly for smooth, periodic, and quasi-periodic manipulation tasks.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The SPECTRA framework integrates a frequency-domain representation, context-conditioned skill generation, and shape-preserving phase regulation. It operates at both task-space and joint-space levels.

**A. Spectral Movement Primitive (SMP) Representation**

1. **Finite-Horizon Fourier Parameterization:**
 - Trajectories `y(τ)` are represented as a truncated Fourier expansion over a finite horizon `H`. Each dimension `y_j(τ)` is defined by a sum of sine and cosine terms up to a maximum harmonic order `K`, along with a DC component.
 - The collection of these Fourier coefficients `(a_j,0, a_j,k, b_j,k)` forms the task-space Spectral Movement Primitive (SMP) parameter vector `c_y`.
 - This representation explicitly exposes the harmonic structure, where low-order harmonics capture dominant geometry, and higher-order harmonics contribute more to temporal derivatives.
 - The same Fourier structure is used in both task space (for skill generation) and joint space (for phase regulation), but with distinct coefficient vectors.

2. **Demonstration-Driven Task-Band Decomposition:**
 - Context-conditioned inference is restricted to a compact, low-frequency "task band" of the SMP.
 - A maximum harmonic order `K` is initially chosen for near-lossless reconstruction.
 - An empirical cutoff `K_task` is selected by analyzing the normalized reconstruction Mean Squared Error (MSE) `e(k)` as `k` increases. `K_task` is the smallest order where the error `e(k)` falls below a floor `e_floor` or where adding subsequent harmonics provides negligible relative improvement `I_k < τ` for `P` consecutive harmonics.
 - This decomposition isolates a "task band" `B_task` (harmonics up to `K_task`) for learning, while higher-frequency components `B_comp` are considered more responsible for derivative growth and local details. The retained task-band coefficients form `c_LF`.
 - The approach leverages Fourier orthogonality, where reconstruction error is tied to the energy of omitted harmonics, and derivative contributions increase with harmonic frequency.

**B. Frame-Aware Context-Conditioned Spectral Prior**

1. **Task-Frame and Phase Canonicalization:**
 - To generalize skills across varying task conditions, demonstrations are canonicalized before statistical learning.
 - **Task-frame canonicalization:** For each demonstration, the end-effector trajectory `y^(w)_n(t)` (world-frame) is transformed into a canonical trajectory `y^(c)_n(t)`. This involves:
 - Defining a task context `x_n` including task-frame origin `p_n`, orientation `η_n`, and in-plane scales `s_n`.
 - Applying an inverse transformation: `y^(c,n)(t) = D^(-1)s,n R^T_n (y^(w,n)(t) - p_n)`, where `R_n` is the rotation matrix and `D_s,n` is a diagonal matrix for in-plane scaling. The surface-normal coordinate is left unscaled.
 - For surface-constrained tasks, the canonical normal coordinate `y^(c,n)_⊥(t)` is set to zero.
 - **Phase canonicalization:** For periodic tasks, demonstrations are circularly phase-aligned to a common reference to remove arbitrary starting phase ambiguities, ensuring geometrically equivalent cycles yield similar coefficient vectors. This involves resampling on a common phase grid and finding an optimal circular shift `ℓ_⋆_n`. This alignment preserves inter-dimensional and inter-harmonic phase relationships.

2. **Context-Conditioned GMM/GMR in the Canonical Task Band:**
 - After canonicalization, each demonstration is encoded into its task-band spectral coefficients `c^(n)_LF,c`.
 - A context variable `x^(n)_gmr` is constructed, containing only variables meaningful after canonicalization (e.g., in-plane scales, `s_n`, and residual task variables `ξ_n`). Global pose `(p_n, η_n)` is not directly regressed as its effect is handled by explicit transformations.
 - A Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) `p(z)` is trained on the joint variable `z = [x_gmr, c_LF,c]^T`.
 - Given a query context `x_⋆`, Gaussian Mixture Regression (GMR) is used to predict the canonical task-band code `c_LF,c` by combining component-wise conditional means weighted by responsibilities `h_m(x_⋆_gmr)`. If no residual context variable exists, the prediction defaults to the empirical mean.

3. **Task-Band Decoding and World-Frame Reconstruction:**
 - The predicted `c_LF,c` is converted into a full canonical spectral code `c_c` (filling non-task-band harmonics with zeros).
 - The nominal canonical trajectory `y^(c)_1:T` is reconstructed using `SMPDecode(c_c)`.
 - This canonical trajectory is then transformed back into the query task frame: `y^(w)_1:T = R_⋆ D_⋆_s y^(c)_1:T + p_⋆`, using the query context `(R_⋆, D_⋆_s, p_⋆)`.
 - This process yields a Cartesian end-effector trajectory, which is then mapped to a continuous joint trajectory via inverse kinematics for dynamic regulation.

**C. Shape-Preserving Dynamic Regulation**

1. **Inverse Kinematics and Joint-Space SMP:**
 - The world-frame end-effector trajectory `y^(w)_1:T` and desired end-effector orientations `R^(w)_1:T` are used to compute a continuous nominal joint trajectory `q_1:T` through sequential inverse kinematics, warm-starting from the previous solution to encourage continuity.
 - This joint trajectory `q_1:T` is then encoded into a separate joint-space SMP coefficient vector `c_q`. This `c_q` is distinct from the task-space code and parameterizes the joint-space realization.

2. **Phase-Coupled Regulation:**
 - The regulator keeps the joint-space spectral coefficients `c_q` fixed and modifies only the phase law `ϕ(t)`, thereby preserving the represented joint-space path.
 - Joint velocities `q̇_d(t)` and accelerations `q̈_d(t)` are expressed analytically in terms of phase `ϕ(t)` and its derivatives `ϕ̇(t)` and `ϕ̈(t)`.
 - For constant phase speed (`ϕ̈_reg(t) = 0`), exact phase-derivative envelopes `Γ^(1)_d` (for velocity) and `Γ^(2)_d` (for acceleration) are computed for each joint over the phase range `[0, 2π]`.
 - Given prescribed joint velocity (`v̄_d`) and acceleration (`ā_d`) limits, admissible phase-speed bounds (`ϕ̇_v`, `ϕ̇_a`) are derived from these envelopes.
 - The regulated phase speed `ϕ̇_reg` is determined by taking the minimum of a desired phase speed `ϕ̇_des` (scaled from nominal `ω`) and the calculated admissible bounds `ϕ̇_v`, `ϕ̇_a`. This ensures the desired timing is kept if admissible, otherwise it is reduced to the fastest admissible constant value.
 - The regulated duration `H_reg` and trajectory `q_reg(t)` are then derived.
 - Shape preservation is assessed by comparing the nominal and regulated end-effector paths on a common phase grid, `x_nom(ϕ)` and `x_reg(ϕ)`. Since `c_q` is fixed, any discrepancy reflects numerical or implementation deviations.
 - Mean joint jerk `J_q` is reported as a smoothness measure but not actively constrained.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments evaluated SPECTRA across task-band reconstruction, robustness, contextual generalization, dynamic admissibility, and real-robot deployment.

**A. Task-Band Decomposition and Empirical Cutoff Selection**
- **Compact Geometric Reconstruction:** The experiments demonstrated that for smooth manipulation trajectories, a compact low-frequency task band is sufficient to represent the dominant motion geometry. For example, a smooth figure-eight trajectory required a small number of harmonics, while a flower pattern required a moderately larger cutoff, and a spiky star trajectory (with sharper local structures) demanded a substantially larger number of harmonics.
- **Reconstruction Saturation:** Reconstruction-saturation curves showed that for smooth trajectories, the reconstruction error decreases rapidly at low harmonic orders and then quickly flattens, indicating that the dominant shape is captured by a small frequency band. The empirical cutoff rule effectively identified the order at which additional harmonics provided negligible improvement.
- **Derivative Contribution:** The observation confirmed that sharper local structures require a broader spectrum for approximation, aligning with the principle that higher-frequency components contribute disproportionately to derivative growth. The method also applied to smooth non-closed motions, demonstrating recovery of dominant geometry with a compact task band.

**B. Robust Reconstruction Under Composite Demonstration Corruption**
- **Superior Reconstruction Fidelity:** When comparing against ProMP, FMP, and MSTOMP under composite demonstration corruption (phase perturbation, Gaussian noise, high-frequency noise, sparse outliers), SPECTRA achieved the lowest Procrustes-aligned MSE (PA-MSE) and mean trajectory jerk across all four tested trajectory families (figure-eight, Lissajous 2:3, five-petal flower, and rounded-star).
- **Noise Suppression:** The fixed task-band reconstruction effectively suppressed derivative-heavy corruption while preserving the dominant motion geometry, indicating its robustness.

**C. Cross-Board Generalization and Representation Ablation**
- **Importance of Canonicalization:** World-frame prediction variants (TD and W-Spec) often retained the basic figure-eight shape but failed to consistently place it correctly in unseen task frames (e.g., vertical boards). Their Procrustes errors were low, but board-local and world-frame errors were substantially higher, indicating inaccurate translation, orientation, or scale. This finding highlights that world-frame GMM/GMR does not inherently enforce geometric equivariance, leading to biases toward training configurations.
- **Canonicalization as Dominant Improvement:** Canonicalization (C-TD and Ours) provided the most significant improvement in both 2D (board-local MSE, shape MSE) and 3D (world-frame end-effector MSE, normal-distance error) cross-board extrapolation. It enabled accurate alignment of the generated figure-eight with unseen board planes, including vertical boards.
- **Task-Band Spectral Inference Benefits:** Within the canonicalized variants, SPECTRA (Ours) further reduced board-local, shape, and world-frame errors compared to C-TD. This indicates that predicting only the retained task-band coefficients offers additional benefits in terms of compactness and phase-consistent representation.

**D. Joint-Space Dynamic Regulation and End-Effector Path Preservation**
- **Dynamic Admissibility:** The phase-coupled spectral regulator successfully converted an intentionally accelerated, dynamically inadmissible motion (exceeding joint velocity and acceleration limits by factors of 1.452 and 2.108 respectively) into a dynamically admissible execution. The regulated execution maintained velocity and acceleration ratios below 1 (0.980 and 0.960, respectively).
- **Path Preservation:** Crucially, the regulation was achieved by reducing the requested phase speed only, without modifying the underlying joint-space or end-effector paths. This was demonstrated by the phase-aligned joint velocity and acceleration profiles, which showed reduced magnitudes but retained the same phase-dependent structure.
- **Reduced Jerk:** While the regulated motion was faster than the nominal and thus had higher jerk, it substantially reduced the jerk compared to the un-regulated accelerated motion (649.6 vs. 2112.3).

**E. Real-Robot Validation**
- **Qualitative Generalization:** Real-robot experiments on a Franka Panda robot demonstrated that motions learned only on a horizontal board could be successfully executed on both the original horizontal board and a vertical board with a significantly different surface orientation. This was shown for circular-like, figure-eight (periodic), and open C-shaped (non-periodic) trajectories.
- **Timing Modulation:** The physical robot execution also confirmed that the execution rate could be adjusted through phase modulation. Changing the phase-speed factor allowed for faster and slower executions (e.g., reducing duration from 19.98s to 15.98s with a 1.25 factor, or increasing to 24.97s with a 0.80 factor).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The SPECTRA framework introduces a method for robot imitation learning that aims to address the common disconnect between skill generation and dynamically admissible execution. Its significance lies in several key aspects:

1. **Unified Spectral Formulation:** By representing robot skills using finite-horizon Fourier coefficients, the framework explicitly separates the geometric shape of a motion (encoded by spectral coefficients) from its execution timing (determined by the phase law). This allows for context-conditioned learning and dynamic regulation within a single, consistent mathematical framework.
2. **Robust Skill Generalization:** The frame-aware canonicalization approach provides a structured way to generalize learned skills across significant changes in task context, such as variations in position, orientation, and scale. This is particularly impactful for tasks where motions are inherently defined relative to a task frame (e.g., surface-constrained manipulation). The ablation studies demonstrate the critical role of explicit geometric canonicalization for reliable out-of-distribution transfer.
3. **Guaranteed Dynamic Admissibility with Path Preservation:** The shape-preserving phase regulation layer directly addresses the challenge of ensuring robot motions respect joint velocity and acceleration limits. By adjusting only the phase progression without altering the spectral coefficients, the method ensures that dynamic constraints are met while maintaining the geometric integrity of the intended end-effector path. This approach avoids the path distortions often associated with post-hoc filtering or clipping.
4. **Practical Applicability for Smooth Manipulation:** The empirical task-band selection process allows the method to adapt to the geometric complexity of demonstrated trajectories. For typical smooth, periodic, or quasi-periodic manipulation skills, a compact low-frequency representation is often sufficient, making the learning and regulation efficient. The real-robot validation on a Franka Panda confirms the practical deployability of the framework for real-world tasks.

The potential impact of SPECTRA includes:
- **More reliable and safer robot deployment:** By inherently generating dynamically admissible motions, the framework can contribute to safer robot operation, reduced wear on hardware, and improved tracking quality, especially for high-speed or repetitive tasks.
- **Improved learning from demonstration:** The ability to generalize skills robustly across diverse task contexts from limited demonstrations can reduce the data requirements for teaching robots new tasks.
- **Enhanced skill transferability:** The explicit handling of task-frame transformations facilitates the transfer of skills to novel environments or object configurations without requiring re-demonstration for every new scenario.
- **Foundation for future work:** The method establishes a strong foundation for extending frequency-domain imitation learning to more complex scenarios, such as contact-rich manipulation, where precise path control and dynamic awareness are even more critical.
