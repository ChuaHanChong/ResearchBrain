# A Task-Centric Theory for Iterative Self-Improvement with Easy-to-Hard Curricula

> [!summary] Summary
> Models each round of LLM self-improvement as maximum-likelihood fine-tuning on a reward-filtered distribution, then derives finite-sample lower bounds on expected reward. The bounds expose a feedback loop where stronger models accept more samples per round, sustaining gains until saturation, and give explicit conditions on task-difficulty separation, sample budget, and initialization under which easy-to-hard curricula provably beat fixed mixtures; validated on synthetic graph and math reasoning benchmarks.

## Problem

- Prior self-improvement theory studies model evolution abstractly through sharpening or solver-verifier gaps, ignoring how the pretrained model interacts with a specific downstream task.
- Population-level analyses imply one round always exceeds the acceptance threshold regardless of initialization, contradicting practice, because finite-sample effects of filtered data are unmodeled.
- Easy-to-hard scheduling across rounds helps empirically, but no theory quantifies when such curricula beat fixed-difficulty mixtures under an equal question budget.

## Method

- Formalizes each round as empirical ==maximum-likelihood fine-tuning== on a ==reward-filtered distribution==, with per-question and global acceptance rates driving a ==finite-sample expected-reward lower bound==.
- Under a ==reward-acceptance coupling== assumption, composes the per-round bound into an ==iterated map== giving multi-step guarantees plus a ==monotone-improvement interval== on initial performance.
- A ==power-law difficulty-separation== assumption with exponents defines the ==staged curriculum==, and explicit feasibility plus improvement conditions compare its composed bound against the ==fixed-mixture baseline==.

## Results

- On synthetic shortest-path with Llama-3.2-1B, only mid-range initializations improved: trajectories from **6%** initial Pass@1 turned unstable or collapsed, and **78%** declined across rounds.
- On GSM8K, Qwen3-32B gained **5.18 points** over three rounds; for Qwen3-8B a question budget of 600 lost **4.06 points** while 2400 gained **3.15**, and 16 answers per question gained **5.34**.
- On DeepMind Mathematics, curriculum gains peaked at interior staging: arithmetic add_sub_multiple reached **+17.08 points** at staging ratio 0.8 versus **+10.64** under fully staged training.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Self-improvement only works when task difficulty is moderate for the base model: too easy yields no signal, too hard risks collapse.
> - Reward-filtered self-training saturates well below perfect accuracy, so pipelines need a further phase such as reinforcement learning to keep gaining.
> - Curriculum benefit is conditional: it needs moderate adjacent-difficulty separation and a question budget above a critical size, below which the advantage collapses sharply.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The development of large language models (LLMs) has demonstrated increasing reasoning capabilities, which are often further enhanced through post-training strategies. A prominent approach among these is iterative self-improvement, where an LLM generates candidate solutions, filters them based on external verification or rewards, and then fine-tunes itself on these curated outputs. A related empirical practice involves structuring these self-improvement iterations with an easy-to-hard curriculum, progressively introducing more challenging tasks over time.

Despite the empirical successes of these methods in domains such as mathematical reasoning, the theoretical understanding of their underlying mechanisms, particularly in practical, finite-sample settings, remains limited. Existing theoretical work has explored aspects of self-improvement such as probability-mass "sharpening" and the learning dynamics from a solver-verifier-gap perspective. Other related areas include self-distillation, where a model generates its own supervision, and the phenomenon of "model collapse" in self-consuming generative loops, along with mitigation strategies.

This paper distinguishes itself by addressing specific theoretical gaps: it provides a finite-sample, task-centric account of multi-step iterative self-improvement, focusing on settings with binary and verifiable rewards typical of mathematical reasoning. It further investigates the quantifiable conditions under which easy-to-hard curricula demonstrably improve performance compared to training on a fixed mixture of tasks. By modeling the discrete, multi-step nature of these processes and their interaction with task characteristics, this work offers a theoretical framework more directly aligned with the empirical realities and observed gains of self-improvement pipelines.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research is to establish a robust theoretical foundation for the empirical efficacy of iterative self-improvement and easy-to-hard curricula in fine-tuning autoregressive LLMs, especially within a practical, finite-sample context. The existing theoretical literature, while making progress, has largely focused on continuous limits or model evolution perspectives, leaving certain practical questions underspecified.

The paper aims to address two specific, practical questions through a task-centric theoretical framework:
1. **For a single task, when does multi-step self-improvement occur?** This objective seeks to understand the conditions and mechanisms that enable sustained performance gains across multiple iterations of self-improvement when the model is applied to a single type of reasoning task. This includes clarifying the roles of model initialization, inherent task difficulty, and the finite number of available samples. A key part of this is to explain both the observed sustained improvement and its eventual saturation.
2. **With a mixture of tasks, when does task scheduling, such as easy-to-hard curricula, provably help?** This objective investigates the conditions under which strategically sequencing tasks from easier to harder levels across self-improvement iterations yields better performance guarantees than training on a static, mixed distribution of tasks. This involves identifying quantifiable criteria related to the separation of task difficulties, the available sample budget, and the initial model performance that contribute to the effectiveness of such curricula.

By providing theory-grounded answers to these questions, the research aims to move beyond empirical observations and offer a deeper, mechanistic understanding that can inform the design and optimization of future LLM post-training pipelines. This includes providing insights into how factors like data acceptance rates, model-generated data, and curriculum design interact to influence the learning dynamics and ultimate performance of self-improving LLMs.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The research establishes a theoretical framework to analyze iterative self-improvement, modeling each iteration as maximum-likelihood fine-tuning on a reward-filtered distribution.

**Problem Setup:**
At iteration *t*, the current model parameters *θ_t* are used to generate answers *a* for sampled questions *q* from a base distribution *p_0*. A reward function *s(q, a)* ∈ quantifies the quality of the answer. Only samples (q, a) where *s(q, a)* ≥ *τ* (a threshold) are retained. This filtering induces a population-level distribution *D'*. In practice, with a finite sample of *n* questions, *n_t* accepted samples are used for empirical maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) to update the model to *θ_t+1*. The objective is to relate this update to the expected reward *V_p0(θt+1)*. The model class *Π* is assumed to be finite.

**Single-Step Self-Improvement:**
Theorem 4.1 provides finite-sample guarantees for a single round of self-improvement. It introduces *m*, the per-question answer budget (number of candidates generated per question), and analyzes its impact on the lower bound of the expected reward *V_p0(θt+1)*. The theorem states that with probability at least 1-δ, *V_p0(θt+1)* is bounded below by a term dependent on the acceptance rate, model class size, and the effective number of accepted samples *n_t*. A key insight from this theorem is that, unlike an infinite-sample scenario where performance above *τ* is guaranteed regardless of *θt*, finite-sample analysis reveals that a stronger guarantee (higher lower bound) is achieved by increasing both the question budget (*n*) and the per-question answer budget (*m*). The ratio *Z^(m)p0(θt)/α^(m)(θt)* (related to the global and essential minimum acceptance rates) is shown to be non-increasing in *m* and approaches 1 as *m* increases, indicating the benefit of generating more candidates per question.

**Multi-Step Self-Improvement (Binary Reward):**
The analysis specializes to mathematical reasoning tasks, where the reward *s(q, a)* is binary ({0, 1}).
Assumption 4.3 posits a positive correlation between the per-question acceptance rate and the expected reward.
Corollary 4.4 establishes an iterated map *F(x)* that lower bounds *V_p0(θt+1)* based on *V_p0(θt)*. This allows for multi-step analysis: *V_p0(θt)* ≥ *F^ot(V_p0(θ0))*. The function *F(x)* involves constants *c_δ* (related to model class size and failure probability), *c_δ'* (related to another failure probability), and *ν* (inverse square root of question budget *n*).
Proposition 4.5 analyzes the properties of this iterated map. It reveals an interval *I(1, ν)* within which the expected reward lower bound is guaranteed to be monotonically increasing across iterations, implying sustained self-improvement. Outside this interval, improvement is not guaranteed. The interval's size and position depend on *ν*, implying that larger budgets (*n*, smaller *ν*) enlarge the range of initial performances where improvement is guaranteed. It also identifies an inherent upper bound, *x^+(1, ν)*, strictly below 1-γ, suggesting a saturation limit.

**Iterative Easy-to-Hard Curriculum for Self-Improvement:**
The framework is extended to compare easy-to-hard curricula with a fixed-mixture baseline.
Assumption 5.1 formalizes task difficulty with *L* levels (p1..., pL), establishing a power-law separation between adjacent tasks using parameters *β'* (minimum difficulty ratio) and *β* (maximum difficulty ratio).
The **easy-to-hard** curriculum involves *L* iterations, where at iteration *t*, training samples are drawn from *p_t+1*.
The **baseline** trains for *L* iterations using a fixed uniform mixture *p_0* of all task difficulties.
Theorem 5.2 provides conditions for the effectiveness of easy-to-hard curricula. It defines **feasibility conditions** (*M_i < 0*) that ensure both training schemes exhibit monotonically increasing lower bounds. Corollary 5.3 interprets these conditions, showing that feasibility is favored by larger budgets and moderate adjacent task difficulty ratios.
The **improvement condition** (*N < 0*) in Theorem 5.2 specifies when the easy-to-hard curriculum provably achieves a strictly tighter lower bound than the baseline. Proposition 5.5 and Corollary 5.6 detail the characteristics of the initialization region (*I_N*) where this improvement occurs. Key interpretations include:
- A **phase transition** with respect to the question budget: The admissible initialization region *I_N* shrinks as *ν* increases, with a sharp collapse near a critical sample size *ν_c*.
- **Smaller uncertainty** in difficulty ratios (smaller *Δ* = *β* - *β'*) is beneficial.
- **Moderate difficulty ratios** between adjacent tasks are most favorable for easy-to-hard curricula to outperform the baseline. Extremely small or large ratios diminish the advantage.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research provides both theoretical predictions and empirical validation across synthetic and standard mathematical reasoning tasks.

**Theoretical Findings:**
1. **Feedback Loop and Saturation in Self-Improvement:** The analysis of single- and multi-step self-improvement reveals an explicit feedback mechanism: as a model improves (achieves higher *Vp0(θt)*), its acceptance rate (*Zp0(θt)*) for reward-filtered data increases. This effectively expands the training data available per iteration, sustaining further self-improvement. However, the theoretical lower bound *F^ot(Vp0(θ0))* cannot exceed an inherent upper bound (*x^+(1, ν) < 1-γ*), providing a rationale for the observed saturation of self-improvement in practice.
2. **Conditions for Sustained Self-Improvement:** Iterative self-improvement is theoretically guaranteed to yield monotonic improvements only when the task difficulty is within a "moderate" range relative to the model's initial performance, such that *Vp0(θ0)* falls into a specific interval *I(1, ν)*. Larger question budgets (*n*) or per-question answer budgets (*m*) enlarge this interval, thus widening the range of initial performances for which sustained improvement is guaranteed.
3. **Quantifiable Conditions for Easy-to-Hard Curricula Advantage:** For multi-task settings, easy-to-hard curricula provably outperform fixed-mixture training under specific, quantifiable conditions:
 - **Moderate Separation of Task Difficulties:** The effectiveness of easy-to-hard scheduling depends on an appropriate balance in the difficulty ratios between adjacent tasks (captured by *β'*). Both excessively small (too similar) and excessively large (too disparate) difficulty ratios are predicted to be suboptimal.
 - **Critical Sample Budget:** A phase transition behavior is predicted, where decreasing the question budget (*n*) severely constrains the range of initializations for which easy-to-hard is advantageous. Beyond a critical sample size, the benefits of easy-to-hard curricula diminish sharply.
 - **Model Initialization and Uncertainty:** The initial model performance (*Vp0(θ0)*) and the uncertainty in difficulty ratios (*Δ = β - β'*) also play roles, with smaller uncertainty generally being more favorable.

**Empirical Validation:**
1. **Synthetic Shortest Path Task:**
 - **Iterative Self-Improvement:** Experiments on a graph-based shortest path task supported the theoretical predictions. Self-improvement was most effective for tasks of moderate difficulty; for overly easy tasks, performance decreased, while for overly hard tasks, it became unstable or collapsed, aligning with Remark 4.6.1. The observed plateauing of performance below 1 confirmed the theoretical saturation limit (Remark 4.6.3). Increasing either the question budget (*n*) or the per-question answer budget (*m*) consistently improved self-improvement, consistent with the finite-sample analysis in Section 4.1.
 - **Easy-to-Hard Curriculum:** The performance gap between easy-to-hard and baseline exhibited a "first increasing then decreasing" trend as the adjacent task difficulty ratio (*β'*) varied, with peak gains around *β'* ∈ [0.2, 0.5]. This supported the prediction that moderate difficulty ratios are most favorable (Remark 5.7.3). A clear phase transition was observed regarding the question budget (*n*); the minimal *n* for easy-to-hard to outperform the baseline clustered in a narrow range, consistent with Remark 5.7.1.

2. **Standard Mathematical Reasoning Benchmarks (GSM8K and DeepMind Mathematics):**
 - **Iterative Self-Improvement:** Experiments across various LLMs (Llama, Qwen) on GSM8K and DeepMind Mathematics corroborated the synthetic findings. Self-improvement was limited or even detrimental for tasks too easy or too hard relative to the base model, while moderate difficulty was conducive to sustained gains (Remark 4.6.1). Incremental improvements tended to decrease over iterations, aligning with the saturation limit (Remark 4.6.3). Larger question budgets (*n*) and per-question answer budgets (*m*) were consistently more favorable to self-improvement (Section 4.1).
 - **Easy-to-Hard Curriculum:** On the DeepMind Mathematics dataset, which offers native difficulty partitions, a parameter *ρ* was used to interpolate between fixed-mixture training and a fully staged easy-to-hard curriculum. As *ρ* (and thus *β'* and *β*) increased, the final Pass@1 gap between easy-to-hard and baseline typically showed an initial increase followed by a decrease, with maximum gains at an intermediate *ρ* value. This provided empirical support for the theoretical prediction of optimal moderate difficulty ratios (Remark 5.7.3).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research contributes a theoretical framework for understanding iterative self-improvement and easy-to-hard curricula in large language models, particularly in finite-sample, task-centric settings common in reasoning tasks. The work provides a rigorous explanation for several empirically observed phenomena, including the dynamics of sustained self-improvement, its eventual saturation, and the conditions under which curriculum learning is beneficial.

The significance and potential impact of this work include:
1. **Enhanced Theoretical Understanding:** It advances the fundamental understanding of how LLMs learn from their own generated data, particularly for generative reasoning tasks with verifiable rewards. By providing finite-sample guarantees and characterizing explicit feedback loops, the theory offers a more granular and practically relevant perspective than prior infinite-sample or continuous-limit analyses.
2. **Guidance for Practical Pipeline Design:** The derived quantifiable conditions and insights offer actionable guidance for practitioners. Developers of LLM pipelines can leverage these findings to:
 - **Select appropriate tasks:** Understanding the "moderate difficulty" regime for sustained self-improvement can inform task selection or dynamic difficulty adjustment.
 - **Optimize resource allocation:** Insights into the benefits of larger question and per-question answer budgets, as well as the critical sample size for curriculum learning, can guide efficient allocation of computational resources.
 - **Design effective curricula:** The identification of optimal moderate difficulty ratios between adjacent tasks can inform the structuring of easy-to-hard learning schedules.
3. **Foundation for Future Research:** The framework lays groundwork for further theoretical extensions. It identifies avenues for future work, such as incorporating reward noise (where verifiers might be imperfect or biased) and extending the analysis to various test-time training methods that also involve gradient-based parameter updates. This suggests a broader applicability of the task-centric, finite-sample approach.

In summary, this paper provides a robust theoretical lens that not only explains existing empirical successes but also offers predictive power for designing more effective and efficient iterative self-improvement strategies for large language models.
