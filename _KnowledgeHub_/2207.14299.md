# Graph Inverse Reinforcement Learning from Diverse Videos

> [!summary] Summary
> GraphIRL learns reward functions for robot manipulation from diverse third-person human videos by abstracting each frame into an object-centric graph of detected bounding boxes, encoding entity interactions with an Interaction-Network encoder, and training it with a temporal cycle-consistency alignment loss. Euclidean distance to a goal embedding gives a dense reward; policies trained purely on it beat prior video-IRL methods and even hand-designed rewards on real xArm pushing.

## Problem

- Manual reward design for robotic RL demands heavy domain knowledge and task-specific engineering, limiting how many tasks can practically be specified.
- Prior third-person IRL such as XIRL degrades sharply when demonstration videos differ visually from the target RL environment, restricting training to narrow video domains.
- Earlier object-centric imitation abstracts single demonstrations with hand-tuned heuristics for same-domain trajectory following, not a general cross-domain cross-embodiment alignment function.

## Method

- Object-centric graph abstraction: an off-the-shelf hand-object detector yields bounding boxes per frame, each node encoding box corners plus inter-object centroid distances, discarding texture and appearance.
- ==Spatial Interaction Encoder== built on ==Interaction Networks== composes self and pairwise interaction MLP features over a fully connected object graph, mean-pooled into a 128-d frame embedding.
- ==Temporal Cycle Consistency== loss aligns graph embeddings across video pairs; the resulting space defines a dense reward as negative scaled distance to the averaged final-frame goal embedding.

## Results

- On X-MAGICAL cross-embodiment cross-environment transfer, GraphIRL reaches **0.58** success for gripper versus **0.35** for XIRL, and **0.99** versus **0.56** for longstick.
- In simulated manipulation, GraphIRL scores **0.832** on Push against **0.512** for the hand-designed environment reward and **0.187** for XIRL; real-robot Push hits **0.60** versus **0.47** and **0.27**.
- Ablations: replacing the Interaction Network with a concatenation MLP drops Push success from **0.804** to **0.61**, and using only **25%** of videos (49) still yields **0.670**.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Discarding appearance via a structured spatial abstraction, rather than augmenting pixels, is what buys robustness when demonstration videos and deployment scenes share no visual statistics.
> - A learned progress metric can beat a hand-written distance reward on precision tasks, because it encodes interaction structure the engineer never explicitly specified.
> - Reward learning is remarkably sample-thin: dozens of videos suffice, but quality still hinges on an external detector and on humans collecting demonstrations.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

This research is situated within the field of reinforcement learning (RL) and, more specifically, inverse reinforcement learning (IRL). Deep RL has demonstrated capabilities in learning policies for various applications, including game-playing and robotics. However, its effectiveness is often contingent on the meticulous design of task-specific reward functions, which can be a challenging and expertise-dependent process.

Inverse reinforcement learning offers an alternative paradigm by inferring reward functions from expert demonstrations rather than requiring their manual specification. A notable sub-area within IRL is learning from third-person video demonstrations. This approach is appealing because it can potentially circumvent the need for specialized data collection methods like kinesthetic teaching or teleoperation, as humans can more readily capture third-person videos of tasks. Such flexibility in data collection is critical for scaling up learning in robotics.

Previous efforts in third-person video-based IRL, such as XIRL, have shown promise by using self-supervised methods like temporal cycle-consistency constraints to capture task progression. However, a significant limitation observed in these prior works is their sensitivity to visual diversity. When the visual appearance of demonstration videos diverges substantially from the target environment, the ability to learn robust task progression measures can degrade. This restricts the scalability and generalizability of these methods.

The current work addresses this limitation by proposing a method that explicitly aims to increase the diversity of videos from which reward functions can be learned. It integrates concepts from object-centric representations and graph networks, which have been explored in computer vision and robotics for tasks such as scene understanding, relational inference, and dynamics modeling. By abstracting scene information into graph structures, the authors aim to overcome the brittleness of vision-based IRL methods when faced with significant visual domain shifts. This positions the work as an advancement towards more robust and scalable IRL from diverse, real-world video data.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation for this research stems from a recognized challenge in inverse reinforcement learning from third-person videos: the limited robustness of existing methods to diverse visual demonstrations. While the concept of learning from third-person videos offers considerable flexibility in data collection—as it is often easier for humans to record such demonstrations—the performance of current IRL approaches tends to degrade when the visual characteristics of the demonstrations do not closely match those of the target environment for robot policy learning. This sensitivity to variations in appearance, embodiment, object categories, and scene configurations hinders the scalability and practical applicability of video-based IRL.

The authors pose the question: "Can we learn to imitate others from (a limited number of) diverse third-person videos?" This question encapsulates the core objective of developing an IRL framework that can transcend the visual domain gap between demonstration and execution environments.

The key objectives of this work are detailed as follows:
- **Increase Robustness to Diverse Videos:** To develop a method that can learn a task-specific reward function from a dataset of visually diverse third-person videos. This involves handling variations in object textures, colors, shapes, scene layouts, and even the embodiments of the agents performing the task (e.g., human vs. robot, different robot end-effectors).
- **Abstract Irrelevant Visual Information:** To devise a representation that captures the fundamental agent-object interactions and task progression while being invariant to superficial visual changes. The hypothesis is that a graph abstraction of entity interactions can achieve this by removing "irrelevant information such as textures."
- **Enable Cross-Embodiment and Cross-Domain Learning:** To facilitate the transfer of learned rewards and subsequent policies across different physical embodiments (e.g., from human demonstrations to a robotic arm) and visual domains (e.g., from real-world human videos to simulated robotic environments, or between visually distinct simulated environments).
- **Train Policies with Learned Rewards:** To demonstrate that reward functions learned by the proposed method are sufficiently accurate and dense to train effective image-based reinforcement learning policies in simulation.
- **Validate on Real-World Robotics:** To show that policies trained with these learned rewards can be successfully deployed on physical robots and perform tasks with precision, potentially even surpassing the performance of policies trained with manually designed reward functions.
- **Bridge the Learning Cycle:** To establish a complete pipeline for learning robotic skills, starting from real-world human demonstrations, inferring rewards, training policies in simulation, and finally deploying them on real hardware.

By addressing these objectives, the research aims to unlock the "true potential of third-person IRL" by making it more scalable and less reliant on pristine, visually consistent demonstration data.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The proposed method, Graph Inverse Reinforcement Learning (GraphIRL), is a self-supervised framework designed to learn reward functions from diverse third-person video demonstrations using temporal matching on graph abstractions. The approach consists of two main stages: representation learning and reinforcement learning with the learned reward.

**Problem Formulation:**
Given a dataset of video demonstrations $D = \{V^1, V^2, \ldots, V^n\}$, where each video $V^i$ comprises image frames $\{I^i_1, I^i_2, \ldots, I^i_k\}$, the objective is to learn a reward function capable of solving task $T$ in any robotic environment. The method does not assume access to expert action information or require visual similarity between demonstration objects/embodiments and those in the target environment.

### 4. Representation Learning

GraphIRL learns task-specific representations in a self-supervised manner by combining object-centric graph representations with a temporal cycle consistency loss.

- **Object-Centric Representation:**
 - For each frame $I^i_j$ in a video sequence, an off-the-shelf object detector is used to extract bounding boxes for all present objects.
 - Each bounding box is represented as a vector $o_l = \{x_1, y_1, x_2, y_2, d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_m\}$, where $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ are the coordinates of the bounding box corners, and $d_1, \ldots, d_m$ encode distances between the centroids of objects.
 - An entire frame $I^i_j$ is thus abstracted into an object-centric representation $I'^i_j = \{o_1, o_2, \ldots, o_m\}$, where $m$ is the number of objects. The dataset of demonstrations $D$ is transformed into $D' = \{V'^1, V'^2, \ldots, V'^n\}$, sequences of these bounding box representations.

- **Spatial Interaction Encoder Network:**
 - This component models explicit object-object interactions, drawing inspiration from methods for physical object dynamics modeling.
 - Each object-centric frame representation $I'$ is treated as a graph $G = (O, R)$, where $O$ is the set of objects $\{o_1, \ldots, o_m\}$, and $R$ defines relationships between objects. For simplicity, all objects are connected to all other objects, meaning $R = \{(i, j) \mid i \neq j \land i \le m \land j \le m\}$.
 - An embedding for each object $o_l \in O$ is computed as $f_o(o_l) = \phi_{agg}(f_s(o_l) + f_{in}(o_l))$.
 - $f_s(o_l) = \phi_s(o_l)$ represents the self-dependent features of the object.
 - $f_{in}(o_l) = \sum_{p=1}^m \phi_{in}((o_l, o_p))$ represents the interactional features, modeling how $o_l$ interacts with other objects $o_p$ in the scene.
 - $\phi_s$, $\phi_{in}$, and $\phi_{agg}$ are Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) networks.
 - The final output of the spatial interaction encoder, $\psi(I')$, for an object representation $I'$ is the mean of all object embeddings: $\psi(I') = \frac{1}{m} \sum_l f_o(o_l)$. This approach ensures that the object embedding captures both individual object properties and their relationships within the scene.

- **Temporal Alignment Loss:**
 - The spatial interaction encoder is trained using a Temporal Cycle Consistency (TCC) loss, adapted to operate on graph abstractions rather than raw image features.
 - This self-supervised task implicitly learns representations that are common across videos and exhibit temporal ordering, making them suitable for understanding task progression and invariant to visual changes.
 - Given a pair of object-centric sequences $V'^i$ and $V'^j$, their encoded features $S^i = \{\psi(I'^i_1), \ldots, \psi(I'^i_{m_i})\}$ and $S^j = \{\psi(I'^j_1), \ldots, \psi(I'^j_{m_j})\}$ are extracted.
 - For each element $S^i_n$ in $S^i$, its nearest neighbor $v^i_{nj}$ in $S^j$ is found. Then, the probability $\beta^k_{ijn}$ that $v^i_{nj}$ cycles back to the $k$-th frame in $S^i$ is computed using Euclidean distance in the embedding space.
 - The cycle consistency loss for the $n$-th element is defined as $L^n_{ij} = (\mu^n_{ij} - n)^2$, where $\mu^n_{ij}$ is the expected value of frame index $n$ after cycling back. The overall TCC loss sums this over all sequence pairs in the dataset.

### 5. Reinforcement Learning with Learned Reward

After learning a task-specific embedding space through temporal alignment, this space is used to construct a reward function.

- **Reward Function Definition:** The reward function $r(o)$ for a given observation $o$ (represented as its graph abstraction) is defined based on its Euclidean distance to a representative goal frame $g$ in the learned embedding space:
 $r(o) = - \frac{1}{c} ||\psi(o) - g||^2$
 - $g$ is computed as the average of the $\psi(\cdot)$ embeddings of the final frames of all training videos, representing the desired completed state of the task.
 - $c$ is a scaling factor, calculated as the average distance between the first and final observations of all training videos in the learned embedding space.
- This formulation provides a dense reward signal in the range $(-\infty, 0]$, where rewards approach zero as the agent's observed state gets closer to the goal state in the embedding space.
- **Policy Training:** Any standard RL algorithm can be used with this learned reward function. The authors specifically use Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) as the backbone RL algorithm. A key aspect of this approach is that, unlike some previous methods, GraphIRL does not rely on any additional environment reward for supervision, depending solely on the learned reward.

### 6. Main Findings and Results

The research evaluates GraphIRL across various settings, including simulated cross-embodiment tasks and real-robot manipulation, comparing it against several baselines.

### 7. X-MAGICAL Benchmark (Cross-Embodiment and Cross-Environment)

Experiments were conducted on a multi-object sweeping task in X-MAGICAL, using two environment variants: "Standard" (object position randomization) and "Diverse" (additional randomization of visual appearance, shape, size, and orientation). Four embodiments were considered: gripper, short-stick, medium-stick, and long-stick.

- **Cross-Embodiment Cross-Environment Setting (Figure 4):**
 - GraphIRL exhibited notable performance improvements over vision-based baselines (XIRL, TCN, LIFS) when learning from demonstrations in one environment variant and performing RL in another. For instance, in the "Standard pretraining → Diverse RL" setting, GraphIRL achieved significantly higher success rates (e.g., 0.58 for gripper and 0.99 for longstick) compared to XIRL (0.35 for gripper and 0.56 for longstick).
 - This performance gap is attributed to the robustness of GraphIRL's graph abstraction in handling visual variations, which vision-based baselines reportedly struggle with.
- **Cross-Embodiment Same-Environment Setting (Appendix D, Figure 12):**
 - GraphIRL achieved competitive success rates when compared to XIRL, while substantially outperforming TCN and LIFS. This demonstrates its effectiveness even in visually similar environments when dealing with different agent embodiments.

### 8. Robotic Manipulation Tasks (Simulation and Real Robot)

Three robotic manipulation tasks were used: Reach, Push, and Peg in Box, involving an xArm robot. Human video demonstrations for these tasks featured diverse objects, goal markers, and scene configurations, creating a significant domain gap with the robotic environment.

- **Robotic Manipulation in Simulation (Figure 5):**
 - **Reach Task:** GraphIRL achieved a 1.0 success rate, matching the performance of the manual environment reward. Vision-based baselines lagged substantially (e.g., XIRL at 0.477 and TCN at 0.155), indicating their difficulty with the visual domain shift.
 - **Push Task:** GraphIRL achieved a success rate of 0.832, outperforming both the manual environment reward (0.512) and all vision-based baselines (XIRL at 0.187). This finding suggests that GraphIRL can learn a reward function superior to a hand-designed one for this task.
 - **Peg in Box Task:** This task, requiring 3D reasoning, saw GraphIRL achieve a success rate of 0.53, comparable to the manual environment reward (0.60). All vision-based methods failed to solve this task, highlighting the challenge of 3D understanding without explicit 3D scene information for these approaches.
 - Overall, GraphIRL demonstrated the ability to solve both 2D and 3D reasoning tasks without a hand-designed reward function or explicit 3D scene input, despite the considerable domain gap.

- **Real Robot Experiments (Table 1):**
 - Policies trained with GraphIRL rewards were deployed on a physical xArm robot.
 - GraphIRL consistently outperformed XIRL across all three tasks on the real robot (Reach: 0.86 vs 0.26; Push: 0.60 vs 0.27; Peg in Box: 0.53 vs 0.06).
 - For the Push task, GraphIRL again surpassed the manual environment reward (0.60 vs 0.47), aligning with the simulation results.

### 9. Qualitative Analysis of Learned Reward (Appendix A)

The qualitative analysis showed that GraphIRL provides a more accurate and consistent reward signal that reflects actual task progress (success or failure) compared to vision-based baselines. Baselines often yielded low rewards even for successful episodes due to visual domain shift, failing to correctly align visual states with task progression.

### 10. Ablation Studies (Section 4.3)

Ablation studies were performed on the Push task to validate design choices.

- **Impact of Modelling Spatial Interactions (Table 2):** Replacing the Spatial Interaction Encoder Network (IN) with a simple Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) (which concatenates object representations without explicit interaction modeling) resulted in a 20% decrease in success rate (0.61 for MLP vs. 0.804 for IN). This supports the importance of explicitly modeling object-object spatial interactions for robust reward learning.
- **Impact of Decreasing Demonstration Videos (Table 3):** Performance gradually decreased with fewer demonstration videos. However, GraphIRL maintained a 67% success rate even with 25% of the total training videos (49 videos), indicating a degree of data efficiency in learning meaningful rewards.

### 11. MuJoCo State Pusher (Appendix F)

On a modified MuJoCo State Pusher environment with randomized goal positions, GraphIRL achieved a success rate (0.455) that was significantly higher than vision-based baselines (e.g., XIRL at 0.125) and close to the task-specific environment reward (0.6133). This further indicates strong generalization capabilities across environments for the same task.

### 12. Significance and Potential Impact

This research presents GraphIRL, an approach that advances Inverse Reinforcement Learning from diverse video demonstrations. The work demonstrates that by employing graph abstraction to model entity interactions and applying temporal matching in this abstract space, robust reward functions can be learned from videos exhibiting significant visual diversity.

The significance of this work can be highlighted through several points:

- **Enhanced Robustness and Scalability:** GraphIRL addresses a key limitation of prior video-based IRL methods, which often struggle with domain shift and visual variations. By effectively abstracting away irrelevant visual details (like textures) through object-centric graph representations, the method demonstrates improved robustness to diverse demonstrations across different embodiments and visual environments. This characteristic is crucial for scaling up IRL to real-world scenarios where demonstration data is inherently varied.
- **Reduced Reliance on Manual Reward Design:** The findings indicate that reward functions learned by GraphIRL can perform comparably to, and in some cases even surpass, carefully hand-designed reward functions. This capability has the potential to significantly reduce the need for manual reward engineering, a process that is often time-consuming, requires specialized domain knowledge, and can be a bottleneck in applying RL to complex tasks.
- **Effective Cross-Embodiment and Cross-Domain Transfer:** The graph-based abstraction enables the learning of task-specific features that are invariant to specific visual appearances or robot embodiments. This facilitates the transfer of skills from human demonstrations to various robotic platforms and allows for policy training in simulation with rewards learned from diverse real-world human videos, effectively bridging the sim-to-real gap for reward learning.
- **Comprehensive Robotic Learning Pipeline:** The research demonstrates a complete and practical pipeline for robotic skill acquisition: inferring reward functions from real-world human videos, training image-based policies in simulation using these learned rewards, and successfully deploying these policies on physical hardware. This holistic approach makes a contribution towards autonomous robot learning from readily available, diverse human data.
- **Data Efficiency:** Ablation studies suggest that GraphIRL can learn meaningful reward functions even from a relatively small number of video demonstrations, implying a degree of data efficiency that is valuable in real-world applications where extensive data collection can be costly.

**Potential Impact:**
This research could impact the development of more autonomous and adaptable robotic systems. By making it easier to acquire reward functions from observational data, it lowers the barrier for applying RL to new and complex manipulation tasks. This could accelerate progress in areas such as manufacturing, service robotics, and assistive technologies, where robots need to learn and adapt to diverse environments and tasks based on human examples.

**Limitations identified by the authors include:**
- The process of collecting demonstration videos still necessitates human labor, even if the method can learn from a relatively small number of videos.
- The complete elimination of human involvement in demonstration data collection remains an open problem for future research.

Despite these limitations, GraphIRL represents a step towards making inverse reinforcement learning more practical and widely applicable in robotics by increasing its resilience to the visual diversity inherent in real-world data.
