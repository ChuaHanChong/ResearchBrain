# How to Probe: Simple Yet Effective Techniques for Improving Post-hoc Explanations

> [!summary] Summary
> Post-hoc explanation quality for deep neural networks can be significantly improved by modifying how the classification layer (probe) is trained, with the study showing that Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss consistently enhances explanations over Cross-Entropy (CE) loss across various models and pre-training paradigms.

## Problem

- Deep Neural Networks often operate as 'black boxes', lacking transparency in their decision-making processes, which hinders their application in high-stakes domains.
- Existing post-hoc explanation methods are commonly assumed to be independent of model training details, an assumption that has been largely underexplored.
- The impact of diverse pre-training paradigms (e.g., self-supervised, vision-language) on the interpretability of models remains insufficiently understood.

## Method

- The authors employed ==linear probing==, freezing the model backbone and training various classification heads (probes) on the frozen features.
- They systematically compared standard ==Cross-Entropy (CE) loss== with ==Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss== for training the classification head.
- Explanation quality was evaluated using a diverse set of interpretability metrics, including object localization, pixel deletion, compactness, and complexity.

## Results

- Training the classification head with Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss **consistently and significantly** improved explanation quality across various interpretability metrics.
- The findings hold robustly across diverse pre-training paradigms (supervised, self-supervised, vision-language), model architectures (CNNs, vision transformers), attribution methods, and datasets.
- Using interpretable B-cos MLPs as non-linear probes led to increased localization performance, demonstrating their compatibility with modern pre-training paradigms while preserving interpretability.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - The quality of post-hoc attributions for pre-trained models heavily depends on how the classification head is trained, even when the model backbone remains frozen.
> - Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss for the classification head consistently yields superior explanation quality compared to standard Cross-Entropy (CE) loss, which is hypothesized to be due to CE's shift-invariance.
> - More complex probes, such as interpretable B-cos MLPs, can effectively distill class-specific features from pre-trained representations, enhancing localization performance.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Introduction

Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have become increasingly powerful, but their decision-making processes often remain opaque, leading to their characterization as "black boxes." This lack of transparency poses significant challenges for applications where understanding model decisions is crucial, such as healthcare, autonomous driving, and legal contexts. Post-hoc explanation methods have emerged as popular tools to interpret these black-box models after they've been trained.

The research paper "HOW TOPROBE: Simply Effective Techniques for Improving Post-Hoc Explanations" by Siddhartha Gairola, Moritz Böhle, Francesco Locatello, and Bernt Schiele addresses a critical but overlooked aspect of these explanation methods: their dependence on how models are trained. The paper demonstrates that the quality of post-hoc explanations can be significantly improved by making simple adjustments to the classification layer (or "probe") of pre-trained models, even while keeping the backbone frozen.

### 2. Background on Post-Hoc Explanations

Post-hoc explanation methods aim to interpret model decisions after training without modifying the original model architecture or training process. Common approaches include:

1. **Feature attribution methods**: These assign importance scores to input features (e.g., pixels in an image) based on their contribution to the model's decision. Examples include Layer-wise Relevance Propagation (LRP), Integrated Gradients, and Grad-CAM.

2. **Local approximation methods**: These create interpretable models that approximate the behavior of the complex model locally around specific inputs. LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) is a prominent example.

These methods have gained popularity because they can be applied to existing models without requiring changes to the training process, making them practical for real-world applications.

### 3. The Dependence Problem

A core assumption in post-hoc interpretability is that these methods can be applied independently of how the models were trained. The authors challenge this assumption by demonstrating that explanation quality is highly dependent on training details, particularly how the classification head is trained.

This insight is especially relevant given the increasing diversity of pre-training paradigms in representation learning:
- Supervised learning
- Self-supervised learning (SSL)
- Contrastive learning
- Vision-language pre-training

While these paradigms have advanced model performance, their impact on model interpretability remains underexplored. The paper addresses this gap by systematically investigating how different training objectives for the classification head affect the quality of post-hoc explanations.

### 4. Methodology

The authors employ linear probing, a technique commonly used to evaluate learned representations of pre-trained models. This approach isolates the impact of the classification head on attributions by:

1. Pre-training models using various paradigms (supervised, self-supervised, vision-language)
2. Freezing the model backbone parameters
3. Training linear and non-linear classifiers (probes) on the frozen features for downstream image classification
4. Applying attribution methods to the classification predictions
5. Evaluating explanation quality using multiple metrics

The key experimental manipulation is the comparison between different training objectives for the classification head, specifically:
- Cross-entropy (CE) loss: The standard loss function for multi-class classification
- Binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss: Typically used for multi-label classification

To evaluate explanation quality, the authors use a diverse set of interpretability metrics:
- Object localization (Grid Pointing Game, Energy Pointing Game)
- Pixel deletion
- Compactness (Gini index)
- Complexity (entropy)

### 5. Key Findings

The study reveals several important findings:

1. **Training dependency**: The quality of attributions for pre-trained models heavily depends on how the classification head is trained, even when the model backbone remains frozen.

2. **Loss function impact**: Using BCE loss instead of CE loss for training the classification head leads to consistent and significant improvements in explanation quality across various interpretability metrics.

3. **Cross-paradigm consistency**: The findings are robust across different pre-training paradigms (supervised, self-supervised, vision-language), model architectures (CNNs, vision transformers), attribution methods, and datasets.

4. **Non-linear probe benefits**: More complex probes, particularly interpretable B-cos MLPs, can better distill class-specific features from pre-trained representations, significantly increasing localization performance.

### 6. The BCE Advantage

A central finding of the paper is that BCE loss consistently outperforms CE loss for training the classification head in terms of explanation quality. The authors hypothesize that this is due to the shift-invariance property of CE loss, which can lead to poorly calibrated attributions.

The mathematical formulation helps illustrate this difference:

**Cross-Entropy Loss (CE)**:
```
L_CE(f(x), y) = -log(softmax(f(x))_y) = -log(exp(f(x)_y) / Σ_j exp(f(x)_j))
```

**Binary Cross-Entropy Loss (BCE)**:
```
L_BCE(f(x), y) = -y log(σ(f(x))) - (1-y) log(1-σ(f(x)))
```
where σ is the sigmoid function.

The key difference is that CE loss is invariant to adding a constant to all logits since the softmax normalizes them. This shift-invariance means that CE loss does not penalize a model for assigning high scores to irrelevant features, as long as the correct class gets the highest score. In contrast, BCE loss enforces more absolute calibration of the logits, leading to attributions that better focus on relevant features.

### 7. Non-Linear Probes and B-cos MLPs

The paper further explores the use of non-linear probes, specifically interpretable Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs), for improving explanation quality. Two approaches are examined:

1. **Conventional MLPs**: Standard multi-layer networks trained with BCE loss.

2. **B-cos MLPs**: A specialized architecture introduced by Böhle et al. (2022) that uses the B-cos transformation to enforce better interpretability.

The B-cos transformation can be expressed as:
```
B-cos(x, w) = ||x|| · ||w|| · cos(x, w)^β
```
where β is a parameter that controls the "sharpness" of the feature selection.

The authors demonstrate that B-cos MLPs are compatible with self-supervised learning approaches, preserving both performance and interpretability. This finding extends the applicability of inherently interpretable models to modern pre-training paradigms.

### 8. Implications for Explainable AI

The paper's findings have several important implications for the field of Explainable AI:

1. **Challenging assumptions**: The work challenges the common assumption that post-hoc attribution methods can be applied independently of model training, suggesting a need for more nuanced approaches.

2. **Practical recommendations**: The paper provides actionable recommendations for improving explanation quality, such as using BCE loss and interpretable MLPs, which can be easily implemented in existing workflows.

3. **Efficient improvements**: Since the proposed modifications only involve the classification head, they are computationally efficient and don't require retraining the entire model.

4. **Future research directions**: The findings open up new research questions about designing attribution methods that explicitly account for training details when generating explanations.

5. **Guiding probe training**: Given that training probes is usually much less expensive than training backbones, these insights can guide practitioners to train models that are inherently more interpretable post-hoc.

### 9. Conclusion

"HOW TOPROBE" makes a significant contribution to the field of Explainable AI by demonstrating the critical relationship between training details and explanation quality. By showing that simple adjustments to the classification head can dramatically improve post-hoc explanations, the authors provide practical tools for enhancing the interpretability of deep learning models.

The paper's findings apply across diverse pre-training paradigms, model architectures, and attribution methods, highlighting their broad applicability. Moreover, the work raises important questions about the assumptions underlying post-hoc attribution methods and points to a need for more training-aware approaches to model explanation.

As deep learning models continue to be deployed in high-stakes domains, the ability to generate accurate, reliable explanations of their decisions becomes increasingly important. This research takes a significant step toward addressing this challenge, offering insights and techniques that can help bridge the gap between model performance and interpretability.
