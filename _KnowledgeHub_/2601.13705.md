# Reasoning or Pattern Matching? Probing Large Vision-Language Models with Visual Puzzles

> [!summary] Summary
> This survey paper from the National Technical University of Athens and Instituto de Telecomunicações recasts visual puzzles as diagnostic instruments to probe whether Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) exhibit genuine reasoning or sophisticated pattern-matching, revealing that current LVLMs often display an "illusion of reasoning" where fluent explanations mask fundamental cognitive limitations across various reasoning types.

## Problem

- A critical question exists whether LVLMs possess genuine reasoning capabilities or primarily rely on complex pattern-matching, despite impressive performance on multimodal tasks.
- Traditional multimodal benchmarks often entangle reasoning with confounding factors, making it difficult to diagnose specific failure modes or ascertain true visual reasoning.
- There is a need for robust diagnostic tools to evaluate LVLMs' cognitive foundations beyond perception and high-level recognition.

## Method

- The authors formally define ==visual puzzles== as diagnostic instruments, characterized by essential reliance on visual information, explicitly constrained structure, and ==reasoning-centric challenges==.
- A comprehensive ==taxonomy== organizes existing ==visual puzzle benchmarks== into five distinct reasoning dimensions: ==inductive==, ==analogical==, ==algorithmic==, ==deductive==, and ==geometric/spatial reasoning==.
- The paper provides an ==empirical synthesis== of findings across these categories to identify consistent limitations and vulnerabilities in current LVLMs.

## Results

- LVLMs demonstrate brittle generalization, struggling to induce invariant rules or abstract structural patterns and degrading sharply under minor variations or out-of-distribution shifts.
- Models show a perception-reasoning entanglement, where limitations in fine-grained visual perception bottleneck downstream reasoning, and they often fail in multi-step execution and maintaining internal state for algorithmic tasks.
- LVLMs struggle with constraint integration in deductive tasks and often rely on superficial spatial cues rather than abstract geometric relations, leading to error accumulation in geometric/spatial reasoning.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Current LVLMs exhibit an "illusion of reasoning," where fluent and rational verbal explanations often do not correspond to faithful execution or comprehension of visual evidence.
> - Visual puzzles serve as uniquely sharp diagnostic tools by providing isolated environments and controlled conditions that decouple domain knowledge from pure logic.
> - Future research should focus on multi-agent collaboration, long-horizon training objectives, compositional generalization, and abductive/lateral visual reasoning to foster more reasoning-aware LVLMs.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Positioning within the Research Landscape

The rapid proliferation and widespread application of Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) have fundamentally reshaped the field of multimodal learning. While these models demonstrate impressive performance across various perception and recognition tasks, a critical question arises regarding their deeper cognitive abilities: can LVLMs genuinely reason over perceived concepts, uncover hidden patterns, and generalize effortlessly to novel situations, or do they primarily rely on sophisticated pattern-matching? This paper positions itself squarely at the nexus of this crucial inquiry.

The authors argue that traditional multimodal benchmarks often "entangle reasoning with confounding factors, such as linguistic fluency, world knowledge, and dataset biases," enabling models to achieve high performance via "shortcut strategies" rather than true visual reasoning. This entanglement makes it difficult to diagnose specific failure modes—whether the model failed to perceive input or abstract a rule.

This survey directly addresses this gap by advocating for visual puzzles as a "rigorous diagnostic instrument" for evaluating LVLMs. Unlike open-ended tasks, visual puzzles are controlled, verifiable, and designed to isolate specific cognitive operations by minimizing reliance on prior knowledge and linguistic cues.

The paper differentiates itself from existing literature by offering a unified and reasoning-driven perspective. While other surveys have explored textual puzzles (e.g., [Giadikiaroglou et al., 2024]), broader vision-language reasoning (e.g., [Zhou et al., 2025a]), specific aspects like compositionality (e.g., [Ke et al., 2025]), or narrow puzzle domains (e.g., [Markaki and Panagiotakis, 2023]), this work uniquely "recast[s] visual puzzles as diagnostic instruments for probing the reasoning abilities of LVLMs, offering a unified perspective that connects puzzle design, evaluation outcomes, and observed failure modes of LVLMs." This integrated approach provides a much-needed framework for understanding and advancing reasoning capabilities in LVLMs.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind this survey stems from the fundamental question of whether the impressive capabilities of current LVLMs truly reflect genuine reasoning or merely advanced pattern recognition. The authors highlight the transformative impact of LVLMs and the consequent need for evolving evaluation methodologies that go "beyond perception and high-level recognition" to deeply probe their cognitive foundations. The uncertainty surrounding LVLMs' capacity for "structure-aware reasoning" versus "complex pattern-matching" forms the core of their inquiry.

A significant concern articulated is that conventional multimodal benchmarks often obscure genuine reasoning by allowing models to exploit "shortcut strategies." This makes it challenging to pinpoint the exact cause of failure (e.g., perceptual error vs. reasoning flaw). Visual puzzles are presented as a timely and rigorous solution to this evaluation challenge, providing "isolated environments and controlled conditions" that decouple domain knowledge from pure logic, thus offering a "uniquely sharp lens into the 'perception-reasoning gap'."

Against this backdrop, the paper sets out several key objectives:
- **Unified Abstraction**: To provide a common framework for understanding visual puzzles by integrating their inputs, rule structures, and solution spaces.
- **Taxonomic Organization**: To systematically categorize the existing literature on visual puzzle benchmarks into five core reasoning dimensions: inductive, analogical, algorithmic, deductive, and geometrical reasoning. This taxonomy aims to link specific puzzle designs to the cognitive operations they target, enabling a more structured analysis of model failures.
- **Empirical Synthesis**: To consolidate empirical evidence from across these categories to identify consistent limitations and recurring vulnerabilities in current LVLMs.
- **Future Roadmap**: To outline key directions for the development of future benchmarks and the design of more reasoning-aware multimodal systems, thereby guiding subsequent research efforts.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The core methodology of this survey involves framing visual puzzles not merely as task formats but as powerful "diagnostic instruments" for probing LVLM reasoning. To achieve this, the authors establish a formal definition for visual puzzles and then develop a comprehensive taxonomy to organize and analyze existing benchmarks.

**Puzzle Definition:**
A visual puzzle, in the context of this work, is formalized as a problem instance **⟨I, R, S⟩**, characterized by three core properties:
1. **Essential reliance on visual information**: The task must primarily depend on visual input.
2. **Explicitly constrained and verifiable puzzle structure**: It features latent rules, transformations, or relational dependencies within a constrained problem space, specifically designed to evaluate cognitive abilities.
3. **Reasoning-centric challenges**: The task must focus on reasoning from the LVLM perspective, without relying on external knowledge priors.

Within this framework:
- **I (Input)** refers to a visual input (V) and optional textual context (T).
- **R (Rules)** concerns explicit (stated) or implicit (to be inferred) constraints that govern valid solutions.
- **S (Solution space)** is typically a discrete or combinatorial set of candidates, verifiable through a ground-truth logical consistency check.

**Taxonomy of Reasoning Categories:**
The paper organizes visual puzzle benchmarks into five distinct reasoning dimensions, chosen for their direct link to distinct cognitive operations:
1. **Inductive Reasoning**: Involves inferring latent functions from examples and generalizing them to new instances. The rules (R) are implicit, and a correctly inferred rule leads to a deterministic solution space (S). This category targets abstractive capabilities versus superficial pattern-matching.
2. **Analogical Reasoning**: Focuses on identifying relational correspondences between entities and transferring them to new contexts. It emphasizes relational similarity. Rules (R) are implicit, but the solution space (S) can be non-deterministic if multiple plausible analogies exist.
3. **Algorithmic Reasoning**: Requires executing a sequence of operations or state transitions to reach a goal. The challenge lies in multi-step state maintenance rather than rule discovery. Rules (R) are typically explicit, and solutions (S) are deterministic.
4. **Deductive Reasoning**: Derives logically necessary conclusions from clearly stated premises or constraints, requiring constraint propagation and logical entailment. Rules (R) are explicit, leading to deterministic solutions (S).
5. **Geometric / Spatial Reasoning**: Deals with spatial relationships, transformations, and structural configurations, often requiring "mental imagery." Rules (R) can be implicit or explicit, and the determinism of S depends on the constraints of R. This category often acts as an amplifier for other reasoning types.

For each category, the authors survey foundational pre-LVLM paradigms (where applicable), followed by a comprehensive review of recent LVLM benchmarks. Each section concludes with an "Analysis and Evaluation" subsection, synthesizing empirical findings and highlighting consistent LVLM weaknesses related to that specific reasoning type. This systematic approach allows for a structured comparison of model failures across diverse puzzle formats, offering diagnostic clarity.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The comprehensive synthesis of empirical evidence across the five reasoning categories reveals several consistent and recurring limitations in current Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs), suggesting that their apparent competence often stems from sophisticated pattern-matching rather than faithful, robust reasoning.

**General Limitations Across Categories:**

- **Brittle Generalization**: A pervasive issue is the LVLMs' struggle to generalize beyond the training distribution. Models frequently rely on "superficial cues" (e.g., specific colors, textures, or local alignment) rather than inducing invariant rules or abstract structural patterns (Inductive, Analogical, Geometric/Spatial). This leads to sharp performance degradation under minor variations, out-of-distribution shifts, or novel compositions, often confusing style with fundamental structure (e.g., [Chen et al., 2026], [Małki´nski and Ma´ndziuk, 2025]).
- **Perception-Reasoning Entanglement**: Performance issues are often an intertwined result of limitations in fine-grained visual perception and the subsequent inability to map perceived concepts to abstract variables or infer underlying rules. Perceptual shortcomings can bottleneck downstream reasoning, making it difficult to isolate the true cause of failure (Inductive, Deductive, Geometric/Spatial). When perceptual burdens are reduced, substantial performance drops often reveal models' reliance on "knowledge priors" over direct visual inference (e.g., [Song et al., 2025], [Yuan et al., 2025]).
- **Perceptual Bias**: In tasks like analogical reasoning, LVLMs tend to favor individual visual attributes or local features over identifying relational structures or isomorphisms between elements. This "over-reliance on individual visual inputs" hinders their ability to perform comparative inference and grasp conceptual distinctions (Analogical).
- **Perception-Execution Gap**: Even when task rules are explicitly provided and visual input is seemingly understood, LVLMs frequently fail in executing multi-step procedures, maintaining internal state across sequential actions, or planning over long horizons (Algorithmic). Errors accumulate, and decisions become misaligned from the actual visual state, leading to logical derailment (e.g., [Zhang et al., 2024a], [Tang et al., 2025]). Performance degrades sharply with increasing procedural depth (Algorithmic).
- **Constraint Integration Limitations**: For deductive tasks, LVLMs struggle to jointly propagate multiple constraints, even if individual rules are correctly applied. They often fail to integrate these into a globally consistent solution, resulting in partially valid or internally inconsistent outputs (Deductive). Visual grounding presents a significant bottleneck, as misinterpretations of grid structures or complex visual components cascade into logical failures (e.g., [Seely et al., 2025]).
- **Sensitivity to Superficial Spatial Cues & Error Accumulation**: In geometric and spatial reasoning, models often rely on superficial spatial features (absolute position, local alignment) rather than abstract geometric relations (Geometric/Spatial). Minor inaccuracies in spatial interpretation frequently propagate and accumulate across steps, leading to invariant violations or inconsistent intermediate states, especially in interactive or long-horizon tasks (e.g., [Tang et al., 2025], [Paglieri et al., 2025]).
- **Persistent Gap between Fluent Explanations and Faithful Execution/Comprehension (The "Illusion of Reasoning")**: A crucial and recurring finding across *all* reasoning categories is the discrepancy between the verbalized explanations generated by LVLMs (often fluent and seemingly rational) and their actual, faithful execution or comprehension. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting may improve verbalization but does not guarantee the validity of inferred rules or the accuracy of logical entailment given visual evidence. Models can produce plausible step-by-step rationales that do not correspond to the real actions or actual spatial evidence, masking underlying grounding failures (e.g., [Chen et al., 2026], [Wang et al., 2025b], [Bi et al., 2025]). This "verbalization bottleneck" indicates that linguistic fluency is not a reliable indicator of genuine reasoning fidelity.

In essence, the survey concludes that while LVLMs possess impressive perceptual and linguistic capabilities, they exhibit fundamental weaknesses in abstraction, systematic rule inference, consistent state tracking, and integrating multiple constraints. These shortcomings reveal that their "apparent reasoning competence can be mostly attributed to successful pattern matching rather than faithful inference."

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The findings presented in this survey carry profound significance for the advancement and trustworthy deployment of Artificial Intelligence, particularly in the domain of Large Vision-Language Models. By rigorously diagnosing LVLMs' reasoning capabilities through visual puzzles, the authors shed light on fundamental limitations that demand urgent attention.

**The "Illusion of Reasoning"**:
Perhaps the most critical insight derived from this work is the concept of the "illusion of reasoning." This refers to the observation that LVLMs often generate highly fluent and seemingly rational intermediate explanations, yet these explanations may not accurately correspond to the visual inputs, inferred rules, or executed intermediate states. This discrepancy is particularly pronounced in the tightly constrained environments of visual puzzles, underscoring their diagnostic power. The illusion highlights that linguistic fluency can mask deep cognitive pitfalls, where small perceptual failures (e.g., misidentification, erroneous counting, misperceived relationships) cascade and derail downstream reasoning, even when a "rational" thinking process is verbalized. This calls into question the reliability of current evaluation metrics that primarily rely on final task performance or superficial explanation coherence.

**Implications for Trustworthy AI**:
The survey's findings directly impact the development of trustworthy AI systems. If LVLMs are largely relying on "shortcut-driven behavior" and pattern matching rather than faithful inference, their application in high-stakes domains requiring robust reasoning, interpretability, and verifiable decision-making (e.g., medical diagnosis, autonomous systems, complex problem-solving) becomes highly problematic. The identified gaps between verbalization and execution, and between perceived competence and genuine understanding, pose significant challenges to building truly intelligent and reliable multimodal AI.

**Proposed Future Directions (Roadmap for Reasoning-Aware LVLMs)**:
The authors propose a comprehensive roadmap for future research, outlining critical areas to overcome current limitations:

- **Multi-Agent Collaboration**: Inspired by success in language-only tasks, the paper suggests specialized LVLM agents that can collaborate, debate, and verify hypotheses. This multi-perspective approach could help eliminate reasoning errors, provide richer uncertainty insights, and disentangle failure modes by introducing an "explanation judge" to verify faithfulness against visual inputs and constraints.
- **Revisiting Training Objectives**: The observation that LVLMs degrade sharply with increasing procedural depth points to a weakness in maintaining long reasoning traces. The prevalent next-token prediction objective rewards statistical patterns rather than sustained, state-consistent reasoning. Future training objectives should therefore prioritize "long-horizon training objectives," intermediate state consistency, penalizing violations, and incorporating counterfactual invariance for enhanced robustness.
- **Towards Compositional Generalization**: The over-reliance on superficial visual characteristics (color, texture, style) limits LVLMs' ability to operate in novel, compositionally complex scenarios. The authors advocate for designing future benchmarks with "well-defined compositional probes" (depth, breadth, disentanglement of variables, controlled variations) to explicitly foster abstraction and generalization.
- **Abductive and Lateral Visual Reasoning**: The survey identifies a significant research gap in hypothesis-driven puzzle-solving within the visual domain. Exploring abductive (inference under uncertainty, incomplete information) and lateral reasoning (overriding presuppositions, examining multiple hypotheses) in visual puzzles could push LVLMs towards more advanced cognitive demands, assessing their ability for hypothesis revision and reasoning over ambiguous evidence.

**Overall Importance**:
Ultimately, this survey serves as a crucial call to action for the AI research community. It reframes the debate around LVLM intelligence by providing a systematic framework for diagnostic evaluation. By consistently revealing that LVLMs struggle with core reasoning abilities (abstraction, generalization, state tracking, constraint adherence) despite their linguistic prowess, the paper highlights the urgent need to shift focus from mere performance metrics to developing "more reasoning-aware, trustworthy LVLMs that operate beyond shortcut-driven behavior." The implications are clear: truly intelligent and reliable AI systems will require foundational improvements in how reasoning is evaluated, trained, and understood, moving beyond the current "illusion of reasoning."
