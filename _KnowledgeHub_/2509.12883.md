# Lego-Edit: A General Image Editing Framework with Model-Level Bricks and MLLM Builder

> [!summary] Summary
> Lego-Edit is an instruction-based image editing framework in which a fine-tuned multimodal LLM, the Builder, composes a library of specialized model-level editing tools, the Bricks, into executable workflows. A three-stage curriculum of supervised fine-tuning, then RL with ground-truth rewards, then RL with critic rewards trains the Builder. It reaches state-of-the-art results on GEdit-Bench and ImgEdit-Bench and can use new tools without retraining.

## Problem

- End-to-end editing models learn instruction understanding and pixel mapping jointly, so they stay bound to instruction patterns in their training data and generalize poorly to flexible real-world commands.
- Prompt-driven agents lack deep knowledge of their editing tools, so they organize the tools poorly and depend on curated prompts and code-based invocation that cap performance.
- Agents built on predefined task-level workflows guarantee success on known cases but cannot handle instructions that deviate from those pipelines, which limits operational flexibility.

## Method

- **Builder** and **Executor**: a MiMo-VL-7B Builder reasons about the instruction and outputs a ==JSON tool-invocation graph==, and an Executor parses and runs it, with the number of tools chosen adaptively.
- **Bricks** ==model-level toolkit==: ==predictive tools== (RES, SOS, ADD-PRED, CAP-PRED, INVERSE) supply masks and captions, and ==editing tools== (FILL, INPAINT, POSE, ENV, STYLE, RCM) are separate ==single-task LoRA adapters== on FLUX Fill.
- ==Three-stage progressive training==: SFT on 500 samples, then ==GRPO== with valid and ==workflow-graph similarity rewards== on 20K samples, then GRPO with a Qwen2.5-VL-72B ==critic effectiveness reward== on 50K unlabeled instructions.

## Results

- Lego-Edit reaches an overall score of **6.64** on GEdit-Bench-EN, the best of all compared methods, with the top perceptual quality score of **7.45**. It scores **3.50** overall on ImgEdit-Bench and leads the Hybrid sub-task at **3.18**.
- Complex-instruction pass rate rises from **55.9%** for SFT to **83.6%** after ground-truth RL and **99.0%** after ground-truth-free RL, beating GPT-4o at **53.8%**. Overall score climbs from **5.89** to **6.64**.
- Single-task LoRAs beat a multi-task LoRA (color alteration **6.83** vs **5.94**). Composing RES with RCM lifts color alteration from **6.83** to **7.70**, and pipeline latency is about **7.2 s** against over **25 s** for Bagel.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Small specialized tools composed by an MLLM agent can beat monolithic generators on flexible instructions, since single-task adapters avoid the task confusion of joint training and need little data.
> - Ground-truth-free RL with a critic on unlabeled instructions extends a tool-using agent beyond its supervised tasks. It lifted complex-instruction success from 83.6% to 99.0%.
> - Tool-level modularity gives adaptability: the agent revises workflows from user feedback and adopts newly added tools without retraining, which end-to-end models and fixed pipelines cannot do.

## Detailed Report

### 1. Technical Report: LEGO-EDIT – A GENERAL IMAGE EDITING FRAMEWORK WITH MODEL-LEVEL BRICKS AND MLLM BUILDER

This report provides a detailed analysis of the technical paper "LEGO-EDIT: A GENERAL IMAGE EDITING FRAMEWORK WITH MODEL-LEVEL BRICKS AND MLLM BUILDER," covering its authorship, contextual placement within the research landscape, objectives, methodology, key findings, and overall significance.

#### 1.1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of instruction-based image editing has rapidly advanced, driven by the desire to enable users to modify images using natural language commands rather than complex manual tools. This paradigm offers intuitive human-computer interaction and holds immense potential across various applications. Lego-Edit situates itself squarely within this dynamic landscape, addressing critical limitations of existing approaches.

Broadly, prior instruction-based image editing methods fall into two main categories:

**a) End-to-End Generative Models:** This category involves training a single, large generative model (often diffusion-based) to implicitly learn both instruction comprehension and pixel-level image manipulation. Prominent examples include Instruct-Pix2Pix, FLUX, HiDream, Step1X-Edit, ILLUME++, GPT-4o, Bagel, and UniWorld. While these models have demonstrated impressive capabilities and strong performance on various editing tasks, especially when trained on massive datasets, they face significant challenges in generalization. They are often constrained by the fixed instruction patterns and data distributions encountered during training, leading to difficulties in handling the immense diversity and open-domain nature of real-world user instructions. Furthermore, these monolithic models can suffer from "task confusion," where jointly trained capabilities interfere with each other, leading to suboptimal results for specific, fine-grained edits.

**b) Agent-Based Schemes utilizing MLLMs:** More recently, approaches have emerged that leverage Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) as intelligent agents to explicitly interpret editing instructions and orchestrate a set of discrete editing tools. Early attempts, such as ComfyAgent, often relied on carefully curated prompts to guide the agents. However, these "prompt-driven" methods lacked a deep, intrinsic understanding of the capabilities and interdependencies of the underlying editing tools, limiting the agent's ability to effectively compose them. Subsequent advancements, exemplified by ComfyMind, sought to alleviate this by constructing complex, predefined workflows as "task-level" tools for agents to invoke. While this improved success rates for known tasks, it inherently restricted the framework's flexibility, preventing it from handling instructions that deviate from these pre-defined workflows or novel editing scenarios. These methods struggled with the "generalization" problem to varying degrees.

**Lego-Edit's Contribution and Position:** Lego-Edit innovatively positions itself as an **agent-based framework** that directly tackles the generalization limitations of both end-to-end models and prior agent-based methods. It moves beyond "task-level" tools to **"model-level bricks"**, providing a finer granularity of control. Crucially, it integrates a sophisticated **three-stage progressive reinforcement learning (RL) strategy** to train its MLLM agent (the "Builder") to achieve truly generalized reasoning and tool composition capabilities. Unlike large end-to-end models that require massive, often custom, datasets, Lego-Edit aims to achieve superior instruction generalization with *minimal training data* by effectively orchestrating specialized, smaller models. It builds on the growing trend of leveraging MLLMs as versatile agents in multi-modal contexts, drawing parallels with work like VisualToolAgent and SeeAct, but extends this by focusing on flexible tool composition and open-domain instruction handling in image editing. This makes Lego-Edit a significant step towards creating more adaptable, robust, and extensible AI systems for creative tasks.

#### 1.2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary motivation behind Lego-Edit stems from a critical challenge in instruction-based image editing: the **inability of existing methods to generalize effectively to the immense diversity and open-domain nature of real-world user instructions**. This limitation significantly restricts their practical applicability, as users often issue commands that are flexible, multi-step, or describe novel editing scenarios not explicitly covered in training data.

The paper identifies specific shortcomings in current approaches:
- **End-to-end models** are fundamentally constrained by the fixed patterns and scope of their training data, leading to a "failure to generalize" when faced with instructions outside this domain. They implicitly learn a mapping, making explicit reasoning and novel task decomposition difficult.
- **Prior agent-based schemes**, while offering more explicit interpretation, either lack a deep understanding of tool capabilities (prompt-driven) or are restricted by predefined "task-level" workflows, making them inflexible and unable to handle instructions that require novel combinations of operations.

To address these limitations, Lego-Edit sets forth the following key objectives:

1. **Develop a General Image Editing Framework:** The overarching goal is to create a robust and versatile framework capable of handling a wide spectrum of instruction-based image editing tasks.
2. **Leverage MLLM Generalization for Tool Orchestration:** To harness the powerful reasoning and comprehension capabilities of MLLMs, the framework aims to use a fine-tuned MLLM as an intelligent agent (the "Builder") to organize and compose a suite of specialized tools.
3. **Enable Fine-Grained Composition of Editing Actions:** By introducing "model-level bricks" (specialized, atomic editing models), the framework seeks to provide the Builder with granular control, allowing for the flexible, step-by-step composition of complex editing workflows. This contrasts with coarser "task-level" tools.
4. **Achieve Generalized Reasoning for Real-World Instructions:** The core objective is to equip the Builder with the ability to reason about and execute instructions even when they are ambiguous, novel, or require sophisticated multi-step planning, going beyond predefined scripts. This involves training the MLLM to understand tool affordances and dependencies.
5. **Facilitate Integration of Novel Tools Without Retraining:** A practical objective is to design a modular architecture where new editing capabilities (new "bricks") can be seamlessly added to the toolkit, and the Builder can learn to utilize them without requiring extensive retraining of the entire MLLM agent.
6. **Attain State-of-the-Art Performance:** Empirically demonstrate that the proposed framework not only generalizes better but also achieves superior or competitive performance on established image editing benchmarks compared to existing SOTA methods.

By achieving these objectives, Lego-Edit aims to significantly enhance the practicality and versatility of instruction-based image editing systems, making them more adaptable to diverse user needs and evolving editing capabilities.

#### 1.3. Methodology and Approach

Lego-Edit proposes a novel agent-based framework for general instruction-based image editing, structured around three core components: a reasoning MLLM agent called the "Builder," a suite of "model-level tools" termed "Bricks," and an "Executor" to process workflows. The methodology is characterized by two key innovations: the design of its toolkit and its progressive reinforcement learning training strategy.

##### 1.3.1. Overall Framework

The system operates as follows: Given an input image `I` and a natural language editing instruction `q`, the **Builder** (an MLLM) analyzes the state `s = (I, q)` and generates a reasoning trace (`Think`) followed by a JSON-formatted workflow `g`. This workflow `g` is essentially a tool invocation graph, specifying a sequence of tool instances (`Bricks`) and their dependencies. An **Executor** then parses `g`, invokes the specified `Bricks` with appropriate parameters, and produces the final edited image `I'`.

##### 1.3.2. Prompt Structure for the Builder

The Builder receives a carefully constructed prompt, comprising three main elements:
- **System Description and Invocation Constraints:** Defines the Builder's role, capabilities, task scope, and valid parameter types for tool invocation.
- **Available Tool List:** Detailed specifications for each "Brick," including its name, functional description, and specific input/output constraints.
- **Workflow Composition Examples:** Few-shot examples demonstrating how valid workflows should be structured, guiding the Builder in generating coherent and executable plans.
After these components, the actual user instruction is presented, prompting the Builder to generate the editing workflow.

##### 1.3.3. Model-Level Editing Tools (Bricks)

A cornerstone of Lego-Edit is its fine-grained library of "model-level" tools, or Bricks. Each Brick represents a single model or atomic function, providing the Builder with maximum flexibility for composition. These tools are categorized based on whether they modify the image pixels:

- **Predictive Models (`M_p`):** These tools extract or process masks and regions, providing spatial constraints or information without altering the image content itself. Examples include:
 - **RES (Segment Specified Objects):** Segments objects based on a text prompt.
 - **SOS (Subject Object Segmentation):** Segments the most salient object.
 - **ADD-PRED (Predict Addition Location):** Predicts optimal positions for adding new objects.
 - **CAP-PRED (Image Captioning/Expansion Prediction):** Generates image captions or predicts regions for image expansion.
 - **INVERSE:** Performs mask subtraction (e.g., inverting a mask).
 - **BBOX:** Computes a bounding box mask from an input mask.
- **Editing Models (`M_e`):** These tools directly modify the image content. To prevent task confusion, Lego-Edit trains independent LoRA adapters for each editing model, leveraging a shared backbone (FLUX-1 Fill). Examples include:
 - **FILL:** Adds objects into a masked region based on a prompt or reference image.
 - **FASTINPAINT & INPAINT:** Tools for image inpainting/object removal, with `INPAINT` offering higher quality but requiring a score threshold.
 - **RCM (Replace Color/Material):** Changes the color or material of an object within a mask.
 - **STYLE:** Performs style transfer on the entire image or a masked region.
 - **ENV (Environment Alteration):** Modifies the environment (e.g., weather, time of day).
 - **POSE:** Changes the posture or expression of an object.
 - **COMPOSE:** Combines two masks or images.
 - **RESIZE:** Resizes a mask or image region.

The Builder invokes these tools by specifying their name, input parameters (e.g., image, mask, prompt), and output parameters.

##### 1.3.4. Three-Stage Progressive Reinforcement Learning Strategy

To train the Builder (`f(θ)`) for effective tool composition and generalization, Lego-Edit employs a progressive three-stage reinforcement learning strategy, gradually increasing task complexity and reducing reliance on ground truth annotations. The GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) algorithm is used for RL stages.

1. **Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT):**
 - **Objective:** Adapt the Builder to the image editing domain and establish foundational reasoning.
 - **Data:** A limited dataset of (image `I`, instruction `q`) pairs, each meticulously annotated with expert-generated reasoning traces (`Think_GT`) and ground truth workflows (`g_GT`).
 - **Method:** The Builder is trained to minimize the negative log-likelihood of generating `[Think_GT, g_GT]`, essentially learning to mimic expert behavior.

2. **Stage 2: Refinement with GT-Based Rewards:**
 - **Objective:** Refine the Builder's tool usage knowledge and reasoning capabilities on specific editing tasks, moving towards generating correct workflows without explicit `Think_GT`.
 - **Data:** (Image `I`, instruction `q`) pairs with only `g_GT` available.
 - **Rewards (`r_stage2`):**
 - **Valid Reward (`R_valid`):** Punishes workflows that the Executor fails to execute (-1 for failure, 0 for success), ensuring syntactically and semantically correct tool invocations.
 - **Similarity Reward (`R_sim`):** Measures the structural and semantic alignment between the Builder's generated workflow `g` and the ground truth `g_GT`. This is computed using hierarchical graph matching, considering node (tool) coverage and similarity of matched nodes (based on model type and parameter identity).
 - **Method:** GRPO is used to maximize `r_stage2 = R_valid + R_sim`.

3. **Stage 3: Generalization with GT-Free Critic Rewards:**
 - **Objective:** Enhance the Builder's generalization to flexible, open-domain instructions without relying on ground truth workflows.
 - **Data:** Abundant unlabeled (image `I`, instruction `q`) pairs beyond specific tasks.
 - **Rewards (`r_stage3`):**
 - **Valid Reward (`R_valid`):** Same as Stage 2.
 - **Effectiveness Reward (`R_effect`):** A sophisticated reward provided by a **large-scale MLLM critic model**. The critic assesses the *semantic alignment* between the *effect* of the Builder's generated workflow and the original instruction `q`. The critic decomposes the workflow into "editing chains" (sequences culminating in an `M_e` tool), abstracts each chain's effect, and then evaluates whether these meta-edits collectively fulfill the instruction. It determines how many chains to remove (`N_remove`) or add (`N_add`) to better match the instruction, applying a penalty `R_effect = 1 - 0.5 * (N_add + N_remove)`.
 - **Method:** GRPO is used to maximize `r_stage3 = R_valid + R_effect`. This stage is crucial for equipping the Builder with generalized reasoning beyond curated datasets.

##### 1.3.5. Implementation Details

The Builder is based on **MiMo-VL-7B** and undergoes full-parameter fine-tuning. The critic model in Stage 3 is based on **Qwen2.5-VL-72B**. Predictive tools like RES use **EVF-SAM** and SOS uses **U2Net**. Editing tools leverage the **FLUX-1 Fill** backbone with specialized LoRA adapters. Training is performed on NVIDIA H20 GPUs with DeepSpeed ZeRO-3.

#### 1.4. Main Findings and Results

Lego-Edit demonstrates significant advancements in instruction-based image editing, substantiated by comprehensive experiments and ablations.

##### 1.4.1. State-of-the-Art Performance on Benchmarks

- **GEdit-Bench:** Lego-Edit achieves state-of-the-art performance on the GEdit-Bench-EN dataset. It obtains the highest overall VIEScore (G O = 6.64) and the best image preservation score (G PQ = 7.7). Notably, it excels in fine-grained sub-tasks such as color change and material replacement, outperforming monolithic end-to-end models (like BAGEL, Step1X-Edit, UniWorld-V1, OmniGen2) and even prior agent-based approaches (ComfyMind). This precision is attributed to the Builder's ability to utilize predictive tools like RES for targeted modifications.
- **ImgEdit-Bench:** The framework also maintains top performance on ImgEdit-Bench, achieving the highest overall score (3.50) among all compared methods. Crucially, Lego-Edit particularly dominates the challenging "Hybrid Editing" sub-task (scoring 3.18), validating its capability to parse composite instructions into atomic sub-tasks and dynamically generate appropriate workflows.

##### 1.4.2. Robust Reasoning and Generalization

- **Zero-Shot Complex Edits with Flexible Tool Composition:** Lego-Edit demonstrates remarkable zero-shot generalization. For complex instructions not explicitly trained for (e.g., "swap the positions of the cat and the dog"), the Builder effectively decomposes the instruction into atomic operations (segmentation, inpainting, object addition) and orchestrates the Bricks to achieve the desired outcome. This highlights its capability for flexible tool composition, which is beyond the reach of end-to-end or manually curated-pipeline models.
- **Zero-Shot Adaptation to Feedback and New Tools:** The framework exhibits exceptional adaptability. In tasks like reflection removal, if an initial workflow fails, users can provide natural language feedback (e.g., "don't use RES before INPAINT"). The Builder intelligently revises its workflow based on this feedback, demonstrating an understanding of tool limitations and alternative strategies. Furthermore, users can introduce *entirely new, dedicated editing tools* (e.g., an RRF model for reflection removal), and the Builder can seamlessly incorporate and utilize them without requiring any retraining of the MLLM agent itself. This modularity is a critical feature for long-term extensibility.

##### 1.4.3. Ablation Studies on Tools

- **Necessity of Task-Specialized Tools:** A comparison between a multi-task LoRA adapter and single-task LoRA adapters for editing models unequivocally validates the benefit of specialization. Single-task LoRAs consistently outperform unified models (e.g., 6.83 vs. 5.94 for color alteration), even when the unified model uses a higher rank. Qualitative results show that joint training leads to frequent "task confusion," confirming that specialized models, coordinated by the Builder, achieve higher fidelity and prevent interference.
- **Effectiveness of Tool Composition:** Explicit tool composition by the Builder significantly boosts performance. For instance, combining RES (segmentation) with RCM (color/material change) leads to higher G O scores than using RCM alone, as RES provides precise spatial control. Similarly, integrating CAP-PRED (captioning) with STYLE (style transfer) improves results by providing semantic textual cues that enforce alignment between stylized outputs and the source image.

##### 1.4.4. Ablation Studies on Builder Training Strategy

- **Effectiveness of Reinforcement Learning Training:** The progressive three-stage RL strategy is shown to be highly effective.
 - **Builder-SFT** (Stage 1) already outperforms the base MiMo-VL-7B model and even powerful GPT-4o in success rates and VIEScores.
 - **Builder-RL w/ GT** (Stage 2) further boosts simple instruction success to 100% and complex instruction success to 83.6%.
 - The final **Builder-RL w/o GT** (Stage 3, using GT-free critic rewards) maintains 100% simple success, critically improving complex success to 99.0% and achieving the highest VIEScores. This improvement on complex tasks, despite already achieving high simple accuracy, indicates that the GT-free RL significantly enhances the Builder's compositional understanding, semantic alignment, and reliability under flexible, open-domain instructions.

##### 1.4.5. Efficiency

Lego-Edit demonstrates superior efficiency compared to end-to-end methods. On a single H20 GPU, the total pipeline latency for Lego-Edit is approximately 7.2 seconds, significantly faster than the >25 seconds reported for an end-to-end method like Bagel under identical settings. This is due to the modular nature of tools and the MLLM's efficient workflow generation.

In summary, Lego-Edit's findings validate its core hypothesis: a fine-tuned MLLM agent intelligently orchestrating a toolkit of specialized, model-level components, trained with a progressive RL strategy including GT-free feedback, can overcome the generalization limitations of previous image editing systems, achieving SOTA performance, robust reasoning, and remarkable adaptability.

#### 1.5. Significance and Potential Impact

Lego-Edit represents a substantial advancement in the field of instruction-based image editing, with profound significance and wide-ranging potential impact:

1. **Breaking Generalization Barriers:** The most significant contribution of Lego-Edit is its successful approach to overcoming the critical limitation of current image editing systems: their inability to generalize to diverse, flexible, and open-domain real-world user instructions. By decoupling instruction interpretation from pixel generation and enabling dynamic tool orchestration, it offers a blueprint for building truly versatile and robust AI editing tools.
2. **Enhanced User Experience and Accessibility:** By allowing users to express complex editing desires through natural language, Lego-Edit makes advanced image manipulation more intuitive and accessible to a broader audience, regardless of their technical proficiency in graphic design software. This reduces the cognitive load associated with manual prompt engineering or navigating complex menus.
3. **Foundation for Future AI Agents:** Lego-Edit provides a powerful conceptual and practical framework for developing more intelligent and autonomous AI agents. It demonstrates how an MLLM, when properly trained with reinforcement learning and equipped with fine-grained tools, can act as a sophisticated planner and orchestrator in complex, multi-modal environments. This paradigm extends beyond image editing to other domains requiring task decomposition and tool utilization.
4. **Modular and Extensible System Design:** The architecture with "model-level bricks" and a flexible "Builder" agent promotes modularity. New editing capabilities can be developed as independent tools and seamlessly integrated into the framework without requiring a complete redesign or retraining of the core MLLM agent. This extensibility ensures the system can adapt to evolving research, new user demands, and technological advancements, future-proofing its utility.
5. **Efficiency and Resource Optimization:** By leveraging a suite of specialized, smaller models rather than a single monolithic model for all tasks, Lego-Edit achieves faster inference times compared to large end-to-end generative models. This has practical implications for deployment on consumer devices or in cloud environments where latency and computational resources are critical.
6. **Practical Industry Applications:** For a company like Xiaomi, this research has direct and immediate implications for enhancing its product ecosystem. Advanced image editing features powered by Lego-Edit could be integrated into smartphone camera apps, photo editing software, and even IoT devices, offering users unprecedented creative control and intelligent assistance. This elevates Xiaomi's AI capabilities in consumer-facing applications.
7. **Advancement in MLLM Research:** The progressive three-stage RL training strategy, particularly the use of GT-free critic rewards in the final stage, represents a significant methodological contribution to MLLM research. It demonstrates an effective way to instill generalized reasoning and tool composition abilities in MLLMs using unannotated data, paving the way for more autonomous and intelligent agents in various domains.

In essence, Lego-Edit moves beyond merely performing individual editing tasks to understanding the *intent* behind diverse instructions and intelligently composing atomic actions. This shift represents a crucial step towards truly general-purpose creative AI assistants.
