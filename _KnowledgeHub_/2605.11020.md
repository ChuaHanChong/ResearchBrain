# Trust Region Inverse Reinforcement Learning: Explicit Dual Ascent using Local Policy Updates

> [!summary] Summary
> TRIRL (Trust Region Inverse Reinforcement Learning) introduces a non-adversarial inverse reinforcement learning algorithm that achieves monotonic performance improvement and stable learning for complex continuous control tasks. It recovers global and transferable reward functions by enabling explicit dual ascent through local policy updates and a novel reward correction mechanism.

## Problem

- Classical Maximum Causal Entropy IRL (MCE-IRL) provides strong theoretical guarantees and monotonic performance improvement but is computationally prohibitive for high-dimensional problems, requiring a full reinforcement learning solution at each iteration.
- Adversarial Imitation Learning methods (e.g., GAIL) are computationally efficient but often suffer from training instability, noise, suboptimal local rewards, and a lack of monotonic dual improvement.
- Existing IRL/IL methods frequently struggle to learn global, transferable reward functions that are disentangled from the specific policy or environment dynamics.

## Method

- **TRIRL** formulates an ==explicit dual ascent== procedure on the ==MCE-IRL objective==, directly optimizing the reward function in function space rather than through an adversarial game.
- It utilizes ==local policy updates== within a ==reverse KL trust region==, significantly reducing the computational cost compared to needing full reinforcement learning optimization at each step.
- A novel ==reward correction mechanism==, derived from theoretical insights, adjusts the learned reward based on the trust-region policy update's ==Lagrangian multiplier==, thereby maintaining ==monotonic improvement guarantees== with local updates.

## Results

- TRIRL consistently matches or outperforms state-of-the-art imitation learning baselines (e.g., GAIL, AIRL, LSIQ) across various Mujoco and complex robotics tasks, achieving an aggregate inter-quartile mean performance factor of **2.4x** compared to prior works.
- The method successfully learns global reward functions that are transferable to environments with altered dynamics (e.g., altered gravity, disabled joints), outperforming AIRL and NEAR in retraining and transferability tests.
- TRIRL demonstrates enhanced training stability and robustness, showing approximately monotonic performance improvement across seeds and insensitivity to key hyperparameters, in contrast to the often unstable behavior of adversarial methods.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A trust-region-optimal maximum causal entropy policy for a given reward function is equivalent to an MCE-optimal policy for a *transformed* and *corrected* reward function.
> - Explicitly optimizing the MCE-IRL dual objective (learning the reward function) can be achieved with computationally cheaper local policy updates if the reward update step size is appropriately adjusted based on the trust-region constraint's Lagrangian multiplier.
> - Maintaining a fixed-size circular buffer of past discriminators allows for approximating recursive functional reward updates efficiently, balancing accuracy and computational memory overhead.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) is a framework designed to infer an unknown reward function from expert demonstrations. By learning the underlying intentions of an expert, IRL methods aim to recover robust policies that can transfer behavior to novel environments. This contrasts with Imitation Learning (IL), which focuses on directly learning a policy to match demonstrations within a given environment, often without explicitly extracting a reward function.

The existing landscape of IRL and IL methods presents a trade-off between theoretical guarantees and practical scalability. Classical IRL formulations, such as Maximum Causal Entropy IRL (MCE-IRL), typically maximize entropy subject to matching expert trajectory distributions. These methods offer monotonic performance improvement but are computationally intensive, requiring a full Reinforcement Learning (RL) problem to be solved at each iteration to compute dual gradients. This computational burden renders them prohibitive for many complex, high-dimensional applications.

More recent advancements have largely come from adversarial methods, exemplified by Generative Adversarial Imitation Learning (GAIL) and its variants (e.g., AIRL, AMP). These approaches recast the dual-ascent procedure as a two-player min-max game, where a discriminator provides local rewards based on how well an RL policy imitates the expert. While adversarial methods circumvent the cost of fully solving an RL problem in each iteration, they often suffer from training instability, noise, and a lack of monotonic dual improvement. The discriminator rewards are local signals, and policy updates are typically sub-optimal (only a few gradient steps are taken), contributing to practical challenges in tuning and inconsistent performance.

A third category includes non-adversarial IL methods (e.g., IQ-Learn, LSIQ, NEAR, SFM) which aim to address some of the issues with adversarial training, often by focusing on distribution matching or value-function based approaches. However, some of these still rely on off-policy RL techniques like Soft Actor-Critic (SAC), which can face scaling challenges in highly parallelized environments, or they may struggle with disentangling the reward function from the policy or environment dynamics.

This work, Trust Region Inverse Reinforcement Learning (TRIRL), positions itself as a bridge between classical MCE-IRL and modern adversarial/non-adversarial IL methods. It seeks to combine the monotonic improvement guarantees and principled reward learning of classical IRL with the computational efficiency of local policy updates, while explicitly avoiding the instabilities inherent in adversarial formulations. The objective is to achieve stable, monotonic learning of a reward function that can be globally optimized and is transferable to new environments, without the need for computationally expensive full RL solutions at every step.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a novel, non-adversarial Inverse Reinforcement Learning algorithm that ensures monotonic performance improvement and stable learning while remaining computationally efficient for complex, continuous control tasks. The authors aim to recover informative reward functions and effective policies in a scalable and principled manner, addressing key limitations of existing approaches.

The motivation for TRIRL stems from a critical analysis of the two dominant paradigms in IRL/IL:
1. **Limitations of Classical MCE-IRL:** MCE-IRL provides strong theoretical guarantees, including monotonic improvement towards an optimal policy and reward. However, its iterative saddle-point optimization requires solving a full maximum entropy RL problem to convergence at each step. This process is computationally prohibitive for deep reinforcement learning in high-dimensional state-action spaces, making it impractical for many real-world applications.
2. **Instabilities of Adversarial Imitation Learning:** Adversarial methods like GAIL significantly reduce computational cost by replacing the global MCE-optimal policy update with local policy optimization driven by a discriminator's reward signal. While sharing the same saddle point in theory, this practical approximation leads to several issues:
 - **Local and Suboptimal Rewards:** The discriminator provides only a local correction signal, not a globally valid reward function, making the learned policies suboptimal in a global sense.
 - **Training Instability:** The min-max game nature of adversarial training is inherently unstable and noisy, making these methods challenging to tune and highly task-dependent in practice. They often lack monotonic performance improvement.
 - **Reward Entanglement:** Some adversarial methods (e.g., AIRL) claim to recover robust rewards, but the reward function can still be "entangled" with the log-density of the learned policy, limiting its transferability to environments with changed dynamics.

TRIRL is motivated by the insight that a trust-region-optimal policy for a specific reward function update can be globally optimal for a *smaller update in the same direction*. This theoretical finding suggests that it is possible to explicitly optimize the dual objective (learning the reward function) while relying only on a local search around the current policy (which is computationally cheaper than a full RL solve).

Therefore, the key motivations include:
- **Achieving Monotonic Improvement:** To ensure stable and consistent learning, similar to classical MCE-IRL, but without its computational overhead.
- **Avoiding Adversarial Instability:** To develop a non-adversarial approach that bypasses the noisy and unstable training dynamics characteristic of GAIL-like methods.
- **Learning Global and Transferable Reward Functions:** To recover reward functions that are disentangled from the policy and environment dynamics, enabling them to be re-optimized from scratch and transferred to new environments with modified dynamics.
- **Scalability:** To propose an algorithm that is scalable to complex, high-dimensional continuous control tasks, allowing for efficient training in real-world or high-fidelity simulated robotics scenarios.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

TRIRL is founded on the theoretical framework of Maximum Causal Entropy Inverse Reinforcement Learning (MCE-IRL) and reverse KL-divergence based distribution matching. The core methodological innovation is to enable explicit dual ascent on the MCE-IRL objective using only local policy updates, rather than requiring full RL optimization at each step.

**Theoretical Foundation:**
The problem is formulated as maximizing entropy subject to matching the expert's occupancy measure, which can be interpreted as minimizing the reverse KL-divergence between the agent's and expert's occupancies, regularized by entropy. This optimization objective yields a dual problem where the reward function is a dual variable. The reward update in function space is given by:
`r^(i+1) = U_rho^(i)_epsilon (r^(i)) = r^(i) - epsilon * (r^(i) - beta * log(rho_E(s,a) / rho_pi^(i)(s,a)))`
where `rho_E` is the expert occupancy, `rho_pi^(i)` is the agent's occupancy for the policy `pi^(i)` (optimal for `r^(i)`), `beta` is an entropy regularization parameter, and `epsilon` is a step size. This update rule, previously shown by Arenz et al. (2016), guarantees monotonic improvement of the dual objective.

**Bridging the Gap: Local Policy Updates with Reward Correction:**
The main challenge for applying the above update in real-world settings is that `pi^(i)` (the optimal maximum entropy policy for `r^(i)`) is typically found by running RL to convergence, which is computationally expensive. TRIRL addresses this with a novel mechanism:
1. **Trust Region Policy Optimization:** Instead of fully optimizing `pi^(i)` for `r^(i)`, TRIRL optimizes the policy within a reverse KL trust region around the previous policy `pi_tr^(i)`. This is formulated as:
 `pi_tr^(i+1) = arg max_pi E_rho_pi[H(pi)] + E_rho_pi[E_pi[r^(i)]] s.t. E_rho_pi[KL(pi || pi_tr^(i))] <= zeta`
 where `zeta` is the trust region bound. Finding a trust-region optimal policy is significantly cheaper than a full RL solve.
2. **Theoretical Insight (Lemma 3.1 & Theorem 3.2):**
 - **Lemma 3.1** demonstrates that a trust-region optimal maximum causal entropy policy for a reward function `r` corresponds to an optimal maximum causal entropy policy for a *transformed* reward function `r_eta = (1/(1+eta)) * r + (eta/(1+eta)) * log(pi^(i))`, where `eta` is the Lagrangian multiplier associated with the trust-region constraint.
 - **Theorem 3.2** extends this, proving that if `pi_tr^(i+1)` is a trust-region optimal policy for an *intermediate* reward `~r^(i+1) = U_rho^(i)_epsilon(r^(i))` (with step size `epsilon`), then `pi_tr^(i+1)` is also the MCE-optimal policy for a *corrected* reward `r^(i+1) = U_rho^(i)_epsilon_tr(r^(i))`, where `epsilon_tr = epsilon / (1+eta)` is a smaller step size.
 This theorem is central to TRIRL. It implies that a local policy update, constrained by a trust region, can be treated as a globally optimal policy for an appropriately *corrected* reward function. This correction ensures that the new policy-reward pair still moves closer to the saddle point of the MCE-IRL Lagrangian, maintaining monotonic improvement guarantees.

**Algorithm 1 (Trust Region Inverse RL):**
The TRIRL algorithm iteratively performs the following steps:
1. **Policy Rollout:** Generate agent trajectories using the current policy `pi^(i)`.
2. **Density Ratio Estimation:** Learn a discriminator `D^(i)` to approximate `log(rho_E / rho_pi^(i))`.
3. **Intermediate Reward Update:** Compute an intermediate reward `~r^(i+1)` using the functional update rule with step `epsilon`.
4. **Trust Region Policy Update:** Optimize the policy `pi^(i+1)_tr` within a trust region around the previous policy `pi^(i)` with respect to `~r^(i+1)`. This step also yields the Lagrangian multiplier `eta^(i+1)`.
5. **Reward Correction:** Calculate the corrected step size `epsilon_tr = epsilon / (1 + eta^(i+1))`. Use this `epsilon_tr` to compute the *final corrected reward function* `r^(i+1)` from `r^(i)` and `D^(i)`. This `r^(i+1)` is the reward for which `pi^(i+1)_tr` is MCE-optimal.
6. **Update:** Set `r^(i)` to `r^(i+1)` and `pi^(i)` to `pi^(i+1)_tr`, then repeat.

**Practical Considerations:**
1. **Density Ratio Estimation:** In continuous spaces, `log(rho_E / rho_pi)` is approximated using a neural network binary classifier `D`. Unlike adversarial methods, TRIRL benefits from an accurate discriminator, as it uses the logits for a function-space reward update without adversarial training instabilities.
2. **Circular Buffer of Discriminators:** To manage the computational cost and memory of recursive functional updates spanning the entire history of discriminators, TRIRL maintains a fixed-size buffer of the `k` most recent discriminators. A parametric reward function `R_fit` is fitted at iteration `i-k` to approximate older contributions, exploiting the exponential decay of past coefficients.
3. **Trust-Region Optimal Policy Updates:**
 - **TR loss variant:** `eta` is treated as a hyperparameter that weights an auxiliary KL divergence loss `L_tr` during policy optimization using PPO.
 - **Max η variant:** Utilizes Differentiable Trust-Region Projection Layers (TRPL) (Otto et al., 2021) for Gaussian policies. TRPL enforces strict analytical reverse KL trust regions by projecting policy predictions back into the valid region. It calculates state-dependent Lagrangian multipliers `eta_mu` (for mean) and `eta_Sigma` (for covariance), with `eta` taken as the maximum across these. This variant provides strict trust-region satisfaction and non-arbitrary multipliers.
 - **Retrospective η variant:** An extension of the max η variant that uses state-dependent step sizes, potentially adapting them in hindsight based on past policies stored in the buffer.
4. **Learning from Observations & Transfer Learning:** TRIRL's general framework allows various discriminator architectures, including those for state-only observations `D(s, s')`. The method aims to learn disentangled reward functions that can be globally optimized and are robust to shifts in system dynamics, enabling policy re-optimization in new environments.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The research paper presents empirical evaluations across various continuous control tasks, including Mujoco benchmarks (Half-Cheetah, Ant, Walker, Hopper, Humanoid) and complex robotics tasks (Unitree G1 Walking/Running, Unitree Go2 Locomotion). TRIRL is compared against several state-of-the-art imitation learning methods: GAIL, AIRL, AMP, LSIQ, NEAR, and SFM.

#### 4.1. Performance on Imitation Learning Tasks

- **Overall Outperformance:** TRIRL, particularly its "max η" variant, consistently matches or outperforms all baseline methods across the majority of environments (Figure 4, Table 2). For instance, it achieves an aggregate inter-quartile mean performance factor of 2.4x compared to prior works (as stated in the abstract).
- **TR Loss Variant:** The "TR loss" version of TRIRL, which is simpler in its trust-region enforcement, also frequently surpasses most baselines but exhibits diminished performance on more challenging robotics tasks like the G1 humanoid, where stricter trust region control appears more critical.
- **Robotics Tasks:** TRIRL demonstrates strong performance on complex robotics tasks (Go2, G1 Walking/Running), including those utilizing motion-capture data and state-only observation discriminators.

#### 4.2. Ablation Studies

A series of ablation studies investigate the contribution of TRIRL's key components (Figure 5).
- **TRPL and "max η" variant:** The "max η" variant, leveraging TRPL for strict trust region enforcement, generally shows the highest performance among TRIRL's configurations. This suggests that precise control over policy updates and the derived Lagrangian multipliers is beneficial.
- **Retrospective η:** Retrospectively computing state-dependent Lagrangian multipliers does not lead to significant performance improvements over the "max η" variant, implying that a global maximum over a batch of `eta` values is often sufficiently conservative.
- **Reward Fitting:** Fitting the reward function onto a parametric model (as part of the discriminator buffer mechanism) has a minor impact on imitation learning performance.
- **Contribution of Core Components:** Ablations removing the discriminator buffer, trust region projection layer, or reward correction (configurations "w/o disc. buffer", "w/o TRPL & disc. buffer", "GAIL w/ TRPL") show significant performance degradation. This indicates that TRIRL's improvements are not solely due to individual components like TRPL or the buffer, but rather the explicit optimization of the dual objective combined with these elements.
- **Hyperparameter Sensitivity:** TRIRL is found to be generally insensitive to the entropy regularization coefficient `beta` (Table 5) and the reward update step size `epsilon` (Table 6) within a reasonable range, implying robustness.
- **Data Scaling:** TRIRL exhibits comparable performance to other methods across varying amounts of expert demonstration trajectories (Figure 10), with NEAR being an exception due to its higher data requirements.
- **Buffer Size Impact:** While a larger discriminator buffer (k) generally improves stability and performance, TRIRL maintains strong performance even with small buffer sizes, outperforming baselines and demonstrating minimal computational overhead for low `k` values (Figure 11, Table 4).

#### 4.3. Global and Transferable Rewards

- **Global Reward Learning:** TRIRL successfully learns global reward functions. This is demonstrated by freezing a trained reward network and re-optimizing a policy from scratch with PPO using new random agent initializations, where TRIRL ensures reliable convergence (Figure 2, Table 1).
- **Transferability:** The learned reward functions exhibit transferability to environments with altered dynamics (e.g., Point Maze Flipped, Ant Disabled, Half Cheetah Windy/Mars Gravity). TRIRL consistently outperforms AIRL and NEAR in these retraining and transfer settings, indicating that it learns more generalized reward signals of the expert's intrinsic motivations rather than merely specific state transitions.

#### 4.4. Training Stability, Runtime, and Memory

- **Stability:** TRIRL demonstrates enhanced training stability and consistency. Performance improves approximately monotonically across seeds, in contrast to adversarial methods like GAIL, which often exhibit arbitrary fluctuations (Figure 8). TRIRL is also robust to overtrained discriminators (Figure 7).
- **Computational Cost:** While more computationally intensive than purely adversarial methods due to the reward correction step and TRPL, TRIRL's implementation leverages JAX and JIT compilation to achieve feasible runtimes (Figure 6, Table 3, 4). LSIQ, being SAC-based, faces scaling challenges with parallelized environments, leading to higher runtimes unless specifically tuned.
- **Memory Usage:** The discriminator buffer incurs some VRAM overhead, but this is managed, and the method's overall memory footprint is comparable to or less than some baselines (Table 3, 4).

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The work presented in this paper offers several significant contributions and potential impacts on the field of Inverse Reinforcement Learning and Imitation Learning:

**1. Bridging Theoretical Guarantees with Practical Scalability:** TRIRL successfully bridges the gap between classical IRL methods, which offer strong monotonic improvement guarantees but are computationally prohibitive, and adversarial IL methods, which are scalable but suffer from instability and lack of monotonic dual improvement. By deriving a procedure that allows explicit dual ascent through local policy updates and reward correction, TRIRL provides a principled approach that is both stable and scalable.

**2. Enhanced Training Stability and Reliability:** Unlike adversarial methods that rely on a delicate balance between discriminator and policy updates and are prone to instability, TRIRL's non-adversarial nature, combined with its monotonic improvement guarantees and robust discriminator usage, leads to more stable and consistent training. This reduces the practical challenges associated with hyperparameter tuning and makes the method more reliable across different tasks and environments.

**3. Recovery of Global and Transferable Reward Functions:** A key advantage of TRIRL is its ability to learn global reward functions that are disentangled from the specific policy or environment dynamics. This enables these reward functions to be re-optimized from scratch and, crucially, to generalize and transfer effectively to environments with shifted dynamics. This capability is vital for real-world applications where agents need to adapt to unforeseen changes or operate in diverse conditions, moving beyond merely duplicating expert trajectories.

**4. Improved Performance Across Challenging Tasks:** The empirical results demonstrate that TRIRL outperforms several prominent state-of-the-art imitation learning methods across a range of complex continuous control and robotics tasks. This indicates a tangible step forward in achieving higher fidelity and more robust imitation.

**5. General Framework for IRL:** TRIRL is designed as a general framework, capable of accommodating various discriminator architectures (e.g., for state-only observations). This flexibility expands its applicability to different types of expert data and problem settings.

**Potential Impact:**
- **Advancement in Robotics:** The ability to learn transferable reward functions for complex humanoid and quadrupedal robots from limited demonstrations could significantly accelerate the development of autonomous agents for real-world robotic tasks, improving their adaptability and robustness.
- **Safer and More Reliable AI:** By providing a more stable and principled method for inferring intentions, TRIRL could contribute to the development of AI systems that are easier to understand, verify, and control, potentially leading to safer deployment in human-centric environments.
- **New Research Directions:** The theoretical insights regarding trust-region optimal policies and reward correction could inspire further research into developing other scalable and principled IRL algorithms, potentially exploring different divergence measures or policy optimization techniques.

**Limitations:**
Despite its contributions, the paper acknowledges several limitations:
- **Approximation Errors:** The theoretical guarantees are derived under ideal conditions (e.g., exact densities), and their translation to real-world settings with function approximation (neural networks for discriminators) may introduce approximation errors.
- **Computational Cost:** While more efficient than classical MCE-IRL, TRIRL's use of a discriminator buffer and the Differentiable Trust-Region Projection Layers (TRPL) can increase VRAM requirements and computational runtime compared to simpler adversarial methods, though parallelization helps mitigate this.
- **Trust Region Parameterization:** The choice of trust region bounds can affect convergence, and finding optimal values for `eta` (especially in the TR loss variant) can be challenging.
- **Ill-Posed IRL Problem:** Like prior work, TRIRL is still subject to the inherent ill-posedness of the IRL problem (temporal credit assignment problem), where different reward functions can lead to the same optimal policy. However, the framework allows for incorporating inductive biases to mitigate this.

In summary, TRIRL represents a notable development by reconciling the theoretical rigor of classical IRL with the practical demands of modern deep reinforcement learning, offering a stable, performant, and principled approach to learning transferable reward functions.
