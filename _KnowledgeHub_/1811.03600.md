# Measuring the Effects of Data Parallelism on Neural Network Training

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers at Google Brain conducted an extensive empirical study across 35 diverse neural network workloads to characterize how increasing data parallelism (batch size) affects training speed and model quality. The study revealed a universal three-regime relationship between batch size and training steps, demonstrating that proper, independent metaparameter tuning prevents inherent degradation in solution quality with large batches.

## Problem

- Neural network training time remains a critical bottleneck, limiting model quality and the scope of their applications despite hardware advancements.
- The effects of large-scale data parallelism, specifically via increased batch size, on training efficiency and final model quality were unclear due to seemingly contradictory findings in prior research.
- Many existing studies used inconsistent experimental protocols, often relying on fixed heuristics for metaparameter tuning, which obscured the true benefits or costs of large batch sizes.

## Method

- Conducted a rigorous, large-scale empirical study across 35 diverse neural network workloads, varying datasets (image, text), models (==CNNs==, ==ResNets==, ==Transformers==, ==LSTMs==), and optimizers (==SGD==, ==Momentum==, ==Nesterov==).
- Evaluated training efficiency by measuring the ==number of training steps== required to reach a predetermined goal out-of-sample error, a hardware-agnostic metric.
- Crucially, all optimization ==metaparameters== (learning rate, momentum, decay schedules, regularization) were independently and extensively tuned for each ==batch size== to accurately assess the impact of ==data parallelism==.

## Results

- All **35** workloads exhibited a three-regime scaling behavior: an initial perfect linear speedup (b-fold benefit), followed by diminishing returns, and ultimately a maximum useful batch size (ranging from approximately **2^9** to **2^16**).
- Momentum-based optimizers (SGD with momentum, Nesterov) consistently extended the perfect scaling regime to batch sizes up to **2^13**, demonstrating superior efficiency for large-batch training over plain SGD.
- When metaparameters were appropriately tuned, large batch sizes did not inherently degrade out-of-sample performance; the best achieved validation error depended more on the total allocated compute budget (total steps or epochs) than on the batch size itself.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - A universal, S-shaped relationship exists between batch size and the number of training steps, characterized by regions of perfect scaling, diminishing returns, and a maximum useful batch size.
> - Momentum-based optimizers significantly extend the perfect scaling region to larger batch sizes, leading to substantial reductions in total training steps compared to plain SGD.
> - Reported degradations in solution quality with large batches are primarily artifacts of suboptimal metaparameter tuning or specific computational budget assumptions, rather than an inherent consequence of increased batch size.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Neural networks have demonstrated effectiveness across various prediction tasks, with performance improvements partly driven by advancements in hardware for training. Despite significant speedups from modern GPUs and custom ASICs, training time remains a limiting factor for achieving peak predictive performance and broad application. Techniques that accelerate training offer benefits such as improved model quality (through training on more data), reduced experiment iteration time, and enabling models to be updated frequently.

Data parallelism, which involves distributing training examples across multiple processors to compute and aggregate gradient updates, is a widely adopted method to accelerate neural network training. This approach is model-agnostic for objectives that decompose as a sum over examples and is simpler to implement than model parallelism. Current hardware development trends towards increasing capacity for data parallelism, making it crucial to understand its costs and benefits, especially at unprecedented scales.

The existing literature presents seemingly contradictory views on the utility of large-scale data parallelism. Some studies suggest that large batch sizes (which correspond to increased data parallelism in synchronous SGD) can be detrimental, leading to poorer generalization or "sharp" minima. Conversely, other work indicates that large batch sizes can be beneficial, offering significant speedups without degrading model quality. This disparity in findings often stems from varying experimental protocols, assumptions about computational budgets, and methods for tuning optimization metaparameters (e.g., learning rate scaling heuristics).

This research positions itself as a rigorous and extensive experimental study designed to characterize the effects of data parallelism on neural network training across a wide range of realistic workloads. By carefully controlling experimental conditions, particularly by independently tuning metaparameters for each batch size, the authors aim to reconcile conflicting claims in the literature and provide a comprehensive understanding of how batch size affects training efficiency and model quality. The study focuses on synchronous mini-batch Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) and its variants, which are the dominant algorithms for neural network training.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to experimentally characterize the effects of increasing the batch size on neural network training time, specifically as measured by the number of steps required to reach a predetermined goal out-of-sample error. This objective is motivated by the recognized importance of training speed in advancing neural network capabilities and deployment.

To achieve this overarching goal, the authors identified three specific questions they aimed to address:

1. **What is the relationship between batch size and the number of training steps to reach a goal out-of-sample error?** This question seeks to empirically establish the functional form of this relationship across diverse neural network tasks, models, and algorithms, without making prior assumptions about optimal metaparameters at different batch sizes. Understanding this relationship is fundamental for practitioners to effectively leverage data parallelism and for hardware designers to assess the value of increased parallel capacity.

2. **What governs this relationship?** The research aims to identify how this relationship varies with properties of the training algorithm, the specific neural network model architecture (including its size and structure), and the characteristics of the dataset. By disentangling these factors, the authors seek to provide insights into what makes certain workloads more amenable to large-batch training than others. This understanding could inform the design of future algorithms, models, and specialized hardware.

3. **Do large batch sizes incur a cost in out-of-sample error?** This question addresses a significant debate in the machine learning community regarding the potential for large batch sizes to degrade the final model quality or generalization performance. The authors aim to investigate whether reported "generalization gaps" are inherent to large batch training or are artifacts of experimental methodology, such as insufficient tuning or specific computational budget constraints. Resolving this controversy has direct implications for how practitioners approach large-scale training.

The motivation for undertaking this comprehensive study stems from several critical needs:
- **Addressing Training Bottlenecks:** Training time limits the scale and speed of innovation in neural networks, hindering the development of more complex models and their application in time-sensitive scenarios.
- **Optimizing Hardware Utilization:** With hardware trends favoring increasing data parallelism, understanding its true benefits and costs is essential for guiding future system designs, ensuring that hardware resources are allocated effectively (e.g., towards more parallelism versus serial execution speed or other design goals).
- **Reconciling Conflicting Literature:** The diverse and often contradictory findings in prior research on batch size effects create confusion for practitioners. A systematic, extensive study with carefully controlled experiments is needed to provide clarity and a unified understanding.
- **Informing Best Practices:** The study aims to offer actionable advice to practitioners on how to configure training (e.g., choosing batch sizes, optimizers, and tuning metaparameters) to achieve desired performance goals most efficiently.

By releasing their extensive experimental data, the authors further motivate reproducibility and deeper analysis by the broader research community.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The study adopted a rigorous experimental methodology to investigate the effects of data parallelism, equating batch size with the degree of data parallelism in mini-batch Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) and its variants. The focus was on synchronous SGD due to its popularity and observed advantages over asynchronous methods.

**Primary Measure of Cost:**
The central metric for evaluating training efficiency was the **number of training steps necessary to reach a goal out-of-sample error** (validation error). This measure was chosen because it is hardware-agnostic and directly relates to total training time in idealized data-parallel systems where per-step time is independent of batch size. The authors explicitly differentiate this from "number of epochs" as a measure of cost, arguing that reducing training time in a data-parallel system can involve processing more epochs if it significantly reduces the number of steps.

**Workloads:**
The experiments spanned a diverse set of workloads, defined as a combination of a dataset, a model, and a training algorithm:

- **Datasets:** Seven datasets were used, encompassing both image classification and text language modeling tasks, with training set sizes ranging from 45,000 to approximately 25.8 billion examples.
 - Image Classification: MNIST, Fashion MNIST, CIFAR-10, ImageNet, Open Images v4 (multi-label).
 - Text Language Modeling: LM1B, Common Crawl.
 Evaluation metrics varied by task (classification error, average precision, cross entropy error).

- **Models:** Six families of neural network architectures were employed, allowing for investigation of architectural effects:
 - Fully Connected Networks (various depths/widths).
 - Simple Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs, various widths).
 - ResNet-8 (without batch normalization for comparison to prior work).
 - ResNet-50 (with batch normalization and residual connections).
 - VGG-11 (without batch normalization or residual connections).
 - Transformer (various sizes: base, shallow, narrow and shallow, wide).
 - LSTM.

- **Optimizers:** The study primarily focused on:
 - Plain Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD).
 - SGD with Momentum.
 - Nesterov Momentum.
 These optimizers were chosen as they are widely used and interact with training examples primarily through stochastic gradient computation, allowing a consistent definition of batch size.

**Metaparameter Tuning Strategy:**
A critical aspect of the methodology was the **independent tuning of metaparameters for each batch size**. This diverged from much prior work that often relied on heuristics (e.g., linear or square-root scaling of learning rates) derived from a single tuned batch size. The metaparameters tuned included:
- Initial learning rate ($\eta_0$).
- Momentum ($\gamma$, for momentum-based optimizers).
- Learning rate schedule parameters (e.g., decay factor $\alpha$ and decay steps $T$ for linear decay).
- Regularization weights (e.g., label smoothing coefficient).

**Tuning Process:**
- **Search Method:** Quasi-random search (Bousquet et al., 2017) was used to explore metaparameter spaces.
- **Trial Count:** Typically 100 non-divergent trials were run for each batch size and workload combination.
- **Search Space Refinement:** Search spaces were defined manually based on preliminary experiments and refined if optimal trials consistently occurred at the boundaries of the defined space or if the goal validation error was not achieved.
- **Optimal Selection:** The metaparameter configuration that achieved the goal validation error in the fewest number of steps was selected for each batch size.

**Learning Rate Schedules:**
The study experimented with both constant learning rates and various decay schedules. Linear decay was ultimately chosen for models like ResNet-8, ResNet-50, and VGG-11 due to its effectiveness and simplicity, requiring only two additional metaparameters ($\alpha$, $T$).

**Regularization:**
Label smoothing was applied in experiments with ResNet-50 on ImageNet and its coefficient was treated as a tunable metaparameter. For ResNet-50, ghost batch normalization with a fixed batch size of 32 was used to ensure a consistent training objective across different data batch sizes.

**Validation of Protocol:**
The robustness of the "steps to result" metric was assessed by varying goal validation errors and comparing results for validation error vs. training error, finding that the overall shape of the curves and transition points remained consistent. Metaparameter search spaces were verified to ensure optimal trials were not at boundaries.

**Data Availability:**
The authors made their raw experimental data publicly available, comprising 71,638,836 loss measurements from 168,160 individual models across 35 workloads, enabling further analysis and reproduction.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The study yielded several findings regarding the impact of data parallelism (batch size) on neural network training efficiency and model quality, primarily focusing on the number of training steps required to reach a goal out-of-sample error.

#### 4.1. Universal Relationship Between Batch Size and Training Steps

The research identified a consistent, characteristic relationship between batch size and the number of training steps required to reach a goal out-of-sample error across all 35 workloads examined. This relationship exhibits three distinct regimes:
1. **Perfect Scaling (b-fold benefit):** Initially, increasing the batch size by a factor of $k$ decreases the required number of training steps by the same factor $k$. This region's extent varied significantly, from batch size $2^4$ to $2^{13}$ across different workloads.
2. **Diminishing Returns:** Beyond the perfect scaling region, further increases in batch size lead to less than proportional reductions in training steps.
3. **Maximal Data Parallelism:** Eventually, there is a "maximum useful batch size" (ranging from approximately $2^9$ to $2^{16}$) beyond which increasing the batch size offers no further reduction in the number of training steps.
This characteristic S-shaped curve was found to be largely robust to small variations in the goal validation error and held for both training and out-of-sample error.

#### 4.2. Factors Governing the Batch Size-Steps Relationship

- **Optimizer Choice:**
 - Momentum-based optimizers (SGD with momentum and Nesterov momentum) significantly extended the perfect scaling region to larger batch sizes compared to plain SGD. This resulted in a substantial reduction in the absolute number of training steps for larger batch sizes.
 - At smaller batch sizes, where all optimizers were within their perfect scaling regime, plain SGD and momentum optimizers performed comparably. Nesterov momentum showed a slight advantage over SGD with momentum in some cases.

- **Model Architecture and Size:**
 - Different model architectures exhibited significant variation in their ability to exploit large batch sizes. For example, ResNet-50 on ImageNet could leverage much larger batch sizes than VGG-11, and Transformer models scaled better than LSTM on LM1B.
 - The effect of model size (width and depth) within a family was inconsistent. For Fully Connected models on MNIST, depth had a more pronounced effect than width. For Simple CNNs on MNIST, width had minimal impact. Conversely, narrower variants of the Transformer model scaled better to larger batch sizes on LM1B.

- **Data Set Characteristics:**
 - The choice of data set influenced the maximum useful batch size, but not in a simple, consistent manner (e.g., larger datasets did not consistently lead to larger useful batch sizes).
 - Experiments with subsets of MNIST showed negligible impact on the maximum useful batch size. For ImageNet, a random subset of half the images reduced the perfect scaling regime, while a subset with half the classes (similar size) showed scaling behavior very close to the full dataset, sometimes even extending the perfect scaling.

#### 4.3. Optimal Metaparameter Tuning

- **Non-linear Scaling:** The optimal effective learning rate (learning rate / (1 - momentum)) generally increased with batch size but did not consistently follow popular linear or square root scaling heuristics. In some cases, the optimal effective learning rate even decreased for very large batch sizes.
- **Joint Tuning:** The study highlighted the importance of jointly tuning learning rate and momentum, rather than keeping momentum fixed, as the optimal strategy for extending effective learning rates for models like Transformer involved increasing momentum while keeping the learning rate constant or decreasing it.
- **Sensitivity:** The metaparameters yielding the fastest training were frequently found near the edge of the feasible region of the search space, implying that small changes could lead to divergent training. This suggests challenges for metaparameter optimization and potentially motivates techniques like learning rate warm-up for stability.
- **Model Size and Learning Rate:** Smaller models were observed to tolerate larger stable learning rates, consistent with theoretical predictions relating learning rate to layer width.

#### 4.4. Solution Quality and Compute Budgets

- **No Inherent Degradation:** The study found no evidence that increasing the batch size *necessarily* degrades out-of-sample performance when metaparameters are properly tuned and sufficient compute resources are provided to reach performance saturation.
- **Compute Budget's Role:** The best achieved validation error was more dependent on the allocated compute budget (total training steps or epochs) than on the batch size itself.
 - Under a fixed **step budget**, larger batch sizes achieved better out-of-sample error, as they processed more training examples within the same number of gradient updates.
 - Under a fixed **epoch budget**, smaller batch sizes achieved better out-of-sample error, as they performed more gradient updates for the same total number of examples processed.
- **Reconciling Literature:** The authors concluded that many conflicting claims in the literature regarding solution quality degradation with large batches could be attributed to:
 - Assuming specific, often suboptimal, learning rate heuristics across batch sizes instead of independent tuning.
 - Comparing batch sizes under different compute budget assumptions (e.g., epoch-fixed budgets inherently penalize larger batch sizes if they haven't reached their maximal data parallelism regime).
- **Regularization:** Techniques like label smoothing were shown to be effective in mitigating overfitting at larger batch sizes (e.g., for ResNet-50 on ImageNet), suggesting they can replace the implicit regularization effect of small-batch noise. The effectiveness of regularization could vary with batch size and problem complexity.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research provides a comprehensive empirical foundation for understanding the intricate relationship between data parallelism and neural network training efficiency and quality. Its significance and potential impact span several key areas:

**For Practitioners:**
- **Actionable Guidance:** The study offers clear, empirically validated guidance: for optimal training speed, practitioners must *independently tune* all relevant optimization metaparameters (learning rate, momentum, schedule) for each chosen batch size. Relying on simple scaling heuristics for learning rates can mask the true benefits of large batches and lead to suboptimal or divergent training.
- **Optimizer Selection:** The strong evidence that momentum-based optimizers significantly extend the perfect scaling regime underscores their importance for large-batch training, suggesting practitioners prioritize their use.
- **Regularization Awareness:** The finding that regularization techniques like label smoothing can compensate for the "implicit regularization" lost with small batches at larger batch sizes means practitioners should actively consider and tune explicit regularization when scaling up batch sizes.
- **Realistic Expectations:** Practitioners gain a more nuanced understanding that the "maximum useful batch size" is workload-dependent, necessitating experimental characterization for new problems rather than assuming universal scaling behavior.

**For Hardware Design and Systems Research:**
- **Informed System Architecture:** The dramatic variation in how different workloads can utilize data parallelism provides critical input for hardware designers. If a workload's perfect scaling saturates early, investing heavily in extreme data parallelism might yield diminishing returns, encouraging consideration of other forms of parallelism or serial execution speed. Conversely, for workloads that scale well, large data-parallel systems are justified.
- **Focus on Efficient Parallelism:** The study validates the benefits of data parallelism but also highlights its limits, helping to balance the push for more parallel computation with the need for efficient algorithms and tuning strategies that can effectively exploit it.

**For Future Research:**
- **Optimizer Development:** The work motivates further research into whether other advanced optimizers (e.g., those using second-order information like KFAC) can consistently extend perfect scaling across a broader range of models and datasets, potentially overcoming the limits observed with momentum-based SGD.
- **Model Architecture Design Principles:** The inconsistent effects of model width and depth suggest a need to uncover general architectural principles that promote better batch size scaling, rather than ad-hoc experimentation.
- **Predictive Methods:** A significant future direction is the development of methods to *prospectively predict* the scaling behavior of a given workload with respect to batch size, reducing the need for expensive, extensive metaparameter tuning experiments at multiple batch sizes.
- **Methodological Rigor:** The study serves as an example of methodical, large-scale empirical investigation in deep learning, emphasizing the importance of careful experimental design (e.g., independent tuning) to disentangle confounding factors and reconcile conflicting findings in a rapidly evolving field.
- **Public Data Resource:** The release of a massive dataset of training measurements is a significant contribution, providing a valuable resource for other researchers to validate hypotheses, develop new analysis techniques, and build upon these findings.

In conclusion, this research moves beyond anecdotal evidence and limited comparisons to offer a systematic characterization of data parallelism's effects. It reconciles previous seemingly contradictory results by highlighting methodological nuances in prior work, primarily related to metaparameter tuning and computational budgeting. The findings provide a more reliable empirical basis for optimizing neural network training processes and guiding the design of future deep learning systems.
