# HuBE: Cross-Embodiment Human-like Behavior Execution for Humanoid Robots

> [!summary] Summary
> Researchers from The Hong Kong Polytechnic University and Eastern Institute of Technology developed HuBE, a bi-level closed-loop framework for humanoid robots that generates behaviors exhibiting both kinematic similarity to human motion and contextual appropriateness. The system achieves millimeter-level accuracy when transferring behaviors across diverse robot morphologies and demonstrates improved computational efficiency compared to existing methods.

## Problem

- Existing robot motion generation methods often prioritize kinematic similarity to human movement but overlook contextual appropriateness, leading to awkward or socially unacceptable robot actions.
- Current human motion datasets lack the fine-grained semantic annotations needed to link specific contextual situations with appropriate behaviors, hindering the learning of nuanced human-like actions.
- Many motion generation techniques struggle with cross-embodiment adaptability, making it challenging to transfer learned behaviors to humanoid robots with different morphologies without extensive re-adaptation.

## Method

- A bi-level closed-loop **HuBE** framework that integrates contextual information and robot-specific constraints into the motion generation and execution process.
- ==The HPose== dataset, which augments existing human motion data with LLM-generated, fine-grained situational annotations and bone scaling for cross-embodiment compatibility, paired with a hindsight training strategy for broader motion semantics.
- A multi-constrained Closed-loop Inverse Kinematics (CLIK) solver built on Pinocchio to accurately retarget generated poses to robot joint space, preserving human-likeness across different robot anatomies.

## Results

- The framework achieved millimeter-level accuracy in bone-size stability and generation errors when adapting to different robot skeletal systems (e.g., GR1, TALOS, G1) after training with the augmented HPose dataset.
- HuBE outperformed state-of-the-art baselines (siMLPe, HumanPlus) in behavioral appropriateness metrics (lower FMD and MM-Dist, higher R-Precision) and achieved superior Human Similarity (H-S) while maintaining acceptable End-effector Accuracy (E-A).
- The system demonstrated high computational efficiency, executing in 0.0207s, which is significantly faster than a pure IK algorithm (0.3618s), contributing to more responsive human-robot interaction.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Explicitly integrating fine-grained, language-based situational contexts significantly enhances both the kinematic similarity and contextual appropriateness of generated robot behaviors.
> - A bone scaling-based data augmentation strategy is effective for achieving millimeter-level cross-embodiment compatibility, enabling a single policy to generalize across heterogeneous humanoid robot designs.
> - Hindsight training, by sampling future poses as targets, substantially enriches the training dataset and improves the model's ability to generate diverse and contextually appropriate human-like motions.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

Humanoid robots are increasingly deployed in various human-robot interaction (HRI) contexts. A significant challenge in this field involves generating robot motions that are perceived as human-like by users. This perception is influenced by two key aspects: *behavioral similarity* and *behavioral appropriateness*. Behavioral similarity refers to the accurate reproduction of human kinematic and dynamic motion patterns. Behavioral appropriateness, conversely, pertains to the alignment of robot actions with situational demands and human cognitive expectations, ensuring that the movements are contextually meaningful and socially acceptable.

Previous research in generating human-like motions for humanoid robots has largely focused on achieving behavioral similarity through methods such as motion synthesis (e.g., using auto-regressive models or diffusion processes) and imitation learning (IL). Motion synthesis often produces physically infeasible poses requiring post-processing, and controlling specific joint signals can be difficult. IL methods, while learning directly from human demonstrations, frequently encounter difficulties with heterogeneous robot morphologies and novel task constraints, and may struggle with generalization. Furthermore, most existing datasets for human motion lack detailed semantic annotations that connect contextual situations to behavioral appropriateness, often relying on simple text descriptions or raw joint positions. The open-loop nature of many pose generation and retargeting processes also introduces structural mismatches between the generated poses and the robot's physical capabilities, leading to degradation in human-likeness. A notable limitation across these approaches is the insufficient attention to cross-embodiment adaptability, which impedes the deployment of learned behaviors on diverse humanoid platforms with varying kinematic parameters.

This work aims to address these identified limitations by developing a framework that explicitly integrates contextual information and robot-specific constraints into the motion generation process. It seeks to move beyond mere kinematic replication to ensure that robot behaviors are not only physically similar to humans but also semantically appropriate for a given situation, while also ensuring robust transferability across different robot hardware. This places the work at the intersection of human motion synthesis, robot control, and context-aware HRI, attempting to bridge gaps in data representation, model integration, and hardware compatibility.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objective of this research is to develop a framework, named HuBE (Human-like Behavior Execution), that enables humanoid robots to perform human-like behaviors characterized by both behavioral similarity and appropriateness, while also ensuring adaptability across diverse robot embodiments. The motivation for this objective stems from several challenges observed in the current state of humanoid robot motion generation:

1. **Ensuring Behavioral Appropriateness alongside Similarity:** Traditional methods often prioritize replicating kinematic patterns (behavioral similarity) but overlook the contextual relevance of actions (behavioral appropriateness). This can lead to motions that, despite being kinematically accurate, appear awkward or socially unacceptable in specific situations, potentially evoking discomfort in human observers, as described by the "uncanny valley" theory. The paper highlights an example where end-effector positions might be similar for "hugging a friend" versus "boxing with a friend," but the subtle full-body pose adaptations (e.g., elbow elevation) dictated by the context are crucial for appropriateness. The motivation is to enable robots to dynamically adapt their full-body poses to align with human expectations in diverse scenarios.

2. **Addressing Data Limitations:** Existing human motion datasets often lack the fine-grained semantic annotations necessary to associate contextual situations with appropriate behaviors. They typically provide 6D joint positions or short, simple text labels that do not capture the rich nuances of human behavioral contexts. This data deficiency prevents models from learning the complex interplay between situation and appropriate action. The motivation is to create a context-enriched dataset that explicitly bridges motion semantics with situational understanding.

3. **Eliminating Structural Mismatches in Motion Generation and Execution:** Many current systems employ an open-loop mechanism where human-like poses are generated independently and then retargeted to the robot without considering its current state or physical constraints during the generation phase. This decoupling can lead to structural mismatches, where the generated pose is physically infeasible or sub-optimal for the specific robot, thereby degrading the human-likeness and semantic integrity of the executed action. The motivation is to establish a closed-loop framework that integrates robot state and physical constraints directly into the motion generation process, ensuring seamless adaptation.

4. **Enabling Cross-Embodiment Adaptability:** Humanoid robots exhibit significant morphological differences (e.g., limb lengths, joint limits) across various commercial platforms. Most existing motion generation methods are designed for specific robot models or lack a robust mechanism for transferring learned behaviors to heterogeneous robots without substantial re-adaptation or retraining. This limits the scalability and practical deployment of human-like behavior generation. The motivation is to develop a strategy that allows a single learned policy to generalize and execute behaviors effectively across different humanoid robot designs at a high level of precision (millimeter-level compatibility).

By addressing these motivations, HuBE aims to provide a robust, transferable, and context-aware solution for human-like behavior execution, thereby enhancing the naturalness and social acceptance of humanoid robots in HRI.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

HuBE is a bi-level closed-loop framework comprising three main modules: data processing, behavior generation, and behavior execution.

#### 3.1. A. Data Specifications

The research introduces **HPose**, a context-enriched dataset derived from augmenting existing open-sourced datasets: KIT, AMASS, and Motion-X.

1. **Data Definition:**
 - The human body model was simplified to 11 main joints, focusing on the upper body, with hand positions being identical to wrist positions.
 - Joint rotations are represented by quaternions.
 - To enrich contextual information, fine-grained situational annotations (`l`) were generated using a Large Language Model (LLM), specifically GPT-4o. This involved providing the LLM with directed frames from motion sequences and existing, simpler annotations, prompting it to produce detailed descriptions of the behavioral situation. For example, a simple annotation like "running" could be expanded to "a human is running while looking ahead and moving arms rhythmically."

2. **Dataset Structure:**
 - Motion data for policy training is formatted into tuples `{(s_i, g_i, l_i, a_i)}`.
 - `s_i`: Apparent state, defined as the 11 joint poses of the current frame.
 - `g_i`: Goal state, representing the poses of the three end-effectors (both hands and head) from a subsequent frame.
 - `l_i`: Situational context, the detailed language annotation.
 - `a_i`: Action, the 11 joint poses from the subsequent frame intended to achieve `g_i` from `s_i` in context `l_i`.

3. **Data Augmentation:**
 - A bone scaling operation is introduced for data augmentation to ensure cross-embodiment compatibility. This generates a "pseudo ground truth" dataset (`H'`) by adjusting bone sizes based on parameters from target humanoid robots (e.g., Unitree H1, PAL's TALOS).
 - Equation (1) describes this scaling, where a directed joint pair `(J_i, J_k)` from the original human dataset `H` is scaled to match the corresponding bone length `||r_k - r_i||` from the robot's body parameters `R`. Algorithm 1 outlines the process.
 - Bones are categorized into "fixed" (stable length) and "floating" (length changes with body poses, e.g., spine) for precise scaling, with floating bones posing a greater challenge for adaptation.

#### 3.2. B. Pose Generation

The problem of humanoid robot motion generation is formulated as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) to learn an imitation policy `π_θ: S, G, l → A`. The policy aims to maximize the probability of demonstrated actions `a_i` given the current robot state `s_i`, goal `g_i`, and situational context `l_i` (Eq. 2). MLP and Transformer architectures are considered for the policy network.

1. **Inputs Alignment:**
 - To incorporate contextual appropriateness, the situational context `l` is integrated.
 - A BERT tokenizer (`BERT(·)`) converts `l_i` into a fixed vector.
 - An MLP (`MLP(·)`) maps the robot's proprioception (`s_i`, `g_i`) to a vector of the same size.
 - These aligned vectors are concatenated (`⊕`) to form `v_alig` (Eq. 3), serving as the comprehensive input for the policy, ensuring both motion (proprioception) and context (language) information are fused.

2. **Hindsight Training:**
 - To address the generation of arbitrary, potentially unseen, motion poses, a hindsight training strategy (inspired by hindsight experience replay) is employed.
 - Instead of always using the immediate next frame as the target pose, the dataset `D` is augmented (`D_new`) by randomly sampling future poses `g_{t+k}` within a frame window `H` as target goals (Eq. 4). This broadens the training experience and enhances the model's ability to generalize to varied target poses, enriching motion semantics.

#### 3.3. C. Behavior Execution

This module focuses on converting the generated human-like actions from Cartesian space into the robot's specific joint space while preserving human-likeness, especially for floating bones.

1. **Multi-constrained IK Solver (CLIK):**
 - A Closed-loop Inverse Kinematics (CLIK) algorithm, built upon the Pinocchio toolbox, is utilized.
 - It employs a two-step approach:
 - **Step 1:** Calculates torso joint angles (`J*torso`), including neck and head, prioritizing rotational pose accuracy due to the variable length of floating bones in the torso (Eq. 5, first part).
 - **Step 2:** A reduced robot model is created by locking the torso joints. Then, dual-arm joint angles (`J*arms`), such as wrists, are calculated (Eq. 5, second part).
 - All joint poses are processed by an SE(3) group filter (using Pinocchio's SE(3) interpolation algorithm) to enhance the stability of the IK solution.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The efficacy of the HuBE framework was evaluated using several metrics across multi-embodiment tests, ablation studies, and comparative analyses.

#### 4.1. A. Evaluation Metrics

- **Behavioral Similarity:**
 - **End-effector Accuracy (E-A):** Measures the pose accuracy of end-effectors (hands and head).
 - **Human Similarity (H-S):** Assesses pose consistency across all selected body markers.
 - Both E-A and H-S are quantified using Mean Per Joint Position Error (MPJPE) and Mean Per Joint Orientation Error (MPJOE). Lower values indicate better performance.
- **Behavioral Appropriateness:**
 - **Fréchet Motion Distance (FMD):** Measures the distributional similarity between generated and authentic human motions in a feature space. Lower FMD indicates higher appropriateness.
 - **Multimodal Distance (MM-Dist):** Calculates the average distance between the situation context and generated motions. Lower MM-Dist indicates better alignment.
 - **R-Precision:** Quantifies the semantic discriminability of generated motions by assessing text-to-motion cross-modal retrieval accuracy. Higher R-Precision indicates better semantic matching.

#### 4.2. B. Multi-Embodiment Testing

1. **Bone-size Stability:**
 - Testing on GR1, TALOS, and G1 robots showed that models trained only on the original Ground Truth (GT) dataset exhibited poor bone-size stability for both fixed and floating bone types. This indicated that the policy was not effectively adapting to different robot morphologies, leading to inaccurate bone lengths in generated poses.
 - After retraining the policy with the augmented "Pseudo GT" dataset (generated via bone scaling), generation errors were reduced to the millimeter level. This demonstrates that the data augmentation strategy successfully enabled the generation module to produce actions compliant with various humanoid skeletal systems.
 - Floating bones consistently showed greater sensitivity and larger errors than fixed bones, even after augmentation, due to their inherent variability in length with different body configurations.

2. **Retargeting Accuracy:**
 - Quantitative results (Fig. 5) demonstrated that the framework could be adopted to selected humanoid robots (e.g., GR1) while maintaining action accuracy and human similarity. MPJPE and MPJOE values for both E-A and H-S were small.
 - The MPJPE for H-S was generally higher than for E-A, attributed to the emphasis on rotational pose during retargeting for floating bones. However, consistent and small MPJOE for both metrics confirmed the accuracy and stability of the retargeting module.
 - Qualitative examples (Fig. 6) illustrated the framework's adaptability for action planning and driving various humanoids, showing planned poses that appropriately follow initial states and goals.

#### 4.3. C. Ablation Study

An ablation study using MLP-based and Transformer-based policies on the KIT ML dataset investigated the impact of various components.

1. **Ablation Study of Frequency:**
 - Training with fixed data frequencies (240Hz, 60Hz, 15Hz) showed performance improvements as frequency decreased, suggesting that very high frequencies might lead to insufficient differentiation between closely spaced frames.
 - The "Random Frequency" method, which is an implementation of hindsight training, significantly outperformed all fixed-frequency approaches across all behavioral similarity (E-A, H-S MPJPE/MPJOE) and appropriateness (FMD, MM-Dist, R-Precision) metrics. This indicates that randomly sampling future poses as targets effectively enlarges the dataset, enriches data range, and improves the model's ability to generate diverse and contextually appropriate motions.

2. **Ablation Study of Motion Situation (`l`):**
 - Models trained with the motion situation `l` consistently showed superior performance in both behavioral similarity and appropriateness compared to models trained without `l`. This underscores the importance of contextual information for generating appropriate human-like actions, especially when similar end-effector poses can signify different underlying intentions.

3. **Ablation Study of Observation:**
 - Missing observations of "pelvis, neck, shoulder, and elbow" had a relatively minor impact on E-A but severely degraded H-S and behavioral appropriateness. This is because these joints are crucial for maintaining the body chain integrity, which is essential for overall human-like pose generation.
 - Missing observations of "Hand and Wrist&hand" had less impact, as these end-effectors are part of the training target (goal poses), making the model implicitly learn to compensate for their absence in the input observation. The study concluded that ensuring the integrity of joint observations is necessary for robust performance.

#### 4.4. D. Comparative Results

The HuBE framework was compared against state-of-the-art methods: IK algorithm, siMLPe, and HumanPlus.

- **Behavioral Similarity:**
 - HuBE achieved superior MPJOE and MPJPE metrics for H-S compared to siMLPe and HumanPlus. These baselines, relying heavily on sequential motion traits, performed less effectively with unseen motion characteristics. HuBE's innovative mechanism, integrating context and hindsight training, contributed to this improvement.
 - Compared to the pure IK algorithm, HuBE exhibited slightly poorer but acceptable E-A (as IK is optimized solely for end-effector targets), but significantly better H-S. This indicates that HuBE prioritizes overall human-likeness while achieving reasonable end-effector accuracy.
 - A notable advantage of HuBE was its computational efficiency, with an execution time of 0.0207s compared to IK's 0.3618s, which relies on a searching mechanism.

- **Behavioral Appropriateness:**
 - HuBE achieved lower FMD and MM-Dist values and higher R-Precision scores than siMLPe and HumanPlus. This demonstrates HuBE's ability to generate motions of higher quality, with distributions closer to real actions, and superior semantic matching accuracy between generated actions and situational descriptions.

- **Action-wise Results:**
 - Evaluation across different actions (e.g., "Dancing," "Exercise," "Playing," "Running") showed stable and small evaluation metrics for HuBE (Table IV, Fig. 8). This confirmed the framework's adaptability and consistent performance in generating human-like poses for various tasks.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

This research introduces HuBE, a framework that advances human-like behavior execution for humanoid robots by concurrently addressing behavioral similarity, appropriateness, and cross-embodiment adaptability.

The primary significance lies in its holistic approach to human-likeness. By proposing a definition that encompasses both the kinematic fidelity (similarity) and contextual relevance (appropriateness) of robot actions, HuBE moves beyond purely aesthetic or functional replication. The integration of fine-grained language-based situational contexts via the HPose dataset and an LLM-driven annotation process directly addresses a gap in existing datasets, enabling the model to generate behaviors that are not only physically plausible but also socially and cognitively acceptable within a given scenario. This capability is critical for enhancing user comfort and acceptance in HRI, potentially mitigating issues related to the "uncanny valley."

The bi-level closed-loop framework, which fuses robot state, goal poses, and contextual information in an end-to-end manner, represents a methodological advancement. It mitigates structural mismatches common in open-loop systems, ensuring that generated poses are physically feasible and well-integrated with the robot's current state. The development of a bone scaling-based data augmentation strategy is significant for promoting cross-embodiment compatibility. This allows the trained policy to generalize across heterogeneous humanoid robots with millimeter-level precision, reducing the need for extensive retraining or complex adaptation mechanisms for each new robot platform. This contributes to the scalability and broader applicability of human-like behavior generation in robotics.

The experimental results demonstrate that HuBE outperforms current state-of-the-art baselines in terms of motion similarity, behavioral appropriateness, and computational efficiency. Its ability to generate contextually relevant and kinematically sound upper-body movements provides a foundation for more intuitive and natural interactions between humans and robots.

The potential impact of this work is substantial for the future development and deployment of humanoid robots in various domains such as assistance, entertainment, and education. By enabling robots to perform behaviors that are both perceptually and socially aligned with human expectations, HuBE contributes to making humanoid robots more effective, acceptable, and seamlessly integrated into human environments. While the current work primarily focuses on upper-body movements, the principles established here are foundational for future research into full-body expressive behaviors, promising further advancements in creating truly human-like robotic companions.
