# GraphThink: Graph-Enhanced LLM Thinking for Long-Horizon Embodied Task Planning

> [!summary] Summary
> GraphThink is a hierarchical LLM planning framework for long-horizon embodied tasks. A task graph of feasible subtask transitions shapes prompts, GRPO rewards, and an inference-time verifier, while a task-relevant dynamic scene graph drives event-driven replanning and a memory-aware low-level policy caches object locations. It tops the ALFRED leaderboard with 68.52% unseen success and 90.04% long-horizon planning accuracy.

## Problem

- General-purpose LLM planners lack physical grounding, so instructions get misread and hallucinated subtasks multiply as task complexity and horizon length grow.
- Supervised fine-tuning on limited in-domain trajectories generalizes poorly to unseen long-horizon tasks unless prohibitively expensive expert annotations are collected.
- Existing replanning triggers only on low-level execution failures, so plans that execute fine yet drift from instruction intent go undetected, causing myopic retries.

## Method

- **GraphThink**'s ==task graph==: directed subtask-transition graph built by LLM precondition/effect compatibility analysis, injected into prompts, used as an inference-time node/edge verifier with up to three verify-feedback-correct iterations.
- ==Graph-based GRPO==: total reward sums format, node-level object-validity (meta-class weighted), edge-level transition legality, and a two-stage instruction reward giving 0.5 for critical-subtask coverage, 1.0 for exact ground-truth match.
- ==Task-aware scene graph memory== plus ==event-driven replanning==: VLM builds filtered relation triples under 20 nodes; replanning fires on execution errors and subtask completion; ==memory-aware low-level policy== caches waypoints and masks.

## Results

- Tops the ALFRED test leaderboard at **67.71%** seen and **68.52%** unseen success rate without step-by-step instructions, beating the prior best by **6.17** points on unseen.
- High-level planner reaches **96.22/96.56%** on validation seen/unseen and **90.04%** on a new **1396**-sample long-horizon set, where SFT collapses to **20.57%** and GPT-5.2 with RAG trails badly.
- Ablations: dropping graph rewards cuts long-horizon accuracy from **86.25%** to **44.63%**; removing replanning costs about **8** points end-to-end; zero-shot generalization to novel actions hits **80.50%** versus **16.25%** for SFT.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Structural rewards over a transition graph beat single-trajectory supervision: rewarding any feasible path, not one annotated path, is what buys long-horizon generalization.
> - Edge constraints matter more than node constraints; pair structural reward with an instruction reward or the policy reward-hacks feasible-but-wrong plans.
> - Replanning on subtask completion, not just on failure, catches executable-but-misaligned plans; task-filtered scene memory keeps context small enough for cheap closed-loop reasoning.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How this Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The field of Embodied AI focuses on developing agents that can interpret natural language instructions, perceive environments through egocentric visual observations, and execute physical actions for navigation and interaction. Early approaches often involved training agents end-to-end using large-scale datasets. While effective for specific tasks, these methods are resource-intensive and exhibit limited generalization to unseen scenarios.

The research landscape has seen a shift towards hierarchical or modular planning methods as a data-efficient alternative. In this paradigm, a high-level planner decomposes human instructions into a sequence of subtasks, which a low-level executor then translates into primitive actions using a skill library. Recent advancements have increasingly leveraged Large Language Models (LLMs) for high-level planning due to their language understanding and reasoning capabilities. These LLM-based planners can also incorporate environmental observations for replanning when initial plans encounter failures.

However, existing LLM-based embodied agents face several limitations, particularly in long-horizon task planning. These include:
- **Physical hallucinations:** General-purpose LLMs often lack sufficient physical grounding, leading to misinterpretation of instructions and generation of non-executable plans, with failure rates increasing as task complexity grows.
- **Poor generalization:** Supervised fine-tuning on limited in-domain data often results in inadequate generalization to novel long-horizon tasks, necessitating expensive data annotation.
- **Lack of environmental awareness:** Many replanning strategies are reactive, primarily addressing low-level execution errors, and may lack the contextual awareness to identify plans that are executable but semantically misaligned with the overall instruction. This can lead to inefficient retries and myopic decision-making.

GraphThink addresses these limitations by proposing a closed-loop planning system that integrates structured knowledge representations—a task graph and a scene graph—into the LLM planning and replanning processes. This work contributes to the development of more robust, generalizable, and environmentally aware embodied agents capable of handling complex, long-horizon tasks. It positions itself as an advancement in grounding LLM reasoning with structured domain knowledge and dynamic environmental memory, moving beyond purely language-driven or reactive planning.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The primary objectives of the GraphThink framework are to mitigate the challenges faced by LLM-based embodied agents in long-horizon task planning. The motivation stems directly from three identified limitations:

- **Addressing physical hallucinations and instruction misinterpretation:** LLMs, despite their reasoning capabilities, often generate plans that are not physically executable or semantically coherent within a given environment. This is due to a lack of physical grounding. GraphThink aims to provide this grounding through a structured **task graph**. The task graph is intended to encode logical and executability constraints over subtask transitions, guiding the LLM to generate plans that are inherently more feasible and reduce the likelihood of "hallucinating" actions or object interactions that are impossible or nonsensical in the physical world.
- **Enhancing generalization to long-horizon and unseen tasks:** Existing methods often struggle to generalize to novel and complex tasks due to reliance on limited in-domain supervised training. GraphThink seeks to improve generalization by employing a **task graph-based reinforcement learning (GRPO)** approach. This method is motivated by the understanding that embodied planning often has multiple valid solutions for a single goal, making traditional exact-match reward designs challenging. The task graph allows for the design of reward signals that acknowledge diverse feasible paths, thereby enabling the LLM to learn more robust planning policies that align with high-level instructions without overfitting to specific training trajectories. This aims to reduce the need for extensive, task-specific annotations for long-horizon tasks.
- **Improving environmental awareness and enabling closed-loop error correction:** Many replanning strategies are reactive, focusing on correcting low-level failures, and often lack a comprehensive understanding of the environment and task progress. This can lead to inefficient plan adjustments or failures to detect plans that are executable but do not align with the instruction's intent. GraphThink is motivated to provide **event-driven replanning** capability powered by a **dynamic scene graph**. The scene graph acts as a task-centric environmental memory, providing relevant contextual cues to the LLM. This enables the agent to continuously evaluate plan feasibility against environmental feedback and make necessary adjustments, addressing both low-level execution errors and higher-level semantic misalignments with the instruction. The goal is to create a closed-loop system that continuously aligns plan execution with the user's intent.

In summary, GraphThink's overarching objective is to build a more robust, generalizable, and adaptive embodied agent by synergistically integrating structured task knowledge (task graph) with dynamic environmental memory (scene graph) to guide and correct LLM-based planning and execution.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

GraphThink is structured as a dual-graph enhanced closed-loop planning system, integrating a task graph and a scene graph across three core modules: High-level planning with task graph, Memory-aware low-level action policy, and Dynamic replanning with scene graph memory.

#### 3.1. A. High-Level Planning with Task Graph

The task graph (G = (V, E)) serves to ground high-level planning by modeling logical and executability constraints between subtasks. It guides plan generation, supports reward design, and acts as an external verifier.

1. **Task Graph Construction:**
 - **Subtasks as Nodes:** Nodes (v_i) represent subtasks, denoted as A_i(o_i) or A_i(o_i, o_j), where A_i is a high-level action and o_i, o_j are relevant objects. Subtasks are grounded in a predefined low-level skill set. Meta-classes (e.g., {obj, rec, mov, spec}) are used to abstract specific object instances, promoting generalization. During plan generation, meta-classes are instantiated with concrete environmental objects.
 - **Subtask Transition as Edges:** Edges (v_i, v_j) ∈ E indicate a feasible transition where v_j can follow v_i. The graph is constructed by LLM-assisted transition compatibility analysis, which considers semantic compatibility and resource-state feasibility. Each subtask's low-level policy is abstracted into a structured transition interface ⟨P_i, E_i⟩, where P_i are preconditions and E_i are effects. An edge exists if E_p satisfies P_q and the transition is semantically valid. This approach aims to capture generalizable dependencies beyond observed training trajectories.
 - **Scalability:** The task graph is designed to be extensible, allowing new subtask nodes and their incident edges to be added via transition compatibility analysis without requiring new trajectory demonstrations.

2. **Prompt with Task Graph:**
 The task graph is integrated into the LLM prompt to mitigate planning hallucinations. Subtasks are encoded as action-target pairs with unique labels, and edges are converted into symbolic connection relationships. Static prompt components (e.g., agent roles, planning rules, output format) are also included to facilitate in-context learning, guiding the LLM to generate subtask sequences that adhere to the graph topology.

3. **GRPO with Task Graph:**
 Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) is employed with a task graph-based reward function to enhance the LLM's reasoning and generalization. This reward function combines graph-based structural rewards with instruction-following rewards to handle the multi-solution nature of embodied planning tasks.
 - **Format Reward (R_fmt):** Encourages the model to produce outputs in the expected XML and JSON structure (1.0 for valid XML and JSON, 0.5 for valid XML only).
 - **Node-Level Reward (R_node):** Dynamically weighted object-validity scores encourage each generated subtask's objects to be valid and present in the environment (O_c^env). Weights are adjusted based on meta-class appearance in the plan.
 - **Edge-Level Reward (R_edge):** Encourages subtask transitions to conform to the feasible edges E in the task graph G (1.0 if (s_t, s_t+1) ∈ E, 0 otherwise).
 - **Instruction Following Reward (R_inst):** A two-stage reward based on the Ground-Truth (GT) plan S\*. It provides 0.5 for covering all critical subtasks (S\*_key) and 1.0 for an exact match with S\*, preventing reward hacking while accommodating multiple solutions.
 - **Overall Reward Function (R_total):** R_total = R_fmt + R_node + R_edge + R_inst.

4. **Verification with Task Graph:**
 The task graph acts as an external validation tool during inference. It verifies plan legitimacy using:
 - **Node-Level Legitimacy Verification (V_node):** Checks if each object in the subtask sequence is valid (i.e., exists in the environment).
 - **Edge-Level Legitimacy Verification (V_edge):** Checks if all action transitions satisfy the task graph's edge constraints.
 If validation fails, an error message is fed back to the LLM, triggering an iterative "Verify → Feedback → Correct" cycle (up to 3 iterations) to refine the plan.

#### 3.2. B. Memory-Aware Low-Level Action Policy

This policy translates high-level subtasks into primitive actions, dynamically grounded through environmental perception. It incorporates proactive caching and instance-level discrimination to enhance navigation efficiency and prevent redundant interactions. The agent proactively detects and caches information (location, segmentation mask) for task-related objects, including secondary targets encountered en route to a primary target. This allows subsequent subtasks to utilize cached positions, reducing re-exploration. It also maintains individual interaction records for multiple instances of the same object type to ensure precise identification and placement.

#### 3.3. C. Dynamic Replanning with Scene Graph

This module enables event-driven replanning, invoking an LLM-based reasoning process to adjust plans based on environmental feedback.

1. **Task-Aware Scene Graph Memory (G_scene):**
 A dynamic scene graph is maintained as a compact, task-aware environmental memory, consisting of semantic triples (o_i, r_ij, o_j) describing relations between task-relevant objects, along with key attributes.
 - **Construction:** Online during execution, not pre-built.
 - **Key Object Extraction:** Language model parses instruction (τ) to identify O_key ⊆ O_env.
 - **Viewpoint Capture:** Agent records egocentric views containing O_key, capturing relations only when the target object is in the forward-facing view (angular deviation < 45°).
 - **Candidate Triple Generation:** A Visual Language Model (VLM) processes RGB images and segmentation maps to generate candidate relation triples and object attributes, with segmentation maps aiding instance alignment.
 - **Scene Graph Update:** Filtered candidate entries are incrementally merged. Interaction-induced changes update the graph by replacing outdated relations.
 - **Error Prevention:** Preventive filtering prunes task-irrelevant or physically implausible triples. Conflicting new observations trigger re-observation and VLM reassessment.

2. **Event-driven Replanning:**
 The agent interleaves execution with reasoning, evaluating plan feasibility against environmental feedback. Replanning is triggered by two complementary events:
 - **Low-Level Action Error:** Handles concrete execution failures detected from environment feedback (e.g., interaction errors) or navigation policy (e.g., exhaustive search failure). The reasoning LLM integrates current scene observations with commonsense knowledge to revise the plan. Recoverable failures are first handled by the low-level policy, escalating only unresolved cases.
 - **High-Level Subtask Completion:** Proactively checks for semantically valid but goal-misaligned plans at subtask checkpoints. The LLM assesses the current plan against the updated scene graph memory and plan execution progress to verify alignment with the final objective, allowing proactive rectification of high-level errors.

When activated, the LLM assesses whether to keep or revise the plan based on the original plan, current subtask, feedback, and scene graph. All proposed revisions are constrained by the task graph to maintain feasibility.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The evaluation of GraphThink was conducted on the ALFRED benchmark, a custom long-horizon dataset, and for generalization to novel tasks in AI2-Thor and cross-environment transfer to VirtualHome.

#### 4.1. A. Comparison on ALFRED Benchmark

- GraphThink demonstrated competitive performance on the ALFRED test set, achieving success rates of 67.71% on 'Tests Seen' and 68.52% on 'Tests Unseen'. These rates represent increases of 2.92 and 6.43 percentage points, respectively, over the next leading method (EPO) on unseen tasks, and outperforming previous methods across all metrics.
- The approach maintained high Goal-Condition (GC) success rates of 73.96% (seen) and 75.76% (unseen).
- GraphThink achieved these results using only the goal instruction, indicating the strength of its high-level planning module without relying on step-by-step guidance.

#### 4.2. B. Ablations of GraphThink's Hierarchical Framework

Ablation studies on the ALFRED validation set revealed the contributions of each GraphThink component:
- Replacing GraphThink's task graph-based planner with a RAG-enhanced Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct planner resulted in a significant performance drop of approximately 27% in success rate (SR) on both seen and unseen validation splits, highlighting the importance of the proposed initial planning method.
- Removing the dynamic replanning module led to an 8-9% decrease in SR, demonstrating the necessity of adaptive error correction and environmental feedback.
- Separately ablating the low-level execution error driver and high-level subtask completion driver showed SR drops of 4-5% for each, confirming that both types of event-driven replanning contribute to robust execution.
- Disabling the object state and location memory in the low-level policy degraded performance by reducing object localization and increasing interaction errors, resulting in SR drops of approximately 1-2%.

#### 4.3. C. Evaluations of High-Level Planning

1. **Comparison with SOTA Planners:**
 - GraphThink exhibited competitive performance on the ALFRED validation set (96.22% on valid seen, 96.56% on valid unseen) and a custom long-horizon dataset (90.04%).
 - It consistently outperformed various leading API-based LLMs (Deepseek-R1, Gemini-2.5-Pro, GPT-5.2) under diverse inference settings (zero-shot, CoT, few-shot, RAG), particularly on long-horizon tasks where other models experienced significant performance degradation. For instance, GPT-5.2 with RAG achieved 82.9% on valid seen but dropped to 41.0% on long-horizon tasks, while GraphThink maintained 90.04%.
 - When using the same Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct backbone, GraphThink substantially outperformed Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) (90.04% vs. 20.57% on long-horizon tasks) and task-graph search baselines, indicating its ability to generalize to complex task compositions without extensive supervised long-horizon data.

2. **Ablations of High-Level Planner Components:**
 - Removing the task graph from the prompt led to a notable reduction in planning accuracy (e.g., from 90.04% to 40.69% on long-horizon tasks), demonstrating the graph's role in grounding LLM plans.
 - Replacing the GRPO-trained model with a base Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct model caused significant performance degradation across all settings, underscoring the importance of the task graph-based reinforcement learning framework.
 - The task graph verification loop, while having a modest impact on the validation set, improved performance by 3.79% on long-horizon tasks by correcting residual planning hallucinations.

3. **Ablations of GRPO Components:**
 - The task graph reward (R_node + R_edge) was found to be crucial for generalization and zero-shot learning; its absence led to a sharp drop in long-horizon performance (from 86.25% to 44.63%).
 - The edge-level transition constraints (R_edge) were particularly important, with its removal causing a more significant performance drop than that of the node-level reward (e.g., 50.50% vs. 76.50% on long-horizon tasks).
 - The instruction following reward (R_inst) was essential for aligning generated plans with user intent, preventing reward hacking. The two-stage design of R_inst (critical steps + exact match) outperformed single-stage variants.
 - The format reward (R_fmt) contributed to structural output consistency, showing a slight performance improvement.
 - Explicitly incorporating the task graph into prompts during training significantly improved training effectiveness, with performance drops (e.g., 86.25% to 59.46% on long-horizon) when this was removed.

4. **Impact of Training Data Volume:**
 - Model performance increased with larger training data volumes, with more pronounced improvements observed on long-horizon tasks compared to validation sets, suggesting that scaling data benefits learning of complex action spaces.

#### 4.4. D. Generalization to Novel Tasks and Environments

- **Novel Tasks in AI2-Thor:** GraphThink achieved a success rate of 80.50% on newly composed tasks incorporating unseen action primitives (e.g., BreakObject, FillObject, ThrowObject) in AI2-Thor. This significantly outperformed RAG GPT-5.2 (65.75%), few-shot CoT, and SFT on the same backbone (both ~16%), indicating strong zero-shot generalization to novel skill compositions.
- **Cross-Environment Generalization to VirtualHome:**
 - With domain-specific adaptation (re-training on VirtualHome data), GraphThink achieved the highest success rates (93.00% on WAH-NL and 88.89% on VirtualHome-HG).
 - Even with cross-domain transfer (using an ALFRED-trained planner with a semantic translation layer), GraphThink (90.00% on WAH-NL, 76.67% on VirtualHome-HG) remained competitive with strong baselines, demonstrating the reusability of high-level planning priors and effective transferability of the methodology.

#### 4.5. E. Performance Analysis across Varying Task Horizons

Detailed analysis across tasks with increasing subtask counts (7 to 12) revealed:
- **Performance Scaling:** GraphThink exhibited moderate degradation in planning success rate (98.73% to 80.71%) and maintained a graph pass rate above 85% as task horizons increased. In contrast, baselines (GPT-5.2+RAG and SFT) showed severe performance drops, with SFT's accuracy nearing zero beyond 9 subtasks.
- **Computational Costs:** GraphThink maintained stable inference times (7–10s for 7–11 subtasks) and remained lower than GPT-5.2+RAG for 12-subtask tasks (23.7s vs. 32.78–46.90s), demonstrating efficient scaling.
- **Reasoning Fidelity:** GraphThink achieved consistently low wrong transfer rates (0.02–2.16%) and near-zero affordance errors, indicating accurate action-object grounding. While moderate fluctuations were observed in missing and additional step rates on longer horizons, these errors remained well controlled compared to baselines.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

GraphThink introduces a framework that addresses several limitations in LLM-based embodied task planning, particularly concerning long-horizon and generalized tasks. The primary significance of this work lies in its integration of structured knowledge representation (task graph) with dynamic environmental memory (scene graph) to enhance LLM reasoning and robustness.

The use of a **task graph** provides explicit physical grounding, which helps to mitigate planning hallucinations, a common issue with general-purpose LLMs. By guiding the LLM's plan generation through contextual prompting, integrating graph-based rewards in a reinforcement learning framework (GRPO), and serving as an external verifier, GraphThink enables the generation of more feasible and logically consistent subtask sequences. This structured guidance is particularly impactful for complex, multi-step tasks where errors can accumulate rapidly.

The **GRPO framework with task graph-based rewards** is significant for addressing the challenge of generalization. By acknowledging multiple valid solution paths, the reward design allows the LLM to learn more robust planning policies that align with overall instructions, rather than over-specializing on specific training trajectories. This contributes to improved zero-shot and few-shot capabilities, crucial for deploying agents in novel scenarios without extensive, costly annotations. The demonstrated ability to generalize to a novel long-horizon dataset, unseen action primitives in AI2-Thor, and different environments like VirtualHome underscores its potential for broad applicability.

Furthermore, the **event-driven replanning module powered by a dynamic scene graph** introduces a closed-loop mechanism for continuous environmental awareness and error correction. Unlike reactive error correction, this module can proactively identify and rectify semantic misalignments between the plan and the environment, not just low-level execution failures. The task-aware nature of the scene graph memory focuses reasoning on relevant information, enhancing efficiency and adaptability.

The collective impact of these components is a more robust, generalizable, and efficient embodied agent. The high-level planner's performance on long-horizon tasks, coupled with controlled computational costs and strong reasoning fidelity across increasing task complexities, indicates a step towards more capable AI assistants for real-world applications. Future work could build upon this foundation by exploring extensions to asynchronous planning or multi-robot collaborative systems, which might involve incorporating temporal-state edge augmentation or multi-layer graph constructions. The framework's modularity and extensibility also suggest potential for integration with evolving low-level policies and larger-scale, more diverse embodied AI benchmarks.
