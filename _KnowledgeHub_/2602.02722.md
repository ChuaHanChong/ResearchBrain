# Hierarchical Entity-centric Reinforcement Learning with Factored Subgoal Diffusion

> [!summary] Summary
> HECRL is a hierarchical entity-centric reinforcement learning framework that integrates unsupervised object-centric representations and a conditional diffusion model for subgoal generation, tailored for offline learning from pixel observations. It demonstrates over a 150% increase in success rate on long-horizon, multi-entity manipulation tasks like PPP-Cube Image compared to non-hierarchical baselines.

## Problem

- Long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards are challenging for standard RL, especially when combined with high-dimensional image observations.
- Environments with multiple interacting entities suffer from combinatorially complex state spaces, making learning and generalization difficult for existing offline Goal-Conditioned RL (GCRL) methods.
- Learning effectively from fixed, potentially suboptimal offline datasets requires robust and sample-efficient algorithms that avoid exploiting out-of-distribution actions.

## Method

- A modular, two-level ==hierarchical== framework is proposed, using pre-trained unsupervised Deep Latent Particles (DLPv2) to extract object-centric representations from raw pixel inputs.
- A low-level GCRL agent, based on EC-IQL and an Entity Interaction Transformer (EIT) architecture, executes actions to achieve immediate subgoals.
- A high-level entity-centric conditional diffusion model (Subgoal Diffuser) generates sparse, multi-modal intermediate subgoals, which are then filtered and selected using the low-level value function to ensure reachability and progress.

## Results

- HECRL consistently achieves state-of-the-art performance on image-based long-horizon multi-object manipulation tasks, exhibiting over a **150%** increase in success rate on the challenging PPP-Cube Image task compared to non-hierarchical baselines.
- The Subgoal Diffuser generates subgoals that modify significantly fewer entities (1.0-1.4 entities on average in state-based tasks) compared to AWR-based approaches (2.8-2.9 entities), providing simpler subtasks for the low-level policy.
- The framework demonstrates non-trivial zero-shot compositional generalization, successfully operating with an increased number of entities (e.g., trained on 3 cubes, tested on up to 6 cubes) and outperforming flat entity-centric baselines in these out-of-distribution scenarios.

## Takeaways

> [!tip] Key Insights
> - Combining hierarchical planning with explicit factored (entity-centric) representations and generative diffusion models effectively addresses the combinatorial complexity and long horizons in multi-object environments.
> - Conditional diffusion models are superior to deterministic regression for generating subgoals, as they can sample distinct, valid multi-modal subgoals that involve sparse entity modifications, rather than ambiguous averages.
> - Value-based filtering and selection of subgoals at test-time are crucial for guiding the high-level policy, ensuring that generated subgoals are reachable by the low-level agent and lead towards the ultimate goal.

## Detailed Report

### 1. How This Work Fits into the Broader Research Landscape

The paper addresses several interconnected grand challenges in Reinforcement Learning (RL) and sequential decision-making: long-horizon tasks, sparse rewards, high-dimensional observations, and combinatorial complexity in multi-entity environments. It positions itself at the confluence of several active research areas:

#### 1.1. Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL)

GCRL is a powerful framework that allows agents to learn policies that generalize across various goals. While it has shown promise with deep RL (e.g., Schaul et al., 2015), its effectiveness significantly diminishes with complex goal distributions, high-dimensional observation spaces (like images), and especially sparse reward signals. The paper acknowledges that current offline GCRL methods, even recent benchmarks like OGBench (Park et al., 2025a), struggle with combinatorial state-spaces and image observations.

#### 1.2. Hierarchical Reinforcement Learning (HRL)

HRL is a well-established paradigm for tackling long-horizon tasks by decomposing them into sequences of simpler subgoals, enabling reasoning over multiple timescales. Techniques like goal relabeling (Andrychowicz et al., 2017) and various hierarchical architectures (Levy et al., 2019; Chane-Sane et al., 2021) have advanced this field. The paper specifically references HIQL (Park et al., 2023), which extracts a two-level policy hierarchy from a single value function, as a state-of-the-art method for offline GCRL. However, it highlights HIQL's limitations when confronting combinatorial complexity and image inputs, which HECRL aims to overcome.

#### 1.3. Entity-Centric / Object-Centric RL

This area focuses on environments where the state can be naturally described as a collection of interacting entities ($S = S_1 \otimes S_2 \otimes ... \otimes S_N$). The combinatorial growth of the state space with the number of entities poses a severe challenge. Entity-centric approaches leverage this factored structure as an inductive bias to simplify learning, improve sample efficiency, and facilitate compositional generalization. This involves learning factored state representations (often from images) and using set-based architectures (like Transformers). The paper builds on prior work in unsupervised object-centric representations (e.g., DLP, Locatello et al., 2020; Daniel & Tamar, 2022) for downstream RL, notably Haramati et al. (2024).

#### 1.4. Diffusion Models in Sequential Decision-Making

Diffusion models have recently gained popularity for their remarkable ability to generate complex, multi-modal data distributions. Their application to sequential decision-making is a rapidly growing area, broadly categorized into diffusion policies (modeling action distributions) and diffusion planners (modeling state or state-action trajectory distributions). The paper mentions hierarchical diffusers that generate subgoal trajectories (Li et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024). HECRL integrates a conditional diffusion model for *generating single immediate subgoals*, distinguishing its application from those that generate full trajectories or act as policies. The use of an *entity-centric diffuser* (Qi et al., 2025) specifically for factored subgoals is a key novelty here.

#### 1.5. Offline Reinforcement Learning

The work is situated in the offline RL setting, where agents learn exclusively from fixed datasets of previously collected (often suboptimal) trajectories, without further environment interaction. This is crucial for real-world applications where data collection can be expensive or unsafe. The challenge here is to learn robust policies that do not exploit out-of-distribution actions.

By combining these elements, HECRL attempts to synthesize the strengths of each field. It seeks to overcome the limitations of hierarchical GCRL in multi-entity environments by incorporating explicit factored structure and leveraging the generative power of diffusion models for subgoal generation, all within the constraints of an offline learning setting. The goal is to move towards more scalable and generalizable RL agents capable of handling real-world complexity, such as robotic manipulation from pixels.

### 2. Key Objectives and Motivation

The central objective of this research is to propose and validate a **hierarchical entity-centric framework for offline Goal-Conditioned Reinforcement Learning (GCRL)**, specifically designed to solve long-horizon tasks in environments characterized by multiple entities.

#### 2.1. Primary Motivation and Challenges

- **Long-Horizon, Sparse Reward Tasks:** These remain a core challenge in RL. Standard GCRL struggles because rewards are infrequent, making it difficult for value functions to propagate meaningful signals over many timesteps (compounding approximation errors, low value signal-to-noise ratio).
- **Combinatorial Complexity in Multi-Entity Domains:** Environments with many objects (e.g., robotics, multi-robot systems) have state spaces that grow combinatorially. This makes learning intractable for traditional RL methods and even for state-of-the-art offline GCRL, including hierarchical approaches like HIQL, especially when combined with high-dimensional image observations.
- **High-Dimensional Image Observations:** Real-world applications often involve raw pixel inputs, which are difficult for RL agents to process directly without good representations. The lack of supervision for object segmentation adds another layer of complexity.
- **Offline Learning Setting:** The constraint of learning from fixed, potentially suboptimal datasets requires robust and sample-efficient algorithms.

#### 2.2. Key Objectives of HECRL

To address these motivations, the authors set out to achieve the following specific objectives:
1. **Integrate Subgoal Hierarchy and Factored Structure:** Develop a framework that explicitly combines subgoal decomposition with the inherent factored structure of multi-entity environments.
2. **Scale to Image Observations without Supervision:** Design a method capable of handling high-dimensional image inputs by leveraging unsupervised object-centric representations, thereby making it applicable to real-world scenarios like pixel-based robotics.
3. **Generate Entity-Factored Subgoals:** Encourage the high-level policy to produce subgoals that involve sparse modifications to entities. This "sparseness bias" is hypothesized to simplify subtasks for the low-level policy, making them easier to achieve.
4. **Modular and Adaptive Design:** Ensure the two hierarchical levels (low-level GCRL agent and high-level subgoal generator) can be trained independently and composed post-hoc. This modularity promotes compatibility with various existing value-based GCRL algorithms and allows for flexible test-time adjustments.
5. **Demonstrate Robust Performance:** Empirically show that the proposed method consistently boosts the performance of underlying RL agents on image-based long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards in combinatorially complex settings.
6. **Show Generalization Capabilities:** Evaluate the framework's ability to generalize to increasing task horizons and varying numbers of entities, which is critical for scalability in real-world scenarios.

In essence, HECRL aims to create an RL agent that is not only capable of long-horizon planning but also intelligently leverages the structured nature of its environment, even when perceiving it through complex, unsupervised visual data, all while learning from limited offline experience.

### 3. Methodology and Approach

The Hierarchical Entity-Centric Reinforcement Learning (HECRL) framework employs a modular, two-level hierarchy to tackle long-horizon, multi-entity tasks in offline GCRL from high-dimensional observations.

#### 3.1. Core Components

1. **Unsupervised Object-Centric Representations:** To handle high-dimensional image observations, HECRL uses pre-trained unsupervised object-centric representations. Specifically, it employs **Deep Latent Particles v2 (DLPv2)** (Daniel & Tamar, 2024), which transforms raw pixels into a set of latent particle vectors, each representing an entity (or part thereof) in the scene. This provides the factored input crucial for the entity-centric processing.
2. **Low-Level GCRL Agent:** This level consists of a value-based GCRL agent responsible for executing actions to reach an immediate subgoal. It comprises a policy $\pi(s, \tilde{g}) \rightarrow a$ and a value function $V(s, g) \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$.
 - **Underlying Algorithm:** The paper primarily uses **Implicit Q-Learning (IQL)** (Kostrikov et al., 2022) as the base offline RL algorithm.
 - **Architecture:** For entity-centric processing, it integrates the **Entity Interaction Transformer (EIT)** architecture (Haramati et al., 2024), adapted for IQL, to handle the set-based latent particle inputs. This forms the **EC-IQL** baseline, which HECRL builds upon.
 - **Training:** The low-level policy and value function are trained on the full goal distribution from the offline dataset, meaning any state reachable in a future timestep can be a goal. This decouples its training from the high-level's fixed 'K-step' horizon.

3. **High-Level Subgoal-Generating Conditional Diffusion Model:** This component, termed the "Subgoal Diffuser" $D(s, g) \rightarrow \tilde{g}$, generates intermediate subgoals $\tilde{g}$ to guide the low-level agent towards the ultimate goal $g$.
 - **Model Type:** It is a conditional diffusion model, adapting the **EC-Diffuser** (Qi et al., 2025). Diffusion models are well-suited for capturing multi-modal distributions, which is important for subgoals, as there can be multiple ways to make progress towards a goal.
 - **Factored Subgoal Generation:** The model operates on entity-centric latent representations. Given sets of state entities $s=\{s_m\}$ and goal entities $g=\{g_m\}$, it gradually denoises a set of noisy subgoal entities $\tilde{g}_\tau=\{\tilde{g}_{\tau,m}\}$. This entity-centric structure, combined with a Transformer denoiser, encourages the generation of **entity-factored subgoals** – subgoals that involve sparse modifications to a subset of entities from the current state, simplifying the subtask.
 - **Training Data:** The Subgoal Diffuser is trained on the offline RL dataset to model the distribution of states $\tilde{g}$ that are at most $K$ timesteps away from a current state $s$, given a future goal $g$. This involves sampling a current state $s_t$, a future goal $g=s_{t_g}$, and an intermediate subgoal $\tilde{g}=s_{\min(t+K, t_g)}$.

#### 3.2. Test-Time Subgoal Generation Procedure (Algorithm 1)

HECRL's modularity allows the low-level agent and Subgoal Diffuser to be trained independently and then combined at test time through a selective subgoal generation and filtering process.
1. **Subgoal Candidate Sampling:** At fixed intervals ($T_{sg}$ timesteps), the agent samples $N$ subgoal candidates $\{\tilde{g}_i\}$ from the Subgoal Diffuser, conditioned on the current state $s$ and the ultimate goal $g$.
2. **Value-Based Filtering (Reachability Constraint):** The candidates are filtered based on the low-level value function $V$. Only subgoals $\tilde{g}_j$ that are considered "reachable" by the low-level policy are kept, meaning $V(s, \tilde{g}_j) > \hat{R}$, where $\hat{R}$ is a learned policy competence radius threshold. This addresses the problem of value approximation errors and ensures the low-level policy has a clear signal.
3. **Value-Based Selection (Progress Towards Goal):** From the filtered, reachable subgoals, the one that makes the most progress towards the ultimate goal is selected: $\tilde{g} = \arg\max_{\tilde{g}_j} V(\tilde{g}_j, g)$. This effectively guides the high-level planning.
4. **Receding Horizon Control:** The low-level policy $\pi(s, \tilde{g})$ is then executed for $T_{sg}$ timesteps using the selected subgoal. This process resembles Model Predictive Control (MPC).
5. **Direct Goal Convergence:** If at any point the current state $s$ is evaluated by the value function to be closer to the ultimate goal $g$ than the generated subgoal $\tilde{g}$ (i.e., $V(s,g) > V(\tilde{g},g)$), the agent directly targets the ultimate goal $g$ to aid convergence.

#### 3.3. Key Design Principles

- **Decoupled Training:** The separation of training for the low-level policy (on full goal distribution) and high-level diffuser (on K-step subgoals) enhances flexibility.
- **Test-Time Flexibility:** The critical parameter $\hat{R}$ (policy competence radius) can be tuned at test-time without re-training, allowing implicit selection of the low-level policy's effective horizon.
- **Inductive Bias for Factored Subgoals:** The entity-centric diffusion mechanism is specifically designed to encourage sparse, interpretable changes to objects, making subtasks simpler.

This methodology constructs a robust hierarchical agent that exploits structural inductive biases from object-centric representations and the multi-modal generative capabilities of diffusion models, guided by a value function, all while operating within the practical constraints of offline learning.

### 4. Main Findings and Results

The experiments conducted across a suite of challenging multi-object manipulation environments—PPP-Cube, Stack-Cube, Scene, and Push-Tetris—demonstrate the significant advantages of HECRL.

#### 4.1. Superior Performance on Long-Horizon Manipulation Tasks

- **Consistent Performance Boost:** HECRL (EC-SGIQL) consistently and significantly outperforms all baselines (EC-IQL, EC-Diffuser, HIQL, IQL) across most tasks, especially on image-based long-horizon tasks with sparse rewards.
- **Dramatic Improvement on Hardest Task:** On PPP-Cube from images, identified as the most challenging task, HECRL achieves over a 150% increase in success rate (64.3% vs. 25.0% for EC-IQL and 0% for others).
- **Entity-Centric Advantage:** EC-IQL, the non-hierarchical entity-centric baseline, is generally the second-best performer, highlighting the crucial role of incorporating factored structure in these domains, even without the hierarchical component.
- **Limitations of Unstructured Methods:** HIQL and IQL, which do not incorporate entity-centric inductive biases, perform poorly, particularly on image-based tasks, consistent with challenges observed in the OGBench benchmark. EC-Diffuser, a behavioral cloning method, also shows limited success compared to HECRL, especially on complex tasks.

#### 4.2. Impact of Design Choices (Ablation Study)

- **Value-Based Subgoal Filtering and Selection are Critical:** Ablations confirm that the combination of filtering reachable subgoals (using $V(s, \tilde{g}) > \hat{R}$) and selecting the one closest to the ultimate goal (using $\arg\max V(\tilde{g}, g)$) is essential for optimal performance.
 - "Random Sample" (no filtering or selection) and "Max Value" (no filtering) variants performed worse or on par but less efficiently, taking more timesteps to reach goals.
- **Diffusion Models Outperform Advantage-Weighted Regression (AWR) for Subgoal Generation:** Replacing the Diffusion model with a deterministic Transformer trained with an AWR objective ("AWR" variant) results in the lowest performance across the board.
 - This is attributed to AWR producing "weighted averages of observations" that often do not correspond to valid states or unambiguous subgoals, in contrast to diffusion models which can sample from distinct multi-modal distributions.

#### 4.3. Quality of Subgoals: Encouraging Factored Changes

- **Sparse Entity Modifications:** In state-based Cube environments, the Subgoal Diffuser (HECRL) generates subgoals that modify only around 1.0-1.4 entities on average (out of 3 cubes + robot arm). In stark contrast, AWR-based approaches (both entity-centric and unstructured) modify nearly all entities (2.8-2.9 entities) in their generated subgoals.
- **Qualitative Visualization (Image-based):** Reconstructions of latent subgoals from the Diffuser in image-based environments visually confirm that subgoals often represent sparse changes (e.g., moving one specific object). Conversely, AWR-generated subgoals frequently exhibit duplicated or ambiguous entities, reflecting the "averaging" effect of regression. This provides the low-level policy with clearer, simpler tasks.

#### 4.4. Compositional Generalization

- **Non-Trivial Zero-Shot Generalization:** HECRL demonstrates promising zero-shot generalization to an increased number of entities (e.g., trained on 3 cubes, tested on up to 6 cubes). While performance degrades gracefully with more entities, it consistently surpasses the flat entity-centric baseline (EC-IQL) in these out-of-distribution scenarios.
- **Scaling Potential:** These results suggest that HECRL's entity-centric and hierarchical structure provides a strong inductive bias that facilitates scaling to more complex, variable-entity environments, hinting at its potential for curriculum learning or offline-to-online fine-tuning.

#### 4.5. Hyperparameter Robustness

- A sensitivity study on the most challenging task (PPP-Cube Image) revealed that HECRL is largely robust to hyperparameters like $K$ (subgoal training horizon), $T_{sg}$ (subgoal rollout timesteps), and $N$ (number of diffusion samples), as long as they are within reasonable ranges. This robustness makes the method more practical to deploy.

In summary, the main findings strongly support HECRL's efficacy. It not only achieves state-of-the-art performance by effectively combining hierarchical planning with factored representations and diffusion models but also provides empirical evidence for *why* it works: by generating clear, sparse, and achievable intermediate subgoals that simplify the overall long-horizon task for the underlying RL agent.

### 5. Significance and Potential Impact

The "HIERARCHICAL ENTITY-CENTRIC REINFORCEMENT LEARNING WITH FACTORED SUBGOAL DIFFUSION" paper makes a substantial contribution to the field of Reinforcement Learning by addressing several critical limitations of current approaches. Its significance and potential impact can be summarized as follows:

#### 5.1. Advances in Solving Complex RL Challenges

- **Long-Horizon, Sparse-Reward Tasks:** The paper demonstrates a highly effective framework for tackling one of the most persistent challenges in RL: achieving long-horizon goals with sparse rewards in complex environments. This is crucial for real-world applications where dense reward signals are often unavailable.
- **Scaling to Combinatorial State Spaces and High-Dimensional Observations:** HECRL successfully scales to environments with combinatorially complex multi-entity state spaces and high-dimensional image observations, a bottleneck for many existing RL methods. This is a significant step towards deploying RL in realistic settings where perception from raw pixels is necessary.

#### 5.2. Synergistic Integration of State-of-the-Art Techniques

- The work masterfully integrates and builds upon several cutting-edge areas: hierarchical RL, object-centric representations, diffusion models for generative planning, and offline RL. By combining these, HECRL creates a more robust and capable agent than any single component could achieve in isolation. This demonstrates the power of interdisciplinary approaches in AI research.
- The use of unsupervised object-centric representations (DLPv2) is particularly impactful, as it enables the system to learn from raw image data without expensive human annotation or ground-truth state information, making it more practical.

#### 5.3. Modularity and Flexibility

- The modular design, which separates the low-level GCRL agent from the high-level subgoal generator and allows for independent training and post-hoc composition, is a key strength. This flexibility means HECRL can be easily adapted to and integrated with future advancements in value-based GCRL algorithms, fostering further research and development.
- The ability to adjust the policy's effective competence radius at test-time (via $\hat{R}$) provides practical control without requiring costly retraining, a valuable feature for real-world deployment.

#### 5.4. Generation of High-Quality, Factored Subgoals

- The novel use of an entity-centric conditional diffusion model to generate subgoals is a core innovation. The empirical evidence that this approach produces "entity-factored subgoals" (sparse modifications to entities) is highly significant. These simpler, more targeted subgoals make the low-level tasks easier to learn and execute, directly addressing the compounding error problem in HRL.
- This finding highlights the advantage of generative models over deterministic regression for multi-modal subgoal distributions, showing that sampling distinct modes leads to clearer subgoals than averaging them.

#### 5.5. Promising Generalization Capabilities

- The demonstration of non-trivial zero-shot compositional generalization to increasing numbers of entities suggests a strong capacity for scaling. This is vital for applications where the exact configuration of objects or agents may vary in deployment compared to training. It points towards future avenues for learning robust skills that transfer across diverse object compositions.

#### 5.6. Potential Applications

- **Robotics:** The results are particularly promising for robotic manipulation tasks, where long-horizon planning, multi-object interaction, and pixel-based perception are common challenges. This could lead to more capable and autonomous robots.
- **Multi-Agent Systems:** The entity-centric nature naturally extends to multi-robot path planning or coordinating multiple agents in complex environments.
- **Autonomous Driving and Video Games:** These domains often involve rich, high-dimensional observations and interactions with multiple dynamic entities, where HECRL's capabilities could be highly beneficial.

#### 5.7. Future Directions and Broader Impact

- The paper openly acknowledges limitations, such as reliance on a sufficiently informative value function and the need for accurate factored state estimators. This sets a clear agenda for future work, including automatically inferring optimal planning horizons ($K$) and further advancements in unsupervised object-centric learning for real-world scenarios.
- By pushing the boundaries of what's achievable in complex, offline GCRL, HECRL contributes foundational insights that can inspire new designs for intelligent agents, ultimately bringing us closer to developing truly general-purpose AI systems capable of learning and reasoning effectively in unstructured, multi-object environments.

In conclusion, HECRL represents a significant methodological leap in hierarchical offline GCRL. Its principled integration of structured representations and advanced generative models offers a powerful new paradigm for tackling long-standing challenges, with substantial implications for the development of more capable and generalizable AI systems.
